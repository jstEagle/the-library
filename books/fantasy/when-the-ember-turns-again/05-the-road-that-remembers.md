## 5. The Road That Remembers

The hounds had found them twice by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and both times Mirabel had bought them an hour with bitterroot smoke and a lie about their trail. Twice was enough. The caravan could not outrun what was bred to find it; they would have to become unfindable, which meant leaving the road everyone knew for roads almost no one did.

Dax Ferrow stood at the fork below Crag's Rest with his one eye narrowed against the glare off the snowfield, and when Mirabel told him where she meant to take them, he said the thing she'd expected and dreaded.

"That's smuggler country. Old country. You know what you're asking?"

"I know there are passes up there that don't appear on any surveyor's map," she said. "Roads cut before the usurper was born, kept alive by people who moved goods the crown didn't tax. My father ran wool through them. I walked them as a girl."

"Your father," Dax said slowly, "ran wool through Thornfell." He didn't laugh, but something in his face shifted, a door opening onto a room she hadn't known he kept. "Then you'll know the naming."

"The naming?"

He tapped the side of his good eye. "Out here, past the last waystation, the maps stop meaning anything. You navigate by names — old names, kingdom names, the ones the usurper struck from every record he could burn. Say the new name of a place out loud on those heights and the mountain doesn't answer. Say the old name, and it does. Ask any of my drivers. Half of them can't read, but they all carry a list of names in their heads like a rosary."

Mirabel looked up at the white shoulders of the pass, at the dark seam of forest climbing toward a saddle she could not yet see, and felt the strange vertigo of a memory becoming useful again. For six years her past had been a liability, a thing to be disguised, diluted, buried under courtly manners and plausible denials. Now it was currency. Now it might be the only coin the mountains accepted.

* * *

They climbed through the morning in single file, the wagons left behind at a stone shelter Dax swore had held his grandfather's brandy, everything now loaded onto mules and backs. The air thinned and sharpened. Pine gave way to birch, birch to bare granite laced with ice, and beneath their boots the path revealed itself gradually as a path at all — a shelf cut into the cliff face, wide enough for two men abreast, its retaining wall fitted without mortar by hands that had trusted the stone to hold itself.

Emeric walked near the middle of the line, wrapped in a driver's oilskin three sizes too large, hood drawn low over a face that was mostly cheekbone and pallor. Nineteen years old and shaped like a boy who'd spent his growth indoors, which, Mirabel understood now, was exactly what he was. Three years in a spice wagon. Before that, other hiding places, other names. He had been raised the way you raise a candle flame inside a lantern — protected from wind, starved of air, never allowed to burn bright enough to be seen.

And yet.

It happened at the first junction, where the shelf split around a shoulder of rock furred with yellow lichen. Dax's lead driver, a leathery woman called Brannoc, hesitated, muttered half a name under her breath — *the Wether's Stair*, or something like it — and got no further. The snow of two hard winters had eaten the cairn that marked the turn.

Mirabel knew the junction. She had stood here at eleven years old, her father's hand on her shoulder, while he made her repeat the rule: *the mountain keeps its own ledger, and the ledger is written in the old tongue.* She reached back for the name her father had given this place, dredging it up from the silt of six court years, and came back with something half-right, half-guessed, a syllable missing like a tooth.

But Emeric, standing quietly behind her with his hands tucked into his sleeves, said: "Vael's Ladder."

Not *the Wether's Stair*. Not anything close. A name she had never heard, in a voice so soft she almost missed it — and yet the moment he spoke it, the mountain seemed to agree with him. Brannoc straightened. Dax turned. And the path beyond the junction, which had looked like nothing but scree and wishful thinking, resolved into a stairway of steps cut clean into the rock, ascending in long switchbacks toward a notch of blue sky.

"You've been here?" Mirabel asked him, thrown.

"No," Emeric said. It was nearly the first full sentence he'd offered anyone since the hounds. "I've never seen these mountains. But I know the names. My grandmother taught me. She said the names were the last thing they couldn't take, because they lived in people, not in paper." He paused, embarrassed, then added, "I say them wrong sometimes. My accent is a wagon accent. But the mountain doesn't seem to mind."

Dax made a sound between a cough and a prayer. "Three years I've hauled that boy like contraband," he said, "and nobody thought to ask if he was also a map."

"He didn't know it himself, maybe," Mirabel said. "You don't inventory what you were raised inside of."

She understood it viscerally, better than anyone in the caravan. Her palate was the same kind of inheritance — a thing built dish by dish, dose by dose, so deep in her body that she had stopped experiencing it as knowledge. It was simply what her tongue did. Emeric's names were what his childhood had done. Neither of them had chosen their gifts. Both of them had survived by keeping them quiet.

Something passed between them at that junction that was not quite trust and not quite gratitude, but adjacent to both. He had given the mountain a name and the mountain had answered. She had led them to the junction at all. Two strangers, each holding half a key, discovering the lock fit either hand.

* * *

By afternoon they were above the tree line entirely, crossing a high shelf the old maps had called — Emeric supplied it without being asked — *the Shepherd's Table*, though the usurper's surveyors had recorded it as nothing at all. The wind up there had teeth. It came off the icefields in long gusts that flattened the mules' ears and made speech a negotiation shouted between gusts, and it carried, Mirabel knew, scent the way a river carries silt. Somewhere behind and below them, the gift-hunters' hounds were reading that river.

So she worked. This was the part she loved, if love was the word for it — the craft of unbeing. At each rest halt she fed the wind false trails: a rag soaked in tallow dragged east along the shelf, a pinch of powdered galbanum scattered to mimic the musk of a larger party, a decoy firepit built of green wood that would smoke wrong and smell wrong and buy them another hour of confusion. Dax watched her do it with the expression of a man revising a ledger he'd thought balanced.

"You're worth double what I charged you," he said at the third halt. "I want that noted. In writing, ideally, since apparently I'm going to live long enough to be sued."

"Noted," Mirabel said. "You charged me double for secrets. Consider this the interest."

"It's more than interest. Brannoc's run these heights twenty years and she couldn't have masked us off a cold trail like that." He glanced sideways at Emeric, hooded and silent against a boulder. "And the boy reads the old names like scripture. Between the two of you, we're navigating with instruments nobody else alive has got."

That was the thought that lodged in Mirabel's chest and refused to leave: *nobody else alive.* For six years she had been singular, solitary, a specimen pretending to be ordinary. Here, for the first time, her strangeness was not a secret to be managed but a tool in a shared kit. Brannoc deferred to her on the wind. Dax deferred to her on the trails. And on the names — the deep names, the ones that predated every border the usurper had redrawn — they all deferred, a little wonderingly, to a sickly nineteen-year-old in a borrowed oilskin who had never seen a mountain until this week.

There was no ceremony in it. No moment where anyone said *you belong*. Just competence, recognized and returned, compounding hour by hour like interest. Brannoc began saving Mirabel the lee side of the fire. Dax began consulting her before decisions instead of after. Emeric, who had spent three years being cargo, began — carefully, tentatively, one name at a time — being crew.

At dusk they crossed beneath a natural arch of granite that Emeric named aloud — *the Crownstone, because the old kings were sworn beneath it* — and the whole caravan stopped, unbidden, and stood in the wind for a moment with their hats off. Mirabel didn't know if the story was true. She suspected Emeric didn't either. But the stopping felt right, the way a pause feels right in music, and afterward the column climbed on in a silence that was less grim than before. Less like fugitives. More like pilgrims.

* * *

The second night they sheltered in a hollow the locals had once called — Emeric again, unprompted, his voice cracking slightly on the consonants — *Emberwatch*, and Mirabel went very still when she heard it.

"Say that again," she said.

"Emberwatch." Emeric frowned. "Why? Do you know it?"

"I know the word ember." She crouched by the fire, turning her hands to the heat, and weighed how much to say. There was a legend her mother's people told, a taster's legend, the kind of story poison-eaters traded in whispers across three kingdoms: that when the dynasty fell, a door was sealed in the high mountains, a gate into the old country, and that it waited for royal blood and a body freely offered. She had always filed it with the other stories tasters told — half comfort, half warning, the folklore of people whose profession was dying politely.

"Emberwatch," she said slowly. "My grandmother used a word like that. In a story about a door."

Emeric's head came up. For the first time since she'd met him, the boy looked fully awake, the pallor burning off like fog. "What door?"

"A sealed one. In the high passes. The stories say it opens for royal blood." She met his eyes across the fire, and saw him understand, and saw the understanding frighten him and light him at once. Around them the caravan slept in shifts, breath steaming, mules stamping, the wind worrying the hollow's rim like a dog at a bone.

Dax, wakeful as always, had drifted close enough to hear. He said nothing. He just looked at the two of them — the taster and the heir, the immunity and the bloodline, each holding a piece of a legend neither had believed in alone — and his one eye did its slow arithmetic.

Above the hollow, far up the black shoulder of the mountain, Mirabel saw a light. One point of flame, steady and wrong — too high for any camp, too still for any torch. It burned for the space of ten heartbeats, then went out.

Nobody else seemed to have seen it. She decided, for now, to say nothing.

But she lay awake a long time after, listening to the wind learn the shape of the hollow, and thinking about doors.
