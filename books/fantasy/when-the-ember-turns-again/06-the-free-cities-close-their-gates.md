## 6. The Free Cities Close Their Gates

Dax Ferrow had a saying, and he'd said it so many times over thirty years of roads that his people could recite it in their sleep: *every road leads somewhere open.* He'd learned it as a boy in the salt-flats caravans, when the free cities still meant what they said on their gates — *all who come in peace may eat at our tables* — and he'd built his whole crooked, careful life on top of it like a house on a foundation stone. Smugglers didn't need kings. Smugglers needed doors that stayed open, and there had always been doors.

He stood now on a ridge above the Vale Road with his one eye narrowed against the morning haze, and watched the door of Vellamarre close.

It was not a dramatic thing. That was the worst of it. No horns, no burning, no soldiers in usurper's black marching up to bar the way. Just workmen. Just carpenters on ladders along the eastern wall, hauling up great pale planks and nailing them over the arch of the Gate of Pilgrims, plank after plank after plank, while a clerk in good wool stood below with a ledger open on a stand, checking names off a list Dax couldn't read but understood perfectly. Refugees had been arriving all night — he'd seen their cookfires strung along the road like a rosary of small desperate lights — and now the clerk was deciding which handful would be let through the one unboarded postern before that too went dark, and which hundreds would be turned back down into the vale to winter in the ditches.

"They're sealing it," said Sefa beside him. She was his wheelwright, sixty years old, hands like tree roots, and she had crossed eleven borders in her life without once being asked for papers. Her voice was flat in the way voices go flat when a person is trying very hard not to feel something large. "Vellamarre's sealing its own gate."

"Not sealing." Dax spat, slow and deliberate, because spitting gave his mouth something to do besides say the other thing. "Nailing. Sealing you can undo. That's carpentry for a funeral."

Below them, the caravan waited in the trees — nine wagons, forty-one souls counting the mule boys, and one nineteen-year-old boy wrapped in blankets in the spice wagon, coughing in the small wet way that had gotten worse every day since the high passes. Emeric. The last grandson of a dead dynasty, hidden in Dax's cargo for three years, and now the whole machinery of the usurper's hunt was grinding down out of the mountains behind them like an avalanche that had learned patience.

They needed a city. Any city. Walls, a bribable harbormaster, a quarter where a sick boy could disappear into ten thousand faces. That was the arithmetic Dax had been doing since Thornfell, and this morning the first term of it turned to ash under his boots.

"Get them moving," he said. "South road. Harrowmere, maybe. Harrowmere's always hated Vellamarre."

Sefa looked at him with her root-hard hands folded, and did not move, and said gently, "Dax. When did you last hear from Harrowmere?"

He hadn't. That was the truth of it, and they both knew it. Six months. Maybe eight. In the old days silence from a contact meant nothing — smugglers ran silent half the year by design. But the old days were the ones being nailed shut behind him, plank by plank, and he knew it the way you know weather coming: in the bones, before the sky agrees.

* * *

Harrowmere answered on the second evening, and the answer was a crossbow bolt through the wagon canvas six inches above Emeric's sleeping head.

Not aimed to kill. Aimed to be *seen*. Dax stood in the road outside the river-gate with his palms open and empty while the bolt quivered in the fabric behind him, and looked up at the wall walk where a man he'd drunk with for twenty years — Odo Brask, harbor-side fixer, godfather to two of Dax's mule boys — stood with the reloading crank already turning in his hands.

"Dax Ferrow." Odo's voice carried flat across the moat water. "You have some nerve showing that face here."

"I've got nerve showing anywhere, that's why I'm alive. Odo. Open the wicket. Five minutes, just us, just talk—"

"There is no *us*." And there it was — not anger in the man's voice, which Dax could have worked with, could have levered and joked and greased until it cracked — but something worse. Embarrassment. Shame, even, worn thin over fear. "There's a treaty now. The city signed in the spring. Free cities stay free by staying neutral, and neutral means no fugitives, no unregistered gifts, no — " the pause was tiny, contemptuous, terrible — "*royalists.* You know what the usurper pays for royalists? You know what he does to cities that shelter them? Ask Corvane. Oh — you can't. Corvane burned in the autumn."

Corvane. Dax felt that land somewhere under his ribs, a cold settling weight. Corvane had been the third-largest of the free cities, fat and proud, famous for its glass. He'd drunk there in the summer. It seemed impossible that a place could simply stop existing between one season and the next, and yet the usurper's armies were marching toward the lowlands even now, and every city on their path was doing the same cold arithmetic Vellamarre's clerk had done with his ledger: how much is our soul worth, and can we get it in writing.

"Odo." Dax kept his voice low, kept it friendly, kept it the voice of a thousand profitable nights. "I'm not asking for the boy. I'm asking for a barn. A cellar. One winter, off your books, the way you've moved things off books for me since before your hair went grey."

For a long moment the only sound was the moat water and the creak of the crank. Then Odo leaned on the parapet, and Dax saw his face properly for the first time — older, yes, but more than older. *Diminished.* A man who had once moved half the contraband on the river, standing now inside walls he clearly no longer trusted, holding a weapon against a friend because the alternative was trusting the wrong ledger.

"The hounds came through last month," Odo said quietly. "Gift-hunters. They brought a sniffer-breed into the market square and made the magistrates watch it taste the air. Then they posted the rates. Registration means bread and a badge. Refusal means—" He stopped. "Go south, Dax. Go past the cities entirely. There's nothing behind these walls anymore but waiting."

The wicket gate did not open. Dax stood in the road a long time after the wall walk emptied, looking at the bolt-hole in his canvas, and then he walked back to the wagons and told Sefa to burn the cloth and re-canvas the wagon, and did not tell anyone what else had been said.

* * *

They tried three more doors in four days, and Dax learned the shape of the new country the way a blind man learns a room — by collision.

Saltmere refused them at the toll bridge with a printed notice nailed to the barrier, the usurper's seal stamped in red wax thick enough to peel: *NO UNREGISTERED PERSONS. NO REFUGEES WITHOUT SPONSOR. THIS BRIDGE IS CLOSED BY TREATY.* The toll-keeper wouldn't even meet Dax's eye; he just pointed at the notice with his chin, again and again, like a man defending himself with furniture.

At Casterhale, Dax got as far as a back room and a cup of wine with a woman named Ilvetta, an old partner in the spice trade, before she put her hand flat on the table between them and said, "I'll buy your cargo. All of it. Full price. But the caravan goes east around the lake, and whatever's in that spice wagon stays out of my city." She'd guessed. Of course she'd guessed — Ilvetta had always been able to smell profit and danger in the same breath, and Dax had never decided which of those she loved more. "Don't look at me like that. If I hide him and they find him, I lose everything, and so does everyone whose name I give up under questioning. I'm not brave, Dax. I'm solvent. There's a difference, and this year the difference is everything."

"And if it were you in the wagon?" Dax asked. "If it were you coughing under the canvas?"

Ilvetta looked at him with something almost like tenderness. "Then I'd hope someone braver than me was driving."

That night the caravan camped in a cut barley field with no fires, because fires drew eyes, and Dax walked the line of wagons doing the count he did every night — forty-one, forty-one, forty-one — and found himself stopping at the spice wagon longer than the others. Inside, by the light of a shuttered lamp, Mirabel sat on the tailgate with Emeric's head pillowed against her shoulder while the boy slept off another fever. She looked up when Dax stopped.

"Harrowmere shot at us," she said. Not a question. She'd heard the bolt, then. Of course she had.

"Warning shot."

"There's no such thing anymore." She shifted carefully so the boy's breathing didn't break rhythm. "You've been gone from camp every day knocking on doors. How many are left?"

Dax did the arithmetic honestly, because she'd earned honesty and lies cost more now than truth. "Ilvetta offered to buy the cargo. Saltmere's closed. Vellamarre's boarded. Corvane's ash. Every city on the map is either bought, burned, or boarding itself up and praying the armies pass it by." He laughed, short and ugly, with no humor in it anywhere. "Thirty years I told my people every road leads somewhere open. You know what I've learned this week? Roads don't lead anywhere. *Cities* used to lead somewhere. And the cities have all decided that the way to stay free is to stop being free first, one plank at a time, before anyone makes them do it the hard way."

Mirabel was quiet a moment. The lamp threw her shadow long across the stubble field.

"In the mountain hollow," she said, "you heard us talking. About the Ember Gate."

"I hear most things."

"You said your eye did its arithmetic." Her mouth curved, tired and dry. "So do mine. Here's mine: there is no asylum left in the lowlands. Not one door. The usurper owns every gate that opens outward, and the hunt is behind us, and the boy gets sicker every day in this cold. Which leaves exactly one direction left on the map, and it isn't south or east or any direction a road goes." She looked at him steadily. "It's *back*. Up. Into the mountains, to a door that officially doesn't exist, that opens for blood and a willing body — and Dax, I need you to understand what I'm about to ask, because I've thought about it for three days and I haven't said it aloud to anyone."

Above them the clouds had torn open, and the stars over the Thornfell massif were hard and clear, and Dax felt the old creed die in him quietly — *every road leads somewhere open* — and something stranger take its place, something that wasn't a road at all but a single door in a mountain, sealed for six years, waiting.

"Ask," he said.

But before she could speak, Sefa came fast out of the dark between the wagons, lantern hooded, walking too quickly for anything good.

"Dax," she said. "Riders on the south road. Lanterns doused. A lot of them. And they're not moving like soldiers."

She paused, and her root-hard hands were shaking.

"They're moving like hunters."
