## 7. The Ember Gate

The legend came to Mirabel the way all true things came to tasters: bitter at first, then unmistakable.

They had sheltered three nights in the hollow beneath a fallen watchtower, a ruin so old that even Emeric had needed to think before he could name it — *Caer Vellin, where the last queen's cupbearer kept her accounts* — and it was Emeric who said it, half-asleep, his fever-flushed face turned toward the fire.

"My grandmother's nurse used to sing about a door. *The ember turns again, the ember turns again.* I always thought it was about the hearth. About keeping fires alive in winter." He coughed. "You're a taster. Do you know the song?"

Mirabel went very still.

Every taster knew the song. It was not sung aloud anymore, not since the dynasty fell and the guilds were broken and the tasters scattered like ash on wind. It was a counting-song, a teaching-song, a way to memorize the poisons of the world in rhyme so a child could hold death on her tongue and not be afraid. But it had verses the guild-masters never taught, verses passed mouth to mouth in the last years, when it became clear the kingdom was dying and something would need to be kept.

*When the ember turns again, when the door drinks deep,*
*What was bought by dish and dish, the mountain's own to keep.*
*A body freely offered, no chain, no blade, no fear —*
*The gate remembers loyalty. The gate remembers here.*

She had learned it at nine years old from an old woman named Sefa, who had tasted for the queen's cousin and who taught Mirabel the forbidden verses as payment for a kindness. *You'll never need them,* Sefa had said. *But a taster should know what she's worth. It's more than you think. It's less than you'd hope.*

"Dax," Mirabel said, and her voice came out strange. "How far are we from the Cinder Stair?"

Dax Ferrow looked up from the map he was pretending to study, his one eye catching the firelight. "Two days, if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't. Why?"

"Because there's a door at the top of it."

* * *

They climbed through cloud the next morning, and Mirabel told them what she knew, because they deserved to know it before they spent their legs on it.

"There's a legend among tasters. The Ember Gate. A door into the old country — not over the mountains, but *through* them, a passage the old dynasty kept for the worst of days. When the usurper took the throne, the last loyal servants sealed it from the far side. It can only be opened by royal blood." She glanced at Emeric, riding slung in the spice wagon like cargo, gray-faced and shivering. "And by a body freely offered."

"Freely offered to do what?" Dax asked. He was not a stupid man, and she could see him already refusing to be stupid.

"The gate drinks protection." Mirabel turned her hands over, looked at them as though they belonged to a stranger. Six years of dishes. Six years of swallowing what would kill a room and walking away with a headache and a story. "Every immunity a taster builds is bought from her own body, dish by dish, year by year. The old songs say the gate was made by taster-alchemists in the dynasty's last century — a vault of protections, poured into the stone, waiting. It will open for a king. But it needs a key of flesh. It takes everything a willing body has hoarded against death, all at once, and it spends it all in the turning."

The silence that followed was long enough for the wind to fill it.

"And the person who pays," Dax said slowly, "walks away with nothing. No protection. No gift. Just—" He gestured vaguely at the world, at its poisons, its crossbows, its winter.

"Just what everyone else has," Mirabel said. "A body that can be killed."

Emeric had gone white. "No," he said. "No, absolutely not. I won't—"

"You'll do nothing," Mirabel said, more sharply than she meant to. "You'll sit in that wagon and stay alive, because you're the only royal blood left and the door doesn't care how sick you are. It only cares whose blood you carry." She looked away from him, toward the cloud-wrapped peaks. "The choice was never yours to make. It was always going to be a taster's."

* * *

They reached the Cinder Stair at dusk on the second day, and Mirabel understood, looking at it, why the legend had survived only in song.

The stair was not built. It was *found* — a seam in the mountain's flank where the rock fell away in steps of black glassy stone, veined with threads of dull red that caught the last light like banked coals. No snow lay on it. That was the first wrongness. The wind screamed around them on the approach, driving sleet sideways, and the stair itself stood dry and faintly warm, as though the mountain kept a hearth behind its ribs.

At the top, set into the living rock, was the door.

It was smaller than Mirabel had imagined — no monument, no arch, just a slab of dark metal furred with age, no taller than a man, no wider than two standing shoulder to shoulder. It bore no sigil. It needed none. The old dynasty had never put its emblem on the things it trusted most.

Around the door's frame, in a script older than the usurper's grandmother, ran a single line. Emeric, half-carried up the last steps with Dax's shoulder under his arm, read it aloud in the old tongue, his voice cracking on the cold.

"*What was freely given is freely spent. What was hoarded is not wanted here.*"

"Charming," Dax muttered. "The door has opinions."

Mirabel stepped forward alone.

Up close she could feel it — not hear, not see, but *feel*, the way she felt a poison's signature under her tongue before her mind had named it. A pressure. A patience. Sixty years of waiting, and beneath the waiting, an appetite as vast and impersonal as winter. The gate was not cruel. It was not kind. It was a door, and doors only wanted one thing.

She thought of the first dish. She was surprised to find she remembered it whole — the winter court, six years ago, the pheasant in plum sauce, the bitter thread of oleander-wine beneath the sweetness, and herself, nineteen years old and terrified, swallowing it and smiling and learning in that one terrible moment what she was. She had spent every day since buying her life one dish at a time, hoarding death's refusals in her blood like a miser, telling herself it was luck, telling herself it was nothing, because to name it was to make it a thing the crown could take.

They had come to take it anyway. The gift-hunt, the registration, the conscription — the usurper wanted exactly what she carried, and he wanted it in chains.

The gate wanted it freely.

That was the difference, and Mirabel stood before the dark metal and felt the strangest thing unfurl in her chest, under the terror, under the cold: *relief*. Not because she wanted to suffer. Not because she romanticized the burning. But because for six years her survival had been a secret, a debt, a lie told daily with her own body — and here, at last, was a door that told the truth about what she was worth. Not luck. Not a curiosity. A fortune, gathered dish by dish, enough to buy back a kingdom.

No one had ever asked her to give anything before. Everyone had only ever asked her to endure.

"Mirabel." Dax's voice, rough. "There has to be another way. Give me a week. There are alchemists in the free cities, someone who knows—"

"The free cities closed their gates, Dax. You watched them do it." She turned and looked at him, and then at Emeric, who was weeping without noise, the way children of hunted families learn to. "There's no asylum left on this side of the mountains. There's a whole country on the other side, and it's been holding its breath for sixty years, and the only thing it's waiting for is standing right here shivering." She smiled, and found the smile was real. "I've tasted poison for a usurper for six years. Let me taste freedom once, on my own terms."

"You don't know what it will do to you," Emeric said. "The songs — Sefa's songs — they say every poison comes back. All of them, at once, when the gate takes the protections. You'll feel everything you ever survived."

"I know," Mirabel said. "I've been counting since I was nine. Forty-one dishes." She had never said the number aloud. It sounded small. It sounded like a lifetime. "Forty-one deaths I've already died and walked away from. I can die them once more, all in a row, if it opens the door."

She pulled off her gloves — her taster's gloves, kid leather, the first luxury she had ever bought with her own coin — and dropped them on the stone.

* * *

The door did not open when she touched it. It opened when she *consented*.

She felt the difference. The metal was warm under her palm, and for a long moment nothing happened, and she understood that the gate was reading her — reading her blood, her history, her intent — the way she read a dish. It would take nothing coerced. It would take nothing accidental. It waited, patient as a guild-master, for her to mean it.

*Take it,* she thought. *Take all of it. I built it for something. Let this be the something.*

The gate drank.

It began at her fingertips and moved inward like cold fire, and Mirabel's knees hit the stone. It did not hurt, not at first. It *emptied*. She felt the oleander-wine immunity go — six years of plum sauce and pheasant, gone in a breath, and then the hemlock, and the gray amanitin she'd survived at twenty-two, and the slow arsenic of the third winter, each protection peeled out of her blood like a thread drawn from cloth, and with every thread withdrawn the poison came back. Not the substance. The *memory* of it, written into her body, and her body remembered everything.

She screamed. She was not ashamed of it later. Forty-one dishes, forty-one deaths, and her body had kept every receipt, and now the mountain collected them all at once, and Mirabel Villalobos lay on the warm black stone and burned from the inside with every poison she had ever conquered.

Through it, distantly, she heard Emeric shouting — felt his blood strike the door, felt the mountain shudder like a waking animal — and then the great slab of dark metal swung inward on silence, and beyond it was not tunnel but *light*, the amber light of a country that had kept its own fires burning for sixty years, and figures moving in that light, armed figures, figures in the livery of a dynasty the world had declared dead.

Hands caught her. Pulled her back from the threshold. Dax's voice, hoarse: "Go, boy. *Go.* It's open — go!"

And Emeric — gray, shaking, nineteen years old and the last of his line — paused at the door and looked back at her, at the woman wrung out and steaming on the stone, and Mirabel, through the fire in her veins, found the strength to mouth one word: *Go.*

He went.

The door began to close. The light narrowed to a seam, to a thread, to a line of ember-red — and in the last instant before the metal met stone, Mirabel saw, on the far side, a figure in old-fashioned armor raise a hand in salute. To her. To the taster.

Then the mountain sealed itself, and the wind came back, and the cold came back, and Mirabel Villalobos lay on the Cinder Stair with nothing left in her blood but blood — mortal, emptied, forty-one deaths echoing through her like bells — and began, weakly, to laugh.

Dax knelt over her, pressing his coat around her shoulders, his one eye wet. "You mad, brilliant, impossible woman. Are you— can you walk?"

"No," Mirabel said honestly. And then, because the wind had changed, because it was carrying a sound down from the northern passes that she knew the way she knew poison — steel, and hounds, and many boots — she opened her eyes.

"Dax. Listen."

The hunt had found them after all.
