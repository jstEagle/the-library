## 8. The Usurper's Hand

Dax Ferrow had spent thirty years learning to read a road, and the road behind them had gone quiet in the wrong way.

Not empty — quiet. No dust of pursuit on the southern ridgeline, no smoke from the lowland garrisons, no hound-song carried down the wind. After three days of being hunted through the high passes like foxes with the whole county's dogs behind them, the silence sat against his ears like water.

"They've stopped chasing us," he said to Mirabel that evening, as the caravan drew up in the shell of an old waystation below the last pass before the free cities' country. "That's what worries me."

The waystation had burned years ago — before Emeric was born, maybe, or shortly after; Dax had never kept careful count of other people's calamities. Its stone walls still stood, blackened at the tops, roofless to the sky. The great hearth at its heart was a cave of soot and old ash. But the walls were thick, and there was only one way a wagon could come in, and after three days of open sky, walls felt like a kindness.

They made camp inside the ruin. Small fires, banked low. The spice wagon was drawn into the corner where two standing walls met, and Emeric — who had spent the day pretending, badly, to be a spice merchant's sickly nephew rather than the last living grandson of the Ember Throne — actually did look sickly now, gray around the mouth, shivering under three blankets by a fire he wasn't supposed to be sitting near.

"Fever?" Mirabel asked, crouching beside him.

"Road fever," Dax said. "He gets it every time we push hard. It passes."

"It had better," she said, and there was something in her voice that made him look at her sideways. She had stopped talking about the boy the way you talk about cargo. Somewhere between the pass where she'd masked the caravan with bitterroot smoke and the ridge where Emeric had named roads for her that hadn't appeared on any map since the old kingdom fell, the boy had stopped being freight.

Dax understood it. He'd told himself for three years that the boy was business — the best-paid run of his career, the one that would let him retire to some warm coastal town where nobody asked about his eye. But you don't carry a living boy in a spice wagon for three winters without the spice getting into you.

He posted watches anyway. Old habit. He took first watch himself, sitting in the broken doorway with his crossbow across his knees, watching the dark road come up out of the valley.

It was near midnight when he saw the fires.

Not one fire. A ring of them, spaced with a soldier's precision along the slopes above the waystation, kindled all at once, as if the mountain itself had opened its eyes. And in the light of each fire, figures — too many to count, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who have already decided how the night ends.

"Hounds," Dax breathed. He could smell them now, faint on the wind — that wet, musky scent the gift-hunters' beasts gave off, bred to taste poison in the very air. They'd been quiet because they'd already finished the hunt. The ring had closed while the caravan slept off its march.

He woke Mirabel first. She was awake before he touched her shoulder — she'd been awake, he suspected, most nights, sleeping the way hunted things sleep.

"The corner," she said, looking at the fires. Not a question. She'd read the ground faster than he had.

"The corner," he agreed. "One way in. We hold it till dawn, then—"

"Then nothing." Her voice was flat and steady. "Look at the spacing. They're not going to rush us. They're going to wait until we're stupid with exhaustion, and then they'll rush us. Or worse — they'll just talk."

"Talk?"

She nodded toward the road below, where a single rider was coming up out of the dark, torch held high, no weapon visible. An old man, straight-backed even in the saddle, wearing the plain gray of the crown's civil service rather than the hunt's black-and-gold.

Dax knew that face. He'd seen it once, years ago, across a customs table in a river port whose name he no longer said aloud: *Corven Hale*, the handler, the man who ran the crown's tasters the way a falconer runs hawks. The man who, by Mirabel's own telling, had caught her six years ago and let her go — and had been letting her go, quietly, ever since, for reasons he had never explained.

"He's here for me," Mirabel said. "Not the boy. Me."

"Then he can have a conversation with my crossbow."

"No," she said. "Let me hear what he's selling. I want to know the price before I refuse it."

* * *

Hale dismounted at the edge of the firelight and stood there, hands open, an old man in a cold ruin with the whole mountainside armed behind him. Up close, Dax could see what the years had done to him — the gray at the temples, the deep lines, the eyes of a man who had spent a long career doing arithmetic with human beings and had finally begun to hate the sums.

"Mirabel Villalobos," he said. His voice was gentle, which was somehow worse than shouting. "Six years. You look well."

"I'm told poison keeps the complexion," she said.

A flicker of something crossed his face — not quite a smile. Grief wearing a smile's clothes. "You know why I've come."

"I know why your masters think you've come."

"Then let me say it plainly, because I don't have the stomach for the long version." He clasped his hands behind his back, a clerk's posture, a man delivering a ledger. "The registration edict offers immunity-bearers full pardon, crown protection, and a salary that would embarrass a bishop. In exchange: service. Your palate, put to work for the throne. There are perhaps a dozen people alive in these mountains who can do what you do. The usurper wants every one of them found, registered, and owned." He paused. "And he wants the boy."

"There it is," Dax muttered.

"You knew this was coming," Hale said, without looking at him. "Ferrow. You've moved him for three years. You knew the dragnet would close eventually. Every road ends somewhere." Then his eyes came back to Mirabel, and Dax saw the thing he would remember longest about this night: the handler was begging. Not with his words — his words were smooth as oiled leather — but with his hands, which had gone white where they gripped each other behind his back. "Take the offer, Mirabel. Both parts of it. Registration, protection, a life. Give them the boy, and you will never taste fear again for as long as you live. That is not a small thing. I have watched you go hungry and cold and afraid for six years, and I am offering you the end of all of it."

"And what happens to him?" Mirabel asked quietly.

Hale didn't answer immediately. When he did, his voice had dropped half a register, as though speaking softly might make the truth smaller. "He'll be kept comfortably. As long as he's useful."

"As long as he's *breathing*, you mean. Until there's another claimant, or another rebellion, or another winter when the usurper's dreams turn bad. And then he'll die of something sudden, and there'll be a report, and men like you will file it." She stepped forward, into the full light, and Dax saw Hale flinch — actually flinch, a man who commanded a hundred soldiers and a hundred hounds. "You spared me once. Six years ago. Why?"

For a long moment the handler said nothing. The fires crackled on the slopes. Somewhere above, a hound whined and was silenced.

"Because you were the best I ever trained," he said at last, "and because I told myself that leaving you loose was mercy. It was neither. It was cowardice. I have been paying for it every year since, and tonight I pay the last installment." He straightened. "I am sorry, Mirabel. Truly. But the order stands. Surrender the boy, or at dawn my people take this ruin, and they are not me. They will not offer terms twice, and they will not be gentle with the others."

"No," Mirabel said.

Just that. One word, quiet as a door closing.

Hale closed his eyes. For a heartbeat he looked less like a hunter than like a man at a graveside. Then he turned, mounted, and rode back down toward the fires, and Dax understood that the conversation was over and the night had only begun.

* * *

"They come at first light," Mirabel said, when she'd rejoined them at the wagon. "Maybe sooner if he loses his nerve. We need to move now, through the dark, over the scree —"

"With a fevered boy and four wagons?" Dax shook his head. "No. We split. I take the wagons out the front and give them something loud to chase. You take the boy and Sera and cut up the goat track behind the ruin. It goes to the high valley. From there—" He stopped. From there was the Gate. He hadn't said it aloud yet, the thing Mirabel and the boy had been circling for two days, the old taster's legend, the door in the mountain. But everyone in the firelight knew.

"You're not staying," Mirabel said.

"Somebody has to make noise."

"Dax—"

"Ferrow," he corrected, grinning. "If we're going to die together, you should use my proper name."

It was almost dawn when they came — earlier than Hale had promised, because somewhere in the dark a hound had caught the boy's scent on the wind and the handlers had lost patience. Dax heard them before he saw them: the jingle of harness, the low voices, the wet snuffling of the beasts. He fired the first bolt into the lead officer's shield and shouted something profane and magnificent, and the night came apart.

They rushed the ruin in three waves, and the smugglers met them with everything a caravan carries when it expects trouble — blades, hooks, burning oil from the lamp-stores, the raw vicious competence of people who have fought customs men in alleys for thirty years. For a few shining minutes it worked. The narrow entry became a throat of fire and screaming, and the black-and-gold fell back twice.

Then Dax saw the boy.

Emeric had not gone with Sera. The fever had broken sometime in the fighting, or the boy's stubbornness had simply overwhelmed it, and he had come out of the wagon with a kitchen knife in his hand and planted himself between a wounded smuggler and a huntsman's blade — nineteen years old, shaking, refusing to run.

The crossbowman on the wall had seen him too. Dax saw the man level, saw the boy's face lit orange in the firelight, and did the only arithmetic that mattered.

He crossed the distance in four strides that he would feel for the rest of his life — however long that turned out to be — and put his body between the bolt and the boy.

It took him high in the chest, punching through leather and ribs, and the world went white and then very, very quiet.

They got him back against the wagon. Mirabel was there — she must have come back down the goat track, gods help her, she'd come *back* — pressing cloth against the wound with hands that had survived every poison in creation and could do nothing at all for this. Emeric knelt in the ash, holding Dax's hand, saying his name over and over like a spell that wasn't working.

"Stop," Dax said. Blood came with the word. "Listen. The gate. It needs a willing body. Yours." He fixed his one eye on Mirabel. "I charged double for secrets my whole life. Here's one free. The pass road — the real one, the old one — forks at the cairn with the iron ring. Left fork looks wrong. Take it anyway. It's the one that remembers."

"Dax—"

"Go," he said, and smiled, and tasted blood. "Every road leads somewhere open. Told you. Thirty years I believed it. Prove me right."

Above the ruined walls, the fires of the hunt were closing in, and beyond them, far to the north, the mountain waited with its sealed door — and Mirabel Villalobos rose from the blood-soaked ash with her face wet and her jaw set, and began, at last, to walk toward the price.
