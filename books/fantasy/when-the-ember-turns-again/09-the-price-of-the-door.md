## 9. The Price of the Door

The Ember Gate did not look like a door.

It looked like a wound in the mountain — a seam of black glass running up the cliff face where no cliff should split, veined with faint orange light that pulsed like a sleeping heartbeat. Snow had drifted against its base and refused to settle there; it slid away in slow curls, as though the stone itself were warm and breathing. Mirabel had heard the legend a hundred times in the tasters' quarters, told in whispers over cheap wine after the lords had gone to bed: *a door for kings, fed by a body freely offered.* She had always assumed the stories were the kind servants tell to make their small lives feel adjacent to greatness.

Now she stood before it with blood freezing in her boots and Dax Ferrow's death three days behind her, and the legend looked back at her without blinking.

Emeric stood beside her, swaying on his feet. The fever from the waystation had never fully left him; his skin was grey under its pallor, and every breath came with a thin whistle at the bottom of it. Nineteen years old, raised in a spice wagon, carrying the last name of a dead dynasty like a stone sewn into his coat. He kept one hand pressed flat against the warm black glass, and the light beneath his palm brightened, dimly, hopefully, like a dog recognizing a voice.

"It knows me," he said. His voice cracked on the word. "Or it knows what I am."

"It knows what you are," Mirabel said. "That's not the same thing."

She set down the pack she'd carried up from the burned waystation — Dax's pack, because someone should carry it, because leaving it among the crossbow bolts and the trampled snow had felt like burying him twice. Her hands were steady. They were always steady. Six years of holding a spoon in front of suspicious lords had taught her hands a discipline her heart had never learned.

*Freely offered,* the legend said. Not coerced. Not traded. A body given, not taken. And the gate would drink from it — drink the one thing she had spent six years building dish by dish, sip by careful sip, pretending each survival was luck while her blood quietly learned to laugh at hemlock and nightshade and whatever the alchemists of the usurper's court had distilled in their brass towers.

Her immunity. Her armor. The only wealth she had ever owned.

"Tell me again why you're the one standing here," Emeric said. He turned to face her, and there was something in his expression she recognized from six years of watching powerful men try to be kind: guilt wearing courtesy's clothing. "You've done enough. You've done more than—"

"You can't open it," Mirabel said simply. "Royal blood turns the lock. It doesn't feed the door." She nodded at the black glass. "It wants protection. Willing protection. And I'm the only person in these mountains who has any worth taking."

"There must be another way."

"There is always another way," she said. "It's called dying in a pass in spring when the usurper's army comes through, or starving behind a sealed wall in the free cities, or living long enough to watch them find you in some cellar in Vellisport. I've priced all of those. This is cheaper."

He laughed — a broken, feverish sound — and then pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Dax said you'd say that. Before he—" His throat worked. "Before. He said, *the taster will do it, don't argue with her, arguing wastes time you won't have.*"

"He was right about roads," Mirabel said. "I'll trust him about this too."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the gate's cliff, because neither of them could face it standing up anymore. Mirabel built a fire with the last of the bitterroot smoke-wood, more for the smell than the warmth, and heated water, and went through the motions of a meal she had no appetite for.

Here was the thing she had never told anyone, not even herself in the dark hours: she was afraid of this not because of the pain.

The pain she could price. Every poison she'd ever survived had a shape, a signature, a season of suffering attached — the copper bite of arsenic, the slow ice of aconite, the alchemists' new blend at the winter court that had tasted of burnt sugar and cost her two days of feigned illness and one very real night of shaking. She knew the catalogue of her own body the way Dax had known the catalogue of his roads. The gate would make her walk the whole catalogue at once. That was terrible, but it was arithmetic.

What frightened her was after.

For six years she had been the safest person in every room she entered. That safety had been a cage, yes — a cage of lies and small compromises, of tasting other people's deaths so they wouldn't have to taste their own — but it had been *hers*. Strip it away and what remained? A woman with a good palate and a forged pass, mortal as a milkmaid, who could be killed by a careless cook or a vengeful lord or a bad oyster. She had built her whole self around the secret, and now she was going to hand the secret to a door and see what was left standing when the door was done.

"I keep thinking," Emeric said quietly, across the fire, "that I should have some grand speech prepared. Nineteen years hiding in a wagon, and my whole contribution to the restoration of my house is going to be walking through a hole in a mountain."

"Kings have been made with less," Mirabel said. "Most of them contributed nothing but their birth and their absence. You're already ahead."

"And you? What does the taster get?"

Mirabel looked at the fire. "Free," she said. "I get free. Though ask me again when I'm not sure I believe it."

* * *

Dawn came grey and windless. The hounds' voices had faded from the passes below two days ago, but the silence that replaced them was worse — the specific silence of hunters who have stopped calling to each other because they've stopped needing to. The handler who had spared her once would not spare her twice. Somewhere below, men who knew exactly which road led to the Ember Gate were climbing with the patience of people who know their prey has nowhere left to go.

Mirabel washed in melted snow. She braided her hair back the way she used to before court dinners, an old ritual, muscle memory from a life that belonged to someone else. Then she took off her boots — the legend said *a body freely offered*, whole and unhidden — and walked barefoot across the snow-margin to the black glass, feeling the impossible warmth rise through the frozen ground to meet her feet.

Emeric stood at the edge of the gate's light, waiting. His eyes were wet. He didn't apologize, which she was grateful for; apologies would have made it a debt, and debts were the one currency she refused to die holding.

"How does it work?" he asked. "Do you just—"

"I think," Mirabel said, "you put your blood on the door, and I put my hands on it, and we both mean it."

She drew the knife from her belt — Dax's knife, recovered from the waystation floor — and opened a shallow line across her palm before her fear could finish pricing the moment. Blood welled dark against the cold. She pressed her hand to the black glass.

The gate woke.

Light ran up the seam like fire through a fuse, orange deepening to white, and the mountain hummed — a sound she felt in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the old scar tissue of every poison she'd ever survived. Beneath her palm the glass softened, not to liquid but to something like skin, something that pulsed.

And then it began to drink.

It started with the newest poison — the alchemists' blend from the winter court, the burnt-sugar taste. It rose in her all at once, complete and vivid: the heat crawling up her throat, the tremor in her hands, the sick sweetness coating her tongue as if she'd swallowed the stew again, whole, unprotected. She gasped. Her knees hit the snow. The gate held her palm fast, gentle and absolute, and moved deeper.

Aconite. The ice spread from her spine outward, six years of immunity collapsing into six minutes of raw experience — every dish, every deliberate swallow, every night she'd lain awake cataloguing symptoms she'd have to fake tomorrow. Arsenic's copper flood. Hemlock's creeping numbness climbing her legs like cold water rising in a locked room. The bitter green of foxglove stopping her heart in stuttering half-beats. Things she'd survived in sips came back in floods. Things she'd survived in floods came back like drowning.

She screamed. She wasn't ashamed of it. There was no one to perform steadiness for, and that, absurdly, was part of the freedom too.

Through the white roar she heard Emeric shouting her name, and beneath it the gate's hum changing pitch — rising, opening, the seam of black glass widening into a doorway of standing light. She felt her body's ledger being read aloud and struck through, line by line, protection by hard-won protection, until there was nothing left between her and the world except her own stubborn flesh.

When it released her hand, she fell forward into the snow, and the snow was just snow — cold, ordinary, indifferent. She lay there shaking, emptied, tasting nothing but blood and winter air, and began, helplessly, to laugh.

"Mortal," she rasped, to no one. "Gods. It's so *quiet* in here."

* * *

Emeric helped her sit up against the warm stone. The gate stood open behind him — a tall arch of soft gold light showing, impossibly, a green country beyond: terraced hills, a river, a sky the color of late summer. The old kingdom, alive behind its sealed door, waiting nineteen years for someone with the right blood to knock.

He knelt in front of her. "Come with me," he said. "You've earned—"

"No." She said it gently. "Your country needs a king who isn't half-dead from paying my toll. And these mountains need someone who knows every smuggler's road better than the usurper's generals ever will." She flexed her cut hand, watching ordinary blood bead on it, feeling the extraordinary ordinariness of pain. "I'm staying. Someone has to hold the passes."

He gripped her shoulders, forehead nearly touching hers, and for a moment neither of them spoke, because everything speakable had already been paid for.

Then Emeric Villalobos-no-more, Emeric of the old line, last grandson of a extinguished house, straightened, squared his thin shoulders, and walked through the door of his ancestors. The light took him gently, the way morning takes a candle.

The gate began to seal — slowly, the gold folding back into orange, the orange sinking into black — and Mirabel watched until the last thread of summer sky vanished, and the mountain closed its wound, and the snow finally, at last, settled against cold stone.

Below her, far down the switchbacks, she heard it: the belling of hounds, and voices, many voices, climbing.

She picked up Dax's knife, wiped it, sheathed it, and turned to face the only thing she had left — the mountains, and the war coming up through them.
