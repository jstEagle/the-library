## 10. What the Mountains Hold

The snow came early that year, and Mirabel learned to read it the way she had once read the surface of a soup.

She stood at the mouth of the Kestrel Cut, breath fogging, watching the white curtain thicken over the pass below. Somewhere down there, the usurper's third column was trying again — supply wagons and pike-men and the pale-faced conscripts from the river provinces who had never seen a mountain in their lives. They would make it as far as the switchbacks below the Widow's Tooth before the smugglers fell on them out of the fog, cut the wagon teams loose, rolled burning barrels of pitch into the column's spine, and melted away into stone corridors that did not appear on any map drawn since the dynasty fell.

They had been doing it for four months now. The usurper called it a delay. His generals called it weather. The men who actually climbed the passes called it nothing at all, because most of them did not climb back down.

"Broth's on."

Mirabel turned. Sefa stood in the doorway of the waystation they had rebuilt from a burned ruin — *this* burned ruin, though not the one where Dax had died; that one they had left standing, charred and open to the sky, because some places should stay wounds. Sefa was sixteen, sharp-eyed, one of the river orphans who had drifted up into the mountains with the refugee tide until the tide stopped and the walls closed and there was nowhere left but up.

"I'll taste it," Mirabel said, and heard herself smile as she said it, because it was still strange, still new after all these months: she said it the way anyone would say it. Not a ritual. Not a performance. Just a woman checking her supper.

The broth was mutton and bitterroot — bitterroot for warmth, and because the mountain taught you to love what kept you alive. She sipped it slowly, deliberately, the way she had watched other people eat for six years at court, the way she had once envied them without ever having a word for the envy. She let the fat sit on her tongue. She waited. Nothing came. Nothing would come, unless someone had poisoned it, and then it would simply kill her, plainly, like any mortal woman, and there was a freedom in even that which she was still learning to hold without flinching.

"Well?" Sefa asked.

"It's good. Too much salt."

"There's no such thing."

"There is, and you've found it." Mirabel handed back the bowl. "Salt is how lazy cooks hide things. A cook who trusts her broth doesn't drown it."

Sefa snorted, but she'd remember it. They all remembered what Mirabel said about food, because that was the one coin she still carried. Her palate had survived the Gate. Everything else had gone through the door instead — six years of swallowed protections, every antidote her blood had learned dish by dish, poured out of her in an hour of fever while Emeric walked into the old country and the mountain sealed itself behind him like a held breath finally released.

She had woken on cold stone, stripped and shaking, tasting copper and ash and the ghost of every poison she had ever survived — the alchemists' signature from the sixth dish, the nightshade in the wedding wine, the slow arsenic they'd fed her in wine-thinned increments her first winter at court so that she'd be ready when it mattered. All of it re-echoing, all of it leaving. She had lain there feeling like a house with every wall torn out at once, and Dax dead two valleys behind her, and Emeric gone beyond any door she could follow, and she had thought: *now I can die of a bad oyster like anybody else.*

And then she had gotten up, because the passes still needed holding, and holding them required no immunity at all. Only memory.

* * *

The war council met in the waystation's long room, around a table made from a wagon bed. There were eleven of them now, and they called themselves nothing — no banner, no oath, because names were how the usurper's hunters found things. Dax Ferrow had taught them that much before he died teaching them everything else.

"The fourth column turned back at Raven's Stair," reported Osk, a grey-bearded ex-tollkeeper who had once smuggled fugitives through the very cuts he now defended. "Didn't even try the high road. Their scouts found the cairns moved."

"You moved the cairns?" Sefa asked.

"The cairns move themselves," Osk said solemnly, "if you know how to ask." Which meant he'd spent three nights rearranging stones that marked safe footing versus avalanche chutes, in a code older than the usurper's grandmother.

Mirabel spread the map — real vellum, salvaged from a dead courier, showing the imperial roads in confident red lines that all stopped dead at the Thornfell massif, as if the mountains were blank paper beyond. The blankness was the point. The usurper's cartographers drew only roads that existed for armies. Everything else — the smuggler cuts, the goat paths, the old kingdom's pilgrim ways with their forgotten names — lived only in heads, and most of those heads belonged to people now sitting at this table.

"They'll try winter quarters at Greyford," she said, tapping the lowland town. "They have to. You don't besiege a mountain range. You wait for it." She looked around the table. "Which means five months of garrison boredom, five months of our people running salt and letters and fugitives under their noses, and five months of them paying us in supplies to leave their wagons alone. We've bled them enough that bribery is cheaper than burial. That's not defeat. That's rent."

Someone laughed. It still startled her sometimes, how much laughter lived at this altitude, in this war. At court, laughter had been a weapon with a blade hidden in it. Here it was just — laughter. Survival shared instead of hoarded. She was still learning the grammar of it.

Osk lingered after the others filed out into the blue evening. He was working up to something; she let him.

"Word came down with the salt run," he said finally. "From the coast. Fishermen talk to smugglers, smugglers talk to us." He hesitated. "There are stories coming out of the east. Out of the old marches, past the sealed border."

Mirabel went very still. She had been still like this exactly once before, at a winter court, tasting a stew, recognizing an alchemist's hand in the poison — the moment the ground shifted under a life built on small compromises.

"What kind of stories?"

"Lights on the peaks where there's no road. Old shrines found swept clean and relit. And—" Osk rubbed his jaw. "A song. Children singing it in villages that haven't heard it in twenty years. Nobody taught it to them. It's the coronation anthem, Mirabel. The old one. The one for the Ember line."

The fire popped. Outside, wind worried the eaves of the ruined waystation like a patient animal.

"They say," Osk went on quietly, "that behind the door, the old country is stirring. That a young man with the old blood stands in the high hall of a kingdom that officially does not exist, and the kingdom is remembering itself around him. Room by room. Bell by bell." He met her eyes. "They say the door didn't seal. They say it *waits*."

Mirabel looked at the map, at the confident red lines dying against blank paper, and thought about blankness — how the usurper's whole kingdom was built on the claim that certain things did not exist: the old line, the old roads, the old country, the girl at court whose luck with poisoned dishes was extraordinary and meaningless. He had ruled by erasure. But erasure only works on paper. In stone, in song, in the mouths of children who could not possibly have learned a dead anthem — the erased things persisted, patient as seed.

"He'll march again in spring," she said. "Harder. Desperate. Because if the stories reach his own soldiers—"

"They've already reached his own soldiers," Osk said. "That's why the columns keep turning back. Half his army thinks the mountains are haunted. The other half hopes they are."

* * *

That night Mirabel sat alone in the doorway with tea, watching stars wheel over the pass. The cold bit honestly at her fingers. She had asked it to. There was something in her that needed the cold to be exactly as cold as it was — no protection between her skin and the world, no invisible armor deciding what reached her. The Gate had taken the armor. What remained was just her, and the world, meeting directly, and after six years of mediation she found the directness almost unbearably sweet.

She thought about Dax, who had charged double for secrets and given his life away for free. She thought about the handler who had spared her one night's head start, and wondered — not for the first time — whether somewhere in the usurper's machinery there was another person like her, being tested with alchemists' poisons right now, waiting for someone to call in a debt. When spring came, the smuggler roads ran south as well as north. There would be room in the caravans.

She finished her tea. She tasted it carefully, like anyone else — leaf, water, a thread of honey Sefa had traded for, the faint mineral bite of snowmelt. No poison. No immunity standing guard either. Just tea, and a woman drinking it, alive by attention rather than by miracle.

Attention, she had learned, was enough. Attention was, in fact, the whole secret she had carried all along beneath the gift: the taster's art was never the body's tricks. It was noticing. The salt hiding in a broth. The fear under a courtier's compliment. The cairn that had been moved. The anthem in children's mouths.

Far up the eastern ridge, above the tree line, where no road ran and no shrine stood on any map — a light kindled. Small, steady, gold. Then another beside it. Then a third, in a pattern she did not know and yet somehow recognized, the way you recognize a face from a song.

Sefa appeared at her shoulder, followed the line of her gaze, and went quiet.

"That's not a signal fire," Mirabel said. "We don't keep fires up there."

"No," Sefa agreed softly. "We don't."

The lights burned on, patient as the door that had made them, and Mirabel understood that spring was going to bring more than armies down out of the mountains. It was going to bring an answer — and it seemed the old country intended to knock.
