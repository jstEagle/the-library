## 2. Prayers Answered Too Well

The trouble with miracles, Oscar Ishikawa had learned over thirty years of bookkeeping, was that they never arrived alone. They came the way mice did—one sighting meaning forty behind the wall.

So when the second prayer landed on Oscar's desk in as many days, they didn't panic. They made tea.

The ledger lay open to its current page, and there it sat between an entry for a luck-debt owed to a cooper on Tanner's Row and a note reminding Oscar to return a borrowed umbrella to a woman who had, in fact, been dead for six years (the umbrella, not the debt; debts outlived everyone). The prayer had arrived the way prayers did when they were misaddressed: as a faint warmth in the paper, a smudge of gold ink that hadn't been there at midnight and was there now, curling into words that smelled faintly of candle smoke and desperation.

*Please. Please let him come back to me. I don't care how.*

No name attached. No god addressed. Just the raw, unaddressed ache of a widow somewhere in the city, flung out like a bottle into the sea—and instead of sinking or washing ashore, it had drifted through a hairline crack in a gate that was supposed to be sealed tighter than a miser's fist, and landed on the desk of the one person in the world whose entire job was making sure this exact thing did not happen.

"Well," Oscar said to the empty office, in the tone of a man finding a second mouse. "That's not ideal."

They copied the prayer into the margin—protocol demanded it, though protocol had never anticipated this particular circumstance, which was becoming a theme—and went to bed telling themselves firmly that one stray prayer was weather, not climate.

By morning, it was climate.

* * *

The first report came from the fishmonger on Bellwether Street, who paid his guild dues promptly and therefore felt entitled to gossip freely. A widow on Candle Lane, he said, her husband drowned off the pier last spring. She'd prayed every night for a month. And then—

"He walked back in," the fishmonger said, lowering his voice with the pleasure of a man delivering excellent terrible news. "Tuesday last. Soaking wet, mind you. Right as rain, if you'll pardon the expression. She fainted dead away."

Oscar, who had come ostensibly to inspect the quality of the mackerel, said, "And?"

"And what?"

"And is he right as rain? Or just wet?"

The fishmonger blinked. "Well, he's home, isn't he? Eats his supper. Says his grace before meals, same as ever." He paused, and something flickered across his face—the small animal unease of a man who has noticed a thing and would very much like to unnotice it. "Talks a bit odd, though. Keeps calling the dog by the wrong name. And he stands at the window at night, facing the mountain. Just standing. Hours."

"Hours," Oscar repeated.

"Like he's listening," the fishmonger said, and then laughed, too loudly, because laughing was cheaper than thinking. "Anyway. Mackerel's fresh."

Oscar bought the mackerel. It seemed important, somehow, to behave normally while the ground quietly rearranged itself beneath the city.

Three more reports by week's end. A cobbler's apprentice who'd prayed for steady hands woke with fingers so precise he could thread a needle blindfolded—but he'd also stopped blinking entirely, and his eyes had gone the flat gray of river stones. A farmer who'd begged for rain got three days of it, warm and sweet-smelling, falling only on his own fields in a perfect circle while the rest of the valley stood dry and bewildered. And a child on Marrow Street who'd wished for her sick cat to live found the cat restored to vigorous health, except that it now watched people the way cats watch birds: patiently, hungrily, without affection.

Each miracle worked. That was the horror of it. Every wish granted, fully, generously, exactly as asked—which meant each one granted by something that had heard the words but not the heart behind them. Something literal. Something old.

Something that should have been on the other side of a closed door.

* * *

Oscar spent Thursday night doing what they did best: accounting.

The Ember Gate sat beneath Mount Cerenne, at the end of a road no map admitted to, and it had been sealed since before the guild had a name. Sealed wasn't merely shut—it was *audited*. Every seal-stone bore a ward keyed to the Ledger of Impossible Things, so that any pressure against the gate, any whisper of divine attention, registered as an entry. In thirty years of keeping the books, Oscar had seen perhaps four such entries, all minor, all resolved with a strongly worded letter to a minor god and a modest offering of honey wine.

Oscar pulled the historical volumes down and laid them across the desk like a general studying old battlefields.

There. Entry 4,411, dated eleven weeks ago: *Pressure noted at northwestern seam. Magnitude: negligible.* Eleven weeks ago. The same week, Oscar realized with a slow cold turn of the stomach, that the cooper's luck-debt had begun accruing interest at an impossible rate, and the umbrella woman's ghost had started appearing in mirrors again, and Old Nan Pell's chickens had begun laying eggs with perfectly spherical yolks—a thing Oscar had logged as a curiosity and filed under *C*, because filing things was easier than worrying about them.

Eleven weeks of negligible pressures. Oscar turned pages backward. Entry 4,409. 4,406. 4,398. The intervals shortened. The magnitudes climbed, each still technically negligible, each still within tolerance, the way a hundred tiny leaks are each technically a drip.

And the locations. Oscar marked them on the city map pinned above the desk, red thread from entry to street. The cooper on Tanner's Row. The umbrella woman near the Weep. Old Nan Pell in the Lowfields. Then Candle Lane, where the drowned husband stood at his window facing the mountain. Then Marrow Street. Then the cobbler's shop.

The threads didn't scatter. They converged—on the gate, yes, but more than that. Each miracle had bloomed along a line running outward from the mountain like cracks spreading through glaze on old porcelain. The gods weren't reaching through the gate at random.

They were listening along the seams. Pressing their ears to the hairline fractures, hearing every desperate word spoken in the city below, and answering—not out of mercy, but because answered prayers were how you widened a crack. Every granted wish was a wedge. Every grateful soul standing at a window, facing the mountain, was another hand pulling at the door.

"Oh, you clever, patient horrors," Oscar murmured, and reached for the tea, and found the cup already cold, and drank it anyway.

Here was the arithmetic that kept them at the desk until the candle drowned in its own wax: the gate held because it was balanced. Pressure out, weight in, the ledger's own mass serving as ballast. But answered prayers weren't pressure—they were *traffic*. Things coming through. And traffic, unrecorded, unweighed, unbilled, threw everything off. Somewhere beneath the mountain, the scales were tipping by fractions so small no ward had flagged them. But Oscar had spent a lifetime learning that catastrophes were just sums that no one bothered to finish.

They finished the sum.

The gate would fail—not today, not this season, but soon enough. Unless someone went up the mountain and re-sealed the seams properly, which required access Oscar technically possessed and authority Oscar very much did not, because re-sealing meant opening, even briefly, even a crack, and opening was the one verb in the guild's entire constitution written in letters an inch high.

Or unless someone found out why the gate had thinned in the first place. Gates didn't crack on their own. Gates were cracked.

Oscar looked at the map, at the red threads converging on the mountain, and let themselves think the thought they'd been circling all night: *Someone on this side helped them. Someone loosened a stone, sold a key, took a payment.* The guild kept invisible by keeping clean, and the cleanest way to hide a sin was inside a seal nobody dared inspect.

Which meant a conversation with Guildmaster Vess, who smiled the way bankers smile, and who had signed off on every audit for the past twenty years.

Oscar blew out the candle. In the dark, the ledger's latest entry gleamed faintly gold, the widow's prayer still warm in the margin, and beneath it Oscar wrote, in their smallest, most careful hand:

*Entry 4,417. Miracles, unauthorized: six and counting. Origin: Ember Gate, via hairline fractures, northwest quadrant. Assessment: the old gods are not breaking in. They are being invited, one wish at a time. Action required: find the hand that opened the door.*

Then, after a moment, because honesty was the whole point of a ledger:

*Action required: find out whether the hand was mine.*

They didn't know yet why they wrote that last line. Only that some part of them, some cellar-deep instinct older than memory, had whispered it—and that the whisper had sounded almost familiar.

Outside, far off toward Candle Lane, a light burned in a widow's window. The husband stood behind it, face to the mountain, listening.

And on the mountain, something listened back.
