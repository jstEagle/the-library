## 3. What the Guildmaster Knows

The Guild of Quiet Accounts kept no sign above its door, which was itself a kind of sign—the way an empty chair at a table tells you someone has just left, or is about to arrive, and either way you should mind your manners. Oscar climbed the stairs to Vess's office with the prayer folded in their breast pocket like a knife they hadn't decided whether to use.

Guildmaster Vess received visitors in a room that smelled of cedar oil and old money, which are nearly the same smell if you're honest about it. He was arranging tulips when Oscar came in. That was the thing about Vess: he always appeared to have been interrupted mid-pleasure, so that whatever he said next would seem like a gift rather than a defense.

"Oscar." He said the name warmly, and then—Oscar would swear to this later—a half-beat too late, as though checking it against something. "Sit. You look terrible. Have you been sleeping?"

"I've been working," Oscar said.

"One often causes the other." He snipped a tulip stem at an angle, held it up to the light. "What brings you up out of the cellar? You only climb these stairs when something's wrong, and you never climb them when something's merely inconvenient."

"The Ember Gate is cracking."

The shears did not pause, but they did slow. Oscar had spent thirty years learning to read Vess the way other people read weather, and this was a cloud crossing a bright field: brief, high, gone before most eyes could catch it.

"Cracking," Vess repeated pleasantly.

"Hairline fractures along the western arch. Prayers are leaking through. Old gods are hearing them and answering literally. Widow Marta on Coppersmith Row wished her husband back from the dead, and something gave her a husband made out of beeswax and memory who eats candlelight and thanks her for it. There's a child in Harrow Lane whose loose tooth was granted by something that now visits nightly to check on the tooth's progress. And yesterday a baker prayed for patience and received forty years of it all at once—he hasn't moved since Tuesday."

Vess set down the shears. He came around the desk and sat on its edge, which was a trick he did to seem less powerful, and which worked on everyone except people who knew him.

"And what," he said gently, "do you imagine I can do about the architecture of gods?"

"You can tell me why the guild ledger shows three separate payments routed through the Gate Maintenance Fund in the last year," Oscar said. "To a mason. We don't have a mason. We've never had a mason. I balance every column in this building, Vess, and those columns don't add up to anything but a hole."

"Mm." Vess smiled at the tulips. "You know, when I found you, you were a scrawny thing of maybe eleven winters, standing in my doorway during a rainstorm, holding a dead man's account book and refusing to let go of it even when your fingers went white. Do you remember?"

"This isn't—"

"You wouldn't speak for two days. Wouldn't eat. But you looked at me—" he tapped his own temple— "right here, with those enormous grave-digger's eyes, and I thought: *there*. There is a person who understands what things cost. I've never once regretted taking you in. So believe me when I tell you that whatever you've found in the numbers, there is a version of events in which knowing less serves you better."

"There isn't," Oscar said. "There's only a version where I find out anyway, later, angrier."

Vess laughed—a real laugh, warm as banked coals—and for one treacherous moment Oscar felt twelve years old again, basking in it.

"That," Vess said, "is exactly what I told your mother."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, a clerk dropped something small and metal and did not pick it up.

"Your mother," Vess went on smoothly, as though he had said nothing at all, "was also a great believer in finding out anyway. It ran in the family. The Ishikawas were never good at leaving doors shut." He picked up another tulip, turned it, considered it. "Ishikawa. Odd little name. Comes with obligations attached, did you know? Practically itemized. I keep meaning to sit down with the paperwork someday."

He said it lightly. He said everything lightly; that was how he carried heavy things without spilling them. But Oscar heard it—the name spoken not as a name but as a *line entry*, something filed, something owed, something with a date beside it and a figure beneath.

"What paperwork?" Oscar asked.

"Old paperwork." Vess arranged the tulips with great tenderness. "From before you were mine. Go home, Oscar. Sleep. Let me handle the gate; handling gates is guild business, and you are the best bookkeeper I have ever trained precisely because you do not touch the things you count." He glanced up, and his charm flickered—just once—like a lamp in wind. "That was always the arrangement."

*Was,* Oscar thought, walking out. Not *is*.

* * *

The archive beneath the guild hall was older than the guild hall above it, which was the sort of sentence Oscar normally refused to write down because it invited questions. The ledgers lived here in ranked rows going back past living memory: debts, favors, names traded, miracles brokered, small mercies amortized over decades. The air tasted of dust and iron and the particular silence of paper that has not been disturbed.

Forty years back meant the shelf behind the false panel, the one Juno the junior clerk called "the tax records" because nobody ever wanted to look at tax records. Oscar pulled the volume down—a fat thing bound in grey leather gone soft as skin—and set it on the reading stand under the lamp.

They began, as always, with method. Alphabetical by debtor. Cross-referenced against the annual summaries. It took four hours, and Oscar drank two cups of cold tea without noticing, and the lamp burned low and was replaced, and still nothing. No Ishikawa. No sale. No entry at all bearing their family name.

Which was, itself, wrong. Everything was in the ledgers. That was the entire point of the ledgers. If a thing wasn't written here, it hadn't happened—or it had happened somewhere the guild preferred not to look, and the guild's whole business was looking.

Oscar sat back. Their hands were steady, which surprised them. Inside, something was pacing.

Then they saw it. Not in the main entries—in the margins. A fainter hand, older ink, the kind of notation bookkeepers make when they're annotating themselves: *see verso.*

Oscar turned the page. And there it was, in the middle column, in a neat clerk's hand that made Oscar's stomach drop because it was almost their own hand, the same slant, the same cramped economy—as though handwriting were inherited like eye color:

**Received, one soul entire, being the soul of the child Ishikawa, age seven years, in settlement of outstanding debt accrued by the estate of Aiko Ishikawa, deceased. Sold unto the Court Beneath the Mountain. Consideration rendered: continuance of the guild's protection, remission of all claims, and the closing of accounts forever.**

Signed in the margin, in a child's careful letters, the letters of someone who had been taught to sign before they understood what signing meant:

***Oscar Ishikawa.***

Seven years old. They had signed it themselves. Or something wearing their hand had. The ink had gone brown with age, but the strokes were confident, unhesitating—a child proud of doing it properly.

Oscar read it again. And again. The words did not change. That was the cruelty of words; they sat still while you came apart around them.

*Settlement of outstanding debt.* Their mother had died owing something. And the guild—Vess—had collected in the only currency that cleared the account. And Oscar, seven years old, had been walked—no, they had no memory of it, that was the point, that was what had been taken along with everything else—but somewhere, somehow, they had been brought before this page, or a copy of it, or a court beneath a mountain, and had signed.

And then Vess had wiped the memory, closed the account, and raised the child as a bookkeeper. As a keeper of the gate. As a person whose entire life was making sure nothing else got sold, while the first and largest sale sat quietly in a margin forty years deep.

*Ishikawa,* Vess had said. Spoken like an itemized debt. Because it was one. Because Oscar had been walking around for decades as a settled account, a receipt with legs.

They laughed. It came out wrong, more breath than sound, the laugh of someone falling. Because it explained things. It explained why prayers addressed to no god landed on their desk. The gate didn't crack randomly—it cracked toward them, the way water finds a drain. Something beneath the mountain had heard a prayer drifting loose and forwarded it to the address it had on file. Oscar wasn't a keeper who'd failed to notice the cracks.

Oscar was the crack.

They looked down at their own hands under the lamp—steady, capable, thirty years of balancing impossible columns—and wondered, for the first time in a long and carefully unlived life, how much of what was sitting here reading was actually *them*, and how much was shell. A vessel maintained in trust. An estate in escrow.

The lamp guttered. In the dark between flame-moments, Oscar heard—very far away, or very close, down through stone and debt and years—a sound like a vast ledger turning its own pages.

And beneath the entry, in ink so fresh it might have been written that instant, in a hand that was unmistakably, horribly their own, a new line had appeared:

**Balance pending. Collection imminent.**

* * *

Oscar did not sleep. They sat in their rooms above the counting floor with every lamp lit and the forty-year-old ledger open on their knees, and they watched the new ink dry, and they thought about mothers.

They had no face for their mother. That was the thing that finally broke them—not the signature, not the debt, not even the word *collection*—but reaching for the memory and finding a warm shape where a face should be, a voice like a voice heard through a wall, a smell of rain and rice and something flowering they couldn't name. Vess had taken that too, or the sale had, or the forgetting was the price of surviving childhood at all. Either way: gone. Sold with the rest.

Toward dawn, footsteps on the stairs. Light ones. Oscar knew every clerk's tread in the building, and this one belonged to Juno Vale, the newest junior, the one who alphabetized too fast and looked at the false panel the way cats look at closed doors.

She stopped in the doorway, took in the lamps, the ledger, Oscar's face.

"I saw the lights," she said. "And I heard the Guildmaster come in an hour ago, and go straight down to the archive, and come back up smiling. He doesn't smile after the archive." She hesitated, then stepped inside and shut the door behind her, which junior clerks did not do. "Whatever you found down there—he knows you found it. And Oscar? When he smiled?" Her voice dropped. "It was the smile he wears when something's finally coming due."
