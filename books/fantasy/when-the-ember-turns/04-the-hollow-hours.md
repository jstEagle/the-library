## 4. The Hollow Hours

The ledger had begun to lie to Oscar, which was the worst thing that had ever happened to a book.

Not in the way ledgers usually lied—rounding up, rounding down, the small dishonesties of arithmetic under pressure. No. The Ledger of Impossible Things had started writing entries Oscar hadn't made. They'd find them in the morning, in their own handwriting, dated three days ahead:

*One color, red, returned by mistake. Do not accept.*

Oscar sat at their desk in the guildhouse attic with a cup of tea gone cold beside them and tried very hard not to think about what it meant that they could no longer remember what red looked like.

They had noticed it first on Tuesday, with the awnings over the fishmonger's row. Those awnings had always been a particular shade—the one that made Oscar think of a word that was now simply *gone*, like a tooth leaving a gap the tongue kept returning to. Now the awnings were just... fabric. Colored, technically. Some frequency of light was bouncing off them and into Oscar's eyes and being reported to a brain that filed it under *miscellaneous*.

"You're staring at laundry," said the kettle, which had opinions, as most guild property did after forty years of proximity to impossible things.

"I'm auditing it."

"Audit harder. It's beige. Beige is a punishment from God."

"Which god?"

"The boring one," said the kettle. "Drink your tea before it gets any colder. You've been holding it since dawn."

Oscar drank. The tea tasted of nothing in particular—not bad, just generic, tea-flavored the way a drawing of a horse is horse-shaped. They frowned at the cup. Tea was supposed to have a top note. Something bright at the front of the tongue.

*Do not accept,* the ledger had written, in their own hand.

Downstairs, the city went about its morning, and somewhere in it prayers were still landing wrong—on old gods who answered too literally, through hairline cracks in a gate Oscar had spent a lifetime keeping shut. A widow's husband had come back last week. Mostly him. The part that was missing had been replaced with something patient and hungry, and Oscar had been the one to put it down again, gently, with an iron poker and an apology. That had been the fourth miracle with teeth that month.

But this morning there were no crises. There was only the ledger, open to a blank page, and Oscar's own future handwriting waiting in it like a trap.

They picked up the pen. Their hand moved without consulting them.

*One memory, maternal face, withdrawn for audit purposes. Estimated return: never.*

"No," Oscar said out loud, and slammed the ledger shut.

The attic went quiet. Even the kettle held its steam.

Because here was the thing Oscar had known since the night the prayer addressed to no god had landed on their desk: they had no soul. Hadn't for forty years. What they'd had instead was a *lease*—a shell that thought and felt and drank cold tea and kept immaculate books, running smoothly on borrowed mechanism, and leases ran out. And when the lease ran out, everything in it went back to the landlord. Not all at once. In installments. Colors first, apparently. Then tastes. Then—

Then the ledger would take even the memory of a face Oscar hadn't realized they were still carrying.

Their mother's face. They reached for it frantically, greedily, the way you grab at something sliding off a table—and found it, still there, warm and half-lit like a kitchen at evening. Dark eyes. A laugh that came out sideways. Flour on her hands because it was always on her hands, she was always baking or kneading or dusting flour off things that did not need dusting.

Oscar sat with it, memorizing the memorizing, while the tea went fully cold and the city hummed below and somewhere beneath the mountain something patient waited for its next installment.

* * *

There was a kitchen once. It is evening, and the kitchen is gold at the edges because evening does that to kitchens, and a woman is standing at the stove with her back turned, humming a song with no words, only a tune that goes up where you don't expect it.

The child sits on the floor under the table, because under the table is where important business is conducted. The business today is a wooden spoon and a pot lid, which together make a drum kit, if you are five years old and ambitious.

"Oscar." The woman says it without turning around. Mothers can do that. "What are we not doing?"

"We're not drumming."

"That's right. And why aren't we drumming?"

"Because Papa's head hurts."

"And what do we do when Papa's head hurts?"

The child considers this. "We are quiet as mice."

"We are quiet as *what?*"

"MICE," roars the child, at a volume that has never once been mistaken for a mouse, and the woman laughs—that laugh that comes out sideways, like water finding its way downhill—and turns around, and there is flour on her hands, there is always flour on her hands, and she scoops the child up and spins them and the whole kitchen spins too, gold at the edges, humming at the center.

"Little keeper," she says, pressing her floury thumb to the child's forehead, leaving a white mark like a blessing or a smudge. "You'll keep watch over everything someday. I can tell. You've got the eyes for it."

"What kind of eyes?"

"Watching ones." She sets them down and taps their nose. "Now. Salt."

The child fetches the salt box from the shelf, walking carefully, solemnly, a courier of great cargo. She pinches some between her fingers and holds it out.

"Taste."

It bites the tongue, sharp and bright and clean, like the sea got small and decided to live in a little blue box on the third shelf.

"That's salt," she says. "Remember it. Some things you should carry with you always."

"Why?"

She looks at the child for a long moment, and something crosses her face that the child is too young to read—a shadow, a subtraction, a mother counting coins she doesn't have.

"Because the world will try to trade it away from you," she says. "And it's worth more than they'll ever offer."

* * *

By Thursday, Oscar couldn't taste salt.

They discovered it at breakfast, in the most mundane way possible: an egg, overcooked, oversalted out of spite by the cook downstairs who believed Oscar's asceticism was a personal insult. Oscar put a forkful in their mouth and got—texture. Warm graininess. The idea of salt, filed under *miscellaneous* along with the awnings and the top notes of tea.

They spat it out and sat very still.

Salt. Of all the things. Their mother had—had—

They reached for the reason and found the shape of a memory with the middle scooped out. A kitchen. Evening. A woman whose face was becoming a warm blur above a white apron. Something about carrying salt always. The words were gone but the weight of them remained, the way a stone leaves a dent in moss after it's been picked up.

"No," Oscar told the egg. The egg declined to negotiate.

That afternoon they went down into the city and bought salt. Every kind. Gray Celtic salt in fat crystals, pink Himalayan slabs, flaky Maldon like shattered glass, cheap iodized salt in a cardboard canister. They sat in their attic among the little paper bags and tasted each one and got nothing, nothing, nothing, and it was somehow the cheap canister that finally broke them—because they remembered *liking* it, remembered the specific guilty pleasure of oversalting eggs late at night over the ledger, and liking something is a memory, and memories were apparently still on the table even after the thing itself was repossessed.

Grief, Oscar discovered, has excellent bookkeeping. It itemizes.

*One taste, saline. Repossessed.*
*One color, red. Repossessed.* (Though honestly, red might have been earlier; it was hard to date these things.)
*One voice, maternal, mid-hum. Pending.*

"Pending," Oscar repeated bitterly. "Pending. Like a delivery."

"You're talking to the inventory," said the kettle.

"I'm talking to myself. There's a difference."

"Not from where I'm sitting."

Oscar wrapped the salt bags up again and lined them on the windowsill, a little garrison against a siege they were losing. And then, because grief is exhausting and the body insists on continuing anyway, they opened the ledger to find the entry Vess didn't know they knew about.

Forty years old. Yellowed page, brown ink, the guildmaster's own precise hand:

*Received: one soul, Ishikawa, O., age five. Delivered beneath the mountain per contract. Compensation: see attached.*

See attached. As if a soul were a shipment of bolts.

Oscar had read the entry a hundred times since finding it. But tonight they read it differently, because tonight they were losing things, and losing things teaches you what reading is for. They weren't looking for the transaction anymore. They were looking for the *child*.

Age five. Someone had carried a five-year-old's name down into the dark and sold it. And the five-year-old had grown up—or grown *outward*, like a tree growing around a fence post—into whatever Oscar Ishikawa was now: competent, punctual, beloved by kettles, hollow as a bell.

A bell, Oscar thought. That's what I am. All ring and no clapper.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the moon was doing something theatrical behind clouds, and far beneath the city, beneath the guildhouse, beneath the bedrock and the older dark under the bedrock, something turned over in its sleep and smiled, possibly, if mountains can be said to smile, which Oscar sincerely hoped they couldn't.

And then—because the night had decided it hadn't finished ruining Oscar yet—a knock came at the attic door. Three raps. Unhurried. The knock of someone who knows you're awake.

Nobody knocked on Oscar's door. Nobody *ever* knocked. The guild preferred its members like its miracles: invisible, untraceable, arriving through doors that officially didn't exist.

Oscar closed the ledger, slid it under a floorboard, and picked up the iron poker out of habit.

"Who is it?"

A pause. Then a voice—low, dry, with the particular rasp of someone who has climbed through a lot of other people's windows and finds stairs almost insulting:

"Juno Vale. Don't bother hiding the ledger, I already read it." Another pause. "We need to talk about what Vess sold, and how much he pocketed. And Oscar—" the voice dropped, almost gentle, which was somehow more alarming than the breaking-and-entering implication, "—I know where the debtor's door is. The one under the mountain. I've been through it before."

Oscar stood in the middle of their attic, surrounded by bags of salt they couldn't taste, holding a poker they hoped they wouldn't need, and felt—for the first time in forty years—something arrive that wasn't an installment plan.

"Well," they said to the door. "You'd better come in, then."
