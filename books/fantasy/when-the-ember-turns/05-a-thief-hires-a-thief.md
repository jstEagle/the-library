## 5. A Thief Hires a Thief

Juno Vale had stolen many things in her thirty-one years: a silver reliquary shaped like a sleeping saint, the mayor's second-best wig (the first-best was already gone when she arrived), a whole afternoon from a customs officer who never noticed it missing. She had never once been blackmailed into *not* stealing something, which she considered a novel indignity and therefore, in its way, a gift.

"Stop Ishikawa," Guildmaster Vess had said that morning, pouring tea he did not offer her, in an office so warm it felt rehearsed. "That's all. Slow them down. Distract them. You're very good at distractions."

"And if I don't?"

Vess smiled the way a ledger balances—everything accounted for, nothing forgiven. "Then I'll have to mention the Harrow Street job to the magistrates. And the one before it. And the small matter of your sister's debts, which I've been carrying so generously, month after month."

Juno had drunk his tea anyway. It was excellent tea. If she was going to be ruined, she refused to do it with a dry mouth.

Now, past midnight, she crouched in the rafters of the guild's archive with a candle stub, a lockpick between her teeth, and the specific joy of a woman about to commit a crime out of spite. Vess wanted her to stop Oscar Ishikawa. Very well. She would start by finding out exactly why.

The archive smelled of dust and old vellum and, faintly, of tallow—the smell, Juno's grandmother had always said, of secrets kept too long in the dark. The Ledger of Impossible Things lived on a lectern in the center of the room under three locks Juno opened in ninety seconds flat, mostly out of professional pride. The guild thought itself invisible. The guild thought its books were safe. The guild, in Juno's long experience, thought a great many flattering things about itself.

She turned pages. Debts and favors marched past in Vess's neat hand: a rain bought for a drought-struck farm, repaid in firstborn laughter; a death postponed twice for a midwife, repaid in years off somebody else's life; a door held shut, entry after entry after entry, marked only *gate — maintained*.

It was in the margins that things got interesting.

Vess was meticulous in the columns. In the margins he was careless—or rather, he was careful in a way meant only for himself, little shorthand ticks beside certain entries. Juno copied the ticks onto her wrist in charcoal, matched them against entries, and began to see the shape of it. Every favor Oscar Ishikawa had traded over forty years, every miracle brokered and balanced, carried a hidden surcharge. A tenth here, a tithe there, siphoned sideways into accounts Vess had never shown anyone.

And then, near the back, an entry from four decades ago, written in ink gone brown as old blood:

*Bought: one soul, name withheld. Sold beneath the mountain. Interest accrued.*

In the margin, in Vess's shorthand: *paid in full — mine.*

Juno sat down on the archive floor, which no self-respecting thief ever does, and read it again.

Oscar Ishikawa's soul wasn't lost. It had been *invested*. Vess hadn't merely sold a child's name to the court beneath the mountain—he'd been collecting interest on it for forty years, skimming from the gate, from the ledger, from the hollow person walking around keeping everyone's accounts straight without ever knowing their own account was the one being emptied.

"Well," Juno said aloud, to the dust. "You absolute bastard."

Somewhere above her, a beam creaked. Juno froze, candle cupped, and heard—not footsteps exactly, but a kind of tired shuffling, like someone who had been awake too long and whose body had begun filing complaints.

*Speaking of whom.*

* * *

Oscar Ishikawa came down the archive stairs holding a lantern and looking, Juno thought, like a man who had been arguing with a wall and losing politely. They wore their exhaustion like a coat that no longer fit. Their eyes went straight to the open ledger, then to the open locks, then—annoyingly fast—to the rafter where Juno sat.

"You might as well come down," they said. "The lectern's worth more than you'll fence it for."

"That's a matter of opinion." Juno dropped lightly, landing without sound, because some habits are load-bearing. "And I'm not here to fence anything. I'm here to blackmail *you*, actually, but the material's disappointing."

"I'm having that kind of week."

"So I hear." She tapped the ledger. "Forty years of entries. Your signature on half of them, though you didn't know your own name until recently. And this—" she turned to the brown-ink page, watching Oscar's face as they read it.

What crossed that face wasn't shock. That was the worst part. It was more like confirmation arriving late, like a letter that had been walking for years. Oscar touched the entry with one finger, the way you'd touch a scar you'd always been told was nothing.

"He told me I sold it myself," Oscar said quietly. "He let me believe that. All these years, I thought—I thought somewhere in me was a version of me who'd made a bad trade, and I've been trying to be worthy of the loss." They laughed, a small terrible sound. "There was no trade. There was a theft. He stole from a child and billed the child for the storage."

"Yes," said Juno. "Also he's been skimming interest off every miracle you've brokered since, so when you get the soul back, mind the fees."

Oscar looked up at her properly then, and Juno saw the calculation behind the exhaustion: not *can I trust her* but *do I have any better options*, which was, frankly, how all the best partnerships began.

"Why are you telling me this?" Oscar asked. "Vess sent you to stop me. I assume. He has a look he gets when he's hired someone, like a man who's just swallowed a compliment."

"He did send me." Juno leaned against the lectern. "Blackmail. My debts, my sister's debts, my entire criminal history laid out like a tasting menu. Very thorough. But he made one mistake."

"Which was?"

"He gave me access to the ledger." She smiled. "Guildmaster, meet thief. You don't hand a thief a book full of your crimes and expect her to read only the assigned chapters."

Oscar studied her for a long moment. Lantern light moved across their face and Juno noticed—now that she knew what to look for—that the light didn't quite seem to land on them. It went *near* them. Around them. Like rain sliding off oilcloth.

"You're coming apart," she said, before she could stop herself.

"I prefer 'under renovation.'" Oscar closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom. "I lost the color green on Tuesday. Yesterday I couldn't remember whether salt is supposed to taste like anything. This morning I tried to picture my mother and got a hat." Their voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "So yes. I am coming apart, at a rate I can now calculate, thanks to your margin notes. Which means I have perhaps a season left to walk into a mountain, argue with a god, and steal back the only thing I've ever owned."

"Steal," Juno repeated, delighted despite everything. "You said steal."

"I said what I said."

"No, no, this is important." She pointed at them with the candle stub. "For weeks the whole city's been whispering about Ishikawa the Keeper, Ishikawa the saint of small mercies, going to *bargain* with the Undercourt. Bargaining is what got you sold in the first place. Bargaining is Vess's game—you'll lose on his board. But stealing?" She spread her hands. "Stealing is mine."

Something shifted in Oscar's expression. Not warmth, exactly—more like a window unshuttered in a house people had assumed was empty.

"You understand," they said slowly, "that I can't pay you. Whatever I recover, whatever's left of me, it isn't currency. And Vess will ruin you the moment he learns you've switched sides."

"Oh, he'll learn the moment he checks the archive and finds his precious ledger handled by someone with clean technique." Juno shrugged. "As for payment—" She hesitated. This was the part she'd been circling all night, the part that had nothing to do with ledgers or blackmail, and saying it plainly felt more dangerous than any roof in the city. "I've worked alone eleven years. Do you know what that's like? Every job, every escape, every cold breakfast, alone. Because trusting anyone means giving them something to take from you, and I learned early that everything gets taken eventually." She nodded at the ledger. "But you—you've had everything taken already. Everything except the part nobody could reach. You're the only person I've met in years who has literally nothing left to steal from me."

"That's either the strangest compliment I've received or the saddest."

"With me those are usually the same thing."

Oscar almost smiled. It was a small thing, crooked and rusty, but Juno watched it happen and thought: *there. That's worth more than the reliquary.*

"All right," Oscar said. "A thief hires a thief. What do you need?"

"Three things. One: we leave before dawn, through the debtor's door under the tannery—old smuggling route, opens inside the mountain's lower courts. Two: you tell me everything Vess ever said about the sale, word for word. Liars reuse sentences; I want his reused sentences memorized." She held up a third finger. "And three: when this is done, whatever name you end up wearing—whether you get the old one back or forge a new one—you let me be the first person to use it."

Oscar went still. The lantern flame bent toward them, or seemed to.

"Why does that matter to you?"

"Because a name's the one thing you can't fence," Juno said. "Believe me, I've checked. Everyone in this city is trading pieces of themselves—Vess trades other people's, the gods collect, the courts compound interest. Somebody ought to give one away for free, just once, to prove it can be done." She picked up her kit and slung it over her shoulder. "Consider it an investment. I hear the returns are excellent."

They stood a moment in the lamplit quiet, two thieves and a book of impossible debts, and Juno felt the strangest sensation—lightness, like a pocket finally emptying of stones.

"Come on, then," she said. "Dawn's a deadline and the mountain keeps worse hours than Vess."

Oscar took up the lantern. At the top of the stairs they paused, glancing back at the ledger on its lectern.

"Juno. The margins. The interest Vess skimmed—if the courts beneath keep accounts the way I think they do, then every coin he skimmed was drawn against *my* soul." Their voice had gone very quiet, and very steady, the way water goes still just before it freezes. "Do you understand what that means?"

Juno grinned, sharp as a pick slipping into a lock.

"It means we're not breaking into the mountain, Keeper," she said. "We're calling in a debt—and the mountain *hates* owing."
