## 6. Descent by Ledger Light

**Oscar**

The debtor's door was behind a fishmonger's stall.

Of course it was. Everything in this city that mattered was hidden behind something that smelled. Juno Vale knocked on the wet brick in a pattern that looked like a mistake—three quick, one slow, then a pause long enough to make Oscar wonder if she'd forgotten it—and the wall exhaled a breath of cold air that tasted of iron and old candle smoke.

"After you," Juno said.

"You first," said Oscar. "It's your door."

"It's my door because I owe it money." She stepped through anyway, and her voice came back thin and echoey. "That's how doors work down here. You don't own anything. You just owe it less than someone else."

The passage beyond was narrow and lit by nothing at all for the first hundred steps, until Juno struck a match and the dark flinched back to reveal stone walls scored with tally marks. Thousands of them. Some were fresh enough to glisten; some had been worn to shallow ghosts by hands that had presumably moved on—or hadn't.

"What are they counting?" Oscar asked.

"Debts. Kindnesses. Apologies." Juno shrugged, lighting their way deeper. "Down here everything's collateral. You smile at someone, you'd better be ready to have that smile called in. I once watched a man repay his mother's funeral with forty years of good posture."

"That's horrifying."

"He had excellent posture." A pause. "He also died owing someone a sneeze. They're still holding it. There's a whole reliquary of unspent sneezes two levels down. Don't ask."

Oscar asked. The answer involved an earthenware jar, a minor god of respiratory etiquette, and a wager gone wrong at what could only charitably be called a dinner party. By the end of the story they had descended past the last natural stone into the mountain's proper interior, where the passages widened into galleries hung with lanterns that burned without oil and cast light the color of ledger ink.

And there—everywhere—the courts. Small ones, mostly. Alcoves where something ancient sat behind a counter of black glass, waiting to appraise whatever a traveler might offer: a memory of rain, a grudge held since childhood, the specific comfort of a familiar chair. The minor gods did not look up as Oscar passed. That, more than anything, told Oscar how deep they were. Upstairs, everyone looked at you eventually. Down here, you only mattered if you owed something.

Juno walked like a person who knew exactly which floorboards would forgive her weight and which would bill her for it. Oscar followed in her footsteps precisely, feeling like a shadow with delusions of independence.

"Left here," she murmured. "And whatever happens, do not accept directions from anyone with too many elbows."

"Too many?"

"You'll know it when you count them."

* * *

**Juno**

The thing about Oscar Ishikawa, Juno decided somewhere around the third gallery of the Underdeep, was that they walked like a man reading a very long letter from himself.

She'd expected the guild's bookkeeper to be timid. Ledgers made people timid—they made *her* timid, and Juno Vale had once stolen the teeth out of a sleeping dragon's mouth on a dare involving a bar tab. But Ishikawa descended into the mountain where souls went to be itemized with the expression of someone checking arithmetic they already suspected was wrong, and being quietly furious about the remainder.

Also, they kept almost smiling at the minor gods.

She noticed it first at the Tollgate of Small Mercies, where a squat thing with a face like a folded map demanded payment for passage. Standard procedure: you gave it a regret, it weighed the regret, it let you pass or didn't. Juno had her regret ready—a small one, pre-packaged, the time she'd let a boy take the blame for a window she broke when she was nine. Cheap, portable, emotionally liquid.

But Ishikawa stepped forward, opened the ledger they carried against their chest like a second heart, and began to negotiate.

"The toll is one regret," the god rasped.

"The posted rate is outdated," Ishikawa said. "You raised it after the Sinking of Vell, but traffic through this gate dropped thirty percent when the ferry district burned, so your volume no longer supports premium pricing. I'll pay half a regret and throw in an entry from my ledger—an unpaid promise of spring rain, dated sixty years ago, still valid, transferable."

The god's face unfolded slightly. Somewhere in its folds, Juno could see, was greed doing math.

"Half a regret and a weather promise," it said.

"A weather promise *and* first refusal on any future tolls I route through your gate rather than your competitor's." Ishikawa tore the page free with the sound of a small, clean wound. "Sign here."

There was a pen. Juno never found out where the pen came from. There was always a pen around Ishikawa, the way there was always weather around a storm.

They passed through the gate, and Juno caught herself staring. "You enjoyed that."

"I did not."

"You smiled. Your whole face did a thing. I've seen pickpockets make that face over an unguarded purse."

"I was conducting necessary financial—" Ishikawa stopped. Recalibrated. "It was efficient."

"It was *fun*. Admit it was fun."

"Ishikawas don't have fun. We have reconciliations."

But later, at the Bridge of Borrowed Breath, where the crossing fee was three seconds of your next inhale, and Ishikawa talked the bridge itself—*the bridge*, which was apparently a very sleepy god named Ommu who had been a river before rivers were fashionable—down to two seconds and a favorable review in the ledger, Juno saw it again. That face. The lean-in. The little spark when Ommu tried to bluff and Ishikawa saw the bluff coming like weather on the horizon.

Power suited them. That was the trouble. Power always fit the people who least trusted themselves to wear it.

* * *

**Oscar**

The minor god of Unfinished Letters lived in a hollow shaped like an envelope flap, and it was weeping.

Or producing moisture consistent with weeping. Oscar was careful, these days, about assuming things had feelings. Assuming things had feelings upstairs got you attached. Down here it got you invoiced.

"You want passage to the Court Beneath," the god said. Its voice was the sound of a page turned halfway and abandoned. "None pass me without paying. My price is a letter you never sent. Everyone has one."

"I have several," Oscar admitted.

"Then choose the heaviest."

And there it was—the trap, laid out politely, the way all the best traps down here were laid out. Because the heaviest unsent letter Oscar owned was addressed to a mother whose face they could no longer hold onto, and handing it over meant losing even the shape of the loss. Down here, kindness was collateral, and so was grief. Especially grief.

"I'll offer an alternative," Oscar said, and opened the ledger.

They had been tearing entries out all night, each removal leaving a faint ache in their palms, like the ghost of a papercut. This entry was small. A promise made to a dying cobbler that his shop would never burn. It hadn't been fulfilled yet. It was, technically, still live.

"This," Oscar said, "is a covenant with a fifty-year yield. Protected premises. The guild enforces it personally. If you hold it as collateral, you gain standing—you're not a gatekeeper anymore, you're an asset holder. Gods talk. Gods compare portfolios. In twenty years you could be sitting on a collection instead of crying over other people's mail."

The god's weeping slowed. "I like mail," it said, sulkily.

"You can keep the mail. This is diversification."

Somewhere in the envelope-dark of the hollow, something rustled with interest. Oscar felt the sensation rise in their chest again—that warm, terrible lift, the one that had been growing all night with every bargain won. It felt like the first sip of wine after a long fast. It felt like being *good* at something, finally, after a lifetime of being merely correct.

They liked bargaining. Not the winning—well, yes, the winning—but the moment before, when two wills leaned across a counter and the world briefly became negotiable. Upstairs, Oscar spent their life keeping accounts balanced, fixed, immovable. Down here, everything moved. Everything could be argued into new shapes.

*Ishikawas don't have fun,* they'd said. And Juno had looked at them with those sharp thief's eyes and seen straight through to the truth: that some part of Oscar had been waiting forty years to find out they were dangerous, and had discovered it the way a drowning person discovers the surface.

They signed the covenant over. The god let them pass, clutching its new asset like a firstborn child.

Juno was waiting on the far side, arms crossed. "You're doing the face again."

"I don't have a face. I have an expression policy."

"One more bargain and you're going to start liking this place. That's how it gets you. First you win a deal, then you want to win another, and by the tenth you're one of them, sitting in an alcove, charging people for their own regrets." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "I've seen it. The mountain doesn't eat thieves first. It eats accountants."

Oscar wanted to argue. The terrible part was that they couldn't—not entirely, not honestly. Their hands still tingled from the signing. Some deep, soulless part of them—which was most of them, these days, the shell wearing thin—was already scanning the dark ahead for the next counter, the next counterparty, the next chance to turn the world's terms to their favor.

"I'll remember," they said, and meant it the way you mean a note pinned above a desk.

* * *

**Juno**

Two levels from the Court Beneath, the corridors changed. The tally marks on the walls got older, and then stopped altogether, replaced by something worse: blankness. Smooth stone, unmarked, as if the mountain had decided that this close to its heart, debts went without saying.

Juno had been here once before, years ago, selling back a year of her childhood to cover a partner's debt. She remembered the way. What she hadn't remembered—and what came back to her now with the force of a slammed door—was the sentry.

It stood at the final archway: tall, courteous, made mostly of folded paper and patience. A collector. It bowed as they approached.

"Travelers," it said pleasantly. "State what you owe."

"Nothing," Juno said. "We're square."

The collector tilted its head, and Juno watched its paper face unfold, crease by crease, into something like a smile. "No one is square," it said gently. "But some are *carried*." Its gaze slid past her shoulder to Oscar, and lingered there, and its smile gained a fold. "Oh," it said softly. "Oh, *there* you are. We've been holding your name for such a long time. It's kept beautifully. Hardly aged at all."

Oscar went very still beside her.

"Announce us anyway," Juno said, stepping between them, because that was apparently who she was now—a woman who stood between bookkeepers and monsters, unpaid and unasked. "Tell the court the guild sends its regards. And tell them Guildmaster Vess follows within the hour. He wouldn't miss this for anything."

The collector's paper face rustled with surprise. "The guildmaster? Why, he has no business here."

"No?" Juno smiled, and showed teeth, because downstairs a smile was collateral and she intended to spend hers freely. "Then he'll be very interested to hear that. Won't you both."

The archway opened.

Beyond it, the Court Beneath glowed like the inside of a bank vault designed by a cathedral, and somewhere in its depths, forty years of compound interest waited to come due.
