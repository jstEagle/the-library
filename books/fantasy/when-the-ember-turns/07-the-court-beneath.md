## 7. The Court Beneath

The Undercourt did not announce itself. That was the first thing Oscar noticed, and the last thing they ever stopped noticing: there was no door, no herald, no moment where the mountain said *you may enter now*. There was only a stretch of tunnel that had been a stretch of tunnel for an hour, and then it was a hall, and then they were standing in it, and their legs reported that they had been walking toward this place since before they could remember.

Juno felt it too. He knew because her hand found his sleeve—not to hold, just to confirm he was still solid.

"Tell me you feel like you've been here before," she whispered.

"I feel like I've been everywhere before," Oscar said. "That's either the mountain or forty years of bad sleep."

The hall was lit by nothing in particular—a warm amber glow with no source, the color of embers banked for the night. The walls were not walls so much as suggestions of walls, stone that kept its distance politely. And the floor—Oscar looked down and immediately wished they hadn't—the floor was a ledger. A vast one, pages of pale vellum fitted edge to edge, each line of script finer than a hair, and every step they took left a faint new entry behind them, as though the floor were taking attendance.

*Oscar Ishikawa. Present,* the floor wrote, and then, after a pause, added something in a smaller hand that Oscar chose not to read.

"Don't look at your feet," Juno advised, having clearly read hers. "Mine says something about interest."

At the far end of the hall stood a dais, and on the dais stood a desk, and at the desk sat—

Oscar had expected a monster. They had spent three days of descent rehearsing for a monster. What sat at the desk was a person of indeterminate age and impeccable posture, wearing robes the color of wet slate, with a face like a portrait that had been painted by someone who'd only ever heard faces described. It was not ugly. It was simply *uncommitted*.

"You are expected," the figure said, in a voice like paper being folded. "Though 'expected' is generous. You have been *on file* since 1187. We do not expect files. We merely keep them."

"I've come about my account," Oscar said.

Something flickered across the uncommitted face. If it had been a human face, Oscar might have called it hunger.

"Yes," the court said. "You have."

* * *

They were given seats. This surprised Oscar more than anything else so far—two chairs of black wood, worn smooth, placed before the desk as though visitors came often enough to wear them down. Perhaps they did. The floor's ledger went back further than the light reached.

"State your business," the court said, though the ledger beneath them had already rustled, pages turning somewhere below, a sound like wind through a wheat field made of debts.

"My name," Oscar said. "My soul. Sold forty-one years ago by Guildmaster Aldous Vess, under contract—" they recited the entry number from memory, because they had not slept much, "—clause nine, sub-clause four, payment rendered in soul-interest quarterly thereafter. I am the asset. I have come to collect myself."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and a filing system.

"You understand," the court said at last, "that most assets do not know they are assets. It is considered a feature of good bookkeeping." It tilted its painted head. "You have been living outside us for four decades. Walking around. Eating breakfast. Writing in little books. All while belonging to this court. Do you know how rare that is? Most souls we take are *used*. Yours has been on loan to yourself, interest-free, out of what I can only describe as sentimentality on the part of your seller. Vess liked the idea of you. A keeper who did not know what he kept."

"I'm glad my suffering was aesthetically pleasing to him."

"It was efficient, which in this court is the same thing." The court opened a drawer that had not been there a moment ago and produced a document. It was old, and it bore a signature Oscar recognized the way one recognizes one's own handwriting on a letter one never wrote—because it was theirs, aged forty years, written by a child of eleven who had been told they were signing a permission slip.

"So you want it back," the court said. "Yourself."

"Yes."

"Then we can arrange it." The court smiled, which involved none of the muscles a smile should involve, and all of the ones it shouldn't. "We are not monsters, Keeper. We honor our contracts. The soul can be released to you today. Returned whole, unworn, exactly as deposited. You could walk out of this mountain remembering your mother's face. The taste of salt. The color blue—you've lost blue recently, haven't you? It goes in patches, like frost on glass. All of it restored. One small formality."

Oscar's heart, which was technically a borrowed organ running on borrowed time, did something complicated.

"The price," they said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. Somewhere on the descent through the debtor's doors, bargaining had stopped being frightening and started being familiar, and they hated how natural the steadiness felt. "There's always a price. Name it."

"Two options," the court said pleasantly. "We are flexible. Option one: another soul, freely given. Not taken—taken souls are worth pennies. *Given*. Volunteered. Signed over by someone standing right here, awake, aware, consenting. We find those hold their value beautifully." Its eyes moved, with terrible casualness, to Juno.

"No," Oscar said.

"You haven't heard option two."

"The gate," Juno said quietly. She'd gone very pale, but her voice was level. "It's the gate, isn't it. Open it wide. Let all of them through."

The court spread its hands, delighted. "The Ember Gate! Sealed these two hundred years by your guild out of what I can only call small-mindedness. Behind it wait gods older than accounting—hungry, magnificent, *generous*, in their way. Open it, Keeper, and we release your soul as a gesture of goodwill between neighbors. Every god passes. Every prayer gets answered. No more widow waiting, no more child praying into the dark. Think of it. You'd be the one who ended the famine of miracles."

"And every miracle would have teeth," Oscar said. "I've seen what comes through the cracks already. A husband returned wrong. A harvest that sang. I traced them all back to hairline fractures in the gate. You're asking me to widen them into doors."

"We're asking you to choose," the court said, folding its hands, "between your self and everyone else's comfort. It is the oldest choice there is. We merely offer better terms than most."

And that was when the doors—real ones, this time, tall and dark at the end of the hall that had definitely been plain tunnel five minutes ago—opened, and Guildmaster Aldous Vess walked in looking like a man arriving fashionably late to a dinner he had catered himself.

* * *

He had dressed for the occasion, which was somehow worse than armor would have been. Silver-grey coat, guild sigil polished, boots that had never touched honest mud. He crossed the ledger floor without looking down, because of course he didn't; men like Vess assumed the ground would bill them later.

"Guildmaster," Oscar said.

"Keeper." Vess smiled the smile he used at negotiations, the one with exactly enough warmth to be deniable. "You've caused a stir topside. Two days of silence from you, and Juno Vale missing, and half the city's prayers going strange. I told them: Oscar Ishikawa is simply balancing the books. But here you are, unbalancing mine." His gaze slid to Juno, and warmed by several dishonest degrees. "And there she is. Juno. You've been hard to reach."

"I've been busy," Juno said.

"I imagine. Reading things above your clearance." Vess sighed with theatrical regret, and turned to the court as though confiding in an old friend. "Forgive the interruption. I've come to settle an outstanding matter." He produced a folded paper from his breastcoat, crisp, official, sealed with the guild's wax. "Contract of service, one Juno Vale, entered into under... let's call them pressing circumstances. Her debts to the guild transfer to this court upon presentation. Standard clause. She works off what she owes—or fails to, and the court does what courts do."

"You sold me," Juno said. Flat. Not a question. "You put me in front of the Ashwick job knowing I'd fail, knowing the failure would trigger the debt clause. You built me into a debt on purpose."

"Juno." Vess's voice went gentle, which from him was the loudest alarm in any room. "I invested in you. Years of training, room, board, opportunities. Is it so wrong to expect a return?"

"You embezzled," Oscar said.

The word dropped into the hall like a coin into a well.

Vess turned slowly. "Excuse me?"

"Soul-interest." Oscar took a step forward, and the ledger floor wrote their name under their feet with something that felt, absurdly, like encouragement. "Every quarter, for forty-one years, payments rendered against my account. Clause nine, sub-clause four. I've read the entries. I've read them *very* carefully, Guildmaster—it's what I do. The amounts recorded in the guild ledger don't match the amounts recorded here." They gestured down, at the vast pale pages beneath them all. "The court received less than the guild paid. Someone skimmed the difference. Forty-one years of skimmed soul-interest, compounded." They smiled, and it was Vess's own negotiating smile, returned with interest. "You didn't just sell me. You've been collecting on me twice."

The court had gone very still. In Oscar's limited experience of uncommitted faces, stillness was worse than any expression.

"That," the court said softly, "would be an irregularity."

"Auditable," Oscar corrected. "Deeply auditable."

"Oscar." Vess's charm shed away like paint in rain. Beneath it he was suddenly, visibly old. "Whatever you think you've found, the arithmetic is more complicated than—"

"Then let the court do the arithmetic," Oscar said. "It's what it's *for*."

For one long moment, nobody breathed. The amber light held. Somewhere far below, the great ledger turned a page.

And then the court rose from its chair—an unfolding motion that took slightly too long and involved slightly too many joints—and looked at Aldous Vess the way winter looks at a late harvest.

"Keeper Ishikawa," it said warmly. "Before we proceed with the audit—and oh, we *will* proceed with the audit—we find ourselves revisiting our earlier offer. Because you've just done something interesting. You've demonstrated value. Judgment. *Bookkeeping.*"

"Which changes nothing," Oscar said.

"Which changes everything," the court said, "because now we would prefer not to lose you at all. So. Revised terms, final terms, offered in good faith: your soul, returned, free of charge—today, whole, blue included—in exchange for the gate. Or another soul, freely given, and you walk out of here yourself, and we will even throw in your friend Vale's debts, cancelled, as a courtesy." Its painted face bent close, and its breath smelled of cold ash. "Choose quickly, Keeper. The audit will take some time, and the mountain grows impatient. And behind that gate of yours—"

Far above them, muffled by miles of stone, something knocked.

Three times. Patient. Familiar, somehow, in the way of a sound heard once in childhood.

"—something has begun asking whether anyone is home."

The court sat back down, folded its hands, and waited.

So did everyone else.

So, Oscar realized with a sinking feeling that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with clarity, did they.
