## 8. Names Are Debts

Juno Vale had been in a great many rooms where she was not welcome. She had picked locks in the houses of magistrates, slipped through the dreams of sleeping bankers (a long story involving a stolen key and an unlicensed somnambulist), and once attended a duchess's wedding purely to steal back the ring the duchess had stolen first. But she had never stood in a court where the walls were made of debt.

That was the only way she could describe it. The Undercourt was not stone and it was not shadow; it was *owing*, given architecture. Pillars of unpaid promises. Arches of favors never returned. Somewhere above them, faint as a heartbeat, the Ember Gate groaned in its hairline cracks, and every groan made the pillars shiver like plucked strings.

And at the center of it all sat Oscar Ishikawa — hollow-eyed, flickering at the edges like a candle in a draft — facing down a bench of gods who looked, disappointingly, like accountants.

"Then we are agreed," said the tallest of them, whose ledger-skin rippled with entries Juno could half-read from thirty feet away. "The soul of Oscar Ishikawa, held forty years against the original signature, shall be released. Provided payment."

"Provided payment," Oscar repeated. Their voice sounded scraped thin.

"A soul freely given," the god said, "or the gate opened wide, that all who listen may pass."

*Or,* thought Juno, *you take the third option nobody's offered yet.* She had read enough contracts in her life to know that the worst ones always came with exactly two choices. Two choices meant someone had already decided for you.

She stepped forward before her good sense — which had always been more of a casual acquaintance than a friend — could stop her.

"I'll do it."

Every pillar in the room turned its attention toward her. That is not a figure of speech. The pillars *turned*. Juno felt forty years of small thefts, broken vows, and one truly regrettable incident involving a bishop's hat settle over her shoulders like a wet cloak.

Oscar turned too, and the look on their face was worse than any god's. It was the look of someone watching a friend walk into traffic.

"No," Oscar said.

"You haven't even heard my terms."

"There are no terms. No."

Juno planted her feet. She had rehearsed this on the way down, in the dark, while Oscar slept badly against a wall of collateralized kindnesses. She had rehearsed it because she was bad at sincerity and needed the practice.

"A thief's soul is already half-spent," she said. "That's not self-pity, that's accounting. Look at me." She spread her arms. "I've sold hours of my life to three separate fences. I owe a debt in Vellum Row that accrues interest I've never even seen the principal on. Half of what I am belongs to other people already. What's left over isn't worth much to me — but to you, it's everything. You get whole. I get out from under Vess. Everyone's books balance." She grinned, because grinning was easier than meaning it. "It's elegant. Even you have to admit it's elegant."

"It's *terrible*," Oscar said, and their voice cracked down the middle, and for one unguarded moment they were not a guildkeeper or a bargainer or a living hinge-in-waiting. They were just a person who had spent a lifetime keeping everyone else's accounts and had never once been on the receiving end of generosity. "You don't get to spend yourself buying me. That's not how—"

"That's *exactly* how it works. It's how everything works down here. You taught me that."

"Not like this."

"Yes, like this!" Juno heard her own voice go sharp, and underneath the sharpness, furious and unwelcome, something that felt suspiciously like grief. "You think I don't know what you're doing? You've been doing it since the day I met you. Trading pieces of yourself away so no one else has to pay. Well, I learned bookkeeping from the best, Oscar Ishikawa, and here's my entry: one soul, freely given, in exchange for yours. Witnessed. Signed." She pulled the little knife she used for signing things — thieves signed a great deal more than people assumed — and held it out.

Oscar did not take it.

Instead, Oscar smiled. And it was not a nice smile. It was the smile of a person who had balanced the Ledger of Impossible Things for forty years and had just, finally, found an error worth correcting.

"Juno," Oscar said softly, "do you remember what you told me about margins?"

* * *

She remembered. Of course she remembered. Three nights ago, in the guild archive, lamplight and dust, Juno's fingers walking down the columns of a forty-year-old entry while Oscar watched her like a man watching his own grave being measured. *One soul, sold beneath the mountain, received: [entry torn].* And in the margin, in Vess's neat, greedy hand, a notation so small most auditors would have missed it:

*Interest to accrue to the undersigned.*

Soul-interest. Forty years of it. Compounding.

"The court keeps meticulous records," Oscar said now, turning to face the bench of accountant-gods, and there was something almost gentle in how carefully they said it, the way you speak when you are about to detonate a building you used to live in. "Meticulous. So let us be meticulous together. Forty years ago, Guildmaster Vess sold my soul to this court. The purchase price was recorded. But the sale was structured as a loan against my name — and the interest on that loan, accruing annually, was directed by contract—" Oscar produced a folded page from inside their coat, and Juno recognized the torn margin, "—not to this court. To Vess. Personally."

The pillars went very still.

"For forty years," Oscar continued, and their voice gathered strength with each word like a wheel rolling downhill, "every prayer answered through the Ember Gate, every miracle with teeth, every soul that passed beneath this mountain carrying a debt — the interest on those debts has been skimmed. Diverted. Embezzled. By the very guild sworn to keep your ledgers clean." They let the page fall open. "I keep the Ledger of Impossible Things, honored judges. I know arithmetic the way you know hunger. Shall I show you the sums?"

The tallest god extended one finger — an appendage that was somehow also a column of figures — and touched the page.

For a long moment, nothing. Then the entire court *inhaled*. There was no other word for it. The pillars drew tight, the arches contracted, and every debt in the room seemed to lean inward, the way a crowd leans toward a fight.

"Embezzlement," said the tall god, tasting the word. "From *us*."

"Forty years' worth," said Oscar. "Compounded."

Juno felt the fury come off the court like heat off a forge, and standing in it, she understood something with perfect, blinding clarity: she had spent her whole life thinking anger was a weakness, a leak, something to be managed. But this wasn't a leak. This was a *verdict*. The gods weren't offended the way kings are offended. They were offended the way arithmetic is offended. Somewhere in the world above, a fat, charming, terrible old man was pouring tea in his study, and he did not yet know that every contract he had ever written had just turned around to look at him.

"He'll claim ignorance," Juno said quickly, stepping up beside Oscar. "He'll charm you. He's very good. He once talked a customs officer into apologizing to him."

"The court does not accept charm as legal tender," said the tall god.

"No," Oscar agreed. "But it accepts *collateral*. And Vess has been posting collateral against debts he never owned. Every favor the guild holds. Every name in its vaults. Every miracle it ever brokered. All of it, pledged against obligations that were never his to pledge." A pause. "Including mine."

The court deliberated. It did not look like deliberation; it looked like ten thousand small figures rearranging themselves, like frost forming on a window, like the world's longest abacus finding its sum.

When the tall god spoke again, its voice had changed. The boredom was gone. What remained was the thing boredom had been protecting everyone from.

"The contract of Vess is hereby called due. In full. Immediately."

Somewhere far above, Juno would later learn, a teacup shattered.

"And the soul of Oscar Ishikawa?" Oscar asked. Their voice was steady, but Juno saw their hands, and their hands were shaking. "The original debt. Is it discharged?"

The tall god regarded them for a long moment. "The debt stands. The payment terms stand. A soul freely given, or the gate opened wide." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "The court honors its contracts, keeper. Even the cruel ones. *Especially* the cruel ones."

"But the embezzlement—"

"Is Vess's crime, not yours. Your soul remains ours until paid for." The god's ledger-skin rippled, and Juno caught, just barely, a new line writing itself across it. "Though the court notes — *notes only* — that the thief Vale offered payment freely given, and that such offers, once witnessed, do not expire."

Juno's stomach dropped. "Wait. Does that mean—"

"It means," Oscar said quietly, "that we need to leave. Now. Before you find a way to make good on that offer, and before—" They stopped. Their head came up, and Juno saw them listening to something she couldn't hear: the Ember Gate, far above, groaning again. Louder this time. Longer.

The pillars shivered. Dust of powdered debt sifted down from arches that had stood since before the city had a name.

"The gate," Oscar whispered. "Vess is going to lose everything, and Vess knows exactly one way to save himself. If he can't own the court, he'll open the door and let the old gods eat it. Let *everyone* eat. Burn the ledgers, burn the city, burn the evidence."

They were already moving, Oscar limping toward the debtor's door with Juno's arm under their shoulder, both of them stumbling up through courts where every kindness was suddenly, urgently, called in. Behind them the tall god's voice followed, dry as a filed receipt:

"Run quickly, keeper. The court would hate to collect on you *early*."

At the threshold, Juno glanced back once. The bench of accountant-gods was already dissolving into motion, ten thousand figures streaming upward like smoke with somewhere to be, and at the head of them, faint but unmistakable, she heard the sound she would hear in her dreams for months afterward:

Arithmetic. Marching.

Above them, muffled through a hundred fathoms of owing, the Ember Gate began — very softly, very patiently — to glow.
