## 9. When the Ember Turns

The Undercourt did not have a ceiling so much as it had a decision not to fall on anyone.

Oscar stood beneath that suspended indecision, in a hall of black stone veined with slow amber light, and watched the oldest god present unclasp a box the size of a prayer book. Inside, coiled like a sleeping ember, was a soul. *Their* soul. It looked smaller than Oscar had imagined — small enough to hold in one hand, small enough to lose down the back of a chair, which was, they supposed, roughly what had happened forty years ago.

"Itemized," said the god, whose name was a sound like a ledger closing. "One soul, Ishikawa, condition: lightly used. Interest accrued. The court honors its terms."

Behind Oscar, Juno Vale made a noise she probably thought was subtle. It was the noise of a thief watching someone else's fortune get handed over and calculating, out of pure professional habit, how she'd steal it.

"The gate," Oscar said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt, which was the only kind of steady they'd ever had. "You release the soul to me outside the mountain. That was the term."

"It is." The god tilted its head, and the amber veins in the walls brightened, as if the whole court were leaning in. "And you will note, keeper, that we have honored every term ever set before us. To the letter. To the *punctuation*." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "It is others who mistake our precision for generosity."

Oscar had spent a lifetime around contracts. They knew exactly what that sentence was for.

* * *

The Ember Gate stood at the mountain's crown, and it was worse than Oscar remembered, because Oscar had never actually seen it — only felt it through forty years of nightly balancing, the way a dentist feels a tooth. Up close it was a door of fused iron and older wood, no taller than a garden gate, absurdly humble for something that held back the hunger of dead gods. Across its face ran hairline cracks, fine as the ones in an old teacup, and through them seeped a light that was not light at all but *attention*.

Juno crouched beside it, pressing her palm flat to the iron. "It's humming," she said. "Doors shouldn't hum. Doors should mind their own business."

"They're listening," Oscar said. "They've been listening for years. Every crack is an ear." They hefted the box against their chest. Inside, their soul turned over like a sleeper. "I open this, carry it through to the far side where the court's writ ends, and it's mine. Whole. Untouched by Vess, by the guild, by any of it."

"And?" Juno said, because she was many things but never stupid.

Oscar looked at the cracks. Through the widest one, something on the other side had noticed them looking, and pressed closer, the way a cat presses to a door it has decided is ajar.

"And," Oscar said, "the old gods have been waiting decades for this door to open from the inside. Not broken. Opened. There's a difference in the law of things, and they know it, and I'm about to hand them the difference gift-wrapped."

"So we don't open it."

"We open it." Oscar heard their own voice go quiet and terrible. "Because the alternative is walking back into the world with a soul the court still technically holds lien on, and waiting for Vess's successors to find a loophole. No. We do it now, while the court is bound by its own terms and watching, and while I am — " they almost laughed — "apparently the most interesting thing these gods have seen in a century."

Juno stood. She dusted off her knees with the brisk efficiency of someone who had decided to be brave and didn't want it discussed. "Right. And when the hungry dead come pouring through the gap like smoke out of a dropped pan?"

"Then I close it."

"From which side?"

Oscar didn't answer, and Juno looked at them for a long moment, and then she swore with the fluency of her profession and punched Oscar lightly on the arm, which was, Oscar would think later, the truest blessing they ever received.

"You could have led with that," she said.

"I was hoping to think of a better plan on the way up."

"Did you?"

"No."

* * *

The gate opened the way grief opens — all at once, and forever after.

Oscar set their palm against the iron and spoke the word of unsealing that had been passed keeper to keeper since before the guild had a name, and the hairline cracks drank the word in and widened, and the door swung inward on darkness that was not empty. It was crowded. It was *so* crowded. Old gods pressed at the threshold like faces at a window: things with too many joints and too much patience, things that had been prayed to when the world was young and had answered, always, too literally, until the prayers stopped and left them starving.

The first one through was small — a god of thresholds itself, ironically, all hinges and hinges' regrets — and it poured past Oscar's shoulder like cold air, and behind it came the pressure of everything else.

Oscar stepped through.

For one heartbeat they were whole. The box opened under their hands and the soul rose out of it and sank into them like warmth into cold stone, and oh — oh, it was *salt*. It was the taste of salt on their tongue, sudden and absurd and holy, the sea they hadn't been able to taste in forty years. It was color flooding back into the world, Juno's coat going from gray to a fierce ridiculous red. It was their mother's face, arriving unbidden and complete: laughing, flour on her hands, saying something Oscar couldn't hear over the roar of returning self, and didn't need to hear, because she was *there*, she was real, she had existed, they had existed —

And behind them, the gods came through.

* * *

"OSCAR!" Juno's voice, from the near side, thin as thread. "Close it! Whatever you're doing, do it FASTER!"

Oscar turned. The threshold was a river of the ancient and the ravenous, and the gate stood open between worlds, and there was no version of this where Oscar walked back through and pulled it shut behind them. The gods were already through. If the gate closed now, it closed on a world with hungry divinities loose in it, and the guild's whole invisible machinery — every favor, every name, every small miracle traded carefully across four hundred years — would be a sandcastle against the tide.

Unless the gate closed *from the inside*. Unless something on the far side took hold of both worlds and became the hinge.

There was a word for that. It wasn't in the ledger. It wasn't in any contract the court had ever drafted, which meant — and here Oscar felt something wild bloom in their newly returned chest — it meant the price was *unset*. Unwritten. For the first time in their life, nobody had already sold the terms.

The nearest old god paused at the threshold. It had been a harvest god once, maybe, or a hearth god; its hunger had eaten its original shape, but something in it still recognized the smell of a bargain.

"What will you give," it said, in a voice like a field going fallow.

"Not give," Oscar said. "*Become.*"

They put their hands on the gate — one palm on the iron of the near world, one on the dark of the far one — and they did the thing they had done every night for forty years, balancing the ledger, holding two sides of an account in perfect tension. But this time the account was creation itself, and the entries were *everything*, and instead of writing the balance they *were* the balance.

It hurt the way birth hurts. It hurt the way names hurt.

The gate began to close, and Oscar closed with it.

* * *

From the outside — and Juno Vale would tell it this way for years, usually with a drink in her hand and her voice not quite steady — it looked simple. The door swung shut. The light went out. The humming stopped, and the mountain settled like a dog lying down.

But just before the seam sealed, Juno saw Oscar's face in the narrowing gap, and Oscar smiled at her — actually smiled, the rare full one, the one that cost them nothing for the first time ever — and mouthed three words. Not *goodbye*. Juno was very clear about that later, furious about it, in fact.

*I choose this.*

Then the gate became a wall, and the wall became stone, and somewhere in the stone was a warmth that had not been there before.

On the far side, the old gods found themselves facing a keeper who was also a door, and doors, as everyone knows, are the one thing gods must knock on. And the keeper of the gate was patient, and fair, and itemized everything, and had learned from the best — from a guildmaster's treachery, from a court's cruelty, from a thief's loyalty — exactly how much a name is worth.

Which is why, months later, when Juno Vale sat at the old desk with the Ledger of Impossible Things open to its first blank page, pen hovering, she knew precisely what to write.

She wrote it slowly, in her best bookkeeping hand:

*Oscar Ishikawa — Keeper of the Third Name. Account status: chosen.*

And beneath the mountain, deep in the sealed dark, something warm knocked twice — the guild's old signal, *all's well, come home* — and waited, contentedly, for the next impossible thing.
