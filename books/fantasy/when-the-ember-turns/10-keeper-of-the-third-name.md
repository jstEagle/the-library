## 10. Keeper of the Third Name

The ledger had opinions about weather.

Juno Vale had learned this in her first month as Keeper. On damp nights the pages swelled and the ink bled sideways, so that old debts seemed to weep into their own margins. On dry ones the paper crackled like an old woman clearing her throat before saying something unkind. Tonight it was dry, and the ledger sat on Oscar's old desk — *her* desk now, though she still knocked on its edge out of habit, the way you'd knock on a doorframe to announce yourself — smelling faintly of ash and patience.

Outside the guildhouse window, the city was doing what it did best: pretending nothing had happened.

That was the strange part, months later. She'd expected cracks in the sky, processions, at minimum some awkward questions from the temples. Instead there had been a season of unanswered prayers, and then — nothing dramatic at all. The widow on Candle Street still lit her lamp each dusk. The baker still burned his offerings to a god who no longer leaned down to sniff them. The prayers went up like smoke from a chimney with a bird's nest in it: sincere, rising, going nowhere.

Because the gate held.

Because Oscar Ishikawa had stood in the doorway between the world and everything hungry behind it, and had chosen not to come home.

Juno turned to the first blank page. It had been blank for six months. Every night she told herself tonight was the night, and every night she found one more entry to copy first, one more margin note to tidy, one more cup of tea that absolutely required drinking. Vess would have called it procrastination. Vess, currently, was being slowly digested by a contract of his own drafting, which everyone agreed was poetic and nobody said aloud twice.

She dipped the pen. The nib hovered.

"You're talking to a book again," said Marta from the doorway, arms full of firewood. Marta was the guild's cook, a broad woman with forearms like dock rope and the only person in the building who had never once asked Juno what she'd done to deserve her post. "I can tell because you've got the guilty face."

"I'm working."

"You're hovering. Hovering's not working. My mother hovered over soup for forty years and it never once improved."

Juno set the pen down. "It's the name," she admitted. "I keep almost writing it and then thinking — who am I to decide? He should get a say."

"He sealed himself into a door, love. His say is limited."

"That's exactly the problem."

Marta dumped the wood in the bin, considered Juno for a moment with the frank appraisal she usually reserved for underproofed dough, and said, "When my husband died, I kept setting two cups out for a year. You know when I stopped? Not when I accepted it. When I realized the second cup wasn't for him. It was for me — so I wouldn't have to look at how empty the table was." She shrugged. "Write the name. Not for him. For you. So the page isn't empty either."

And she left, because Marta understood exits better than anyone Juno had ever known, including professional thieves.

* * *

Here is what the guild knew about the Ember Gate now, which is to say here is what Juno had written in the ledger and therefore what was true:

Entry one: the gate could not be closed by force, by prayer, or by payment. It could only be closed by consent, freely given, from both sides.

Entry two: Oscar Ishikawa had given theirs.

Entry three: the guild's records listed one soul, sold beneath the mountain, forty years past. Status: returned. Location: n/a. The clerk's box where a location went contained, in Juno's handwriting, a small drawing of a door.

Entry four, added last week after considerable deliberation: the Keeper reports that on quiet nights, if one knocks twice on the foundation stone beneath the east stair, something knocks back. Twice. Always twice. Juno had written this in the formal register voice and then, unable to help herself, added in the margin: *it feels pleased.*

The guild had voted on whether to keep her. It had taken four minutes, three of which were spent arguing about whether tea during votes counted as bribery. (It did. Marta had been fined.) But the vote itself was unanimous, because the guild preferred to stay invisible, and invisible things needed someone to remember they existed. That was the whole job, really. That had always been the whole job. Oscar had done it for thirty years without ever once appearing in their own ledger, and Juno thought about that more than was probably healthy.

A keeper of impossible things, erased from the book of them. It was the kind of joke the universe told when it thought no one was listening.

Well. Juno was listening now.

* * *

She went down to the stone that night instead of writing, because Marta's advice had backfired spectacularly and made her want to procrastinate with purpose.

The foundation stone beneath the east stair was ordinary granite, cold as any other rock, except that it wasn't, quite. There was a warmth in it, deep down, like a banked fire — like an ember, if she were feeling literary, which she was, because the alternative was feeling sad, and Juno Vale had spent enough of her life being sad to develop standards.

She knelt. Knocked twice.

A pause. Long enough for doubt. Then: knock, knock.

"Evening," she said, to a rock, in a normal speaking voice, because dignity was for people with better options. "Ledger's fine. City's fine. Nobody's praying at you, which I'm told is the point. The baker's furious, incidentally. Says his god used to give feedback. Marta says good riddance, gods meddle. I didn't vote but I lean toward Marta."

Knock. Knock.

"Yeah, all right, I know why I came down here."

She pressed her palm flat against the stone. The warmth rose to meet her, patient as tide.

"I nearly wrote it tonight," she said. "Your new name. And then I thought — what if it's wrong? What if you hate it? What if you're down there — in there — wherever — thinking, *Vale, you absolute hack, I had a perfectly good name picked out and it was Gerald.*"

The warmth pulsed. She chose to interpret that as laughter. She needed to interpret it as laughter.

"Here's the thing though." Her voice went quieter, the way it did in the mountain courts, where volume was collateral. "You spent your whole life keeping a book about everyone else. Everyone got to be real in those pages except you. So this one page — this one's yours, and I can't fill it in for you, and I also can't leave it empty forever, because empty pages are how this whole mess started. Somebody left a space where a person should be, and the court moved in."

She took a breath.

"So here's what I'm going to do. I'm going to write it. And if it's wrong, you knock three times, and I'll cross it out, and we'll try again tomorrow. Deal?"

Silence. Then, softly, the stone warmed under her hand like a hand squeezing back.

Deal.

* * *

Back upstairs, the candle had burned low and the ledger waited with its blank page open like a held breath.

Juno sat. Dipped the pen. This time she didn't hover.

In the space where a soul had once been sold, she wrote:

*Oscar Ember-Ishikawa. Keeper of the Gate. Neither sold nor given nor taken — chosen. Chosen twice, actually, which in this guild counts as a majority.*

She stared at it. It was too long for a registry entry and too short for everything it meant, which felt correct. Names were debts; Oscar had taught her that, or rather the ledger had, through Oscar's margins, in that cramped careful hand she could still see in every old entry, holding the whole book together like stitching.

Below it, because she couldn't stop herself, she added: *Correspondence may be delivered via the east stair. Knock twice.*

Then she closed the book, and the guildhouse settled around her — creaks and coals and Marta humming off-key somewhere above — and Juno Vale, thief, debtor, Keeper, put her head down on the desk and slept without dreaming of doors for the first time in months.

* * *

Spring came the way it always did in the city: rudely, then beautifully.

On the first warm evening, Juno carried the ledger out to the courtyard to air it, and found the widow from Candle Street waiting at the guild's unmarked door. The woman held a lamp, unlit, and wore the expression of someone who had rehearsed a speech many times.

"They say prayers don't work anymore," the widow said.

"They say right," Juno said carefully.

"Mm." The widow looked at the lamp. "My husband used to say the old gods answered too fast anyway. Never gave you time to figure out what you actually wanted." She held out the lamp. "Could you take this down to your stair? Not a prayer. Just — a light. For whoever's keeping it dark down there."

Juno took it. Their fingers brushed the glass, and inside, without flame, the wick glowed — faintly, warmly, the exact color of a banked ember.

"Well," said the widow, satisfied. "Seems it knows the way."

That night, Juno set the lamp on the foundation stone, knocked twice, and got her two knocks back — and then, for the very first time, a third.

Three meant *wrong name*, they'd agreed.

Her heart dropped through the floor. She snatched the ledger open, scanning the fresh ink, already composing apologies — and stopped.

Because below her entry, in cramped, careful handwriting she knew like her own pulse, in ink that had not been there an hour ago, a line had added itself to the page:

*Not wrong. Three means: thank you. And — Vale. About time you learned to knock properly.*

Juno laughed until Marta came downstairs to check on her, and then she laughed again, and the lamp on the stone burned steady all night, and in the ledger of impossible things, the first page stayed warm to the touch until morning.
