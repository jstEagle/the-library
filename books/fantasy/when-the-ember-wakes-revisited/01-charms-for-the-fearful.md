## 1. Charms for the Fearful

The tide came in gray at Greyhollow, and it always smelled of rust and old fish and the particular melancholy of a city that had stopped believing in better weather three generations ago. Yara Ishikawa had learned to love it anyway, or something close enough to love that she could live inside it without flinching.

She opened her shop before dawn because that was when the harbor folk came—those who wanted their purchases unseen, unremarked, tucked into pockets like small guilty prayers. She lit the lamp behind the counter with a taper struck on flint, the ordinary way, though her fingers itched to simply *will* the wick alive as she once had. Three years of striking flint. Three years of pretending her hands were only hands.

The shop itself was narrow and deep as a coffin stood upright, shelved floor to rafters with the inventory of a life she'd built from nothing: dried sea-lavender bundled in twine, whale-oil candles, river-stones polished smooth, copper wire, needles, thread the color of every grief. And along the back shelf, mixed carelessly among the trinkets so that no one's eye would snag on them, the charms.

She made them slowly, one at a time, in the hours after closing, working plain craft over quiet power until the seam between them was invisible even to her. A knotted red cord to keep a fisherman's nets from tearing. A bent iron nail wrapped in waxed linen to nail above a door against bad dreams. A river-stone with a hairline crack filled with gold leaf—a *kintsugi* charm, her mother would have called it, though Yara had not spoken her mother's name aloud in longer than she had spoken Vessia's.

None of them glowed. None of them hummed. That was the art of it. In Greyhollow, where the crown's edicts were nailed to the customs-house door and magic was a word people said the way they said *plague*, a charm had to be small enough to be doubted. Belief did the rest. Belief, and whatever thin thread of ember-light Yara still dared to feed through the knot or the wax or the stone, so faint that even she sometimes wondered if she imagined it.

Her god had gone silent the same winter the frost first refused to leave. Three years of silence. She still woke some nights with the old covenant-sigil burning cold on her inner wrist—the coiled flame, Vessia's mark, inked into her skin at nineteen when she'd sworn herself to a war she'd believed was holy—and she would lie in the dark and press her thumb against it and feel nothing at all. Like pressing a scar. Like pressing a grave.

*You hid me well,* she thought some mornings, addressing the sigil, or the silence, or herself. *Perhaps too well.*

* * *

The dockworker came an hour past dawn, when the fog off the harbor was thick enough to drink. He was a big man gone soft in the middle, with rope-burned palms and eyes red-rimmed from more than salt spray. He stood in the doorway a long moment, dripping, working his cap in his fists.

"Closed," Yara said, not looking up from the candle she was trimming. It wasn't true, and they both knew it. The ritual was part of the transaction. Deny them once, so that later, if anyone asked, they could say they'd been turned away.

"Please." His voice cracked on the word. "They said—I was told you keep charms. Small ones. Harmless things."

Yara set down her knife and looked at him properly. Marta's husband, she thought. Or Marta's brother—one of the family that worked the herring boats out of the third slip, the ones who'd lost two sons to the ice floes last spring when the season turned wrong and the harbor froze in what should have been thaw-month. She'd seen him at the mourning fires, standing very straight, the way men do when falling apart is not permitted.

"Harmless things," she echoed. "I sell candles and thread, friend. Lavender for headaches."

He crossed the room in three strides and put both hands flat on her counter, leaning close, and she caught the smell of him—tar and herring brine and underneath it something sourer, fear-sweat, the smell she'd learned to read during the war when soldiers came to the camp healers too late to be saved and knew it.

"My daughter," he said. "Sera. She's six. She won't stop coughing since the cold came back, and the physic says her lungs are filling like—like bilge water, like there's nothing—" His jaw worked. "Marta's mother had a charm from you. Years ago. For the baby's fever. She swore by it. She said you were the only one in Greyhollow who—"

"Keep your voice down." Yara said it quietly, but the man flinched as if she'd shouted. He glanced over his shoulder at the fog-shrouded street, and Yara followed his gaze out the window: empty cobbles, gulls wheeling, the customs-house bell tolling slow across the water. Empty. But emptiness meant less than it used to.

"The fever-charm," she said. "That was before the proclamations. Before all this."

"I know what I'm asking." His hands were shaking now, big rope-scarred hands turning over and over on her countertop. "I know what it costs you. Name your price. Name anything. I've got four months' wages saved and I'll empty it, I'll—"

"Stop." She closed her eyes briefly. This was the moment, every time, the moment she'd rehearsed refusing a hundred ways and never once managed. *Invisibility is an art,* she reminded herself, *and every charm you sell is a brushstroke of evidence.* But the man's face was Sera's face, six years old and drowning on dry land, and Yara Ishikawa had spent too many years watching people die within reach of her hands to pretend she didn't have them anymore.

"Sit," she said. "And don't touch anything."

* * *

She made the charm while he watched, because letting him watch was safer than secrecy—if he saw only craft, he could honestly say he'd seen only craft. A river-stone from the basket beneath the counter, pale gray, round as a sleeping bird. She wrapped it in three turns of copper wire, whispering the knots tight, and then, when his attention was on her face rather than her hands, she let it come: a breath of warmth drawn up from somewhere below her sternum, from the banked hearth where Vessia's fire had once roared, down through her arm, into her fingertips.

It barely answered. It was like coaxing a spark from ash—but ash remembered being flame, and something stirred, faint and amber, sinking into the wire, into the stone. The metal warmed between her fingers. If the dockworker noticed, he gave no sign; he was watching her eyes, hungry for hope, and hope made people blind in convenient ways.

"There." She pressed it into his palm and closed his fingers over it. "Under her pillow. Not around her neck—under the pillow, where the physic won't see it. And if anyone asks, you bought a candle. Say lavender. Everyone buys lavender."

"What does it do?" His voice had gone hushed, reverent, the way people got near anything they half-believed in.

"It doesn't cure," Yara said, and hated how much truth she was giving him. "It eases. It holds the line while her body fights. The rest is her own fire." Her own words snagged on something. *Fire.* She almost laughed. "Come back in nine days and tell me how she sleeps."

He paid too much. She took half of it. He tried to argue; she put the coins back in his hand with a look that ended arguments, and then he was gone into the fog, moving faster than a man that size should move, clutching the stone like a talisman, which was exactly what it was.

Yara stood alone in her shop with her lamp guttering and her heart doing something complicated and tired in her chest.

*Nine days,* she thought. *If the girl lives, he'll tell someone. He won't mean to. Gratitude wants witnesses.* And if the girl died, grief also wanted someone to blame, and a witch was more satisfying to blame than winter.

Either way, the fuse shortened.

* * *

The proclamation was nailed to the customs-house door by midday, and Yara heard about it before she saw it, the way you hear a storm coming in the changed behavior of birds.

Tamsin from the chandlery next door burst in without knocking, cheeks bright, breathless with the particular excitement of people carrying news that frightens them. "Have you seen? They've posted it on the harbor board and the market square and the church doors—gold, Yara. Actual crown gold. Ten crowns for reporting a mage. Ten! Old Pell's whole roof for a winter costs eight." She leaned on the counter, dropping her voice to a conspirator's register that carried perfectly to the street outside. "And twenty if it's a *registered* covenant-mage. Twenty crowns. There must be dozens of people in this city who'd sell their own mothers for twenty crowns."

"Mm," Yara said, trimming her wick.

"And the burnings start at solstice, they're saying. Right there on the pier, public, so everyone can see the crown keeps its promises. They burned one in Karst already—a healer, can you imagine, a woman who'd delivered half the town's babies." Tamsin shivered deliciously. "Well. Better safe than sorry, I say. You never know who's hiding what, these days."

"No," Yara agreed mildly. "You never do."

When Tamsin had gone, Yara walked the three blocks to the customs house with her market basket on her arm like any other woman out for candles, and she stood in front of the proclamation and read it twice.

The vellum was fine, the seal heavy, the hand of the scribe beautiful. *By order of the Crown Regent, in these times of unnatural winter and divine displeasure, let it be known that the practice of sorcery constitutes treason against realm and gods alike...* The reward figures were printed large. Below them, smaller, almost an afterthought, a line that made the cold walk up Yara's spine and settle between her shoulders:

*Covenant-trained mages will be remanded to the Crown's custody for purposes of interrogation and service.*

Not killed, then. Not immediately. *Service.* The word sat strangely, and Yara found herself reading it again and again, the way you probe a sore tooth. The witch-hunts she understood—fear, scapegoats, the old arithmetic of crowds. But the crown wanting mages *kept* was different. The crown wanting them kept was purposeful.

Around her, the crowd moved past the proclamation the way crowds do: pausing, murmuring, edging away, some spitting, one old woman making a warding sign that hadn't been orthodox in a century. And Yara watched their faces instead of the page, cataloguing hunger and fear in equal measure, and understood with terrible clarity that ten crowns was more money than most of these people would see in two years.

Someone in this crowd would remember the woman who sold charms. Someone's cousin had bought a knotted cord once, or a sister had a lucky stone, or a drunk had sworn he'd seen light in her window late at night—light with no candle in it. Memory was cheap. Gold was gold.

She walked home slowly, basket swinging, past the shrine to the quiet gods where fresh snow had drifted unmelting against the steps even though it was nearly planting season. She paused there without meaning to, the way you pause at the bedside of someone dying.

*Where are you?* she asked the silence, the way she asked it every day, and the silence answered the way it always did: with snowfall, and distance, and the distant bell of the customs house ringing the hour.

Back in her shop, she barred the door, drew the shutters, and went to the back shelf. One by one, she began taking down the charms—the luck-cords, the fever-stones, the dream-nails—wrapping each in plain cloth and lowering them into the false bottom of her flour chest, erasing three years of careful, patient, invisible work.

There were eleven left unsold. Eleven small threads of ember-light in a city full of hungry eyes.

She had just buried the last one under the meal when she heard boots in the lane outside. Not one pair. Many. Moving with the unhurried coordination of men following a list.

Through the gap in the shutters, Yara watched them stop—not at her door, not yet, but at Tamsin's chandlery next door, where the chandler's apprentice was already pointing.

Pointing at her shop.

Yara blew out the lamp, knelt, and pulled up the loose flagstone at the back of the cellar where the dark went down farther than any cellar had a right to. From beneath the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth she hadn't opened in three years, she drew out the only thing she'd kept from her other life: a scrap of blackened standard bearing the coiled-flame sigil of the Ember Covenant, stiff with old blood that was not entirely her own.

The boots reached her door. Fists hammered.

Yara folded the standard against her heart, whispered an apology to the shop, to the city, to the woman she had pretended to be—and went down into the dark.
