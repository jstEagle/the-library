## 2. The List

The proclamation went up on the Saltgate post before dawn, and by the time Yara opened her shutters, the whole street had gathered beneath it like gulls over a gutted fish.

She watched from behind her curtain, a cup of barley tea cooling in her hands, and read the words twice through the frost-ferned glass. *By order of the Crown: any person harboring knowledge of, or concealing persons gifted with, unlawful sorcery shall be entitled to ten crowns upon proof given.* Ten crowns. Enough to buy a winter's coal and a fat goose besides. Below that, in smaller letters meant for slower readers: *The gift is a sickness. The sickness is a treason.*

Someone in the crowd laughed—a short, ugly sound—and Yara set down her cup because her hands had begun to tremble.

Three years she had lived on Tallow Row, three years of selling luck-charms braided from river-reed and horsehair, of charging two coppers and smiling at old women who believed in nothing but wanted to believe in something. Three years of keeping the ember banked so deep in her chest that some mornings she woke unsure it was still there at all. She had made invisibility an art. She had thought herself its master.

Now the city had hung a price on her kind, written in ink that would not smudge, and every neighbor she had charmed with free mending and winter onions was counting their coppers against her name.

* * *

The morning passed the way all her mornings passed, which was the point of them. She swept the step. She sorted reeds by stiffness. A fishwife bought a charm against storms and haggled her down to one copper, and Yara let her, because the woman's hands were cracked raw from the nets and because kindness was the best camouflage ever devised.

But she caught herself watching the door. Watching the street. Old Marta across the lane stood talking too long with a man Yara didn't know—a lean man in a gray traveling cloak, boots too good for Tallow Row, eyes that moved along the shopfronts the way an assessor's eyes move along cargo. When he left, Marta stood a moment in the cold, looking at Yara's window.

Looking, Yara thought, at the charms hanging there. At the little brass bells and knotted cords she'd sold for years without one question asked.

Her gift stirred, low in her sternum, like a cat turning over in its sleep. *They know,* it seemed to say. *They have always known. You only taught yourself not to hear it.*

"Quiet," she murmured to no one, and the word came out rougher than she intended.

Vessia had been silent for three years—silent since the night the ember-god's voice simply stopped, mid-prayer, mid-breath, leaving Yara alone with a covenant-sigil burned into her palm and no god answering when she pressed it. But silence was not absence. She could feel Vessia the way you feel a fire burning in a room you cannot enter: warmth through a wall, light under a door. And lately—these last weeks, since the first proclamations, since the burnings began in the low towns—that warmth had been changing. Sharpening. Like a banked coal waking.

She did not know what it meant. That frightened her more than the hunters did.

* * *

It happened at dusk, as such things always do—not with trumpets but with the ordinary sound of boots.

Yara was banking her forge-fire, blowing the coals down to a dull red sleep, when she heard them on the cobbles: six sets of feet, moving in the unhurried rhythm of men who own the street they walk. Then the knock. Not a knock at all, really—the flat slap of a gauntleted hand against wood, once, twice.

"Open in the name of the Crown."

She stood very still in the red dark of her workshop. Through the wall she heard Marta's voice next door, high and eager and terribly sorry: "That's the one, that's the charm-seller, I told your man this morning, I saw the knots she ties, my grandmother tied such knots and she was taken in the Purging, may the gods forgive me, but gold is gold—"

*Marta.* Who had wept in Yara's shop when her son shipped out. Whose floor Yara had warded against rats with a whisper and a knot of rowan, free of charge, three winters running.

Yara's rage came up hot and total, a tide that surprised even her. Not fear—rage. The city she had bled for. She had held the breach at Coldspring for nine hours while Greyhollow slept safe behind walls she made with her own burned hands, and now Marta was selling her name for ten crowns and a clear conscience.

The door shuddered under a boot.

There was no way out through the shop. She knew that before she finished thinking it; she had known it for three years, actually, the way you know where the exits are in every room you love. The cellar. The old drainage channel, dry these hundred years, running out beneath the wall toward the tidal flats. She'd told herself she'd never use it. Telling yourself things was how invisibility worked.

The door broke.

She was already through the trapdoor, pulling it shut above her, already descending into the cold black by feel, when the light of lanterns spilled down through the floorboards and cut the darkness into bars. Boots crossed overhead. A man's voice, bored: "Charm-seller's gone out the back, maybe."

"Gone nowhere," said another voice, younger, sharper. "Look at this forge. Look at the ash. She was here a breath ago." A pause, and then, softer, almost gentle, the way men speak when they are enjoying themselves: "Search the cellar."

Yara pressed herself into the mouth of the drainage channel, stone cold against her spine, and did the thing she had sworn off for three years.

She reached for the ember.

It came like a dog hearing its name after long neglect—all at once, trembling, too much. Heat flooded her palms, her wrists, the hollow places grief had carved in her. She cupped her hand over the covenant-sigil at her belt—the old bronze disc, Vessia's flame-in-circle, the last thing she owned from the war—and whispered the smallest working she knew: a cantrip for dimming, for turning the eye aside. *Let them look and not see. Let the dark seem empty.*

Lantern-light swung into the cellar and lay across the channel mouth like a blade.

For one heartbeat, two, three, the light rested on her. On her face, her hands, the faint orange glow bleeding between her fingers where the sigil drank her heat and gave it back as shadow. The young hunter frowned. His companions waited behind him on the stairs.

And then his gaze slid off her, the way water slides off oiled cloth, and he grunted—"Nothing. Rats and reed-dust"—and turned away, and Yara learned in that moment what three years of hiding had truly cost: not safety, only the illusion of it, and the terrible skill of needing it.

She did not wait for them to finish ransacking her life. She crawled.

* * *

The channel ran forty paces under the wall and ended in a grate crusted with salt-bloom, and beyond it the tidal flats lay open under a sky the color of a dead man's lips. Yara came out on her belly in frozen mud, scraped and shaking, and looked back once at Greyhollow.

The city glowed against the dusk, a thousand windows lit against the cold, smoke rising straight from a thousand chimneys. From here it looked like what it pretended to be: a haven, a hearth, a place where people kept each other warm. She had defended that glow with her blood. She had believed in it the way converts believe.

Behind her, faintly, she heard her shop door splintering further, heard glass break—her mother's blue jar, probably, the one thing she'd allowed herself to keep—and something in her chest tore loose and burned.

"You took everything," she said to the city, quietly, and the breath of it steamed silver in the air. "I held your walls. I held your *walls*."

The ember answered. Not in words—never in words anymore—but in pressure, in pulse, a slow double-beat against her ribs like a heart remembering how. And beneath the rage, for the first time in three years, she felt something else from behind the wall where Vessia burned: attention. A vast, patient, wounded attention, turning toward her like a head lifting in the dark.

*You felt that,* Yara thought at it, half prayer, half accusation. *Didn't you. You're still there.*

The warmth flared once, and faded, and said nothing.

She pulled the covenant-sigil from her belt and held it in her fist until its edges bit. Bronze, flame-in-circle, worn smooth by another woman's thumb a generation before hers. It was all she had. It was, she understood suddenly, all she had ever had—the city had never been hers, the quiet life had never been real, and the god she served had not abandoned her so much as been *taken*, the way she herself had just been taken from her own shop, dragged out through a hole in the world.

Ten crowns. Her whole invisible life, priced at ten crowns.

Yara got her feet under her and walked east, away from the lights, out onto the flats where the ice sang and shifted with the tide. Behind her, Greyhollow rang its evening bell, indifferent, beautiful, already forgetting her name.

Ahead, somewhere past the salt-marshes and the pine-dark hills, the road ran north toward the wilds—and toward whatever it was that had made a god go silent and a child, if the rumors were true, burn a village bright enough to be seen from the walls.

She walked toward it. There was nothing left behind worth being invisible for.
