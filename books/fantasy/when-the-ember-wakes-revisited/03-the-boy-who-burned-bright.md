## 3. The Boy Who Burned Bright

The frost-road out of Greyhollow was a gray ribbon stitched through white country, and Yara walked it with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves, feeling like a woman made of paper. Three years of careful living had taught her the weight of every small thing she carried: the awl, the spool of copper wire, the little tin of beeswax she used to seal her charms into shapes that looked like nothing more than trinkets. She had left everything else behind in the cellar—the shop above it already burning in her memory, the witch-hunters' boots hammering across her floorboards, a neighbor's voice cracking with fear and greed as she gave Yara's name to men who paid in silver.

*Marla*, Yara thought, and could not decide whether the name tasted like betrayal or grief. Marla had bought a fertility charm from her two winters past, paid in eggs and mended stockings, had wept over it when it worked. And when the proclamations went up—*ten crowns for a reported mage, fifty for one taken alive*—Marla had looked at Yara across the market square and seen not the woman who mended her luck but a purse with legs.

The wind came down off the northern hills carrying the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet. Yara pulled her cloak tighter and kept walking. Behind her, Greyhollow's walls sank into the haze, its watchtowers crowned with the new banners of the hunt—white fields bearing a black hand, fingers spread, the sigil of the Office of Purity. She had served under better banners once. She had served under fire.

*Vessia,* she thought, the way she did every morning, a prayer worn smooth as river stone. *If you're there. If you can hear me.*

Nothing answered. Nothing had answered for three years. The silence was so complete it had stopped feeling like silence and started feeling like weather—a pressure, a cold, an absence she dressed herself around every day the way she dressed around an old wound.

She made eight miles before dusk, following the frozen river north because north meant away, away from the roads the hunters would watch, toward the hill country where a woman alone might pass for a widow going to kin. She found a hollow beneath a leaning birch where the snow drifted shallow, scraped out a nest with her boot, and sat with her back against the bark watching the light die over the hills.

That was when she saw the glow.

It came from the treeline east of the road—not torchlight, which flickers and breathes, but something steadier, a soft amber pulsing like a heartbeat seen through skin. Yara went still. Every instinct honed by three years of hiding told her to pull her hood lower, to become a stone, a stump, a shadow. But the pulse stuttered, quickened, flared—and she recognized the rhythm of it the way a musician recognizes a wrong note.

That was magic. Young magic. Frightened magic. Magic on the edge of doing something terrible to whoever carried it.

"Gods damn me," she whispered, and got up, and walked toward it.

* * *

She found him in the ruins of a charcoal-burner's camp, a scatter of blackened shelters at the edge of a stand of pines. He was curled in the lee of a collapsed drying-rack, maybe ten years old, all elbows and hollow cheeks, wearing a coat three sizes too big with scorched hemlines. The glow poured off him in waves—his hands, his throat, the wet tracks on his face all lit from within, amber-gold, unmistakable, a beacon visible for miles in the failing light.

He was crying without sound. That was the worst part. A child that age should have been sobbing loud enough to wake the dead; instead he'd learned to weep silently, jaw clenched, tears cutting clean lines through the soot on his cheeks, and each tear seemed to feed the light until his whole body shimmered like a coal breathing.

Yara knew what that was. Grief-magic, the covenant called it in its oldest texts—gift that answered feeling instead of will, common in children before training taught them walls. Untrained, ungoverned, it burned brightest exactly when its bearer was most broken. And every hunter within ten miles would be able to see it.

"Hey," she said softly.

He bolted upright like a startled bird, scrambling back against the rack, one hand thrown out before him. The glow flared white-hot. Heat rolled across the clearing, enough to make the snow at his feet slump and steam, enough to raise blisters on Yara's cheeks if she stood closer.

"Easy." She knelt, slow, palms open, the way you'd approach a wounded animal or a drawn blade. "Easy. I'm not going to hurt you."

"You're lying." His voice was raw, cracked from disuse or screaming. "Everyone lies. The soldiers said—they said if I came out they'd help me, and they had nets, they had *chains*—"

"I know." She kept her voice level, low, the voice she'd used on green recruits and panicking civilians in another life. "I know they do. Look at my hands. Empty. Look at my eyes. Do hunters wear hoods like this and walk alone? They come in packs, boy. In wagons, with horns and hounds."

His breath hitched. The glow dimmed a fraction—not gone, never gone, but banked, like a fire settling. His eyes were enormous, brown ringed with exhaustion, and fixed on her with the desperate arithmetic of a child deciding whom to trust with the only thing he had left, which was himself.

"They burned my village," he said.

Not *they took me*. Not *I ran*. *They burned my village.* Yara felt the words land somewhere old and scarred.

"Why?" she asked, though she feared she knew.

"'Cause of me." The glow surged again; he pressed his fists against his eyes. "I didn't mean—I got mad at Thom, he broke my kite, and then the hayrick caught, and Da put it out but the reeve saw, the reeve *saw*, and he sent word to Greyhollow, and then the men in white came and—" His voice shattered. The light burst outward; a pine bough overhead blackened and dropped. "They said everyone who hid me was guilty too. They said it was *law*."

Yara closed her distance while he was blinded by his own grief, and took his wrists gently in her hands. His skin was feverish, humming, alive with power looking for anywhere to go. She had felt this before—in young soldiers, in herself, decades ago, kneeling before Vessia's altar with her gift waking in her chest like a second heart.

"Listen to me." She waited until his eyes found hers. "This isn't your fault. Fire doesn't ask permission, and neither does grief. What's your name?"

A long tremor. "Oren."

"Oren. My name is Yara. I'm going to teach you something tonight, if you'll let me. It's called banking. You don't fight the fire—you give it somewhere to go. Can you try?"

He nodded, small and terrified.

She drew his hands together between her own, cupping them like a bowl, and spoke the first words of the old litany under her breath—words outlawed now, words that could hang her twice over. *"Ember to ember, breath to breath, what burns in sorrow shall burn no less in shelter."* Warmth moved through her palms, her own gift rising after three years of starvation, and she guided his wild heat into the space between their hands until it glowed there, contained, a small captive sun.

Oren stared at it. For the first time since she'd found him, he wasn't crying.

"It's warm," he whispered.

"It is," Yara said. "Now hold it. Hold it like it's a bird that trusts you."

And in the silence that followed, deep beneath the frost and the dark and three years of nothing, Yara felt something she had not felt since the night her god went quiet.

A crack. Faint as ice shifting on a spring river. Faint as a door opening one hair's width in an empty house.

Warmth. Not hers. *Answering.*

Her breath stopped. *Vessia?*

The warmth faded, uncertain, like a candle flame doubting itself—but it had been there. It had *answered*. Yara knelt in the snow with a child's trembling hands in hers and felt the first hope in three years rise in her chest, terrifying as any enemy.

* * *

They traveled north through the hill country for two days, moving at dawn and dusk, hiding through the bright hours in barns and culverts and once, memorably, a hollowed-out grain silo that smelled of mice and older, safer years. Oren learned fast. By the second evening he could hold his glow banked for a full hour, could breathe it down to almost nothing when Yara counted him through the litany, his small face fierce with concentration.

"It's like holding my breath," he explained solemnly, "but inside."

"That's exactly it," Yara said, and something in her chest ached at how easily he trusted her now, how completely, how a child who had lost everything had decided she was the thing worth losing it *for*.

On the third morning, the hounds found them.

Yara heard them first—a thin music of belling drifting down the frozen river valley, still miles off but closing. She stood on the ridge with the wind pulling at her hood and counted handlers' horns answering through the white country, and her stomach turned to iron. Not one party. Three, fanning out from the south and east, driving the valley like beaters driving game.

They'd found the charcoal camp. Someone had read the scorch marks and known a mage-child had passed through, and the crown did not send one leash-pack after confirmed prey.

"Oren." Her voice was calm, and she hated how calm it was, how practiced. "We need to move. Now. Off the river, into the rocks."

They climbed. The hills above the valley were a chaos of frost-cracked stone and wind-scoured pine, hard going for a ten-year-old, and Oren's breath came in ragged white flags behind her. The horns swung west, paralleling them, patient as winter itself. Hounds didn't tire. Hunters didn't sleep. The law, Yara thought bitterly, had always been excellent at endurance and terrible at mercy.

By late afternoon they reached a saddle between two crags, and below them, filling the valley floor like spilled ink, Yara saw them: twenty riders in white-and-black, torches unlit in the daylight, hounds straining at leads, and at their head a banner she knew. The black hand, fingers spread.

There was no way through. The valley was the only pass north, and it belonged to them.

Oren tugged her sleeve. His face was pale, his lips blue-tinged with cold and fear, and the glow was starting again at his throat—that terrible telltale shine that would light them up in the gloaming like a festival lantern.

"Yara," he whispered. "It's happening again. I can't—I can't hold it, I'm sorry, I'm *sorry*—"

"Don't apologize." She crouched, gripping his shoulders, and looked into his frightened eyes, and made the decision she had spent three years refusing to make. "Oren. Listen. When it comes, don't fight it. Give it to me."

"What?"

"Just trust me. Like the bowl. Make the bowl, and I'll be the walls."

Below, a horn sounded—found. Torches bloomed along the column like a field of orange stars. Hounds gave tongue, a rising shriek of pursuit, and the riders began to move toward the slopes.

Yara rose. She pulled off her right glove, and for the first time in three years, deliberately, in open sight of gods and men, she let her gift come fully awake.

It rose through her like dawn. Heat flooded her veins, her scars, the hollow places where Vessia's silence had lived; frost flashed to steam in a two-foot radius around her; and she lifted her bare hand toward the descending hunt and spoke, aloud, in the old tongue of the covenant, words that had not been legally spoken in Greyhollow's borders since before she was born:

*"Vessia, mother of ember, witness me."*

The world held its breath.

And the cold that had lain over the land for three unbroken years—for the first time, in one small circle on one forgotten hillside—*broke*. Snowmelt burst from the rocks around her. A single thread of true flame, gold-edged, ancient, impossibly warm, wound up her arm and settled in her palm like a hawk coming home.

Oren stared at it, and at her, and forgot to be afraid.

"Whoa," he breathed.

"Yeah," Yara said grimly, watching the riders come. "Hold on to me, kid. And whatever happens—"

The lead rider crested the slope. Reined in. Raised a hand, and the whole hunt halted as one animal.

Through the wind and the steam of her own burning, Yara saw the man's face under the white hood—weathered, hard, and briefly, terribly, stricken with recognition as his eyes fell on the flame in her palm, and then on the old sigil glowing through her soaked sleeve at her wrist.

The captain's sword stayed sheathed. His voice, when it came, was rough as gravel.

"That mark," he said. "Where in the hells did you get that mark?"

Behind him, twenty hunters waited. Before her, a god's first breath in three years warmed her hand. And between them, a ten-year-old boy gripped her coat with both fists, his light and hers burning together against the dark.
