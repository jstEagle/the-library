## 4. The Hunter's Confession

The frost-road north of Greyhollow was a scar of white through black pines, and Dain Ferro had ridden it before—though never with this weight in his chest, never with orders that tasted like ash.

He rode alone. His squad had split at the treeline to work the flanks, six hunters fanning out into the snow-hushed woods like crows scattering from a gibbet. Dain kept the road itself. That was tradition: the captain took the straight path, the honest path, while his people did the crooked work in the dark. He had told himself for three years that this arrangement meant something—that keeping to the open road kept some part of him clean.

The snow squeaked beneath his mare's hooves. His breath plumed and froze in his beard. Somewhere ahead, maybe two miles, maybe three, a woodcutter's hut smoldered on the horizon of his memory—he'd burned it himself, two winters past, a man inside who could make plants grow out of season. The man had wept and called him *brother*, because they had both worn grey in the war, both bled at the Cinderline, and Dain had struck the flint anyway.

He thought about that fire more often than he admitted. He thought about most of them more often than he admitted.

The report had come down from Greyhollow at dawn: a charm-seller fled the city with a boy. A woman, mid-thirties, dark-haired, seen working small magics near the docks. And a child whose grief made fires—a ten-year-old named Oren, last of Ashmill village, which no longer existed except as a scorched place on the crown's maps where a village used to be.

*Ashmill.* Dain had read the name twice. He knew what had happened there. Everyone in the order knew, though none said it aloud: the village hadn't burned by accident. The boy's magic had flared when the hunters came for his mother, and Commander Vael had ordered the survivors counted as casualties of the mage's own making. *Let the monster's crime be the monster's.* Clean arithmetic. Dain had signed the ledger.

Now the boy was running, and Dain was riding to catch him, and somewhere behind them all stood the crown's true purpose, wrapped in parchment and sealed with wax the color of old blood.

The wind shifted. Dain raised a fist, and far off in the trees, a whistle answered—Renn, on the east flank. *Contact.*

He spurred the mare forward.

* * *

They were in a hollow where the road dipped between drifts taller than a man, and they had done well with it—he'd give them that. A snow-cave dug into the bank, brush dragged over the entrance, the boy's small footprints carefully swept with a pine bough. But smoke had betrayed them, a thin gray thread rising from the brush like a confession, and Renn had marked it before the wind erased it.

Dain dismounted at the mouth of the hollow and drew his blade—not the longsword of the hunt but the shorter arming sword he'd carried through the war, the one with the notched guard he'd never had repaired. Old habit. Old guilt.

"Come out," he said. "Slowly. I have you surrounded."

A lie. Renn was half a mile south chasing a false trail. The others were scattered ghosts in the forest. It was just Dain and the snow and the thin thread of smoke.

The brush moved. A woman stepped out with her hands empty and her eyes full of winter, and Dain felt the world tilt beneath him like a deck in a storm.

He knew her.

Not her face—the years had carved it leaner, harder, and grief does its own masonry—but her stance. The way she stood with her weight slightly back, hands loose, ready to move in any direction at once. The way her chin lifted a fraction when she looked at him, not defiance exactly, but assessment. He had seen that posture across battlefields, across campfires, across the smoking ruin of the Cinderline where the ember-priests had held the pass while the rest of the world ran.

"You're the charm-seller," he said. His voice came out wrong. Rough. "From Greyhollow."

"I'm nobody." Her voice was low and even. "Just a woman walking."

"The reports say you worked charms by the docks. Luck-knots. Warming-stones." He stepped closer, boots crunching. "The reports say you fled with the boy from Ashmill."

Behind her, deeper in the hollow, something small shifted in the snow. A child's breath, quick and frightened.

"I'm asking you once," Dain said, and hated how the words tasted, "to come quietly. There's a wagon on the north road. If you come without trouble, the boy won't be separated from you. I can promise that much."

It wasn't even a lie, which was the worst part. He *could* promise that much. What happened after the wagon reached the capital was above his promise, above his rank, above anything he could reach.

The woman tilted her head. And then, slowly, deliberately, she pulled the collar of her coat aside—not exposing her throat to the knife, but showing him what hung against it on a frayed leather cord.

A sigil. A flame within an open hand, wrought in copper gone green with age, the kind of thing a soldier might wear and a wise woman might pretend was only a trinket.

Dain stopped breathing.

He knew that sigil. He had knelt before that sigil. In the last spring of the war, when the Cinderline was breaking and the ember-god's light was failing over the pass, he had watched a covenant of battle-mages stand in the breach wearing that mark—and he had watched them die holding it, one by one, buying hours for an army that retreated and never looked back. An army that included a young sergeant named Dain Ferro, who had run when the order came, and lived, and been decorated for it.

"You were at the Cinderline," he said. The words fell out of him like stones. "You were one of hers. One of Vessia's."

The woman's eyes flickered—something moving behind them, deep and drowned. "That god is dead," she said. "Everyone says so."

"Everyone says so." He laughed, and it came out broken. "Yes. Everyone says so."

His sword arm dropped. The point of the blade sank into the snow and stayed there, and neither of them moved, and somewhere in the brush the boy made a sound like a held breath finally released.

* * *

Her name was Yara Ishikawa. She gave it grudgingly, standing with the boy pressed against her side—a thin, dark-eyed child with burns healed badly along one forearm, watching Dain with the flat stare of someone who had already seen the worst thing the world could do and expected to see it again.

Dain sat on a fallen log with his sword still in the snow between them, and he talked, because if he stopped talking he would have to act, and he did not know yet what his hands would choose.

"I was at the Cinderline," he said. "Third company, crown regulars. When the line broke, your people held the pass so we could fall back. Forty of you. I watched the ember-light go out over the ridge and I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought the gods had abandoned us. That's what the priests said afterward. Vessia spent herself saving mortals and guttered out, and the long winter came because her fire was gone. That's the doctrine. That's what I built my life on top of."

"And now?" Yara asked quietly.

"Now I burn children in villages and call it justice." He said it plainly, because there was no other way left to say it. "Three years of witch-hunts. Do you know what happens to the ones we take alive? You'd think hanging. You'd think pyres. No." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious. "The wagons go north to the capital. To the Sanctum under the palace. The ones with strong gift are kept. The crown's ritualists need mage-blood for the workings that hold the winter in place—the great wards, the cold-anchors. Every ward they raise needs fuel. Living fuel."

Oren made a small sound. Yara's arm tightened around him.

"The hunt isn't justice," Dain went on. "It's harvest. Commander Vael knows. Half the captains know. We tell ourselves the pyres are mercy compared to what the Sanctum does, and then we sleep at night because sleeping is easy when you've practiced." He picked up his sword, weighed it, and drove it point-down into the log beside him, where it stood quivering like a grave-marker. "I helped lose that war. Then I helped lose everything the war was supposed to protect. And now I've been sent to bring a child to the altar, and I rode here this morning knowing it, and my feet knew it, and I rode anyway."

"Why?" Yara asked. Not cruelly. That was somehow worse.

"Because the alternative was thinking." He bowed his head. "Because obedience is warm, and doubt is a draft you can't shut out."

The wind moved through the pines above them, sifting snow through the branches in slow white veils. For a long moment no one spoke. Then Oren, in a voice barely louder than the wind:

"You burned Ashmill."

Dain looked at the boy. There was no way to soften it, and the boy deserved better than softening anyway.

"Yes," Dain said. "I signed the ledger that called it your fault. It wasn't. It was ours. Vael's order, my signature, the whole rotten machine." He swallowed. "I can't unburn it. I can't give you back your mother. But I'm not taking you north. Whatever that costs me."

Yara studied him for a long time. He let her. He had been studied by better judges and found wanting.

"You recognized the sigil," she said at last. "Before I showed it to you. You hesitated at the treeline—I saw you from the brush. Why?"

"Because I've spent three years telling myself the ember-god died at the Cinderline and took the world's warmth with her, fair and square, nothing anyone could do." His laugh was hollow. "And then a charm-seller from the docks wears her mark, and I think: *what if nothing is fair and square? What if she didn't die? What if she was taken?*" He met Yara's eyes. "You know something. I saw it when you looked at me. You know why the winter came, and it isn't the doctrine."

Yara said nothing. But her hand closed around the sigil at her throat, and that was answer enough.

Dain rose. He pulled his sword from the log, considered it—the instrument of his trade, his identity, thirty years of his life—and then hurled it into the deep drift at the hollow's edge, where it vanished with a soft sound, like a word swallowed.

"I can't go back," he said. "Vael will have my head for losing you, or the Sanctum will have it for knowing too much. So it's exile either way. The only question is whether I spend it running or—" he gestured at the two of them, at the swept footprints and the buried smoke and the desperate, stubborn shape of their flight, "—or whether I spend it on something."

"You don't know where we're going," Yara said.

"No. But you do. And you'll need someone who knows the hunter's craft from the inside—which roads are watched, which passes have bells, how Vael thinks." He almost smiled; it didn't take. "Besides. Someone has to carry the supplies. I brought a packhorse."

Oren tugged Yara's sleeve and whispered something too low for Dain to hear. Yara looked down at the boy, then back at the disgraced captain standing weaponless in the snow, and whatever war she waged behind her eyes, it ended in a kind of exhausted surrender.

"There's a shrine in the mountains," she said quietly. "East, past the frozen pass. And Dain Ferro—if you follow us, you should know the truth you're exiling yourself for." Her hand rose, and the sigil at her throat began, faintly, impossibly, to glow—a coal's heartbeat of orange light against the endless white. "Vessia isn't dead. She's imprisoned. And I mean to free her."

The light died. The wind sighed. Dain stood very still, feeling three years of doctrine crack down the middle like river ice in thaw.

"Then the gods help us," he said hoarsely, "because the crown certainly won't."

Far to the south, faint but unmistakable, a hunting horn sounded—Renn, or one of the others, circling back toward the hollow. Dain turned toward the sound, and his face hardened into something older and colder than shame.

"They'll have found my horse," he said. "We have perhaps an hour. Tell me about these shards, Yara Ishikawa—and tell me fast."
