## 5. Shard of the First Flame

Ashfall had drowned a century ago, and still it refused to die.

They came down to it at dusk, when the light went thin and copper-colored and the water of the flooded valley turned to hammered bronze. The temple's spires rose out of the shallows like black fingers reaching for a sky that had forgotten them—seven towers, six broken, and one still whole, leaning as if it were listening. Somewhere beneath that water, Yara knew, lay the first ember-shard. She had known it since she was nineteen years old and swearing oaths in a chapel that no longer existed, because Vessia's priests had always told their battle-mages where the pieces of the First Flame slept. *So that you may find them again,* the old hierophant had said, *if ever the world grows cold enough to need them.*

She had thought it a metaphor then. A pretty story for young soldiers with too much fire in their blood.

Now the world was cold enough.

"Is it deep?" Oren asked. He stood at the waterline with his boots off and his trousers rolled to the knee, though he hadn't touched the water yet. The boy had learned caution on the frost-road—the hard way, with fever in his lungs and Yara bargaining with a river-god while he shivered half to death against her back. He tested everything now before he trusted it. Even water. Especially water.

"Deep enough," Yara said. "But we don't swim to it. There's a causeway." She pointed to where a line of pale stones broke the surface, running out toward the whole spire like the spine of some enormous drowned animal. "The temple builders wanted pilgrims to walk the last stretch themselves. Humility before the Flame."

Dain Ferro crouched at the edge of the shallows, turning a wet stone over in his scarred hands. Three weeks on the road had worn him down to sinew and silence; the fine captain's coat was gone, traded in a river town for oilskins and a boat they'd since abandoned, and without it he looked less like a witch-hunter and more like what he apparently had been all along—a soldier from the borderlands who'd made one bad covenant and spent ten years paying for it. "I don't like it," he said. "Water this still, around ruins this old. It's not empty."

"It isn't," Yara agreed.

He looked up at her. "You know what's in there."

"I know what *should* be in there." She pulled her cloak tighter. The evening air came off the flood-water cold enough to ache in the teeth. "The shard doesn't sit unguarded, Dain. Nothing of Vessia's does. The temple takes the dead who swore to her and—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, flatter. "It keeps them."

Oren's eyes went wide. "Ghosts?"

"Worse than ghosts." Ghosts only haunted you. What waited in Ashfall would *know* her.

* * *

They walked the causeway as the last light failed, three figures on a spine of stone above black water, and the silence of the place pressed against Yara's eardrums like a held breath. No birds. No insects. Not even wind. The flood had taken the town of Ashfall in a single night, the histories said—taken it so completely that the drowning felt less like disaster and more like decision, as if the valley itself had been asked to keep something safe and had answered by closing its mouth over the whole of it.

The whole spire rose ahead of them now, its doors standing open above the waterline twenty feet up, reached by a stair that climbed out of the flood like a wound that wouldn't close. Someone had lit lamps inside. That was the detail that stopped Yara's heart for a full three beats: warm yellow light in windows that should have been dark for a hundred years, flickering in a rhythm she recognized before she could stop herself from recognizing it.

Vessia's vigil-lamps. Lit left to right, seven flames, never snuffed until all seven burned together.

"You feel that?" Oren whispered. His hand had found hers without either of them deciding it. His palm was hot—too hot, the way it got when his gift stirred, magic rising in him like a tide answering some moon he couldn't see. "It's like... someone's waiting for us. Like the whole building is holding its breath."

"It is waiting," Yara said. "For me, I think."

The stair was slick with a century of spray, but her boots found the grooves worn by pilgrim feet long dead, and the climb was easier than it had any right to be—as if the temple were helping her up, or reeling her in. At the top, the doorway breathed out warmth and the smell of woodsmoke and tallow, and underneath both, faint as memory, the scent of the war-camp chapels of her youth: iron, incense, and scorched earth.

Yara stepped across the threshold into the drowned temple, dry as a bone.

Inside, the nave stretched away under a vaulted ceiling furred with pale mineral growth, and the vigil-lamps burned along both walls exactly as they had in every chapel of the covenant—and between the lamps stood soldiers.

A dozen of them. Rank upon rank, in the armor of the Ember Covenant, tabards bearing the seven-flame sigil, faces shadowed under helms that had not been polished in a hundred years but showed no rust. They did not move. They did not breathe. But the nearest one turned its head as Yara entered, slow as a door swinging, and beneath the helm's rim she caught the gleam of eyes like banked coals.

"No," Dain said behind her. His hand went to his knife out of pure reflex, and Yara heard him force himself to stop drawing it. "No, those are—those are men."

"They were." Her voice came out strange and far away. "They're what's left."

She walked forward, because there was nothing else to do, and the ranks parted for her the way they had parted a thousand times on parade grounds, and the wraith at the front removed its helm.

Brennan's face looked back at her.

Not aged a day. Twenty-six years old, crooked grin gone, jaw set in the hard line he wore before a fight—but Brennan, her shield-brother, who had taught her to drink plum brandy without coughing and to hold a shield-wall through a night of arrows and to laugh, sometimes, even at funerals. He had died at the pass at Kellmoor eleven years ago, holding the line for six hours so that refugees could reach the boats, and Yara had survived because he ordered her to carry the wounded back and she had obeyed.

"Brennan," she said. Her voice cracked on the second syllable.

"Battle-mage Ishikawa." The wraith's voice was his voice, but flattened, resonant, like speech heard through a wall of ice. "You are late."

* * *

The nave became the past around her as she walked, and she let it. That was the trap of Ashfall—or the mercy, she couldn't decide which. The lamps guttered and the walls seemed to recede, and suddenly she was not in a drowned temple but on the ridge at Kellmoor, snow coming down sideways, the shield-wall braced and singing with tension, and Brennan beside her saying the same thing he had said eleven years ago:

"Take the wounded down, Yara. That's an order."

And she had looked at him—at all of them, Corvin and Tammas and little Wren with his first beard coming in patchy—and she had seen that they already knew. All of them. They had done the arithmetic of the pass and arrived at the same sum, and none of them had said it aloud, because saying it aloud would have made it true, and unspoken things could still be outrun.

"I can stay," she had said. "My fire can hold the left flank another hour—"

"Your fire is the reason any of them get out alive," Brennan said. "You burn bright enough to cover the retreat, you walk down that hill, and you live. Those are your orders, sister. Obey them."

She had obeyed. She had always obeyed. And from the ridge above the boats, wrapped in smoke and the screams of horses, she had watched the line break at dusk and seen the seven-flame banner go down in the snow, and she had carried that image out of the war like a splinter carried to the heart.

Now the wraith wearing Brennan's face stood in the nave of a drowned church and asked her, in a voice like ice over deep water:

"Why did you survive?"

The question hit harder than any blade. Behind her, she heard Dain shift, and Oren make a small sound—not fear, she realized. Recognition. The boy knew grief when he heard it coming.

"Because you told me to," Yara said. It came out fast, defensive, the answer she'd been carrying for eleven years like a stone in her chest. "Because it was an order, and I was sworn, and—"

"That is why you obeyed." The wraith did not move, but the coal-light of its eyes brightened. "It is not why you lived. Answer honestly, battle-mage. It is the last duty I will ask of you."

The silence of the temple closed around her. The vigil-lamps burned. Somewhere below, the flood-water lapped against stone with the patience of centuries.

Yara opened her mouth to give the true answer—the one she'd given commanders, inquisitors, her own reflection—and found there was nothing left of it worth saying. So instead, for the first time in eleven years, she told the truth.

"Because I was afraid." The words came out of her like shrapnel. "Because in that moment, looking down that hill, I wanted to live more than I wanted to be worthy of you. Because I told myself it was obedience, that carrying the wounded was the braver work, and maybe some of that was even true—but underneath it, Brennan, underneath all of it, I didn't want to die on that ridge. You did the dying and I did the surviving and I have spent eleven years dressing it up as duty because the truth was unbearable." Her voice shook. She let it. "I survived because I chose to. And I have hated myself for the choosing every day since, and I hid in Greyhollow and buried my gift and called it peace, and it was never peace. It was just the wound, kept clean."

The wraith regarded her for a long moment. Then, slowly, the hard line of its jaw softened, and what moved across the dead man's face was not accusation.

It was relief.

"Yes," it said. "That is the answer. Not the order. Not the oath. The truth." It stepped closer, close enough that she could see the snow of Kellmoor still melting in the creases of its ghost-flesh. "We did not die so that you would spend your life kneeling at our grave, Yara. We died so that someone would carry the flame forward. You hid it in charms and luck-knots and small kindnesses for frightened people—and hear me, that was not nothing—but the ember was never meant to be hidden. It was meant to wake."

"The shard," she whispered.

"Below the altar. Where it has always slept." The wraith raised one translucent hand, and the ranks behind it sank to one knee, twelve ghost-soldiers bowing their heads in the nave of the drowned church. "Take it. And take our forgiveness with it—it has been waiting here longer than the shard has."

Yara's legs nearly gave way walking past them. She touched Brennan's shoulder as she passed, and the touch felt like sun-warmed stone, like a hand clasped across a decade of silence, and none of the wraiths spoke, and none of them needed to.

The altar was submerged to the waist in clear black water, and the shard lay in its recess like a sleeping coal: a fragment of something vast, veined with light that pulsed slow and deep, slower than a heartbeat, patient as a god. When Yara lifted it, warmth flooded up her arm and into the hollow places the winter had carved in her, and for one impossible moment she smelled plum brandy and woodsmoke and heard, very faintly, laughter.

Then the lamps along the walls began to go out—one, two, three, left to right—and the wraiths with them, dissolving into embers that drifted upward and vanished, and Brennan was last, and as he went he smiled the crooked grin she remembered, and said:

"Walk her home, sister."

* * *

They camped that night in the temple doorway, above the waterline, because none of them could bear to leave immediately. Oren fell asleep against Dain's shoulder within minutes, exhausted, his fingers curled loosely around a stick he'd been using to draw seven-flame sigils in the dust.

Dain watched the boy breathe for a while. Then he said, quietly, "What did it cost you? Back there. Truth like that—it always costs."

Yara turned the shard over in her hands. In the dark it glowed faintly, like a coal remembering fire. "Nothing," she said. "That's the strange part. Eleven years I thought the truth would kill me, and instead it just..." She searched for the word. "Lightened."

Dain nodded slowly. Then his gaze went past her, east, toward the mountains where the pass climbed toward the frozen high country—and his whole body went rigid.

"Yara." His voice had dropped to the flat tone he used for reports. "Look at the peaks."

She followed his eyes. Along the eastern horizon, above the pass they would have to cross, signal fires were kindling one after another—red points blooming in the dark like a chain of wounds. Too many. Too deliberate. Crown-fire, the kind the witch-hunters used to call their companies to hunt.

"They know where we're going," Dain said. "Someone talked. And they're not sending patrols anymore." He looked down at the shard glowing in her hands, then back at the burning skyline, and his jaw set the way Brennan's had once set, before a fight. "They're sending the whole order."
