## 6. What the Winter Remembers

Oren had never seen a sky so wide.

In Millbrook, the mountains had been walls on every side, and the sky was the strip of blue between them—close enough that you could almost touch it if you climbed the bell tower and stretched. But here, above the frozen pass, the sky went up forever, gray-white and endless, and the wind came down out of it like something falling.

He pulled his scarf higher over his mouth and trudged in Yara's footsteps because her boots broke the crust of snow and his legs were shorter. Dain walked behind him, which Oren had decided was good. Behind meant watching over. Ahead meant leading into trouble, and Yara did enough of that for all three of them.

"How much farther?" he asked, and the words came out small, eaten by the wind.

"Past the saddle," Yara said without turning. "There's a shrine at the far side. Old road-shrine. We'll warm there."

She'd said *we'll warm there* twice already. Oren had started counting the things she said twice. It was a game, kind of. A game where losing meant something bad.

The pass had been white for two days. Not soft snow like stories—hard snow, blue-shadowed, squeaking underfoot like packed tallow. The wind didn't howl so much as it *sang*, one long thin note with no end, and sometimes Oren thought he heard words inside it. His ma used to say the winter talked if you listened long enough. He hadn't believed her then. He believed everything now.

That was what the burning had done to him. Taken away not-believing.

"Oren." Yara's hand closed on his shoulder, and he realized he'd stopped walking, staring at nothing, at the singing air. "Stay with me. Eyes open. If you feel sleepy, you tell me. Sleepy is the enemy."

"I know." Everyone knew. Cold-touched, the old drovers called it, when the frost got inside you instead of just on you, and it made you warm-feeling and dreamy and then it made you dead. "I'm not sleepy."

"You were somewhere else."

"I was listening to the wind."

Yara crouched to look at him. Her eyes were dark and sharp, ember-colored when the light hit them right, though Oren figured that was just how her face was. She looked at him too long, the way grown-ups did when they were deciding something and didn't want you to know.

"The wind isn't saying anything," she said finally. "It's just wind."

But she said it the way people say things they want to be true, and Oren had gotten very good at hearing that tone since the fire.

* * *

It happened at the saddle, where the pass narrowed between shoulders of black stone and the wind funneled through screaming like a kettle.

One moment Oren was walking. The next he was sitting in the snow and he couldn't remember sitting down, and the cold wasn't cold anymore—it was *soft*, like his blanket at home, like the corner by the hearth where the cat slept. Warmth was spreading through him from somewhere deep, blooming outward, and it felt wonderful, and some small faraway part of him—the part that still had a ma and a burned village attached to it—was shrieking.

*Sleepy is the enemy.*

"I'm—" he tried to say. His lips wouldn't work right.

Then hands were on him, shaking him, and Yara's face filled the world, furious and frightened, and she slapped him across the cheek, hard, once, twice.

"Oren! *Look at me.*"

"It's warm," he said, and heard himself giggle, and hated the giggle, hated how it sounded coming out of his mouth in this place.

"No it isn't." She hauled him upright, dragged his arm over her shoulders. She was smaller than Dain but she lifted Oren like he weighed nothing, and there was a flicker of light around her fingers where they gripped—a flicker she smothered fast, glancing down the pass both ways before letting it die. "Ferro! Get ahead, find the shrine, break the door if you have to!"

Dain ran. Oren watched him shrink against the whiteness and thought, distantly, that it must be strange for a witch-hunter to run toward a shrine full of old magic instead of burning it down.

The warmth kept blooming. It felt like being loved. That was the worst part, Oren thought, as Yara half-carried, half-dragged him along the wall of stone. The cold-touched sickness didn't feel like dying. It felt like going home.

"Talk to me," Yara said. "Tell me about Millbrook. Tell me anything."

"There was a mill," Oren said, and laughed again, stupidly.

"Gods help me. *Talk.*"

So he talked, or tried to—about the cat, whose name he suddenly couldn't find; about his ma's bread, which he could smell suddenly, impossibly, right there in the freezing air, fresh and brown-crusted—and Yara's grip tightened each time his sentences drifted, her voice pulling him back like a rope. *Stay. Stay. Stay.*

He didn't see the shrine when they reached it. He saw it later, in pieces: low walls of gray stone furred with frost-ferns, a roofless cell, an ancient carved doorway worn nearly smooth. Dain had a fire going in the lee of the wall—actual fire, ordinary fire, built from scrub-pine and a striker, no magic in it at all—and he fed it like a starving man feeds a child, handful after handful.

They put Oren against the warm stones and wrapped him in both blankets and Yara held her palms over the fire and then, when Dain turned away, over Oren's chest, and light came off her skin like heat-shimmer, gold-red, and sank into him. It hurt, a little. Like blood coming back into frozen feet. But it was *hers*, that pain, alive-pain, and it pushed the soft warm death back from the doors of him.

"Better?" she asked.

Oren nodded. Then, because he was tired and sick and ten years old and had lost everything: "Is Vessia going to save us?"

Yara went very still.

The fire cracked. Somewhere above, the wind sang its one note. Dain had stopped moving by the woodpile, listening without looking like he was listening.

"She's asleep," Yara said at last. "Far away. That's why we're doing all this. So she can wake up."

"But if she's a god, why does she need us? Gods don't need anything. That's what makes them gods."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind her face—old grief, maybe, or old fear—but what she said was: "Eat your ration."

* * *

The river came out of the mountain below the shrine.

Oren found it the next morning while the other two slept in shifts, following a sound like whispering down a shelf of ice to where black water slid up through a crack in the glacier—water that did not freeze, though everything around it wore armor of ice a foot thick. It pooled in a basin of blue glass stone and slid away again beneath the ice, and the sound it made was not quite the sound of water.

It said his name.

Not *said*. Nothing so simple. But the whispering bent itself into the shape of *oren oren oren* the way wind bends smoke, and Oren should have run back up the slope and shaken Yara awake, and he knew that, and his feet carried him closer anyway. Because the divine world had been real ever since the fire, real and vast and paying attention to *him*, and he was so tired of being small.

"Hello?" His voice fell flat in the white silence.

The pool's surface shivered. Beneath it—not reflected, *present*—something opened one eye the size of a shield-boss, silver-gray, ringed in dark slate like storm-cloud seen from above. It was old. That was the thing Oren understood first and would understand forever after: it was so old that the ice around it seemed young, and the mountain seemed young, and the idea of Millbrook seemed like something that had happened that morning.

**Little ash-child,** the river said, and the words came up through the stone through his boots through his bones. **You carry her mark.**

Oren's hand flew to his chest, where beneath his shirt the brand Yara had given him—a tiny sigil of protection, inked in ember-touched ink—sat warm against his skin. "Are you—" He swallowed. "Are you one of hers?"

A sound like laughter moved through the water, and ice flowers bloomed and shattered along the basin's rim.

**No. I am none of hers. I am the Grey Sister. I am the water under the winter. And I will tell you a secret, little ash-child, because you asked a question, and questions are expensive, and you have no idea yet what you are buying.**

Oren's mouth was dry. "What question?"

**You were wondering why a god needs mortals.** The eye regarded him without blinking, without mercy. **Come closer.**

He shouldn't have. He did.

**Your ember-goddess was not always imprisoned. Once she walked your world like weather. Once, when your ancestors grew proud and their wars scorched three kingdoms to glass, she answered their prayers and gave them fire enough to win—and fire enough to burn the harvests, the forests, the rivers, the children. Fire does not ask permission. It is her nature and her glory and her horror.** The water rose an inch in the basin, quivering. **So we—the Quiet Court, the elder realms' thrones, my cold brothers and patient sisters—we took her while she slept off her last conflagration, and we sealed her beneath the peak, and we laid our winter over the world like a bandage over a wound that would not stop bleeding flame. The mortals call it punishment. It is not punishment. It is *quarantine*.**

"You're saying she's dangerous," Oren whispered. "Vessia's dangerous."

**We are saying she is a wildfire wearing a crown,** the Grey Sister said, **and that the last mortal who trusted her with his whole heart died holding a line at Ashfall while she slept. Ask your mistress about Ashfall sometime, ash-child. Watch her face when you ask.**

Something cold went through Oren that had nothing to do with the ice.

**Now. The bargain.** The eye drifted nearer the surface, vast and silver. **The cold-touch has its hooks in you still; I can taste it in your blood, sweet and slow. I can draw it out. I ask a price.**

"What price?" Yara's voice, behind him—sharp, awake, furious at herself. Oren spun. She stood at the top of the ice shelf with her hand raised and her covenant-sigil flaring faint gold in her palm, and Dain was behind her with his blade drawn, though drawn against what, Oren couldn't imagine.

**Ah. The widow of the covenant.** The river's voice curled with something like amusement. **Nothing you need fear, mage. From the boy I take only this: he will carry my message to your goddess when she wakes. One sentence, spoken in my voice, at the hour of her rekindling. He will not know the sentence until the moment comes. He will speak it whether he wishes to or not. That is all.**

"Don't," Yara said, and her voice cracked on it, just barely. "Whatever it is—whatever you want said to her—*don't.*"

**Too late. He asked a question.** The eye began to sink, satisfied, unhurried. **Questions are expensive, little ones. You have paid the first installment.**

"Wait!" Oren lunged forward. "Wait—is she really dangerous? Should we wake her? Tell me if we should—"

The pool went still. The eye was gone. Only the black water whispered now, wordless, indifferent, sliding away under the ice toward places no map had ever reached.

For a long moment nobody moved. Steam rose where Yara's breath met the cold.

"Oren," she said quietly, when she finally crouched beside him, and her hand on his shoulder trembled in a way he'd never felt it do. "What did it say to you?"

And Oren, who could still feel the message coiled in his chest like a swallowed coal, waiting—Oren, who understood suddenly that he was no longer only a boy carrying a goddess's mark but a boy carrying a god's *letter*—looked up at her with tears freezing on his lashes and told her the truth:

"I think we woke something up when we asked. And I don't think it wanted to be woken."

Behind them, far up the pass, a horn sounded—one long note, thin and hard, like the wind had learned to sing through metal.

Dain was on his feet instantly, shading his eyes, and the color drained out of his face.

"That's a hunter's call," he said. "Two companies, at least. They've found our trail."
