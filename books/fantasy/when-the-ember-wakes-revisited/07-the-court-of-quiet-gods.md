## 7. The Court of Quiet Gods

They took us at the ford below Vell's Crossing, where the river ran black under ice and the reeds stood like spears broken in retreat. I heard the hounds before I saw the lanterns—six of them, maybe eight, moving through the fog with the discipline I had drilled into them myself in another life. I had time to wake Yara. I had time to put Oren behind me and draw steel. What I did not have time for was the crossbow bolt that took me through the shoulder and put me on my knees in the freezing shallows, watching my own blood bloom and thin in the current like smoke.

"Captain Ferro." The voice belonged to Serah Vane, and it was worse than hatred, because it was respectful. She had been my lieutenant once. She knelt beside me in the water, checked the wound with two fingers, professional as a surgeon. "The Order will want you alive. The others too."

"The Order," I said, "doesn't exist anymore."

She didn't answer that. She just signaled, and the hunters closed around us like a fist.

* * *

They held us three days in the undercroft of the Chapterhouse at Vell's Crossing, a fortress I knew every stone of, built into the bones of an older ruin whose gods had been quiet so long even the priests had forgotten their names. They kept Oren separate from us. That was the part that gnawed. Yara sat across the cell from me with her back to the wall and her eyes closed, and I could feel her working at something—not magic, not exactly, but the same stubborn pressure of will she brought to everything, like a woman pushing against a door that would not open.

"He's ten," I said.

"I know how old he is."

"They'll question him alone."

Her eyes opened. In the dark of that cell they were the color of banked coals, and for a moment I saw what the witch-hunters must see when they looked at her—the thing that made them burn people. Not evil. Certainty. "Then we'd best make sure there isn't much left to question," she said, "by the time they come for us."

There wasn't going to be a rescue. There was no one to send one. We were three people in a hole in the ground, and above us the Order of the Quiet Flame was assembling its tribunal, and I was going to be tried by men I had trained, judged by laws I had enforced, condemned—if it came to that—in a hall where I had sworn oaths I no longer believed in.

I should have felt despair. What I felt instead, lying on the cold flagstones listening to Yara breathe, was something closer to relief. For three years I had carried the war like a stone in my chest, and now, at last, someone was going to ask me about it out loud.

* * *

They brought us up on the fourth morning. The great hall of the Chapterhouse had not changed: the vaulted ceiling ribbed with black timber, the banners of the nine northern realms hanging dead-still in the windless air, the long table of the tribunal raised on a dais beneath the carved sigil of the Quiet Flame—a candle with no fire in it, which had always struck me, even as a young man, as the most honest emblem any order ever chose for itself.

High Inquisitor Malrec presided. He was older than I remembered, or perhaps I had simply never looked at him properly before. His face was smooth and gray as riverstone, his voice soft, and he spoke to the hall the way a man speaks to a room he already owns.

"Dain Ferro. Captain, Third Vigil, retired under censure. You are charged with desertion, consorting with proscribed mages, and treason against the crown's writ of purification."

"And you're charged with nothing," I said.

Malrec smiled faintly. "We are the law here, Captain. The law cannot charge itself."

Behind me, Yara laughed. It was not a kind sound. It was the laugh of a woman who has watched a city burn from inside its walls, and it went down the length of the hall like a blade drawn slowly from a sheath. Heads turned. Hunters who had spent their lives never looking directly at a accused mage turned and looked at her.

"You may speak when spoken to," Malrec said.

"No," Yara said. "I don't think I will. You've been speaking for everyone for sixty years, High Inquisitor. Sixty years of winter, and not one of you has ever asked why."

"Bind her tongue if she—"

"Ask me," she said, and her voice changed then, dropped into something older and colder, the voice I had heard once on the frost-road when she spoke to the river-god and the river-god answered. "Ask me why the winter came. Ask me what your crown does with the mage-blood it harvests from your burning-cages. Ask me who your gods are afraid of, Malrec, because it isn't mortals. It has never been mortals."

The hall went very quiet. I have stood in battle-silences, in the pause after a volley, in the held breath before a charge, and none of them were like this. This was the silence of a hundred armed men and women realizing, all at once, that they had never actually asked a question in their lives.

Malrec leaned forward. And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I'm owed: he did not deny it. He did not rage. He looked at Yara Ishikawa with the mild, patient expression of a man about to correct a child's arithmetic, and he said, "The winter is punishment. The gods decreed it after the Sundering War. Humanity burned the world, and humanity was given cold in exchange. This is doctrine. This is mercy."

"It's a leash," Yara said. "Vessia wanted to end it. She wanted to give fire back to the hearths and spring back to the fields. So her brother-gods chained her under Halveth's mountain and let you build an order to hunt down anyone who might remember her name. You're not servants of justice, High Inquisitor. You're kennel-masters. And every mage you burn is a dog put down for barking at the truth."

"Blasphemy," someone shouted from the ranks.

"Is it?" Yara spread her hands, and I saw the covenant-sigil flare on her palm, ember-red, unmistakable, the mark that had not been seen openly in this hall since the war. "Then strike me down. But first tell these good hunters why the crown pays triple bounty for mages taken *alive*. Tell them where the wagons go. Tell them what the rituals under the palace need all that blood *for*."

And that—that was the crack that split the stone. Because every hunter in that hall had heard the rumors. Every one of them had escorted a prisoner-wagon and been ordered to look away at the crossroads. I watched Serah Vane's face do something complicated and terrible. I watched a young hunter named Pell, barely twenty, take one step backward, then another, until he was standing beside the door instead of guarding it.

Malrec saw it too. "Enough," he said softly. "Take them down."

What happened next happened fast, and I will set it down plainly because history deserves plainness. Yara moved first. She drove her sigil-hand against the floor and spoke a word in the old covenant-tongue, and every unlit candle in the Chapterhouse—hundreds of them, the Quiet Flame's own devotional candles, stacked in iron racks along the walls—ignited at once. Not gently. Like a breath blown across a field of dry grass. Fire leapt the length of the hall in a golden wave, and in that wave everything became noise and shadow and screaming.

Pell opened the door. I will remember that too. A boy I had once taught to hold a shield opened the door for the witch he'd been sent to catch, and four more went with him—Serah among them, though she did not meet my eyes, and I understood that she might never forgive herself for it, and that she had done it anyway.

We fought our way down the east stair. Oren came apart from his handlers like water finding a crack—he was crying, and where his tears fell the stone smoked—and when a hunter raised a blade against Yara's back, the boy screamed, and the man's sword ran red-hot in his grip and he dropped it howling. I killed two men on that stair. I want that recorded without ornament: I killed Brannoc Hale, who had served under me six years, and Tam Wicke, who had named his daughter after mine. My shoulder was torn open and my hands shook and I killed them anyway, because the alternative was watching Yara die, and I found, in that moment, that I had run out of shame to stop me. Perhaps that is what defection truly is. Not a decision. Just the place where shame runs out.

Yara killed no one. I want that recorded too. She fought with flat of blade and open hand and walls of flame that pushed but did not catch, and when we burst out through the postern gate into the falling snow, she was the only one of us without blood on her, and she looked like it was the heaviest thing she carried.

* * *

We ran north through the pine-dark with the Chapterhouse burning orange against the grey sky behind us, and the horns sounding pursuit, and Oren's small hand locked in mine hard enough to hurt. At the tree line Yara stopped. Turned. Looked back at the hall of the Quiet Gods with its roof beginning to glow.

"Some of them stayed," she said. "Some of them heard it."

"Most of them didn't," I said.

"No." Her breath steamed. "But most was never the point. One was the point. One is always the point." She looked at me then, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen in three years of knowing her—something almost like hope, worn thin as old coin. "The second shard is in the mountains. The shrine at Karvek's Tooth. Do you know what it asks as payment?"

"I know the stories."

"Then say it anyway. Say it out loud, so I can't pretend later that I didn't know."

I told her. She listened the way soldiers listen to casualty reports—still, level, taking each name. Then she nodded once, adjusted the strap of her pack, and started up the slope toward the peaks, where the winter lay deepest and the god Halveth kept his long cold watch.

Oren tugged my sleeve. "Dain. Is she going to be all right?"

I watched her climb, small and straight-backed against all that white, and I thought about fires lit in halls full of sleeping men, and mothers' faces, and the price of truth.

"No," I said. "But she's going to do it anyway. Come on, lad. Keep up."
