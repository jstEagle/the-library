## 8. Two Shards, One Price

The mountain had a name once, before the winter ate it. The old maps in Dain's saddlebag called it Vessin's Crown, and the shrine at its summit had been a place of pilgrimage when Yara was young enough to believe pilgrimages meant something. Now the road up was a white scar through black pine, and the wind came down it like something with teeth.

They climbed for two days.

Oren went first on the second morning, small boots punching holes in the crust, because he had decided—without consulting anyone—that leading made him brave instead of afraid. Dain brought up the rear with his sword slung across his back like an apology, and between them Yara walked with the first ember-shard wrapped in wool against her sternum, where it beat against her heart in a rhythm that was not quite her own.

It had been beating faster since the pass. Since the river-god's voice had come up out of the ice and told her what she already suspected: that the shards were not objects but *organs*, cut from a living god, and that each one would know her before she knew it.

"Tell me about the shrine again," Oren said, breath streaming behind him like smoke from a small chimney.

"There's nothing to tell. It's a room in the rock. There's a brazier that never went out, even after everything." She kept her eyes on the path. "And there's a doorkeeper."

"What kind of doorkeeper?"

"The kind you don't lie to."

Oren considered this with the terrible seriousness of ten-year-olds. "I'm good at not lying. I just don't say things."

Yara's chest tightened. *Yes,* she thought. *That's one way.* She had spent three years perfecting it.

* * *

They reached the summit at dusk, when the sky over the peaks had gone the color of a bruise healing. The shrine was exactly as memory had drawn it: a hollow of granite, half-swallowed by snow, its entrance framed by pillars carved with flames that some pious hand had chiseled flat during the purges—smoothing fire into blankness, as if stone could be made to forget what it remembered.

Inside, the air was warm.

Not comfortably warm—the warmth of a fever, of a forge-room, of skin held too close to a candle. The brazier still burned at the chamber's center, though no fuel fed it, and its light was wrong: gold shot through with veins of red, pulsing slowly, patiently, like something asleep that had not stopped dreaming.

Above the brazier, suspended in the heat-haze, hung the second shard.

It was smaller than the first—a splinter of light the length of a finger, turning end over end without ever falling—and where the first shard had hummed against Yara's ribs like a second heartbeat, this one was silent. It simply watched. She felt its attention settle on her the way sunlight settles: total, weightless, inescapable.

"It's looking at me," Oren whispered.

"It's looking at all of us." Dain had stopped at the threshold, as if the doorway were a line he hadn't earned the right to cross. His hand hovered near his sword out of habit, then dropped. "What do we do?"

"We pay."

* * *

The doorkeeper did not appear so much as condense. Out of the brazier's shimmer rose a figure of ash and ember-light, tall as two men, its face a smooth oval where features should have been—except for the eyes, which were old, and kind, and unbearably sad.

**WHO COMES TO THE SHRINE OF THE SECOND FLAME?**

The voice was not sound. It arrived inside the skull, the way memories do.

Yara stepped forward and pulled the covenant-sigil from beneath her shirt. Three years of hiding it had worn the metal thin; the flame-and-oath design was nearly smooth now, a ghost of itself. "Yara Ishikawa," she said. "Sworn of Vessia, last of the Emberline covenant. I've come for the shard."

**THE SHARD IS NOT TAKEN,** the doorkeeper said. **IT IS BOUGHT.**

"I know. The river-god told me. Tell me the price."

The figure tilted its featureless head, and Yara had the sudden, vertiginous sense of being read—not her words, not her face, but the whole archive of her, every page turned by fingers of light.

**THE FIRST SHARD COST YOU GRIEF,** it said. **YOU PAID IT AT ASHFALL WHEN YOU ANSWERED THE DEAD HONESTLY. THE SECOND COSTS JOY.**

The warmth in the chamber seemed to lean closer.

**BRING ME YOUR HAPPIEST MEMORY. ALL OF IT. ROOT AND BRANCH. AND IT WILL BE MINE, AND YOU WILL NOT MOURN IT, BECAUSE YOU WILL NOT KNOW IT WAS EVER YOURS.**

Silence. Even the wind outside seemed to hold itself against the stone.

"That's it?" Dain said from the doorway, and his voice cracked in the middle. "One memory? That's—" He stopped, because he understood, and understanding made him go gray.

Yara did not need to search herself for the answer. It rose immediately, unbidden, the way your tongue finds a broken tooth: a kitchen in Greyhollow's foreign quarter, thirty years gone. Steam on the window. Her mother humming off-key, some song from across the sea that Yara could never find again in any market or temple. Flour on dark hands. And her mother turning, laughing at whatever small solemn thing five-year-old Yara had said, and—

There it was. The face. The exact face, which Yara had guarded through three winters of silence and two years of war and one long night holding the line at Ashfall while her sworn brothers burned around her. On the worst nights she took it out and looked at it, the way other people prayed.

She had thought the price would be pain. Pain she could budget for. She had not imagined it would be *this*—not agony, but subtraction. A room in the house of herself, emptied, and never knowing there had been a room.

"You can refuse," Dain said quietly. "We find another way. There's always another way."

"No," Yara said. "There isn't. That's the lesson of the last eight months." She looked at Oren, who was watching her with those too-bright eyes that flared when he cried, and she smiled at him, and hated how easily the smile came, how much practice she'd had. "Wait outside with Dain."

"I want to stay."

"Oren." Gentle. Firm. "This part is mine."

He held her gaze for a long moment—longer than a child should be able to—and then nodded once and let Dain draw him back toward the entrance. At the threshold he turned. "You'll still remember me?"

The question went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. Because she didn't know. She didn't know what else the doorkeeper might take, whether joy came bundled with everything adjacent to it, whether Oren's face lived in the same neighborhood as her mother's laugh.

"Yes," she said, and made it true by force of will alone.

* * *

When they were gone, she knelt before the brazier.

**DO YOU UNDERSTAND WHAT YOU SURRENDER?** the doorkeeper asked. Not cruelly. Almost gently, the way a priest asks before a rite that cannot be undone.

"I understand that I won't miss it," Yara said. "That's the cruelest part. You're not taking my grief. You're taking the *cure* for grief. Every hard night I've survived since Ashfall, I survived because I could open that door in my mind and stand in a warm kitchen for a moment. You're asking me to burn the door and forget there was ever a house."

**YES.**

"And if I say no?"

**THEN THE WINTER CONTINUES, AND THE CHILD IN THE DOORWAY BURNS BRIGHT UNTIL THEY CATCH HIM, AND YOUR GOD SLEEPS UNTIL THE WORLD FORGETS HER NAME ENTIRELY.**

Yara closed her eyes.

Here is what no one tells you about sacrifice: in the stories, it is a single moment, a blade-flash, a leap. In truth it is a negotiation conducted in the space of three heartbeats, and in those heartbeats you weigh everything you are against everything you might become, and there is no scale fine enough, and you choose anyway.

Her mother's face. Steam on glass. Off-key humming. Flour, and laughter, and the smell of cardamom she had never found again in any market in Greyhollow.

*I'll carry it as far as I can,* she told the memory silently. *I'm sorry I can't carry it home.*

"I pay," she said aloud.

She reached into her own mind the way you reach into a fire to pull out a child—knowing you will be burned, doing it anyway—and she found the door, and she opened it one last time, and she stood in the warm kitchen for the space of a single breath, looking at the face she loved most in all the world.

Then she let go.

* * *

The shard fell into her hands, light as a coal, warm as a sleeping bird. The doorkeeper bowed—a slow, grave motion, ash drifting from its shoulders like black snow—and dissolved back into the brazier's pulse.

Yara stood.

She felt... fine. That was the horror of it. She felt precisely as she had felt before kneeling, complete, unhollowed, a woman with a task and two companions and a god to wake. She turned the word *mother* over in her mind and found it smooth and meaningless, a coin worn blank, belonging to a country she'd never visited.

Something should hurt. Something in her insisted that a wound had been taken, that a price this steep must ache. But the doorkeeper had spoken true: you cannot mourn what you cannot remember losing. The grief had been amputated along with the joy, cleanly, painlessly, and what remained was a scar with no history.

*This,* she thought, climbing to her feet, tucking the second shard beside the first, feeling them beat together now, a two-chambered heart against her own, *this is what reforging the covenant means. Not glory. Arithmetic. A god's life weighed against a mortal's wholeness, paid in installments.*

And the third payment waited at the prison-peak, in the hands of Halveth himself, and she already knew it would not be a single memory. It would be all of them. Everything. Herself, poured out like oil onto an altar, so that something greater could burn.

She walked toward the entrance, and the light of the brazier followed her to the door and stopped there, as if even fire knew better than to follow a woman who had learned to walk away from warmth.

Outside, the night had come down blue and vast, stars sharp as shattered glass. Oren sat on a stone with Dain's cloak around his shoulders, and when he saw her he scrambled up, searching her face with that fierce, frightened attention.

"Did it work? Are you okay? What did it take?"

And here was the moment Yara understood completely, in her bones, in the hollow new room inside her: she could have lied. It would have been easy. *Nothing important. An afternoon. A dream.*

But she had answered the dead honestly at Ashfall, and honesty was a discipline like any other, and she was so tired of being invisible even to the people beside her.

"It took my mother," she said. "My memory of her. Her face, her voice, all of it. I know I had a mother. I know I loved her. I can't remember why." She heard her own voice stay level and marveled at it. "So when we finish this—if we finish this—I may not remember you either. Either of you. I need you to know that now, while I can still mean it."

Oren's eyes filled, and his magic stirred—the snow around his boots beginning to steam, frost flowers blooming and melting on the stone—but he didn't cry. He crossed the space between them and pressed his forehead against her sternum, right against the shards, right against the borrowed heartbeat.

"Then I'll remember for both of us," he said. "That's what family does."

Yara put her hand on his head, over the wild dark hair, and found that she could not picture her mother's face, and could not grieve it, and could feel, distantly, like pressure behind a wall, that somewhere inside her something was screaming.

Dain stood apart, watching the northern sky, where beyond the jagged line of the peaks a faint red glow stained the clouds—the prison-peak, days away, burning eternally cold.

"We should move tonight," he said. "Storm's coming down off the ice fields. If it reaches us before we reach the pass..." He didn't finish. He didn't have to.

Yara looked north at that impossible glow—at the god who slept there, at the price waiting in a winter-god's frozen hands—and felt the two shards pulse against her heart like a countdown.

"Then we move," she said.
