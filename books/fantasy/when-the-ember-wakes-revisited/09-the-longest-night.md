## 9. The Longest Night

### Yara

The mountain did not end. It simply stopped pretending to be stone.

Yara felt it in her teeth as they climbed the last switchback of the prison-peak—a vibration like a held breath, like a choir that had been singing one note for three hundred years and could not stop. Above them the sky was torn open by aurora, green and violet light rippling over black rock, and at the summit stood something that was neither temple nor fortress: a ring of ice so vast it might have been carved from a single frozen sea, and within it, sleeping, a shape of ember and ash.

Vessia.

Even dimmed, even buried under centuries of imposed winter, her presence reached down the mountainside like heat from a distant forge. Yara's scars—every sigil branded into her skin during her swearing—ached in answer. *I'm coming*, she thought. *Hold on.*

Between them and the ring knelt Halveth.

He had not been there a moment before; gods were like that now, she understood. He rose out of the snowfall itself, tall as a watchtower, robed in drifting white, his face an oval of smooth ice with two dark hollows where eyes should have been. In his cupped hands, held out before him like a priest presenting an offering—or a jailer displaying his keys—glowed the third ember-shard.

It was small. That was the obscenity of it. A splinter of fire no larger than a knife-blade, and it had taken three realms of jealous gods to bury its mistress beneath a mountain.

"You are late," Halveth said. His voice came as wind through a crevasse, patient, enormous. "The others said you would never come. I said mortals always come. It is the one thing you cannot help."

"We've come for what's yours to give back," Yara said. Her breath plumed white. Oren pressed against her side, small and shaking, and behind them, far below on the frost-road, she could hear the horns—the crown's army coming up the pass, hunting them through the dark.

"Give back." The god tilted his head, and somewhere deep in the glacier something groaned. "Do you know why we did it, little mage? Not cruelty. Fear. Your lady's fire does not warm. It unmakes. One spark escaped her prison and burned a realm to glass—we watched it happen. We built this winter to keep the world *small*. Cold things do not spread." The hollow eyes fixed on her. "And here you come anyway. Three insects carrying two sparks toward a bonfire. Tell me why I should not end you where you stand."

Yara looked up at him, and found—to her own surprise—that she was not afraid.

"Because you're tired," she said. "Because you've been holding this winter for three hundred years and your arms are shaking. Because a god who has to imprison someone is a god who's already lost the argument." She stepped forward, boots crunching on blue ice. "You want to know why we came? Because justice isn't a law, Halveth. It isn't your order or the crown's edicts or the quiet of a frozen world. Sometimes it's just a woman walking up a mountain to say: *this ends*."

The god was silent. Snow drifted between them.

Then he laughed—a sound like an avalanche deciding, at the last moment, not to fall—and opened his hands.

"Then take it," Halveth said. "But understand what you carry. Fire asks a price of everything it touches. And you—" the hollows of his eyes seemed almost sorrowful— "you have so little left to pay with."

The shard fell into Yara's palms, and it was warm, warm as a living hand, warm as everything she had lost.

### Dain

Dain Ferro had held lines before. At Cael Ford he'd held a bridge against four hundred raiders with sixty men and a broken shield-wall. This was worse, because this time he was alone, and this time he believed in what he was holding.

They came up the pass in torchlight, the crown's army, ranks of witch-hunters in their grey-and-silver, pikes bristling like a steel thicket. Commander Vess's banner at the front—the twin axes, the crown above them. Dain had marched under that banner for fifteen years. It looked smaller than he remembered.

"Hold," he said to the empty air, because there was no one to say it to, and drew his sword.

They stopped thirty paces out. Torches hissed in the wind. Somewhere above, the aurora pulsed, and Dain let himself look up—one heartbeat only—at the ring of ice on the summit, where a woman he'd once hunted was about to wake a god.

"Captain Ferro." Commander Vess rode forward, grey-bearded, weary. "Step aside. You were a good soldier once. Don't die as a traitor."

"I died as a traitor three days ago," Dain said. "Everything since has just been paperwork."

Laughter rippled down the hunter lines—nervous, quickly smothered. Vess didn't smile.

"You know what's up that mountain," Vess said quietly. "You know what happens if she wakes it. The old wars. Realms burning. We built peace on that winter, Ferro."

"No," Dain said. "We built *silence*. There's a difference, and you've known it for years. You knew when you started shipping mage-children south in sealed wagons. You knew what the rituals needed." He raised his voice, letting it carry down the ranks. "Every man here who's ever wondered what happens to the ones we take—wondered and then stopped wondering—I'm telling you now. They're not imprisoned. They're *spent*. Like candles. Like fuel. That's what your crown calls peace."

Silence, except for the wind.

Then a young hunter—Maren, Dain thought, Maren who had cried after her first burning—lowered her pike. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Take him," Vess said, tired, and the line surged forward, and Dain set his back against the rocks and began to hold.

* * *

### Oren

Oren had never seen a god sleep before, and now he couldn't stop looking.

She lay inside the ring of ice like a woman lying inside a window: vast, curled around herself, made of coals banked low under grey ash. Every few heartbeats something moved in her chest—a flicker, a dying pulse of orange—and each time it flickered, Oren felt it in his own chest, answering, like two hearts trying to find the same rhythm.

"She's so cold," he whispered. "How can fire be cold?"

"She's not," Yara said. She knelt at the ring's edge, the three shards laid out before her on the ice—two gathered in blood and memory, one given grudgingly by a god who was already fading into the storm. "She's starving. There's a difference too."

"How do you know all these differences?"

Yara smiled at him, and Oren saw that her hands were trembling—not from fear. From something else. Something that had started hollowing her out back at the mountain shrine, when she'd traded away her mother's face, and had gotten worse with every mile.

"Because I used to be her sworn mage," Yara said. "Before you were born. Before the silence." She touched his head, briefly, the way his mother used to. "Listen to me, Oren. What happens next, I have to do alone. But the anchor—the thing holding the winter shut—it's woven through the ice, and it's strong, stronger than me. I need it broken. Can you break it?"

Oren looked at the ring. The anchor wasn't visible, but he could feel it, the way he'd learned to feel everything since his village burned: a wrongness in the air, a note held too long, a cage made of *quiet*. When he got scared it flared. When he cried, it burned. Everyone had always told him that was the worst thing about him.

Now a grown-up was asking for it like it was a gift.

"What if I can't stop?" he asked. "What if it's too big?"

"Then be too big," Yara said simply. "That's the whole secret. Everything else is just practice."

Down the mountain, faintly, steel rang against steel. Dain.

Oren closed his eyes. He thought about his village. About the soldiers and the smoke. About being cold every night for a year, colder than any child should be, while the whole world froze and pretended that was normal. He thought about how angry that made him, and how the anger wasn't bad, actually—the anger was just love with nowhere to go.

He opened his eyes and let it all go.

Light poured out of him—not the wild flare of the road, not the frightened stutter that had betrayed him a hundred times, but a column of white-gold that struck the ring of ice like dawn striking a mirror. The anchor screamed. Oren heard it scream, a thin crystalline shriek, and he pushed harder, crying without knowing he was crying, and the great ring *cracked*—a fissure racing around its circumference like lightning frozen mid-strike—

—and the prison broke open, and Vessia's eyes opened.

They were the color of the first fire ever lit.

* * *

### Yara

She walked into the ring across ground that was suddenly, impossibly warm.

Up close, Vessia was not the towering icon of the covenant murals. She was a woman-shaped absence of cold, ash-skinned, embers guttering along her veins like a city seen from far away at night. Her eyes found Yara, and in them Yara saw three years of silence explained—not abandonment, never abandonment. Captivity. Watching. Grief.

*Little ember,* the goddess said, and her voice was hearth-warm and world-old. *You should not have come. The price—*

"I know the price." Yara knelt, pressing the three shards together in her cupped palms. They sang to one another, a triad, hungry. "I've been paying it since the shrine. I know what reforging the covenant costs. I know what kindling means." She looked up. "Halveth told me I had little left. He's right. So I'll give all of it."

*Your memories cannot—*

"They're not payment." Yara's throat tightened. "They're fuel. That's different. Fuel gets *used*. Gets made into something warm." She thought of Greyhollow, of the dockworker with the luck-charm, of Oren asleep against her shoulder on the frost-road, of Dain's face when he recognized her sigil. All of it, offered. All of it, spent. "You taught us that, my lady. Nothing burns that doesn't become light. Take everything. Make spring."

Vessia's ember-veins brightened.

*Then remember this,* the goddess said, as the shards leapt from Yara's hands and sank into the ash of her chest like seeds into soil. *When you wake among strangers, and no one knows your name, and you think you gave away everything—you did not give away love. Love is not a memory. It is what remains when the memories burn.*

The fire took her.

It went through Yara like a tide going out—Greyhollow first, the shop, the smell of cedar shavings; then the war, the faces of her sworn brothers, the line at Ashfall; then her mother, again, though the face was already gone, only the warmth of the word *mother* left, and she gave that too; then Oren, laughing, crying, calling her name; then Dain; then her own name; then—

Light. White and total and gentle, a dawn three hundred years overdue breaking over the roof of the world, and far below, all across the frozen realms, ice groaned, sighed, and began, at last, to melt.

* * *

### Oren

Spring came down the mountain like a released animal.

Snowmelt roared in every ravine. The pass, where Dain still stood bleeding over his sword among men who had finally—some of them—turned around, grew soft underfoot with mud and the first green shoots of grass. The army broke. Not all at once, but in patches, hunters dropping pikes and staring east at a horizon that was no longer white, at clouds gathering warm and heavy with rain. Commander Vess sat his horse a long moment, looking at the flooding valleys, and then turned his mount downhill without a word, and no one followed him, and everyone followed him, both at once.

Oren ran up through the mud and the meltwater to the ring of ice—which was no longer ice, but a ring of standing stones dripping in the rain—and found her.

Yara lay in a bed of wet grass that should not have been growing yet, breathing, alive, her covenant-scars faded to pale silver threads. But when Oren took her hand and called her name, she looked at him with eyes like a stranger's: kind, empty, searching.

"Do I know you?" she asked softly.

And Oren, ten years old, orphaned twice over, held her hand tighter and said, "Not yet. But you will. I'll tell you everything."

Behind him, limping down through the melting snow, sword still drawn, came Dain Ferro—ready, Oren could see, to spend whatever years it took doing exactly the same.
