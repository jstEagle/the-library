## 10. Emberlight

The first thing she knew was water.

Not the cold kind—the drowned-temple kind, the kind that had pressed against her lungs in Ashfall while a wraith wearing her brother's face screamed her name. This was gentler: the sound of it, running somewhere below the window, loud and alive, chattering over stones. A river waking up.

She opened her eyes to a ceiling of grey timber crossed with old smoke-stains, and light coming through a window in long gold bars, thick with dust that drifted like slow snow falling upward. Her body felt wrung out, hollowed, a garment worn by someone else for years and handed back still warm.

She did not know where she was.

She did not know who had hung the sprig of dried rowan above the door, or why the sight of it made her throat tighten as though a word she couldn't remember was caught there.

She lay still and listened. Below, two voices—one low and rough, shaping words slowly, like a man reading aloud to a child; one bright, quick, correcting him.

"—and then she said *that's not how you hold a shieldwall, Ferro*, and she walked right through the—"

"You've told this one four times."

"It's a good one. She'll want to hear it right."

Yara's hand went to her chest before she knew why. Beneath her sternum, something burned. Not pain—warmth, banked and patient, like coals under ash. When she breathed, the warmth breathed with her.

She pushed back the blanket and stood. Her legs held, barely. There was a looking-glass on the wall, cheap tin, spotted with age, and the woman in it was a stranger: thin, grey-streaked, a face mapped with fine scars she could not account for. One scar ran from her left ear to her jaw like a river drawn on skin. The stranger's eyes were the worst part. They looked at Yara with an expression she recognized from nowhere—from everywhere—and they were full of grief with no object, mourning without a grave, love with no one attached to its other end.

She went downstairs because standing still felt more frightening than moving.

* * *

The room at the bottom smelled of bread and woodsmoke and river-water. A boy sat cross-legged on the table itself, because chairs were apparently a suggestion he had declined. Ten years old, maybe eleven now—there was a new sharpness at his jaw that hadn't been there, she somehow knew, though she didn't know how she knew. His hair was singed short on one side, growing back in patches. When he saw her, he went very still, the way small animals go still.

Then he slid off the table and crossed the room slowly, like a man approaching a deer, and stopped just inside arm's reach.

"Do you know my name?" he asked.

She searched herself. Nothing. But the ache in her chest leaned toward him like a plant toward sun.

"No," she said. "But I know I'd burn down the world if anything happened to you."

The boy's face crumpled—not with sorrow but with relief, the way grief looks when it has been carried too long and is finally allowed to be put down. He laughed wetly and wiped his nose on his sleeve.

"Oren," he said. "My name's Oren. You gave me your rations on the pass when I was dying of cold-sick, even though you were starving too, and you told me being brave was mostly just being scared in front of other people." He swallowed. "You're Yara Ishikawa. You're the most famous person in the world. And you don't have to be scared, because we're not going anywhere."

From the doorway behind her came a cough, deliberately loud. She turned. A man filled it—big, grey at the temples, one eye clouded white, a hunter's coat brushed clean but patched at both elbows. He held his cap in his hands like a man at a funeral.

"Captain Dain Ferro," he said. "Formerly of the Crown's Order of the Quiet Flame. Currently of... nothing much." He almost smiled. "We served the same covenant, once. You don't remember. That's—" his voice caught, steadied, "—that's the price you paid. We're paying the rest."

* * *

They told her everything, or they tried to. They told it in pieces, over days, because the whole shape of it was too large to hold at once—like trying to drink a river.

She learned that winter had ended not gradually but violently: that on the longest night of the year, on a prison-peak whose name the maps had begun to forget, a god named Halveth had lost his grip on three thousand years of frozen punishment, and fire had come back into the world like blood into a numb limb—agonizing, glorious, unstoppable. That the floods had taken Greyhollow's lower streets and given it back its harbor; that ships were running again, and in the markets people sold little clay figures of a woman holding a sword aloft, and called her Vessia Waking.

She learned that she was in those clay figures too. Smaller. Beside the goddess. Sometimes holding the sword for her.

"They call it the Emberlight Covenant," Oren said, sitting on the table again, legs swinging. "Everyone wants to sign it. Guilds, kings, the river-gods, even some of the quiet ones who locked her up—they're all lining up to swear they were on our side the whole time." He spat, with great precision, off the porch. "Liars."

"And us?" Yara asked. "What do they say about us?"

Dain looked at the fire. "That you died. It's cleaner, for the histories. A martyr needs no debts paid." He turned the cap in his hands. "I told them otherwise. Once. To the King's own herald. Told him exactly what the hunt was for, what the mage-blood was fed to, which commanders signed the orders. Gave him names." His jaw worked. "Half the Order's in irons now. The other half deserted rather than follow them anymore. Justice came, in the end. Just not through any law's front door."

"And you?" she asked him. "What did it cost you?"

He met her eyes—her strange grieving eyes—and for the first time since she'd known him, which was never, he let her see all the way in.

"My name," he said. "My rank. My tomb, someday, in the hunters' yard where I buried every man I ever led." He shrugged, a big slow movement like a hill settling. "Cheap. Compared to yours."

* * *

The price revealed itself in small ways, the way frost reveals itself one fern at a time on a windowpane.

She knew how to fight—her body remembered, flowing through forms she had no names for—but she didn't know why her guard always favored the left, until Dain quietly said, "Shield-side scar. Ashfall," and stopped speaking for a while.

She knew songs she'd never heard sung, and cried at one of them without knowing which line broke her.

She reached for her mother's face at night, in that half-place before sleep, and found only a warm shape where a face should be, like a portrait hung in a house after the family has moved out. She sat up in the dark with her hands pressed flat against her sternum, feeling the coal of Vessia breathe there, and understood, in a way that needed no memories to understand: this was what she had bought the spring with. Every face. Every name. Herself.

Oren found her like that once, and didn't ask, and didn't leave. He just sat beside her on the cold floorboards until dawn came up grey through the shutters, and then he said, "You don't have to remember us to keep us. I checked."

"Did you," she said.

"I'm famous for it. Boy Who Burned Bright. They write ballads. They're terrible ballads." He bumped his shoulder against hers. "But that part's true."

* * *

Spring deepened. The river dropped back within its banks and left the streets scrubbed and gleaming. In the market square, mages walked openly for the first time in living memory—a hedge-witch selling weather-charms beside the fishmonger, an old storm-speaker telling fortunes with lightning caught in a bottle. Children ran beneath their stalls playing *ember-shard*, shrieking, taking turns being the god.

And every evening, without fail, Dain Ferro came to the table with his cap in his hands and told her a story.

Some were true. Some were the truth bent into better shapes, the way a smith bends iron. He told her about a mercenary company that had held a bridge at Ashfall for six hours against ten times their number, about a woman who walked into witch-hunter custody with her head high so a frightened child could escape over the walls, about a captain who chose exile over an order he finally read properly. He told them slowly, carefully, watching her face each time, and when something landed—if her breath caught, if her hand drifted to her chest—he would mark it, and tell it again another night, and another, until the story stopped being his and started being hers.

"You're rebuilding me," she said to him once, catching him at it.

"No," he said. "I'm keeping your seat warm. There's a difference." He looked into the fire. "Memory's a house, they say. Yours burned down. But I figure a person isn't the house. A person's the fire that lived in it." He cleared his throat roughly. "House can be built again. Fire remembers how to burn."

* * *

On the first truly warm night, they walked down to the harbor together—all three, because Oren refused on principle to let either of them go anywhere alone, a rule he enforced with the terrifying moral authority of a ten-year-old who had once watched everyone he loved disappear.

The water was black silk. Boats rode at anchor, lamps strung along their rails like fallen constellations. On the far quay, someone had lit a bonfire, and people were dancing around it—dockworkers, fishwives, a one-eyed soldier who moved like a man who had forgotten how and decided to do it anyway.

Yara stood at the rail and felt the coal in her chest turn gently, like a sleeper rolling over.

"She talks to me sometimes," she said quietly. "Vessia. Not in words. More like... warmth with intent."

"What does she want?" Oren asked.

"The same thing she always wanted, I think. Not worship. Not law." Yara watched the dancers, the firelight on their faces. "Witnesses. Someone to carry the flame forward and choose, freely, to keep it lit. The old covenant was broken because the gods demanded obedience. Maybe the new one is simpler." She turned to look at them—the boy with the singed hair, the disgraced captain, these two strangers her bones insisted were home. "Maybe it's just this. People who refuse to let each other go."

Oren slipped his hand into hers. Dain, after a moment, put his big scarred hand on her shoulder, awkward as a bear trying to be gentle.

Above the harbor, high past the last lamplight, the peaks that had held the world in ice for three thousand years stood bare and green-shouldered for the first time in living memory. And on the highest of them—so faint it might have been sunset lingering, might have been imagination—a point of ember-red light burned steady against the blue dusk, like a lamp left burning in a window.

Like a promise that someone, somewhere, was awake.

Oren saw it too. He squeezed her fingers. "Do you think she'll ever come down?"

Yara watched the light, and felt the coal in her chest answer it, pulse for pulse, like two fires learning each other's rhythm across an impossible distance.

"No," she said, and smiled, and found that the smile belonged to her, whoever she was. "But I think she'll keep the lamp lit. And I think—" she looked east, where the road ran out of the city toward mountains no map had finished redrawing, toward valleys that the floods had uncovered and the thaw had opened, places where old altars were surfacing from the ice with their fires still warm, "—I think she's going to need someone to answer it. All of them will. Every altar waking up out there, every god remembering how to be known."

Dain followed her gaze and sighed the sigh of a man who already knew how this story went. "When do we leave?"

Oren was already grinning. "Tomorrow?"

Yara laughed—a sound that surprised her, that rose from somewhere deeper than memory, from whatever it was that had survived the burning of the house—and turned her face to the emberlight on the peaks, and the wind off the spring water, and the two people beside her whom she did not remember and would not, ever, let go.

"Tomorrow," she said. "First light. We'll need to pack."

And far away on the mountain, the lamp flared—just once, briefly, joyously—like a heart recognizing its own beat returned.
