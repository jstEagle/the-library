## 1. The Quiet Valley

The trouble with carving a child's bone, Silas Galloway had learned over thirty years at the workbench, was that children's bones were small. Not fragile—bone was bone, and a femur that had carried a girl through eight years of running through barley fields and falling out of apple trees was tougher than any oak—but small. There was no room for error. One slip of the chisel and you'd taken the only thing her mother would ever hold again and made it worse.

Silas held the femur up to the window light and turned it slowly.

It was a good bone. Clean. The river had kept it three days before giving it back, which the river sometimes did, as if it felt guilty. The girl's name had been Tansy Bell. She'd slipped off the mill weir chasing a duck she had no business chasing, and the whole valley of Hollowmere had agreed, in its quiet way, that this was a tragedy and also nobody's fault, and also everyone would be more comfortable if we never spoke of it again.

That was Hollowmere for you. A valley so peaceful it had made peace into a kind of furniture. You sat on it. You didn't examine where it came from.

Silas set the femur in the vise, padded with wool, and picked up the finest chisel—the one they called *the whisper*, though only to themselves, because naming your tools out loud in Hollowmere was the sort of thing that got you a visit from the Ashen Order's gray-cloaked courtesy.

"Right," Silas said softly. "Let's make them remember you properly, little duck-chaser."

The plan was lilies. Tansy's mother had asked for lilies winding down the shaft of the bone, and maybe a duck, if it wasn't too much trouble, and Silas had said of course not, because what kind of monster charges extra for a duck?

The first petal went well. The second. By the third, Silas had found the rhythm—that place where the hands knew the work and the mind could drift somewhere soft, watching the light move across the shavings, listening to the particular music of steel on bone. *Tik. Tik-tik. Tik.* A sound like rain deciding to be patient.

Hollowmere was quiet the way deep water is quiet. No bells—the Order had melted those down two centuries ago, "for the good of all souls." No shrines. No prayers spoken above a mutter, and none at all in public. The valley's founding law hung carved over every gate in letters worn smooth by weather and deliberate forgetting:

*What is given to silence stays silent.*

Silas had lived under that law their whole life, the way a fish lives under water: completely, invisibly, without ever once thinking of air.

Mostly without thinking of it.

Because there was the other thing. The thing Silas did not discuss, did not write down, did not so much as hum about while working. The thing that had started when they were six years old, holding their grandfather's whittling knife for the first time, and an old sheep's knucklebone had said—clear as the kettle singing—

*Cold up here.*

Silas had dropped it. Had run crying to Grandmother, who had looked at them for a long, long moment with an expression Silas hadn't understood until decades later, when they finally recognized it as fear wearing kindness like a mask.

"Bones are just bones," Grandmother had said. "And you're just a Galloway. And Galloways keep their heads down and their doors shut. Say it back."

*Bones are just bones. I'm just a Galloway. Head down, door shut.*

Thirty years of saying it back. Thirty years of hearing, faint as breath on a winter window, the murmurs that lived in everything Silas carved. Old bones mostly, and old bones say old things—complaints about the weather, mostly, and the occasional wistful note about somebody's stew. Sheep were grumblers. Cattle were sentimental. The one time Silas had been asked to carve a human bone—a grandfather's finger joint, set in a ring—they'd heard nothing at all, just a warm silence like a sleeping cat, and had gone home and shaken for an hour.

Magic couldn't cross the wardline. Everyone knew it. That was the entire point of Hollowmere. Whatever the world outside still had of wonders and horrors, the founders had built a wall against it, and inside the wall there was only work, and weather, and the long polite hush of people who had traded mystery for safety and agreed never to bring it up at dinner.

So whatever Silas heard—and Silas had spent years convincing themselves they heard *nothing*, that it was imagination, that imagination was safe, that imagination wasn't a crime—it couldn't be magic. It was just... the bones being bones. Echoes. Memory soaked into material, like smoke into rafters. Natural. Explainable. Harmless.

Head down. Door shut.

Silas carved the fourth lily petal. The fifth. Started the stem, a curling line down toward the duck, which was going to be frankly magnificent, all suggestion and swagger, three strokes and a dot for an eye.

*Tik. Tik-tik. Ti—*

"—didn't mean to," said the bone.

Silas stopped.

Not jumped. Not gasped. Stopped, the way a clock stops, every gear locking at once, because the voice had come from *inside the vise*, from the femur itself, small and wet-sounding and terribly young, and it had not been a complaint about the weather or a wistful note about stew.

"I didn't mean to," the bone said again, closer now, the way a sound gets close when you lean toward it. "The duck was going to the reeds and I wanted—I only wanted to—"

"Tansy?" Silas whispered.

Which was absurd. Which was impossible. Which was, Silas realized with a cold spreading feeling under their ribs, exactly the sort of thing you should never, ever say aloud in a workshop with the window open, because the Ashen Order had ears, and worse, because if you said a name to a dead thing and the dead thing answered, then it wasn't imagination anymore, and then you had to decide what it was instead.

The bone said nothing more. The light moved across the shavings. Somewhere outside, a cart creaked past on the mill road, and a neighbor called a greeting to someone else, ordinary as bread, and Silas stood at the workbench with their heart going like a trapped bird and their hands perfectly, professionally steady, because thirty years of practice will do that—teach your hands to lie even when the rest of you has come apart.

"It's memory," Silas told the empty room. "Smoke in the rafters. That's all."

The room declined to confirm or deny. Rooms in Hollowmere were very good at that.

* * *

Silas finished the lilies. It took twice as long as it should have, because every few strokes they paused—not listening, they told themselves firmly, absolutely not listening, simply *checking the grain*—and heard nothing but the ordinary hush of the afternoon. The duck got its eye-dot. The whole piece, when Silas held it up, was lovely: a small white river of flowers flowing around a bird that seemed forever about to lift off.

Mrs. Bell came at dusk, wrapped in her grayest shawl, because grief in Hollowmere dressed like everyone else and hoped not to be noticed. She stood in the doorway wringing her cap in her hands.

"You've made it beautiful," she said, when Silas lifted it from the cloth.

"She helped," Silas said, before their brain could stop their mouth.

Mrs. Bell's face did something complicated. "She always did have opinions."

"She chased a duck," Silas said, "and the duck, I think, is sorry."

And Mrs. Bell laughed—one cracked, astonished note of laughter, the first Silas had ever drawn from anyone in this valley, the first anyone had ever dared to let out over a grave—and then pressed her hand to her mouth as if she'd sworn in chapel, and paid too much, and left with the bone held against her chest like a second heartbeat.

Silas watched her go down the lane and thought: *I am going to be burned in the market square someday, and it will be worth it.*

Then thought: *Don't be an idiot. Head down. Door shut.*

Then swept the floor, banked the fire, and tried very hard not to think at all.

* * *

The dreams that night were of water. Not drowning—Silas had never drowned and resented the implication—but of standing in a river shallows at dusk, and the water full of voices the way water is full of light, all of them talking at once, none of them words yet, just the *shape* of words, a crowd pressing politely at a door. And far downstream, so far it might have been imagination, something enormous turned over in the dark and began, very slowly, to pay attention.

Silas woke before dawn with the sheets twisted and the taste of river-mud in their mouth and lay staring at the ceiling beams, doing arithmetic. Three days since the femur had spoken. Two hundred years, by the oldest carvings, since anything magical had happened inside the wardline. One of these numbers was wrong, and Silas had a bad feeling about which one.

"Smoke in the rafters," they said to the ceiling.

The ceiling, like the room, declined to comment.

* * *

Work, as always, was the answer. Work was always the answer in Hollowmere; it was practically the valley's second law. So Silas opened the shutters, lit the lamp, and got to the order list: a button for the cooper's wife, cut from antler. A needle case for the schoolmaster. A set of dice for young Bram Ostler, who had lost his last set to a creek bet and his dignity along with them.

Ordinary things. Dead things, technically—antler and ivory and old cow bone—but dead in the way furniture is dead. Silent. Safe.

Silas took up a rib blank for the dice and set the saw to it, and the rhythm came, *tik, tik-tik, tik*, and the mind drifted soft, and for one golden hour the world was just shavings and lamplight and the smell of bone dust, which Silas had always privately thought smelled like libraries wished they smelled.

Then, sorting the morning's shavings into the scrap basket—curly ones for kindling, fine ones for the mortar Silas sold to the apothecary, who claimed it settled stomachs, which Silas chose not to think about too hard—Silas's fingers found paper.

Paper. In the shavings.

A strip of it, thin as a moth wing, covered edge to edge in a tiny, cramped, beautiful hand. Silas held it to the lamp.

It was a prayer.

*O Shepherd of the Ninefold Fold, who walks the star-road, hear your servant Maren, who is afraid of the dark in the cellar and the dark in her heart, and—*

Silas read it twice. Three times. The hand grew no less beautiful and no less impossible. Because Silas knew that script—every scribe-trained child in the outside world learned it, and Silas had learned it from a peddler's stolen primer at age eleven, reading by candle grease with the blanket over their head like a criminal, which, technically, it had made them. It was liturgical script. Church-hand. The writing of people who prayed to gods with names and faces and ninefold folds, gods that had not been permitted inside Hollowmere for two centuries, gods whose names the founding law forbade you to carve, speak, or think too loudly.

And it was addressed—to whom? Prayers went up. That was the whole architecture of prayer. Up, and out, and away, to wherever the gods sat listening.

This one had come *down*. Into a workshop. Into a scrap basket. Onto a workbench in the godless valley, among the bone dust, addressed to a god who was two hundred years forbidden, written in a hand that did not belong to anyone in Hollowmere.

Silas stood very still in the gray early light.

There were explanations. There were always explanations; Silas had built a life on them, a nice warm burrow of them. Someone had smuggled the paper in. Peddlers brought contraband scripture tucked between bolts of cloth all the time, probably, certainly, and it had blown in through the window, yes, on a wind, indoors, uphill, into a closed shutter.

Head down. Door shut. Bones are just bones.

But the femur had said *I didn't mean to*, and the dream had said *listen*, and the paper in Silas's hand was warm.

That was the detail that undid the burrow. The paper was warm, like a hand, like something freshly alive, and when Silas turned it over, the cramped beautiful hand had added a second line, in ink that had not been there a moment ago, still shining wet:

*Who answers?*

Silas looked at the window. Closed. Latched. Beyond it, Hollowmere lay blue and quiet in the dawn, chimneys just beginning to breathe their morning smoke, the whole valley folded into its ancient peace like a sleeper who dreamed of nothing, because dreaming had been outlawed, along with bells, and shrines, and asking questions.

Somewhere out there, someone named Maren was afraid of the dark, and had prayed, and her prayer had fallen—like a bird with a broken wing, like a letter to a dead address—into a bone-carver's scrap basket.

And something had written back.

Silas Galloway, who had spent thirty years being no one, carefully, on purpose, stood in the middle of the workbench's lamplight holding a warm impossibility, and understood—with the strange calm that arrives just before the earthquake—that the quiet valley was not quiet at all. It never had been. It was *holding its breath*. And this morning, for reasons that had everything to do with a drowned girl and a duck and a strip of paper that shouldn't exist, it had begun, very slowly, to exhale.

The knock came at the door just as the sun cleared the ridge.

Three knocks. Even. Unhurried. The knock of someone who has never once in their life needed anything badly enough to hurry.

Silas tucked the prayer into their shirt, next to skin and heartbeat, and went to see who, in the godless valley of Hollowmere, had come knocking at a bone-carver's door at dawn.
