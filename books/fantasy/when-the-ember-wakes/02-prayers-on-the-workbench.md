## 2. Prayers on the Workbench

The trouble with living in a valley that had outlawed wonder, Silas Galloway had long ago decided, was that wonder did not take the hint. It simply went quiet, the way a cat goes quiet when it has knocked something off a shelf and is waiting to see whether you noticed.

Silas had spent forty-one years not noticing. It was a discipline. It was practically a craft, and Silas was very good at crafts.

So when the first prayer appeared among the bone shavings on the workbench—small, folded, written in a careful clerk's hand on paper that smelled of tallow and road dust—Silas's first instinct was to assume someone had dropped it. The second instinct was to burn it. The third instinct, arriving late and unwelcome like a relative at harvest, was to read it.

Silas read it.

*To the One Who Waits Beneath the Water,* it began, *I have been good. I have been quiet. My mother coughs blood in the night and the herb-woman says there is nothing more to be done, but you are older than herbs, and I am asking anyway. Please. —Tamsin, age nine, Millrow.*

Silas set the paper down with the exaggerated care of a person trying not to startle a snake.

The valley of Hollowmere forbade the naming of gods. This was not a law so much as a habit grown into law, the way a fence post grows into a tree if you leave it long enough. Three hundred years ago, the founders had built the wards—great buried rings of carved stone at the valley's mouth—and ever since, nothing had come in and nothing had gone out, spiritually speaking. No miracles. No hauntings. No voices in the bones.

Except, of course, for Silas.

That was the thing nobody knew. That was the thing Silas had spent a lifetime making sure nobody knew. Bone-carvers were respected in Hollowmere precisely because their work was *dull*: they made buttons and knife-handles and memorial beads from the bones of the beloved dead, and the bones said nothing, because in Hollowmere the dead said nothing, because in Hollowmere *nothing* said anything, and that was the whole blessed point of the place.

But Silas heard them. Had always heard them. A drowned child's last word. An old man's joke, told once, decades ago, still warm. The bones remembered, and Silas listened, and told no one, because the alternative was the Ashen Order asking questions in their ash-grey cloaks with their ash-grey faces, and questions from the Order had a way of ending in ash.

And now here was a prayer. A real one. Written by a child who believed—impossibly, dangerously—that somewhere in the godless valley, something was listening.

Silas looked around the workshop as though the walls might explain themselves. They declined. The morning light came through the south window the way it always did, catching the dust, gilding the half-finished rosary of knucklebones on the rack. Everything was exactly as it should be.

Except for the second prayer, which had not been there a moment ago, sitting now atop the shavings beside the first, folded neat as a moth's wing.

Silas did not touch it for a full minute. Then Silas touched it, because curiosity is a bone-deep condition and there is no carving it out.

*To the Shepherd of the Last Field,* this one read, *my husband walks in his sleep toward the river every night since the fever. I tie the door and he unties it. I am afraid he will walk into the water and not come back. Tell him to stay. Tell him I will make his favorite bread. —Marta, Weir's End.*

Two prayers. Two different gods. Both gods forbidden. Both prayers addressed, apparently, to Silas's workbench.

"Well," Silas said aloud, to the empty room, in the reasonable tone one uses with unreasonable things. "No."

The room did not answer. But under the floorboards, in the little box where Silas kept the offcuts too small to carve—the finger-bones and toe-bones and splinters of rib that no family had claimed—something rustled. Not moved. *Rustled*, the way paper rustles.

Silas opened the box.

There were eleven prayers inside.

* * *

By evening, Silas had arranged them all on the workbench in a row, like specimens, which was the only way Silas knew how to be brave about anything: turn it into a collection, catalog it, pretend it was under control.

Eleven prayers. Twelve, if you counted the one that had arrived while Silas was arranging the other eleven, which Silas chose not to count, on principle.

They came from all over the valley. Millrow, Weir's End, the terraces above the slate quarry, even two from Hollowmere town itself, which took a special kind of courage or desperation, since the Order kept its largest house there. They asked for healings, for rain, for a lost goat, for the return of a son who had walked out the wardline three years ago and never come back. They were addressed to seven different gods whose names Silas had only ever seen scratched furtively on the undersides of tables, gods with names like *the Shepherd of the Last Field* and *the One Who Waits Beneath the Water* and *Grandmother Ember*—names that sounded less like divinity and more like neighbors. Old names. Names from before the wards.

And every single one of them had landed here. On this bench. In this shop. Among the shavings of a bone-carver who had spent four decades being aggressively, meticulously unremarkable.

"They're not for me," Silas told the bones, because talking to the bones was an old habit and the one indulgence Silas allowed. "I'm nobody. I'm a button-maker."

The femur on the rack—the memorial piece for old Aldous Pike, may he rest, may he stop humming—gave off its faint familiar warmth of attention. The bones couldn't speak words, exactly. It was more like standing near a fire: you felt what they'd been. And what they felt right now, all of them, the whole boxful, was something Silas had never felt from bone before.

Anticipation.

They were waiting. The way a dog waits by a door. The way a congregation waits.

"Oh, don't you start," Silas muttered. "You don't get to have opinions. You're *buttons*."

The bones radiated a warmth that could only be described, in Silas's professional opinion, as smug.

Silas sat down heavily on the stool and put both elbows on the bench, careful of the prayers, and stared at the middle distance for a long time.

Here was the arithmetic of it, laid out plain. Prayers were appearing physically, out of thin air, among the shavings. Which meant they were traveling somehow—from mouths or pens across the valley, through wards that had held for three centuries, to this bench. Which meant either the wards were failing—which would be news of continental consequence—or they had never worked the way everyone believed. And either way, the prayers were coming *here*, to Silas, which meant that whatever hole in the world they fell through, its other end was shaped exactly like Silas Galloway.

Which meant, and here Silas's stomach performed a slow, cold roll, that somebody up the line of causality had finally noticed. Prayers didn't just appear. Somebody was praying. And if eleven people in Hollowmere had risked heresy to pray to forbidden gods, then sooner or later, one of them would want to know if anyone was home.

The terrifying thing—the thing Silas turned over and over like a stone with something underneath it—was not the dread. The dread was familiar; Silas and dread went back decades, comfortable as old boots. The terrifying thing was the other feeling. The one underneath.

Someone needed answering. Eleven someones. A child named Tamsin with a dying mother. A woman named Marta whose husband walked toward the river in his sleep. For the first time in forty-one years of careful invisibility, Silas Galloway was *needed*—not as a button-maker, not as a quiet man who did decent work for fair prices, but as the thing at the end of all those folded papers. The listener. Maybe, if the bones' smug warmth meant anything, the answerer.

It felt like standing at the edge of a frozen pond in early winter. You know the ice. You know your weight. And still, some traitorous part of you wants to step out onto it just to hear it sing.

Silas blew out the lamp, locked the prayers in the offcut box with the bones—who rustled contentedly, the traitors—and went to bed, and lay awake until well past midnight listening to the house settle, and got up three times to check that the box was still shut.

On the third check, there were fourteen prayers inside.

* * *

The fifteenth arrived in person.

It was the fourth morning, grey and drizzling, the kind of day when the valley seemed to hunch its shoulders against the sky. Silas was at the bench working a memorial bead—a child's tooth this time, which was always delicate work, requiring the kind of patience that looked, from outside, like doing nothing—when the shop bell rang.

Silas looked up and forgot, briefly, how doors worked.

The stranger in the doorway was young, maybe twenty, maybe younger—it was hard to tell, because the stranger had the specific look of someone who had been running for a while and had run clean past tired into a kind of brittle brightness. Road-dust to the knees. A satchel worn crosswise, patched at the strap. Hair the color of wet sand, chopped short with what looked like a knife and a grudge. And eyes—grey-green, sharp, red-rimmed from lack of sleep—that swept the workshop the way a bird sweeps a field, cataloging everything, trusting nothing.

The stranger's gaze snagged on the workbench. On the shavings. Something in that sharp face went tight and hungry and terrified all at once, and Silas recognized the expression, because Silas wore a version of it every day of their life. It was the face of a person about to ask a question they could not afford to have answered wrong.

"You're the carver," the stranger said. Not a question. "Galloway."

"Depends who's asking," Silas said, which was as close to wit as survival allowed before breakfast.

"Wren." The stranger stepped inside without being invited, which took a certain nerve given Silas's size and the carving knives within reach, and stopped, hands half-raised, palms out—the universal gesture of *I'm not going to hurt you, please don't hurt me, we're both pretending this is normal.* "I need—I've been looking—" Wren stopped. Started again, quieter. "Three weeks ago, prayers started getting answered. Not answered answered. Nothing grand. But they started *going somewhere*. People would write them and burn them, like the old rites, and instead of burning they'd—vanish. Just vanish off the coals. Whole pages. And the ones that vanished, things got... a little better. Marta's husband stopped walking to the river. Old Hesk's goats stopped dying." The words were tumbling faster now, a dam with cracks in it. "Nobody talks about it openly, obviously, but there's a—there's a current, under the talk. Hope. And hope is the most dangerous thing there is in this valley, do you understand me? Because the Ashen Order hears hope the way sharks hear blood, and I've seen what they—"

Wren stopped again. Drew a breath. And Silas watched, with a strange twisting feeling under the ribs, as the young stranger visibly decided to spend the last coin of their caution.

"I was a scribe," Wren said. "Order-trained. Prayer-registry, third archive. I transcribed petitions, filed them, burned the copies. Standard work. Except three weeks ago I was assigned to trace the vanished prayers, and the assignment wasn't *find out why*. It was *find out who*, and then report the name and walk away and let the Custodians handle the rest." Wren's jaw tightened. "I didn't walk away. I ran. Because I wanted to find them first. Because whoever they are, they're the first honest miracle this valley has seen in three hundred years, and I'll be damned before I hand them to men who burn people for hoping."

The rain ticked softly on the roof. Somewhere down the lane, a cart rattled past, ordinary as bread.

"And?" Silas said. "Did you find them?"

Wren looked at the workbench. At the shavings. At the little locked box beneath the window, which—Silas noted with distant horror—was rustling. Audibly. Like a box full of moths. Like a box full of *paper*.

"No," Wren said slowly, eyes fixed on the box. "But I followed the trail to Millrow, and Millrow pointed to Weir's End, and Weir's End pointed here, and the trail doesn't point anymore. It just..." Wren swallowed. "It stops at your door. Every thread ends at your door, carver. So I'm going to ask you the only question that matters, and I need you to understand that I burned my own life down behind me to be able to ask it."

Wren crossed the room in three strides, dropped to their knees—not in worship, Silas realized; in exhaustion, the way a runner drops—and pulled a folded paper from the satchel. Held it up in both hands like an offering. Like a torch.

"*Who has been answering them?*" Wren whispered. "Because somebody is. Somebody real. And I have to find them before the Order does."

Silas looked at the paper. At the box, rustling. At Wren's cracked lips and shaking hands and absolutely unconcealable, unbearably hopeful face.

Every instinct Silas owned—forty-one years of them, bred deep, polished smooth as river stone—said the same thing: *deny it, close the door, go back to buttons, survive.*

But Silas had spent three nights reading those prayers. Tamsin, age nine. Marta and her sleepwalking husband. Fourteen strangers pouring their hearts into the dark and having the dark, impossibly, pour them onto a workbench. And here was a fifteenth, delivered by hand, by someone who had thrown away everything to carry it.

The ice sang underfoot. Silas stepped out onto it.

"That depends," Silas said quietly, and reached past Wren to unlock the box, "on whether you believe in answers."

The lid came up. Fourteen folded prayers sat among the finger-bones and toe-bones, and as Wren stared, one of them unfolded itself—slowly, deliberately, like a flower deciding to commit—and stood upright on its creased edge, facing the stranger, as if presenting itself for inspection.

Wren made a sound that was not quite a word.

"Yeah," Silas said, with a helplessness that was halfway to laughter and entirely new. "That's about how I reacted."

And that was when the knocking started at the front door—heavy, official, in no hurry whatsoever—and a voice like cold gravel spoke through the wood.

"Ashen Order. Open in the name of the Custody."

Wren went white. The bones in the box went utterly, perfectly silent.

Silas closed the lid, very gently, and thought: *so much for breakfast.*
