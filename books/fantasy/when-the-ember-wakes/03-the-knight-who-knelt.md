## 3. The Knight Who Knelt

Ser Odile Vane had knocked on a great many doors in her seventeen years with the Ashen Order, and she had developed a taxonomy of them.

There was the guilty door, which opened too fast, its owner desperate to seem innocent. There was the defiant door, which did not open at all until you threatened to take it off its hinges. There was the terrified door, which opened a crack, on a chain, with one eye showing. And there — rarest of all — was the honest door, which opened at an ordinary speed by an ordinary person who had nothing to hide.

The bone-carver's door in Hollowmere was none of these. It opened slowly, at the speed of a man who had heard the knock coming for some time and had used the interval to decide exactly how much trouble he intended to be.

"Ser Knight," said Silas Galloway. He was tall and lean, with sawdust in his hair and ink on his fingers, and he looked at her armor the way a farmer looks at weather he cannot do anything about. "You'll want to wipe your boots. I just swept."

Odile blinked. In seventeen years, no one had ever asked her to wipe her boots.

She wiped her boots.

* * *

The house smelled of cedar shavings and tallow smoke, and it was very tidy in the way of rooms where someone is trying very hard not to be noticed. Odile catalogued it automatically: workbench under the window, tools hung in careful rows, a cold hearth, two cups on the table — *two*, when the census rolls listed Galloway as living alone — and a lidded box of pale wood sitting on the bench with the particular stillness of a thing recently hidden.

Her second, Ser Brannock, stood by the door with his hand on his sword hilt, radiating the patient menace the Order trained into its knights along with horsemanship and scripture. He was good at it. Odile had once watched him make a smuggler confess to a crime the man hadn't committed, purely through the application of standing still.

"Silas Galloway," she said. "Bone-carver. Licensed under the Custody's craft-ordinances, third renewal."

"That's me." The carver leaned against his workbench, arms folded. "You've done your reading."

"I do everyone's reading." She let her gaze travel the room again, slow as honey. "Your neighbors say you've been hearing things."

"Have they." His voice was perfectly level. His knuckles were not; they'd gone pale where they gripped his own elbows. "Hearing things. What sort of things?"

"That's what I'm here to find out."

"Well," said Silas Galloway, "I hear my neighbor's goat. It gets into my yard at night and eats my rosemary. If that's heresy, we should both prepare for trial."

Behind her, Brannock made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Odile did not turn around. She had learned long ago never to turn around when a suspect made her second want to laugh; it broke something essential in the dynamic.

"The Order has received reports," she said, "of prayers being answered in Hollowmere."

The change in the room was subtle but total, like the moment before a storm when the birds all shut up at once. Galloway's face didn't move. But the silence moved, pooled, deepened.

"Prayers can't be answered here," he said. "That's rather the point of the valley."

"Yes," Odile agreed. "It is."

The wards had held for three hundred years. That was the whole covenant: the valley gave up magic, and magic gave up the valley. No miracles, no curses, no voices from beyond the veil. The founders' bargain, written into the boundary stones in letters so old nobody could read them anymore. The Ashen Order enforced it from the outside, and the valley council enforced it from within, and between the two of them, Hollowmere stayed quiet as a held breath.

Which meant that if prayers were landing somewhere in this valley — and three separate reports, cross-checked against courier logs, said they were — then either the wards were failing, or someone had found a way through them, or something worse than both.

"Galloway," she said. "Where were you on the night of the ninth?"

"Asleep."

"All night?"

"It was raining," he said. "I sleep beautifully in rain. Ask anyone."

"Who would I ask? You live alone."

A pause. A very small pause, but Odile had spent seventeen years listening for pauses the way other people listened for music.

"There's a cat," he said. "Sometimes. Not mine. It judges my sleeping from the windowsill. You could ask the cat."

Brannock made the strangled sound again.

* * *

Here is what Ser Odile Vane did not say, standing in the tidy workshop with her hand resting on the pommel of a sword she had not yet drawn:

Three years ago, in the town of Marrowgate, a woman had come to a carver's stall in the market square and commissioned a headstone. Her name had been Vessa. She was a knight of the Ashen Order, out of uniform, on leave, and she had cried the whole time she described what to carve — a wren, wings folded, perched on a branch above the words *she was only seven*. Her daughter. Fever. The kind of death the valley's peace was supposed to make impossible, and hadn't.

And the carver — a lean man with sawdust in his hair, working a market stall far from any valley — had listened without once looking away, and when she finished he had said, quietly, "Tell me about her laugh. Not what she laughed at. What it sounded like."

Nobody had ever asked Odile Vane that question. She had talked for an hour. About the laugh, and then about everything else, because it turned out that grief is a door and once it opens everything behind it comes pouring through. The carver had carved the wren while she talked, unhurried, and when she left she had pressed silver into his hand and he had pressed the first shaving back — a curl of pale wood, smooth as a ribbon — and said, "For the pocket. When the stone's too heavy."

She still carried it. It lived in the lining of her gauntlet, which was a regulation violation and a sentimentality and the single most honest thing about her.

She had never learned his name. She had never needed to. And now here he was, three years later, in the godless valley at the center of the Order's oldest maps, leaning against a workbench and lying to her badly about a cat.

*The gods are dead,* she thought, with the flat clarity that came to her sometimes, uninvited, in the space between one duty and the next. *Or the wards are failing. Or both. And the man who carved my daughter's wren is standing in the middle of it.*

Duty said: search the house. Duty said: open the box on the workbench, whatever it holds. Duty said: the Order does not permit hesitation, and hesitation is how heresy spreads, and Ser, you have read the same ledgers we all have.

Memory said: *tell me about her laugh.*

Odile Vane looked at the box. Then she looked at Silas Galloway, and she watched him watch her look at the box, and she understood — with a certainty that settled over her like snowfall — that whatever was in it would change everything, and that once she opened it there would be no closing it again.

"Brannock," she said. "Walk the perimeter. Check the boundary stones on the north lane. Reports mention disturbances near the wardline."

"Ser, shouldn't I—"

"*Perimeter,* Ser Brannock."

He went. The door closed behind him, and the room exhaled.

"You know," Galloway said, when the footsteps faded, "most knights don't send their muscle away."

"Most knights aren't me." She crossed to the workbench. She did not touch the box. Instead she picked up a carving knife, turned it over, set it down. "Marrowgate. Three years ago. Market square. You carved a wren."

Something went out of him — the performance, the careful weightlessness of an innocent man. What remained underneath was tired, and human, and watching her with new eyes.

"You're the mother," he said.

"I'm the knight," Odile said. "The mother was on leave that week. She doesn't get leave anymore." She turned to face him fully. "So. Prayers are landing on your workbench. Written ones. Am I close?"

His jaw worked. "How—"

"Because the reports describe answered prayers, and answered prayers require an answerer, and every answerer in this valley is supposed to be three hundred years dead or three hundred years banished. So either you're a fraud running a very strange con on people who have nothing worth stealing—" she tapped the box, once, lightly, "—or you're something the founders didn't plan for. Which is it, carver?"

For a long moment he said nothing. Then, slowly, he lifted the lid of the box.

Inside, on a bed of cedar shavings, lay bones. A femur, half-carved, its surface already blooming with delicate filigree. And scattered among the shavings, dozens of small slips of paper — no, not paper. She bent closer. Bark. Thin-shaved bark, covered in cramped, desperate handwriting.

Prayers. Dozens of them. *Please. Please. My son won't wake up. Please. Is anyone there. Please.*

"They started arriving eight days ago," Galloway said quietly. "Not addressed to me. Addressed to — whoever. To the old names. The forbidden ones. They land in my shavings like leaves. I don't know how. I don't know why me." He looked up, and his eyes were the eyes of a drowning man who has just realized the shore is further than he thought. "But I can tell you one thing, Ser Knight, and you can put it in your ledger however you like. Whatever's answering them isn't answering kindly. I heard a drowned child through a bone last week. Magic doesn't cross the wardline. Everyone knows that. So either I'm mad, or the rules have changed, and I don't think I'm mad."

"No," Odile said. "I don't think you're mad either."

She straightened. Seventeen years of discipline came down over her face like a visor, and she felt him register the change, and she hated how easily it came, and she hated more that it had to.

"Here is what happens now," she said. "I report to the chapter-house that I investigated the bone-carver Galloway and found nothing but a licensed craftsman and an untidy rumor. I log the prayer-reports as misdirected pilgrim mail — it happens, at the wardline crossings, more than the Order admits. And you—" she fixed him with a look, "—you will burn those bark-prayers, all of them, and you will stop carving anything that listens, and you will tell no one. Not the council. Not your neighbors. Especially not whoever drank from that second cup."

Galloway glanced at the table. At the two cups. Back at her.

"There's no second cup," he said.

"There are two cups."

"...The cat drinks tea," he said. "It's a long story."

"Carver." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice to something that wasn't a knight's voice at all, just a woman's, tired, three years heavy. "If the Order learns what's happening here, they won't send one knight and her sycophant. They'll send Custodians. They'll send Marrow. And whatever is answering those prayers will get exactly what it wants, which is a valley full of frightened, praying people. Do you understand me? Fear is fertilizer. I've seen what grows in it."

Something flickered across his face — recognition, maybe, or resistance. "And what do you want, Ser Vane? For this valley? For yourself?"

What she wanted was impossible. What she wanted was to go back three years and sit in a market square forever, talking about a child's laugh while wood curled away under a stranger's knife. What she wanted was an Order worth serving and a world where seven-year-olds didn't die of fevers and prayers landed somewhere soft.

What she said was: "I want the truth to stay buried until I'm the one holding the shovel."

* * *

She found Brannock at the north lane, crouched by a boundary stone, wearing the expression of a man who has found something and wishes profoundly that he hadn't.

"Ser." He didn't stand. "You need to see this."

The stone was ancient, taller than a man, furred with moss. The old letters crawled across its face in the founder-script that no living scholar could read. But that wasn't what Brannock had found.

At the base of the stone, the earth was disturbed. Freshly. And in the fresh-turned soil, half-buried, lay a bone — long, yellowed with age, and carved. Covered, edge to edge, in the same founder-script as the stone itself, except that the carving was newer. Much newer. Days old, at most.

And where the script touched the wardline — where the stone's shadow fell across it — the moss was dead. A perfect line of brown, a finger's width wide, running the length of the boundary like a scar.

"Magic's leaking," Brannock said. His voice had lost its menace entirely; what remained was young, and scared. "Through the stones. Through *that.* Ser, the wards are supposed to be permanent. The founders—"

"I know what the founders built," Odile said.

She picked up the carved bone. It was warm. Bones in a godless valley, in autumn shade, should have been cold as riverbed stone, and this one was warm as a sleeping animal, and against her palm she felt — or imagined she felt, and would spend many sleepless nights deciding which — a faint rhythm. Like a pulse. Like breathing. Like something vast turning over in its sleep and murmuring a name.

She couldn't make out the name. But it had two syllables, and it sounded almost like *Silas.*

"Ser?" Brannock was watching her. "Orders?"

Odile Vane looked down the north lane, toward the chapter-house where her report was due by nightfall. Toward the ledgers she had read and reread and never quite believed. Toward the life she had built out of duty because duty was the only thing that had held after Vessa died.

Then she looked back toward the village, at the thin thread of chimney smoke rising from a carver's roof, and thought: *tell me about her laugh.*

"Log the stone as weather damage," she said. "The bone goes in my saddlebag. And Brannock — the investigation is concluded. Nothing here but rumors and rain."

He stared at her. "Ser, the moss—"

"Weather damage," Odile said, in the voice that had made hardened smugglers weep, "is what the ledger will say."

He wrote it down. He was well-trained. She wondered, distantly, how long that would last — how long any of them would stay well-trained, once the valley's held breath finally came out.

In her gauntlet, against her wrist, the little wooden wren seemed warmer than usual. Or perhaps that was the bone in her saddlebag, breathing its slow two-syllable secret. Or perhaps — and this was the thought she rode back with, the thought that would keep her armor feeling heavier than regulation — perhaps the whole valley had begun, very quietly, to wake.

* * *

That night, in the chapter-house at the valley's edge, Ser Odile Vane sat down to write her report.

*Investigated subject Galloway, S., bone-carver, licensed. Found no evidence of heretical practice. Rumors attributable to pilgrim mail misdirection at wardline crossings. Recommend continued standard observation.*

She read it over twice. It was clean. It was professional. It was, in every particular that mattered, a lie — and beneath it, in the space where her signature would go, her hand hovered, and shook, and did not move.

On the desk beside the report lay the carved bone from the boundary stone, wrapped in her cloak. It pulsed. Faintly. Patiently. Like something that knew it had all the time in the world.

Outside, far off across the dark fields, a light burned in a carver's window. And Odile Vane, knight of the Ashen Order, keeper of the valley's oldest lie, dipped her pen and signed her name to the falsehood — then added, in small letters beneath it, where only a reader paying very close attention would ever notice:

*One matter remains unresolved. Requesting extended assignment to Hollowmere.*

*Personal reasons.*

She blotted the page. Somewhere in the walls, or in the bone, or in the space between — something laughed.
