## 4. What the Bones Remember

The relic bone arrived on a Tuesday, which Silas Galloway would later think was typical of the whole affair. Catastrophe, in their experience, rarely had the decency to schedule itself for a dramatic evening. It came on market days, at supper, in the middle of mending a fence post—ordinary hours, when a person's guard was down and their hands were busy.

This particular Tuesday, the bone came wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string that had been knotted by someone who cared very much about knots.

Silas set it on the workbench beside the morning's shavings and regarded it the way one regards a sleeping wasp. The bone was long as a forearm, dense, and the color of old ivory gone faintly amber at the joints. It was not cow, not pig, not deer, not any of the honest animals Silas had spent thirty years carving into memorials and keepsakes. The valley of Hollowmere had no word for what it *was*, because no one in living memory had seen its like—and yet every instinct Silas possessed, the same instincts that let them hear the dead through fresh-carved bone, stood up and took three steps backward.

"Right," Silas said to the empty workshop. "You're not going to tell me anything useful, are you."

The workshop, being empty, did not answer. This had been the arrangement between Silas and their gift for most of their life: they heard things, and they told absolutely no one, and in exchange the world left them alone to drink weak tea and complain about damp weather like a normal person.

The bone had come from Tam Hesk's cart, passed along with Tam's usual discretion—which was to say he'd set it on the doorstep, knocked twice, and been halfway back to his mule before Silas opened the door.

"Found it at the valley's edge," Tam had said over his shoulder, not slowing. "Where the stones go strange. Digger up from Marsh's lot turned it out of the earth. Nobody'll touch it. Thought maybe you'd know what to do with it."

*What to do with it.* As though it were a stray dog. As though there existed some civic office in Hollowmere for Unusual Bones, and Silas held the post.

Silas untied the string. The knots came apart too easily, as if the bone wanted unwrapping, and that was the second wrong thing about it. The first wrong thing had been the weight. Bone should be light-ish, forgiving, full of the memory of whatever it had carried—a leg, a wing, a life. This bone was heavy the way a bell is heavy. Heavy with intent.

They turned it under the lamplight. Along its length ran fine channels, natural-looking, the sort of grooves you'd find in any long bone—but these were arranged in patterns. Repeating. Deliberate. Not carved, exactly. *Grown*, if bone could be grown into meaning.

Silas had carved a femur last week for a drowned child, and heard her last words through the shavings—*tell my mum I wasn't scared*—which was impossible, because magic could not cross Hollowmere's borders. The wards saw to that. Everyone knew the wards. The wards were why the valley was quiet, safe, godless; why the Ashen Order tolerated them; why nothing holy or unholy ever troubled the fog-line. Silas had spent four sleepless nights deciding the child's voice must have come *with* her, trapped somehow, carried across the border inside her small body before the wards closed around the valley like a fist.

It had been a comforting theory. Silas had held onto it the way a drowning man holds a plank that is visibly on fire.

Now they picked up their smallest knife—the one they used for detail work, the one with the worn walnut handle their mother had owned—and looked at the relic bone for a long moment.

"Don't do anything foolish," said the sensible voice in their head, which sounded remarkably like their late aunt Maren, who had never once in her life done anything foolish and had died comfortable, dry, and profoundly bored.

"I'm always foolish," Silas told her, and touched knife to bone.

The first curling shaving fell away, and the workshop went silent—not quiet, *silent*, the kind of silence where sound has been removed rather than merely absent. Even the rain on the roof stopped mid-drop. Silas felt their own heartbeat pause between beats, hanging there, undecided.

Then the bone screamed.

Not through the ears. Through the bones themselves—every bone in Silas's body ringing like struck glass, and pouring into that ringing came *voices*, hundreds of them, thousands, layered and vast and terrible, and none of them were human.

They were singing. That was the worst part, the part Silas would carry for the rest of their life: they were *singing*. A chorus so enormous that a single note of it would have filled a cathedral, harmonies stacked on harmonies going up past hearing into registers that made Silas's teeth ache and their eyes water. Joyful. Triumphant. The sound of beings doing whatever it is gods do on an ordinary day in eternity—no grief in it, no warning, just vast and glad and utterly unguarded.

And then, without transition, without even a breath between:

Fire.

The song tore. Silas heard it tear the way you'd hear cloth rip, except the cloth was the sky and the rip went all the way down. Voices broke mid-note. Something immense fell somewhere impossibly far above, and the falling took years, and while it fell it sang a different song—a thin, high, wounded keening that Silas recognized with a lurch because it was the sound the drowned child had almost made, the sound of someone realizing, in their last moments, that no one was coming.

*No one is coming,* said a voice like deep water. *No one is coming. No one—*

*Hold the line,* said another, cracked and furious, a soldier-god's voice. *Hold, hold, we are eternal, we cannot—we cannot—*

*We can,* whispered a third, small and wondering, and the wonder in it broke Silas's heart more cleanly than any scream. *Oh. We can die.*

*They are cutting the roots,* said the water-voice. *Brother, sister, they have found the roots—*

And then names. Silas understood, dimly, through the pain and the noise pressing against the inside of their skull, that they were hearing names—true ones, the kind of names that are less labels than essences, syllables that meant *the tide that remembers* and *the lamp at the road's turning* and *the patience of stone*. And around each name, like hands closing around a throat, something else: chains. Chains of words. Prayers, inverted. Worship, bent backward, used not to feed but to *bind*.

The founders. The thought arrived complete and cold, wearing no emotion at all, which was how Silas knew it was true. *The founders of Hollowmere.*

The vision—because it was a vision now, Silas was somewhere else, standing in a place that was not a place, watching—showed them a circle of robed figures at the valley's heart, where the council hall stood today, where the market square smelled of bread and horse dung every seventh day. The figures' faces were blurred, deliberately, as if the memory itself refused to look at them. But their hands were visible. Their hands were steady. They worked with the unhurried competence of people doing a difficult job well, and the job was the murder of everything holy, and they were *good* at it.

One by one, the great voices went out. Each death changed the shape of the world—Silas felt it, a lurch like a stair missing in the dark—as if reality had been a table standing on many legs and now stood on fewer, wobbling, learning to balance wrong.

The last god to fall did not fight. It was small compared to the others—or it seemed small, the way a candle seems small next to a bonfire—and it did not scream or curse. It looked, Silas thought, directly at the circle of killers, and then—there was no other way to say it—it *hid*. Folded itself down and down and down, out of the light, into the smallest vessel available, into blood and bone and the ordinary stubborn persistence of a family line, and vanished into the future like a seed into winter soil.

*Find me,* it seemed to whisper, not to its murderers. To the dark. To whoever might someday be listening. *I hid. Find me.*

Then silence again, real silence, and Silas was on the floor of their own workshop with no memory of sitting down, cheek pressed to the cool boards, shavings scattered around them like pale petals.

* * *

They lay there for a while. It seemed only polite, given the circumstances. Aunt Maren's voice, when it returned, was unusually subdued.

"Well," she said. "*That* was foolish."

"That," Silas agreed hoarsely, "was the truth."

They got up in stages, tested their limbs, found everything attached and mostly obedient. The relic bone sat on the bench where they'd left it, half-shaved, its channels exposed like the rings of a felled tree. It looked smaller now. Spent. Whatever it had carried, it had delivered.

Silas poured tea because tea was what one did when the foundations of one's world shifted, and drank it standing at the window, watching the fog lie along the valley floor like a patient animal.

Everything reorganized itself while they drank. That was the horror of it—not the screaming, not the dying, though both would revisit Silas at 3 a.m. for weeks. The horror was how neatly everything fit. How the story of Hollowmere, the story Silas had swallowed whole with their mother's milk, slid into its new shape with barely a click.

The wards kept magic out. That was the catechism. The founders, fleeing a wicked and wonder-ridden world, had raised a wall of quiet, and inside it the valley lived plain and godless and free. The Ashen Order enforced the exile—for everyone's protection, they said. No prayers crossed the border. No miracles. No voices from beyond. Peace.

Except magic hadn't stayed out. Magic had gotten *in*—in Silas's own two hands, in the drowned girl's voice, and now in a relic bone dug from the valley's edge. And the wards hadn't stopped any of it. Which meant the wards had never been a wall at all.

They were a lid.

Hollowmere wasn't a refuge. It was a grave, and the founders had built their tidy houses on top of the body, and raised generations of children to call the silence *peace*. Three hundred years of peace. Three hundred years of the dead gods' blood soaking into the soil under the barley fields, under the churchyard, under the council hall where respectable people discussed drainage and festival budgets atop the murder site of heaven.

And the Order. The Order with its ash-grey cloaks and its solemn faces, riding the border, turning back pilgrims, confiscating charms, burning—what had they actually been burning? Silas thought of Ser Odile Vane, who had knelt once at a grave Silas carved and wept with a grief too raw and too specific to be performance. Had she known? Did they all know? Was the whole grey brotherhood one long conspiracy of janitors, keeping the crime scene clean?

Or—worse, in a way—had the knowledge rotted over centuries, until the jailers forgot there'd ever been prisoners, and simply patrolled the walls out of habit?

Silas set down the cup. Their hands were steady, which surprised them. Inside, they felt like the bone had sounded: torn, ringing, rearranged.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing that made them laugh, once, short and unamused, alone in the lamplight.

They weren't afraid of the gift anymore. They had spent their entire life afraid of it—hearing the dead, flinching from shavings, keeping careful invisibility like a second cloak. And it turned out the gift wasn't a curse or an oddity. It was *evidence*. It was the one thread of truth left dangling from the founders' careful knot. The wards hadn't suppressed the gift. Nothing could have suppressed it, because it hadn't leaked through from outside. It had grown here, from here—from a seed a dying god had planted in a family line, whispering *find me* down through the generations until it reached a bone-carver in Hollowmere who just wanted to drink their tea in peace.

"Find me," Silas repeated softly. "Well. I found you. Now what?"

The question hung in the workshop. And then—Silas's blood went cold and slow—

Something answered.

Not from the bone. From everywhere and nowhere: a pressure against the edge of hearing, like a face pressed against frosted glass. Faint. Patient. Familiar in a way Silas couldn't name, the way you know footsteps on your own stairs.

*...found...*

One word. Barely a word. More like the shape a word leaves behind after it dies.

The lamplight guttered though there was no draft. Outside, the fog along the lane stirred without wind, and for half a heartbeat Silas saw—or thought they saw—a figure standing at the garden gate. Small. Indistinct. Looking at the house with the particular attention of someone who has been looking for a very long time.

Then the gate stood empty, and the rain resumed mid-drop as though it had never stopped, and Silas Galloway stood alone in their workshop holding a dead god's bone and the absolute certainty that tomorrow, the world outside their door was going to want things from them.

They looked down at the shavings on the bench. Among them, pale against the pale curls of wood, lay a scrap of paper that had not been there before. Written prayers, arriving among the bone dust—Wren's voice, later, would explain that. For now Silas only read it, and felt the floor tilt beneath everything they believed about home.

Three words, in a hand that shook:

*It knows your name.*
