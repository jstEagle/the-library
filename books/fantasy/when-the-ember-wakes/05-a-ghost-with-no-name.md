## 5. A Ghost With No Name

Wren had been stealing bread in Hollowmere for exactly four days, and they were getting worse at it, not better.

That was the trouble with guilt. It made your hands loud. Back at the Ashen cloister, Wren's hands had been the quietest part of them—hands built for copying, for the small precise labor of transcribing prayers that other people were too important to write themselves. Those hands could lift a candle from under a sleeping brother's nose and leave a written apology in its place. But four days of running, four days of eating food bought with a purse that wasn't theirs, and now their fingers trembled like a first-year scribe's.

They stood in the market's edge with half a loaf down the front of their coat and no memory of having taken it.

"You're doing it wrong," said the baker's boy, not looking up from his ledger.

"I'm—what?"

"Stealing. You're doing it wrong." He was perhaps twelve, freckled, profoundly unbothered. "You're supposed to look bored. You look like you're about to be sick. Also you've got crusts coming out of your sleeve."

Wren looked down. Crusts. A whole constellation of them, drifting from their cuff onto the cobblestones like terrible snow.

"I'll pay," Wren said, which was a lie, because their purse contained two coppers and a button.

"Mm." The boy finally looked up, considered them with the weary patience of someone who had inherited both an oven and all of its problems. "Third one this week who's come through here looking haunted. You're all the same. Big eyes, bad coats, that walk like the ground owes you money."

"The ground doesn't owe me money."

"No? Then why do you walk like it does?" He tore a heel off a fresh loaf, wrapped it in cloth, and held it out. "Take this instead. The one down your shirt is stale anyway; I was going to feed it to the ducks."

"Why would you—" Wren took the bread because their hands had decided before the rest of them did. "Why would you give me this?"

The boy shrugged and went back to his ledger. "You looked hungry. And you're funny-looking when you're scared. Two good reasons."

* * *

It happened that evening, at the edge of the millpond, where Wren had gone to eat their charity-bread in peace and review, for the hundredth time, the thing they'd stolen that mattered more than any loaf.

They had taken pages. Not copies—the originals. Eleven sheets in a hand they knew better than their own heartbeat: prayer-requests routed through the Order's receiving desk, each one marked *unanswered*, each one bearing the same notation in High Custodian Marrow's own ink. *Reroute. Deep archive.* Prayers meant for gods, redirected somewhere below the earth. Wren had read those notations every day for three years and thought nothing of it, until the day they'd thought everything of it, all at once, and walked out of the cloister with the evidence folded against their ribs.

Now they sat on the millpond's flat stone and read the pages by moonlight for the dozenth time, hunting for what they might have missed.

"You won't find it there," said a voice.

Wren did not scream, which they counted later as a personal triumph. What they did was fall backward off the stone into six inches of freezing pond water, holding the pages above the surface with both hands like a drowning scholar rescuing scripture.

The voice belonged to a duck.

Not a talking duck. That distinction took Wren several horrible seconds to establish. The duck stood on the bank, opening and closing its bill, and what came out was not quacking but words—dry, unhurried, faintly apologetic words, spoken through a beak that clearly had no business forming them.

"I'm sorry about that," said the duck. "I forget how much it startles people. I should have led with something gentler. A cow, perhaps. Cows have calming faces."

Wren scrambled upright, dripping, pages clutched high. "What are you?"

"A fair question. I've been asking it myself." The duck's black eye regarded them without malice. "I don't remember. That's rather the whole of my situation. I'm here, I think, and I speak, but only through things that already have mouths. Or beaks. This gentleman was kind enough to lend me his."

"This is—I can't be having this conversation with a duck."

"Of course not. Shall I try the reeds? They don't talk back as well, but they whistle nicely in the wind."

"It's not the *medium* I object to, it's the entire—there is a dead person talking to me through waterfowl."

"Ah." Something in the duck's stillness changed, a settling, the way a house settles at night. "Yes. Dead. I suppose I am. It explains a great deal, actually. Thank you. You've solved in ten seconds a puzzle I've been chewing for—" a pause, and the beak hung open while whatever spoke through it counted something it couldn't count, "—for some time."

Wren's teeth were chattering. Some of it was cold. "Why me?"

"I followed the prayers." The duck turned its head with a slow, mechanical grace. "Eleven of them. Little burning threads, all leading to the same place, all leading to *you*. You carry them. So I followed you. And then today you sat down by my pond and read them aloud under your breath, and I heard my own—" the beak stopped working, opened, closed, tried again, "—I heard something I recognized. Something that was taken from me."

Wren looked down at the wet pages in their shaking hands.

"What did you hear?"

"A word. My word. The one that was mine before—" The duck ruffled its feathers in what might have been frustration or grief or simply a duck being a duck. "I don't know how to explain what I don't remember. Imagine waking up and everyone has taken your name away, and also your name was the only thing keeping you *you*. Now imagine that instead of one thief there was a very large, very patient one, and it eats names the way I eat—well. Whatever I eat."

"Bread," Wren said weakly. "Ducks eat bread."

"So I'm told. I haven't been able to test it."

And despite everything—the cold, the fear, the eleven stolen prayers heavy as stones against their ribs—Wren laughed. A short, cracked, ridiculous laugh, the first one in weeks, aimed at a possessed duck by a millpond in a valley where magic wasn't supposed to exist.

The duck seemed pleased. Its feathers smoothed.

"There it is," it said. "You laugh like someone who hasn't in a while. I know the sound. I think I used to make it."

* * *

The duck—it refused all nicknames, insisting that accepting one would make the absence of its real name permanent—led Wren, at a waddling pace, across half of Hollowmere by moonlight. To the bone-carver's shop at the crook of the lane, where lamplight still burned past midnight.

"He hears them too," the duck had said. "The dead. He thinks he's the only one, and he's spent his whole life being careful about it, and careful people are lonely in a way they never admit. Knock. Tell him the bones sent you."

So Wren stood in the cold outside Silas Galloway's door with wet boots and stolen pages and a duck at their ankle, and knocked, and when the door opened a crack—guarded, tired, the face of a man who had learned to expect nothing good from visitors—they said, "Your bones sent me," and watched him go pale and then gray and then, strangely, almost relieved.

Now, an hour later, the three of them were crowded around the workbench: Wren damp and shivering in a borrowed blanket that smelled of wood shavings; Silas turning the eleven prayer-pages over with hands that handled paper the way Wren handled knives—like it might go off; and the duck, perched on a shelf between a skull and a jar of something ominous, speaking through its borrowed bill in the lamplight.

"Say it again," Silas said quietly. "The warning. Exactly as you said it to Wren."

The duck sighed—a strange sight, a sigh with a beak. "*Something is wearing the voices.* That's all I have. No shape to it, no face. Only that when the prayers began to burn, I went to listen, the way I used to listen, and what answered wasn't—" The beak worked uselessly. "You must understand, I knew these voices. Knew them the way you know your own cough. And what I heard wore them the way a thief wears a stolen coat. Right fit. Wrong man inside."

Silas set down the pages. His face had gone very still, the way faces go when someone is holding a door shut behind it.

"When I carve," he said, "I hear the dead. Always have. Small things, mostly. Last words. Grumbles. Once, a truly vicious argument about a fence line." He almost smiled. "Lately the voices have been... louder. Nearer. Like something leaning against the wall between us, trying to get its shoulder through. I told myself it was the wards failing."

"And now?" asked the duck.

"Now I think the wards aren't failing." Silas looked at the prayer-pages, at the notation Wren had copied into the margin for him—*reroute, deep archive*—in his own careful transcription. "I think something down there has gotten strong enough to press. And it's using the voices of the dead as its hands."

"That's what I came to say." The duck hopped down to the bench, absurdly dignified. "Whatever answers those prayers—and something does answer them, I felt the pull of it, hungry as a drain—it isn't your grandmother. It isn't anyone's grandmother. It is wearing them. And it will wear anything else it finds useful. Voices. Faces." The black eye turned, slowly, deliberately, toward Wren. "Names."

The room went quiet enough that Wren could hear the fire ticking in the grate and their own heart doing something undignified.

"You think it knows about me," Wren said. "About the pages."

"I think," said the duck, "that you stole the only honest record of what it's been fed. I think that makes you either very brave or very stupid, and I've noticed those often look identical from the outside." It paused. "I mean that kindly. I've been both."

Silas laughed—one short note, surprised out of him—and then pressed his knuckles to his mouth like he was embarrassed to have done it.

"We should be clear about what we are," he said finally. "Because I've lived thirty years in this valley learning to be nobody, and in one week I've become a heretic, a fugitive's accomplice, and a man taking theological advice from a duck. I'd like to know if we're allies or just three disasters sheltering from the same rain."

"Four," said the duck. "Don't forget the gentleman whose mouth I'm borrowing. He seems a steady sort."

"Allies," Wren said. It came out faster than they meant it to, raw-edged. They pulled the blanket tighter and made themself keep talking, because three years in the cloister had taught them that the unsaid things were the ones that rotted. "I don't—I should tell you both what I am. Before you decide. I was a scribe for the Ashen Order. I didn't steal these pages out of heroism. I stole them because I wrote out the routing orders for years and told myself they were filing. Filing. Thousands of prayers, real people's worst nights, rerouted into a hole in the ground, and I *filed them*. I only ran when I finally couldn't not-know anymore. So if you want to hand me to Odile Vane's order and be done with it, I'd understand, and I wouldn't even—"

"Wren." Silas said it gently, the way you'd calm a spooked animal. "I hear dead people through bits of carved femur. Nobody in this room gets to be the normal one."

The laugh that escaped Wren was wet and ugly and real.

"And for the record," Silas went on, turning back to the pages, "guilt that keeps you up at night is worth more than innocence that sleeps fine. I've met plenty of the second kind. They build valleys." Something crossed his face when he said it—some private, grim arithmetic—but he didn't share it. "Allies. Fine. But we do it properly. We pool what we know. You start with the Order's records—"

"The deep archive," Wren said, sitting forward. "Marrow's notation. If the prayers are being rerouted somewhere physical, there's a place. Under something. The old custodians' wing, maybe, or beneath the chapel—"

"—and I start listening harder," Silas said. "On purpose, this time. If something's pressing against the wall, I want to know what shape its shoulder is."

"And I," announced the duck, "will continue being dead and unhelpful, though I've grown rather fond of the role."

Wren looked around the little lamplit shop—at the shavings on the floor, the skull on the shelf, the blanket around their shoulders that a stranger had given them without being asked twice—and felt something loosen in their chest that had been clenched since the night they'd climbed the cloister wall with pages hidden against their ribs. It wasn't safety. They weren't fools. It was smaller than safety and stranger: the specific warmth of being *included in a plan*. Of having a bench to sit on and a job to do and two beings—man and bird—who knew the worst of them and hadn't reached for the door.

Three years of copying other people's prayers in silence, and it had taken a possessed duck to get anyone to listen to theirs.

"We'll need a signal," Wren said, because plans were easier than feelings. "If any of us learns something. We can't be seen meeting."

Silas nodded slowly. "There's a hollow oak behind the tannery. Nobody goes there. Leave chalk marks on the north side—one slash for meet, two for danger."

"Three for 'the duck has news,'" said the duck.

"Can you even leave a place? Can you—travel?"

A long pause. The duck's head tilted, considering its own existence with visible distaste.

"I follow the prayers," it said at last. "And lately, apparently, I follow you. Wherever the threads lead. Which means, I suppose, that where you three go, I go." It ruffled its feathers, settled, and added, with what sounded suspiciously like satisfaction: "Congratulations. You appear to have acquired a ghost."

The fire ticked. Somewhere out in the lane, a dog barked at nothing—or at something only dogs and dead things could see—and Silas rose to bank the coals, and for a moment the shop was just a warm room with three odd souls in it, planning in whispers like children.

Then Silas stopped mid-motion, his hand hovering over the hearth.

"Wren," he said, and his voice had changed entirely—all the warmth packed away, replaced by something careful and cold. "When you copied Marrow's notation. *Deep archive.* Did the Order's records ever say what the archive was *built from*?"

"No. Why?"

"Because I carved a relic bone last week," Silas said slowly, "from the valley's edge. Old beyond old. And what I heard screaming inside it—" He turned to face them, lamplight catching the lines of his face. "Wasn't one voice pretending to be many. It was many voices, screaming *together*, like they'd been fused. Like something down there is made of them."

The duck went utterly still.

"Wearing the voices," it whispered through its borrowed beak. "I said it as a figure of speech. I didn't dream it was literal. I didn't dream it had a *body*."

Outside, far across the sleeping valley, in the direction of the Ashen cloister and whatever lay beneath it, a bell began to ring—not the alarm bell, not the hour bell, but the deep calling-bell that Wren had heard exactly once before, on the night a custodian died. Someone was summoning the Order. At midnight. In force.

Wren looked at Silas. Silas looked at the duck.

"Two slashes," Wren said quietly, reaching for their boots, "means danger. Is there a mark for *it's starting*?"
