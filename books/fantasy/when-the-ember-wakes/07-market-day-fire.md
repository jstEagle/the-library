## 7. Market Day Fire

Market day in Hollowmere began the way all good things did: with someone arguing about turnips.

Silas Galloway had a stall at the far end of the square, wedged between the candle-dipper and a man who sold honey and opinions in equal measure. It was not, strictly speaking, legal for a bone-carver to sell at market—the council preferred that Silas's trade stay quiet, tucked behind a workshop door where no one had to think about it—but Old Marta had declared years ago that anyone who carved a memorial for her drowned grandson could sell whatever they liked wherever they liked, council be hanged. Nobody argued with Old Marta. She was ninety-one and had buried three husbands and one husband's ghost, though she told that last story differently every time.

So Silas sat behind a folding table of small carvings—whistles for children, buttons, little round beads of sheep bone polished smooth as river stones—and let the morning wash over them.

The square smelled of woodsmoke and frying onions. Wren was there somewhere, Silas knew, because Wren was always there on market days now, drifting through the crowd with their scribe's ink-stained fingers and their habit of standing too close to people while memorizing what they said. The nameless ghost drifted after Wren like a dog that had decided, without consulting anyone, that this person was its person.

"You're not selling those whistles," said the honey-seller, whose name was Bram and who had never once been right about anything. "Children don't want whistles anymore. They want toys that move."

"They are toys that move," Silas said. "They move air."

Bram considered this with the gravity of a man judging scripture. "That's not moving. That's blowing."

"Everything is blowing, if you're honest about it."

Bram laughed so hard he knocked over his own honey jars, which Silas privately considered the day's first miracle.

It was a good morning. That was the thing Silas would remember afterward, the thing that would sit in their chest like a swallowed stone: it was an ordinary, unremarkable, *good* morning, and nobody knew it was the last one.

* * *

The pilgrim arrived around noon.

Hollowmere didn't get pilgrims. That was rather the point of Hollowmere—a valley warded into silence three centuries ago, where prayers went nowhere and gods were a rumor from before grandmothers' grandmothers. But sometimes travelers came through anyway, drawn by the same vague itch that drew moths to windows, and the valley tolerated them the way a house tolerates weather.

This one came up the east road alone, dusty, gray-cloaked, walking with the careful steps of someone very tired or very old. He stopped at the well in the center of the square and stood there, hands folded, head bowed.

Praying, Silas realized. Actually praying. To whom, in a valley where prayer was as useful as shouting down a well?

Silas felt it before they understood it—that prickle along the spine, the one that had started weeks ago, the one that meant bone-things were listening nearby. Their workbench at home had been filling with written prayers for days now, appearing among the shavings like frost forming on glass. Prayers addressed to gods the valley forbade naming. Prayers that someone—or something—had apparently been answering.

Wren appeared at Silas's elbow so suddenly that Silas jumped and nearly swept their whistles off the table.

"That's him," Wren whispered. They were pale under their freckles. "That's the pilgrim from the shrine road. I saw him two nights ago, kneeling at the broken waystone, and he was—" Wren's throat worked. "He was talking in voices, Silas. Plural. Like a room full of people using his mouth."

The ghost chose that moment to surface through the cobblestones beside them, which it did only when something was genuinely wrong, having learned—apparently—that materializing at mealtimes was poor manners. It had no face to speak of, being more suggestion than substance, but Silas had gotten fluent in its silences. This silence said: *danger.*

"He stopped," Silas murmured. "Look. He's stopped praying."

The pilgrim had gone still. Perfectly still, in the way living bodies aren't—not breathing, not swaying, just planted at the well like a fence post. Then his head turned. Not toward Silas. Toward the crowd. Slowly, joint by joint, like a door opening on rusted hinges.

And he smiled.

Silas had heard the dead speak through bone for their entire life. They had heard grief, terror, love, the small mundane complaints of the dying (*tell my wife the second spoon is hers, she'll know what I mean*). They thought they had heard everything a voice could carry.

They had never heard a voice made of many voices wearing one throat like a stolen coat.

"BURN THE OLD YEAR DOWN," the pilgrim said, pleasantly, at conversational volume, in a chorus of perhaps thirty overlapping tones—deep and bright, male and female, ancient and young, all saying the same words slightly out of step with each other. "THE HARVEST IS RIPE. THE SILENCE IS A LIE. BURN IT AND BE HEARD."

For one heartbeat, the whole square did nothing. That was the horror of it—three hundred years of trained stillness, a whole valley of people who had learned that strange things were best ignored until they went away.

Then the pilgrim raised one hand, and fire bloomed in it like a flower deciding to be rude.

* * *

Later, Silas would try to reconstruct the order of events and fail. Memory, they decided, was a bone that broke clean when you needed it whole.

What they knew: the fire left the pilgrim's hand and touched the candle-dipper's stall, and the candle-dipper's stall—wax and tallow and dried rushes—went up like it had been waiting its whole life for permission. What they knew: the crowd's stillness shattered into screaming, and screaming crowds move like water, which is to say everywhere at once and mostly downhill. What they knew: the smoke came fast and low and black, and within thirty seconds the square was a blind place, full of coughing shapes.

What Silas knew, that no one else did: they could hear through it.

Not see—hear. Every bone in the square was singing to them. Panic has a sound in the skeleton; Silas had heard it in drowning victims pulled from the mere, in the old men who collapsed at harvest. Fear rattles the ribs. Grief hollows the sternum. And children—children's bones cry out in small sharp notes, high and clear, like struck flint.

Through the smoke, Silas heard six of them.

One was under the candle-dipper's overturned cart, curled small, ribs heaving with sobs. Two were pressed into the corner behind Bram's stall, holding each other, heartbeats hammering so fast their breastbones blurred. One was lost near the well itself, stumbling in circles, too frightened to move toward any voice. One was up—gods, *up*—clinging to the lintel of the baker's awning where she'd climbed to escape the crush and now couldn't get down with fire licking the canvas above her.

And one was very close, closer than the rest, and its bones were going quiet. Going still. Smoke-hushed, breath failing, a small body shutting down in the dark.

Silas moved.

They didn't decide to move; the body simply went, table overturning, whistles scattering—and here was the absurd grace note Silas would cling to later, the detail that kept the memory from being pure nightmare: as Silas ran into the smoke, they stepped on their own scattered merchandise, and half a dozen bone whistles shrieked at once under their boots, a wild discordant chorus, like the valley's whole dead past clearing its throat.

"HERE!" Silas bellowed, to no one, to everyone. "CHILDREN, MAKE NOISE! SCREAM IF YOU CAN HEAR ME!"

And because children are wise in the ways that matter—they screamed.

Silas followed the sounds. Not the voice-sounds; the bone-sounds underneath, the true ones. They found the girl under the cart first, hooked their arms under her shoulders, and dragged her out through the gap beneath the axle, whispering nonsense—*there you are, there you are, hold my neck, you're doing beautifully*—the way they'd talked to corpses their whole life, except this one answered, which was new, which was *everything*. They deposited her at the fountain's edge where the air was cleaner and plunged back in.

The baker's awning was burning properly now. Silas climbed the rain barrel, reached up, and pried the clinging child off the lintel finger by finger, her tiny hands fierce as burrs. She was maybe five. She bit him. He loved her instantly.

"There's two more behind the honey stall," he gasped at Odile—who had appeared out of the smoke with her sword drawn and her Ashen gray cloak wrapped over her mouth, because of course she had; duty was a bone in that woman that even a burning market couldn't crack. "Corner by the wall. Go left of the flames."

"How do you know that?" Odile demanded, even as she moved.

"I'll explain never," Silas said, and went for the quiet one.

The sixth child was behind the well, in the pocket of dead air where the smoke pooled thickest. A boy, seven or eight, sitting perfectly still with his arms around his knees, lips blue, breath a thread. Silas gathered him up and felt the boy's ribs against his chest—felt them the way he felt all bones, as speech—and the ribs said, in their wordless way, *cold, dark, tired,* and Silas said back, aloud, into the boy's hair: "I've got you. You're found. You're found."

He carried the boy out into the light and the cold water of the fountain both at once, and the boy gasped like a landed fish and then howled, and Silas had never in their life been so glad to hear a child scream.

* * *

The fire burned itself out against the stone flags of the square, as fires in market squares do when the market is mostly stone and stubbornness. The bucket line—organized, inevitably, by Old Marta, who directed operations from a safe distance with the imperious calm of a woman who had survived worse husbands than fire—drowned the last of the candle-dipper's stall while the candle-dipper wept and swore vengeance on all things flammable.

The pilgrim stood exactly where he had stood.

The fire hadn't touched him. That was the detail that silenced the square, that turned three hundred neighbors from rescuers back into statues. Flames had crawled across the cobbles, around him, past him, and he stood in the middle of it untouched, gray cloak unsinged, smiling his pleasant smile, speaking his chorus:

"THEY ARE NOT DEAD. THEY ARE ONLY HUNGRY. FEED THEM AND THE VALLEY WILL SING AGAIN. WE MISS YOU. WE MISS YOU. WE MISS—"

Odile hit him with the flat of her sword.

Silas would learn later that she'd aimed for the temple with surgical care—not to kill, but to stop—and that the blow dropped the pilgrim like cut rope. The moment he fell, the chorus stopped. All of it, mid-word. And the man on the ground was just a man again, old and dusty and breathing hard, blinking up at the sky with wet, bewildered eyes.

"What—" he croaked, in one voice, only one, his own. "Where am I? I was on the road. I was walking. Why does my mouth taste of ash?"

Nobody answered him. Everybody was looking at Silas.

Because Silas was still standing in the fountain, soaked to the waist, a rescued child in each arm, and the whole square had seen it—had seen Silas walk blind into smoke that killed breath and come out six times, six times, unerring, straight to children no one else could find. Had heard, some of them, the whistles shrieking underfoot and the bone-carver talking softly to the dark as if the dark were answering.

Old Marta broke the silence, as Old Marta broke most things.

"Well," she said, to the square at large, in the tone of a woman settling an argument about turnips. "I always said the Galloway one was odd." A pause. "Useful, though."

A ripple of uneasy laughter. Then the crowd began to move, to murmur, to gather in knots and glance—at Silas, away, at Silas again. Wren pushed through to Silas's side, white-faced, and the ghost rose up out of the cobbles beside them both, visible now, unmistakably visible, to everyone.

No one screamed at the ghost. That somehow made it worse. The valley simply looked, and looked, and *remembered*, and Silas felt three hundred years of careful invisibility crumble like wet chalk.

Odile sheathed her sword and knelt beside the fallen pilgrim, checking his pulse, and when she looked up at Silas her expression was a locked door with smoke coming under it.

"We need to talk," she said quietly. "All of us. Tonight. Because that thing used his mouth, and I've read the Order's accounts of possession, and none of them say *chorus.* None of them say *many.*" Her jaw tightened. "Whatever answered your workbench prayers, Silas—it isn't one voice pretending to be gods."

"It's all of them," Silas said. The words came out before they could stop them, and they tasted of the relic-bone chorus, of gods screaming in the dark of the valley's edge. "Or what's left of all of them. Wearing whatever it can reach."

Across the square, the rescued girl who had bitten him was telling her mother, loudly and with great authority, that the bone-man had *sung to her bones*, and her mother was staring at Silas with an expression Silas couldn't read and didn't dare try to hear.

By nightfall, Silas knew, there would be a knock at the workshop door. Possibly the council's. Possibly the Order's. Possibly both, and possibly neither, and possibly something else entirely—because the pilgrim, before they carried him off to the healer's hut, had caught Silas's sleeve with sudden lucid strength and whispered, in his own single trembling voice:

"It knows your name now. It said it, in the fire. Didn't you hear? Everyone heard."

Silas hadn't heard. But lying awake that night, listening to the valley's bones settle and murmur, they finally caught it—faint, patient, threaded through the smoke-smell still clinging to their clothes, spoken in a chorus of thirty dead voices with terrible tenderness:

*Silas Galloway. We saved you a seat.*
