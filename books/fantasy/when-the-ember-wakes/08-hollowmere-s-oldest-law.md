## 8. Hollowmere's Oldest Law

The summons came the way all official business came to Hollowmere: by a boy with muddy boots and a note that smelled faintly of tallow and fear.

*Silas Galloway is required to appear before the Valley Council at the tolling of the evening bell. Come alone. Bring nothing.*

"Bring nothing," Wren read over my shoulder, their nose an inch from the paper the way they read everything, as if words might bolt if startled. "That's ominous. That's *very* ominous. Councils that say 'bring nothing' mean 'bring your affairs in order.'"

"It means bring nothing," I said. "It's a valley of farmers and bone-carvers. What would I bring? A spoon?"

"You'd be surprised what people bring to tribunals," said Odile from the corner, where she was cleaning her armor with the grim thoroughness of someone who had decided that if she could not fight her former Order, she could at least defeat some rust. "I once saw a man bring a live goose to a heresy hearing. As a witness."

"What did the goose testify?"

"That's classified under Order law."

Wren laughed — a bright, startled sound, like they were still surprised to find laughter available to them — and for one moment the workshop felt almost safe. Almost ordinary. The shavings on my bench caught the afternoon light, pale curls of sheep bone and river-antler, and above them hung the memorial pieces I had carved for thirty years without anyone looking twice at me.

Nobody looked twice at Silas Galloway. That had been the whole architecture of my life. Carve the dead their last words, keep your own.

Then the market burned, and I pulled three children out of the smoke by hearing their heartbeats through the ash, and now everyone in Hollowmere was looking at me exactly twice.

* * *

The council hall had been a tithe barn before it was anything else, and it still smelled like it — old grain, old dust, old decisions. Seven elders sat behind a table built for threshing disputes about fence lines, not deciding the fate of human beings. Elder Maren sat in the middle, because Maren always sat in the middle; she had been running this valley since before I could walk and would presumably run it after we were all politely dead, which in Hollowmere was less a figure of speech than a scheduling matter.

The hall was full. Not with soldiers — the Ashen Order kept its distance tonight, which told me Odile's report had landed somewhere uncomfortable — but with neighbors. The miller. The midwife. Old Tam Broadfoot, whose hip I had carved a new socket for eleven years ago and who had never once thanked me but had also never once reported me, which in this valley passed for devotion.

They had come to look at me. I tried to be worth looking at. I wore my good coat, the one without antler dust on the cuffs, and I stood where they put me, on the worn flagstone in front of the table, and I waited.

"Silas Galloway." Maren's voice hadn't changed since she'd scolded me for stealing apples at nine. It had the same terrible patience. "You are accused of practicing unwarded craft. Of hearing the dead. Of — " she consulted the page in front of her with visible distaste, "— *singing children out of fire.*"

"I'd have thought the singing was the least of it," I said.

Someone in the crowd snorted. Maren did not.

"The law of this valley is older than any of us," she went on. "Older than the chapel ruins. Older than the wards themselves, or so near as makes no difference. The law says magic does not cross into Hollowmere. The law says what cannot happen here does not happen here. And when something happens here anyway—" her eyes lifted from the page, and there it was, the thing beneath the thing, the fear she would never name in open session, "—the law says it must be warded, bound, or removed."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you are exiled, Silas. Across the wardline, past the standing stones, into whatever is out there." She said *out there* the way other people said *the sea*: vast, cold, and full of things that ate valleys like ours for breakfast. "You would not be the first. You would not even be the first Galloway."

That landed harder than she knew. My grandmother had gone across the line sixty years ago, carried out by four men and one very determined mule, and no one in the family ever spoke her name again except my mother, who spoke it every night like a prayer to a god we weren't allowed to have.

"I'd like to say something before you decide," I said.

Maren sighed the sigh of a woman who has heard a great many things before people decide them. "You may speak."

And then, from the back of the hall, Wren stood up.

They had promised — sworn on their own handwriting, which for a scribe is a serious oath — that they would stay outside. They were wearing a hood and everything. But Wren had the particular courage of people who have already lost everything once and found it made them clumsy rather than careful.

"They can't decide this without hearing the whole truth," Wren announced to the room, in the voice of someone reading a proclamation. "Because the whole truth is that your gods are dead. Have been dead for centuries. And the wards didn't keep the magic out — they sealed the murder in. And the prayers you've been sending up for three hundred years have been landing on a workbench in a bone-carver's shop because there is nothing up there to catch them except—"

"*Sit down,*" Maren said, and the whole hall flinched, because Maren's quiet voice was worse than her loud one.

But Odile was already moving. She came down the aisle in her half-polished armor, and I watched her make the choice in real time — duty on one side, memory on the other, and her boots walking straight down the middle of both.

"Elder Maren." She stopped beside me, close enough that I could hear her breathing, which was steady, which meant she'd been practicing. "Ser Odile Vane, formerly of the Ashen Order, Third Circle. I am the one who filed the report on the market fire. I am the one who can tell you what the Order knows about what killed those pilgrims' voices. And I am telling you, as a knight of eleven years' service, that exiling this person will not save this valley. It will only make sure no one is left inside it who can hear what's coming."

The hall went very quiet. Somewhere a shutter banged. Old Tam Broadfoot leaned forward on his cane with the expression of a man settling in for the best evening he'd had since his wife died.

Maren looked at the three of us — the witch, the runaway, the broken knight — and I saw something flicker across her face that I had never seen there before. Not anger. Recognition. The look of a woman who had spent her whole life keeping a lid on a pot and had just heard it start to rattle.

"This session is adjourned," she said. "The accused will return at dawn for judgment. Everyone else — go home. Lock your doors. Say nothing to anyone."

We said nothing to anyone. We said quite a lot to each other.

* * *

Back at the workshop, Wren paced. Wren pacing was a weather system; you learned to read it or get rained on.

"We leave tonight," they said. "Right now. Before dawn, before the judgment, before Maren remembers she owns a jail. There's a gap in the wardline past the quarry — I mapped it when I ran, I *know* it holds — and by morning we're over, and by noon we're in the low country where nobody's ever heard of Hollowmere, and we live. We *live*, Silas. That's allowed. That's actually the entire point of being alive."

"And do what?" I asked. I was packing, because I'm not a fool, but I was packing slowly, giving my hands something honest to do while my mouth worked out the rest. "Carve bones in some town where the dead talk all the time and nobody thinks it's strange? Open a shop? 'Galloway's Honest Hearings, no god too dead'?"

"Yes!" Wren threw their hands up. "Yes, exactly that! Why is that a bad thing?"

"Because I'd spend every day listening to strangers' dead," I said, "and every night hearing *this* valley's dead, because they don't stop at the wardline, Wren. They're mine. They've been mine since I was six years old and carved my first sparrow bone and heard it say *warm, I remember warm*. I can run. The voices come with me. All I'd be leaving behind is everyone who can't hear them and doesn't know why their prayers land nowhere."

Odile spoke from the doorway, arms crossed, watching us the way she watched everything — like a woman cataloguing exits.

"She's right," she said. "Flight solves nothing. Worse — it confirms the Order's story. A witch flees, the valley breathes easier, Marrow gets his clean narrative, and in ten years the vault is awake and nobody in Hollowmere knows to be afraid until it's standing in the market square wearing their grandmother's face." She paused. "The only defense this valley has is the truth. All of it. The massacre, the theft, the ledger. In daylight. From the council floor. Let them choke on it."

"Let them *exile* you, you mean," Wren shot back. "You've seen what these people do to truth-tellers. Your own Order stripped you in absentia, Odile. You think farmers with pitchforks are gentler?"

"I think," Odile said, with the terrible calm she deployed instead of shouting, "that I knelt in the mud over a grave Silas carved, and I wept, and I did not know why the grief was so large. And I think there are two hundred people in that hall tonight carrying griefs exactly that size, and they deserve to know the grief isn't theirs. It was *installed*. Like a fence post. And I will not stand in a tribunal and recommend running away from a lie while calling myself a knight of anything."

The workshop got quiet. Wren stood in the middle of it, small and furious and frightened, hugging their scribe's satchel like a shield.

"You both want to be brave," they said finally. "Fine. Be brave. But somebody in this room has to want to *survive*, and apparently it's me." Their voice cracked, just slightly, just enough. "I ran from the Order once. Do you know what it cost? I'm not — I can't watch you two walk into that hall tomorrow and get taken apart for a valley that would have handed us all over last week without blinking."

"Wren." I crossed the room and took the satchel strap gently off their shoulder, set it on the bench among the shavings. "Nobody's walking anywhere yet. Sit down. I'll make tea. We'll argue properly, with cups."

"Grief tea," Wren muttered, sitting. "This is how it starts. First tea, then tribunals."

"First tea," I agreed, "then tribunals."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat at my workbench with a candle down to its last inch and a piece of river-antler in my hands, turning it over, not carving. Listening.

The dead of Hollowmere were restless tonight. I could feel them the way you feel weather coming in an old scar — a pressure, a murmur, hundreds of voices just below the threshold of words. Since the wardline had begun to thin, they'd gotten louder. Or I'd gotten more open. Hard to say which.

One voice came clearer than the others. An old woman's, dry as seed husks.

*Tell them,* she said. *Tell them what we paid.*

I set the antler down. "Who are you?" I whispered, which was foolish, because the dead don't answer questions, they only repeat.

*Tell them what we paid,* she said again, patient as stone, and faded into the general murmur, and I sat there with my heart going hard against my ribs and understood, finally, what I was going to do at dawn.

Not Wren's plan. Not Odile's plan either, exactly — though closer to hers than I'd ever admit, because Odile was right that the truth was the only weapon we had. But there was a difference between telling the truth and *demanding* something, and I had finally figured out what I demanded.

Wren found me there at grey light, wrapped in a blanket, glaring at me with red-rimmed eyes.

"You didn't sleep."

"No."

"You're going to do something stupid."

"Probably."

"Silas." They sat down across from me, and underneath the irritation was the fear, and underneath the fear was the thing I'd only recently learned was there: they cared. This ridiculous, runaway, prayer-scribbling disaster of a person cared about me, and about Odile, and possibly about the whole doomed valley, and it was making them miserable. "Whatever you're planning — whatever speech you've got folded up in that coat — just. Come back. After. Whatever happens, come back to the shop. Promise me."

I looked at my workbench. Thirty years of shavings. Thirty years of last words, faithfully carved, faithfully kept, faithfully silent about the one secret that mattered.

"Wren," I said, "when I was a child, I thought the wards were a kindness. A wall against a loud world. I built my whole life inside that idea — carve quietly, listen quietly, want nothing, be no one. And it turns out the wall wasn't keeping the world out. It was keeping us *in*. With it. On top of it." I picked up the antler again, turned it in the grey light. "I love this valley. That's the whole problem. You can't betray a place you don't love. And I am done letting it be betrayed on my behalf."

"That's not a promise," Wren said.

"No," I said. "It's better. It's a reason."

* * *

The bell tolled dawn, and the hall was fuller than the night before — word travels fast in a valley with one road — and Maren sat in the middle of her table with seven elders flanking her and the whole weight of Hollowmere's oldest law stacked behind her like cordwood.

"Silas Galloway," she said. "The council has deliberated. You will submit to full warding — binding of gift, sealing of ear, silence for life — or you will be exiled across the line by sundown. Those are the terms. Speak your choice."

I stepped onto the flagstone. My knees were steady, which surprised me. Behind me, I felt Odile take up position at my left shoulder and Wren at my right, neither of them asked, both of them there.

"I choose neither," I said.

The hall inhaled as one body.

"Here is my counter-offer," I went on, and my voice carried better than I expected, maybe because three hundred years of dead were pushing it along. "You want to ward me? Ward the *valley* first. Ward the truth. Because here is what your oldest law is actually made of: it isn't a law against magic. It's a law against remembering. The founders of Hollowmere didn't build peace here. They built a *lid*. They killed the gods of this valley — murdered them, ground them to bone, and buried the pieces in a vault under the Order's house — and then they raised the wards not to keep danger out, but to keep the screaming in. Every prayer spoken in this valley for three hundred years has fallen on deaf air. Every harvest blessing, every funeral rite, every child's bedtime plea — unanswered, unheard, landing nowhere. And the council has known. Or suspected. Or chosen, generation after generation, not to ask."

Maren was on her feet. "That is *sedition*—"

"That is your *history*," I said, and the word cracked across the hall like a whip, and I heard my own voice shake and let it. "I know because I heard it. In the bones. In the relic-bone from the valley's edge, I heard the gods die, Elder Maren. I heard them scream for days. And I heard the ones who survived it — the fragments, the scraps, the little leftover holinesses hiding in roadside shrines — and do you know what they want? Not worship. Not sacrifice. They want to be *remembered honestly*. That's all. That is the entire price of what your founders stole."

Silence. Real silence, the kind that has weight. Old Tam Broadfoot had both hands on his cane and tears running freely down his face, and he wasn't the only one.

"So here are my terms," I said. "Open the records. Open the vault. Let the valley see what it was built on — all of it, the massacre and the theft and the ledger of harvested prayers. Let the people decide what to do with the truth, instead of deciding for them the way your founders did. Do that, and I'll kneel for any warding you like. Refuse—" I spread my hands. "Refuse, and exile me, and know that you're not exiling a witch. You're exiling the only witness this valley has left."

Maren stared at me for a long, long moment. Around the table, the other elders were doing arithmetic — I could see it, the frantic silent math of people calculating how much of what I'd said might already be common knowledge, how many of their neighbors had heard rumors, how long the lid could hold.

It was Maren herself who broke first. Not with rage. With something worse. She sat down slowly, folded her hands, and said, in a voice gone suddenly old:

"You speak of a vault under the Order's house. Child, there *is* no Order house anymore. There hasn't been for forty years. The Ashen Order's valley chapterhouse burned the winter of the great fever, and what was in its cellars—" she stopped. Started again. "What was in its cellars was moved. And nobody living knows where."

Behind me, Odile went rigid. I felt it more than saw it.

"But," Maren continued, and now her eyes had that rattling-lid look again, "there is one who might. The last custodian who served before the fire. She took a vow of silence and a hut on the fen road, and she has not spoken to a living soul in forty years." She drew a breath. "The council will deliberate on your terms, Galloway. Until then, you are remanded to your shop under watch. And Ser Vane—" her gaze slid past me, sharp as a boning knife, "—you will accompany me. You and I are going to pay a call on the fen road. I think you and the old woman may have *much* to discuss."

Outside, the morning was cold and clear, and somewhere far off — beyond the standing stones, beyond the wardline, out in the loud world — I felt the vessel turn over in its sleep, and whisper my name.
