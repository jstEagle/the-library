## 9. The Deep Vault

I had knelt before altars in nine countries. I had fasted in desert cloisters until I saw the sun split into seven flames. I had kissed the ring of the High Custodian himself, and felt nothing but the cold of old silver against my lips.

None of it prepared me for the way my stomach dropped when Wren asked, cheerfully, over breakfast: "So how do we break into an Order vault, exactly?"

"You don't," I said.

"Right," said Wren, already packing dried plums into their coat pockets with the efficiency of someone who had stolen food from better people than me. "But suppose we did."

"Wren."

"I'm just asking hypothetically. For a friend."

"The friend being you."

"The friend being all of us, collectively, as a group activity." They bit a plum in half and talked through it. "Because here's my thinking: Silas can hear the dead. The dead know where the vault is. The ghost knows where the vault is. And you—" they pointed the plum pit at me like a small accusation "—were an Ashen knight for eleven years. You know how Order strongholds breathe. Where the lungs are. When it sleeps."

"That knowledge is exactly why I'm telling you no."

"And yet," Wren said, "you're already thinking about which door we'd use."

I hated that they were right. I hated more that I'd been thinking about it since dawn — since Silas had come out of their workshop gray-faced and said *it whispered my name*, and I had felt something old and cold turn over in my chest, the same feeling I used to get before battle drills, except this time there was no drill, no sergeant, no clean structure of duty to hide inside. There was only the truth I'd burned out of my orders three nights ago, still smoking in my memory: *the Order has known for centuries, and has been feeding it.*

"They'll expect me," I said finally. "That's our advantage and our problem. Every sentinel between here and the reliquary will be watching for a disgraced knight in Ashen grey." I stood, brushed the crumbs from my cloak, and made the decision the way I once made all of them: quickly, and then refused to look at it too closely afterward. "Which is why none of us will be wearing anything that looks like us."

* * *

The Ashen Order kept its valley seat at Greyfall Priory, a fortress built into the cliff on Hollowmere's western edge, all vertical lines and weeping stone. Three hundred years ago it had been a temple. Then the founders sealed the wards, renamed everything, and turned a house of worship into a warehouse — which, I understood now, was rather the whole history of Hollowmere in miniature.

We went in through the laundry.

This was not my idea. It was the ghost's.

The ghost — who still had no name, and who communicated by borrowing the mouths of whatever was handy, which made planning sessions feel like a very grim puppet show — had led us along the cliff path at dusk, past the sentry walk, to a seam in the rock below the priory's eastern wall. There was nothing there. Then Wren squinted, said "huh," and found the hairline crack in the stone that wasn't a crack at all but a door, warded so subtly that even I, who had walked those halls for eleven years, had never known it existed.

"How do you know about this?" I demanded of the empty air.

Wren, standing closest, shivered and then spoke in a voice that was half theirs and half something older, dry as leaf-mold: "*Laundry.*"

"What?"

"*They built the vault under the wash-house. Water. Old pipes. The founders were practical people. Gods forgive practicality.*" The voice left Wren mid-word, and they staggered, caught themselves, and finished in their own voice: "—sorry. That one rattles going down. Anyway! Laundry."

And that was how Ser Odile Vane, formerly of the Third Circle, breaker of heresies and hearts (only the one heart; mine), entered the most secure sanctum of the Ashen Order pushing a cart of wet sheets.

Silas came behind me as my "novice," hood up, hands folded, radiating the particular nervousness of someone whose entire life had been spent avoiding attention and was now pushing a linen cart directly beneath it. Wren had wanted to come as a second novice. I had looked at them — a wiry runaway scribe with ink permanently on their chin and the posture of a startled cat — and said, "Absolutely not."

So instead Wren came as the laundry-master's mute assistant, which required them only to keep their mouth shut, a challenge they met with visible, heroic suffering.

The wash-house itself was steam and lye-smell, half-sister novices wringing sheets in the yellow lamplight, and nobody looked twice at us because nobody looks twice at laundry. This, I reflected, pushing our cart across the wet flagstones, might have been the Order's true genius. Not the wards. Not the swords. Just the profound human talent for ignoring the ordinary. We had guarded the gates against armies while the enemy could have rolled straight through with a basket of towels.

The ghost tugged us — I felt it as a cold hand at the back of my neck, gentle as a nurse waking you for night watch — toward the far wall, behind the great copper cistern. Another hidden door. This one opened onto stairs going down, and down, and down.

"Nobody knew about these?" Silas whispered beside me, voice barely stirring the dark.

"The founders' circle knew. The High Custodian's line. Maybe a dozen living people." I lit the stub of candle I'd brought, cupping the flame. "The Order believes the deep vaults are legend. Novices dare each other to find them. I once spent a whole winter searching, as a girl." My throat tightened. "Marrow found me in the scriptorium at midnight and asked what I was looking for. I told him the truth. He smiled and said, *Good. Wanting things is the beginning of faith,* and gave me extra penance."

Silas was quiet a moment. "He sounds lovely."

"He's the reason I joined." I started down the stairs. "That should tell you everything."

* * *

The deep vaults were cold in a way that cold isn't supposed to work. Surface cold sits on your skin. This cold came from underneath, from the stone itself, as if the mountain had been holding its breath for three hundred years and the air down here had never been invited back.

The stair ended at a corridor lined with niches, and the niches held bones.

Not buried bones. Not stored bones. *Displayed* bones — arranged with reverence, each niche lamp-lit by little votive flames that should have gone out generations ago but hadn't, each bone labeled in founder-era script. I read them as we passed, because reading was easier than screaming.

*Femur of the Shepherd of Flocks, who taught the valleys to sing.*

*Rib of the Mother-of-Embers, who carried fire to the drowned.*

*Hand-bone of the Laughing One, whose name was lost even to gods.*

"These aren't trophies," Silas breathed. Their hand rose toward one niche and stopped short, trembling. "Odile, these are *relics*. These are — the Order taught us the old gods were demons. That the founders drove out demons. But this is a shrine. Someone *loved* them."

"Someone killed them first," I said. My voice came out flat, which was how I knew I was close to tears. "Look at the cuts. I've studied enough battle remains to know a killing stroke. These weren't gathered after some war." I pointed to the fine, careful scoring along a femur's length. "These were taken *deliberately*. Ritual dismemberment. The founders didn't defeat the gods, Silas. They butchered them and kept the pieces."

Somewhere near my shoulder, cold bloomed, and Wren — walking behind us — stopped dead and spoke in the borrowed voice, low and cracked:

"*It hurt.*"

We all stopped.

"*Being taken apart. It remembers. I remember. I ran. I hid in a bloodline and I let my brothers and sisters die and I have been sorry for three hundred years and I cannot even say so because I have no name and no mouth of my own—*" The voice broke off. Wren swayed, pressed a hand over their own mouth, and when they lowered it their eyes were wet. "Okay," they whispered thickly. "Okay. Not doing that again without warning."

Silas reached back and gripped Wren's shoulder. Didn't say anything. Just held on, the way you steady someone on a high rope, and I watched something pass between them that had no words attached, and I thought: *this is what the Order replaced with incense and hierarchy. This. Exactly this.*

"Come on," I said, and my voice was gentler than any order I'd ever given. "Let's go see what they built with their theft."

* * *

The reliquary door was black iron taller than two men, worked with the sigils of every god the Order had ever denied existing. The irony would have been funny if my hands hadn't been shaking. The lock answered to a phrase, not a key — the founders trusted words above metal — and I stood before it for a long moment, gathering the sentence I had been taught at nineteen, sworn to secrecy, told I would die before speaking it aloud.

"Say it," Silas said quietly.

"If I speak the opening words of the deepest vault of the Ashen Order," I said, "there's no coming back from it. Whatever happens after — exile, execution, worse — I'll have done the one thing that can't be undone."

Silas considered this with the unhurried calm of someone who carved bones for a living and therefore had opinions about permanence.

"Odile," they said. "Three hundred years ago your founders took apart the holiest beings this land ever had and built a quiet little valley on top of the sound of them dying. Everything else is bookkeeping."

So I spoke the words. They tasted like ash and candle smoke, like every oath I'd ever taken burning off my tongue at once.

The iron door swung inward on hinges that had waited so long they sang.

* * *

I want to describe what we saw in a way that does it justice, and I can't, so I'll do what soldiers do and give you the inventory.

The chamber beyond the door was vast and round, a hollowed heart of the mountain, and its ceiling vanished upward into darkness that swallowed the candlelight. Around its walls ran shelves, and the shelves held jars, and the jars held bones — thousands upon thousands of them, sorted by type, by age, by some taxonomy I couldn't fathom, glowing faintly with the same unkillable light as the corridor lamps.

And in the center of the floor lay the vessel.

It had begun, I think, as an altar. A basin of black stone. But the bones had grown — fused, knitted, braided themselves together over three centuries of harvested prayer into something like a nest, something like a ribcage, something horribly like a body trying to remember how bodies worked. Ribs of god-femurs curved upward. A pelvis of fused skulls. Spines of vertebrae wound through it like ivy through a fence. It was perhaps ten feet across and it *breathed*, slow and tidal, though it had no lungs, and with each breath the jars around the walls glowed a little brighter, a little dimmer, in time.

Every relic in the room was feeding it. Had always been feeding it. Every prayer the Order collected, every tithe of worship funneled down through the wards, had been dripped into this thing drop by drop for ten generations.

"It's beautiful," Wren whispered, and then, horrified at themselves: "Oh no. Oh no, it's beautiful and I hate that."

"I know," I said, because I felt it too. Awe sat in my chest right alongside revulsion, and they were not fighting each other. They were the same feeling. Standing before the accumulated holiness of twelve murdered gods, assembled by thieves into an engine, I understood at last why the Order needed doctrine so badly. Because if you looked at this with honest eyes, the only possible responses were weeping or violence, and the Order had never allowed either.

Silas stepped forward.

"Don't," I said sharply.

"They called me." Silas's voice was strange — level, distant, the voice they used when the bones spoke loudly. "From the wardline. From my own workshop. They've been calling me for weeks and I need to know why."

They crossed the floor. I moved to follow and the ghost stopped me — cold fingers closing hard on my arm, and in my own ear, using my own voice, which I will never entirely forgive: "*Wait. Let them hear it first.*"

Silas laid one bare hand on the vessel's outermost rib.

The chamber changed. The lamps flared. Every jar hummed a note slightly different, and together they made a chord that I felt in my teeth, in the scar tissue of my old wounds, in the place behind my breastbone where I kept my worst memory. And Silas — Silas went white as the sheets we'd pushed through the laundry, and swayed, and caught themselves on the bone-rib, and began to laugh. Quietly. Terribly.

"What is it?" Wren cried. "What does it say?"

"It says my name." Silas lifted their head. In the flare of the lamps their eyes were wet and wide. "It's been saying my name because — Odile, it knows my voice. It's been listening for my voice. The prayers landing on my bench weren't accidents. It's been *reaching for me*. Through every prayer that got lost, through every scrap of worship the wards misdirected — it's been calling the one person in this valley who can hear the dead." They swallowed. "It wants a translator. Or it wants — "

The vessel exhaled. The whole mass of it sighed, ribs flexing, and the sound it made was not one voice but many, layered, harmonized, the way a choir sounds from outside the cathedral — and it said, distinctly, in a voice like warm honey poured over broken glass:

"*Carver.*"

Then, softer. Almost tender. Almost exactly like the voice Silas used when speaking to the ghost.

"*Little listener. Come home.*"

I drew my sword. It was absurd — steel against divinity — but eleven years of training doesn't ask whether a fight makes sense. And in the ringing silence that followed the vessel's words, from far above us, muffled through three hundred feet of mountain, came a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat.

Bells. The priory bells. Ringing the alarm.

And beneath them, faint but unmistakable, the cry of the watch-sergeant I'd trained myself, bellowing through the corridors of Greyfall:

*"The disgraced knight — she's inside the priory — seal the lower levels — seal the VAULTS—"*

The vessel's ribs curled, slowly, like a hand opening.

"Well," said Wren, backing toward the iron door. "On the bright side, we definitely found it."
