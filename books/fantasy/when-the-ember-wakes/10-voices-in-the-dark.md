## 10. Voices in the Dark

The vault door closed behind them with a sound like a book snapping shut.

Wren had heard that sound before. They'd heard it every evening of their childhood in the Scriptorium, when the last copyist left and the wardens locked the prayer-archives for the night. It was the sound of being shut in somewhere on purpose. Of someone deciding you belonged inside.

"Everyone breathe," Odile said. Her voice was steady in the way Wren had learned to distrust—steady like a rope bridge, which is to say it held, but only if nothing moved. "The ghost said there's a way out through the reliquary gallery. We go slow. We don't touch anything."

"The ghost also said," Silas pointed out mildly, "that the thing down here eats worship and wears voices, so maybe we shouldn't hang our whole escape plan on its advice."

"You're arguing with the only ally who doesn't have a body to lose."

"I'm just saying we should weigh the source."

Wren hugged their satchel to their chest and let the two of them bicker, because bickering meant alive, meant ordinary, meant three people standing in a corridor of black stone while something vast breathed somewhere below them. The corridor was lined with niches, and each niche held bones. Not arranged, not honored—*piled*, the way a miser piles coins, and Wren recognized the femurs before they recognized anything else, because they had spent four years transcribing prayers addressed to those exact femurs.

God-bones. Hundreds of them. The Order hadn't lost the gods' remains. The Order had *filed* them.

"I transcribed a prayer once," Wren heard themself say, without meaning to. "A woman from the eastern farms. She prayed to Vessia-of-the-Threshing-Floor for her son's fever. I copied her words into the ledger and sealed the ledger and sent the seal down—" They stopped. Their throat had gone tight around the rest.

Silas's hand landed on their shoulder. Warm. Calloused. Smelled faintly of bone dust and beeswax. "Hey," Silas said. "Whatever's about to happen down here—we're all carrying the same kind of luggage."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"It comforts me. Misery loves company, but shame *requires* it."

Despite everything—the dark, the breathing stone, the chorus Wren could feel pressing up through their boot soles like heat through a hearthstone—Wren laughed. Just a small one. Just enough.

Then the walls began to speak.

* * *

It started as a hum, the way a struck tuning fork hums, except the fork was the entire vault and the note was Wren's mother's voice.

*"Wren."*

They stopped walking. Odile's sword came out with a whisper of steel. "That's not real," she said. "Nobody's here but us."

*"Wren, love, come here. Come see what I found."*

It was her exactly. The soft burr at the back of her throat, the way she stretched love into two syllables, the little catch when she was excited about something—a beetle, a bird's nest, a strange-shaped root she'd dig up and present like treasure. Wren's mother had been dead six years, and Wren had run away from the Order the year after her funeral, and they had never once said out loud that they'd been glad to go, because the Scriptorium was where her prayers were stored, and leaving meant leaving her voice behind in a locked room forever.

"Mother?" Wren's mouth made the word before their brain could stop it.

"Wren, no." Silas grabbed their arm. "Listen to me. That thing doesn't know your mother. It knows your *memory* of her. It's reading you like I read bones."

"It sounds like her."

"I know."

"It *sounds*—"

"I know," Silas said again, gentler now, and Wren hated how much that gentleness undid them.

Across the corridor, Odile had gone rigid. A man's voice was coming out of the wall beside her—deep, clipped, warm in a way military men are warm only toward very few people. *"Odile. Attaboy. You always were the one who did it right."* Her father? No—her jaw said *no*, her white-knuckled grip on the sword said *no*, but her eyes said *yes*, yes, yes, and Wren understood with a sick lurch that this was how it worked: the vessel didn't need to be clever. It only needed each of you alone, even in company. Especially in company.

And Silas—

Silas had gone pale as one of their own carvings. Whatever voice was speaking to them, Wren couldn't hear it. It seemed to come from inside rather than outside, because Silas's lips were moving, answering, and the answer was "*I'm sorry,*" repeated twice, in a voice Wren didn't recognize at all.

Then the corridor tilted.

Not physically. Worse. The floor stayed put; the *wanting* tilted. Wren felt it happen in their own chest, a slow lean forward, like standing on a ship's deck. Down the passage, past the piled god-bones, the dark ahead had begun to glow—not firelight, not lamplight, but the particular gold of a Scriptorium lamp at midnight, the exact color of the light under which Wren had done their best work, their worst work, four years of beautiful transcription that had fed pilgrims down into the deep places like grain poured into a mill.

*"Come finish the ledger, love,"* said their mother's voice, from the direction of the light. *"You were always so good at finishing things."*

Wren took a step.

They would remember later, in the long retellings, that they didn't decide to take the step. That was the horror of it. The step simply happened, the way a sneeze happens, the way falling happens. One moment their boots were planted next to Silas's; the next, the floor between them and the glow had been crossed, and their hands were opening the satchel strap because some deep animal part of them thought maybe, maybe if they showed the vessel the ledgers, showed it everything they'd written, it would understand that Wren had never meant any harm, had never known, had been a child of nineteen copying prayers in a cold room—

"Wren!"

Silas's voice. But not the way it had sounded all these weeks—gruff, dry, fond in a sideways fashion. This time Silas was *singing.*

* * *

It wasn't a song anyone knew. Later Wren would learn it was half a lullaby from Hollowmere's eastern farms and half pure improvisation, because Silas Galloway could not carry a tune in a bucket and had clearly decided that this was irrelevant.

It went:

*"Oh, the kettle's on the hob and the hob is on the fire,*
and the fire is in the house where my grandmother lies,
and she snored like a landslide and burned the Sunday bread
and we loved her anyway, we loved her anyway—"*

It was ridiculous. It was the worst singing Wren had ever heard. It was the most beautiful thing they had ever heard, because it was *real*, because no dead thing could produce it, because Silas was standing right there in the black stone corridor with their terrible voice cracked and wavering and absolutely, unforgivably alive—and the living, Wren realized, the living sing badly on purpose, sing badly *together*, sing badly as a way of saying *I am here and imperfect and present and so are you.*

The gold light guttered.

Their mother's voice tried again—*"Wren, love, come here"*—but it came out thinner now, stretched, a glove with no hand in it. Because Wren was crying, actually, ugly crying, and their tears were salt and water and entirely mortal, and because Odile had grabbed Silas's shoulder and joined in—*Gods preserve us*, Wren thought wildly, *the knight can't sing either*—bellowing some marching song about ale and artillery, and because the nameless ghost had poured itself up through the floor and wrapped around Wren like cold silk and spoken, in borrowed words, directly against their ear:

*"This one is true. Hold onto this one."*

Silas caught Wren's wrist. Pulled. Wren came, stumbling, sobbing, laughing at the absurd lyrics, back across the stones, back into the circle of two badly singing humans and one bodiless dead thing who had chosen, out of everyone and everything in the world, *them*.

The glow went out.

In its place: darkness, honest darkness, the plain unmagical kind, and the sound of three people breathing hard, and Odile saying, between verses, "What in the hells *is* this song?"

"My gran made it up," Silas gasped. "Verse changes every time. That's the point. You can't memorize a song that keeps changing. Well—you can't unless you're me, and I get the words wrong anyway, so."

"It's a song you can't counterfeit," Wren whispered. Understanding arrived cold and clean. "Because it's never the same twice. The vessel can only repeat what it's eaten. It can't invent."

"And I," Silas said, "have been inventing nonsense since before I could hold a chisel."

* * *

They sat in the dark for a while after that. There didn't seem to be any hurry anymore, oddly. The pressure in the air had lifted—or rather, it had drawn back, the way a predator draws back to reconsider. Somewhere far below, the fused mass of stolen god-bones turned over in its half-sleep, and the whole vault groaned like a ship changing course.

Wren sat with their knees pulled up and their face wet and discovered they felt lighter. Not forgiven—there was nobody down here to do the forgiving—but *carried*. Silas sat close enough that their shoulders touched. Odile had sheathed her sword and was cleaning her gauntlets with a rag, which Wren had learned was what Odile did instead of crying.

"What did it say to you?" Wren asked finally. "Both of you. If you want to say."

Silas was quiet a long time. "My sister," they said at last. "Maren. She drowned when we were children, before the wards finished settling over our farmstead. I heard her that night—through a deer rib I'd been whittling. First time I ever heard the dead. She asked me to pull her out." Silas's thumb worked along the seam of their sleeve. "I was nine. I couldn't have. I know I couldn't have. The vault sang her voice at me and asked why I'd carved ten thousand names for strangers and never once carved hers."

"Oh, Silas."

"I told it I was sorry." A breath that wanted to be a laugh and mostly managed. "It's going to use that. Sorry is such a useful lever. That's the whole trick of the thing, isn't it? It doesn't lie. It just holds up what we already believe about ourselves and lets us do the rest."

Odile set down her rag. For a moment Wren thought she wouldn't answer at all. Then: "My first posting. A village called Alderfen, on the wardline. We burned a family's shrine—order was order—and a girl died in the fire. Eleven years old. My commander wrote the report saying she'd been a casualty of the family's own resistance." Her voice was flat as a planed board. "I signed beneath his name. My father trained me to obey, and I have used him as an excuse for eleven years, and that thing down there recited my signature back to me in his voice, and for about four seconds I wanted to walk into it and never come out."

The silence after that was full and warm and terrible, the silence of people who have shown each other the bottoms of themselves.

"Well," Wren said, wiping their nose on their sleeve, "we're a fine trio. A coward, a killer, and a liar."

"Speak for yourself," Silas said. "I'm a coward, a killer, *and* a liar. I've been lying to this whole valley about what I am since I was nine years old." They knocked their shoulder gently against Wren's. "But here's what I keep coming back to. That thing offered each of us a door out of our own heads. And none of us walked through. Do you know how rare that is? Marrow's faithful will walk into it by the thousand. We stood in the dark and sang badly and *stayed*."

"The ghost helped," Odile said.

"The ghost helped," Silas agreed.

The ghost, coiled in a corner niche atop a pile of somebody's sacred femurs like a cat claiming the best chair, made a gesture Wren had come to think of as its shrug—an indistinct rippling, like heat haze. Then it raised one translucent finger and pointed down the corridor, past where the gold light had been, and spoke in the borrowed voice of Odile's dead commander, which it had apparently kept, the way a magpie keeps buttons:

*"There. The reliquary door. And Wren—"*

It paused. Switched voices—to Wren's own voice, which was deeply unsettling, and then, mercifully, to its own non-voice, the one like wind through a keyhole that needed no mouth at all:

*"The vessel knows your name now. All three names. When it wakes—and it is closer to waking than we hoped—it will call you by the ones you love. Prepare your answers."*

"Charming," Odile muttered.

But Wren was barely listening, because down the corridor, where the ghost pointed, a door stood open on a chamber lit by no lamp at all—lit by the bones themselves, hundreds of god-bones stacked in silent shelves, and among them, on a reading desk exactly like the desks in the Scriptorium, lay a fresh ledger. Open. With a pen beside it, ink still wet.

And in the ledger, in Wren's own handwriting—which they had never used down here, which they had not touched a pen to since fleeing the Order—in Wren's own handwriting, the newest entry read:

*Prayer received. Prayer answered. One (1) scribe, delivered.*

Below it, in a different hand entirely—loopy, ancient, delighted—someone had added a single line:

*Thank you for coming. We saved your seat.*

* * *

Behind them, very softly, from everywhere and nowhere, the vault began to hum a tune it had never hummed before.

Badly. Off-key. Deliberately.

Learning.
