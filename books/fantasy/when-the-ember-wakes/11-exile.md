## 11. Exile

They came for Silas at dawn, which everyone in Hollowmere agreed was the polite hour for ruin.

Silas heard them coming the way they had learned to hear everything now: as a pressure behind the eyes, a faint ringing like a wet finger circling the rim of a cup. Boots on the mill road. Six sets, maybe seven, walking in step because the council wanted to look orderly even when it meant doing something shameful before breakfast.

"You should eat," Wren said from the corner of the workshop, where they had spent the night sitting cross-legged among bone shavings like a cat that refused to acknowledge it was being put out. They were still wearing the same ink-stained scribe's coat they'd arrived in three weeks ago, sleeves too long, hands wrapped around a mug of barley tea that had gone cold an hour past cold. "It's bad luck to face a tribunal hungry."

"It's bad luck to face a tribunal at all."

"Then at least be full for it." Wren pushed the mug across the bench. "My grandmother used to say a person who argues on an empty stomach argues twice as loud and half as well."

"Your grandmother sounds wise."

"She was executed by my former employers for forging a hymnal. Wise and doomed. It's a family tradition I'm trying very hard not to continue." Wren's joke landed soft and crooked, the way all their jokes did—the humor of someone who had learned early that laughter was cheaper than crying and lasted longer.

Silas drank the tea anyway. It tasted like wet straw. It tasted like home.

The ghost drifted through the wall without bothering to use the door, which it never bothered to do, mostly out of what Silas suspected was pure spite toward architecture. It had no name yet—had remembered nothing new since the vault—but it had opinions, and its opinion this morning was delivered by borrowing the voice of the old weathervane rooster above the coop, so that Silas's own house crowed warnings at him in a dead woman's cadence.

*"They will ask you to kneel,"* the ghost said through the rooster's iron beak, tinny and strange. *"Do not kneel. Kneeling is how they get your neck at the right height."*

"Noted," Silas said.

"And Silas—" Wren stood, and for once their voice lost its flippant edge entirely. "Whatever happens in there. Whatever they call you. You're not a witch. You're just the only person in this valley with working ears."

Silas looked at their hands. Carver's hands, scarred and steady, stained faintly yellow at the fingertips from decades of marrow-fat and beeswax. Hands that had made memorials for three hundred funerals' worth of grief and only recently learned that the grief talked back.

"That's the problem, isn't it," Silas said quietly. "In Hollowmere, working ears are heresy."

The boots stopped outside the door.

* * *

The council met in the tithe-house, which was the largest building in Hollowmere and the ugliest, raised by the founders out of grey fieldstone and joyless ambition. Seven elders sat behind the long table on the dais. The head elder, Mother Cassia Hale, wore her grandmother's chain of office, which was iron and heavy and, Silas had always privately thought, looked less like authority than like an apology she'd never learned to deliver.

The valley had turned out to watch. Of course it had. Hollowmere had three entertainments—market day, weddings, and other people's disasters—and Silas Galloway had become all three at once.

They stood Silas in the center of the floor on a chalk circle someone had drawn badly. Beside them, to their mild astonishment, Ser Odile Vane stood in full Ashen plate, uninvited and unbudging, her helm under one arm and her jaw set like a foundation stone.

"This is Order business," Elder Bramm said nervously.

"Then it is my business," Odile said. "I am the Order's presence in this valley. Unless you'd like to send word to the High Custodian explaining why you conducted his inquiry without me. He does so love correspondence."

Nobody wanted to correspond with High Custodian Marrow. The silence that followed had texture.

Mother Hale rose. She was seventy if she was a day, and her hands shook, but her voice didn't. "Silas Galloway. Bone-carver of Hollowmere. Three weeks ago you pulled four children from a burning market by means no honest craft can explain. Two nights past, witnesses heard you speaking with the dead at the ward-stone. And yesterday—" here she faltered, and Silas saw that she hated this, actually hated it, which was somehow worse than if she'd relished it— "yesterday you stood in the square and told the whole valley that our founders murdered the gods who made our peace."

"I told them what the bones remember," Silas said. "I don't decide what's true, Mother Hale. I just listen."

"The law says otherwise." She lifted a scroll. "Hollowmere's oldest law. Silence within the borders, silence of the soul, silence before the wards. Magic cannot enter this valley. Therefore anything within these borders that works magic is not magic—it is *corruption*. And corruption is cut out."

"So that's the verdict," Silas said. "Decided before I walked in. Why draw the circle at all?"

"For form," said Elder Bramm, and had the decency to look embarrassed about it.

"Form," Silas repeated. They felt strangely calm. That calm frightened them more than the tribunal did; it felt like the deep water feeling, the stillness over where the drowned child had sunk. "May I say one thing? Not in defense. In *memory*."

Mother Hale's mouth thinned. But she nodded, because whatever else she was, she had buried a husband under Silas's chisel and knew the shape of his honesty.

"Three hundred years ago," Silas said, voice carrying to the back of the tithe-house, to the farmers and fishwives and children perched on fences, "the founders of this valley killed seven gods and built their peace on the grave. The wards aren't protection. They're a lid. And every prayer whispered in Hollowmere for three centuries has been landing on the thing that ate our gods—and feeding it. I didn't make that true. Your grandparents' grandparents made it true. All I've done is hear it, and tell you, and now you want to brand *me* for the noise."

A murmur ran through the crowd. Not agreement, exactly. Not disagreement. The sound of people discovering that a story they'd lived inside had walls.

"Enough," Mother Hale said, and there was real grief in it. "By unanimous vote of this council—"

"It wasn't unanimous," said Elder Sefa, the youngest of the seven, standing up so fast her chair scraped. "I voted no. I'm voting no again right now, publicly, so it's written down somewhere besides your ledger."

"Sefa—"

"Somebody has to write down the true version, Mother. Apparently that job's open."

Mother Hale closed her eyes for one long breath. When she opened them, she held out her hand to Bramm, who passed her the branding iron—not hot, not burning flesh, but the cold sigil-iron of the wards, which pressed its mark into skin like frost into a windowpane. A permanent scar. A warding-glyph, they called it lawful. Silas knew better. Everyone in this room knew better. It wasn't a ward against magic. It was a leash.

"Kneel," Mother Hale said, "and it will be done gently."

And Silas thought of the ghost's warning, borrowed through an iron rooster: *kneeling is how they get your neck at the right height.*

"No," Silas said. "I'll stand. If you must do it, do it to someone standing."

So they did. Two elders took Silas's arms—gently, shamefacedly, one of them whispering *forgive me* —and pressed the cold iron to the hollow of the throat, below the collarbone, where the mark would show above any shirt they ever wore. The cold went in like a nail of winter. Silas gasped. Not from pain—from silence. The moment the sigil bit, every voice they had ever heard, every whisper of the dead that had lived behind their sternum since childhood, cut off like a door closing on a lit room.

For the first time in their life, Silas Galloway heard nothing at all.

It should have been relief. It was the loneliest silence in the world.

"There," Mother Hale said softly, and her hand, pressing the sigil home, trembled like an old woman's after all. "You are warded, Silas Galloway. What remains of you belongs to Hollowmere."

* * *

Odile Vane made her mistake—her magnificent, deliberate mistake—in front of the entire valley.

She stepped forward while the elders were still packing away their instruments, and she pulled off her gauntlet, and she knelt. Knelt beside Silas in the chalk circle, in full view of everyone, and took Silas's branded hand in her bare one.

"On behalf of the Ashen Order," she said, in the parade-ground voice that could carry across a battlefield, "I formally declare this tribunal irregular. The Order's mandate is the suppression of heresy. What occurred here today is not suppression. It is silencing of a witness—which, by the Order's own codex, article nine, constitutes the greater crime. I am invoking my rank to contest it."

"You have no rank to invoke," snapped Elder Bramm. "Not here. This isn't Order jurisdiction—"

"Article nine doesn't care whose dirt it stands on." She rose. She put her helm back on, which was, Silas understood later, the equivalent of putting up a wall. "Furthermore, I am lodging a formal complaint with the High Custodian regarding this council's concealment of Order property in its deep vaults. Property which, I might add, has been *screaming*, according to witnesses, for three weeks."

"You would turn traitor over a bone-carver?" Mother Hale asked, and there was wonder in it, and disappointment, and underneath both, horribly, hope—as though part of her had wanted someone, anyone, to stand up sooner.

"I would turn traitor over the truth," Odile said. "I was trained to. That's the joke of it. Nobody at the citadel mentioned that the training would eventually apply."

Bramm sent a boy running for the Order's local garrison—three men and a sergeant, currently eating breakfast—and for one suspended moment Silas genuinely believed there would be blood in the tithe-house before noon.

What happened instead was worse and better. The garrison arrived, took one look at Ser Odile Vane—veteran of the Border Wars, holder of the Iron Vigil, the woman who had once held a bridge alone for six hours—and declined, collectively and without a word, to arrest her. The sergeant studied his boots. The youngest soldier found something fascinating about the ceiling beams.

"Cowardice," Odile observed.

"Wisdom," said the sergeant, and saluted her anyway, which was somehow the most treasonous act of the morning.

So the council fell back on what councils fall back on. Paper. By afternoon, Odile Vane's commission was revoked—stripped of rank, colors, and standing by couriered decree, the seal of High Custodian Marrow dripping red wax like a wound. She read the letter standing in Silas's doorway, and then she folded it with great care, and tore it into pieces exactly small enough to burn, and fed it piece by piece into Silas's forge.

"Fifteen years," she said, watching it curl. "Fifteen years of service, and it ends with me committing arson in a bone-carver's workshop."

"Is that grief or satisfaction?"

"Yes," said Odile.

* * *

They left at night, because staying was no longer possible and because Wren argued—with genuine passion—that fleeing in daylight lacked drama and invited archers.

The ghost led. It had grown stronger since the vault, or bolder, or simply more desperate; it moved ahead of them along the dark mill road as a pale suggestion, borrowing voices as it went—the creak of a gate, the hoot of an owl, once, absurdly, the groan of Odile's own knee joints, which made her stop mid-stride and say, "That was uncalled for."

*"The north ford,"* it said through a fencepost. *"The wardline runs shallow there. Where the river crosses, the founders' work thins. Even a lid leaks at the seams."*

"How do you know that?" Wren asked.

A pause. The fencepost's voice, when it came, was smaller. *"Because I helped lay it."*

Nobody answered that. There was nothing yet to answer it with. Somewhere inside the nameless ghost was a name, and the name was going to change everything, and all three of them were carrying that knowledge the way you carry a full pot across an icy yard—carefully, not looking down.

Wren walked close to Silas the whole way, close enough that their shoulder bumped Silas's arm every few steps, and kept up a stream of low chatter that was obviously and touchingly a fear-management strategy. "So when we get outside the wards—I've never been outside, obviously, none of us have, except Odile—what's it like? Odile, what's it like?"

"Loud," Odile said.

"Loud how?"

"Just loud. Everything. The wind has opinions. Trees gossip. After ten years I learned to sleep through it, but the first month I thought I was losing my mind." She glanced sideways at Silas, and her voice gentled in the way it only ever did by accident. "For you it'll be worse. You've been listening your whole life through a keyhole. Outside the door is an ocean."

"I can't wait," Silas said, and meant it, and was terrified of it.

They came to the ford at moonset. The river ran black and quick over its stones, and beyond it the land rose into hills that no one in Hollowmere had legally seen for three hundred years. The wardline itself was invisible—of course it was—but Silas felt it the way you feel a change in air pressure before a storm: a flatness, a held breath, a wall made of quiet.

They stopped at the water's edge.

"Once we cross," Odile said, "there's no crossing back. Marrow will have the wardline watched within the week. Everything we are, everything we know—we carry it out of this valley tonight, or it dies in here with us."

"My whole life is in there," Silas said. It wasn't self-pity. It was inventory. The workshop. The drying racks. The memorial femurs, forty years of them, standing in the churchyard like a silent congregation. Mother Hale's trembling hand. Elder Sefa's lone raised vote. "Everyone I've ever carved for."

"And they'll still be here tomorrow," Odile said. "Which is more than the vessel's next meal will be able to say, if we fail."

Wren took a breath, hitched their satchel higher, and stepped into the water first, because of course they did; courage had always come easiest to them when disguised as impulsiveness. The ghost streamed after them like smoke drawn through a crack. Odile went second, armor and all, sinking to mid-thigh and emerging streaming on the far bank, turning to wait.

Silas stood alone on the Hollowmere side.

Behind them, the valley slept—thatched roofs, banked fires, the churchyard full of names they'd carved. Ahead, the hills, the ocean of noise, the war the founders had pretended never happened.

"I'm coming back," Silas told the valley quietly. "I don't know how, and I don't know when, and you'll probably try to brand me again. But this isn't exile. Exile is being thrown out. This is being sent ahead."

Then they crossed the water.

The world broke open.

There was no gentle transition, no threshold sensation, no slow adjustment. One stride Silas was wading in black water, and the next stride the flood hit them—magic pouring back into a limb that had been numb since birth, blood rushing into a sleeping foot, and oh, it *hurt*, it hurt like waking, like thawing, like a scream stretched out into a season.

Silas dropped to their knees in the shallows on the far bank and the ocean crashed in.

Every sound. Every voice. The hills themselves spoke—a slow granite patience older than language. The river gossiped in ripples. Grass sang in frequencies meant for roots. The wind carried conversations from miles away, arguments between crows, a shepherd's lullaby, the creak and complaint of ruined watchtowers left over from the god-war, each one crowded with the small persistent ghosts of the people who had died defending them. And beneath it all, deeper, vast and wounded and *near*: the dead gods. Fragments of them, scattered like embers across the whole broken land, still smoldering after three hundred years, still singing snatches of their own funeral songs.

And threaded through everything, everywhere, greasy as oil on water: the vessel's hunger. Listening. Reaching. Already aware that something with ears had stepped outside the lid.

"*Silas.*"

The name came from a hundred directions at once—from the water, the stones, the wind—and it was not the ghost, and it was not the gods. It was the thing in the deep vault, and it had followed them across the line the way a smell follows a coat.

On the bank, Wren had gone white and grabbed Odile's arm. Odile had drawn steel. But Silas stayed kneeling in the river, head bowed under the avalanche of sound, and slowly, painfully, began to laugh.

Because under the terror, under the roar, they could feel it settling into them—not like possession, not like invasion, but like a tool finding its proper hand. Forty years of carving. Forty years of listening through a keyhole. And now the whole door was open, and the whole house, and the whole country beyond it.

"Silas?" Wren's voice, thin with fright. "Are you—are you all right? Say something."

Silas lifted their head. Their eyes had gone silver in the moonlight, reflecting light that wasn't there.

"I can hear the watchtowers," they said, and their voice came out layered, resonant, a chord instead of a note. "I can hear the roads. I can hear where Marrow's army is camped—two days east, Wren, and there are thousands of them, and every one of them is praying." They stood, water streaming from their clothes, and pointed east with a hand that faintly glowed at the knuckles like banked coals. "And I can hear the roadside shrines. The little gods. The fragments. They know I'm coming."

"They're afraid of you?" Odile asked.

Silas smiled, and it was not entirely a comfortable smile.

"No," they said. "They're waiting for me."
