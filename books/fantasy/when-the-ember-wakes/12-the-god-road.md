## 12. The God-Road

Wren had always imagined that leaving Hollowmere would feel like falling.

They had rehearsed it a hundred times in the margins of prayer-books, in the little secret alphabet they'd invented at nine years old for thoughts too dangerous to write plainly: *someday I will walk out of the valley and the world will open like a hand.* In the rehearsals there was always triumph. There was always a road, and the road always went somewhere.

In practice, the road went uphill, and it went through ash, and it smelled like a hearth that had been left untended for three hundred years.

"Is it supposed to look like this?" Wren asked, for the fourth time since the wardline.

"No," said Odile.

"It looks like the end of a story," Wren said.

"It looks like the end of *several* stories," Odile corrected. "And then someone built a road through the wreckage and called it a pilgrimage route, because people will walk anywhere if you tell them it's holy."

The God-Road wound north out of the valley through country Wren had no vocabulary for. They had grown up in Hollowmere, where the hills were green and modest and the worst thing the landscape ever did was rain sideways during the barley harvest. Here the hills had been *broken*. Not eroded, not weathered — broken, the way a bone breaks, with the fracture still visible centuries later. Whole ridgelines had been snapped and left askew. Rivers ran through channels that looked gouged rather than carved. And everywhere, everywhere, the ash: gray drifts piled against stones like snow that had forgotten how to melt.

Silas walked a little ahead, one hand trailing along a leaning milestone as if listening to it. Which, Wren was learning, Silas possibly was.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said.

"What thing?"

"The touching-things thing. You've touched every rock between here and the wardline. If you're checking them for messages, you could just tell us."

"I'm not checking them." Silas's hand lingered on the milestone. Their face had gone strange — not frightened, exactly. *Attentive.* Like a person listening to a house at night, deciding whether the creak was the wind or not. "There's nothing in them. That's what's strange. Out here, everything should be *loud*. The bones in Hollowmere were quiet because of the wards. These aren't warded. These should be screaming."

"And?"

"And they're whispering. Like they're afraid of being overheard." Silas took their hand off the stone and wiped it on their coat, which Wren had noticed was a thing Silas did when a bone said something unpleasant. "Come on. We should make the ridge before dark. Odile says there's a shrine."

"There's always a shrine," Odile said. "The Order built them every seven miles along the God-Road. Way-stations for pilgrims. Which means dry floors, and if we're lucky, an iron stove some farmer didn't steal."

"Did the Order build them," Wren said, "or did the Order *take credit* for them?"

Odile gave Wren a look that was, by Odile standards, practically a compliment. "You've been talking to the ghost again."

"I always talk to the ghost. The ghost is the only one of you who doesn't sigh when I ask questions."

*The ghost is correct,* said the ghost, out of Wren's own mouth, in Wren's own voice, which still made Wren's skin crawl in a way they were trying very hard to get used to. *Odile sighs constantly. I have counted.*

"You can't count," Odile said. "You don't have a body. You're making that up to seem impressive."

*I was a god of small accurate things,* said the ghost, with what Wren could only describe as dignity. *Counting was my whole personality.*

They climbed. The ash got deeper. Somewhere behind them, Hollowmere sat in its green bowl, pretending nothing had ever happened there, and Wren found they could not stop looking back at it — the way you look back at a house you've just realized you were never really safe in, and miss it anyway.

* * *

The shrine was exactly as advertised: a low stone building with a roof that mostly worked and an iron stove that some farmer, in fact, had not stolen. Odile got a fire going with the brisk competence of a woman who had slept in worse places and been betrayed in better ones. Wren spread out their satchel — the satchel, the one they'd run away with, the one full of stolen prayer-copies they still hadn't found the courage to burn — and tried not to look at it.

Silas stood at the shrine's little alcove, where a niche in the wall had once held some icon. The icon was gone. In its place, scratched into the stone in a hand Wren recognized with a lurch — because it was the hand of every transcription template Wren had ever been forced to copy in the Order's scriptorium — was a single line:

*WE HEARD YOU. WE COULD NOT ANSWER. FORGIVE US.*

Silas went very still.

"Silas?" Wren said.

"They're here." Silas's voice was careful, the way it got when the bones were saying something and Silas was deciding how much of it to say out loud. "Fragments. Little ones. This whole road — Odile, these shrines aren't way-stations. They're *anchors*. Someone strung them along the road like stitches in a wound. Every seven miles, a little pocket of the old gods, tucked into the stones so they wouldn't scatter entirely."

"The Order built the shrines," Odile said slowly.

"The Order *found* the shrines. Or the Order's founders built them, back when they were still pretending to be shepherds instead of butchers." Silas crouched by the empty alcove. "They're so small. Like — you know when a fire's gone down to coals, and you think it's dead, and then the wind moves and one of them glows?"

Wren did not know. Wren had never been allowed near a fire unattended, on account of the Incident with the Scriptorium Candle, which had not technically been Wren's fault. But they nodded anyway, because it seemed important.

And then the ghost spoke — not through Wren this time.

It came out of the alcove. Out of the stone. A voice like a struck bell heard through a wall, thin and resonant and *old*, and Wren watched Silas's face as it happened, because Silas was the only one of them who could hear the dead properly, and Silas looked like a person who had just been handed a letter addressed in their own handwriting.

"*...hush now,*" said the stone. "*...little one... you hid well... you hid so well...*"

"What is it saying?" Odile was on her feet, hand on her sword out of pure reflex, which Wren had learned was Odile's answer to most things, including breakfast.

Silas didn't answer. Their eyes had gone bright and unfocused, the listener's stare, and their lips moved slightly, translating. When they finally spoke, their voice came out rough.

"It's talking to the ghost."

The ghost, who had been quiet since the ridge, made a sound through Wren's mouth that Wren had never heard themselves make — a small, cracked, involuntary noise, like a child hearing its name called from a house it thought had burned down.

"*We looked for you,*" the stone-voice said, or Silas said for it, translating as fast as they could. "*After. We looked. The youngest went down and did not come up, and we thought — we thought you had gone into the dark with the others. But you hid. Clever one. Clever, frightened, clever one. You hid in the walking world. You hid in a bloodline.*"

Wren felt the words arrive a half-second before they understood them, the way you feel a stair that isn't there.

"Silas," Wren said. "What bloodline."

Silas turned around very slowly.

"*The carver's line,*" said the stone, gentle as a lullaby. "*The hands that listen. We put our listening in their hands, long ago, so that someone would hear us if we ever managed to speak. The youngest of us curled up small inside that gift and rode it down the generations like a leaf on a river. Three hundred years. Three hundred years of quiet. And then the wards cracked, little one. And you woke up.*"

The stove ticked. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved the ash around like it was still looking for something.

"Silas," Wren said again, because someone had to, and it was apparently going to be Wren, as usual, saying the thing everyone was thinking. "The ghost. The ghost is—"

"*Me,*" said the ghost, through Wren, and Wren's own voice cracked down the middle on the word. "*I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I didn't know how to say it. I still don't have all of it — my name is in pieces, I've been carrying the pieces for three hundred years and they don't fit together — but the shape of it. The shape of it is that I am the youngest of the gods of this valley, and I survived the night they were murdered by hiding inside a human family. And that family is standing in front of me holding a bone-carving knife.*"

Silas sat down. Not dramatically. Just — their knees stopped being interested in the project of standing, and they sat down on the shrine floor, in the ash, and looked at their own hands.

"My grandmother," Silas said. "My grandmother used to hold my hands over the bones when I was small and say, *listen with your whole wrist.* I thought it was a carver's trick. I thought it was — family technique. Like her bread recipe."

"It is a family technique," said the ghost. "It is a family technique three hundred years old. You are the end of a very long line of people who were asked to carry something they never agreed to carry, and who did it anyway, and who mostly did it kindly. I have watched them. All of them. I slept in their ears. I heard your grandmother sing to the bones when she thought no one was awake. I heard your mother—" The ghost stopped. Started again, smaller. "I heard a great deal. I never meant to be a burden. I only meant to survive."

Nobody said anything. Wren discovered they were gripping their own satchel strap hard enough to hurt.

It was Odile, of all people, who broke the silence — Odile, who had spent the whole journey from the vault acting like her entire personality had been confiscated along with her rank. She crouched down in front of Silas, the way you crouch in front of a spooked horse, and said, gruffly:

"Well. That explains why you're the only person in three centuries who could hear them. I'd been wondering."

"Odile."

"What? It's useful information. We're at war with a thing made of stolen god-bones and it turns out our bone-carver comes with a god pre-installed. That's not a tragedy, that's *reconnaissance*."

"It's not—" Silas pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "It's not reconnaissance. It's — I didn't *ask*—"

"No," Odile agreed. "You didn't. Neither did I ask to spend twenty years serving a lie. Neither did Wren ask to be born into a scriptorium. Welcome to the club. The dues are terrible but the company is improving."

And that — that stupid, gruff, knightly joke — was the thing that made Silas laugh, wetly, into their hands, and Wren felt something in their own chest unknot by one careful degree.

* * *

Later, when the fire had settled and Odile was pretending to sleep with one hand on her sword and Silas was actually asleep, curled against the warm stones of the stove like a cat who had decided the day had taken enough from them — later, Wren sat up and spoke quietly into the dark.

"Hey. Ghost."

*Yes.*

"You could have told me. Before. That it was Silas. That you'd been — riding along in their family the whole time. You told me things. You told me about the vessel. You told me about the voices. Why not this?"

The ghost was quiet long enough that Wren thought it might not answer. When it did, it borrowed Wren's voice so gently that it barely felt borrowed at all.

"Because I have watched people learn what Silas learned tonight, and I have seen what it does. It is a heavy thing, to find out your whole life was a vessel. I thought — if I told you first, you might look at them differently. And you look at them like they're a person. I wanted one person to keep doing that."

Wren stared at the fire. Their eyes were doing something embarrassing, and they were very glad Odile was fake-sleeping.

"That's the loneliest thing I've ever heard," Wren said. "Three hundred years hiding in someone's ears, and you spend your first honest conversation worrying about *my* feelings."

"I was a god of small accurate things," said the ghost. "Small accurate things include feelings. Especially the ones people don't say."

Wren pulled their knees up and rested their chin on them. "What was it like? Before. The valley, the gods, all of it. The real version."

The ghost made a sound Wren eventually identified as a laugh — a real one, rusty, like a door opening in a house long shut.

"Loud," it said. "Wonderful. We were not grand gods, Wren. We were *small* ones. A god of counting. A god of hearth-smoke. A god of the moment when rain starts. People didn't kneel to us — they *talked* to us. They complained to us. They told us jokes. That's what the Order could never steal, because they didn't know it was the treasure: we were never worshipped. We were *kept company*. And then the founders wanted power instead of company, and—" The voice frayed. Wren waited. "And I ran. The youngest always runs. I have been running for three hundred years and I am so tired, Wren. I am tired in a way that doesn't have a word."

"You could rest," Wren said. "After. When this is done. If we win."

"Gods don't rest. Gods *end*, or they *continue*."

"Well." Wren poked the fire, sending up a constellation of sparks. "Maybe it's time somebody invented a third option. Seems like this whole valley's been running on bad options for three hundred years. Might as well draft a new one. I'm a scribe. It's literally my job."

The ghost was quiet again. But this time it was a different kind of quiet — the quiet of someone turning a strange new idea over, checking it for flaws, and not finding any.

* * *

They made the next shrine by midmorning, and the next by dusk, and in each one the stones murmured to Silas in fragments — *the youngest, the hider, the carver's-line, our clever frightened one* — and each time, Silas's jaw got a little tighter and their shoulders a little higher, until by the third evening Wren couldn't stand it anymore.

"You're doing the thing again," Wren said. "The quiet thing. The thing where you decide something terrible all by yourself and carry it around like a stone in your boot."

Silas was crouched at the alcove, fingers resting on the scratched plea — *WE HEARD YOU. WE COULD NOT ANSWER* — and for a moment they didn't move. Then they said, without turning around:

"The fragments are getting stronger as we go north. Toward the vault. They're pooling, Wren. And they're not pooling around the vessel." Silas finally looked back at them, and their eyes in the shrine-dark were the eyes of someone listening to a very large, very distant sound. "They're pooling around *me*. Every shrine we pass, a little more of them settles in. They're not asking. They're just — coming home. Like water finding the low place."

"Is that bad?" Odile asked from the doorway, where she stood watch with the ash-wind pulling at her cloak.

"I don't know," Silas said. "But I heard something tonight, at the last mile-marker. One of the fragments. It said the vessel knows about me now. It's felt the pieces moving. It said—" Silas's throat worked. "It said, *tell the youngest's child that the thing in the deep vault has started saying their name in our voices. And it's learning to say it well.*"

The wind moved the ash outside the shrine like snow that would never melt, and somewhere north of them, down the scarred old road, something that wore the faces of murdered gods practiced a name in the dark — and got it almost right.

Odile reached for her sword.

"No," Silas said quietly, standing, brushing the ash from their knees. "Swords won't help with this one. We need to get to the vault before it finishes learning."

They walked on into the dark, three outcasts and a god with no name, down a road built on a massacre — and behind them, faint as a coal's last glow, the shrines began, one by one, to sing.
