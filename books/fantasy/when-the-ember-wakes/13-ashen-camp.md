## 13. Ashen Camp

The trap was, Odile had to admit as the net came down out of the dark, a good one.

It was strung between two dead trees with counterweights of river stone and greased hemp, and it dropped without a whisper until it whispered all at once, hauling Silas off their feet, tangling Wren into a flailing knot of elbows and profanity, and catching Odile's sword arm mid-draw so that her blade sang free and spun away into the bracken like a coin tossed by a disappointed god.

The ghost, being uncatchable, simply drifted up through the mesh and looked down at them with an expression that, on a face made of borrowed memory, managed to convey *I told you we should have gone around*.

"Nobody told us anything," Wren said from inside the net, upside down, hair full of leaves. "You never tell us anything. You're a ghost. You're constitutionally incapable of helpfulness."

"I said *the birds stopped singing*," the ghost said, through its own mouth this time, which it could do now, sometimes, when it had the strength. "That is practically a speech."

"Both of you be quiet," Odile said, because torches were coming through the trees, and she knew those torches. She knew the pitch of the resin in them, knew the spacing of the bearers, knew the particular disciplined silence of soldiers who had been taught that noise was a form of doubt.

Ashen Order remnants. She'd hoped they were farther behind. She'd hoped wrong, and hoping wrong was going to get them all killed if she didn't start doing arithmetic instead.

A dozen figures resolved out of the dark. Not the white-and-ash parade armor of the Order's public face — these were field leathers, travel-stained, hoods back, faces gaunt with hard road. And at their center, walking with the unhurried gait of someone who has never once needed to hurry because the world hurries toward him, came a broad man in a grey cloak with his hands folded behind his back like a scholar strolling a garden.

Odile's stomach did something complicated and painful.

"Ser Vane," said High Custodian Marrow.

He looked older than she remembered. That was the first obscene thing about him — that he had aged at all, that time still applied to him, that the man who had taught her to hold a shield line and recite the Litany of Ash could have crow's feet and a grey thread in his beard like any other mortal man. His voice was exactly as she remembered: warm, low, patient, the voice of a man explaining something to a beloved student who is being very slow about understanding it.

"High Custodian," she said. Her mouth was dry. "You're a long way from the Chapterhouse."

"I go where the work is." He crouched, unhurried, and peered into the net at Silas with genuine, terrible curiosity. "And here is the work. The bone-carver. The one who hears." He tilted his head. "Do you know how long I've prayed for you, Galloway? Do you know how many nights I've knelt on cold stone asking for a sign, an instrument, *anything*? And all that time you were sitting in your valley, whittling."

"They're not an instrument," Wren said furiously, still upside down. "They're a person. I would know, I've checked extensively, they're insufferably a person—"

"Hush, scribe." Marrow didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to; two soldiers took Wren's shoulders and the net and began, gently, efficiently, to extract them. "You've done enough. More than enough. We'll discuss your ledger later."

Wren went white. Odile saw it happen — saw the guilt land on them like a thrown stone, saw them stop struggling. She wanted to say something. There was no time, and anyway what was there to say? *It wasn't your fault* was a lie, and *it was* was cruelty, and both of them were true, which was the whole problem with the world lately.

They were bound — properly bound, Order knots, the kind you couldn't pick with fingers, only with patience or a miracle — and marched.

* * *

The camp sat in the bowl of a dead hillside two miles north, where the old war-road crossed a stream that ran grey with ash-silt. It was bigger than remnants should be. Three hundred souls at least, maybe more, and not all of them soldiers. Odile counted cookfires and found pilgrims among them — ordinary people, farmers and millers and a woman mending a child's coat by firelight, their belongings bundled, their faces turned north toward Hollowmere with an expression Odile recognized from every shrine she'd ever policed.

Hope. Weaponized hope, marching on foot.

"They followed the answered prayers," Silas said quietly beside her. Their hands were bound but their eyes were working, reading the camp the way they read bone. "Word spread outside the valley. Prayers getting answered wrong, but answered. People are desperate enough not to ask *answered by what*."

"Marrow asked," Odile said. "He's known for centuries. He just doesn't care what's answering, as long as he's the one holding the ear trumpet."

"You sound sure of that."

"I was raised on his knee in the Chapterhouse library. I know his sermons better than my own name." She flexed her bound hands, testing. "Which means I also know he doesn't do anything without three reasons and a contingency. Watch."

She watched. They were given a tent — not a cell, a tent, with a guard outside who saluted her, which was somehow worse — and left alone for an hour, and then Marrow came himself, carrying a tray of bread and stewed apples like a host rather than a captor, and that confirmed everything.

"Eat," he said. "You'll want your strength. All of you." He set the tray down and settled onto a camp stool with the ease of a man visiting old friends. His gaze went around the tent: Silas seated and watchful, Wren pressed against the far wall radiating the specific fury of the young and guilty, the empty corner where the ghost stood invisible to everyone but the three of them. "Even the quiet one. Though I confess I don't know what to feed a ghost. Old hymns, perhaps."

"You know about the ghost," Wren said.

"I know about everything, child. It's my job. It has been my family's job for eleven generations." Marrow laced his fingers. "Ah. You've made the face. The face Ser Vane is making too. Go on, Odile. Ask me. You've been composing the question since the net."

"The Ledger of Founding," Odile said. "Chapter nine. The wards weren't built to keep magic out."

"No."

"They were built to keep the screaming in."

Marrow smiled, and it was the worst thing she'd seen all year, because it was fond. "There's my best student. Yes. The founders killed the gods — or most of them; divinity is stubborn stuff, it doesn't die clean — and they built Hollowmere over the body, and they warded it, and for three hundred years the Order has kept the peace by keeping the secret. And feeding it." He said it plainly, without shame, and that plainness was its own horror. "Grief wants somewhere to go, Odile. Worship is grief with better posture. Every prayer the Order ever collected, every candle, every whispered confession — it all drains downhill, into the vault, into *it*. And it grows. Slowly. So slowly. But lately—" his eyes brightened, and there was something in the brightness that had nothing human in it, some reflected light from a source Odile didn't want to name — "lately it has begun to *dream aloud*. And it dreams of waking. And I intend to let it."

"Why?" Silas asked. Flat. No fear in it, which Odile suspected cost them something.

"Because the gods are dead, carver, and dead things cannot answer prayers, and people are *drowning* in unanswered prayers. Have you seen the roads? Have you seen what hopelessness does? I have. For thirty years I have read the ledgers of despair that come into my office disguised as tithes." He leaned forward. "The vessel can answer. All of them, at once, forever. Every prayer heard, every grief held, every sick child healed and every lost traveler found. It only needs to finish waking. And when it wakes, it will need a voice — a single authorized voice, one interpreter, one custodian, so that its mercy arrives ordered instead of chaotic. Mine. I have earned it. Eleven generations of my family carried the secret so that I could end the silence. I am not a villain, Odile. I am a *deliveryman*, and the parcel is finally arriving."

"You're a leash-holder," Odile said. "For a thing wearing dead gods' faces. You think it'll honor your little contract?"

"I think," Marrow said, rising, smoothing his cloak, "that it already whispers my name sweetly enough. And I think you'll come to see reason, because you always do — eventually, painfully, and then all at once. You were the best of them, Ser Vane. It broke my heart when you fell. It will mend my heart when you kneel." At the tent flap he paused. "Oh — and the carver. The vessel asks after you. By name. It says it knows your grandmother's voice. Sleep well."

He left. Nobody ate the apples.

* * *

"He knows about Gran," Silas said, very quietly, once the footsteps faded. "My gran's been dead six years. How does it know her voice?"

"Because it eats voices," the ghost said from the corner, fading into visibility like breath on glass. "Prayers carry voices. Your grandmother prayed, presumably. Everything anyone in that valley ever said skyward went downhill into its mouth." A pause. "Including mine, probably. Which may be why I can't remember my own."

"That's cheerful," Wren said, and then ruined it by wrapping their arms around themselves. "Odile. What do we do? Because I've done the arithmetic and it's bad. Three hundred soldiers, us with no swords, and the vault a day ahead of us."

"We're not getting out of this camp by force," Odile said. "So we get out by faith."

Silas raised an eyebrow. Wren said, "Explain, because that sounds like a plan with a typo."

"Marrow wants me to kneel. He said so. He believes I can be recovered — that I'm a fallen asset, not an enemy." She stood, rolling her shoulders, feeling the old familiar weight settle onto them, the weight of the Order's drill yard, of kneeling vigils, of Marrow's hand on her shoulder saying *again, from the top, Vane, perfection is a habit.* "So I'm going to give him what he wants. Publicly. I'm going to request reinstatement, confess my errors, swear the Litany. He'll want witnesses. He'll want it done properly, in front of the pilgrims, because my return validates everything — the Order, the secrecy, the whole rotten architecture. And a reinstatement ceremony means an audience, and an audience means crowds, and crowds mean confusion, and confusion is a door."

"And while everyone's watching you kneel?" Silas said slowly.

"Somebody picks the locks on three sets of Order knots. I'd suggest the person whose entire childhood was spent picking the seal-wax off forbidden prayer scrolls." She looked at Wren. "Can you?"

Wren's chin came up. "I once unpicked a reliquary lock with a quill and spite. Yes."

"It won't be that simple," the ghost said. "It's never that simple."

"No," Odile agreed. "But simple is for other people."

* * *

The ceremony was held at dawn on the road's central mound, because Marrow understood theater the way rivers understand downhill.

Three hundred people assembled. Soldiers in ranks, pilgrims pressing close, the ash-grey light coming up behind the eastern hills like a slow apology. An altar had been improvised from a wagon bed and draped in white. Marrow stood beside it in full ceremonial grey, and Odile walked out to him across the trampled grass in her ruined traveling clothes with her head high and her heart going like a siege drum, and she thought: *this is the last time I wear this lie, so let me wear it well.*

"Ser Odile Vane," Marrow announced, in the voice that carried to the back of every chapel she'd ever stood in. "Once the pride of the Third Vigil. Fallen to heresy. Returned to us." He extended his hand. "Kneel, daughter."

She knelt. The crowd murmured — she heard it, the rustle of hope, *even the traitor comes home, even the fallen are forgiven,* and she hated how much comfort she was handing them and hated more the small treacherous part of herself that remembered how warm this was, the liturgy, the certainty, the belonging.

"Do you renounce the heresy of the bone-hearers?"

Here it was. The hinge. Behind her, somewhere in the press of bodies, Wren and Silas were moving — she'd arranged it, a stumble, a guard distracted, Wren's clever criminal fingers already at work on Silas's knots under the cover of steadying them. Seconds. She had seconds.

"I renounce it," Odile said.

Marrow smiled and lifted the ceremonial ash — the grey thumbprint on the brow, the oldest rite, the one he'd given her at seven years old when she'd sworn her first oath with a wooden practice sword clutched in both fists.

And then, because she was about to run, because this was the last coin she had and she meant to spend all of it, she said, loudly, clearly, in the carrying chapel-voice he had trained into her:

"On one condition. Tell them what the prayers feed."

The smile froze on his face. The crowd went quiet in that particular way crowds do when they sense, animal-deep, that something is about to go wrong.

"Daughter," Marrow said softly, "you're tired."

"Tell them what's in the vault under their valley. Tell them what their grandmothers' prayers have been feeding for three hundred years. Tell them why the gods never answered — tell them the gods are *dead*, and who killed them, and who's been collecting the mail ever since." She was on her feet now. She hadn't decided to stand; her body had simply refused to kneel one second longer. "Tell them, Custodian, or I will, and I have a much better speaking voice than you remember."

What happened next happened fast and happened badly, which Odile should have expected, because honest words in a dishonest place land like sparks in a hay barn.

The crowd didn't riot. Worse — it *wavered*, and wavering frightened the soldiers, and frightened soldiers reached for the tools they had. Someone shouted *blasphemy*. A pilgrim woman began to weep. Marrow's face went through grief and regret and arrived, with terrible smoothness, at sorrowful resolution, and he said, "Take her," and four men she had drilled with, eaten with, bled with, took her arms, and she fought — she fought the way he'd taught her, economical and vicious, and put two of them down, and then a third got her sword arm and a fourth got her throat and the ground came up and met her face.

The last thing she saw clearly was the mound: Silas breaking free of the crowd's edge, Wren sprinting, the ghost rising over the altar like smoke given intent — and Marrow looking down at her with genuine, bottomless sadness, the saddest look in the world, the look of a man watching a beloved student fail an examination he'd set her up to fail.

"Oh, Odile," he said. "You were the best of them."

Then the boot, and the grey light, and the grass against her cheek, and the sound of Wren screaming her name from very far away, and the crowd's murmur turning over and over like a stone in a stream: *the fallen one, the fallen one, even the best of them fall.*

* * *

They put her in a supply wagon under guard, which was an insult, and left her unbound, which was a mistake, and did neither because of mercy. Both because Marrow wanted her to watch. She understood that with perfect clarity as the camp struck its tents around her and the great column formed up — three hundred strong, banners of ash-grey silk, pilgrims singing the Processional with tears on their faces — and began to move north toward Hollowmere, toward the wardline, toward the vault where a thing made of murdered gods dreamed her name along with everyone else's.

Her ribs were cracked. Her pride was somewhere past cracked, in country where cartographers gave up. She lay in the swaying wagon and inventoried her failures with the thoroughness Marrow had beaten into her: she had gotten captured, gotten used, gotten beaten in front of the people she'd meant to wake, and freed no one, including possibly herself.

And yet.

Because she had also seen, in that wavering crowd, faces that hadn't wavered toward Marrow. Faces that had wavered *away*. A soldier who had looked at the altar and then at his own hands. The weeping pilgrim woman, who had stopped weeping at the words *who killed them* and started listening. Doubt, planted publicly, in front of three hundred witnesses, in soil Marrow had spent three centuries keeping fallow. It would grow or it wouldn't. She wouldn't be there to tend it.

Because she knew Marrow, and Marrow did not leave loose threads, and by nightfall he would decide she was worth more as an example than as a prisoner, and examples were made at the front of columns, at wardlines, at moments of maximum theater.

Fine, she thought, testing her wrists against the wagon's slats, finding the nail-head loose exactly where a lifetime of habit said a nail-head would be loose. Fine. Let him make his example. She had one move left, and it wasn't a good one, and it was going to hurt, and she found — lying in the ash-smelling dark, listening to three hundred hopeful voices sing a lie northward — that she didn't mind. Humiliation burned clean, if you let it. It burned away everything except the next thing.

The next thing was escaping before nightfall. The next thing was finding Silas and Wren, wherever the ghost had dragged them in the chaos. The next thing was beating Marrow's column to the valley — broken ribs, no sword, no rank, no order, nothing but a stolen knife and a grudge with a litany attached.

The wagon jolted. Somewhere ahead, faint on the wind off the northern hills, the roadside shrines had begun again — that thin, impossible singing, the dead gods' fragments calling out into the dusk like lamps lit in windows.

And Odile, working the nail loose with bleeding fingers, began to laugh — quietly, painfully, so the guards wouldn't hear — because she knew that song. Marrow had taught it to her as a lullaby.

He just had never known what it was actually for.
