## 14. The Bargain of Small Gods

The first shrine was so small Silas nearly stepped on it.

It sat in the crook of a dead oak's roots, three days past the wardline, no bigger than a bread loaf—a squat cairn of river stones with a hollow at its heart where something had once rested. Moss had taken the stones the way moss takes everything patient enough. But when Silas knelt to brush it clean, the hollow sang.

Not loudly. Not the way the vessel in the vault had sung, all hunger and borrowed faces. This was thin as winter light through a shutters' crack, and old, and tired.

*Bone-carver,* it said.

Silas's hands went still over the stones. Behind them on the road, Wren was arguing with the ghost about whether ghosts could get bored—"You *hum*, that's basically complaining"—and Odile walked point with her sword loose in its scabbard, because Odile always walked point now, even when there was nothing ahead but mud and honest rain.

"Hello," Silas said to the shrine, quietly, so the others wouldn't hear and come running. Talking aloud to empty stone felt foolish. Then again, so did hearing voices from femurs, and Silas had made a life of that.

*You hear us,* said the small voice, wonderingly. *After all this time. You actually hear us.*

"I heard you dying," Silas said. "In the relic bone. I heard all of you."

A long silence. Rain ticked against the oak's bare branches. When the voice came again it was smaller still, if smallness could go further.

*Yes. That was us. What is left of us.*

---

They found six more shrines before nightfall.

That was the thing about the god-road—once Silas knew what to look for, the land confessed itself. A dip in a field where wildflowers grew in a perfect ring: a shrine buried under centuries of plow-soil. A leaning milestone with a cup-shaped wear in its crown, worn by ten thousand offerings that had stopped coming three hundred years ago. A spring that tasted faintly of iron and, when Silas cupped a hand into it, whispered.

The fragments were everywhere. Scattered like shrapnel across the whole scarred face of the world outside Hollowmere, each one a splinter of something that had once been vast. The god of thresholds lived in a milestone. The god of harvests slept in a buried ring of stones. Something that might have been a god of rivers or of grief or of both pooled cold in a spring and spoke in water-drip syllables.

And every one of them knew Silas's name.

"That's the part I don't like," Wren announced around a mouthful of travel bread, sitting with their back against the milestone while the ghost hovered near the wildflower ring, listening with an attention that had no eyes to focus. "Gods knowing your name. In my experience, when something powerful knows your name, the next sentence is usually 'bring me your firstborn' or 'kneel.'"

"The vessel knew my name too," Silas said.

"The vessel," Odile said from where she stood watch on the road's edge, "wanted to eat you politely." She said it flatly, but Silas caught the flicker at the corner of her mouth. Three weeks on the road and Ser Odile Vane had developed something almost resembling humor, dry as old kindling and about as warm, but hers. "These are different."

"How can you tell?"

"Because the vessel never would have let me sleep." She nodded at Silas's bedroll. "You've been sleeping. Badly, snoring, but sleeping. Whatever these things are, they're not circling."

Wren pointed the bread at them both. "Counterpoint: we are outside the wards, in the open, following a trail of whispering rocks toward we-don't-know-what, based on the advice of a ghost who until recently could only communicate by possessing farm animals."

"I apologized about the goose," the ghost said, through Wren's own mouth, which made Wren jump and swat at their own lips.

"You don't apologize for the goose! You apologize for the *goat*. The goat is still haunted!"

Silas laughed—actually laughed, out loud, under an open hostile sky—and was surprised by how much room there was inside the sound. Back in Hollowmere they had learned to laugh quietly, folded up small, laughter kept in a drawer like contraband. Out here it just came out of them, big and unguarded, and nobody flinched, and the world went on turning.

The fragments waited. They were very good at waiting. They'd had three hundred years of practice.

---

It was the seventh shrine, on the fourth morning, that made the offer.

This one stood alone on a hilltop where the god-road crested above a valley of ruined terraces—the bones of old farms, old walls, an old town with nothing left standing higher than a knee. The shrine was larger here, waist-high, and instead of one hollow it had seven, arranged in a pattern Silas's fingers understood before their mind did: a constellation. Someone had built this place to hold seven gods at once, back when there were seven gods to hold.

Silas climbed the hill alone at dawn, because the fragments had asked—not commanded, *asked*, which was itself strange—and because some part of them had known since the first cairn that this conversation was coming, and wanted to have it standing up.

The hilltop smelled of wet grass and far-off woodsmoke. Below, their camp was a smudge of grey: Odile drilling forms in the mist with the grim discipline of a woman who trusted nothing, not even mornings; Wren still asleep, curled around their satchel of stolen prayer-ledgers like a dragon on a hoard of guilt; the ghost sitting—ghosts shouldn't be able to sit, but it managed it somehow, perched on a stone with its knees drawn up, watching Silas climb with an expression that had been worried for three days straight.

*Bone-carver,* said the hilltop shrine, and this time the voice was not one voice. It was many, braided thin, a cord spun from seven fraying threads. *We will not waste your morning with pleasantries. You know what we are. You heard us die.*

"I heard you screaming," Silas corrected. "There's a difference."

*Is there?* A ripple through the braid, almost wry. *You sound like our youngest.*

"The ghost?"

*Our youngest hid rather than burn. Clever child.* Fondness moved through the chorus like wind through wheat. *We sent it dreams for three hundred years and it remembered none of them. And then it found you. Or you found it. The order of such things matters less than people think.*

Silas crouched by the shrine and put their palm flat on the cold stone. The chorus gathered beneath their hand like fish under ice.

"What do you want from me?"

*What we want,* said the threads, *is to ask. No one has asked anything of us in three hundred years except to give. So: we ask. The vessel wakes within the season. Marrow marches with thousands behind him, and every prayer those poor souls speak feeds the thing that ate us. When it wakes fully, it will wear our faces and speak our names and rule the living through the shape of our love. Unless.*

"Unless."

*Unless someone carries what remains of us. Our strength is scattered, bone-carver. Seven shrines on this road, forty more beyond it, embers banked in stone and spring and root, each too small to strike a spark alone. Gathered—carried together, held in one living frame—we might be enough to do what must be done at the vault. To unmake what was made of us. To sing our true names into that thing until it remembers its own belly and grieves apart.*

"And you need a body to carry you." Silas's throat was dry. "Mine."

*Yours,* the chorus agreed, gently. *You who already listen. You who already grieve with strangers' bones in your hands. We could not ask a farmer. We could not ask a knight—she burns too bright, she would spend us in a week. We could not ask our youngest; it has nothing left to carry with. But you—you were made for holding. Every memorial you ever carved was a small act of carrying. We have watched you do it your whole life.*

The dawn wind moved the grass. Somewhere below, Wren had woken and was complaining audibly about the quality of road porridge, and Odile was telling them, in her flattest voice, that road porridge had killed fewer people than Wren's cooking, and Silas loved them both so suddenly and so fiercely that it hurt, right in the sternum, where a person keeps the things they can't set down.

"And if I say yes?" Silas asked. "What happens to me?"

The pause was long. Honest things pause; lies answer instantly. Silas had learned that from bones.

*You remain,* the chorus said at last. *That is the truth of it and we will not soften it. You remain yourself—your fears, your stubbornness, your terrible singing, all of it. But you will never again be only human. You will hear what we hear. Carry what we carry. Every prayer spoken anywhere near you will land on your skin like weather. Every death within a day's walk will pass through you on its way to wherever deaths go. You will be a door, bone-carver, and doors do not close only when it is convenient.*

"For the rest of my life."

*For the rest of your life. Which will be shorter than it should be. Divinity is heavy, and mortal frames pay interest.* Another ripple, and this one was unmistakably sorrow. *We will not pretend otherwise. The last human who carried a fragment of us lasted nine years, and he called them the best years of his life, and we have never decided whether that comforts us or shames us.*

Silas looked down the hill at their people. At Odile, mid-form, precise and unyielding, a woman who had burned her orders and was still deciding daily whether she regretted it. At Wren, arguing with the ghost now, gesturing with a spoon, alive and guilty and trying. At the nameless one, perched on its stone, wearing worry like a borrowed coat.

Three weeks ago Silas Galloway had been invisible. Carefully, expertly invisible—a lifetime of quiet work and quieter words, carving memorials for people who never learned their carver's name, hearing the dead through bone and telling no one, because in Hollowmere the price of being seen was exile, and the price of being *known* was worse.

Now two people and a ghost would follow them off a cliff. And the gods themselves had learned Silas's name and *asked*.

It would be so easy to say yes. That was the trap of it, and Silas saw the trap clearly, because they'd spent forty years studying traps—it was easy the way the vessel's offer had been easy, the way every temptation worth fearing is easy. Say yes, become more than human, stop being small. Stop being the one who kneels in the dirt with shavings in their hair. Become the thing people look *up* at.

And then Silas thought of the drowned child, the first voice, the little girl whose last word had been *mama*, and how Silas had carved her name into the femur and wept alone at the workbench because there was no one to tell. How the worst part hadn't been the hearing. It had been the *alone*.

Every god in this shrine had died alone. That was what the wards had really sealed—not magic out, but the sound of it in. A whole valley taught, for three hundred years, that the dead are silent and the divine is distant and you keep your grief in a drawer.

"No," Silas said.

The chorus went very still.

"Not like that." Silas stayed crouched, hand on the stone, and chose the words the way they chose cuts in bone—slowly, along the grain. "I won't be your vessel. I won't gather you into my body and become a door that's always open and a name people pray to. I've met the thing that happens when worship pools in one place. I've *heard* it digesting." They took a breath. "But I'll carry you anyway. Just—differently."

*Speak,* said the seven threads, and underneath the calm Silas heard hope, raw and startled, like a dog that has forgotten what kindness sounds like.

"One ember each. Not all of you—one fragment, one spark, shared out among everyone willing to hold one. Me, yes, if you'll have me—but Wren too, and Odile, and the ghost most of all, and when we get home—" Silas's voice caught on *home* and steadied. "When we take Hollowmere back, every household that wants one gets a listening-bone with an ember of you in it. Not a god to pray to. A god to *talk to*. Small enough to sit on a shelf. Close enough to answer. You don't need one body, any of you. You need a thousand small homes."

Silence on the hilltop. Long enough that Silas began to wonder if they'd just insulted the last remnants of the divine into fading out entirely.

Then the chorus broke—not apart, but *open*, the way ice breaks in spring, and the sound that came out of the shrine was laughing. Seven-threaded, rusted, disused laughter, the laughter of things that had not been offered anything but fear or feeding in three hundred years.

*A thousand small homes,* it repeated. *Our youngest told us you were stubborn. It undersold you.*

"It does that."

*Very well, bone-carver. Not a vessel. A carpenter.* Warmth moved up through the stone into Silas's palm, small and bright as a coal wrapped in wool. *Take the first ember now. Learn its weight before you promise others theirs. And know this: what we give, we give freely, and you may set it down whenever you choose. That is the whole of the bargain. That has always been the whole of the bargain. The ones who stole us never understood that giving can be free.*

The ember settled into Silas's chest like a swallowed star.

It didn't hurt. That surprised them most. It was warm, and aware, and *small*—humbly, deliberately small, a candle-flame's worth of divinity curled up behind the breastbone, and through it Silas felt the shrine's attention fold away contented, like an old man pulling a blanket to his chin.

Then Silas stood up too fast, and the world tilted, and every death within a day's walk brushed past them like wind through an open window—faint, distant, a hare somewhere in the bracken, an old woman asleep in a town two valleys over, her passing gentle as a lamp going out—and Silas gripped the shrine and breathed until the window narrowed back to a crack.

"Oh," they said, to no one. "Oh, that's going to take practice."

---

"So let me summarize," Wren said an hour later, pacing the length of the camp with the intensity of a scribe who had found an error in a holy text. "You met the actual gods. The murdered gods. The ones the entire Ashen Order is built on top of. And your opening move was *negotiation*."

"They asked nicely."

"They're *gods*."

"They're *fragments*," Silas said. "There's a difference. Ask Odile."

Odile was cleaning her sword and pretending very hard not to have been listening. "Fragments behave differently than wholes," she allowed. "A whole god expects a throne. A fragment is grateful for a shelf." She paused. "My order's oldest heresy texts say something similar. I used to think they were forgeries." Her jaw tightened. "I'm going to have to reread a great deal with new eyes."

"You're all taking this remarkably well," the ghost said, through the mouth of a crow it had borrowed for the occasion, because apparently direct speech was still tiring. It hopped along the camp's edge, feathers ruffled with something between awe and alarm. "The gods are awake, they've moved into Silas, and everyone's having *breakfast*."

"Porridge," Wren corrected darkly.

"An ember moved into me," Silas said. "One. There are six more shrines on this stretch of road and forty beyond, and I promised them a thousand small homes, which means I have just volunteered all of us to spend the march to Hollowmere knocking on every rock and spring between here and the wardline." They looked around the fire—at Odile's raised eyebrow, at Wren's slow spreading grin, at the crow's tilted head. "Unless anyone objects."

Wren sat down hard, hugged their knees, and started laughing—that cracked, relieved laughter of someone who had expected to be damned and gotten drafted into a rescue mission instead. "I ran away from the Order to escape responsibility," they said, "and now I'm a *reliquary intern*."

"We'll put it on your headstone," Odile said. "'Here lies Wren. Answered prayers, expected none, received several anyway.'"

The crow made a sound that was almost a laugh, and then went still, head cocking toward the south road. Its borrowed voice, when it came, had lost all humor.

*Riders,* the ghost said. *Many. Banners I remember from the Order's ledgers. And at their center, a palanquin, and in the palanquin—*

It didn't finish. It didn't need to. Silas felt it through the new ember: a wrongness on the southern wind, vast and patient, wearing seven familiar shapes.

Marrow wasn't marching for Hollowmere anymore.

He was marching for *them*.
