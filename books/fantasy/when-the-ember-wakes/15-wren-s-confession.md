## 15. Wren's Confession

The fire had burned down to that particular hour of orange and gray when Wren finally said it out loud.

They had been circling the words for three days. Circling them the way a moth circles a lamp, closer each time, wings singeing, unable to land. They had helped Odile bind her split knuckles. They had cooked the rabbit badly on purpose, because burnt rabbit bought time, and time was the only currency they had left to spend before someone asked the question Wren could not answer honestly.

Silas sat across the fire mending a carving knife with a whetstone and infinite patience. Odile stood watch at the treeline out of habit, though there was nothing left to watch for except her own reflection in the dark. The nameless ghost — *the youngest*, they knew now, though it still could not say its own name aloud without borrowing a mouth — hovered near Wren like a second shadow that had opinions.

"I have to tell you something," Wren said, "and you're going to hate me, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide how much."

Silas set down the whetstone. That was the thing about Silas: they gave every conversation their whole hands.

"Go on," Silas said.

Wren's mouth went dry. Their hands, which could copy two hundred prayers a day in a hand so beautiful the Order used it for official documents, shook like a novice's.

"When I was twelve, the Ashen Order took me into the scriptorium. Not as punishment. As an honor. They said I had a gift." Wren laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "They were right. I did have a gift. I just didn't know what it was *for*."

* * *

Here is what Wren told them, and here is what Wren had been carrying like a stone sewn into the lining of their coat:

The Order's scriptorium did not just record prayers. It *collected* them. Every pilgrim who came to the valley's edge shrines wrote a petition — for rain, for a sick mother, for forgiveness, for a child to come home — and every petition passed through the hands of a scribe before it was filed away in the deep archive beneath the Order's citadel. The scribes believed they were preserving history. Preserving faith. Keeping the old words safe until the gods returned.

Wren had been the fastest scribe in a generation. Wren's copies were the ones chosen for the *inner* archive. The one below the other one. The one nobody called by its real name because nobody below High Custodian rank knew it existed.

"I copied thousands," Wren said. "Tens of thousands. Prayers written by people who were grieving, starving, desperate, hopeful. Beautiful prayers. Stupid prayers. A man once prayed for his cow to stop being haunted, and I copied that too, because a prayer is a prayer." Their voice thinned. "And every single one of them went down into the dark. And something down there drank them."

Silas said nothing. Odile, at the treeline, had gone very still — stiller than watchfulness, stiller than stone.

"And it wasn't just copying," Wren went on, because now that the dam had cracked they couldn't stop the flood. "There were days — feast days, mourning days — when the Order would send criers out to the shrine roads. *Come pray*, they'd say. *The gods are stirring. Your voice matters.* We'd get four hundred pilgrims where we usually got forty. And I sat at my desk with my beautiful ink and my beautiful hand and I transcribed every petition, and I felt *good*, do you understand? I felt useful. I felt holy. I thought I was building a bridge back to the divine." Wren pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "I was building a feeding trough."

The fire popped. Somewhere an owl complained about the owl business of owls, which was the only honest commentary anyone offered for a long moment.

"The pilgrims," Silas said quietly. "You said you led them. How?"

"There was a route." Wren's voice had gone flat, the way voices go when they've been rehearsed too many times in the dark. "The inner archive wasn't fed directly from the scriptorium. Petitions had to be carried down in sealed cases, once a season, by a trusted junior. Someone young enough not to ask questions, good enough to be trusted, and — this is the part I've never said to anyone — someone whose handwriting matched the case seals, so no one else could open them en route." They swallowed. "I carried the cases. Eleven times. Down through the vault corridors, past doors that hummed, to a chamber where a custodian in a gray mask took them from me and never let me see inside. Eleven times I walked away feeling like a saint."

"And the humming doors," Silas said. It wasn't a question. They had heard those doors in their bones; Wren had heard them with ears. Two instruments recording the same catastrophe.

"The humming doors," Wren agreed miserably.

Odile turned from the treeline. She didn't come closer. She just turned, which for Odile was practically an embrace.

"The route," she said. "The shrine road circuit. Did it pass through Ferrow's Hollow?"

Wren's stomach dropped. "Yes. Second shrine on the eastern loop. Why—"

"Because I escorted those caravans," Odile said. Her jaw was tight enough to crack walnuts. "For six years, I walked pilgrims down a road toward a thing that ate their prayers and gave them nothing, and I called it protection duty. You weren't the only one being used, scribe. You were just the only one who didn't know."

That should have made it better. Somehow it made everything worse, because if Odile was guilty then Wren couldn't be uniquely damned, and Wren had built quite a comfortable little cathedral out of being uniquely damned. Guilt, Wren was beginning to learn, could be a form of pride. It was the pride of the self-flagellant: *no one has ever sinned as specifically as I have.*

"That's not the worst part," Wren said, because apparently they were determined to excavate all the way down to the aquifer tonight. "The worst part is Tam."

Silas looked up sharply. "Tam?"

"A pilgrim. Old man, bad knees, walked the eastern loop every season for nine years praying for his daughter to come home. She'd run off to the lowlands. He wrote the same prayer every time, and I recognized the handwriting after the third year, and I started — I don't know what I thought I was doing — I started writing little answers." Wren's voice broke clean in half. "Nothing false. Just — *your prayer has been received. Be patient. She thinks of you.* Things the vessel whispered through the bones, things I heard when I delivered the cases, things I copied down and slipped back into the shrine boxes so he'd keep walking. Keep praying. Keep *coming*. Because the Order wanted regulars, and regulars needed hope, and I was so good at making hope."

Silas's face did something complicated. "Did his daughter come home?"

"No." Wren wiped their nose on their sleeve, gracelessly, because dignity had left the conversation several minutes ago. "He died on the road two winters ago. Fell asleep in the snow between shrines. They found him with his last petition clutched in his fist, and I'm the one who transcribed it, and it said—" Wren stopped. Couldn't. Started again. "It said *I know you're listening. I know someone hears me. Thank you for hearing me.*"

The fire had burned very low. Nobody moved to feed it.

"He thanked you," Silas said softly.

"He thanked *it*. He thanked the thing that ate his prayers for nine years and never gave him anything but the taste of almost. And I put the words in his mouth. I taught him to be grateful to his predator." Wren curled forward, arms around their knees, small as a comma. "So. That's what I am. That's what I did. Say whatever you're going to say. I'd rather be hated accurately than loved on credit."

* * *

What followed was not absolution. Wren wants it noted, for the record of their own conscience, that what followed was not absolution.

Silas was silent for a long time. Long enough that the ghost drifted closer, restless, and borrowed the wind in the chimney-crack of the ruined wall to make a sound like a held breath.

"You want me to hate you," Silas said at last. "Or forgive you. Those are the two doors you've built."

"Isn't that how it works?"

"No." Silas turned the carving knife over in their hands, considering it the way they considered everything: as material. "Hate is easy and forgiveness is cheap, and both of them end the conversation, and I don't want this conversation to end. I want to ask you questions first. Is that all right?"

It was not all right. It was much worse than either door. But Wren nodded.

"Were you lied to?"

"Yes. Completely. Thoroughly. For years."

"Do you believe, honestly, in your gut, that if you had known — truly known — you'd have done it anyway?"

"No." Wren's throat closed. "I'd have burned the scriptorium down at fifteen and been executed for it, probably."

"Then here's what I hear," Silas said, and their voice had that quality it got sometimes, the bone-hearing quality, like they were listening to something underneath the words. "I hear a child who was taken by an institution and given a purpose-shaped hole to pour themselves into. I hear someone who did enormous harm inside a machine designed to hide the harm from the people operating it. And I hear someone who, the moment they saw the truth, ran. Not away. *Toward.* You crossed the wardline knowing what the Order does to deserters. You walked into a godless valley to find the person the dead spoke to. You didn't come confess because you got caught. You came confessing because you couldn't carry it alone anymore, and you chose us."

"That doesn't unmake Tam," Wren whispered.

"No," Silas said. "It doesn't. Nothing unmakes Tam. And I'm not going to tell you it's fine, because it isn't fine, and anyone who tells you it's fine is selling something." They poked the fire awake. Sparks climbed like tiny orange souls with somewhere to be. "But I'll tell you what I know about guilt, Wren Grieve, because I've studied it professionally. Guilt that gets confessed becomes weight you can carry. Guilt that stays hidden becomes a shape that wears you. You've been wearing yours so long you thought it was your body." They looked up, and their eyes in the firelight were steady and kind and absolutely unwilling to lie. "Take it off for one night. Not forever. One night. Tomorrow you pick it back up and we figure out what to *do* with it. But tonight, you're allowed to just be a person sitting by a fire with people who aren't leaving."

Wren cried then. Ugly, snotty, full-body crying, the kind they'd been rationing out in private teaspoonfuls since the vault. The ghost tried to comfort them by borrowing a moth's flutter against their cheek, which was sweet and also tickled, and Wren laughed wetly in the middle of sobbing, which is the most human sound there is.

* * *

Odile took longer.

She came and sat down across the fire with the deliberate heaviness of a woman preparing for a hard task, and Wren braced for judgment, because judgment was Odile's native language even when she was fluent in kindness.

"I have a question," Odile said. "Answer honestly and I'll tell you what I've decided. When you found out what the cases were for — really found out, in the vault, when it whispered our names — what was your first instinct?"

"To run," Wren said. "My first instinct is always to run. I'm a scribe. We're not brave, we're fast with our hands."

"Mm. And your second?"

"To burn the ledgers. All of them. Every petition, every record, so it would starve." Wren looked up. "Why does that matter?"

"Because," Odile said, "when I read my orders in the citadel — the ones telling me to bring you in alive and Silas in pieces — my first instinct was obedience. Twenty years of training. My second instinct was to burn them." She held up her bandaged knuckles. "We are not so different, you and I. The Order builds people like buildings. Load-bearing walls of duty, and rooms behind the rooms where they keep the things they don't want seen. You were a room behind a room. So was I." She paused. "I'm not forgiving you tonight. I want to be clear about that. Forgiveness is a ledger-entry and I've stopped trusting ledgers. What I'm doing is *reclassifying* you. From asset of the enemy to comrade with debts. Debts get paid. Assets get used. I'd rather march beside someone paying debts than someone collecting on mine."

Wren blinked. "That's the least comforting comfort I've ever received."

"I'm told I have that effect." But the corner of Odile's mouth moved, one degree, which from Odile was a festival.

Then Silas, who had been quiet in the way that meant gears were turning, said: "Tam's last prayer. Do you remember it? Word for word?"

Wren flinched. "Every word. I transcribed it twice. Once for the archive and once because I couldn't stop."

"Say it."

"Silas—"

"Say it. Not for punishment. Because he wrote it to be heard, and everyone who was supposed to hear it was a thief. Let it be heard once by people who won't steal it."

So Wren said it. Into the fire, into the dark, hands shaking, voice cracking on the third line and holding after that. *I know you're listening. I know someone hears me. I don't need her to come home anymore, I just needed to know the words went somewhere. Thank you for hearing me. Whoever you are. Whatever you are.*

When they finished, the ghost did something none of them expected. It gathered itself — all its borrowed winds and moth-wings and cold-spot shivers — and it pushed, gently, against the edge of the world, and for perhaps three seconds Wren heard a voice that was not borrowed at all. Thin. Young. Rusty from centuries of disuse.

*"Heard,"* it said. *"He was heard. Tell him."*

Then it frayed back into silence, exhausted, and the fire settled, and nobody spoke for a while because some silences are full to bursting.

"We'll tell him," Silas said finally. "At the funeral. Our funeral. The honest one. When this is over, Hollowmere is going to have its first real funeral in three hundred years, and Tam is going to be on the list, and you're going to stand up and read his prayer yourself, out loud, to living people, and it will finally go where it was always supposed to go." They pointed the carving knife at Wren with great gentleness. "That's your debt-payment plan. First installment. There will be more. Possibly involving manual labor."

"I can't write a eulogy," Wren said. "I only write prayers."

"Then you'll write him a prayer that expects no answer," Silas said. "Just words going somewhere. It turns out that's the only kind that ever worked anyway."

* * *

Later — much later, when Odile had taken first watch and Silas slept with the bone-carver's ability to fall asleep anywhere, mid-sentence, like a dropped puppet — Wren sat up and pulled out their journal.

They did not sleep. Sleep was for people whose insides matched their outsides. Instead they wrote, because writing was the only prayer Wren had ever honestly known how to make, and tonight the prayer went like this:

*Tam, I can't give you back nine years or one winter. I can't give your daughter back to you, wherever she is, whether she knows her father froze walking toward her ghost. Here is what I can give: I will spend what's left of my life making sure no one else prays into a mouth that eats. I will unbind every stolen word in that vault if it takes my hands to the wrist. I will read your prayer to people with ears. And I will never again mistake being useful for being good.*

*This is not a request for forgiveness. This is a work order.*

They closed the journal. Across the dying fire, Silas opened one eye — of course Silas was awake, Silas was always awake at exactly the wrong and right moments — and said, sleep-roughened:

"For what it's worth, the burnt rabbit was terrible on purpose, wasn't it."

"It was terrible entirely on purpose."

"I respect the craft." Silas closed the eye again. Then, a breath later: "Hey, Wren? Belonging isn't a reward for being clean. It's just what happens when people keep showing up. You showed up. That's the whole liturgy."

Wren lay back on the cold ground under a threadbare blanket and discovered that belonging, it turned out, felt approximately like exhaustion and ash-smell and a ghost humming something tuneless nearby, and that they would fight anyone who tried to take it away.

They were nearly asleep when Odile's voice cut sharp across the camp from the treeline.

"Firelight. East road. A lot of it."

Wren sat up. Far off, down the valley's throat, the darkness was studded with lights — dozens, then scores, a slow river of torches winding along the old shrine road, moving toward Hollowmere. Moving the way pilgrims move. Moving the way a tide moves when something big underneath it pulls.

"They're coming," Odile said grimly, counting lights with a soldier's eye. "Hundreds of them. Thousands, maybe. Marrow's not sending soldiers." She turned to them, firelight carving her face into angles. "He's sending the faithful. He's marching them straight at the vault, and every one of them is carrying a prayer."

Wren's blood went cold, because Wren knew — better than anyone alive — exactly what a prayer could feed.
