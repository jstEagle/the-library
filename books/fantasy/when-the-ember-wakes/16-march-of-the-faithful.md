## 16. March of the Faithful

Odile Vane had marched behind many banners in her life. She had marched behind the Ashen Order's grey standard through two border wars, behind a funeral procession for her father, behind a wedding party she'd been paid to guard and secretly wept at. She knew what marching looked like from above — the way it moved like water finding its level, patient and unstoppable.

What came down the god-road toward Hollowmere did not march. It *poured*.

From the ridge above the ruined toll-arch, she counted them the way her instructors had taught her: by fires, by dust, by sound. Fires first — hundreds of small cook-fires strung along the road like beads on a wire, stretching back past the horizon's curve into the dark. Dust second, a pale haze that hung over the column even in the cold night air, kicked up by feet that would not stop moving. Sound last, and worst: not the barked cadence of soldiers but singing. Thousands of voices, ragged and off-key and utterly sincere, singing hymns Odile herself had transcribed copies of as a girl.

"They're not resting," Wren said beside her. The scribe was flat against the heather, knuckles white around a spyglass they'd stolen from a dead man who no longer needed it. "I've been watching for an hour. They stop to eat standing up. They stop to sleep — I saw people just lie down in the road and get up again an hour later. Nobody argues. Nobody cries. It's like—"

"Like being prayed into," Silas said quietly.

Odile looked at them. The bone-carver lay on their other side, chin propped on folded arms, watching the river of fire below with an expression Odile was beginning to recognize: not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of someone hearing a familiar voice from an unexpected room.

"Explain," she said.

"Every one of them has prayed to something," Silas said. "And something has answered. Once you've heard an answer — felt it, even if you can't prove it — everything else gets quieter. Food. Sleep. Sense." A pause. "The drowned girl's mother walked into my shop every day for a month after I carved the memorial. Not because I helped. Because someone had listened, and once you know listening exists, you can't unknow it."

"So Marrow hasn't compelled them," Odile said slowly. The thought arrived cold and settled colder. "He doesn't need to. He gave them answers, and hope did the rest."

"He's a shepherd," Wren said bitterly. "A very good one. I should know — I wrote half his sermons." They said it flatly, the way they always said such things now, offering the wound before anyone else could. Odile had learned, in the weeks since the vault, to take those offerings gently and put them somewhere safe rather than hand them back.

"You wrote sermons," she said. "Marrow wrote this one." She nodded at the column. "Look at the edges of it. Look at the flanks."

Wren raised the glass again. A long silence. "Riders," they said. "Maybe forty. Grey cloaks."

"Order remnants, like us. But look where they are. Not driving the pilgrims. *Herding* them. Keeping the column tight so nobody wanders off and starts thinking their own thoughts." She traced the line of it with one finger against the ground. "And there—" the vanguard, a knot of torches moving a half-mile ahead of the main body, "—that's the point of the spear. That's where Marrow will be. He'll want to be first through the wardline when it breaks. First to the vault. He won't leave his miracle to chance or to anyone else's hands."

"Then we don't fight the river," Silas said. "We race it."

Odile had already drawn the map in her head, because of course she had; she'd spent the ride up from the ash-camp doing nothing else, and had dreamed about it too, which she considered excessive. "Two roads to Hollowmere. This one — the god-road — is wide and graded and built for processions. Ours cuts over the fells through the old quarry. Half the distance, three times the misery. If we push hard tonight and harder tomorrow, we reach the wardline a day ahead of them. Maybe two."

"And then what?" Wren asked. "We get there first and do what? Knock politely?"

"No," Odile said. "Then we find whatever's left of the wards and we learn how to break them ourselves, before Marrow does — because if he breaks them with ten thousand faithful standing behind him, every soul in that valley hears the gods' death-scream at once, and some of them will kneel to whatever comes out of the vault wearing their grandmother's voice." She rolled onto her knees. "But if we break them first — if we break them with just the three of us and one ghost who owes us nothing — then maybe, maybe, we can be standing between the vessel and the faithful when they arrive. A wall of three people. Against ten thousand."

"That's a terrible plan," Wren said.

"It's the only plan."

"It's still terrible. I just wanted it noted for posterity." Wren scrambled up, brushing heather seeds from their coat with the fussiness Odile had come to understand was how they handled terror. "Posterity will note that I objected."

"Noted," Silas said, and smiled at them both — a small, tired, real smile — and Odile felt the thing she always felt lately, the treacherous warmth of it, and stamped it down where it couldn't slow her feet.

They went down into the dark, away from the singing.

* * *

The old quarry road had been abandoned for longer than Odile had been alive, and it showed. The switchbacks were grown over with gorse that grabbed at her cloak like small desperate hands. In places the path simply wasn't anymore, and they picked their way across scree while loose stone clattered down into blackness below, each clatter sounding, to Odile's trained ear, exactly like an ambush that never quite committed.

By the third hour of climbing, Wren had begun narrating their own suffering.

"I want the record to show," they panted, hauling themselves over a ledge, "that I was a scribe. Scribes sit. Scribes have chairs. Sometimes scribes have cushions. There were cushions in the Order's scriptorium, Odile, I sat on them for years, and now I am dying on a mountain and my last words are going to be about *cushions*."

"Your last words will be 'I told you this plan was terrible,'" Odile said, reaching back to grip their wrist and pull. "Which is a better epitaph than most soldiers get."

"What do yours will be?"

"'Should have taken the long road.'"

Silas, ahead of them both, laughed — actually laughed, short and surprised out of them — and Odile found herself absurdly pleased, in a way she filed under *later, deal with later*, because the bone-carver laughed perhaps once a fortnight and never on purpose.

It was near midnight when the ghost stopped them.

It didn't appear so much as arrive — the air between two leaning stones going suddenly the temperature of a cellar, and then the shape was there, faint as breath on glass, its borrowed face flickering between a dozen features like a coin spun too fast to read.

"*They've started,*" it said, in a voice assembled from scraps — a child's lilt, an old man's rasp, none of them its own. "*Listen.*"

Odile listened. At first: wind. Stone settling. Her own pulse. And then, threading underneath, thin as wire—

Prayers. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Rising off the distant road like heat-shimmer, except these weren't sounds she was hearing. She understood that dimly, the way you understand a dream's logic. They were arriving directly behind her eyes, uninvited, in voices she almost recognized.

*"Blessed vessel, wake, my daughter is sick and the valley doctors have nothing—"*

*"Wake, wake, I buried three children and I never got to say—"*

*"Whatever you are, whoever ate you, I don't care, just tell me there's more than this—"*

Odile pressed her palms against her ears and it made no difference whatsoever. The prayers weren't coming through her ears. They were coming through the fact of her, the way cold comes through a wall.

Beside her, Wren had gone rigid. "That's my handwriting," they whispered. "Those are my transcriptions. I copied those petitions into the ledger myself, I — that woman, the third voice, she brought her prayer to the Order house in Corvasser, I remember the ink she used, she'd mixed it with ash because she was poor—" Their voice cracked clean through. "She's walking toward Hollowmere right now because of me."

"Because of Marrow," Odile said.

"Because of my *pen*." Wren wrapped their arms around themselves. "Do you hear what they're asking? They're asking it to *wake*. They think waking it is mercy. We fed them that word until it tasted like bread."

Silas had gone very still. Odile knew that stillness too now — the bone-hearing, the gift that had made them a heretic and a hero in the same week. When Silas finally spoke, their voice had the careful flatness of someone carrying something fragile across ice.

"They're not all wrong," Silas said. "Some of those prayers are honest. Grief is honest. Hope is honest. That's the horror of it — Marrow didn't have to lie to them. He only had to aim them." They turned to the ghost. "Can you tell how far ahead we are?"

The ghost's borrowed mouth worked. When it spoke, it chose — deliberately, Odile thought — a soldier's voice, clipped and measured. "*A day. Perhaps less, if the rain holds off and the road stays kind. More if it doesn't.*"

"The road won't stay kind," Odile said, looking up at clouds stacking themselves against the stars like siege works. "Nothing stays kind out here." She checked the sky, checked her bearings, and made the decision she'd been circling all evening. "We don't camp. We walk through. Cold rations, no fire, shifts sleeping while moving. I know what that costs. I also know what ten thousand kneeling worshippers costs, and I choose the blisters."

"You're enjoying this," Wren accused.

"Immensely," Odile said. "This is the happiest I've been since I was stripped of rank and branded a traitor. Can't you tell?"

* * *

They walked through the night, and the night walked through them.

Somewhere past the second hour, Odile realized she could still hear the prayers, fainter now, a tide going out — and layered beneath them, another sound she refused to examine too closely: her own name, spoken in her father's voice, coming from the direction of Hollowmere. *Come home, little owl. Come see what I built you.*

Her father had died eleven years ago. He had never called her *little owl* in his life. Whatever was doing the calling had read her file, not her heart, and somehow that distinction was the only thing keeping her sane.

She didn't mention it. Neither did Wren, though she caught them flinching at nothing twice. Neither did Silas, though their lips moved sometimes, silently answering someone. Only the ghost seemed untouched, drifting alongside like a lantern with no candle, and once it looked at Odile with its borrowed face and said, in her own mother's accent, "*It saves its best lies for those with the most to lose. Walk faster.*"

So she walked faster.

Dawn found them at the top of the fells, and Odile permitted them twenty minutes of sitting, which was not rest but was adjacent to it. Below and behind, the god-road glowed with the banked coals of a thousand cook-fires, the column still pouring east. Ahead, in the grey light, Hollowmere lay in its bowl like a secret kept too long — smoke rising straight from morning chimneys, fields stitched neat and green, utterly unaware of what was walking toward it.

"Look at it," Wren said softly. "Three hundred years of quiet. My whole life I thought the valley was a punishment. Exile. Now I'm looking down at it and it looks like—" They searched for the word.

"Home," Silas said.

"I was going to say 'a painting,' but yes. Also that."

Odile said nothing. She was watching the far side of the valley, where the land rose toward the vault's hidden mouth beneath the shrine-hill, and she was doing arithmetic she hated. Even at their best pace, Marrow's vanguard would reach the wardline within a day. Which meant whatever they intended to do — find the wards, learn them, break them first, stand in the gap — they had one day to become scholars of a magic the Ashen Order had spent three centuries hoarding.

One day, and three people, and one ghost with no name and no army and no body.

"We should go down," she said. "There's a track off the north shoulder, past the shrine at Miller's Bend. It'll bring us to the wardline by dusk."

As she stood, the ghost drifted up beside her — close enough that the cellar-cold of it touched her cheek, close enough that when it spoke, it used a voice she knew intimately, because she'd heard it every day for fifteen years of training.

It used *her* voice.

"*Knight,*" it said, in perfect Odile Vane, "*do you truly believe three can hold a door against ten thousand faiths?*"

Odile met the empty eyes without flinching. "No," she said honestly. "But I've seen what one honest voice can do to a lie. We're bringing three."

Below them, far off across the fells, the wind changed — and carried with it, thin and clear and terribly joyful, the sound of the faithful singing as they marched.

And underneath the singing, so faint she might have imagined it, the earth itself began — very slightly, like a sleeper turning toward an alarm — to hum.
