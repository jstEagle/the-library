## 17. The Wardline Breaks

The storm came up the valley from the south like a debt collector—slow at first, then all at once, and rude about it.

Silas felt it in the bones before the first thunderclap. They were sitting on their workbench stool, sanding a listening-bone for the Hallow family—the third one that week, because word had gotten around after the market fire, and now half of Hollowmere wanted to hear what their grandmothers had to say, and the other half wanted to make very sure they didn't. The femur in Silas's hands hummed faintly, the way a struck tuning fork hums, and then it hummed louder, and then it wasn't humming so much as *screaming*, politely, the way bones do.

"Oh," said Silas. "Oh, no."

Wren looked up from the corner, where they'd been practicing writing prayers that expected no answer—a skill they insisted was harder than it sounded, like learning to whistle backward. "What? What's 'oh no'? I don't like 'oh no.' We've had enough 'oh no' for one lifetime."

"The wards." Silas set down the bone. Their hands were shaking, which was inconvenient, because hands were rather the whole business of bone-carving. "They're not failing. They're being *pulled out*. Like teeth."

Odile was already moving. She'd been staying in the workshop's back room since the branding, sleeping in her armor out of what she claimed was habit and everyone else knew was grief, and she came through the doorway buckling her sword belt with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had done it ten thousand times and hated every one of them. "How long?"

"I don't know. Minutes. Maybe less." Silas pressed their palms flat against the workbench, against the wood that had held three hundred years of quiet shavings, and listened the way only they could listen. Beneath Hollowmere, beneath the fields and the millpond and the churchyard where nobody had ever heard a ghost because there was nothing left to hear them with—the dead were stirring. All of them. Every soul the valley had ever buried, waking at once like a congregation told the sermon was finally over.

And underneath them, deeper, something vast turning over in its sleep.

"It's Marrow," Odile said. Not a question.

"It's Marrow," Silas agreed. "He's at the wardline. He's—" They stopped. Swallowed. "He's singing the wards apart. He has the vessel's voice, and the wards were built to hold that voice in, and he's using it like a crowbar."

Wren stood up so fast their stool went over. They didn't pick it up. In three months Silas had never once seen Wren leave a stool standing; it was practically a religious observance with them. "Then we run. We get ahead of this, we warn the council, we—"

"The council exiled us, Wren."

"The council is *wrong*, Silas, that's not the same as deaf! You can be wrong and still have ears!"

Silas opened their mouth to argue—and the world broke.

* * *

There was no way to describe it afterward. Silas would spend years trying, carving it into bone for people who hadn't been inside the valley when it happened, and every version fell short. But the closest they ever got was this:

Imagine you have lived your whole life in a house beside a river, and you have never once heard the river, because the founders built the walls thick and the windows sealed, and you grew up believing silence was simply what the world sounded like. And then one night every wall comes down at once, and the river doesn't trickle in—it *arrives*, whole and ancient and enormous, and you discover that the silence was never empty. It was full. It had always been full. It was pressing against the walls the entire time, waiting.

Every dead voice in Hollowmere spoke at once.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way Silas usually heard the dead—one at a time, through carved bone, polite as letters. This was all of them, unmediated, unfiltered, pouring through the broken wards and into the valley air and into Silas's skull like water into a cracked cup.

The drowned child from the memorial femur, three years gone, saying *it was cold, it was so cold, why did nobody come.* The miller's wife who had died in childbirth forty years before Silas was born, humming a lullaby to a baby who had lived. Farmers. Soldiers from a war the histories called the Unrest and refused to describe. A man who had hanged himself behind the tannery in 211 and wanted, more than anything, someone to know it hadn't been his fault, the rope had been old, he'd only been testing it. Grandmothers. Children. A dog, somehow, though Silas couldn't begin to explain how a dog got into the afterlife's choir, and honestly the dog was the least of it.

Three hundred years of unsaid words, arriving in a single instant.

Silas went down. Not gracefully—they took the workbench with them, and a tray of carving knives, and Wren's abandoned prayer practice, which fluttered around the room like startled birds. They heard Odile shouting their name from very far away, and Wren's hands on their shoulders, warm and frantic and alive, and none of it mattered, because there was no room in Silas's head for anything but the dead.

*It was cold.*

*I never told her.*

*Who will tend the bees?*

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.*

*Is anyone there? Is anyone—*

*IT WAS COLD IT WAS SO COLD WHY DID NOBODY COME—*

Silas screamed. They were dimly aware of screaming, distantly embarrassed by it, the way you're embarrassed of things happening to a stranger across a crowded room. Then even that distance collapsed, and there was nothing but the flood.

And here is the thing about drowning: at some point, if you stop fighting, you notice the water isn't trying to kill you. It's just *there*, vast and indifferent and full of everything, and the drowning was always your own thrashing.

Silas stopped thrashing.

It happened without decision. Somewhere in the roar, some part of them—the part that had spent thirty years listening to one bone at a time, patiently, lovingly, the part that knew the dead weren't a chorus but a crowd, each one a person, each one *specific*—that part simply began doing its work. Sorting. One voice from another. The drowned child from the miller's wife from the dog. It was like tuning an instrument in the middle of an avalanche, and it should have been impossible, and Silas did it anyway, because it was the only thing they knew how to do.

The flood became a crowd. The crowd became individuals.

And individuals could be *heard*.

"—hear me, oh gods, please, someone has to hear me—" That was Tam Broadhurst, dead eleven years, buried under the rowan tree because his family couldn't afford a stone. He'd been saying it for eleven years into a silence that couldn't answer, and now the silence was broken and he was saying it into a valley where exactly one living person could hear him.

"I hear you," Silas said aloud, lying on the floor of their own workshop, blood or something like it crusted under their nose. "Tam. Tam Broadhurst. I hear you."

The voice stopped. Started again, smaller, disbelieving. "*You can—you can hear me? After all this time?*"

"I hear you."

"*Oh,*" said Tam Broadhurst, and wept, and eleven years of loneliness came off him like mud in a rainstorm, and somewhere in the middle of it Silas understood two things at once, the way you understand things when your skull has been cracked open and refilled.

The first thing: this was what the wards had stolen. Not safety. *This.* Three hundred years of the dead calling into a padded room, and the living never knowing. The valley's peace had never been peace. It had been a gag.

The second thing: Marrow hadn't just broken the wards. He'd broken them *open toward the vault*. Every voice in Hollowmere was pouring south, funneling, drawn like iron filings to the deep places where the vessel waited, hungry, and Marrow intended to feed it a valley's worth of souls and stand at its shoulder wearing the crown of sole interpreter.

"Get up," Silas said—to themselves, mostly, but Odile hauled them up anyway, which was faster. "We have to go. Now. Tonight."

"You can barely stand," Odile said.

"I can hear eleven thousand people, Ser Vane. Standing is the least of what I can do." Silas wiped their face, took stock of their body—it obeyed, mostly, in the way of a borrowed horse—and found, underneath the wreckage, something new. The flood hadn't receded. It had settled. The dead of Hollowmere were everywhere in their hearing now, a constant weather, and they could feel the shape of the whole valley in their head the way you feel your own hand in the dark.

Wren was staring at them with an expression caught between terror and awe. "Your eyes are doing something."

"What kind of something?"

"Glowy something."

"Wonderful. Add it to the list." Silas grabbed their coat, their knife roll, and—the thought arrived fully formed, non-negotiable—the unfinished listening-bone for the Hallow family. If they were going to march into the end of everything, they were going to do it with a half-carved femur in their pocket, because the Hallow family had paid a deposit and Silas Galloway did not die in debt.

Outside, the storm had arrived properly, rain coming down in ropes, lightning standing the world up in white flashes. Down the valley, toward the southern ridge where the wardstones had stood for three centuries, the sky was the wrong color—not storm-gray but ember-orange, lit from below, as though the ground itself had opened a furnace door.

And the voices. Gods, the voices. Every house in Hollowmere had someone dead in it, under it, near it, and now every one of those dead was awake and speaking, and the living were coming out of their doors into the rain to stand in their yards and look at the orange sky and feel—for the first time in three hundred years—the unmistakable sensation of being *listened to from below*.

An old woman stood in the road outside the workshop, soaked through, arms wrapped around herself. Silas knew her—Marta Quill, who sold apples and had buried a husband and two sons. She was looking straight at Silas with an expression of terrible hope.

"They're talking," she said. "I can't hear them. But I can feel them *trying*. Like birds against a window." Her chin trembled. "Boy. Girl. Whatever you are. Are they angry?"

Silas listened. Really listened, past the fear and the cold and the eleven thousand overlapping sorrows, to the actual grain of it.

"No," they said, and found it was true. "They're lonely. They've been lonely for three hundred years, and they don't know why, and tonight the wall came down and they can finally see the house they died in." They knelt in the mud so they were eye level with her. "One of them is asking about bees. Someone needs to tend the bees. Does that mean anything?"

Marta Quill put a hand over her mouth. "Aldous," she whispered. "My husband kept bees. Nobody's touched the hives since he—" She stopped. Straightened. Something moved through her face like weather clearing, and what was underneath it was not comfort, exactly, but it was adjacent to comfort, and it was more than she'd had five minutes ago. "Go," she said. "Whatever you're going to do. Go do it. I'll tend the bees."

* * *

They went south through the rain, the three of them and the ghost, who had appeared at the wardline of the workshop gate looking rattled in the way only a dead person can look rattled—which is to say, flickering.

"That's my name," the ghost said, using Wren's mouth, because that was the arrangement, and Wren had long ago stopped flinching at the possession and started complaining about the taste instead ("like licking a candle," they'd said, "a *sad* candle"). "The youngest god. The one who hid. Silas—I can feel them. My siblings. All of them. The vessel is pulling what's left of us south, and if it finishes swallowing—" The ghost's grip on Wren's jaw tightened. "There won't be enough left of any of us to remember what we were. Including me. Including *you*. Your line carried me, Silas. If I go, whatever I am in you goes too."

"Then we walk faster," Silas said.

They walked faster.

The valley unrolled around them in the lightning flashes—Hollowmere, home, the place that had branded Silas a witch six days ago and driven them out, the place whose dead were currently screaming their three-hundred-year confession into the rain. Silas had every right to let it burn. They turned the thought over as they walked, examining it honestly, because Odile had taught them that much: examine the ugly thoughts or they'll examine you.

No. Not burn. Never burn. The valley had made them, ward-silence and all, the way a shell makes a pearl out of an irritation. And besides—

Besides, the dead were *talking*, and most of what they were saying, underneath everything, was the same thing Tam Broadhurst had said. *Is anyone there? Is anyone—*

"Yes," Silas said aloud, to all of them, walking south into the fire-colored rain. "We're here. Hold on. We're coming."

Odile glanced over. "Talking to yourself?"

"Talking to eleven thousand people. It's different."

"How?"

"Eleven thousand people don't interrupt."

That earned the ghost of a smile—pun acknowledged internally, apology never issued—from Odile, and a snort from Wren, and even the ghost laughed in Wren's mouth, which produced a sound like a hiccup played backward, and for one absurd moment the four of them were laughing in a storm at the end of the world, and Silas thought: *this. Whatever happens tonight, this was worth it. This is what the gods died never having. Somebody walking beside you in the rain, making terrible jokes.*

The ridge rose ahead, black against the ember light. At its crest, where the last wardstone had stood, the air itself was torn—a seam in the world, edges curling like burnt paper, and through it Silas could hear not the dead of Hollowmere but something older, vaster, a sound like a cathedral full of whales grieving.

And beneath it, threaded through it, clear as a bell in a landslide, a voice Silas knew.

Marrow. Singing.

And answering him—or rather, being dragged toward him, mile by mile, voice by voice—the vessel, waking, wearing its stolen faces, opening what could only be called a mouth.

Silas stood at the foot of the ridge with rain in their glowy eyes and a half-carved femur in their pocket and every dead soul in Hollowmere listening to see what they would do next.

"Well," they said. "Let's go meet the neighbors."

They climbed.
