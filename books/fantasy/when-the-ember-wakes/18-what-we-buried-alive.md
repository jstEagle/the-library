## 18. What We Buried Alive

Odile Vane had knelt in many vaults before. She had knelt to be sworn, knelt to be punished, knelt once, memorably, to retrieve a dropped stylus while wearing full plate, which had taught her humility and bruised both knees.

She had never knelt because the floor itself demanded it.

The deep vault beneath the Ashen Order's citadel was not stone. That was the first thing she understood, standing at its threshold with Silas and Wren behind her and the nameless ghost pressed close against Wren's shoulder like a cold draft with opinions. The walls were bone. Not carved bone, not arranged bone — *grown* bone, fused and whorled, pale as milk and veined with gold that pulsed faintly, slowly, like something breathing in its sleep.

"The founders built the citadel on top of it," Odile said. Her voice came out flat. She was proud of how flat. "Not near it. On top of it."

"They didn't build anything," Silas said quietly. Their hand rested against the wall, and Odile watched their face do the thing she had learned to dread — the listening-face, the look of someone hearing a room scream. "They grew it. Or they let it grow them. I can't tell where the bones stop and the masonry starts."

"Is that supposed to be comforting?"

"No," said Silas. "It's supposed to be true. You asked for truth. This is what it looks like. It looks like a wall made of god."

Wren made a small sound — not quite a laugh, not quite a sob, the particular noise of a scribe confronting a sentence no one should have to write down. "I transcribed prayers for eleven years," they said. "Eleven years of 'O Hidden Ones, keepers of the Quiet Mercy.' We were told the gods' names were too holy to speak." They swallowed. "We were told wrong, weren't we."

"Yes," said the ghost, through Wren's mouth, so that Wren flinched at their own voice saying it. "You were told wrong about most things. Come and see. Seeing is worse than knowing. But you've come all this way."

* * *

They descended by a stair that had not been there when Odile last inspected the vault maps — and she had inspected the vault maps obsessively for three weeks, ever since she'd burned her orders and discovered that burning orders in a fireproof archive is harder than it sounds. The stairs spiraled inward, and each landing bore an inscription, and each inscription was a lie so old it had worn smooth.

*Here the gods departed willingly,* said the third landing.

*Here the gods gave their strength to the valley,* said the seventh.

*Here we are only stewards,* said the twelfth, and beneath it, scratched small and recent, in a hand Odile recognized with a sickness that started in her stomach and worked outward: *stewardship has costs. M.*

"Marrow," she said.

"He comes down here often," the ghost said, through Wren again. "He talks to it. He thinks it listens to him. It listens the way a trap listens."

Silas paused on the fourteenth step. "It knows we're here," they said. "It's been saying my name since the second landing. Very politely. Like a host greeting guests it intends to eat."

"Charming," said Odile. "Keep walking."

"I intend to. I want it on record that I would like very much not to."

"Noted," said Wren. "For the record. Which I am apparently still keeping, out of habit, on my own skin, in charcoal." They held up their forearm, where they'd been scribbling notes all the way down: *stairs: 14 landings. lies: 14 landings. odile's jokes: improving under stress?*

"My jokes have always been excellent," Odile said, and found, absurdly, that the exchange steadied her. That was the thing about these two — about this strange little family she'd acquired like burrs on a cloak. They made the horror walkable. One step of terror, one step of charcoal-scribbled nonsense, repeat.

At the bottom, the vault opened into a chamber the size of the citadel's great hall, and in its center stood the vessel.

Odile had imagined a chest. A reliquary. Something with hinges, something that could be locked, something with a lid you could theoretically close. What stood before her instead was a mass of fused bone taller than two men, shaped like nothing and suggesting everything — here the curve of a skull, there the long sweep of a wing, everywhere the gold-veined pulse of stolen divinity, beating slow as a heart that had learned patience over three hundred years.

And it was whispering.

Not words. Not yet. A sound like wind through a field of dry grass, like ten thousand voices trying to remember how to be one voice, like —

Like prayer, Odile realized. It sounded exactly like prayer. Of course it did. It was made of prayer. Eleven generations of the valley's devotions, harvested and hoarded and fed into this thing by the Order she had given her life to, until worship itself had become a kind of fattening.

"We fed it," she said. Her flat voice cracked down the middle. "Every dawn service. Every tithe. Every name spoken into the quiet. Three hundred years of Hollowmere kneeling, and every knee bent pushed another mouthful into *that.*"

"Odile." Silas touched her arm.

"Don't." She stepped back from the touch. "Don't be gentle with me right now. I need to be furious for a moment. I've earned my fury. I'll be sad after."

"Furious is fine," Silas said. "Furious is honest. Just don't aim it at yourself. Save some for the people who deserve it."

"There are so many people who deserve it."

"Then pace yourself," said Wren, and even now, even here, there was a flicker of the runaway scribe's gallows humor, the joke that keeps a person upright when upright seems impossible. "Ration your rage. Scribes' tip. You can't burn a whole archive in one sitting; you'll singe yourself and miss half the books."

* * *

The truth did not come gently. Truths this old never do.

It came when Silas laid their palm flat against the vessel's lowest curve — against what might once have been a god's ankle, or a god's knee, or something that had never needed knees at all — and listened.

Odile watched her friend's face go through grief the way weather goes across a mountain: fast, total, changing everything it touched.

"Oh," Silas said. "Oh, you *fools.* You magnificent, cowardly, unforgivable fools."

"What?" Odile crossed the chamber in six strides. "What is it?"

"They didn't die fighting." Silas's voice shook. "There was no war. There was no invasion, no corruption, none of the founding stories — none of the ones the Order taught you, none of the ones the council teaches the children. The founders were the gods' own priests. Their closest servants. The ones the gods trusted with their names." They pulled their hand back as if the bone had burned them, though Odile saw the gold veins reach after it, hungry. "The gods came down to give the valley a blessing. A harvest blessing. And the priests killed them while they knelt."

The chamber went very quiet. Even the whispering seemed to hold its breath.

"While they *knelt,*" Odile repeated.

"Blessing posture," said the ghost softly, through Wren. "Hands open. Throat bared. Trusting. It is the easiest way to kill a god, if you are the one they trust. It is the only way." A pause. "I know. I hid. I hid inside a human bloodline and I let my siblings kneel, and I have carried that for three hundred years, and I am telling you this now because you deserve to hear it from someone who was there and did nothing."

Wren's breath caught. "Your siblings."

"You're the youngest," Silas said. It wasn't a question. "The one who survived by hiding. In my line."

"In your line," the ghost agreed. "Which makes this family reunion deeply awkward for everyone, and I apologize. Later. Grieve later. Understand now."

And then the vault showed them.

Whether the vessel did it, or the ghost, or the bones themselves finally tired of silence, Odile could never afterward say. But light rose out of the gold veins — not warm light, memory-light, the color of things remembered rather than seen — and the chamber filled with the past the way a cup fills with water, all at once and overflowing.

She saw the harvest festival, three hundred years gone. She saw the gods descend — seven of them, vast and gentle, wearing shapes like weather and seasons, kneeling among the wheat in their blessing-posture while the whole valley sang. She saw the founders rise behind them with tools that had been blessed that very morning for the harvest. She saw the first blow fall, and the second, and she saw the gods *not fight back*, because they could not believe it, because trust dies slower than flesh.

She saw the wards raised — not to keep magic out of the valley, as every child was taught, but to keep the screaming in. To seal the murder scene. To muffle seven dying voices so the theft could proceed undisturbed.

She saw the first High Custodian kneel beside the heap of god-bones and begin, carefully, lovingly, to feed it.

The vision ended. The light sank back into the veins. Odile became aware that she was on her knees — actually on her knees, in the dust, in the house of her order, and that she had been crying without noticing, and that her hands were fists so tight her nails had drawn blood from her palms.

Three hundred years, she thought. Three hundred years of Ashen Knights sworn to guard the valley's peace. And the peace was a lock on a larder. And the guarding was feeding. And every knight who had ever died honorably in the Order's grey cloak had died protecting a *meal.*

"The council knew," she said aloud. It was important, suddenly, to say it aloud, to make it real air rather than real poison. "Some of them. The senior seats. They had to have known."

"Some," the ghost confirmed. "Not all. The truth was passed seat to seat like a wound. Some elders chose ignorance. Ignorance is comfortable and requires no kneeling."

"Kneeling," Odile said, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, nothing like a laugh should be. "Yes. Let's talk about kneeling."

* * *

The council arrived an hour later. Of course they did. Marrow had known they were coming — the vault had probably sung his name the moment they crossed the threshold — and he had sent word up, and the twelve seats of Hollowmere's council came down the fourteen lying landings in their good robes with their good faces, led by Elder Cassia, who had once given Odile a honey-cake at her swearing ceremony and told her she had her mother's shoulders.

Elder Cassia stopped at the sight of them. At the sight of the vessel. At the sight of Odile Vane, disgraced knight, kneeling in the dust with blood on her palms and tears drying salt-stiff on her cheeks.

"Ser Vane," Cassia said carefully. "You should not be here."

"No," Odile agreed. She rose. Her knees ached. Good. Let them ache. "None of us should be here. That's rather the entire point." She turned to face the twelve seats, and she found that her fury had settled at last into something colder and more durable, the way forge-fire settles into steel. "Would you like to know what you've been governing? Would you like to know what the tithes feed? Or shall I let the bones tell you? They tell it better. They've had three hundred years to rehearse."

"Ser Vane—" began Elder Pram, the treasurer, a man whose jowls Odile had always privately considered sculptural.

"High Custodian Marrow has known for forty years," she said. "His predecessor knew for sixty. The founding twelve knew from the first day, because they were the knives." She gestured at the vessel, and twelve heads turned, reluctantly, the way heads turn toward a thing they cannot bear to see and cannot bear not to. "That is what remains of the seven gods of this valley. Your gods. My gods. Murdered by their own priests during a blessing, and fed ever since on every prayer this valley has ever whispered. The wards don't protect us. The wards are a *lid.*"

Silence. The bad kind. The kind with calculations happening behind it.

Elder Cassia's face had gone the color of the bone walls. "That's heresy," she said, but her voice had no floor under it.

"It's *archaeology,*" snapped Wren, stepping forward, charcoal-stained and trembling and absolutely fearless in the way of people who have already lost everything they were afraid of losing. "I was the Order's scribe. I kept the prayer-ledgers. I can show you the intake records — yes, *intake,* that's what they're called, the prayers are logged like grain — going back two centuries. Ask me anything. I dare you. I *beg* you. Give me one question I can't answer with a ledger citation."

Elder Pram looked at Elder Cassia. Elder Cassia looked at the vessel. And Odile watched — she would remember watching it for the rest of her life — the exact moment when twelve men and women who had spent decades believing themselves stewards understood, all at once, that they were jailers. Or cooks.

Two things happened next, and both of them were honest, which was more than the vault had seen in three hundred years.

Elder Hask, the oldest seat, the one who had held his position through four High Custodians, looked at the vessel for a long moment and then walked to the far wall and vomited, quietly and with dignity, and then sat down on the floor and did not get up.

And Elder Cassia — sweet-honey-cake Cassia, shoulders-like-your-mother Cassia — went down onto her knees. Not collapsing. Kneeling. Deliberately. Facing the vessel, facing the murdered gods, in the old blessing-posture, hands open, throat bared.

"I didn't know," she said, to the bones, to the dead, to whatever was left to hear. "But I knelt every morning of my life, and my kneeling fed it, and so my not-knowing doesn't matter, does it. Does it?"

The vessel's whisper changed. Just slightly. Just enough. The grass-wind sound bent toward her, and Odile felt Silas stiffen beside her, heard them breathe: "*Don't answer her. Whatever it offers — it's not forgiveness. It doesn't know how. It only knows hunger.*"

But three of the others — Pram and two more — were already backing toward the stairs, faces grey, hands shaking, and Odile let them go. Let them run. Cowardice, honestly performed, was at least truthful. They would spread the story whether they wanted to or not. Terror was a better herald than any crier.

"Where is Marrow?" she asked the remaining councilors.

Cassia looked up at her, kneeling, ruined, honest. "Gone," she whispered. "He took the pilgrims. All of them. Thousands. He's marching them to the wardline. He says—" her voice broke, reformed, broke again. "He says it's time for the god to wake up. He says the valley's faith has ripened. He's going to break the wards himself, Ser Vane. From outside. With the whole faithful world singing behind him."

The vessel's whisper swelled, just barely, just perceptibly — and Odile would have sworn, then and forever after, that it sounded pleased.

Silas closed their eyes. When they opened them, they were already somewhere else, somewhere Odile couldn't follow, listening to something at the edge of the valley, and what they said was:

"Then we run. Now. Tonight. Because when those wards come down, every dead voice in Hollowmere speaks at once — and I'm the only one alive who can hear them all."
