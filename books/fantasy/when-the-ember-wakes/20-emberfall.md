## 20. Emberfall

### Odile

The door to the bone-vault was older than the Order, older than the valley's peace, and it had one hinge that sang when it moved—a thin, mournful note like a wet finger on glass. Odile Vane had knelt before that door for twenty years without ever hearing it sing. Now she stood in front of it with her back to the dark and her sword drawn, and she thought: *I will make this hinge the last thing Marrow hears.*

Behind her, Silas was singing.

It did not sound like singing, at first. It sounded like a man clearing his throat in an empty chapel, uncertain whether anyone was listening. But the vault answered. The walls of fused god-bone—white and yellow and the deep bruised purple of old ivory—began to hum along, and the vessel at the center of the chamber, that mountain of stolen holiness, shuddered like a sleeper trying to wake from a nightmare.

"Odile." Wren's voice, tight, from somewhere behind her left shoulder. "How long can you hold?"

"As long as it takes," Odile said. "Ask me again when I'm dead."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke. It was a schedule."

Boots in the corridor now. Many boots. The Ashen Order had come down the mountain behind their High Custodian like ash sliding off a burned log, and they had come singing too—their hymns, the ones Odile had memorized as a girl, the ones she now knew were lullabies for a thing that ate worship and called it devotion. The hymns echoed up the stairwell ahead of them, and every note made the vessel behind her swell a little fatter, a little brighter, its thousand stolen faces pressing against the inside of itself like moths against a lantern's glass.

Odile set her feet. She rolled her shoulders once, the way her first swordmaster had taught her, back when the Order still meant something clean. *A knight holds the line,* he'd said. *That is the whole of it. Everything else is commentary.*

She had spent two decades believing the commentary mattered more.

The first of Marrow's knights came around the stairwell's turn, torchlight climbing his armor, and Odile recognized him—Brother Cassian, who had once shared his rations with her on a winter patrol, who had laughed at her terrible jokes about the cold. He saw her standing there and stopped, and for one heartbeat neither of them moved.

"Ser Vane," he said. "Step aside."

"I'm afraid I've been stripped of the 'ser,'" Odile said. "You'll have to settle for Vane."

"You know what's down there. You've seen it. Marrow says it must be woken so it can be *guided*—"

"Cassian." She kept her voice gentle, because underneath the fear and the fury she was, absurdly, fond of him. "Marrow says a great many things. Have you ever once heard the thing answer him? Truly answer? Or does it only ever say what he needs it to say?"

Cassian's jaw worked. Behind him, more knights pressed up the stairs, and behind *them*, faint but unmistakable, came the voice of High Custodian Marrow himself, smooth as oil poured over water: *"Forward, children. The door is guarded by one traitor and two heretics. What could they possibly—"*

Odile hit Cassian with the flat of her blade before he finished deciding, because kindness only goes so far, and then the stairwell was full of steel and shouting and the terrible singing hinge, and Odile Vane held the line.

She held it longer than any commentary would have believed.

### Silas

Silas Galloway had never sung in front of anyone. Not properly. In Hollowmere you learned early that your voice was a private room—you kept the door shut, you kept the windows curtained, and if you absolutely had to make noise, you made it small. A lifetime of careful quiet sat in Silas's throat like a stone.

But the stone had cracked, back in chapter after chapter of this long awful year, and now Silas stood in the heart of the murdered gods' tomb and opened the door all the way.

The names came up out of the bones.

Not the names the founders had given them—not Custodian titles, not the tidy liturgical labels in the Order's ledgers. The *true* names, the ones the bones remembered the way a riverbed remembers water. Silas heard them through the soles of their feet, through their palms where they pressed against the vessel's surface, through the hollow places in their own chest that had been listening since childhood. And Silas sang them back.

The first name sounded like frost forming on a window. When Silas sang it, the vessel flinched.

The second name sounded like a mother calling children home at dusk. When Silas sang it, something deep inside the mass of bone began to weep—a sound like rain finding its way into a cellar.

"It's working," Wren gasped. They were on their knees amid the scattered prayer-scrolls they'd carried down from the Order's archive, thousands of them, every petition the faithful had ever fed into the dark. Their hands were bleeding; unbinding stolen prayers turned out to be slow, stubborn work, each scroll needing its seal broken and its words spoken backward, gently, the way you'd talk someone down off a ledge. "Silas—it's *listening*—"

"Good," Silas said, and sang the third name, which tasted of woodsmoke and forgiveness.

The vessel screamed.

Every face it wore screamed at once—the drowned child from Silas's very first memorial femur, the pilgrims, the knights, the founders, the gods themselves—and the sound knocked Wren flat and made the vault's ancient bones ring like a struck bell. Silas staggered but did not stop. That was the whole trick of it, they realized. That was the whole gift. Anyone could sing *at* a god. What Silas could do—what only a bone-carver who had spent a life listening could do—was sing *with* one.

So Silas put their hands flat against the vessel's weeping surface and sang the fourth name, and the fifth, and did not stop even when the vessel's grief came pouring up through their arms like winter water, even when it showed them everything it had eaten, even when it whispered in a hundred borrowed voices:

*We didn't want to be this. We didn't want to be this. Who let us become this?*

"You did," Silas sang, gentle as a lullaby, terrible as a verdict. "And so did we. Remember. Remember all of it. Remember, and grieve, and let go."

### Wren

Wren had transcribed eleven thousand four hundred and six prayers for the Order. They knew this because they had counted, in the worst months, the way other people counted sheep—except the sheep were petitions from desperate people and the counting was a way of not thinking about where the prayers went.

Now they knelt in the wreckage of their own careful handwriting, breaking seals and speaking words backward, and discovered that guilt, it turns out, makes excellent fuel.

"This one's from a farmer," they muttered, tearing a scroll free of the vessel's gravity. "'Please let my wife's fever break.' Unbound. Go be unanswered somewhere honest, friend." Another. "This one's from a child asking for a dog. A *dog*. They fed a child's dog-prayer into—that. Into *that*." Their voice cracked, and the nameless ghost, hovering at their shoulder in its borrowed half-shape, made a sound like wind through a keyhole.

*They are not angry with you,* the ghost said, using the mouth of no one, the way it always did. *The prayers. They wanted to be heard. You were the only one who read them.*

"That's worse," Wren said. "Don't you get it? I was the only one who *read* them. Every hope that got eaten, I held it first. I proofread grief for a monster."

*And now you are un-reading it,* said the ghost. *One page at a time. In stories, that is called the same thing as redemption. I have been dead a long time. I have read a great deal.*

Despite everything—despite the screaming vessel, despite Odile's sword ringing somewhere behind them, despite the dust and the blood and the absolute certainty that they might die in a hole under a mountain—Wren laughed. Just a little. Just enough.

Then the vessel turned its attention on them.

It happened between one scroll and the next: the pressure of it, the vast drowsy hunger, swinging toward Wren like a lighthouse beam. And out of its thousand mouths came Wren's mother's voice, warm and tired and exactly right, saying exactly the thing Wren had never forgiven themselves for needing to hear:

*"Come here, little scribe. You did your best. Come rest."*

The scrolls fell from Wren's hands. Their feet, without permission, took a step.

*No,* said the ghost sharply—but it was Silas's song that caught them, Silas's voice rising over the false one, real and rough and alive and slightly off-key in the way only living voices are, singing the true name of the youngest god, the hidden one, the ghost's own lost name. And the ghost blazed. For one instant it was not a shape of borrowed air but something enormous and bright and *theirs*, and it wrapped around Wren like a coat against cold, and the false voice shattered.

Wren stood shaking in the circle of it, tears freezing on their cheeks.

"I'm here," they said—to Silas, to the ghost, to nobody, to everybody. "I'm here. I'm staying."

And then they picked up the next scroll, and broke its seal, and went on.

### Odile

By the time Marrow himself reached the landing, Odile had broken three swords and both bones of her left forearm and was fighting with a shard of the third blade held in her right hand, and the stairwell behind her was carpeted with knights who had chosen, most of them, merely to sleep.

She had been precise about that. Flat of the blade, pommel, boot, elbow. These were not enemies. These were children raised on lies, and she would not add their deaths to the mountain of things this valley had to answer for.

Marrow surveyed the carnage with the mild disappointment of a man inspecting a poorly kept garden.

"Odile." He said her name the way he'd said it in lessons, decades ago, when she was young enough to think his approval was a form of sunlight. "You were my finest student."

"No," Odile said, breathing hard. "I was your finest *tool*. There's a difference, and I've finally learned it, and it cost me—" she gestured at her arm, at the sleeping knights, at the whole ruined architecture of her life, "—considerably."

"The vessel will wake regardless. You cannot carve a god apart with songs."

"Watch us."

Something moved behind Marrow's eyes then—not doubt, nothing so useful, but calculation. He drew his own blade, and it was beautiful, relic-forged, and he came at her the way he had taught her to come at everyone: without hatred, without mercy, with perfect economy.

They fought. It lasted less than a minute and it contained Odile's entire life. He was better than her—he had always been better than her—and she knew it, and so she did the one thing he had never taught her, because it was the one thing the Order forbade:

She cheated.

She let his blade take her shoulder—she felt it bite, white and enormous—and used the grip it gave her to pull him close, head-height, and break his nose with her forehead, and while he reeled she swept his legs and put him down on the stone with her knee on his chest and the sword-shard at his throat.

"Yield," she said.

"You're bleeding out, Odile."

"Probably. Yield anyway."

Above them, from the heart of the vault, Silas's song reached a name that sounded like dawn, and the whole mountain shivered. Marrow looked past her, toward the light beginning to pour out of the doorway—warm gold, ember-colored, the light of something grieving itself free—and for the first time in all his long custodianship, High Custodian Marrow looked *afraid*.

"What is it doing?" he whispered.

Odile followed his gaze. The vessel was coming apart—not violently, not like destruction, but like ice going out of a lake in spring, like a knot understanding itself untied. Faces rose off its surface and dissolved into the gold light, and each one, as it went, seemed to sigh.

"It's remembering," Odile said. "It's remembering what it ate. And it's sorry."

She pressed the shard a little firmer. "Yield, old man. The gods are waking up, and they're going to want a full accounting, and I promise you—" she smiled, and it hurt, everything hurt, and it was the best smile of her life, "—you would rather face me."

### Silas

The last name was the hardest.

It was the name of the god whose death had started everything—the eldest, the one who had stood in this chamber when the founders came with their knives and their ledgers, the one who had refused to fight back because fighting back would have burned the valley and everyone in it. Silas heard that name in the deepest layer of the bones, worn nearly smooth, and understood suddenly why the wards had been built. Not to keep magic out. To keep the *sound* in. To muffle, forever, the moment the world's oldest kindness was murdered by people who wrote very neat reports about it afterward.

Silas sang it anyway.

The vessel came apart like a held breath released. Three centuries of stolen prayer rose off it in a single golden exhalation—every fever-broken wife, every wished-for dog, every widow's whisper and soldier's bargain—and the light did not judge them, did not spend them, simply *carried* them, upward, outward, home. The dead gods' faces went last, each one pausing—as if in a doorway—to look at Silas. Not with gratitude. With something better: recognition. One listener to another.

And then the vault was quiet, and empty, and ordinary, and Silas Galloway stood alone in the gold-dust with their hands still raised, feeling smaller and lighter than they had ever felt in their life.

Footsteps. Odile, gray-faced, arm cradled, leaning on Wren, who was filthy and tear-streaked and grinning like a fool. Behind them, the ghost drifted, and for the first time its outline looked less like absence and more like a person almost remembered.

"Well," Wren said. "Did we win?"

Silas looked down at their hands. Bone-carver's hands, scarred and steady. In the center of one palm, small as a spark, a fleck of the golden light had settled and stayed—an ember, refusing to go out, warm as a hearthstone.

"I don't know," Silas said honestly. "But listen."

They listened. Beneath the mountain, beneath the silence, faint and far away and unmistakably *real*: the dead of Hollowmere, all of them, waiting patiently to be heard.

And knocking, from far above, came another sound—the council's wardens, sealing the vault door, deciding their fate without them.

"Someone," Silas said, "is going to have a lot of explaining to do."
