## 21. The Honest God

The god-fragments came to Silas at dawn, the way honest things usually do.

They gathered in the workshop like shy relatives at a funeral—drifting in through gaps in the shutter boards as motes of ember-light, settling on the workbench, the rafters, the rim of Wren's abandoned teacup. There were nine of them. Silas had counted them every morning for a week now, the way you count spoons when you suspect someone's been pilfering.

"We have decided," said the largest one, which had taken up residence inside the knucklebone of the old drowned child's memorial femur, and spoke with a voice like wind through winter wheat, "that you will carry us."

Silas didn't look up from the carving knife. "Have you, now."

"You refused the vessel," the fragment continued, with the patience of something that had been dead for three centuries and had learned not to rush. "We understand why. Marrow's thing was an abomination—a stomach wearing a crown. But you need not be a vessel the way it was a vessel. You could be a hearth. A small fire, tended. We would live in you, and you would speak for us, and the valley would finally have gods who tell the truth."

"And what would I be?" Silas asked the knife, to the shavings, to the light coming gold and gray through the shutters.

"Ours."

"There it is." Silas set down the knife and finally looked up. The fragments pulsed softly in the dimness, nine little suns the size of coins, and Silas felt the old familiar pull—the same pull that had whispered through the vault, through the waking, through the terrible beautiful moment when the vessel had offered everything. *Stand aside,* it had said. *Be adored.* It had been lying, mostly. These fragments weren't lying. That was somehow worse.

"Let me ask you something," Silas said. "When you were alive—all nine of you, whole, with your rivers and your harvests and your little temples—did people talk to you? Or did they pray?"

The fragments flickered. It was the closest thing to flinching Silas had ever seen divinity manage.

"They prayed," said the smallest one, from the teacup, in a voice like a child's.

"They prayed," Silas agreed. "On their knees. In formal language they'd been taught by people paid to teach it. And did you ever once get to just—chat? Did anyone sit down with a cup of tea and say, *look, the well's gone brackish, can you do something about it, and also my mother's driving me mad?*"

"No," said the child-voice, very quietly.

"That's how you died." Silas stood, joints cracking, forty-one years old and feeling every one of them. "Not because Marrow's ancestors were strong. Because nobody talked to you anymore. They performed at you. And performance is a kind of hunger, and hungry things get eaten." Silas picked up a bone shaving and turned it over in calloused fingers. "If I carry you, in ten years I'll be a temple. In fifty, an institution. In a hundred, someone like Marrow will be interpreting my silence for profit, and you'll be dead again, and I'll be the reason. No."

"We would be gentle—"

"I know you'd be gentle. That's not the problem." Silas smiled, tired and crooked. "The problem is that Hollowmere has spent three hundred years being quiet. Quiet prayers, quiet griefs, quiet graves. You want to give them a god to listen *to*. What they need is someone to teach them to listen *with*."

The fragments were silent for a long moment. Ember-light moved across the ceiling like water.

"What," said the wheat-voice carefully, "are you proposing?"

Silas picked up the memorial femur. The drowned child's last words still lived in it—*it's warmer than I thought*—and Silas held it the way you hold anything precious: loosely, and with both hands.

"I'm proposing homework."

* * *

It took four months.

Four months of Silas Galloway standing in the market square—which was no longer burned, though the new timber was paler than the old, like a scar going white—with a table, a chair, a sign Wren had painted in enormous cheerful letters reading **BONES CARVED, DEAD HEARD, NO APPOINTMENT NECESSARY**, and a queue that started out as three curious children and ended up wrapping past the baker's and down toward the well.

The principle was simple, which was fortunate, because Silas had to explain it roughly six thousand times.

Every bone carried an echo. Not a soul—that was priest-talk, and there were no priests left worth the title—but an echo, the way a struck bell remembers ringing. Most bones forgot. But bone carved by a hearing-hand, cut along the grain of its own remembering, became a listening-bone: hold it, speak near it, and the dead it belonged to could answer. Not with thunder. Not with prophecy. With the ordinary, maddening, precious voices of ordinary dead people.

Old Marta Corvane held her husband's listening-bone—a shin, he'd always been a long-shanked man—and heard him complain about her stew for the first time in eleven years, and wept, and laughed, and then went home and made the stew better, which was more than any god had ever gotten out of her grief.

The Tulliver family learned that their grandfather hadn't died angry at their father after all; he'd died angry about a horse, which was somehow worse and much funnier, and the family argument that had split them for two decades collapsed under the sheer stupidity of its foundation, and there was a wedding that spring.

And Tam—the boy who'd pulled children out of the market fire with Silas, the one with the burn-scarred hands—came every single week with his grandmother's rib, which she'd willed to him specifically, and sat on the workshop step asking it questions about bread.

"She says the trick is patience," Tam reported one afternoon, swinging his feet.

"The dead are full of wisdom," Silas said, carving.

"She says the *other* trick is not opening the oven door every five minutes because you're nervous. She says you're nervous too. About something. She says you smell nervous."

"Your grandmother is a menace."

"She knows." Tam grinned, gap-toothed. "She says that's why she likes you."

Odile Vane came by less often but stayed longer. She'd rebuilt the Ashen Order from the ground up—or rather, torn the old one down to its foundations and built something smaller and sturdier in the ruins. Twelve knights now instead of three hundred. No vault. No ledger. Their new oath, drafted by committee between Odile, Wren, and a frankly tyrannical amount of input from the nameless ghost, was four words long: *Remember. Record. Refuse lies.*

She sat on the workshop step in her plain gray half-armor, sharpening a sword she never seemed to need anymore, and watched the queue with an expression Silas had slowly learned to read as contentment wearing a guard dog's costume.

"You realize," she said one evening, "that you've done exactly what the Order spent centuries preventing. Every household in this valley is about to be in direct contact with the dead. Unmediated. Ungoverned."

"Mm."

"It's chaos. People are renegotiating inheritances based on ghostly testimony. Two families nearly came to blows over whether Great-Aunt Bess actually promised the loom to the Fenwicks. The council has petitioned me twice to 'regulate' the listening-bones." She tested the blade's edge with her thumb. "I told them regulation of the dead was how we got High Custodian Marrow, and if they wanted a second one they should try harder, because the first one's ash."

"What did the council say?"

"They said I was insubordinate. Then they said thank you. Councils are like that." Odile slid the sword home. "You've noticed, haven't you. What's happening to you."

Silas kept carving. "Say it plainly. You're better at that than I am."

"The ember. The one you kept. It's spreading—not in you. Through the valley." She nodded toward the window, where evening was coming down blue and soft over the rooftops. "Tam hears things without a bone sometimes. Old Marta dreamed true twice last month. Wren swears the well water tastes like memory. You didn't hoard it and you didn't spend it. You gave it away one household at a time, and now it's—" she gestured vaguely, "—everywhere. Like yeast."

"Aye." Silas blew a curl of shaving off the femur in hand. "Turns out divinity's less like a treasure and more like sourdough starter. Everyone gets some, everyone tends theirs, nobody owns the loaf."

"That's a horrible metaphor."

"I'm a bone-carver, not a poet."

"You're a bit of both," Odile said, and got up to check on her knights, and Silas let the compliment land where it fell and pretended it hadn't.

* * *

Wren found Silas at midnight on the equinox, sitting on the hill above the wardline with nine embers arranged in a circle on a flat stone, talking to them like a mad person, which Silas had decided was fine, since the alternative was pretending not to be one.

"They're asking again," Wren said, dropping down cross-legged beside Silas. The ghost drifted up behind them, faintly luminous, wearing the shape it had settled into these days—a young man with river-gray eyes and a smile that arrived late but sincerely. He'd gotten his name back in the vault, in the grieving; he was **Cael**, youngest of the murdered nine, and he wore the name now the way you wear a coat that almost fits, warming up by degrees.

"They're always asking," Silas said.

"You always say no."

"I'm consistent. It's my best quality."

Wren hugged their knees. They were nineteen and looked older lately, in the good way—the way of someone whose spine had straightened. "Can I tell you something I've never told anyone? Including Cael?"

"He's right there."

"He knows anyway. He's polite about it." Wren took a breath. "When I was the Order's scribe—I transcribed prayers. Thousands of them. And I used to think, *these are so sad, these are so lonely, all these people shouting into the dark and nothing answers.* And part of me—the ugly part—liked being the only one who knew where the prayers went. Being close to the secret. When the vessel answered me that first time, do you know what I felt?" Their voice cracked. "*Chosen.* And I'd feed it anything to keep feeling chosen. So when I watch those fragments ask you to carry them, and you say no, and you mean it—" They turned, eyes bright. "How do you do that? How do you not want it?"

Silas considered the nine small lights. They pulsed patiently. Dead things, Silas had learned, were excellent at waiting.

"Honestly? Practice," Silas said. "I spent thirty years wanting to be invisible, and then six months learning that being seen doesn't kill you, and somewhere in between I figured out the trick of it." Silas reached out and tapped the stone between the embers. "Wanting to matter isn't the trap. Wanting to be *needed*—needing to be needed—that's the trap. The moment they can't live without you, you'll do anything to keep them needing. That's how Marrow started, I'd bet my best chisel. Some apprentice who heard one voice and couldn't bear to lose the hearing." Silas shrugged. "So I keep the ember lit and I keep my hands open. If it stays, lovely. If it goes—" Silas opened the fingers. "Lovely too. That's the whole religion, Wren. That's it. That's the sermon."

Wren laughed wetly. "That's a terrible sermon."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"It needs a better metaphor."

"Sourdough starter is a *fine* metaphor."

"It really isn't," said Cael, from behind them, in the tone of a god discovering humor for the second time in three hundred years and finding it improved with age.

* * *

In spring, Silas carved the nine-hundredth listening-bone, and the council came to the workshop—not to petition, not to threaten, but to kneel, which was far more alarming.

There were seven of them, gray-robed, ancient, led by Councilor Hesh, who had voted for exile and witch-branding and had spent the intervening year being publicly, unforgivably wrong. She knelt on Silas's floorboards among the shavings with the stiff grace of a woman whose knees had opinions, and she held out her hands, palms up.

"Galloway. We come to ask—" she swallowed pride like medicine, "—how we may serve."

Silas put down the knife. Looked at seven of the most powerful people in the valley kneeling in bone dust. Felt the ember in the chest do its slow warm pulse, the one that meant *careful now, this is the moment, this is exactly the moment Marrow would have waited for.*

Then Silas walked over, took Councilor Hesh by the forearms, and hauled her upright with a grunt.

"First," Silas said, "you can stand up. Nobody kneels in this shop. Second—you can fund the lumber for the listening-house. Big room, market side, benches, a stove. Third—you can retire the law that forbids naming the dead at table. Fourth—" Silas counted on fingers, enjoying the way Hesh's expression moved from supplication to bewilderment to the dawning horror of a woman realizing she'd traded worship for *administrative tasks*, "—you can make sure the Corvanes and the Fenwicks stop suing each other over that loom. Honestly, the dead sorted that out in March."

"...Is that all?" Hesh asked faintly.

"For now. There'll be more. Governance of the living is endless; I don't envy you." Silas turned back to the workbench. "Oh—one more thing. When you go home tonight, hold whatever bone of your dead you've kept, and listen. Really listen. That's the service. Everything else is carpentry."

They filed out looking shell-shocked, and Odile, who had watched the entire audience from the corner with her arms crossed, waited until the door closed before she said:

"You just declined godhood and accepted a municipal works budget in the same breath."

"Somebody's got to fix the market roof. Gods never think about the roof."

"No," Odile agreed solemnly. "They never do."

* * *

Summer came, and with it the anniversary, and Hollowmere held its first honest funeral in three hundred years.

But that is another day's telling, and Silas was not thinking of it yet—Silas was thinking of it later, that evening, alone at the bench while the valley's windows went gold one by one, when the nine embers rose from their various lodgings around the workshop and arranged themselves in the air before Silas in a slow, deliberate ring.

The wheat-voice spoke first, and it sounded different. Lighter. Like something setting down a load it had carried since before the valley had a name.

"We have finished deciding," it said.

"Have you."

"We watched you all winter. We watched you give us away a household at a time. We watched you refuse us in front of a kneeling council, and carve a child's bone the next morning, and eat soup with a knight, and argue about metaphors." A pause, in which nine small lights shimmered like laughter remembering itself. "You are the worst vessel ever proposed. You are stubborn, irreverent, mortal to the marrow, and you will absolutely die someday, probably of soup-related causes."

"That's slander. It'll be tea."

"And therefore," the wheat-voice went on, gathering itself, "we have chosen you after all—not as our vessel, Silas Galloway, but as our keeper. Not the fire. The *hearth-tender*. Keep the ember lit. Keep the doors open. Teach them until they no longer need teaching, and then keep the shop anyway, because somebody has to mind the roof." The nine embers drifted closer, warm against Silas's weathered face, and each one pressed forward briefly—like a kiss, like a blessing, like a handshake—and sank into the old memorial femur lying finished on the bench, all nine together, glowing there like coals in a grate.

"One ember," said the child-voice sleepily, from within the bone, "shared rather than held. As you said. We listened."

"You lot," Silas said, throat tight, "are going to be *terrible* houseguests."

Outside, the first star came up over the wardline—no longer a wall, just a line of old stones now, moss-grown, harmless. Down in the valley, someone lit the listening-house stove, and Tam's laugh carried up the hill, and Wren was writing something by lamplight that expected no answer, and Cael stood in the doorway watching it all with river-gray eyes, wearing his name like a coat that finally fit.

Silas picked up the femur, warm in both hands, and set it on its shelf of honor beside the window, where the morning sun would find it.

And in the dark of the workshop, soft as banked coals, the ember woke—and began, very gently, to hum.
