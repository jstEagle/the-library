## 22. When the Ember Wakes

The morning of the funeral, Silas Galloway woke before dawn out of habit rather than necessity, lay in bed listening to the house settle, and realized—for perhaps the thousandth time since the vault—that the silence was no longer empty.

That was the thing about Hollowmere now. Before, the valley's quiet had been a held breath, three hundred years of it, the kind of silence that made your ears ring if you listened too hard. Now the quiet was full. The dead were still here, all of them, generations of farmers and midwives and children who had died believing their prayers went nowhere. But they weren't shouting anymore. They had learned, in the seasons since Silas carved the first listening-bones and taught the households how to hold them, that they didn't have to shout. Someone was listening now.

Silas got up, put the kettle on, and stood at the window watching frost make lace of the herb garden Wren insisted on planting every year and forgetting to water every summer.

"You're making the face," Wren said from the doorway, hair a catastrophe, clutching a mug like a lifeline. "The funeral face."

"I don't have a funeral face."

"You absolutely do. It's the one where you look like you're about to apologize to someone." They squinted. "Who is it today? Please tell me it isn't old Marta. If I have to hear about her prize turnips one more time—"

"It's Tam Broadfield," Silas said. "He was eighty-seven. He asked for me specifically. Said—" and here Silas smiled despite themselves— "said he wanted his bones done proper, not 'that whispery nonsense the council tried last spring.'"

Wren snorted into their tea. "The council's *communal remembrance circle*. Odile called it 'a séance with better chairs.'"

"She did. Loudly. In session."

"Which is why half the Order adores her and the other half is terrified of her." Wren leaned against the doorframe, and for a moment their expression went soft and strange—the way it always did when they talked about funerals, which was often, which was its own kind of healing nobody had planned. "You know what I keep thinking? Three hundred years. Three hundred years of people dying in this valley and being *processed*. Filed. Turned into ledger entries." They turned the mug in their hands. "And today somebody gets to be mourned. Out loud. On purpose."

"That's the whole job," Silas said. "Somebody gets to be mourned."

* * *

The funeral was held in the market square, because Tam had been a fixture there for sixty years—selling apples, cheating tourists gently at dice, teaching every child in Hollowmere how to whistle through a blade of grass—and his daughter said he'd have wanted to see everyone one last time, even if everyone had to stand in the cold.

They came anyway. Half the valley came. Silas stood at the bier with the finished work in their hands: Tam's femur, carved over eleven days with everything Silas had heard him say in the last hours of his life, and everything his family had wanted him to carry. Not prayers. That was the difference, the thing that made this funeral honest where the old rites had been theft. Nobody spoke *to* Tam. They spoke *about* him. Stories, mostly. His daughter told the one about the year he'd dyed all his apples blue to spite a rival and accidentally started a fashion among children that lasted a decade. The baker told the one about the dice. A boy of six, standing on a chair so he could be seen, told the one about the grass-whistle lessons, badly, weeping, while the whole square waited without hurrying him.

And through the bone in Silas's hands, warm as a loaf fresh from the oven, Tam listened. Silas could feel him—not words exactly, not yet, but attention. Presence. The particular gravity of an old man delighted to be talked about.

When the stories were done, Silas raised the bone and spoke the words they had written themselves, the ones now carved above the door of every household in the valley:

*"Tam Broadfield. You are heard. You are remembered. Go home now, or stay and visit—it's your choice, and it always should have been."*

The bone sang. Not loudly—a low, sweet tone like a wet finger on a glass rim—but everyone heard it, because everyone in the square was holding their own listening-bone, small ones, keepsake-sized, and the note passed from hand to hand like warmth passing down a row of shoulders. Somewhere near the back, an old woman laughed out loud, then covered her mouth, embarrassed, and Silas knew exactly why: Tam had just made a joke, and she'd caught it, and it was a good one, and it was nobody's business but hers.

That was the other difference. The dead could talk to whoever they liked now. They didn't need Silas as a middleman. Silas was just the carver—the person who made the doors. The walking through them belonged to everybody.

Afterward, as the crowd dispersed toward Tam's daughter's house for the eating-and-drinking portion of grief, Ser Odile Vane appeared at Silas's elbow in her rebuilt grays—no more ash, the Order had voted, ash was for hiding things; the new livery was plain wool the color of river stone—and looked out over the thinning crowd with her arms crossed.

"Well?" she said. "Verdict?"

"He'd have hated the chairs comment."

"He'd have loved it. He'd have repeated it for a month." Odile's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling when she was trying not to smile. "Three hundred years," she said again, quieter. "My Order spent three hundred years guarding a murder scene and calling it holiness. And the fix took you"—she gestured at the square, at the bones in a hundred pockets and a hundred windowsills—"eleven days per person and a lot of arguing."

"Somebody had to do the arguing."

"I'm aware. I lost most of it." She said it without rancor. Losing arguments to Silas had become, over the seasons, something close to a hobby for her. "Council meeting Thursday. They want to talk about the shrine-road."

Silas went very still. "The roadside shrines?"

"The fragments. What's left of them." Odile watched Silas's face carefully. "They're asking whether the fragments should be gathered somewhere central. A temple, maybe. An official place." She paused. "I told them I'd ask the carver."

"Tell them no."

"They haven't actually asked yet—"

"*Tell them no,*" Silas said, and heard the edge in their own voice, and breathed, and let it go. "Sorry. No. Tell them—the fragments spent three hundred years scattered along the road because scattered was how they survived. Gathered in one building, they'd be a relic. Relics get guarded. Guarded things get owned. Owned things get—" *fed*, Silas almost said, and didn't. "Let them stay by the road. Let travelers stop or not stop. That was always their way."

Odile nodded slowly, writing it in the air the way she wrote everything, filing it in that iron archive behind her eyes. "Scattered but connected," she said. "Like the bones."

"Like the bones."

She left for the wake, and Silas stayed a moment in the emptying square, in the cold, listening to the faint chorus of the valley's dead going about their afterlives—visiting kitchens, haunting turnip patches, following grandchildren around complaining affectionately about their posture. Tam's voice was already fading toward wherever old dead men go when there's apple cake waiting somewhere. Before it faded entirely, it said, clear as a bell through the bone still warm in Silas's pocket:

*Told you you'd figure out the whistling eventually.*

Silas laughed alone in the square, and the sound rang off the mountains, and nothing in the dark answered it except, distantly, a blade of grass on a child's lips three streets away, playing a tune that was almost a name.

* * *

Winter came early that year, the way it liked to, and found Wren at the long table in Silas's workshop doing what Wren did now: writing.

Not transcribing. Never transcribing again—they'd burned their Order scribe's kit in the first spring and kept only the pen, refilled with ordinary ink, for ordinary words. What Wren wrote were prayers the way poems are prayers: addressed to nobody, expecting nothing, meant to be read aloud by firelight or copied onto scraps and tucked into listening-bones so the dead could enjoy them. *Prayers that expect no answer*, Odile had once called them, and Wren had thrown a bread roll at her, and the name had stuck.

"This one's for the harvest festival," Wren said, reading aloud, ink to the second knuckle. "*To whoever's listening, or nobody: thank you for the rain, which was inconvenient and necessary, like most true things. We ate well. We argued. We buried two elders and a dog, all of whom are missed in completely different ways.*"

"The dog's going to love that," said a voice from the corner.

Everyone in Hollowmere had gotten used to the ghost. It had taken the valley a season; it had taken Silas and Wren and Odile rather less, since they'd known it first, back when it was a terror with no name borrowing mouths in the dark. Now it lived mostly in the workshop, drifting between the rafters like a draft that had opinions. In the months after the vessel shattered—after Silas had sung the murdered gods' true names into that fused mass of stolen bone until it remembered what it had eaten and grieved itself apart—the youngest god, the one who had survived by hiding inside a human line for three hundred years, had slowly come back into focus. Not power. It had given the last of that away, pressed it into the ember Silas carried. Just shape. Just self. Just enough to finally answer the question it had been asking since the beginning.

Its name was Ellin. It was small and quick and had been, in life—in godhood—the patron of thresholds, of doorways, of the exact moment when you step from inside to out. It suited it. It had spent three centuries as a hinge.

"Wren," Ellin said now, coalescing near the window where the light could find it, because Ellin liked being seen, having spent so long unseen, "read the part about the dog again."

"*Buried two elders and a dog, all of whom are missed in completely different ways.*"

"Perfect. The dog will be insufferable." Ellin settled on the windowsill it couldn't quite touch. "Speaking of which—I've decided."

Wren looked up. Silas, at the carving bench, kept working but slowed, the way they always did when Ellin announced decisions, because Ellin's decisions tended to rearrange rooms.

"I'm going," Ellin said. "Soon. This winter, maybe spring."

The chisel stopped.

"Not far," Ellin hurried on, hands raised in the gesture it had learned from watching living people, which was slightly wrong and very endearing. "Just—through. The ember's steady now, the valley can hear its own dead, the shrines are resting easy along the road. There's nothing holding a hinge in place except the door it's in. And this door"—it gestured around the workshop, at the shavings, at Wren, at Silas—"this door opens both ways now. It doesn't need me in it."

"You could stay," Wren said, and their voice cracked on *stay*, and they didn't pretend it hadn't. "Ghosts stay. It's allowed. We're very permissive."

"I know." Ellin looked at them with an expression three hundred years deep. "That's why I can go. Don't you see? I hid because staying alive mattered more than anything. I clung to your line, Silas, generation after generation, because I thought if I ever let go I'd stop existing." It shook its head, slow. "But existence isn't a grip. It's a gift. Somebody gave it to me—several somebodies, actually, one of whom is pretending to carve right now and failing." The chisel had indeed gone still. "I want to find out what a god becomes when nobody needs it to be anything. I think it might become weather. Or music. Or a story people tell without knowing why it comforts them. I'd like to be that."

"When?" Silas asked quietly.

"First thaw." Ellin grinned, sudden and young. "Thresholds are best crossed at transitions. Ask any god."

They worked in companionable quiet after that, Wren writing and sniffling and denying the sniffling, Ellin humming a tune no one had heard in three hundred years, Silas carving the winter's listening-bones—one for a newborn in the miller's family, one for the schoolhouse so the children could ask the founders' dead what the valley had really been built on (the answers were coming in, unflinchingly catalogued by Odile's new Order, and they were ugly and true and printed plainly in the town record now, because Hollowmere had voted, overwhelmingly, to know). The ember sat in Silas's chest where it had sat since they refused godhood and asked instead for a single coal of it, shared rather than held, enough to keep the hearing alive in the bones they made and not one spark more.

It woke sometimes. Tonight it warmed gently, like a cat settling, and Silas understood it was saying goodbye too—or hello, or whatever embers say. *When the ember wakes*, Wren had titled a poem once, about mornings generally. Silas had liked it enough to let the phrase stick.

* * *

First thaw came on a morning of running water and mud, the whole valley dripping like a thawing heart.

They walked Ellin to the wardline—the old wardline, broken now, its stones left standing as a monument with a plaque Odile had fought the council for three sessions to word correctly: *HERE THE VALLEY WAS SILENCED. HERE IT LEARNED TO LISTEN AGAIN. FORGIVE US. WE ARE TRYING.*

Beyond the stones, the world. The scarred lands, greening now in patches where the Order's memory-keepers had planted memorial groves over the old war ruins. The road where the small shrines stood, tended by travelers, never gathered, never owned. The wide uncertain future of it all.

Ellin stood on the threshold stone—which of course it did, which was the entire point of Ellin—and turned around.

"No speeches," it said, before Wren could inhale. "Wren. No speeches. You wrote me four already; I read them while you slept; they were lovely and I'm keeping them." Wren closed their mouth, wet-eyed, furious about it. "Odile. Guardian of memory. Keep remembering. It's the holiest job there is, and I would know, professionally." Odile bowed, knight-straight, and did not trust herself to speak. "Silas Galloway. Bone-carver of Hollowmere. First honest god of a valley that doesn't believe in gods." Its grin went soft. "Stay human. Stay home. Stay *you*. That's the whole blessing. That's all I've got, and it's everything."

Then it stepped backward off the threshold like a swimmer into deep water, and unfolded—not wings, nothing so useful, just light, just becoming-more-of-itself—and poured out across the land the way dawn pours, everywhere at once, unhurried, asking nothing.

The wind that moved through the wardline afterward smelled like bread baking somewhere far away.

None of them spoke for a while. Then Wren blew their nose catastrophically and said, "I'm putting it in the festival prayers," and Odile said, "It would want that," and Silas said, "It *is* that, probably, now—the wind, the bread smell, all of it"—and they walked home together down the muddy road, three ordinary mortals in a valley full of the honest dead, talking about what to plant.

* * *

That evening, Silas sat alone in the workshop, as they did most evenings now, and carved.

The bench was the same bench. The tools were the same tools, worn smooth by twenty years of the same patient work. Nothing about the room had changed except everything about the world, and Silas had decided long ago that this was the whole miracle: that the ordinary could survive the extraordinary, if you loved it enough and swept it regularly.

On the bench lay a small piece of bone—sheep, nothing special—shaped into a door. Just a little carved door, hinged with wire, sized for a dollhouse or a dream. Wren had commissioned it that afternoon with suspicious cheerfulness and refused to explain, and Silas, who knew better than to interrogate gifts, had simply carved it well.

Now, alone, Silas set the little door on the windowsill where the light could reach it, and touched it once, and whispered the only prayer they ever said anymore, the one they'd never written down, the one that wasn't really a prayer at all:

"Safe travels, Ellin. Knock anytime."

And because the valley listened now, and because some doors open both ways forever, the little door creaked—just barely, just once—on its wire hinges, though there was no wind in the workshop at all.

Silas smiled, picked up the next bone, and began to carve.

Outside, the first star came up over Hollowmere, and the dead murmured kindly in their kitchens and fields and graveyards, and somewhere on the road a traveler paused at a small roadside shrine, felt suddenly, unaccountably accompanied, and said good evening to the dark.

"Good evening," said the dark, warmly, in a voice like a threshold opening—

—and the ember in Silas Galloway's chest glowed, and glowed, and woke.
