## 2. What the Candle Saw

The candle guttered at the hour of Vespers, and Zephyrine's hand moved before their mind did.

It was a small thing—a curl of warmth drawn up from somewhere behind the sternum, pushed out along the fingers, and laid over the flame like a blanket laid over a shivering child. The wick steadied. The light swelled back to its proper height, and the shadow it threw against the scriptorium wall settled again into the shapes of shelves and saints. No one saw. No one ever saw. In nine years of copying tithes, ledgers of grain owed by villages whose names Zephyrine had never visited, no one had ever seen.

They let the breath out slowly and went back to the page.

*From the parish of Lowmere: four measures of barley, two lambs, one bolt of undyed wool.* The ink was iron-gall, brown as dried blood, and it bit into the vellum with the small vicious scratch that Zephyrine had come to think of as the true voice of Saint Verane's temple. Not the bells. Not the choir. This—the sound of debt being written down in a hand too beautiful to argue with.

Their own debts were written elsewhere, in a plainer book. Father's borrowing against the cottage. Mother's medicine, winter after winter, until the winters stopped taking her and the money kept going anyway. The temple had absorbed it all with the serene appetite of a large thing consuming a small one, and then, finding Zephyrine could form letters without blots, had offered them a way to pay it down in ink instead of coin. Nine years of ink. There remained perhaps three years more, if the Provost was feeling generous, or five if he was not, or a lifetime if he decided that a scribe who knew the tithing rolls of half the eastern parishes was worth keeping past the point of fairness.

Zephyrine had learned not to hope for generosity. They had learned something better: to be unremarkable. To be the person whose name arrived a half-second late when visitors asked who kept the archives. To sit in the refectory at the end of the bench, near the draft, where conversation naturally thinned. Usefulness without presence. A lamp that burned in a room nobody entered.

And beneath all of it—folded small, like a coal wrapped in ash—the gift.

*Gift.* Even alone, in the privacy of their own skull, Zephyrine flinched from the word. The proclamation nailed to the temple gate three days past used other words. *The crown requires that all persons possessed of unlicensed workings present themselves for registration within sixty days. Registered workings shall be bound to lawful service. Unregistered persons shall be accounted enemies of the realm.*

The wax seal on that proclamation had been the size of a plum. Zephyrine had stood in the cloister among the other scribes and read it twice, keeping their face arranged into the mild blankness of a man reading about weather, while inside them everything went quiet and cold, the way a house goes quiet when someone is walking through it checking the locks.

Registration. They knew what binding meant. Every child raised on temple stories knew: the witch-fires of the old reigns, the collars of cold iron, the gifted dragged to the capital and returned hollow, smiling, obedient, *empty*. The stories were told as history, safely over. Now the history had grown a date.

Three years of debt, weighed against that. Zephyrine had gone back to their desk and copied forty lines of barley and lambs with hands that did not shake until the candles were lit for the evening office and no one was looking.

Now, this evening, the candle had guttered anyway. Old drafts in the scriptorium; the windows were original to the building and admitted the mountain wind through gaps the masons had long ago stopped pretending to fix. It happened twice, three times a week in the cold months. Each time, the same small theft of warmth, the same quick covering of the flame, the same held breath. Zephyrine had gotten careless with ease. That was the danger of a life built on being overlooked—you began to believe the world had agreed to it.

The scratch of pens around them continued. Brother Aldous two desks down, wheezing over his capitals. Sister Maren, who hummed through her nose when she worked and did not know she did it. Beyond the scriptorium door, the corridor ran to the Provost's study, and beyond that the stair, and beyond that the whole patient machinery of the temple, grinding its tithes and its prayers toward heaven.

Zephyrine wrote *one bolt of undyed wool* and felt, rather than heard, the door open.

They knew the tread before they turned their head. Provost Anselm Corvane walked the way heavy furniture is moved—deliberately, with pauses—and he paused now just inside the door, letting the silence do his announcing for him. He did that. He believed a room should rearrange itself around him, and mostly it did.

"Brother Aldous. Sister Maren." Nods, gracious as alms. "Scribe Dravenmoor. A word."

Not a request. Zephyrine rose, set their pen across the inkwell with the care of ritual, and followed him out. Behind them, faintly, Maren's humming resumed. Whatever this was, it would be spoken of by supper, dissected, assigned its proper size. Let them assign it small. Small was safe.

The Provost's study smelled of beeswax and old leather and, beneath both, the stone-cold breath of the mountain that came up through the floors even here. He closed the door. He did not offer a chair, which meant the interview would be brief, or he wanted Zephyrine standing off-balance, or both. Anselm Corvane wasted very little, but what he spent, he spent on purpose.

"You were at Vespers," he said, settling behind his desk, "in the scriptorium."

"I copy the tithing rolls at that hour, Reverend Provost. As always."

"As always." He folded his hands. His rings caught the lamplight—three of them, and the third, the plain iron band worn under the others where few would notice, that was the one that mattered. Zephyrine had never learned what it signified. They suspected they were about to. "Tell me about the candle."

The room did not move, but Zephyrine fell through it anyway—that specific vertigo, floor becoming ceiling, certainty becoming air. They had rehearsed this moment for nine years. They had never once believed it would come.

"The candle, Reverend Provost?"

"The third candle on your row. It gutters in the wind from the east window. It has guttered every winter since I have held this office." His voice stayed level, almost gentle, which was worse than shouting. Gentle was what he used when he had already won. "Tonight it guttered, and then it did not. And I watched you, Scribe, because I have watched you for some time, and I saw your hand move. Not to trim the wick. You did not touch it at all. And yet the flame stood up straight as a soldier called to attention."

Every answer Zephyrine owned presented itself and was found worthless. *I don't know what you mean.* He would smile. *A draught must have shifted.* He would stop smiling. Running was pointless; the study had one door and the Provost sat between them and nothing heavier than a letter knife. So they did the only honest thing left, which was also the most dangerous: they said nothing, and let the silence confess for them.

Anselm Corvane studied them for a long moment. Then, to Zephyrine's everlasting confusion, he sighed—not the sigh of a hunter closing his trap, but of a tired man setting down a weight he had carried farther than he'd wished.

"So," he said. "Nine years. Do you know how many times I have nearly reported you? Twice the visiting examiners noticed the residue. Once, old Brother Fenwick swore the archive lamps never needed trimming on your nights, and Fenwick missed nothing in thirty years. Each time I smoothed it over. Each time I told myself I was being prudent, managing the temple's assets." He unfolded his hands. "I am tired of lying to myself, Scribe. I will not lie to you either. I have known since your second year."

The floor tilted again, but differently now. Zephyrine gripped the edge of the visitor's stool to keep the world honest. "You've—known. For seven years. And you said nothing."

"I said nothing because reporting you would have been the destruction of something the temple may yet need." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and crossed to the far wall—to the shelf Zephyrine had dusted a thousand times without ever once wondering why it stood empty, why its wood showed no sun-fade where books should have leaned. He pressed the iron ring of his third finger into a knot in the grain and turned it, and the shelf swung inward on a hinge older than any hinge had a right to be.

Cold air breathed out. Not cellar-cold. *Mountain*-cold, deep and mineral, carrying a smell Zephyrine had no name for—stone and snow and underneath it something sweeter, like the ghost of flowers pressed in a book for a hundred years.

"Come," said the Provost. "And touch nothing until I tell you what you are touching."

* * *

The stair went down farther than the temple had foundations.

Zephyrine counted the turns out of habit—scribe's habit, counting everything, trusting numbers when nothing else held—and lost count at eleven. The walls changed as they descended: dressed granite, then rougher blocks, then stone that was not built at all but *grown*, veined and knotted like the heartwood of some impossible tree, and warm. That was the wrongness that made the hair rise along Zephyrine's arms. The deeper they went, the warmer it became, and the warmth had a pulse to it. Slow. Patient. The tempo of a sleeper's heart.

"What is this place?" Their voice came out smaller than they intended, and echoed strangely, as if the walls repeated it to each other before letting it go.

"The oldest part of this temple," the Provost said. "Older than Saint Verane. Older, I suspect, than the kingdom, though our charters do not enjoy admitting it." Lamplight—he carried a lantern now, its flame burning oddly still in the moving air—threw his shadow huge against the living stone. "You have spent nine years copying the tithes, Scribe. Grain, wool, lambs. Do you know what those tithes are *for*?"

"Upkeep of the temples. Alms for the poor. The—"

"The first answer they teach children, yes. And above it, the second answer: the glory of the realm." He stopped before a door. Not a door, exactly—an archway filled with a slab of dark wood banded in metal gone black with age, sealed with a lock the size of a fist and, around the lock's edges, a thin seam of wax stamped with a sigil Zephyrine had seen only once before, on the seal of royal charters. "The truth is the third answer, and it has been kept so well that even most of my own clergy die without learning it. The tithes buy silence, Scribe. Ours and everyone else's. Because there is a place in these mountains where magic was born, and a covenant that bound it, and the binding is failing—and every institution in this realm, crown and church alike, is built on top of that fact like a house built over a grave."

He produced a key from around his neck—another thing Zephyrine had dusted past daily and never questioned, thinking it a reliquary—and turned the great lock. The wax seal cracked with a sound like a knuckle.

Inside lay a scroll case of lead, and inside the case, when he drew it out with both hands as one lifts an infant, a scroll wrapped in silk gone grey with centuries. He laid it on the stone table at the chamber's center and unwrapped it, and the smell that rose up was the flower-smell again, stronger now, sweet enough to ache.

"This is the Covenant of the Sundered Garden," the Provost said. "The original account of the binding of magic—the true one, not the psalm-taught version. It was written by witnesses. Some of them were kings. One of them, I believe, was a woman the stories call the Gardener, though whether she was a person, an office, or something else entirely, the text does not say plainly." His eyes, pale and tired, lifted to Zephyrine's face. "It has been copied only six times in four hundred years, always by a scribe chosen for discretion, always destroyed afterward, the copies burned and the ash scattered. The temple does not trust memory. Memory can be interrogated. Paper can simply be burned."

"Why are you telling me this?" Zephyrine's mouth had gone dry. The pulse in the walls was louder down here, or perhaps they were only hearing their own blood and attributing it to the stone. "Why show me?"

"Because you are going to copy it."

The words dropped into the chamber and rang there. Zephyrine stared at him. "Reverend Provost—the proclamation—the crown is hunting people like me, and you want me to—"

"To copy, in your hand, which is the finest in this temple and which I have spent seven years making certain stayed here, a document that must exist in duplicate before spring." He began rolling the silk back from the scroll itself, and Zephyrine caught a glimpse of the first line—a script older than any they had studied, yet shaped like the ancestor of every alphabet they knew, the way a river valley resembles the river that cut it. "The crown's new Inquisition will reach us within the month. When it comes, they will audit our archives, and they will find this chamber, because they have ways of finding things that do not respect locks. If they find one covenant scroll, they take it and we lose everything. But paper burns, Scribe, and ash scatters, and a text that exists in two places cannot be wholly killed. I need a second copy hidden where not even I know its resting place. And I need a copyist who understands what the words *mean*, because some passages require interpretation to transcribe at all."

"And a copyist who can be trusted with the greatest secret in the realm," Zephyrine said slowly, "is best chosen from among people the realm already wants dead. We have nowhere else to go. Our silence comes free."

Something flickered across the Provost's face that might have been shame, quickly filed away. "I prefer to call it aligned interest."

Zephyrine looked down at the scroll. At the first line, which they could almost—almost—read, the shapes pulling at some faculty they had never known they owned, the way a tuning fork pulls at a note sleeping in a wire. The gift stirred behind their sternum, leaning toward the ancient writing like a plant toward light, and for the first time in nine years Zephyrine did not smother it. They let it look.

And the scroll—there was no other way to say it—looked back.

Warmth flooded up their arm, not their own warmth, something older and vaster, and for one heartbeat Zephyrine smelled rain on hot stone and flowering vines and heard, far away, a sound like a bell made of water. Then the Provost's hand closed on their shoulder, breaking the contact, and the vision snapped shut.

"It does that," Anselm Corvane said quietly. "To the gifted, especially. Which is the last thing I have to tell you, and then you may ask your questions." He took a breath, and for the first time since Zephyrine had known him, the Provost of Saint Verane looked afraid. "The Garden is not a story, Scribe. It is a real place, in the Thornfells, north of the high pass. And it is waking up. The stone that holds the old binding—we keep it below the reliquary, have kept it for three hundred years—is singing. Faintly. More each month. And when a bound thing sings long enough, Scribe, it eventually remembers that it was once free, and everything the realm has built upon its captivity comes down with it."

He laid the quill in Zephyrine's hand. It was a good quill; Zephyrine had cut it themselves.

"Begin tonight. Copy nothing out of this chamber—not one word, not on scrap. And Scribe Dravenmoor." His grip tightened once, hard. "Whatever the scroll shows you, whatever you feel it wanting—remember that the temple's survival depends on what we are doing here. Not the crown's. *Ours.*"

Above them, faint through eleven turnings of stair, the Vespers bell rang out over the valley—clear, silver, certain of itself—and Zephyrine, standing in the warm dark over a secret that rewrote nine years of their life, understood suddenly that they had never once known what they were working for. Every ledger, every tithe, every lamb and measure of barley: not alms, not glory. Hush-money. Paid upward, paid outward, paid to everyone who might otherwise look down and see the grave beneath the house.

They bent to the first line, and began to write.

And outside, far down the mountain road, unseen by any watchman, a rider in grey was already climbing toward the temple gate, carrying a list of names—and the list had grown shorter with every mile, and there was room on it still.
