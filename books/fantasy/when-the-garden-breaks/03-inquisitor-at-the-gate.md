## 3. Inquisitor at the Gate

The road to Saint Verane's climbed through orchards gone feral with late autumn, and Halvard rode it slowly, because he had learned that a man who arrives slowly is feared more than a man who arrives fast. Speed suggested urgency, and urgency could be managed. Slowness suggested certainty, and certainty could not.

He had not been back to the temple in eleven years. The last time, he had come as a novice with ink-stained cuffs and a head full of canon law, walking beside Zephyrine Dravenmoor through these same orchards while they argued about whether mercy was a debt or a gift. He had argued for debt. Zephyrine had argued for gift. He remembered thinking, even then, that Zephyrine argued like someone who had already lost the argument in private and was only going through the motions of hoping.

Now he came back with a warrant, a list of names, and two clerks who rode behind him like well-trained shadows. The list was folded inside his breast pocket, against his heart, which was where the Provost's letter had instructed him to keep it. *Names are sacred things,* the letter said. *They must be carried close.* Halvard had read that line four times before he understood it was not piety. It was instruction in how to hold a knife.

The temple appeared around the last bend the way it always did: suddenly, and all at once, as if the mountain had been keeping it and grew tired of the game. Grey stone, slate roof, a bell tower that leaned two degrees eastward out of stubbornness rather than age. Smoke from the kitchen fires. The smell of woodsmoke and wet leaves and, beneath it, the particular cold-clean scent of sanctified ground that every Inquisitor learned to distrust on principle, because sanctity was the most commonly counterfeited currency in the realm.

He dismounted at the gate and did not knock. That was the first courtesy of an audit: you did not knock. Knocking implied you might be turned away.

The porter opened the gate anyway, because porters always did, and Halvard walked his horse into the outer court with the two clerks trailing him, and the bell above began to ring—not the hour, not the office, but the alarm pattern, three strokes and a pause, three strokes and a pause—and he stood in the middle of the courtyard listening to it with something that was almost nostalgia.

"They'll be gathering in the chapter house by now," he said to the younger clerk, whose name was Pellin and who flinched at being addressed. "Count the doors as we go. I want to know how many ways out of this building there are."

"There's always one more," Pellin said, with the flat wisdom of a boy who had grown up in a smuggling town and never quite shed the accent.

"Then find it."

* * *

The Provost received him in the abbot's parlor, which was the wrong room and both of them knew it. An audit belonged in the scriptorium, among the records, among the light. A parlor was for conversations that would never be written down. Father Ambrose—Provost now, though Halvard had known him as a lean young canon with a stutter he'd prayed away through sheer repetition—sat behind a table too small for the space, so that he seemed to float in the middle of the room like a man standing on ice over deep water.

"You've come about the proclamation," Ambrose said.

"I've come about the list." Halvard set his satchel on the table but did not open it. "The crown requires every person gifted with unbound magic to present themselves for registration within sixty days. The temples are required to assist. I'm here to assist the assistance."

"And if we decline?"

"Then I write down that you declined, and someone less patient than me comes next spring." He let that sit. "You won't decline. You've never declined anything the crown has asked. That's rather the problem with this place."

Ambrose's hands were folded on the table, and Halvard watched them—the way the right thumb pressed against the left knuckle, once, twice, a metronome of thought. Eleven years ago that thumb had corrected his Greek conjugations. Now it counted something else.

"The temple cooperates fully," Ambrose said. "You know that. We have always believed that the gifted are best served by order."

"*Served*," Halvard repeated softly. "That's an interesting word for it."

"It's the word the canons use."

"The canons use a great many words." He opened the satchel at last and drew out the list—not the master list, which stayed against his heart, but the working copy, which he laid flat and turned to face the Provost. "Sixty-three names in this diocese. Forty-one have registered already. Nine are dead—I've verified six of those personally, which was unpleasant work, and I'd thank you not to make me do it again here. Thirteen remain unaccounted for." He tapped the page. "I'm going to say them aloud, Father, and you're going to tell me what you know. Not what you suspect. What you know. There's a difference, and I've built a career on the difference."

Ambrose read down the column. His face did the thing that trained faces do, which is nothing, which is worse than anything. But his thumb stopped its counting.

"Say them," Halvard said.

And Halvard said them. Name after name, some of them half-remembered faces from his novitiate years—the gardener's daughter who could coax blight out of apple trees with her bare hands; the old brother who kept bees and whose hives never swarmed because, everyone whispered, he asked them nicely; a lay sister whose name Halvard had never learned, only her habit of candles lighting themselves when she walked past, which the temple had explained for thirty years as drafts.

He reached the bottom of the column. The last name sat there in his own careful hand, copied from the Provost's own letter, and when he spoke it his voice came out level and cold and entirely unlike the voice of the boy who had once shared a cell with its owner.

"Zephyrine Dravenmoor."

The silence afterward had texture. It had weight. Somewhere outside, a crow was worrying the orchard, and its cawing came through the window glass like a needle through cloth.

"Zephyrine is my most reliable scribe," Ambrose said finally. "Meticulous. Devout. Unremarkable."

"Unremarkable." Halvard almost smiled. "Yes. That's what the report says. Three separate informants, independently, used exactly that word. *Unremarkable.* Do you know what I call a person whom three people describe identically as unremarkable?"

"A scribe."

"A *discipline*," Halvard said. "Being unremarkable is the hardest magic there is, Father. It takes practice. It takes daily effort. And no one practices that hard without something remarkable underneath." He closed the working copy. "I want to see the scriptorium."

"Of course."

"And I want to see Zephyrine Dravenmoor."

"Of course," Ambrose said again, and his thumb resumed its counting, faster now.

* * *

They walked him through the cloister, and Halvard made himself walk slowly, look at everything, touch nothing. This was the second courtesy of an audit: you touched nothing, because touching told the watched what you wanted. He looked instead—at the frost-killed herb beds, at the rain barrel with its skin of ice, at the young brothers scurrying along the covered walk with their eyes down and their pace just slightly too quick, the pace of people carrying news ahead of them.

He knew this place. That was the cruelty of it. He knew which flagstone rocked underfoot near the lavatory, knew the smell of the tallow they used in the north corridor, knew that the third window in the scriptorium stuck and had to be lifted with the heel of your hand. He had kissed a girl behind the bee-brother's hives at midsummer fair—no, not a girl, he corrected himself, a novice named Sera who had left the order two years later and married a wheelwright, and good for her—and he had learned to read in the very room he was now walking toward with a list of names in his pocket.

The scriptorium was warm and smelled of oak gall ink and tallow and the faint animal sweetness of vellum. Twelve desks. Six occupied. The brothers bent over their work like men praying with their hands.

Zephyrine was at the fourth desk.

Halvard saw them before they saw him—or before they admitted seeing him, which with Zephyrine had always been the same thing. They sat the way they had always sat, spine curved slightly forward, shoulders drawn in, taking up as little of the world as a body could manage. Their hair was pinned back without ornament. Their robe was the plainest in the room, mended at the cuff. Everything about them was arranged to be passed over, and it struck Halvard, with a small cold shock, that he had nearly done exactly that—he had scanned the room twice before his eye found them, and both times it had slid off like water off wax.

That was the thing people didn't understand about the gifted, he thought. It wasn't fireballs. It wasn't storms. It was this—the quiet, patient, practiced art of not being seen, which was far more dangerous, because a fireball announced itself, and a hidden thing could grow.

Or—said another voice, smaller, older, wearing his own face from eleven years ago—it was simply Zephyrine, who had always been good at disappearing, who had taught Halvard half of what he knew about moving through a crowded refectory unnoticed, who had once gotten them both out of trouble with the precentor by the simple expedient of standing still behind a pillar until the man's anger found nowhere to land.

He stopped at the foot of their desk.

"Scribe Dravenmoor."

Zephyrine looked up. Their eyes were the same—grey-green, watchful, with that quality he had never had words for, a kind of held breath behind them, as if they were perpetually waiting for a door to open somewhere. They had lines at the corners now that they hadn't had at nineteen. Otherwise time had barely troubled them. Time, apparently, also found them easy to overlook.

"Inquisitor Halvard," they said. Their voice was steady. Their pen, he noticed, had been set down with exaggerated care, parallel to the desk edge, as if putting it down neatly might somehow put down everything else. "Welcome back to Saint Verane's."

"You remember me."

"Eleven years doesn't burn a man out of memory." A pause. "Though I hear it burns other things."

He should have laughed. Once, he would have laughed—that was their old rhythm, the exchange of small barbed courtesies, affection disguised as fencing. Instead he heard himself say, quietly, "We need to talk. Not here."

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "Not here."

The Provost cleared his throat somewhere behind them, and Halvard became aware that the entire scriptorium was listening with its pens—six brothers writing nothing, scratching nothing, merely holding their nibs above the page like a congregation holding its breath.

"The east parlor is free," Ambrose offered.

"The courtyard," Halvard said. "I prefer the cold. Cold keeps conversations honest."

* * *

They stood between the rain barrel and the dead herb beds, out of earshot of the cloister walk, or near enough. Frost had silvered the raised beds. Their breath hung between them in small clouds, meeting and mingling, and Halvard hated that detail, hated the intimacy of it, hated that his body insisted on remembering a hundred winter mornings when they had stood like this on chores duty and talked about everything except the thing between them.

"You look well," Zephyrine said.

"I look like what I am."

"Yes. That's what I meant."

He turned his face toward the mountains. Thornfell stood up beyond the orchard wall, white-capped, indifferent, and he looked at it because it was easier than looking at them. "Do you know why I took the white cord, Zephyrine?"

"Because you believe the world can be saved by filing it correctly."

"Because of the night my mother died." He said it flatly, without drama, because drama was a luxury he had surrendered with his grey robes. "You remember. You were there. You held my hand in the infirmary while the fever took her, and afterward the temple gave us both a blessing and a bill. The blessing was free. The bill was for the candle, the bed, the oil, the prayers. Fourteen marks. My father sold the boat to pay it." He finally looked at them. "I decided that night that the world was full of small debts, and someone had to keep the ledger honestly. That's all an Inquisitor is, Zephyrine. A ledger-keeper. I keep accounts. When someone's account comes due, I collect."

"And mine has come due."

"Yours came due the moment the crown signed the proclamation. I didn't write your name on the list. I only carried it."

"Someone wrote it."

"The Provost wrote it." He watched them take that blow, watched it land somewhere behind the eyes, and felt nothing—which was a lie, he felt a great deal, and the feeling had the specific shape of grief, but he had become expert at setting feelings aside the way a mason sets aside stones that don't fit the wall. "Three informants. Independent. One of them described a candle."

Zephyrine's face went very still. Stillness, in them, had always been louder than motion.

"A candle," they repeated.

"Guttering. In a closed room. With no draft." He kept his voice low, almost gentle, and gentleness from an Inquisitor is the cruelest register there is. "You know what happens next. Registration. Examination. If the examination finds corruption—" He stopped. He made himself finish it, because they deserved to hear it from a friend, if he was still that, and not from a stranger with a warrant. "If it finds corruption, there's a pyre. If it finds talent, there's a collar and a life of licensed service. Either way, Zephyrine, the person who walks out of that examination isn't the person who walks in."

"And you'll conduct it yourself? For old times' sake?"

"If I don't, they'll send Cailean." The name dropped between them like a stone into a well, and he watched them understand how deep it went. Brother Cailean's reputation preceded him the way a landslide precedes its dust. "Cailean doesn't remember anyone fondly. Cailean has never been nineteen in a scriptorium with anyone. So yes. I'll do it myself. Because I am, whatever else I've become, still the gentlest option available to you."

"That's a terrible sentence, Halvard."

"I know."

"You've rehearsed it. I can hear the rehearsal in it. You've said it to yourself on the road, polishing it, making sure the mercy sounded like mercy and not like a trap." Zephyrine's voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "When did you stop hearing the difference?"

Something moved behind his ribs, some old hairline fracture flexing under load. He didn't answer. He looked at the mountains again, at Thornfell, white and vast and full of roads that led away from everything.

"Present yourself for registration," he said. "Day after tomorrow. I'll delay my departure report until then. After that, my hands are tied, and I mean that literally—the report goes to the diocesan office, and the diocesan office sends riders, and riders don't remember anyone fondly either." He drew a folded paper from his sleeve and held it out. "The examination questions. Advance copy. It's irregular. It's the last irregular thing I will ever do for you."

Zephyrine took it. Their fingers brushed his, briefly, cold against cold, and he felt the contact like a brand.

"Halvard." They looked up at him, and for a moment—for one unguarded moment—the held-breath quality behind their eyes broke open, and he saw what was actually in there, and it frightened him, because it wasn't guilt and it wasn't fear. It was pity. "What did they promise you? When they gave you the white cord. What did they tell you the ledger was *for*?"

"Order," he said. "Safety. A world where no child watches a fever take her mother because the prayers cost fourteen marks."

"And who keeps the temple's account? Who collects *their* debts?" Zephyrine folded the paper into their sleeve with neat, scribe's precision. "You're auditing the wrong books, old friend. You always have."

He wanted to argue. He opened his mouth to argue. And then, across the courtyard, a bell rang—one clean stroke, the hour, noon—and every bird in the orchard rose at once, a dark sudden cloud of wings, and the sound of them filled the whole sky over Saint Verane's, and Halvard stood in the middle of it feeling, for the first time in eleven years, that he was standing inside a sentence he had not written and could not edit.

Zephyrine bowed their head slightly—not submission; acknowledgment, the acknowledgment one professional gives another—and walked back toward the scriptorium door, unhurried, unremarkable, taking up as little of the world as a body could manage.

At the threshold they paused. They did not turn around.

"The candle wasn't guttering," they said, just loud enough to carry. "It was bowing. There's a difference. I'd have thought a ledger-keeper would know the difference between a thing that's failing and a thing that's paying respect."

Then they were gone, and the door closed behind them, and Halvard stood alone in the courtyard with the frost coming up out of the ground and the birds settling back into the trees, and he took out the master list from his breast pocket, unfolded it, and read the last name again.

*Dravenmoor, Zephyrine — scribe — unremarkable.*

Underneath it, in the margin, in his own hand, in ink he did not remember using, there was a note. Small. Careful. Written in a script that was almost—but not quite—his own:

*Ask him about the Garden.*

He had no memory of writing it. He had never heard of any garden. And the ink was still wet.
