## 4. Theft of a Stone

The Provost's order came at dusk, delivered on vellum so fine it felt like touching a moth's wing.

*Burn it,* the note said, in Brother Cailean's cramped and pious hand. *The covenant scroll. Tonight. No record of its copying is to remain. Bring me the ash.*

Zephyrine read it three times in the scriptorium's failing light, while around them the other scribes packed their inkstones and blew out their candles one by one, and the great room settled into its nightly hush of wood-cool air and tallow-smell. The note was small enough to close a fist around. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

They had copied that scroll eleven days ago. They knew every line of it now the way a person knows a scar—by touch, by ache, by the way weather changes it. The Garden. The binding. The first kings kneeling in a sanctuary older than their crowns, taking what had been given freely and making of it a possession, a chain, a treasury. And beneath all of it, written in a hand so old the ink had gone the color of dried blood: *the stone remembers what the realm would rather forget.*

The temple had ordered them to copy a secret, then ordered them to destroy it. As if paper could be made to forget what it had said. As if a scribe could.

Zephyrine folded the note into quarters, then eighths, until it was a hard little seed in their palm, and did not burn it either.

* * *

They waited for the third bell, when even the brothers who prayed badly were asleep, and the temple became what it truly was underneath its incense: a building holding its breath.

Zephyrine moved through the cloister like a shadow that had learned manners. They knew this place the way they knew their own hands—not because they loved it, but because they had spent nine years studying its indifference, mapping every corner where attention refused to fall. The loose flagstone by the almonry where the mortar had crumbled and no one had ever bothered to look beneath. The stretch of corridor where the torch-brackets stood empty because the Provost considered flame near the archives a sin against economy. The door to the undercroft whose lock was older than the temple itself and opened, Zephyrine had discovered two winters ago during a fit of insomnia they'd never confessed to, to a hairpin bent twice.

Nine years of being overlooked had made them a scholar of overlookment. Every blind spot in Saint Verane's vast and watchful eye, catalogued. Every hour when the brothers' footsteps thinned. They had built their invisibility plank by plank, and now, tonight, they were going to walk across it and out.

But not empty-handed.

The reliquary vault lay beneath the eastern transept, down stairs worn into shallow smiles by centuries of pilgrim knees. Zephyrine had carried tithes down those stairs a hundred times—candle-wax, grain-scrip, the small silver of penitents—and had passed the iron-banded door at the bottom with eyes lowered, the way one passes a rich man's coffin. The covenant stone slept behind that door. Everyone in the temple knew it existed; no one below the Provost knew what it was. A relic, the brothers called it. *The Founding Stone of Saint Verane's Grace.* They touched their foreheads toward it on feast days without knowing they were saluting a prison.

The hairpin worked as it always worked, with a click so soft it might have been an apology. Inside, the vault was cold enough to see breath, and dark except for the stone itself.

It sat on a plinth of black basalt, the size of a loaf of bread, unremarkable as river-rock—and yet the moment Zephyrine's eyes found it, the candle they carried leaned toward it. Not the flame. The whole candle, wax and wick and all, tilting in its holder like a plant toward sun. And beneath Zephyrine's sternum, in the locked cellar where they kept their gift buried alive, something turned over in its sleep and pressed its face to the wall.

*Oh,* said the gift, in the voice it never used aloud. *There you are.*

"I know," Zephyrine whispered. "I know. Hush."

The stone's surface was carved all over with writing so fine it might have been frost-work, spirals within spirals, and where the candlelight touched it the characters seemed to move—not flicker, not shimmer, but *move*, the way water moves under ice. Zephyrine had seen it once before, years ago, carried past in the Provost's procession, and had felt then only a vague unease, the way one feels a word remembered from a dream. Now they understood the unease. The stone was not a relic. It was a lock. And locks, Zephyrine thought, lifting it from its plinth with both hands, are only interesting because of what they keep.

It was heavier than bread. Heavier than it should have been, as though weight were a thing it could choose. As they lifted it, the pressure behind their sternum rose to meet it, gift straining toward stone like a dog toward a whistle only it could hear, and for one vertiginous heartbeat the cellar-door inside them rattled on its hinges—

Zephyrine breathed. Counted. Four in, seven hold, eight out—the old trick, the hiding-trick, the trick that had kept them alive through fevers and audits and the long slow suffocation of a life spent pretending to be less. The gift subsided, sulking. The stone went quiet against their chest, or perhaps not quiet; perhaps listening.

Wrapped in altar-linen, tucked into the satchel beneath the scroll-case, it looked like nothing. Like laundry. Like a debt paid in kind.

* * *

The scroll came last, and it came easily, which frightened Zephyrine more than the stone had.

The archive at night was a country of shelf-shadows and dust that hung in the candlelight like held breath. The covenant scroll lived in the sealed case in the inner alcove, behind the Provost's own key, and Zephyrine had expected to stand before that case for an hour, sweating, improvising. Instead they found the case already open. Empty brackets. A single wax seal cracked neatly down the middle and left lying on the shelf like a shed skin.

Someone had gotten there first—or someone had left it open *for* them. Zephyrine stood in the cold dark doing smuggler's arithmetic with no numbers to count, and could solve none of it. Cailean wanted ash. The Provost wanted silence. And the case stood open like a mouth mid-word.

In the end it didn't matter. The scroll was where scrolls always were when temples wanted things forgotten: in the fire-alcove, laid on the grate beside kindling already stacked, waiting for morning. Zephyrine took it up and felt the vellum warm from some heat of its own, faint as a sleeping animal, and slid it into the scroll-case, and closed the case, and stood a moment in the dark with their heart going like a fist on a door.

Eleven days ago they had copied these words. The copy still existed—in their memory, in the practice-sheets hidden beneath the floorboard under their cot, in the fair hand they'd made and hidden inside a psalter of Cailean's own devotionals, where zealotry made such excellent camouflage. Let the temple burn its secrets. Secrets copied are secrets multiplied. That was the first law of scribes, and Zephyrine had been a scribe longer than they had been anything else.

They left the fire-alcove exactly as they'd found it, minus one scroll. If the gods of small mercies were watching, let them note the tidiness.

* * *

The smugglers' road out of Saint Verane began, absurdly, in the tithe-cellar.

Everyone knew the temple had been built on the bones of something older—every temple was—but few knew that the older thing had included a gate. Zephyrine had found it the way they found everything: by being sent somewhere tedious. Three years ago, inventorying wine casks in the deep cellar, they'd noticed the draught. Cold air moving where no air should move, sliding along the floor like a cat. They'd followed it past the last cask, past the bricked-up arch the inventories called *the Old Gate, disused*, to a section of wall where the bricks were newer than their neighbors and mortared with a haste the rest of the masonry would have been ashamed of.

Behind the bricks: a passage. Behind the passage: the mountain.

The smugglers of the Thornfell foothills had run this vein for generations—salt out, tin and quieter goods in—and the temple had bought its silence with tithes and blindness, the way institutions buy everything. Zephyrine had never used the passage. Had never dared. But they had *measured* it, in the way of people who keep escape plans the way other people keep prayers: privately, shamefully, against a day they hoped would never come.

The day had come.

The bricks came away in courses, quietly, each one numbered in chalk on its inner face—Zephyrine's own chalk, their own numbering, from three years of nights spent loosening mortar a fingertip at a time. Nine years in the temple and three of them spent digging out, which meant that for a third of their time in Saint Verane, Zephyrine Dravenmoor had been leaving. They almost laughed, easing the last brick free and feeling night air pour through the gap, cold and pine-sharp and *enormous*. Almost. Their throat was too tight for laughing.

They went through sideways, satchel first, the stone pressing its strange chosen weight against their spine.

The passage was low and dry and smelled of rock and old smoke, and Zephyrine walked it by feel and by the memorized count of paces—four hundred to the first fork, bear left, two hundred more to the drainage cut where the roof dipped and you had to go bent double like a penitent. The dark was total. Their candle they'd pinched before the vault and did not relight; flame could be seen for miles through the wrong eyes, and tonight every eye in the valley might be watching. So they went blind, one hand on the wall, counting, and the stone rode against their back and *hummed*—not sound, not quite, but a vibration in the teeth, in the breastbone, a note too low for ears and exactly right for the thing caged behind Zephyrine's ribs.

And the thing behind their ribs answered.

Zephyrine stopped in the blackness, hand flat against cold rock, and let it happen for three full seconds—an eternity, a recklessness—let the gift rise up through the floors of them and press outward, and felt the mountain answer back. Not words. *Weather.* A sense of vast slow attention turning toward them the way a sleeper turns toward a window left open. The pass above them, the roots of the Thornfells, the whole patient mineral dark—all of it aware, mildly, incuriously, of a small warm creature walking its veins carrying a piece of its oldest grief.

Then Zephyrine shut the door again, four in, seven hold, eight out, and walked on with their heart slamming and tears standing freezing in their eyes, and could not have said whether the tears were terror or joy, and suspected, with a giddiness that felt like falling, that they were the same thing.

* * *

They came out of the mountain an hour before dawn, through a screen of juniper onto a ledge above the treeline-road, and the world was there waiting for them, all of it, indecently large.

Stars in their thousands, sharp as nailheads. The valley below drowned in blue fog with the temple's bell-tower rising out of it like a drowning man's last finger. Wind moving through the pines with a sound like the sea deciding something. Zephyrine stood on the ledge with the satchel against their chest and breathed, and kept breathing, and the breath went all the way down for the first time in nine years—all the way to the bottom of their lungs, to the silted-over places, and stirred them.

Free. The word arrived without permission and Zephyrine examined it warily, the way one examines a coin that seems too bright. Free was a word for other people. Free was a word for people whose names weren't on lists, who hadn't spent a decade paying a family debt in ink, who hadn't just committed theft against the most patient institution in the realm. The crown's proclamation was pinned to every church door from here to the coast: *all gifted must register.* Halvard was coming—Halvard, whose name had arrived in the Provost's correspondence two weeks ago like a stone dropped down a well, still falling, not yet landing. Somewhere behind them in the fog, bells would be ringing prime soon, and a brother would walk to the fire-alcove with kindling, and find the grate bare.

Zephyrine counted the ledger of it, because counting was how they survived everything: one stolen scroll, one stolen stone, one forged writ sewn into their coat lining, forty-one miles of Thornfell between them and Grell's Hollow, and a name—*Ossa Vane*, whispered once in a tavern years ago by a dying smuggler who owed the temple nothing and had said it like a password—attached to a town where passage could be bought by anyone who didn't ask why.

Against that: pursuit. Discovery. The gift, awake now and refusing, Zephyrine suspected, to go fully back to sleep. The stone humming against their spine like a second heart that wasn't theirs.

The arithmetic did not balance. It didn't need to. For nine years Zephyrine had balanced every column, hidden every flare, swallowed every flare of light when candles guttered, made themselves small enough to slip through the world's attention like a coin through fingers. The columns had never once, in nine years, come out even—they had only come out *survivable*. This was different. This was a debt taken on willingly, in broad starlight, with open eyes.

Below, the temple bell began to ring prime, and the sound climbed the cliff face and broke over the ledge like a wave.

Zephyrine pulled the coat tighter, shifted the satchel high on their back, and turned north, toward the pass, toward Grell's Hollow, toward the sundered Garden they had copied in a dead language and now carried on their spine.

The first step off the ledge was the hardest. The second was easier. By the tenth, they were running—down the switchbacks, through the juniper, along the old drover's road with the fog opening before them and closing behind—and the wind took their breath and gave it back colder and cleaner, and somewhere inside the satchel the stone sang one clear silent note that no one in the world heard but them.

Behind them, very faint, very far, a second bell began to ring. Not prime. Alarm.

Zephyrine Dravenmoor did not look back. They had spent nine years learning exactly how not to be seen.

Now they intended to learn, quickly, whatever came next—how to be *gone*.
