## 5. Smuggler's Arithmetic

Grell's Hollow announced itself the way all honest places do: by smell.

Zephyrine smelled it before they saw it—woodsmoke and wet slate, tanneries and tar, the sour-sweet rot of the distillery that gave the town its only real export. Then the road bent around a shoulder of black rock, and there it was below them, folded into the mountain's armpit like something the Thornfells had hidden from the world on purpose. Forty roofs of grey shingle. A single crooked street running down to a river the color of old pewter. Chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless cold, as if even the air here had agreed not to draw attention.

Zephyrine stood at the edge of the overlook for a long moment, letting the ache in their legs become a thing they simply carried rather than a thing that owned them. Three days of walking. Two nights in a shepherd's bothy with the satchel clutched to their chest like a child. One morning spent crouched behind a cairn while a patrol of crown riders passed below, close enough that Zephyrine could hear the jingle of their tack and one soldier complaining about his boots.

Through all of it, the stone had been quiet. Not silent—it was never quite silent anymore—but quiet, the way a sleeping animal is quiet, its warmth seeping through the leather of the satchel into Zephyrine's hip until they had stopped being able to tell which heartbeat was whose.

*Not yet,* Zephyrine told it now, the way you'd speak to a dog scenting game. *We need food first. We need a road.*

The warmth pulsed once, unimpressed, and settled.

The path down was steep and greasy with meltwater, and by the time Zephyrine reached the first outlying houses their boots were soaked through and their hands were shaking—from cold, they told themselves, only from cold. The street smelled of onions frying somewhere, and under that of the river's mineral breath, and under *that* of something fainter and stranger: a thread of dye-work, madder root and iron mordant, the smell of cloth being made to hold a color it hadn't been born with.

*Everyone here is making things be other than what they are,* Zephyrine thought, and felt the first loosening in their chest since the alarm bell. *This is a town built on forgery. I might survive it after all.*

* * *

The inn was called the Drowned Bell, which struck Zephyrine as either very honest or very stupid advertising for a smugglers' den, and it sat at the bottom of the street where the river came closest, close enough that in flood season the water would surely lap its foundations. Inside: low beams, a fire that had opinions about who deserved its heat, and perhaps a dozen people who all stopped talking at exactly the same moment when the door opened.

Zephyrine knew this silence. They had spent nine years studying silences the way scholars studied scripture.

This one was professional. These were people who noticed doors because noticing doors kept them alive, and they were measuring the newcomer against three questions at once: *Is this someone sent? Is this someone worth robbing? Is this someone worth remembering?*

Zephyrine made themselves answer *no* to all three. Shoulders down, chin tucked, eyes moving with the dull efficiency of a traveler looking for a seat and nothing else. They had perfected this at Saint Verane's—the art of being furniture—and it held now too. The room's attention slid off them like rain off oilcloth. Conversation resumed, lower than before, and the woman behind the bar—a broad, grey-streaked woman with forearms like mooring rope—gave Zephyrine a nod that said, in the local dialect, *you may exist here briefly.*

"Broth," Zephyrine said, setting two coppers on the wood. "And a word about passage."

The woman looked at the coppers, then at Zephyrine's temple-grey traveling cloak, then at the satchel, which Zephyrine shifted casually behind their hip.

"Passage costs more than broth."

"I know what it costs. That's why we're having a word."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite amusement, but adjacent to it. "End table. Back of the room. Ask for Vane." She swept up the coins. "Eat slow. People who eat fast are people running toward something."

"And people running away?"

"People running away don't ask for Vane. They just leave." She paused, ladle dripping. "So which are you?"

Zephyrine met her eyes for exactly one second longer than a frightened person would have dared, and no longer than a confident one would have needed. "A customer," they said, and went to find a table.

* * *

Ossa Vane turned out to be the small woman in the corner booth playing cards against herself, and Zephyrine knew her at once, though they'd never seen her before, because everything about her was designed to be known late.

She was maybe fifty, narrow as a boat oar, with cropped iron hair and a face that might have been handsome once and had decided instead to be useful. Her hands were the tell: scarred across every knuckle, rope-callused, one finger missing at the second joint on the left hand. Those were working hands, but she wore them folded and idle over a scatter of cards, and her eyes—pale grey, unremarkable, forgettable on purpose—did not look up when Zephyrine approached. She finished laying out a card. Considered. Laid another.

"Sit," she said, when Zephyrine was still two steps away.

Zephyrine sat.

"You're the one asking after passage." Still not looking up. "Barkeep says you've got a word for me. I hate words. Words cost me money. Make yours short."

"The Thornfell high pass. Through to the far side. Within the week."

That got her eyes up. Grey, flat, quick as a fish under ice. They went over Zephyrine the way an assessor goes over cargo—cloak, boots, hands, face, the shape of the satchel—and Zephyrine watched themselves being weighed and understood that the weighing had started the moment they'd walked in, and probably the moment they'd walked into the valley.

"The pass is closed," Ossa said.

"It's snowed shut, not closed. There's drover's roads above the drift line."

"There are. And there's weather coming down off the shoulder that'll kill anyone on those roads by Thursday. And there's—" she tapped the table once, lightly, with one scarred finger, "—the matter of what's being carried. Because I don't move people up that mountain this time of year for love or coin. I move *cargo*. So. What's the cargo?"

"You," Zephyrine said carefully, "are assuming I'll tell you."

"I'm assuming you'll lie, and I'll price the lie accordingly." She gathered her cards with a sound like dry leaves. "Let's skip it. Passage through the pass, one passenger, one bag, no questions answered on my side and none asked on yours. Four hundred crowns."

Zephyrine did not flinch, because they had learned not to flinch before they'd learned to write, but inwardly some part of them that had done arithmetic on tithe ledgers for nine years whistled. Four hundred crowns was a chapel roof. Four hundred crowns was a decade of a dyer's wages.

"That's a number for someone who can pay it," Zephyrine said.

"It's a number for someone who's carrying something worth four hundred crowns. If your bag were empty you'd have argued already. You haven't argued. Which means either you're rich, you're desperate, or your bag's worth more than four hundred crowns, and desperate people argue worst of all." She leaned back against the wall. "So let's see the writ."

Zephyrine had expected this, but expectation is a thin coat in cold country. "I haven't shown you any writ."

"No. But you've got one. Temple courier, or near enough—you walk like ink, you sit like ink, and nobody else dresses that badly on purpose. Temple writs get my people past the toll-house at Wren Ford. Show me it's good and we'll talk about four hundred. Show me it's bad and we'll talk about what happens to people who try to buy me with bad paper."

Here it was, then. The moment the whole plan balanced on, and Zephyrine felt the familiar vertigo of the copyist's life—that strange double vision in which you are always both yourself and the thing you are imitating, always aware of the original hovering behind your hand.

They had written hundreds of writs at Saint Verane's. Real ones, with the Provost's seal and the chancellery flourishes. And they had, in the last fevered hour before the flight, written one more—copying not from memory alone but from muscle memory, the seal pressed in wax salvaged from the Provost's own rejected correspondence, the countermark faked with a pin heated over a candle. It was good work. It was possibly the best forgery ever attempted by a person whose hands were shaking so hard the wax had dripped.

It was also, Zephyrine knew, a death sentence if read closely, because the one thing they could not fake perfectly was the Provost's private cypher in the lower margin, which changed weekly and which Zephyrine had last seen six days ago.

They set the writ on the table.

Ossa picked it up. She didn't rush. She turned it in the light from the window, held it at arm's length, brought it close, tilted it so the wax caught the glow. Somewhere behind her eyes Zephyrine could feel the assessment happening, item by item, and each second stretched like taffy.

Then she set it down, face up, and pushed it back across the table with one finger.

"Seal's good. Hand's good. Margin cypher's a week stale." Her voice didn't change at all; it was the voice of a woman reporting the depth of a river. "Which means either you stole this from someone who hadn't used it yet, or you wrote it yourself, and if you wrote it yourself then you were inside the temple chancellery within the week, and if you were inside the chancellery within the week then whatever's in that bag is why half the bells between here and Verane's city are ringing."

The fire popped. Across the room, someone laughed at something, the ordinary laughter of ordinary people, and it sounded like it came from another world.

"So," Ossa said, folding her hands again. "Two hundred crowns. Half up front. And I choose not to care what you are."

Zephyrine blinked. "You just said the writ was bad."

"Bad paper, good story. The story's worth more than the paper. A person who steals from the chancellery and runs for the pass has enemies with deep pockets, and my whole trade is selling safe roads to people with deep-pocketed enemies. Besides—" and here something moved in her face, brief as a fish turning, gone before it could be named, "—I've been bought by temples and I've been bought by crowns, and both times I ended up wishing I'd been bought by the other one. You're neither. Yet. That's novel. Novelty's expensive, but I collect it cheap when I can."

"And if I'm what the bells say I am?"

"Then you're trouble, and trouble pays better than pilgrims." She stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and began sweeping the cards into a worn leather case. "Half now. We leave tomorrow before dawn, ahead of the weather and behind the patrols. There's a stable behind the tannery—you'll sleep there, because if you sleep here someone will remember your face, and faces are currency I don't carry." She paused at the edge of the booth, looking down, and for a moment the flat grey eyes were anything but flat.

"One question I do ask, since I'm not charging for it. That bag. Is it going to make things better or worse? For the likes of us. The unremarkable. The ones who just want the river to keep running."

Zephyrine thought about lying, because lying had kept them alive for nine years, and found—to their genuine surprise—that they didn't want to. Perhaps it was exhaustion. Perhaps it was the stone's warm weight against their hip, patient as a held breath. Perhaps it was simply that this woman had looked at the worst forgery of Zephyrine's life and priced it honestly, and honesty deserved a coin back.

"I don't know," Zephyrine said. "But I think it's going to make things *different*, and different is the only thing left to buy."

Ossa considered this for the length of one breath.

"Different," she said. "Huh." She dropped a battered leather purse onto the table—two hundred crowns' weight in it, Zephyrine realized dizzily; she was paying *them* an advance against future trouble. "Stable behind the tannery. Don't light a lamp. Don't sing. Don't do whatever it is you do that makes candles behave oddly, because the dyer next door notices odd, and he's got a daughter who notices it faster."

She was three steps away when she stopped, without turning around.

"For what it's worth, ink-walker. Nobody buys a road to the top of the world carrying an empty bag. Whatever's in there—I hope it's worth what it's going to cost you." Then she was gone into the smoke and noise of the common room, and Zephyrine sat alone with a purse of crowns, a forged writ, and the sudden, disorienting realization that for the first time in nine years, someone had looked directly at what they were—

—and chosen, calmly and for a fee, not to care.

It should have felt like danger. Instead it felt like the first warm room in a very long winter.

* * *

The stable behind the tannery smelled of hides and ammonia and old hay, and it was, Zephyrine decided, the finest lodging they had ever occupied. They paid the tanner's boy a copper to say nothing and got a nod and a gap-toothed grin, which meant the boy would tell everyone by morning, but that couldn't be helped and perhaps didn't need to be. In a town built on forgery, a stranger with temple money was just Tuesday.

Zephyrine made a nest in the cleanest straw, ate the bread and hard cheese they'd bought on the way down, and finally, finally opened the satchel.

The covenant stone lay in its wrapping of altar linen, and in the dark of the stable it was not dark at all. It gave off a light like moonlight remembered rather than seen—a pale, sourceless glow that moved when Zephyrine moved, brightening when their hand drew near as if leaning into a touch.

Up close, Zephyrine could see what they had never been permitted to see in nine years of dusting the reliquary case: marks on the stone's surface. Not carvings. *Scars.* Thin lines radiating from a central point, like cracks in ice, like the veins of a leaf, like—

Like a signature torn in half, Zephyrine thought. Like something written and then ripped apart.

And threaded through the scars, faint as breath on glass, movement. Light flowing along the fissures in slow circuits, returning always to the center, always beginning again. Bound, Zephyrine thought. Circling and circling and never getting out. Nine years I've copied ledgers of debts I didn't owe. What must it be, little stone, to be a ledger yourself?

The warmth rose to meet their palm through the linen. And this time, when Zephyrine closed their eyes, they saw—not a vision, nothing so grand—just an impression, brief and clear as a bell-note:

*A garden. Green light. Many voices saying one word together, and the word was yes.*

Then it was gone, and the stone dimmed to its banked embers, and Zephyrine sat in the straw of a smuggler's stable with tears they couldn't account for cooling on their face.

*Tomorrow,* they thought, wrapping the stone away. *Tomorrow the pass. Tomorrow the mountain that remembers. Tomorrow—*

Outside, boots on cobblestones. More than one pair. Moving with the particular unhurried rhythm of men who are searching rather than walking.

Zephyrine snuffed even the memory of light, drew the satchel into the straw, and held their breath as lamplight swung past the stable's gaps in long amber bars—and stopped.

Voices, low. The creak of a gate. The tanner's boy, awake past his hour, saying something frightened.

And then a voice Zephyrine knew—knew from a thousand shared meals, from study benches and choir lofts, from a friendship that had died without either of them attending the funeral—said, mild as morning:

"Then you won't mind showing me what your visitor carried in."
