## 6. The Hunt Begins

The road into Grell's Hollow smelled of mordant and wet wool.

Halvard Vess had ridden through a hundred such towns in his years for the Inquisition—river towns and mining towns, towns that clung to the skirts of the mountains like children afraid of being left—and he knew their smells the way other men knew the faces of their wives. Tanneries. Smoke. The sour-sweet reek of dye vats left too long in the sun. Grell's Hollow was a dyer's town above all else, its terraced houses stained blue to the second story where generations of indigo workers had leaned out to shake their cloths, its gutters running violet when it rained, so that the whole town seemed perpetually bruised.

He rode at the head of twelve men, and he did not like the town, and he did not trust it, and both of those things were correct.

"The ledger-keeper says a scribe came through three nights past," said Sergeant Ivo, drawing alongside. Ivo was a solid man with a soldier's talent for making bad news sound like weather. "Paid for passage upriver with a temple writ. Ossa Vane's boat."

"Vane." Halvard turned the name over. He had a file on her, thin as winter ice—a smuggler who specialized in the Thornfell passes, who had never once been caught carrying anything worth hanging for, which was itself a kind of signature. "And the writ?"

"Forged. Good forgery. Temple hand, temple seal wax, but the seal was pressed from a copy—the grain runs the wrong way under glass."

Of course it did. Zephyrine Dravenmoor had spent eleven years copying documents in the scriptorium of Saint Verane; of course they could forge one well enough to pass a river clerk at midnight. Halvard had watched them work once, years ago, before all of this—had stood behind their shoulder in the cold scriptorium while they laid down a line of tithes in ink so even it looked printed, and had thought, *there is a person who will never be noticed as long as they live*, and had meant it as a kindness.

He had been right about the not-being-noticed. He had simply failed to notice what else that kind of invisibility was for.

"Then we have them," he said. "A forged writ gives us warrant to search every boat Vane owns, and every loft she rents, and every cellar her cousins drink in. And a scribe does not run toward the Thornfells without a reason. Find out what's in the mountains that a scribe wants."

Ivo nodded and dropped back to give the order, and Halvard let his horse find its own pace down the last stretch of road, and made himself look at the town properly, because looking was the work.

That was what he told the novices, back when he'd still been allowed novices: *the Inquisition is not cruelty, it is attention*. Anyone can burn a witch. It takes discipline to find one—to read a town the way you'd read a suspect document, noting the small inconsistencies, the watered ink, the line that trembles where the hand pressed too hard. A gifted person always leaves residue. Not magic, exactly—magic burned clean—but the shape of accommodation around it. The neighbor who never quarreled with the dyer whose colors never ran. The well that never fouled. The child born crooked who walked straight by midsummer. Look for the places where luck pooled too thickly, and beneath the luck you would find the gift, and beneath the gift, if you were patient, you would find the person.

He had learned this from the Provost herself, twenty years ago, in a lecture hall that smelled of candle smoke and lilies. *The gifted are not monsters, Inquisitor*, she had said, and he had been young enough to be surprised. *They are leaks. And a leak is only tragic until it floods your cellar. Then it is a duty.* He had written it down. He still had the notes. He kept them the way some men kept locks of hair from women they had loved and lost—not because the feeling remained, but because the person he had been when he felt it deserved to be remembered accurately.

Because there had been a time when he believed the hunt was mercy. He wanted to be honest about that, at least with himself, in the privacy of his own skull, which was the last unsearched room in the realm. There had been a time—before the Rookery fires, before the drowned village at Marle, before the list grew long enough to need a second courier—when he had genuinely believed that registration protected the gifted more than it endangered them. That a name in a crown ledger was a wall between a hedge-witch and a mob. That the pyres were regrettable arithmetic, the price of a peace that held.

He no longer told himself that. He told himself something harder and cleaner: *the gifted are dangerous, and dangerous things must be governed*. It required less comfort. Comfort was how Inquisitors went soft, and soft Inquisitors got people killed in larger numbers than cruel ones ever managed.

* * *

They found the dyer on the second day, because Halvard made them search slowly.

Her name was Maren Ostreyer, widow, forty-one, proprietor of the largest vat-yard in the Hollow, and her file was two pages of the most damning ordinariness Halvard had ever catalogued. Registered at nineteen, as the law required, after her mother's death made concealment pointless. No incidents. No flare-ups. Three inspections, passed clean. She paid her annual tithe to the Office of Contained Persons with a punctuality the clerks had annotated approvingly: *pays early.*

She dyed cloth. Her blues were famous two valleys over. And according to the ledger-keeper and four other witnesses, on the night the scribe came through, Maren Ostreyer had fed him supper in her kitchen and put him on Ossa Vane's boat with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and had done it knowing—had to have known—that the bundle was stolen temple property and the scribe was a fleeing criminal.

Halvard sat across from her in her kitchen while his men went through the vat-yard, and studied her the way he'd study a difficult page.

She was not frightened. That was the first thing. She sat with her hands flat on the table—dyer's hands, stained permanently blue at the knuckles, the nails cracked from lye—and looked at him with the weary patience of a woman who had been inspected three times already and knew the choreography. Behind her, copper pots hung in gleaming ranks. The room smelled of oak galls and iron and, faintly, underneath everything, of woad.

"You know why I'm here," Halvard said.

"I'd guess the scribe." Her voice was low and even, a working voice. "Everyone in town knows why you're here. You rode in with twelve men and a banner. Subtlety's never been the Inquisition's dye."

"We're looking for Zephyrine Dravenmoor. You gave them passage."

"I gave a tired traveler supper and a bed, and my cousin's boat happened to be leaving." She said it without heat, almost gently, the way you'd explain rain to a child. "What they carried wasn't mine to open. What they were isn't mine to say."

"They are gifted, Mistress Ostreyer. You know that. You're gifted yourself—you registered nineteen years ago. Which means you also know the law: harboring an unregistered flight is itself a containment violation. Assisting one is conspiracy against the covenant statutes." He laid the writ on the table between them, the wax seal dull red in the lamplight. "You can help me, and this stays an administrative matter. Or you can obstruct me, and it becomes whatever I have to make it."

For the first time, something moved behind her eyes. Not fear. He would spend a long time afterward trying to name it, and the closest he ever came was *pity*—which was worse, somehow, much worse, pity aimed at *him*.

"Inquisitor," she said, "do you know what indigo costs?"

"I beg your pardon?"

"Woad and indigo. The true blues. They don't dissolve in water. They don't dissolve in anything, not really—you have to reduce them, starve the oxygen out of the vat, make the color soluble in a way that isn't natural. And then the cloth goes in yellow. Did you know that? It comes out of the vat yellow, and it turns blue in the air, as it dries. As it breathes." She turned her hands over, showed him the stain ground so deep into her skin it might have been born there. "Thirty years I've worked this craft. My mother taught me. Her mother taught her. And every year I've paid your office its tithe, and every year I've shown your inspectors my vats, and every year I've used maybe a thimbleful of—" she paused, chose the word with care, "—of help, to keep the vats from souring. A thimbleful. To feed my apprentices' wages. To keep this town's one industry alive. That's my whole crime, Inquisitor. A thimbleful, spread over thirty years."

"And if everyone takes a thimbleful," Halvard said quietly, "you get Marle."

The word landed between them like a dropped weight. Marle. The village where a storm-gifted boy had lost control of a sky that didn't want him, and the flood had taken forty-one houses and sixty-three people, and the Inquisition had arrived to find a lake where a town should be. Halvard had walked the mudflats for six days. He had found a christening gown in a willow, forty feet above the old waterline. He did not speak of Marle. He did not need to. Everyone spoke of Marle for him.

Maren Ostreyer's jaw tightened. "Marle was a boy nobody taught. Nobody helped. Your office took his mother's registration fee and never sent anyone back. You people count the gifted like coin and guard us like none, and then you act surprised when one of us breaks." She leaned forward. "You want to prevent the next Marle? Teach us. Don't hunt us."

"The law doesn't permit teaching."

"Then the law is a vat with no vent," she said, "and one day it blows, and it won't be our fault, whatever you write in your report."

It was, Halvard would think later, the most honest conversation he'd had in a decade, and he ended it the way the law required him to end it, and that fact sat in his chest like a swallowed stone for the rest of the day.

"Mistress Ostreyer. You assisted the flight of a fugitive carrying stolen consecrated property. Under statute nine of the Covenant Acts, I'm required to—"

"I know what you're required to do." She stood. Straightened her apron. There were woad stains on it shaped like continents. "Will you let me send for my daughter first? She's at the Latchmere house. She'll want—I'd rather she heard it from me."

Something in Halvard's chest pulled tight, a stitch catching. "How old is your daughter?"

"Twelve."

He should have said no. Procedure said no—the child of a condemned conspirator became a ward of the Office, processed, registered, placed. Every hour of delay was an hour in which evidence could vanish or sympathies could organize. Twelve years of discipline said *no*.

"Yes," he heard himself say. "One hour. Then we do what must be done."

* * *

They did what must be done at dusk, in the vat-yard, because Halvard refused the square. Executions in squares drew crowds, and crowds were tinder, and tinder near a gifted person was how towns burned. The vat-yard was walled, private, industrial. It was, he told himself, a kindness to everyone including her.

Maren Ostreyer declined the hood. She stood beside the post with her hands bound in front of her—blue-stained hands, steady hands—and looked out past the wall toward the mountains, where the evening light was turning the snowfields the exact color of her life's work.

"Inquisitor." Her voice carried in the cold air. "Ask yourself what the temple was so afraid of that they'd steal their own stone to hide it. Ask what's in those mountains that a scribe would run toward instead of away from. You've read the old scrolls. You've seen the gaps in the histories—don't tell me you haven't, I saw your face just now. Somebody bound something, a long time ago, and somebody's been paying to keep it bound, and it isn't us."

"Prepare," Halvard said, to no one, to everyone.

"I'm not asking you to believe me," she said. "I'm asking you to wonder. Wondering was the first thing they took from you, and you let them take it, because wondering hurts." She smiled then, small and terrible. "My mother said the gift skips like a stone across water. Hers touched the weather. Mine touches color. My girl's hasn't shown yet, but it will, and when it does, someone like you will come for her, and she'll run, and I hope to every saint whose bones are dust that she runs faster than I did."

The arquebus squad raised their pieces. Halvard gave the signal because the law was the law and Marle was Marle and sixty-three people were dead in the mud, and a thimbleful was never only a thimbleful, and if he began deciding which laws bent, then he was no better than the smugglers and the harborers and the soft-eyed Provosts who had built the whole rotting edifice he served—

The shots came. The body slumped. The yard went quiet except for the drip of the vats.

And that was when the door of the dye-house banged open, and the child came out.

* * *

She was small for twelve, dark-haired, wearing an apprentice's apron far too big for her, and she walked past the soldiers as though they were furniture. Later, reviewing the moment a hundred times, Halvard would realize that none of them had moved to stop her—not Ivo, not the sergeant-at-arms, not one of the twelve hardened men of the Inquisition's mountain detail. Something about her walk had made stopping her unthinkable, which was itself an answer to a question he hadn't asked yet.

She stopped in front of him. She looked up. Her eyes were her mother's eyes, and they were dry, and that was the worst thing in the whole bruised evening.

"You're the one who decides," she said. Not a question.

"I carry the warrant," Halvard said. His voice came out wrong, gravelled. "Yes."

"She told me about you people. She told me you'd come someday, and she told me what to say when you did, and I promised her I wouldn't say it, because she said hate eats the hater first." The child's voice did not shake. Somewhere behind her, Ivo shifted his weight, and the shift sounded very loud. "But she's dead now, and promises to dead people don't bind, so I'm going to say it."

"Child—"

"My name is Sef. Say it or don't, but I'm not *child* to you, I'm Sef Ostreyer, and here is what I see." She drew a breath, and the air in the vat-yard changed—it thickened, the way air does before summer lightning, and every man present felt the hairs rise on his arms, and not one of them reached for a weapon, because there was no weapon for this. "You think you're a wall between us and the fire. That's what you tell yourself at night, in your little room with your little lists. But walls don't choose which side burns, Inquisitor. Walls just stand there while the house goes up, and call it order."

The words landed with a precision that had nothing to do with a twelve-year-old's vocabulary. Each one found a joint in his armor he had not known was there. *Walls don't choose.* He thought of the Rookery fires. He thought of the list of names, and of one name on it, copied in a hand he'd once admired across a scriptorium desk. He thought of the Provost's voice: *a leak is only tragic until it floods your cellar,* and heard, for the first time in twenty years, how much the sentence sounded like a man refusing to look at the sea.

"You'll follow them into the mountains," the child said. "You'll find what they find. And when you find it, you'll have to choose whether you're a wall or a man, and I curse you with having to make that choice honestly. That's the curse. That's all of it. May you see clearly, Inquisitor Halvard Vess, and may seeing cost you everything it cost my mother, and may you deserve it."

The pressure in the air released, all at once, like a held breath let go. The ordinary dusk came back. The vats dripped. Somewhere beyond the wall, a dog began to bark at nothing.

Sef Ostreyer stepped back from him and waited, chin up, to be seized.

Halvard looked at her for a long moment. Around him, twelve men waited for the order that procedure demanded: take the minor into custody, register, process, place. The words were ready in his mouth. They had been ready for twenty years. *Take her.*

"Let her go to the Latchmere house," he said. "She's a dependent minor with no manifested gift. Note it as discretionary wardship pending review." He was already turning toward his horse. "Ivo, we ride at first light. The trail goes up the Thornfell road, and I want Vane's boat found before the trail goes cold."

"Sir," Ivo said carefully, "the girl—statute says—"

"*Discretionary,* Sergeant." The word came out with an edge that surprised even him. "Write it down."

He did not sleep that night. He sat in the inn's back room with his case files spread around him like a card game played against himself, and he read Maren Ostreyer's two pages again, and then again, and the sentence kept circling: *somebody bound something, a long time ago, and somebody's been paying to keep it bound.*

He had read the old scrolls. He had seen the gaps in the histories—he had catalogued them himself, in his diligent, dutiful way, and filed them under *lacunae, historical, non-actionable*, because that was what a good Inquisitor did with questions that had no permitted answers.

Zephyrine Dravenmoor had run *toward* the Thornfells. Toward the sundered Garden that appeared in no map and every old song. Carrying the covenant stone that the temple had ordered destroyed rather than recovered—which made no sense, unless destruction was the point, unless the stone said something the temple could not afford to have said.

Halvard poured himself the inn's bad wine and did not drink it, and stared at the single candle, and felt the itch begin—not doubt, he told himself, never doubt, only *attention*, only the discipline of looking closely at a document whose grain ran the wrong way.

Outside, far off in the dark, the mountains stood up against the stars like a wall.

He wondered, for the first time in twenty years, what was on the other side of it—and the wondering hurt, exactly as promised, and did not stop.
