## 8. Ossa's Price

The cave Ossa chose for the night was the kind of place Zephyrine would once have walked past without a second glance—a seam in the mountainside above a frozen stream, half-hidden by a curtain of dead ivy, breathing out air that smelled of stone and old smoke and something faintly animal. But Zephyrine had learned, in three days of following her down out of the high pass, that Ossa Vane did nothing without a second glance. She had chosen this hollow the way a moneylender chooses a debtor: for its advantages, its concealment, its history of holding.

She built no fire. Instead she unrolled a felt blanket against the rock, set out hard bread and a wax-wrapped lump of cheese, and began cleaning her knives with the unhurried thoroughness of a woman saying her prayers.

Zephyrine sat across from her with the covenant stone wrapped against their chest, its song muted now to a low hum they felt more than heard, like a hive sleeping under floorboards. Their hands still ached from the pass—from gripping rock, from the cold, from the strange resonance that had poured through them on the heights until they'd wept without deciding to. The visions had left sediment behind. They could still see it when they closed their eyes: kings in bronze, hands closing over light, a temple rising on a foundation no one was permitted to look beneath.

"You eat," Ossa said, not looking up from her blade. "You've been chewing the same bite since the last switchback."

"I'm not hungry."

"Nobody's hungry up here. You eat anyway. Hunger is a liar; it tells you you're fine right up until your legs stop being fine." She flicked the whetstone along the steel, a small bright sound in the dark. "Eat."

Zephyrine ate. The bread tasted of ash and rye and the long gray miles between them and everything they had been. Above the cave mouth, through the ivy, stars were coming out in their hundreds, cold and precise as nails driven into black wood.

They had made good time off the pass—better than Zephyrine's body wanted, worse than their nerves required. Somewhere below and behind, Grell's Hollow lay in the valley like a coin dropped in a well. Somewhere behind that, Halvard. Zephyrine tried not to think of his face by lantern-light, tried not to hear the particular gentleness his voice took on when he was about to do something terrible. *You know I have to write it down, Zeph. That's all this is. Writing things down.*

Ossa finished with the third knife, sheathed it, and looked up.

"Now," she said. "We settle accounts."

* * *

Zephyrine had known, in some chamber of themselves they kept locked even from themselves, that this moment was coming. Smugglers did not carry strangers over the Thornfell passes out of charity. Smugglers carried cargo, and cargo had a price, and Zephyrine had paid theirs with a forged writ and a story about a reliquary delivery—and Ossa had looked at the writ for exactly as long as it takes to read a lie, and then said *done*, which should have been the first warning. No one who read forgery that easily accepted it that cheaply.

"What accounts?" Zephyrine asked. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. Years of practice. Years of being the person whose voice never shook, because shaking invited attention, and attention was death by inches.

"The writ was worth maybe a tenth of what I'm charging the other party." Ossa folded her hands over her knee. She had taken off one boot to warm her foot at her own armpit, an intimacy of discomfort that somehow made what she said next worse. "So let's talk about the other party."

The stone hummed against Zephyrine's sternum. Or perhaps that was their own heart, learning a new rhythm.

"A temple courier came to Grell's Hollow in early autumn," Ossa said. "Before you ever ran. Before the proclamation, even—I want you to understand the order of events, because the order matters. The temple hired me before the crown started hunting anyone. Somebody in Saint Verane's knew the stone was going to move, and knew it before you did."

"That's not possible. I didn't know myself until—"

"No," Ossa agreed. "You didn't. Which is the interesting part, isn't it?" She reached into her coat and produced a folded paper—not the forged writ but another document, creased soft with handling, sealed with a blob of plain brown wax bearing no sigil at all. She did not hand it over. She held it where the starlight through the ivy could find it, and let Zephyrine read upside down.

The hand was elegant. Ecclesiastical. Zephyrine knew that hand. They had spent six years copying beside it in the scriptorium, had watched it move across vellum with the patience of water wearing stone.

*To the bearer: ensure the relic does not reach the Garden. Recover the carrier alive and unharmed if practicable. Deliver both to the Provost's agents at the Hollow. Payment as previously arranged.*

There was no name. There didn't need to be. Zephyrine read the words three times and each time they rearranged themselves into the same sentence: *the temple has known, and the temple has sold you.*

"Alive and unharmed if practicable," Zephyrine repeated. The words tasted like the ink they'd spent their life grinding. "How generous."

"He pays well for generosity," Ossa said. "That's the thing about him people get wrong. They think a man like that is cold. He isn't. He's warm the way a banked fire is warm. He'll keep you comfortable right up until he needs the heat elsewhere." She tucked the letter away. "I've worked for him four years. Never asked what the cargo was. Asking is how smugglers die; knowing is how they die slower and worse."

"And now you're telling me." Zephyrine's hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the altitude. "Why?"

"Because the terms changed." Ossa pulled her boot back on, laced it with two efficient pulls. "The contract says deliver the carrier to the Hollow. The Hollow is ash, or near enough—the crown got there ahead of everyone, and my contact in the valley says there are soldiers on every road out of it wearing the sun-and-chains. So the delivery point is gone, and when a delivery point is gone, a contract is a piece of paper with a dead man's signature." She shrugged. "Also, you crossed my pass. My pass, Zephyrine Dravenmoor. Nobody crosses Thornfell without me knowing, and nobody crosses it carrying what you're carrying without the mountain itself getting loud about it. I felt it from two ridges away. Whatever that stone is, it isn't a reliquary, and I don't take temple pay to walk a war into my own mountains."

"So you're reneging."

"I'm renegotiating." Her smile showed teeth but no warmth. "There's a difference. Reneging is what cowards call it when they're ashamed. Renegotiating is business."

Zephyrine's mind was moving fast now, faster than fear, the way it always did when the trap sprang—the scribe's reflex, the clerk's arithmetic, six years of finding the third option in a ledger built for two. "And the new terms?"

Ossa drew a knife. Not quickly—nothing Ossa did was quick—but the sound of it leaving the sheath was very clear in the cave, and the starlight found the edge and ran along it like water finding a channel.

"Here are the old terms," she said. "I put you in irons tonight, walk you down to whatever's left of the temple's reach, collect my fee, and go back to pretending the world is simple." She turned the knife so it lay flat across her palm, offered hilt-first, an odd, deliberate courtesy. "Here are the new terms. I walk you the rest of the way to wherever that stone is singing toward, I get paid by whoever's still standing when this is over, and in exchange you tell me—one honest answer, freely given—what it is I've been hired to stop."

The silence stretched. The stream below the cave muttered over its stones. Against Zephyrine's chest, the covenant stone had gone quiet—not silent, but listening, the way a room listens when two people stop pretending.

"Why would I tell you anything?"

"Because the old terms are still on the table, and I haven't decided yet." Ossa's voice was level, almost gentle, which from her was more frightening than shouting. "Because you're going to need someone who knows these mountains when the crown comes up behind you, and the crown *is* coming—I saw their fires in the valley at dusk, three columns, moving like they've got a map they shouldn't have. And because—" she paused, and for the first time since Zephyrine had met her, something moved behind her eyes that wasn't arithmetic, "—because I've spent four years not asking questions, and lately the not-asking has started to feel like a debt too. I'd like to know what I owe."

Zephyrine thought of the pass. Of the visions: hands closing over light. A temple raised on a sealed foundation. *Built to keep the secret.* They thought of the Provost's face when he'd ordered the scroll copied, then ordered it burned—kindness and cruelty coming from the same mouth, indistinguishable, because they had always been the same thing.

They thought of Halvard, and of how the worst betrayals wear familiar faces.

And they understood, suddenly, that Ossa was offering them the only currency they had left: truth. Not safety. Truth, traded for passage. It was, they realized with something close to laughter, the first honest transaction of their entire life.

"It's called the covenant stone," Zephyrine said. "And the Garden is real. And everything the temple taught us about magic—that it's corruption, that it must be registered, bound, erased—is a lie built on top of a theft. The gifted aren't sick, Ossa. We're robbed. And I'm taking the robbery back."

Ossa looked at them for a long moment. Then she flipped the knife, caught it by the flat, and slid it back into its sheath.

"Well," she said. "That's worth more than the Provost's fee." She tore the contract in half, then in half again, and fed the pieces to the cold air rather than any fire, letting the wind take them out through the ivy. "Consider our account settled. New terms: I get you to the Garden, you give me a share of whatever's at the end of it, and if the crown catches us, we die with better stories than most."

It was, Zephyrine would think later, almost funny how quickly relief arrived—how the body accepts a new cage if the door of the old one closes. They were still smiling at the absurdity of it when Ossa went rigid.

Her head tilted, the way a deer's does. "Douse yourself," she whispered. "Now."

Zephyrine pressed back against the rock. Below them, far down the valley, where the frozen stream bent through the pines, lights were moving. Not the scattered orange of a camp. Lanterns in formation, climbing. And threading up through the dark behind the lights, faint but unmistakable, came voices singing—the low, disciplined cadence of soldiers on the march, men who sang to keep their feet in rhythm and their hearts from thinking.

"Crown regulars," Ossa breathed. "Off the road. Off *my* road." Her face, in the starlight, had gone very still and very pale. "They're not following the pass. They're cutting straight up. Someone told them exactly where we'd sleep."

Zephyrine's blood went to ice. Only one person knew this cave. Only one person had known the route down from the high pass, the seam in the mountain, the curtain of ivy.

Only one person besides Ossa.

"They can't have followed us," Zephyrine whispered. "Not this fast. Not here."

"Someone did." Ossa was already moving, kicking dirt over the ghost of their trail, sweeping the bread into her pack with one arm. "Question's whether they followed *you* or followed *me*." She looked at Zephyrine then, and the arithmetic was back in her eyes—but pointed, now, in a different direction. "Either way, Dravenmoor, we're about to find out what our new partnership is worth. Because those soldiers aren't stopping at the treeline, and I don't think they're here for the view."

The first lantern cleared the pines below, and its light caught the wet stone of the stream, and Zephyrine—hand pressed flat against the covenant stone through its wrappings—felt it wake against their chest like a second heartbeat, singing now not a song of grief or memory but something sharper, brighter, almost glad.

As if it had been waiting for exactly this.

As if it knew that some covenants are only ever reforged in fire.
