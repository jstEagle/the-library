## 9. Ash on the Wind

The order came at dawn, written in Halvard's own hand because he had insisted on writing it himself. *Let the town be an example.* Four words, and beneath them the seal of the Inquisition, red wax pressed into the shape of a sun with a sword through it. He had carried that seal for eleven years. He had never once thought about how much it weighed until this morning, when he held it over a candle flame to soften the wax for a second impression and watched it begin, very slightly, to weep.

Grell's Hollow sat in the crook of two mountains like a bird in a cupped hand. From the ridge above it, Halvard could see the whole of the town: the slate roofs furred with frost, the smoke rising thin and gray from a hundred chimneys, the river running bright through its middle like a vein of glass. A dyer's yard on the eastern side bled color into the water—madder red, woad blue—so that the river seemed to carry a bruise out of the town and into the valley below.

He had come here three days ago with a list of nine names. He had found eight of them. The ninth was the dyer, Maren Kell, who had not run, though her neighbors had begged her to, though her own feet had carried her twice to the mountain road before turning back. She had refused to run, she told him in the square, because running was a confession, and she had done nothing but mix colors the way her mother had taught her, colors that came out too true, too deep, as if the cloth remembered something the dye had only suggested.

"You're gifted," Halvard had said. It was not a question. It was never a question. That was the first thing they taught you: certainty is a weapon, and hesitation is a wound you give yourself.

"I'm a dyer," she said.

He had taken her statement down anyway, in his neat clerk's hand, and read her the proclamation, and given her until dawn to decide whether she would register or run. She had chosen neither. She had chosen to stand in her doorway with her arms folded and look at him the way you look at weather—as something vast and impersonal and already decided.

Now he stood on the ridge and gave the signal, and the fire arrows went down into the town like a handful of thrown stars.

* * *

There is a way fire behaves when it is given permission. Halvard had learned it young, in his first posting, when a granary full of tainted grain was put to the torch and he had watched the flames move through it with what he could only call appetite—not wild, not chaotic, but purposeful, as if the fire understood itself to be an instrument. His instructors had called this discipline. Fire, properly commanded, does not rage; it *administers*.

So it went in Grell's Hollow. The soldiers moved through the streets in pairs, driving people out ahead of them, and the fires were set in sequence—the dyer's yard first, then the tannery, then the row of houses along the millrace—so that the destruction would read, from a distance, as orderly. A lesson. A sentence being carried out in grammar everyone could understand.

Halvard walked the streets himself. This was policy—he had written it into his own protocols years ago: *the Inquisitor witnesses*. You did not order suffering from a distance and remain clean; you stood in the smoke and let it settle into your coat and your hair, and you carried it with you, because a man who ordered burning and smelled nothing was a man who would soon order anything.

But there was a flaw in his protocol, one he had never noticed until now, which was that witnessing worked both ways. The smoke got into him, yes—but the town got in too. The sound of the slate cracking as roofs heated past their tolerance, a sound like slow applause. The dyestuffs catching, so that the smoke ran blue and crimson and gold, and the sky above Grell's Hollow became, for one obscene hour, the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. He stood in the square with ash sifting down around him like gray snow and hated himself for noticing the beauty, and noticed it anyway.

Maren Kell's house burned last, by design. She was made to watch from the square, flanked by soldiers, while everything her mother had left her went up in colors no ordinary fire could make. She did not cry out. She watched the way she had watched him in her doorway—with that terrible patience—and when the roof beam fell inward and the flames roared up through the stairwell, she closed her eyes as if in prayer, and Halvard, watching her, felt something shift in his chest like a bone setting wrong.

"Where is the child?" he asked.

She opened her eyes. "What child?"

"The child you were seen with. The one who cursed me."

"I don't know what you mean."

He looked at her for a long moment. She looked back with the flat calm of a woman standing in the ruins of her life, and he understood suddenly that she was not protecting herself. There was nothing left to protect. She was protecting someone else, and she would go to the pyre with the name locked behind her teeth, and no instrument the Inquisition owned would open them.

He did not send her to the pyre. Registration, the law said. She had refused. The law said refusal was obstruction, and obstruction was the stake. But standing there in the square, with the ash coming down and the child's curse still ringing somewhere in the back of his skull—*your hands will burn what you love, Inquisitor, and you will call it justice each time*—he found that his pen, for the first time in eleven years, would not write the word he needed it to write.

He sentenced her to labor instead. Confiscation of property, ten years' service in the crown works at Corvane. Her neighbors would say he was soft. Let them say it. He told himself it was strategy—a living example was worth more than a martyr—and the telling almost held.

Almost.

* * *

It was near dusk when he found the cellar.

His men had finished their accounting—structures lost, persons registered, persons detained, persons fled—and Halvard had dismissed them to their billets and gone walking alone through the burned quarter, because he could not sleep and would not lie in a bed smelling of other people's homes. The dyer's yard was still smoking. The vats had cracked in the heat and their contents had boiled away into the ground, and the earth there was stained in great spreading blooms, red and violet and a green so deep it looked like a hole cut in the world. Even ruined, the place was more honest than any church he had served.

The door to the cellar was hidden under a collapsed awning, and he found it only because a thread of blue smoke was rising from beneath the stones—too thin, too steady, to be residual fire. Someone was down there keeping warm.

He drew his knife, not his sword. He told himself this was prudence; a narrow space, a cornered fugitive. Later, he would understand it was something else.

The steps went down into darkness that smelled of wet stone and mordant and, faintly, of woodsmoke that had gotten into wool. At the bottom, behind a rack of empty dye-frames, a candle stub burned on an overturned crate, and beside it, curled small against the wall, was the child.

Not a child, quite. Twelve years old, perhaps thirteen, all elbows and wariness, with dye stains worked so deep into the knuckles they looked like bruises that had healed into jewelry. Maren's face, unmistakably, younger and harder. The child looked up at him and did not scream, did not beg, did not run. Looked at him the way the mother had. Weather.

"You're the one," Halvard said. "You cursed me."

The child's chin came up. "I didn't curse you."

"Three days ago. In the square. You said—" He stopped. He had not told anyone the exact words. He had not wanted to hear them again. But here, in the dark, with the candle between them, they came out of his mouth in the child's own cadence: "*Your hands will burn what you love, and you will call it justice each time.*"

"That's not a curse," the child said. "That's just looking."

The words went into Halvard like a splinter going into the quick. He crouched down, slowly, bringing himself below the child's eye line, an old technique for frightened suspects that he had never once used on someone who wasn't afraid.

"What's your name?"

Silence.

"Maren wouldn't tell me either. She's being sent to Corvane. Ten years' labor. She'll live." He said it carefully, watching the child's face for the flicker that would tell him whether mercy was currency here or just another coin the child had learned to distrust. "If you come out and register, I can't promise you Corvane. I can't promise you anything. The law is the law."

The child studied him. Studied the seal on his chest, the sun with the sword through it, and something moved behind those eyes—something old, older than twelve, older than the temple, older than the crown. Recognition, maybe. Or arithmetic. The same arithmetic Zephyrine Dravenmoor must have done a hundred times in a scriptorium lit by guttering candles: *what am I worth to this person alive, and what am I worth to them dead, and which number do they believe?*

"My mam says you were her friend once," the child said. "Before. She says you used to bring us honeycomb."

Halvard went still.

Maren Kell. He knew the name from the list, from the parish records, from the interrogation. He had not known—had not let himself know—that Maren Kell was Maren *Vale*, who had grown up two lanes from him in Corvane, whose father had repaired his first pair of boots, who had shared stolen honeycomb with him on the wall of the tannery when they were both children sneaking away from bell practice. He had filed her under *Kell, Maren, dyer, gifted, unregistered* and the filing had been complete, and complete filings are a kind of blindness. You write a person into a category and the category closes over them like water.

"She remembers," the child said, reading his silence the way gifted children apparently read everything. "She says the man who brought honeycomb wouldn't have burned the yard."

"The man who brought honeycomb doesn't exist anymore."

"No," the child agreed, with terrible mildness. "But he's sitting right in front of me."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved. Somewhere overhead, a beam settled in the ruins with a groan, and ash sifted down through the broken grate in the ceiling, fine as flour, catching in the candlelight like snow falling indoors.

Halvard looked at the child—at the dye-stained hands, the level gaze, the absolute absence of fear—and felt the machine in him grind against something it could not process. Eleven years of procedure lived in his body. Procedure said: *arrest*. Procedure said: *the child is unregistered, the child is evidence, the child is leverage against the mother*. Procedure had a form for this. He had filled it out a hundred times. His hand knew the motions the way a hangman's hand knows the knot.

And underneath the procedure, quiet as groundwater, the child's voice: *that's just looking.*

"Stand up," he said.

The child stood.

"Walk up the stairs ahead of me. Don't run. If you run, I have to chase you, and I'm tired, and I'm very good at chasing."

The child climbed. Halvard followed with his knife hand loose and empty, which was itself a kind of heresy—he could not remember the last time he had climbed stairs behind a suspect without a grip on their collar.

At the top, the burned quarter lay open under the first stars. Smoke still rose from the dyer's yard in a gray column, bending east. The mountain road went north from the edge of town, up toward the pass, dark and indifferent. The road back to the billets went south, toward his men, toward the forms, toward the machine that had made him and would unmake him if it learned what he was considering.

The child stood in the rubble and waited. Not hopefully. Waited the way stone waits.

"There's a shepherd's hut," Halvard heard himself say, "two miles up the north road. Old Tamsin's, before the switchbacks. It has a roof. There's a family of stonemasons who graze sheep up there in summer; they won't be back till thaw. No one will look for you there, because no one will know to look." He was speaking quickly now, quietly, the way men speak when they are stepping off a cliff and want to get the falling part over with. "Your mother will be sent to Corvane within the week. The convoy stops at the Waymeet inn the first night. It's badly guarded. The guards drink. I will not be able to tell you any of this, because I will not see you again, and if anyone ever asks, I arrested a child who escaped custody, and I filed a report saying so, and the report will be thorough and full of my own incompetence."

The child looked at him for a long time.

"That's a curse," the child said at last. "What you're doing. Did you know? You're cursing yourself. All those lies in your mouth, all those forms with your name on them. Your hands will burn what you love, Inquisitor. They already have." A pause. "But maybe you're learning to burn something else instead."

Then the child turned and went, up through the rubble and onto the north road, small and quick and gone, and Halvard stood alone in the ashes of Grell's Hollow with the seal heavy on his chest and the first open disobedience of his eleven years dissolving behind him like footprints in snow.

* * *

He wrote the report that night by lamplight, in his neat clerk's hand, and it was thorough, and it was full of his own invented incompetence, and every word of it was a stone laid in the road of his own ruin. *Subject escaped custody during search of premises. Pursuit attempted. Terrain unfavorable. Subject not recovered.*

When it was done he sat for a long time with the pen uncapped, listening to the wind come down off the Thornfells carrying, faintly, the smell of the high passes—cold stone and pine and, beneath it, something else, something older, like the memory of a garden.

Zephyrine Dravenmoor was up there somewhere. In the mountains, with the stolen stone, heading for a place the temple had spent four centuries pretending did not exist. Halvard had known Zephyrine since they were both novices—had shared bread and doubt and long arguments about the nature of grace—and he had put that name on his list with a steady hand, because certainty is a weapon and hesitation is a wound.

But the child's voice would not leave him. *That's just looking.*

He dipped the pen. Below the report, in the margin where no clerk's discipline would ever require him to write, he added a single line, small enough that only a careful reader would find it:

*The hollow burned clean. Nothing of value was lost.*

He looked at the lie for a long moment. Then he blotted it, sealed the report, and understood, with the clarity that comes only after the point of no return, that he had just begun to keep two ledgers—and that sooner or later, every double ledger comes due.

Far to the north, past the switchbacks, past the pass, the mountains went on rising into the dark, and somewhere among them a sundered Garden waited, and somewhere on the road toward it, a thief with a scribe's hands was carrying a stone that sang.

Halvard snuffed the lamp and sat in the dark, and did not sleep.
