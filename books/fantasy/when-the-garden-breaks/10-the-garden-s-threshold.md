## 10. The Garden's Threshold

The last mile of the world was a staircase of roots.

Zephyrine climbed it with the covenant stone strapped against their chest in Ossa's smuggler's rig—canvas and waxed cord, the kind of harness built for kegs of brandy or bundles of untaxed silk—and felt every step through their ribs. The stone had grown heavier as they climbed. Not its weight, exactly; Zephyrine had carried enough sacks of tithe-grain up temple stairs to know weight, and this was something else. It was the heaviness of a thing becoming more itself. Each switchback of the Thornfell high pass they'd gained, each hour closer to whatever waited at the top, the stone had thickened like a voice gathering breath.

Ossa Vane went ahead of them, one hand on the root-stair, the other never far from the knife at her hip. She had not spoken much since the ambush at the ford—since the moment her contract and Zephyrine's survival had collided and then, improbably, aligned—but she spoke now, without turning around.

"Smell that?"

Zephyrine breathed. The mountain air had been thin and mineral for days, all granite and snowmelt and pine pitch. But beneath it now ran something sweeter. Green things. Growing things. Honey, almost—or the memory of honey, the way a dead woman's house still smells faintly of her soap.

"Flowers," Zephyrine said. "Up here? Above the treeline?"

"Nothing grows above the treeline." Ossa's voice was flat, but her hand had left the knife and pressed flat against the rock instead, palm-down, the way a person steadies themselves against a ship's deck. "That's what I've been telling myself for the last hour. Nothing grows here. And yet."

They came over the shoulder of the pass as the sun broke the eastern ridge, and the light fell into a valley that should not have existed.

* * *

Zephyrine had spent eleven years copying descriptions of places they would never see: coastlines rendered in careful merchant's ink, mountain ranges sketched from travelers' testimony, the fabled terraces of the southern kingdoms drawn secondhand and annotated *per report of Brother Anselm, who claims to have counted the steps*. They knew how the world looked on paper. They knew, better than anyone in the temple of Saint Verane, how much paper lied by omission.

No paper had ever mentioned this valley.

It was vast—a bowl of land perhaps three miles across, walled by peaks that leaned inward like elders bending over a cradle. And it was green. Not the green of alpine meadow, sparse and wind-bitten, but a deep, deliberate green, the green of gardens, of orchards, of intention. Terraces descended the inner slopes in great concentric rings, and though most had crumbled into grass-grown ruin, the bones of them remained: stairways of white stone, archways whose keystones had fallen but whose pillars still stood, rows of trees that might once have been pruned and were now merely ancient. A river ran through the center of it, silver-bright, and where it pooled the water glowed.

That was the part no ink could hold. The glow.

Zephyrine stopped at the head of the root-stair and stared, and the gift—the wild, unsanctioned, registerable, hangable-for magic that had lived coiled and hidden in their blood since childhood—rose up inside them like a dog hearing its name called from a distant house.

Because the air itself was full of it. Visible. Shimmering. What the temple's doctrine called *residue*, what Inquisitors swept for with censers and cold-iron needles, what Zephyrine had spent a lifetime smothering under discipline and tallow-smoke and smallness—that same substance lay over the whole valley in drifting, luminous sheets, pooling in the hollows like morning fog, rising off the river in slow spirals, catching the sunrise so that the entire Garden seemed lit from within and without at once. Where it gathered thickest, flowers bloomed out of season on the ruined terraces; where it thinned, the old white stone showed bare and bleached, like skin through torn cloth.

"It's leaking," Zephyrine whispered. "All this time, it's been—"

"Leaking," Ossa agreed. She had gone very still. "I've run this pass eleven times, scribe. Eleven. I've seen the valley from the ridgeline in fog, in rain, in snow. Never once saw it shine." Her eyes cut sideways. "Never once smelled flowers. Whatever you're carrying in that canvas, it knows it's home."

The stone pulsed against Zephyrine's sternum. Once. Twice. Like a heartbeat remembering its rhythm.

And then it began to sing.

Not aloud—not entirely. The sound came partly through the ears, a high, clear tone like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, but mostly it arrived some other way, through the bones, through the teeth, through the place behind the breastbone where Zephyrine had always kept their fear. It was a chord. It was many chords. It was, Zephyrine realized with a vertigo that buckled their knees slightly, the sound of the visions from the high pass—the covenant's making, the kings' seizing, the binding—all of it compressed into music, all of it *saying* something, and the saying had just noticed them listening.

"Scribe." Ossa had backed up two steps. Her face, usually arranged in the pleasant blankness of commerce, had come apart at the seams. "Scribe, your bag is glowing."

"I know."

"It's singing."

"I know."

"People don't pay me enough for singing bags." But she didn't leave. That was the thing about Ossa Vane that Zephyrine was only beginning to understand: she complained about every price, and then she paid it. "Well? Are we going down or are we going to stand here until the mountain notices us?"

Zephyrine adjusted the harness straps, took a breath that tasted of honey and old stone, and started down into the Garden.

* * *

The descent took the rest of the morning, and with every terrace they crossed, the air grew thicker with the shining stuff, until walking through it felt like wading upstream. It moved around them. Zephyrine tried not to think about that—tried not to think about how it parted for Ossa like water around a stone, and how it did not part for them at all, how it drifted toward them in slow filaments, threading through their hair, sinking into their sleeves, finding the hidden coil of their gift and pressing against it the way warmth presses against a window in winter.

Their gift answered. Of course it answered. Heat rose along Zephyrine's arms, that familiar dangerous bloom, and for the first time in their life they did not smother it. There was no one here to see. There was nothing here to hide from. The realization arrived slowly, like dawn, and when it fully landed Zephyrine had to stop on a broken stairway and press a hand over their mouth, because eleven years of clenching down, of flinching from candle-flames, of making themselves small enough to be overlooked—it had all been practice for a war that ended before they were born, and here, in the place where the magic had been bound, there was simply nowhere to put the hiding anymore.

"You're crying," Ossa observed, from several paces off. She said it the way she'd say *you're bleeding*—diagnostic, unsentimental, already calculating whether it would slow them down.

"The air," Zephyrine managed. "It's—everything I ever hid. It's all still here. It remembers."

"Mm." Ossa looked around at the ruined terraces, the glowing pools, the trees heavy with blossom in defiance of the altitude. "Place like this, I'd want paying by the hour just to look at it. Which way to the middle?"

Zephyrine didn't need a map. The stone was pulling now, gently but unmistakably, a fish on a line, and they followed it down through ring after ring of garden—past a fountain basin cracked down the middle and full of luminous water, past a colonnade where every pillar bore carved figures whose faces had been deliberately chiseled away, past an orchard of trees so old their trunks had fused into braided walls. The chiseled faces troubled them. Someone had wanted these people forgotten. Someone had come here with hammers and doctrine and done work.

Near the heart of the valley, the ruins gave way to something intact.

It was a sanctuary—there was no other word. A hall open to the sky, its roof long fallen, its walls standing to twice a man's height, built of the same white stone as the terraces but worked with a care that made everything else look rough-hewn. The floor was a single sweep of pale marble, uncracked after however many centuries, and inlaid across it in metal darkened almost to black was a pattern Zephyrine knew intimately: the same diagram, line for line, that was etched into the covenant stone sleeping against their chest. A map of a binding. Drawn large enough to stand inside.

At the center of the pattern, the air itself was dense with light—a column of the shining substance rising from the marble and climbing toward the empty sky, slow as smoke, patient as prayer.

The stone sang louder. Zephyrine's hands shook as they unbuckled the harness.

"What are you doing?" Ossa's voice had gone sharp.

"What I came to do."

"You don't even know what you came to *do*. You've got a stolen rock and half a scroll you can't read and a plan I've heard you describe as 'walk north and hope.'" She caught Zephyrine's arm. Her grip was strong, calloused, honest. "Scribe. Zephyrine. Whatever this place wants from you—and places like this always want something—you don't walk into the middle of a glowing circle and hand over the merchandise before you've negotiated terms. That's rule one. Rule one of everything."

Zephyrine looked at her. Something in their chest was breaking open, and it hurt, and it was also the first clean pain they'd felt in years.

"Ossa. I've been negotiating my whole life. With the temple. With the crown. With every room I've ever walked into—I walked in already knowing what I'd have to be in it to survive." They touched the harness buckle. "I'm tired of being the negotiator. I want, just once, to find out what happens if I'm true."

"That's not a plan. That's a eulogy."

"Maybe." Zephyrine smiled, and felt the smile crack, and let it. "But it's mine."

They stepped into the circle.

* * *

The light took them gently.

That was the worst part, afterward—how gentle it was. No violence, no seizure, none of the punitive fire the temple's texts promised for the unbound gifted. The column of radiance simply opened around Zephyrine like a hand closing, and the world changed texture. Sound deepened. Color sharpened until the ruined sanctuary blazed. And the stone, freed from its canvas, rose from Zephyrine's hands—actually rose, hanging in the air at the height of the heart, turning slowly, singing now in full voice, a chord that filled the valley and came back from the surrounding peaks in echoes that harmonized with it, as if the mountains themselves had been waiting centuries to sing counterpoint.

Zephyrine stood in the light and felt every hidden thing come unlocked.

It was not painful. It was worse than painful. It was *exposure*. Every suppression, every swallowed flare, every night they'd lain awake cataloguing their own strangeness and burying it deeper—each one surfaced now, and the light received it, and held it up, and *looked at it*. Zephyrine felt seen the way a ledger is seen when the auditor arrives: completely, impartially, line by line. There was nowhere in themselves to stand that was not visible. The performed self—the useful, unremarkable, forgettable scribe, the one who had survived eleven years by being furniture—peeled away like wet paper, and underneath it there was not, as Zephyrine had always privately feared, nothing. Underneath it there was something enormous and frightened and bright, and it had been waiting its whole life for permission.

Then the Garden spoke.

It did not speak in words, exactly. It spoke the way the stone sang—in meaning that arrived pre-translated, bypassing the ear and landing directly in understanding. The voice, if voice it was, seemed to come from everywhere: from the marble, from the column of light, from the faceless statues in the colonnade, from the blossoming trees. It was neither male nor female, neither old nor young. It sounded like a library. It sounded like weather.

***Who comes to the sundered Garden?***

Zephyrine's mouth was dry. They found their voice anyway. "Zephyrine Dravenmoor. Scribe of the temple of Saint Verane. Formerly." The title tasted like ash; they discarded it. "Thief, I suppose. Fugitive. Carrier of what your stone remembers."

***Why have you come?***

"To reforge the covenant." The words came easier than expected—they had been rehearsing them across half a kingdom without knowing it. "The binding is failing. The magic is leaking, the crown is hunting the gifted, the temple is lying about everything. I brought the stone back because someone has to mend what was broken, and apparently that someone is me, which frankly seems like poor planning on everyone's part, but here I am."

Silence. The kind of silence that weighs.

Then, softly, terribly:

***The last who stood where you stand offered their whole self, and the covenant was made, and the covenant was taken. The kings came after, and bound what was free, and built their temples on the theft, and you carry their stone and speak of mending.*** The light around Zephyrine tightened, not cruelly, but with the pressure of attention absolute. ***Mending requires surety, little scribe. The first covenant cost a self entire. What will you give?***

"I—" Zephyrine's prepared answers, all of them, evaporated. *Whatever it takes.* *My life if needed.* The noble phrases they'd composed on the road, the ones that had made the terror bearable by giving it a script. None of them would come. Because the Garden was looking at them, truly looking, and it would know a performance, and Zephyrine understood suddenly, with total clarity, that they could not bargain here. Not with the mask on. The mask was already gone.

***What will you become?*** the Garden asked again, and the question was not the same question. ***Not what will you pay. Payment is a merchant's word. What will you BE, when the covenant takes hold and cannot be undone? Answer not with what you will lose. Answer with what you are willing to be seen as. For that is the price. To be seen. Entirely. By the stone, by the realm, by yourself—forever.***

Zephyrine stood in the light with their hands empty and their name in their mouth, and shook.

This was the thing. This had always been the thing. Not death—death was easy, death was a single payment, death was what martyrs got and martyrs got to be remembered bravely. This was harder. This was the annihilation of the hiding-place. To reforge the covenant was to be *known*—to have the gift named openly in their body, in front of Halvard, in front of the realm, in front of every future morning—to walk out of this valley visible, marked, legible, never again able to be the unremarkable scribe in the corner, never again safe in the blessed gray anonymity that had been the only home they'd ever had.

Terror rose in them like floodwater. Their knees wanted the marble. Their lungs wanted to be small.

And beneath the terror, quiet as a seed under snow, another feeling: a fierce, furious, unfamiliar *want*. To be seen. To stop paying the endless installments of smallness. To sign their own name.

"I don't know yet," Zephyrine said at last, and their voice cracked, and they let it crack. "What I'll become. I don't know. I've spent my whole life being what other rooms required. If I answer you now with a promise, it'll be a performance, and you'll know it, and I'll know it, and the covenant will fail the way it failed before—built on a self somebody was pretending to be."

The light stilled. Listening.

"But I know what I'm *willing* to try to become," Zephyrine went on, and the shaking slowed, and something steadied in them, scribe-steady, the steadiness of a hand about to set ink to a page that matters. "So ask me properly. Show me what the covenant was—what it really was, before the kings, before the theft, before the temple turned your mending into their vault. Show me the truth of it, all of it, and let me answer with my eyes open. That's the only surety I have that's worth anything. Not my death. My *attention*."

For a long moment the Garden said nothing, and the stone hung silent in the air, and Zephyrine wondered if they had failed some final test by refusing to fail gracefully.

Then the column of light bent downward, and spread, and became—for that was the only word for it—a doorway. And through it, rising like images surfacing in dark water, came the memory of the world when the world was young and the magic was free.

***Watch,*** said the Garden. ***Then answer.***

The first image resolved: a circle of people, thousands of years ago, standing hand in hand on this very marble, and none of them hiding anything at all.
