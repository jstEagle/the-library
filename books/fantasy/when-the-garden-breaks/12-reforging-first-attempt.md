## 12. Reforging, First Attempt

The Garden did not sleep, and so neither did Zephyrine.

They had tried. After the guardian's question—*what are you willing to become?*—had hung in the ruined air like a blade waiting to fall, Zephyrine had walked back along the broken colonnade to the chamber Ossa had claimed for her fever-sleep, and they had lain down on cold marble, and they had closed their eyes. Sleep had come for perhaps the length of a held breath and then departed, taking its warmth with it. What remained was the singing.

The covenant stone sang from its cradle of roots at the heart of the sanctuary, a sound that was not entirely sound—more a pressure behind the ears, a sweetness on the tongue, the memory of a chord struck somewhere in the body's oldest rooms. It had been singing since they crossed the threshold. It sang now in the dark, patient as water wearing down stone, and Zephyrine lay beneath it and understood that it was not singing *to* them. It was singing *through* them, testing them, the way a bellringer tests a bell before trusting it with the hour.

Ossa slept badly three paces away, her bandaged side rising and falling in shallow tides. The wound Cailean's men had given her had gone hot; even in the dark Zephyrine could feel the fever coming off her skin like heat off a forge-stone. She had refused to be left behind at Grell's Hollow, refused everything except the chance to keep moving, and now she paid for that stubbornness in sweat. Twice in the night she murmured names Zephyrine did not know—a brother, maybe, or a debt—and once she said, very clearly, "Don't sign anything," and laughed at some joke the fever told her, and Zephyrine had sat up in the dark with their heart hammering, because they had signed things. They had been signing things their whole life. Tithes. Confessions of small errors. The ledger lines of a life measured out in ink and obedience.

*What are you willing to become.*

The guardian's voice had not been cruel. That was the worst of it. It had asked the way an honest scale asks—not accusing, only weighing. And Zephyrine had stood before it with the stolen scroll against their chest and the stone's song in their teeth and answered, after too long a silence, "Whatever is required."

Even now, lying sleepless under the root-vaulted dark, they could feel how the answer had landed: like a coin dropped into a dry well. Not rejected. Just—unheard. A nothing-coin. Because whatever is required was not an answer. It was what they had said to the Provost when he ordered them to copy the sealed scroll. It was what they had said to every superior, every rule, every candle guttering in the scriptorium that they had steadied with a thought while their heart screamed *don't see me, don't see me.* Whatever is required was the whole architecture of their survival, and the guardian had looked at it the way one looks at a mask nailed over a face, and waited.

Zephyrine rose before the false dawn, when the Garden's light came grey-green through the shattered roof, filtered through leaves that grew where glass should have been. They dressed in the dark, took up the scroll case, and left a note beside Ossa's bed in their careful scribe's hand: *Gone to the stone. If I don't return, the map in my pack shows the deep ways south. Take it. Take care. —Z.*

Then, because honesty was apparently going to be the theme of whatever happened next, they crossed out the *Z* and wrote *Zephyrine Dravenmoor*, full and unhidden, and felt the letters burn slightly, like frost on skin.

* * *

The heart of the Garden opened around them as they walked, and each time it was new.

That was the thing no scroll had captured—the Provost's sealed covenant, for all its careful archaic language, read like a description of a house by someone who had only ever seen the chimney smoke. The scrolls said the Garden was a place where magic was bound. They did not say it was alive. They did not say the air itself was thick with the gift, visible in slow drifting motes like pollen, like snow that fell upward, gold and green and occasionally a color Zephyrine had no name for and suspected the realm's languages had lost. They did not say that walking through it felt like wading into a river that remembered every swimmer who had ever crossed it.

The motes gathered toward Zephyrine as they walked. They always had, since childhood—that was the secret, that was the sin, that was the flare the candle had seen—but here it was not a flicker to be smothered. Here the light came to them openly, settling on their shoulders and hair like a flock of tame birds, and Zephyrine walked crowned in it and wept without quite deciding to, because they had spent thirty years being unremarkable, useful, overlooked, and all that time this had been waiting to love them.

At the center, the stone.

It sat in a cradle of living root, half-buried, the size of a kneeling child, and it was not beautiful in any way the temple would have approved. It was scarred. Its surface was a map of old violence—chisel marks, binding-runes cut deep and then, in places, gouged at as if someone had once tried to claw them out. Light moved beneath its skin like blood under bruised flesh. And the song, here at the source, was almost unbearable: not melody but *accounting*, Zephyrine realized. A ledger. Every gifted soul in the realm, entered line by line, their magic drawn up through the stone's deep roots and held, and held, and held, the way the temple held tithes. Held for the crown. Held since the first kings.

Zephyrine knelt in the moss before it and unrolled the stolen scroll across their knees.

They had copied it once, under the Provost's eye, in the scriptorium's yellow light—had felt then the vertigo of realizing their masters had known about this place all along, had built their power on its silence. Now they read it again in the stone's own presence, and the words seemed ashamed of themselves. *Let the bearer come alone. Let the bearer offer the self entire. What is bound may be bound anew; what is offered wholly may be remade wholly.* The old formulae marched down the page in their severe hand, and at the bottom, in a different ink, a later annotator had written: *Surety required. No substitution. No partial payment. The Garden does not accept collateral.*

"No partial payment," Zephyrine said aloud, and their voice sounded very small under the vault of roots.

They knew what it meant. They had known since the guardian asked its question, if they were honest, which was precisely the problem. The covenant could not be reforged by a scribe reciting formulae. It demanded surety—a whole self, staked like a coin on a table. And Zephyrine Dravenmoor, who had survived by division, who kept a performed self for the temple and a hidden self for the candlelight and a third self, nameless, for the worst hours of the night—that person had no whole self to stake. They had fragments. They had masks. They had a lifetime of *whatever is required.*

But the soldiers were coming. Brother Cailean was coming, with his sincere eyes and his doctrine of chains, and Ossa was bleeding fever in the dark, and Halvard—Halvard was somewhere on the mountain with his list and his fire, and there was no more time to become ready. There was only now.

"Very well," Zephyrine whispered, and laid both hands on the stone.

* * *

The song stopped.

The silence that followed was worse than any sound—the whole Garden holding its breath, the motes of light hanging motionless in the air like a congregation frozen mid-prayer. Then the stone warmed under Zephyrine's palms, and the warmth became a pulse, and the pulse became a voice that did not travel through the ears at all but arrived directly in the marrow:

***Bearer. You come to reforge.***

"I come to reforge." Their voice shook. They made it steady. Steadiness was a skill; they had practiced it for decades. "I've read the rite. I know the terms."

***You know the words.*** Something moved in the stone's depths, vast and slow, an eye opening behind the scars. ***Show me your surety.***

And Zephyrine, because there was nothing else to do, because the plan had always ended here, reached inward and gathered up everything they were willing to give, and offered it.

They offered the scribe. The careful hands, the flawless copies, the ledger of small debts paid down in ink across nineteen years. They offered the survivor—the one who had learned to make itself small, to stand in doorways at the angle least likely to be noticed, to laugh at the right moments in the refectory and never first. They offered the thief, too, the new self forged on the smugglers' roads, the one who had stolen a stone and outrun an Inquisitor. They offered their grief for their family, their fury at the temple, their tenderness for Ossa, their tangled history with Halvard—all of it, laid out on the scale like coins counted carefully from a purse.

For a moment, it almost worked. The stone drank the offering greedily; light climbed Zephyrine's arms; the old runes began to glow; somewhere far above, the ruined roof let down a shaft of true dawn. The covenant was reaching for them. Zephyrine felt it take hold of the edges of their soul and begin, gently, terribly, to pull—

—and then it stopped. Everything stopped. The light went out of the runes like breath blown from a candle.

***This is not a self,*** the guardian said. Not angry. Never angry. That was the horror of it. ***This is a costume with pockets.***

"What?" The word tore out of them. "What do you mean? That's—I gave you everything. Everything I *am*—"

***You gave me what you show.*** The stone's voice moved through them like a hand sorting grain, separating wheat from chaff, and Zephyrine felt each piece of themselves lifted, examined, set aside. ***The obedient scribe: a shape you built so the temple would not look closely. The clever thief: a shape you built so the roads would let you pass. Even your grief—you carry it folded, presented, arranged to be bearable to witnesses. These are performances. Well-made. I do not scorn them—they kept you alive, and life is not a small thing. But I cannot found a covenant on a performance.*** A pause, and then, with terrible gentleness: ***A mask cannot swear an oath. Only a face can.***

"I don't—" Zephyrine's hands were still on the stone and they could not make themselves lift them. "I don't have a face. Underneath. I've been performing so long I don't know what's underneath. That's not a choice I made, that's what surviving *was*. You want a whole self? Ask the people who were allowed to have one. Ask anyone who wasn't born wrong in a kingdom that burns the wrong."

The silence stretched. The motes of light drifted again, slowly resuming their fall-that-was-not-fall, and Zephyrine knelt in the heart of the Garden with their heart slamming and their palms burning and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that they had just done the one thing they had promised themselves they would never do here: they had bargained. They had come to the altar with excuses in their mouth instead of themselves.

***Then let us find what is underneath,*** the guardian said. ***Not because you owe me. Because you owe yourself, and you have been defaulting on that debt for thirty years.***

The stone flared white.

* * *

Later—hours, minutes, Zephyrine would never be able to say—they were on their back in the moss, and the sky through the broken roof was the deep blue of late morning, and every part of them hurt in a way that was not injury. It was exposure. It was the feeling of a wound cleaned.

The guardian had not tortured them. It had simply *looked*, the way the mountain had looked on the high pass, the way the candle had looked that night in the scriptorium—and Zephyrine had been unable to perform, because the guardian's gaze went to the layer beneath the performance and the layer beneath that, and at the bottom of all the layers it had found something small and terrified and real: a child crouched behind a tithe-barrel, watching soldiers drag their aunt away, learning in one afternoon that the price of being seen was fire, and deciding—choosing, with a child's terrible seriousness—to never be seen again.

That was the foundation. That was the face under the masks. Not the scribe, not the thief, not the grief arranged for witnesses. A frightened child who had made a vow, and kept it so long they had mistaken the vow for a soul.

"You could have just told me," Zephyrine rasped at the stone. Their voice was wrecked. "You could have said, *go home, learn who you are, come back in ten years.* Instead you nearly—"

***I did not nearly kill you,*** the guardian said. ***You nearly killed you. You offered a mask as surety, and the covenant began to collect on it, and a mask has nothing behind it, so the collection went deeper, looking for payment, and you fought it. Of course you fought it. Fighting is what you know.*** The stone dimmed, thoughtful. ***Another would have died. You did not, because at the last moment—do you remember what you said?***

Zephyrine remembered. At the very bottom of the pulling, when the covenant's grip had been around the child-behind-the-barrel and Zephyrine had felt themselves about to be spent like a counterfeit coin, they had screamed—not a prayer, not a formula, but the truth: *"I'm not finished! I haven't decided who I am yet!"*

And the covenant had stopped. Because that, at least, had been honest.

***Undecided is not nothing,*** the guardian said. ***Undecided is a door still open. I can wait at an open door. I cannot wait at a wall painted to look like one.***

Zephyrine lay in the moss and laughed, or sobbed, or something between—the sound came out cracked and ugly and real, and they did not arrange it for any witness, because the only witness was a rock that had already seen everything. "So that's it. I failed. The great reforging, attempted by the last scribe of Saint Verane, and I couldn't even pay my own toll."

***You failed at the rite,*** the guardian agreed. ***You succeeded at something harder. You told the truth while dying. Most souls manage it only once, and usually by accident.*** The light beneath the stone's scars pulsed, slow, like a heartbeat deciding to continue. ***The covenant remains unreforged. The binding holds. Your enemies hold also—and they are closer than you think, little undecided one. The zealot's fires are two valleys east. And the one who hunts you has turned off the road you expected him to take.***

Zephyrine sat up too fast. The Garden tilted. "Halvard? Which way—"

***East,*** said the guardian, ***and he is not alone. And he is carrying something that will change what you believe about the night your family burned.***

The words landed like ice down the spine. Zephyrine scrambled for the scroll, for their pack, for the note they'd left Ossa—*take care, Zephyrine Dravenmoor*—and paused, one knee in the moss, looking back at the scarred stone that had refused them and spared them in the same breath.

"I'll come back," they said. And then, because masks were heavy and they were tired of carrying this one up the mountain: "I don't know yet who'll be coming back. But someone will. Someone true."

The stone sang one low note, and for the first time, it sounded almost like laughter.

Zephyrine ran for the colonnade, and for Ossa, and for the east—where an old friend carried a truth that would burn hotter than any pyre.
