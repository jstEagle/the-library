## 13. What Halvard Carries

The road to Vell's Crossing ran white with frost, and Halvard rode it like a man riding toward his own trial.

He had told himself, for three days now, that the summons waiting for him in the low country was routine. A review of expenditures. A question of jurisdiction—the temple's writ against the Inquisition's, an old friction, nothing more. He had recited these comforts to himself the way a man counts coins he knows are short. But he had been an Inquisitor too long not to recognize the shape of a noose when it was being braided around him, and the letter that had reached him at Grell's Hollow's ashes had been written in the careful, bloodless hand of the Provost's own chancery.

*Inquisitor Halvard will present himself at the chapter-house of Saint Verane to account for the disposition of certain temple properties.*

*Certain temple properties.* As if the covenant stone were a censer gone missing. As if Zephyrine Dravenmoor were a set of silver spoons.

He did not go to the chapter-house. That was the first disobedience of his life, and it sat in him strangely—not like guilt, which he knew well, worn smooth as a river stone from long handling, but like a splinter working deeper with every mile. Instead he turned north, toward Vell's Crossing, where the mountain roads came down to meet the king's highway, because an Inquisitor who could no longer trust his own chancery learned quickly to trust the roads instead. Everything the temple moved, it moved through Vell's Crossing. He had audited enough ledgers to know that much.

The frost thickened as he climbed. The hedges along the way wore it like ermine, and the air had the clean, empty smell of a world holding its breath. His horse's breath plumed ahead of them both. Behind him, somewhere in the white country, his own shadow had begun to move: Brother Ferren, whom he'd trained, dispatched with two wardens "to assist" him—assist, as though assistance ever traveled three days behind a man without word sent ahead.

He thought about the dyer's child often now. More than was useful. The cellar in Grell's Hollow, the dark smelling of lye and wet wool, and the small face looking up at him out of the lamplight with an expression he had never been trained to answer: not fear, exactly. *Assessment.* The child had looked at him the way one looks at a large animal whose temper is unknown, and had said nothing at all, and he had stood there with irons in his hand feeling the weight of them for the first time in nineteen years of service.

*You burn what you are sent to burn,* he had told himself on the ride down from the Hollow. *That is the office. The office does not ask whether the fire is just; it asks whether the fire is obedient.*

And then, quietly, in a voice he did not recognize as his own: *And who taught you that obedience was the same as faith?*

He found the envoy at dusk, camped in the lee of a shrine to some roadside saint whose face the weather had eaten away. Three riders, a mule, and a strongbox chained to the mule's back—and Halvard knew the lead rider by his boots before he knew him by his face. Temple couriers wore soft-soled boots so their footsteps would not carry in cloistered halls, and this habit, absurdly, they kept even on the open road, so that they stepped through frozen mud like men walking on eggs.

Brother Anselm. He had signed receipts under Halvard's audits a dozen times. A mild man, ink-stained, forgettable by profession.

Halvard did not announce himself. He sat his horse at the edge of the firelight for a long moment, watching them, and saw the moment recognition broke over Anselm's face like ice cracking—a courier's instinct, the flinch of a man who has carried things he should not have carried and knows that someday the carrying would be noticed.

"Inquisitor." Anselm rose, bowing with the precise depth required of his order to the crown's office—deep enough to honor, shallow enough to remind them both that the temple bowed to God first. "We did not expect—"

"No," Halvard said. "You didn't."

He dismounted slowly, letting them watch him do it, letting them see that his hands were steady. Fear was a tool like any other. He had used it on a hundred frightened people across a hundred years of other men's sins, and it shamed him now how easily it came to hand, how natural the instrument felt, as though his whole life had been practice for making people afraid.

"Sit," he said. "All of you. I have questions, and you will find the evening goes better for everyone if the answers arrive faster than the cold."

They sat. The younger couriers kept their eyes on the fire. Anselm kept his eyes on Halvard, which was wiser and worse.

"You're carrying something north," Halvard said. "Not the stone—that's gone, we all know it's gone—but instructions. Orders. Perhaps money. The Provost doesn't send three men and a mule into the mountains in winter for tithes."

"We carry only correspondence between—"

"Anselm." He said the name gently, and watched it land harder than a blow would have. "I have read your hand for eleven years. You dot your i's like a man stabbing something small. You are carrying orders concerning the covenant stone, and I am going to hear them tonight, and the only variable left in your evening is whether I hear them from you or from the inside of your mind."

That last was a lie. He had never taken a mind by force; the Inquisition's arts of unbinding were reserved for the gifted, and Halvard had always counted himself among those who merely watched. But Anselm did not know that. No one knew what an Inquisitor could or could not do anymore, least of all the Inquisitors themselves—that had always been part of the design.

The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope a branch gave under snow with a sound like a distant door closing.

"The Provost ordered the scroll destroyed," Anselm said at last, very quietly. "The covenant scroll. Before the Inquisition arrived to audit us. And after the scribe fled with the stone—" He stopped. Started again, in the voice of a man reading from a ledger he hates. "After the theft, the Provost ordered that any copy, any fragment, any record of the original covenant be burned, and that the burning be witnessed and sworn to. We were sent to collect the sworn witnesses' affidavits and deliver them to the chapter-house at Vell's Crossing, where they will be held under seal until—"

"Until when?"

Anselm's mouth worked. The younger courier stared into the fire as though it might offer him a different script.

"Until the Inquisition concludes its inquiry," Anselm said. "Which the Provost has arranged to conclude... favorably. There is a payment. To the Grand Inquisitor's office. For the expedient resolution of the matter."

Halvard heard the words. He understood each of them individually. It took a long moment for the sentence they made together to finish forming in his chest, and when it did, it did not feel like anger. It felt like standing in a room he had lived in all his life and noticing, suddenly, that the walls were painted on.

"They want it destroyed," he said. "The stone. Or the proof of it. They want the whole matter to end in ash and a receipt."

"The Provost wants the matter to end," Anselm corrected, with the pedantic courage of a lifelong clerk. "What the Provost wants, brother, I have wondered about for twenty years and been glad not to know. But I will tell you what I have carried, since you ask, since you'll take it from me anyway." He drew a breath. "I carried the order for the scroll's destruction myself, weeks ago. I watched it burn. And I will tell you what struck me, standing there, watching a document older than the kingdom go up in a brazier." His eyes came up, and for the first time the mild clerk looked at Halvard as an equal, which was somehow more frightening than hatred. "It burned green. Did you know that? Old vellum, old ink, soaked in whatever they preserve such things with—it burned green, like something alive objecting. And the Provost stood there warming his hands at it. Warming his hands, Inquisitor. At a fire that was objecting."

Halvard said nothing. The splinter in him had gone deep enough now that he could feel it against everything.

"And the second thing I carried," Anselm went on, softer now, almost gentle, which was crueler, "is a name. The Provost's instruction, verbatim, for the affidavit of the scroll's destruction: *the scribe acted alone, in corruption, and no doctrine of the temple was implicated.* No doctrine implicated. Do you understand what that means? It means the temple knew what the stone was. Knew what it held. Knew what was done to make it. And when one small scribe ran into the mountains carrying the truth, the temple's first act was not to recover the truth." He shook his head slowly. "It was to destroy every other copy of it, and sell the Inquisition the ash."

The fire settled. One of the young couriers had begun, very quietly, to pray.

Halvard stood. His legs felt borrowed, operated by someone else's certainty. Nineteen years. Nineteen years of warrants and pyres and ledgers of names, of Zephyrine Dravenmoor's name on his list like a wound he could not stop touching, of *they are dangerous, they must be bound, the realm survives by the binding*—and underneath all of it, all along, the temple had known. Had built itself on the knowing. Had fed the Inquisition its targets the way a farmer feeds chaff to fire, to keep the flames busy and the harvest hidden.

Every heretic he had burned. Every gifted soul dragged to the registers. Every name—and the names came, he realized now, came *always*, with such convenient regularity, from the temple's own confessional seals, passed to the crown through channels he had never been permitted to audit—

"You look ill, Inquisitor," Anselm said.

"I look like a man doing arithmetic," Halvard said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "Nineteen years of sums. And the total is that I was the broom."

He walked to the mule. The strongbox was locked with temple work, good work, but locks were a language and he had been raised on it. He opened it with two picks and a patience honed on the reliquaries of condemned houses, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth, he found what he already knew he would find: the affidavits, sealed in green wax. And beneath them, a folio in the Provost's own hand—orders, schedules, a map of the Thornfell passes marked with search grids, and a single line, underlined twice, that stopped his breath:

*If the stone reaches the Garden, all is lost. Spare no expense. Spare no souls.*

*Spare no souls.* From the church of Saint Verane, whose entire visible doctrine was mercy.

He wrapped it all again, closed the box, and then—after a moment—did not relock it. Let them explain the opened strongbox to whoever asked. Let the temple learn what it felt like to be audited.

"Brother Anselm."

"Inquisitor?"

"When you deliver your affidavits to Vell's Crossing, you will find the chapter-house expecting you. Tell them whatever you like. But know that copies of everything in this box now exist in three places, and that I wrote none of them down, which means they cannot be burned." He let that sit. "Your Provost taught me that trick. Destroy the copies, keep the memory. It seems the lesson ran both directions."

Anselm regarded him for a long moment. Then, incredibly, the mild clerk smiled—a small, tired, genuine smile, the smile of a man setting down a weight carried for twenty years.

"I dotted my i's like a man stabbing something small," Anselm said. "You always did read a person's hand well, Inquisitor. Go carefully in the mountains. The Provost's agents there aren't soldiers. They're believers. Brother Cailean's people." He said the name the way one names a disease. "Soldiers can be bought. Believers only burn."

* * *

He rode north through the frozen dark, and the arithmetic would not stop.

That was the cruelty of it. He had expected rage, and rage came, but rage was simple and burned out fast. What remained was colder and more patient: the ledger-work of a lifetime, every entry needing revision. The dyer in Grell's Hollow, who had refused to run—who had stood in his doorway with dye-stained hands and said, *I have done nothing but color cloth, Inquisitor, and you will tell my daughter why that was worth dying for*—that man had died to protect a secret the temple already knew. Had died, in truth, to protect the temple. Every pyre had warmed the same hands.

And Zephyrine. He made himself think the name plainly, without flinching, the way you press a bruise to learn how deep it goes. Zephyrine, who had copied tithes for eleven years in a cell the size of a confession booth. Zephyrine, who had once—before the lists, before the warrants, before Halvard had traded friendship for office—used to catch fireflies in the seminary garden and hold them cupped in both hands, and the light had come through the fingers, and Halvard had thought, even then, even as a boy: *that light is coming from between the hands, not from the fireflies.*

He had known. Some buried part of him had always known, and he had built his career on not-knowing, and the temple had built its empire on his not-knowing, and the two architectures had leaned against each other comfortably for nineteen years.

Now the frost was thick on the hedges and the mountains stood black against the stars, and somewhere up in that darkness a stolen stone was singing its way toward a sundered Garden, and Halvard found that he was not riding to stop it.

He was riding to see it.

Or perhaps—he admitted, in the honest hour past midnight, when the road was empty and the lie had no audience—to kneel at it. He did not yet know what he believed. He knew only that belief purchased with lies was counterfeit coin, and he had spent nineteen years of it, and the accounts were called in.

Behind him, far back on the white road, torchlight: three riders moving fast. Ferren and his wardens, no longer pretending to assist.

Ahead, in the high passes, other lights moved too—too many, too organized, descending toward the Garden's threshold with the terrible patience of the sincere. Cailean's believers, come to spare no souls.

Halvard turned his horse off the king's highway onto the smugglers' track, the narrow way, the way that did not appear on any crown map—and rode into the mountains, carrying what he carried: a strongbox of proofs, a head full of Inquisition tactics, and the first true prayer of his adult life, offered up to no saint he could name:

*Let me arrive while there is still something left to save.*
