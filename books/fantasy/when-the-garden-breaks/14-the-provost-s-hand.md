## 14. The Provost's Hand

The Garden taught me its weather before it taught me anything else.

Three days after the guardian first spoke to me—three days of failed attempts and aching sleep—I had learned to read the light that pooled between the broken colonnades like standing water. When the magic ran calm, it lay gold and slow along the flagstones, warm as bathwater against the ankles. When something troubled the deep ways, it shivered. And on the morning Brother Cailean came, it went out.

Not dimmed. *Out*, the way a lamp is out—snuffed by a hand cupped over the flame—and every hair on my arms rose as though a door had opened somewhere onto winter.

I was kneeling at the heart of the sanctuary, where the covenant stone sat in its cradle of living root, when I felt it. The stone had been singing to me in fragments all night, half-words, half-griefs, teaching me the shape of what it had once been: not a prison but a promise, not a chain but a knot tied by ten thousand willing hands. I had been trying to learn the knot. My hands were still trembling from the last attempt, from the memory of the Garden reaching into me and finding only the performed self, the scribe-self, the careful nothing I had built to survive Saint Verane's corridors. *You cannot bargain with a mask on,* the failure had said. So I had come back anyway, maskless or trying to be, and I had been sitting with my palms flat on the stone's warm surface when the light died.

Ossa felt it too. She was up on the western wall where she always went at dawn, checking her sightlines with the methodical patience of a woman who had spent thirty years staying alive by knowing what was coming. Her voice carried down through the ruined sanctuary, stripped of all its smuggler's music.

"Zephyrine. Smoke. North road. A lot of it."

I climbed to her. The Thornfell mountains fell away below us in blue-green folds, and far down, where the pass narrowed to a throat of rock, I saw what she saw: a column of men in temple grey, moving with the unhurried discipline of people who believed God marched beside them. Behind them, torches already lit in broad daylight—not for warmth, but for burning. At their head rode a figure in white and gold, and even at that distance I knew the set of his shoulders, the way he sat his horse like a man nailed upright.

Brother Cailean. The Provost's own hand.

"He'll have forty men," Ossa said. "Maybe fifty. Temple regulars, and those are worse than soldiers—they think dying for the Provost is a promotion." She spat over the wall, an old habit that looked absurd and magnificent in this holy place. "We have you, me, a guardian spirit that talks in riddles, and whatever's left of the outer gate."

"The gate held against the Inquisition for a thousand years."

"The Inquisition wanted to knock politely." Her eyes were narrow, reading the road the way she read ledgers and faces. "That man down there wants to take the stone back to Verane and burn it in front of the whole conclave so no one ever asks about the Garden again. He won't knock."

* * *

We spent the morning preparing, and the preparation itself was a kind of prayer I had never been permitted before. In the temple, readiness meant hiding—smoothing the ink, banking the candle, folding the wild thing inside me small enough to fit through a keyhole. Here, readiness meant opening doors. The guardian showed me the Garden's old defenses, and they were not weapons; they were invitations turned inside out. Paths that remembered being walked and could forget. Stairs that had learned patience. Water that remembered every oath ever sworn beside it and grew heavy with them.

"You're asking the ruins to fight," Ossa said, watching a collapsed archway knit itself halfway closed at my touch, stone groaning like an old man rising from a chair.

"I'm asking them to remember what they were built for."

"And if they remember wrong?"

"Then we die in very interesting architecture."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she went back to the gate.

The outer gate of the Garden was not a gate anymore—it was a wound, a gap in the sanctuary wall where the mountain itself had been cut open centuries ago, wide enough for processions of thousands in the days when the gifted came here freely, on pilgrimage, on festival, to be seen. To be *seen*. That was the part that kept catching in my chest like a fishbone. This place had been built for visibility. Everything I had ever survived by doing, this place existed to make impossible.

Ossa fortified it the smuggler's way: oil, caltrops made from broken statuary, choke-points mapped in charcoal on her forearm because paper was for people who expected to live long enough to file things. I watched her work and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she did not expect to hold it. She was buying time. She had been buying time since Grell's Hollow, since the knife-edge standoff in the pass, since the moment she'd told me her contract was my capture and then chosen, with a shrug, to tear it up. Smugglers' arithmetic, she called it. But there was nothing arithmetic about the way she checked the tension on the winch above the gate twice, three times, four.

"Ossa." I said her name and she didn't turn around. "When they come—if it goes badly—you can still run. The deep ways go south. You know them better than anyone alive."

"Now you listen to me, scribe." She turned, and her face in the strange lightless morning was older than I'd ever let myself see. "Thirty years I've moved other people's goods past other people's laws, and do you know what I learned? Every debt gets paid. Every one. You just get to choose which ones, and to whom." She tapped her chest, once, hard. "I owe you. You stood in front of me in the pass when the soldiers came, and you didn't calculate it first. I've watched clever people my whole life. You're the first one who stopped being clever at exactly the right moment." She picked up her crossbow. "So I'm paying. Don't make it sentimental. I hate sentimental."

It was, from Ossa Vane, the most naked declaration of love I had ever received, and I understood that too, and I hated that I understood it, because understanding it meant knowing what it would cost.

* * *

They came at dusk, because Cailean was a zealot and zealots love symbolism more than tactics.

He came on foot at the head of his column, white robes immaculate despite two days' march, and he stopped beyond crossbow range and raised his voice—a beautiful voice, trained on decades of sermons, a voice built to fill cathedrals and now filling a mountain valley instead.

"ZEPHYRINE DRAVENMOOR." My name rolled off the rocks and came back to me doubled, tripled, until the whole Garden seemed to be saying it. "You have stolen what the Holy Church of Saint Verane has kept safe for nine hundred years. Return the covenant stone, and the Church in its mercy will remember only that you were misled. Resist, and every soul who aids you will share your excommunication—your goods forfeit, your name struck from the rolls, your body given to the pyre."

Struck from the rolls. I stood on the wall above him and thought: *that used to terrify me.* There had been a time—the whole of my life, really—when erasure was the worst thing I could imagine. To be unwritten. To become a blank space in the ledger where a name should be.

"Brother Cailean!" I called back, and my voice surprised me by holding. "Ask your Provost why the scroll ordering the stone's destruction bore his seal! Ask him why the temple fears what it claims to guard!"

For a moment, silence. Then, softer, and somehow worse for its softness, carrying up the slope with terrible gentleness:

"Sister Zephyrine. I read that scroll. I burned its ashes myself." He spread his hands, white sleeves falling like wings. "Yes. The Provost feared the stone would fall into unbound hands. Yes. The temple has guarded imperfectly, by human means, with human weakness. But hear me." His voice rose again, filled the valley, and I heard in it not cynicism but faith, real faith, the kind that moves armies. "Unbound magic drowned the kingdom of Vel Sarran. Not metaphor. *Drowned*. Forty thousand souls beneath black water because gifted hands refused binding. The covenant is not theft. It is a dam. And you—" he pointed, and his finger found me across all that distance as though the light itself obeyed him, "—you are a child playing with the floodgates."

And the horror of it, the thing I would carry long after, was that I believed he believed it. There was no lie in him to find. Only a truth worn smooth by repetition until it fit his hand like a weapon.

"Come and see what the floodgates were hiding," I answered, and stepped back from the wall.

* * *

They came at moonrise, and the gate held for six hours, and I will not pretend those hours were noble. War, when you are close enough to smell it, is mostly fear and noise and the animal business of staying alive.

Ossa fought the way she bargained: economically, without flourish, every bolt and blade placed where it bought the most. The Garden fought the way it remembered: paths shifted under charging feet, stairs lengthened, the oil she'd laid caught fire in patterns that funneled the temple regulars into killing grounds no one had designed. For six hours it worked. For six hours I stood behind her on the gate, feeding the ruin's memories into its stones, feeling the whole sanctuary strain around us like a great beast waking stiff and arthritic after nine hundred years of sleep.

And then Cailean stopped sending men at the gate and started singing.

I heard it first as a wrongness in the stone under my palms—a stutter in the old defenses, a forgetting. He stood beyond the firelight with a censer swinging, chanting the Rite of Unmaking, the same rite they used to strip wild-magic from the condemned before the pyre, and the Garden's magic recoiled from it the way flesh flinches from a brand. Where his voice touched, the walls forgot themselves. An archway I had knitted closed unraveled. A stair that had swallowed three soldiers yawned them back out, dazed, into the arms of their brothers.

"He's unbinding the defenses," I gasped. "He's using the binding-rite on the Garden itself—"

"Of course he is." Ossa reloaded, fired, reloaded. "They wrote the rite *here*, didn't they? First they steal the house, then they use the house keys." Her mouth twisted. "Story of this whole bloody realm."

The breach, when it came, was almost gentle. A section of wall simply *agreed* with him—nine hundred years of stolen obedience answering the call of its thieves—and folded inward like a knee giving way. Grey-robed men poured through the gap, and the firelight showed me their faces, and they were not monsters. They were tired farm boys and second sons and one elderly brother with spectacles fogged by the cold, all of them certain, all of them sincere, all of them coming to put the world back the way their masters had told them it should be.

"Hold the inner gate!" Ossa shouted. "Get to the stone!"

"You come with me—"

"*Move!*"

I moved. It is the decision I will reckon with for the rest of my life, however long the third covenant lets that be: I moved, because she ordered it, because some part of me had already done the arithmetic she never allowed herself—that one of us defending the stone was worth more than both of us dying at the gate. I reached the inner sanctuary. I put my hands on the stone. And through its singing I felt everything that happened next at the outer gate, felt it the way you feel a blow to someone standing behind you.

I felt Ossa Vane hold the inner gate alone for eleven minutes.

I felt her spend her bolts, then her blades, then her tricks—oil and caltrop and collapsing stair—then her breath, shouting threats in three languages and inventing a fourth. I felt the moment the Rite of Unmaking finally chewed through the last of the Garden's cooperation and the gate became only stone, only wood, only a barrier between fifty believers and one woman who had spent thirty years surviving by running and had finally, fatally, decided to stand.

I felt the sword.

Not in detail. The stone spared me detail. But I felt the weight of it land, and I felt her go down among the roots of the old gatehouse, and I felt—this is the part I cannot write cleanly, and I am a scribe, writing cleanly is the one thing I was ever good at—I felt the exact moment her fierce, transactional, unsentimental heart decided to keep beating anyway, out of what I can only assume was spite.

I was moving before I knew it. Down through the sanctuary, past the guardian's pool where the light lay dead and cold, out into the smoke. Later, the guardian would tell me I had carried more raw magic in those hundred yards than in all my failed attempts at reforging—grief does that, grief burns hotter than purpose—but I remember none of it as power. I remember it as debt. Thirty years I had paid down my family's obligations in ink, in invisibility, in the slow currency of being useful and unremarkable, and I had believed debts were chains. Standing over Ossa Vane in the wreckage of the gate, her blood black in the firelight, her hand still locked around a crossbow with no bolts left, I understood at last what she had tried to tell me on the wall.

Some debts are worth owing. Some debts are the only thing that tells you what you are.

There were three temple regulars still standing over her. What I did to them, I did without a word, and afterward they fled, and I let them flee, and I have decided that letting them flee was the whole of my virtue that night.

She was breathing. Shallow, wet, wrong, but breathing. I tore my scribe's coat—my last garment from Saint Verane, the uniform of the person I had pretended to be—and packed the wounds the way the herbals in the scriptorium described and the reality of it was nothing like the diagrams. Her eyes opened. Found mine.

"Told you," she whispered. "Every debt... gets paid."

"Not this one." My voice cracked straight down the middle. "This one stays open. You hear me? I'm keeping this one open forever."

Her mouth twitched toward the ghost of a smile. "Sentimental," she breathed, and went unconscious rather than agree with me, which was the most Ossa thing she could possibly have done.

I carried her into the deep ways, following the map she'd pressed into my hands in the siege's first hour, and laid her in the guardian's pool where the water remembered every healing ever worked in this place. The light began, slowly, painfully, to come back—gold rising through the water like dawn underwater—and I knelt there with my hands on her sternum and my forehead against hers and I made the Garden a promise I had refused to make before.

*I will finish it. Whatever it costs. Not surrender—terms. My terms. But I will finish it.*

The stone, deep in the sanctuary, sang back a single note. And then, fainter, from far up the northern pass, carried on wind that smelled of snow and coming change—

bells. Inquisitor's bells. Someone else had entered the Garden's approaches, someone the temple regulars had not expected, and the last thing I heard before exhaustion took me was the sentry-fire on the heights signaling a second army on the road.

Grey robes at the gate. And now, behind them, black.
