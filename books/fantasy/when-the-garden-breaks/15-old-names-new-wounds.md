## 15. Old Names, New Wounds

The mountain had stopped pretending to be merely stone.

Halvard felt it in his teeth first—a low hum, like a struck bell that never finished ringing—and then in the air itself, which grew thick and sweet as he climbed, sweet in a way that had no business existing at this altitude where nothing grew but lichen and stubborn pine. The magic of the Garden leaked from its broken walls like light under a door, and every step closer made the hair on his arms rise, made the old scar across his palm ache with a heat he had not felt since boyhood.

He came unarmed. That was the point of it. He had left his sword wrapped in oilcloth beneath a cairn three miles back, along with his warrant, his seal, and the black-and-silver sash of his office—all of it buried like a corpse he wasn't yet ready to admit was dead. What remained was a man in a road-stained coat with frostbitten fingers and a ledger-book full of sins, climbing toward the one place in the realm his Inquisition feared more than any army.

*Supplicant*, he thought, and the word tasted like ash. *You are a supplicant now.*

Behind him, somewhere down in the white folds of the pass, his own people were coming. Not all of them. Not even most. But enough—three wagons of reliquary-iron, forty sworn blades, and Brother Cailean's voice like a whetstone drawn across all of it. Halvard had read the dispatches by candlelight in Grell's Hollow while the ash of that town was still settling on the rooftops, and what he had read had finally, irrevocably, broken something in him.

Not the burning. He had ordered burnings; he understood their arithmetic, terrible as it was. No—it was the *other* order. The one that had come under temple seal, signed in the Provost's own careful hand: *Ensure the covenant stone is destroyed or recovered. Under no circumstances permit it to reach the Garden intact. The truth must not be permitted to speak.*

*The truth must not be permitted to speak.*

He had spent twenty years hunting gifted children in the name of an order that had built its power on a theft so old it had become scripture. And Zephyrine Dravenmoor—the quiet scribe whose ink-stained fingers he had once held over a guttering candle in the temple library, laughing about nothing—had run into the mountains carrying the proof of it in their two hands.

Halvard climbed.

* * *

The Garden announced itself before he saw it.

The pass opened like a wound between two shoulders of rock, and beyond it the air changed—not warmer exactly, but *fuller*, the way a room feels when a hundred candles have just been snuffed and the smoke still holds their shape. Then the walls rose out of the morning mist: vast, sundered, beautiful. Columns the size of watchtowers lay toppled like felled trees, furred with moss the color of jade. Arches leapt from nothing to nothing, their keystones missing, and through those empty frames Halvard could see the Garden's heart—a sunken sanctuary where light pooled like water, gold-green and slow-moving, though there was no sun above the mist to make it.

And everywhere, everywhere, the hum. It was in the stones. It was in his blood. It knew he was coming.

They were waiting for him at the threshold—of course they were waiting; the mountain had probably told them—and Halvard stopped ten paces out, hands open and empty at his sides, the oldest gesture of surrender in the world.

Ossa Vane stood in front, a smuggler's knife held low and easy despite the bandage wrapping her ribs, her face arranged into an expression of profound unwelcome. Behind her, half-shadowed by a fallen column, stood Zephyrine Dravenmoor.

They had changed. That was the first thing. The person Halvard remembered had been built entirely of smallness—shoulders drawn in, eyes down, a life conducted in the margins of other people's pages. This Zephyrine stood straighter. Their eyes, when they lifted them, did not drop away. But there was something else too, something that made Halvard's chest tighten: a weariness around the mouth, a hollowness under the eyes, the look of someone who had recently died a little and not yet decided what to do about it.

"Inquisitor," Ossa said. "You're a long way from your pyres."

"I burned them," Halvard said. "Grell's Hollow. I gave the order myself." He said it plainly, because anything less would be a lie, and lies were the currency he had come to stop trading in. "I executed Maren Ostwyck in the square because she refused to kneel. I let her child watch."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the ruined heights, a bird called once and thought better of it.

"You've come to confess?" Ossa asked, and there was no softness in it.

"I've come to trade." He reached slowly into his coat—saw Zephyrine tense, saw Ossa's knife shift—and drew out the ledger-book. His whole career, bound in grey leather. Every name. Every order. Every dispatch he'd copied before his superiors could burn them. He knelt, stiff-kneed, and laid it on the frozen ground between them, then pushed it forward with two fingers. "Every plan the Inquisition has. Cailean's route. His numbers. Where the reliquary-iron is weakest. What the Provost promised the crown in exchange for silence. It's all in there. I wrote most of it myself."

Zephyrine moved for the first time, stepping past Ossa, and Halvard looked up at them from his knees and found he could not read their face at all. Twenty years ago he had known every flicker of it. Now it was a closed book written in a hand he'd forgotten how to parse.

"Why?" Zephyrine asked. One word, quiet, and it carried further than any shout.

"Because I was wrong." The words came harder than he expected. He had rehearsed them on the climb, polished them like stones, and now they fell out of his mouth rough and unlovely. "Not mistaken—wrong. There's a difference. Mistaken is when you lack information. I had the information. I had Maren Ostwyck's face. I had her child cursing me with my own name, my own sins, accurate to the day. I had—" He stopped. Started again. "I built a life on the belief that what we did was necessary. And then I read the Provost's seal on an order to destroy the truth, and I understood that everything I burned, I burned for a lie. And either I could keep burning, or I could stop. Those were the only choices left."

"And you expect us to believe that," Ossa said, "because you brought a book."

"No," Halvard said. "I don't expect you to believe anything. Belief is your prerogative, not mine to demand. I expect you to be pragmatic. Cailean reaches this place in four days. He has forty men, siege equipment, and enough reliquary-iron to blind every gifted soul within these walls. Without what I know, you hold this ruin for perhaps a day. With it—" he shrugged, a small, tired motion, "—perhaps you hold it long enough for whatever you mean to do here. I'm not asking for forgiveness. I'm asking to be useful. It's the only honest thing left to offer."

Zephyrine crouched, slowly, and picked up the ledger.

For a long moment they did not open it. They only held it, weighing it, looking at Halvard over the top of it with an expression he almost recognized—almost—and then they opened it to the middle and began to read. Halvard watched their eyes move down the page and saw the moment they found their own name. It was early in the book. It always had been.

"'Subject shows restraint unusual in the gifted,'" Zephyrine read aloud, voice flat as slate. "'Recommends continued observation rather than registration. Subject may prove useful.'" They closed the ledger with a soft sound. "You wrote that eight years ago. When the proclamation came. You could have put me on the list. You didn't."

"I told myself it was mercy," Halvard said. "It wasn't. It was ownership. I wanted you kept safe the way a man keeps a thing safe—because it was mine to protect. My friend. My responsibility. My—" He exhaled. "I never once asked what you wanted. That's the shape of it. That's the whole ugly shape of what I did to you, and it started years before the fires."

"Yes," Zephyrine said. "It did."

They stood there, the ledger between them like a border crossing, and Halvard waited. He was good at waiting; it had been his profession. But this waiting was different—this was the waiting of a man at a door he had nailed shut himself, asking to be let through.

"The child," Zephyrine said suddenly. "Maren's child. The reports say you let them go."

Halvard went still. "Who told you—"

"A dyer's cousin came through Grell's Hollow after. She talked. A cellar. An unlocked door, and an Inquisitor who turned his back." Zephyrine's head tilted. "That doesn't sound like the man who wrote this ledger."

"It isn't," Halvard said. "Or it's the same man, finally reading his own book. I don't know which. Maybe both. Maybe mercy was in me all along and I kept calling it weakness." He laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what the worst part is? For twenty years I thought I was the strong one. The one who faced what needed facing. And you—you spent all those years being small and useful and invisible, and you were braver than I ever was. You survived them. I served them."

"Surviving isn't bravery," Zephyrine said quietly. "Some days it's just the absence of options."

"And some days it's the presence of a choice everyone else is too afraid to see." Halvard got to his feet, knees cracking, and spread his hands again. "I can't undo Grell's Hollow. I can't unwrite a single name in that book. All I can do is stand where I stand now and tell you what's coming, and let you decide whether my knowledge is worth my company. If you say no, I'll walk back down the mountain and take Cailean's blade myself before I let him use mine. If you say yes—" he hesitated, and for the first time since childhood, his voice wavered, "—then teach me the walls. Let me spend what I know the way I should have spent it years ago. On defense. On *them*, for once."

Ossa spat to the side. "Sentimental," she muttered. "But he's right about the numbers. Forty blades and iron—that's not a raid, that's an erasure."

Zephyrine hadn't moved. They were looking at Halvard with an expression he couldn't name, and the hum of the Garden seemed to rise around them, attentive, as if the place itself were leaning in to hear the answer.

"You want to know if I trust you," Zephyrine said at last. "I don't. I want to be very clear about that. Trust is a thing that gets rebuilt in pieces, over years, and you have given me hours." They held up the ledger. "But this is worth more than trust. This is *terms*. So here are mine. You will give Ossa everything—routes, numbers, weaknesses—and she will verify what she can. You will carry no weapon inside these walls. And when this is done, if we live, you will stand before whoever survives and answer for Grell's Hollow. Not to me. To *them*. To the ones who lost people. Do you accept?"

"I accept."

"And Halvard." Zephyrine stepped closer, and up close Halvard could see the cost written on them plainly now—the hollowed look, the tremor in the hands they folded to hide it. Whatever the Garden had demanded of them, they had paid part of it already, and there was more owing. "I am not forgiving you. I want that clear too. Forgiveness would be a lie right now, and I've had enough of lies to last several lifetimes. But I will work beside you. That's not the same thing. Don't mistake it for more than it is."

"It isn't," Halvard said. "But it's more than I carried up the mountain."

Something passed between them then—not warmth, not yet, maybe not ever, but a kind of recognition. Two people who had loved each other once, standing on opposite banks of a river they had both helped flood, agreeing to build a bridge neither fully believed in.

Ossa broke it, because someone had to. "Right. Sentiment's done. Inside, both of you, before the mist burns off and every crow in the Thornfells sees us standing here like a painting." She jerked her thumb toward the sundered archway. "Inquisitor. You're going to show me exactly where Cailean thinks our weak points are. And then you're going to be wrong about some of them, because I've been lying to officials since before you could grow that beard."

Halvard almost smiled. Almost.

As he crossed the threshold into the Garden proper, the hum swelled around him, and for one vertiginous instant he felt it *look* at him—the ancient awareness in the stones turning its attention like a great eye, weighing him, measuring the shape of his guilt and finding it, perhaps, an acceptable currency. Frost bloomed and melted on the fallen columns. The pooled light in the sanctuary deepened.

And far below, faint but unmistakable, carried up through miles of living rock, Halvard heard—or dreamed he heard—the sound of drums. Cailean's vanguard, moving faster than any dispatch had promised.

Four days had just become three.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon in the Garden's outer cloister, where Ossa had chalked a rough map of the approaches onto a slab of paving stone the size of a table. Halvard gave her everything: the order of march, the placement of the reliquary-iron cages on the supply wagons, Cailean's habit of splitting his force at chokepoints, the signal-codes the Inquisition used, the fact that six of the forty were temple agents in soldier's coats who would obey no retreat order but Cailean's voice.

Ossa absorbed it all with the flat attention of a woman counting coins. Twice she caught errors in his memory—gaps where loyalty had blurred detail—and twice she made him go back until the picture was true. By the time the light failed, the map was dense with her annotations, and Halvard felt strangely lighter, as though the knowledge had been ballast he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten it was weight.

Zephyrine watched from the edge of the cloister, saying little. Once, when Halvard described the reliquary-iron's effect—how it didn't kill the gift but *deafened* it, leaving the gifted screaming silently inside their own skulls—he saw them flinch, and hated himself anew for knowing exactly why.

When full dark came, Zephyrine finally spoke.

"There's something else you should know." They stood at the cloister's edge, and behind them the sanctuary's pooled light pulsed slow as a sleeping heart. "What I mean to do here. Why the stone matters. Why Cailean is right to be afraid." They told him—all of it. The original covenant. The theft. The Garden's demand. The first attempt, and what it had cost. "The Garden wants a whole self as surety. Everything. Total surrender. And I nearly gave it, because surrender felt like honesty." Their laugh was thin. "But I've decided there's a third option. I just don't know yet what it costs, or who pays it."

Halvard considered this for a long time. "And if the third option fails?"

"Then the Garden takes what it takes, and Cailean takes the rest, and the realm goes on deaf and dreaming." Zephyrine met his eyes. "Which is why I need to ask you something, and I need you not to flinch. When the moment comes—if it comes—will you witness? Not fight. Not save me. Just *see*. Someone has to see it truly, or none of it counts. That's what a warden does. And I think—" their voice caught, steadied, "—I think that might be what you were for, all along. Not the hunting. The witnessing. You just aimed it wrong."

Halvard stood very still. The hum of the Garden filled the silence like held breath.

"I spent twenty years watching people," he said finally, "and seeing nothing. If you'll have my eyes, they're yours. They're poor instruments. But they're honest ones, now."

"That's all I need."

Above them, the missing keystone of the great arch framed a sky going hard and clear with frost, and in that sky, moving south in ragged skeins, the birds were fleeing the high passes. All of them. At once.

Ossa looked up at the emptying sky, then at Halvard, and her face was grim as winter.

"They're not waiting four days," she said. "Cailean's driving them through the night marches. We have until tomorrow's dusk. Maybe less."

Somewhere in the deep of the Garden, the covenant stone began—very softly—to sing.
