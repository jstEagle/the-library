## 16. The Siege of the Garden

The first horn came at dawn, and it was wrong.

Zephyrine knew it was wrong the way a scribe knows a forged hand—not by any single flaw but by the whole weight of it. The horns of Saint Verane were long and mournful, made to carry grief across courtyards. This one was short, doubled, struck twice like a fist on a door. A siege-horn. An announcement: *we are here, and we are not leaving.*

They stood on the broken colonnade above the Garden's western terrace, Ossa leaning against a pillar that had been green stone once and was now something softer, almost living, veined with light. The morning fog had not yet burned off the terraces, and through it Zephyrine could see the pilgrim road far below, black with men. Torches still lit, though the sun was rising—soldiers who wanted to be seen coming.

"How many?" Ossa asked. Her voice was rougher than it had been three days ago. The wound in her side, taken at the gate when Brother Cailean's vanguard first tested the approaches, had gone hot. Zephyrine could see it in the set of her jaw, in the sheen of sweat that no mountain cold could explain.

"Two hundred. Perhaps more behind the ridge." Zephyrine's hands were steady. They noticed this about themselves with a kind of distant wonder. For years their hands had trembled over every flare of magic, every near-discovery. Now the discovery had come with an army, and the trembling had simply stopped, as if some part of them had finally run out of fear to spend.

"And Halvard?"

"Gone down to watch the road since before first light." Zephyrine hesitated. "He said he'd know their order of march by the banners."

"He would." Ossa said it without bitterness, which frightened Zephyrine more than bitterness would have. She pushed herself off the pillar and swayed, caught herself. "Get me to the map room. If I'm going to bleed out on somebody's floor today, I'd rather it be mine."

* * *

From the ridge above the pilgrim road, Halvard counted what he had spent his life learning to count.

Four hundred and sixty, by the standards he'd trained himself to—counting heads in a column by the width of the road they filled, counting supply carts by axle-grease and wheel-rut, counting officers by how the men arranged themselves around them. Four hundred and sixty soldiers under the white-and-silver of the temple guard, with perhaps eighty camp followers and a train of siege ladders that told him everything about Cailean's intentions. You did not bring ladders to arrest a thief. You brought ladders to take a fortress.

And at the center of the column, riding a mule because he was too pious for a warhorse, rode Brother Cailean himself, bareheaded, hands folded, lips moving in prayer. Even at this distance something radiated off him that Halvard had felt only twice before in his career—the first time standing beside a condemned witch as her bindings took hold, the second time watching a mob decide, all together and wordlessly, to burn a district. Conviction. The pure, terrible kind.

*You were one of us,* Halvard thought at him. *We wore the same warrant. We said the same oaths.* And then, because honesty had become a habit he could no longer break, even inwardly: *I said those oaths louder than you did.*

He turned back down toward the Garden, moving fast along the goat track, cataloguing as he went. He knew Inquisition assault doctrine better than anyone alive; he had helped write two of its manuals. They would not storm the outer wall at dawn. Dawn assaults were for armies that feared defenders. Cailean had four hundred against perhaps six, and so he would do the thing the manuals recommended against a weakly held position: he would advance slowly, in worshipful silence, and let the silence do his work. Fear fills empty hours. By the time the first soldier touched the gate, half the defense would have spent itself shaking.

Unless someone knew to spend the hours instead.

The Garden received him at its threshold without ceremony anymore. Three days ago the guardian's presence had pressed against him like a thumb testing fruit. Now it merely watched, a vast attention coiled in the flowering ruin, and Halvard had learned to speak to it plainly, the way you speak to a magistrate whose favor you need.

"They'll come up the pilgrim road in good order," he said aloud, to the air, to the stone, to Zephyrine who was hurrying across the terrace with a bandage roll under one arm and ink stains still on their fingers from a night of writing. "Silent approach. No horns after the first. They want us listening to our own hearts."

"You're certain."

"I wrote the page." He said it flatly, and watched Zephyrine absorb it—that their old friend was not guessing, not hoping, but reading the enemy from memory of his own authorship. There was something in Zephyrine's face when they looked at him now that he could not name and did not ask about. Not forgiveness. Terms. He could live with terms. Terms were a language he was fluent in.

"Then tell me where we put ourselves," Zephyrine said.

* * *

The morning passed in work, and the work was strange and holy and desperate all at once.

Halvard walked the outer sanctuary with Zephyrine at his shoulder and the guardian's attention flowing beneath their feet like water under ice, and he gave orders as naturally as breathing, and hated himself a little for how naturally. "Here—the breach in the colonnade. They'll funnel through it; widen the rubble at the edges so they can't come four abreast. There—those fallen capitals. Roll them to make a second line. Your magic—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "Your gift. Can it move stone?"

"A little. Slowly."

"Then start now. Slow is fine. Stone doesn't have to hurry to be a wall."

By midday the outer sanctuary of the sundered Garden had begun, impossibly, to mend itself—not to its ancient wholeness but to a rougher, newer wholeness, rubble dragged into ramparts, broken pillars interlocked like the fingers of clasped hands. The magic pooled visibly in the air here, gold-green motes drifting like pollen, and where Zephyrine worked it thickened until Halvard could feel it on his skin, a warmth without heat, a pressure without weight. He had spent thirty years believing this stuff was rot in the world's flesh. It felt, up close, like the world's blood.

Ossa directed the last preparations from a chair carried up to the terrace, refusing a litter, refusing rest, her face grey as old tallow. She had mapped this ruin years ago, back when it was only a smugglers' legend—a place where crown patrols wouldn't go because the compasses spun—and her knowledge of its deep ways was stitched into every plan. But her voice kept fraying, and by afternoon she was shivering in mountain summer, and Zephyrine knelt beside her and saw the wound had gone the color of a bruised plum, spreading.

"No," Ossa said, before Zephyrine could speak. "Don't waste your miracle-work on me yet. Save it for walls. I've survived worse than a knife." Her eyes glittered with fever-brightness. "I survived a marriage, didn't I."

"Ossa—"

"Map room. Below the eastern stair. Bring paper." She gripped Zephyrine's wrist with a strength that surprised them both. "Everything I know is in my head, girl, and heads leak. Come take it out before I spill."

* * *

The map room had been a chapel once, or something older than chapels. Its ceiling was a dome pierced by a single oculus, and through it fell a column of late-afternoon light full of drifting motes, and the motes moved wrong—purposefully, patiently—like insects tending a garden. Ossa lay on a bench of living stone while Zephyrine ground ink and stretched fresh paper, and Ossa talked, and drew, and talked.

"The deep ways. Listen. Every great sanctuary built itself over something older—that's not poetry, that's engineering, the founders always squatted on the sacred places of whoever came before. The Garden has cellars under its cellars. Cisterns. A processional tunnel that runs from the heart of the inner sanctuary out to the cliff-face on the north side, behind the waterfall—dry in autumn, roaring in spring, and it's autumn now." Her finger traced lines that appeared under her touch as if the paper remembered them. "There's a door in the inner sanctum's floor. Round. Looks like a meditation mosaic. It isn't. It opens on stairs going *down*, and the stairs go everywhere."

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because tomorrow there won't be time." Ossa's breath caught; she rode it out with the discipline of a woman who had been tortured once and had decided never again to give pain the courtesy of a scream. "Listen to me, scribe. Cailean doesn't want to capture you. Capturing means questions, witnesses, paperwork. He wants the stone destroyed and everyone who's seen it buried under it. When he breaks the outer line—and he will, four hundred against six, don't flatter yourselves—you can't stand in the inner sanctuary and make your last stand like heroes in a ballad. Heroes in ballads die. You need to be somewhere he can't imagine, holding something he can't burn, when the moment comes."

"And you'll be—"

"I'll be where I'm most useful." She smiled, and the smile had bloodlessness in it. "Doors. Locks. Making his victory cost him an hour for every step. An hour is a fortune in a siege. Ask your Inquisitor."

"He's not my—" Zephyrine began, and heard how young the protest sounded, and stopped. "Why?" they asked instead. "Truly. You were paid to deliver me to the temple. Every reason you had to hate me, you earned honestly. Why bleed for this?"

Ossa looked at them for a long moment. Above her, the motes drifted in their slow constellations, and one of them settled on her forehead like a blessing or a snowflake, and she didn't brush it away.

"I ran numbers my whole life," she said. "Cargo, tolls, risk, bribe. Numbers keep you honest about other people—they're just weights and measures. But I never once ran the number for what happens to me. What I'd stand *in*. And then a temple scribe with candle-smoke on their fingers stood in front of a crossbow bolt meant for my chest, and I found out there was a ledger I hadn't been keeping." She closed her eyes. "You paid a debt I didn't know I owed myself. So now we're square, and being square with someone, scribe—it's the closest thing to family a smuggler believes in. Draw the north tunnel. Draw it twice."

* * *

They came at dusk, exactly as Halvard had written they would.

No horns now. No drums. Just the sound of four hundred boots finding a shared rhythm on the pilgrim road, soft as rain beginning. From the rebuilt rampart Zephyrine watched the dark column resolve out of the blue evening, torches unlit, faces indistinct, and felt the guardian rise through the stones beneath them like a slow tide—the whole ruined sanctuary waking, every fallen pillar remembering its purpose if not its height.

"Hold until they're in the funnel," Halvard murmured at their shoulder. He had insisted on standing the first watch of the wall himself, sword drawn, wearing no armor because armor would mark him as a commander worth killing first. "Not before. Fear spends early. Let them climb and climb and doubt."

The first soldier reached the breach in the colonnade. Then ten. Then fifty, packed in the throat of broken stone that Zephyrine had spent the day narrowing with patient magic, rubble interlocked overhead so that the passage ran under a shelf of hanging ruin.

"Now," Halvard said.

What followed, Zephyrine would remember afterward in fragments, the way trauma keeps its own ledger. The shelf of stone coming down on the head of the column—not crushing, Halvard had been precise about that, *maiming slows an army more than killing, killing makes martyrs*—and the screaming, and the torches finally lit and throwing everything into stuttering orange, and the gifted light rising off Zephyrine's hands in ropes as they pulled more stone into the gap. Arrows falling among the defenders like black rain. A soldier's face below, young, terrified, mouthing what might have been a prayer or Zephyrine's name—he couldn't have known it—or nothing at all.

And Halvard beside them, calm as a clerk at audit, calling corrections. "Left flank's thinning—two of yours are chasing kills, pull them back. They're testing the cistern entrance, they know the deep ways somehow, seal it. Seal it *now*."

Somehow. The word snagged in Zephyrine's mind even as they obeyed, dragging a slab of ancient paving across the cistern stair with a gesture that left their vision swimming. How would Cailean's zealots know the Garden's ways? Smuggler maps went many directions. Temple coin went further.

The line held through dusk. Held through the first night-watch. Held, incredibly, through midnight, when the attackers withdrew to their fires below and the defenders stood gasping in the moted dark among the wounded, and Zephyrine knelt in the gold-green drift of the Garden's magic and learned, with Ossa's grudging instruction, to close flesh instead of stone.

"You've a knack," Ossa said, watching them knit a militiawoman's gashed arm. "Waste of talent, smuggling. Should've been a surgeon."

"Should've been a lot of things."

"Mm. Most people should've." Her hand found Zephyrine's shoulder, grip lighter than yesterday. Lighter than this morning. "Second wave comes at dawn. That's doctrine too, isn't it? Ask your Inquisitor."

But Halvard was already climbing the rampart, staring south along the pilgrim road with an expression Zephyrine had never seen him wear, not even at the moment he'd chosen treason.

"What?" Zephyrine asked. "What is it?"

"Reinforcements." His voice was very quiet. "Another column. Banners I know." He turned, and the firelight showed Zephyrine his face stripped of everything except the arithmetic of ruin. "It's not Cailean's reserve. It's the Provost's own household guard. They've come to finish what the temple started."

Behind them, deeper in the Garden, faint through three walls and a courtyard of sleeping flowers, the covenant stone began to sing—and this time, Zephyrine realized with a chill that had nothing to do with mountain air, it was singing a warning. Something had already entered by the deep ways. Something was already inside.

And Ossa's map, spread open on the bench below, bore a fresh smudge of blood across the north tunnel, as if the paper itself were trying to point.
