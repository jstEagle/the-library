## 17. Cailean's Sermon

Brother Cailean had preached in the great basilica of Saint Verane three times in his life, and each time the incense had been sweeter, the choir better rehearsed, and the truth further from his lips. Here, at the broken heart of the sundered Garden, with ash on his boots and blood drying stiff along the seam of his sleeve—not his own; he had not yet earned his own—he found he had no need of either. The sanctuary itself was a sermon. It only wanted a voice.

He stood on the steps of the drowned altar, where the stone had been worn smooth by centuries of kneeling that had stopped, abruptly, some two hundred years ago. Water still moved somewhere beneath the floor. He could hear it through the soles of his feet, a patient circulation, as though the ruin were a body whose heart had been cut out but which refused, out of stubbornness or sanctity, to stop breathing. Above him the vaulted ceiling opened onto sky where the roof had fallen, and the light came down in slanted columns full of drifting pollen—or spores, or motes of raw gift, for this was a place where such distinctions had rotted away—and Cailean watched them turn in the gold and thought: *this is what unbound looks like.* Beautiful. And beautiful things, unbound, were how kingdoms died.

The fighting had moved outward, back toward the gate Ossa Vane was bleeding behind, but the sound of it reached him still—steel on steel, thin and distant, like rain on another man's roof. He did not hurry. Sermons could not be hurried. His brothers of the Provost's hand had taken the outer ring at dawn, had breached the colonnade by midmorning, and now held the inner sanctuary's threshold while their master's true errand was accomplished. Twelve men. Three dead already, their bodies laid out under a fallen statue with their hands folded—a courtesy Cailean had insisted upon. You did not leave the faithful facedown in the dirt. Even the faithless deserved folded hands.

And in the center of it all, cornered at last against the singing stone, stood Zephyrine Dravenmoor.

Cailean looked at them the way a physician looks at a wound he has long anticipated. There was the scribe he had passed a hundred times in the copying halls, ink-stained, quiet, always seated near the door as if proximity to an exit were a form of prayer. There was the thief who had carried off the covenant stone and half the temple's certainty with it. There was the gifted one—the *corrupted* one, the word came readily, the old word, the true word—who had led the crown's hunt across the Thornfells and into the last place the crown should ever want them.

"You've made this very difficult," Cailean said, pleasantly. "I want you to know I don't hold it against you."

Zephyrine said nothing. They were pale, and there was a burn along their jaw from the breach-magic the vanguard had used to open the sanctuary doors—holy fire, consecrated iron, the tools of binding turned briefly to breaking—and they stood with their hand pressed flat against the covenant stone as though it were the only solid thing left in the world. Perhaps it was. Cailean had read the old accounts. He knew what the stone did to those who touched it without surety.

"I mean it," he went on, stepping down from the altar, his voice carrying in the ruined acoustics the way voices carry in wells. "Difficult is good. Difficult means you understand something is at stake. The ones who frighten me are the easy ones—the dyers and hedge-witches and children with candle-tricks who go to the pyres confused, as if none of it were their fault." He smiled, and meant it, and hated that he meant it. "None of it *is* their fault, Zephyrine Dravenmoor. That is the first mercy I have to offer you tonight, and it is a greater mercy than Halvard ever gave anyone on his lists. It is not your fault. It is your nature. And nature—" he spread his hands, "—is exactly what we are here to discuss."

* * *

They had given him a congregation, in a way. Zephyrine at the stone. Ossa Vane's smugglers—two of them still breathing, dragged inside when the gate fell, kneeling now with rope at their throats because Cailean had ordered no killing in the inner sanctuary until the sermon was done. He was not a cruel man. Cruelty wasted suffering; suffering was a currency and he did not believe in debasement. And at the far edge of the light, half-shadowed behind a pillar of veined marble, Inquisitor Halvard, who had come to this place as a supplicant and traitor and whom Cailean had expected, because Halvard had always been legible, Halvard had always gone where his guilt pointed him.

"Sit," Cailean told the bound smugglers, unnecessarily—they could do nothing else. "No. Stay standing. Standing is honest."

He walked the length of the sanctuary as he spoke, unhurried, letting the ruined acoustics do their work. Somewhere above, wind moved through the broken vault and made a sound almost like a choir clearing its throat.

"You were taught," he began, "that the gift is a corruption. That it entered the world through some failure, some sin, some crack in creation, and that the crown's registries and the temple's bindings are medicine. Am I wrong? That is what the parish priests say. That is what the broadsheets say. That is what the Inquisition burns people for denying." He glanced at Halvard's shadow. "You know it best of all, old friend."

"That isn't why I'm here," Halvard said.

"No one is ever here for the reason they think." Cailean turned back to Zephyrine. "Here is what the parish priests do not say, because they have not been permitted to say it for two hundred years. Here is what is written in the sealed archives beneath Saint Verane, in the Provost's own keeping, in a hand older than the crown itself." He let the silence stretch. He had learned from the basilica that silence was a rope; people hanged themselves on it gladly. "There was no corruption. There was no sin. The gift was *given*. Freely. To everyone. It was in the water and the wheat and the blood of every child born between the mountains and the sea. And then a kingdom drowned."

Zephyrine's breath caught. Cailean heard it, and pressed the advantage gently, the way you press a bruise to prove it's real.

"The kingdom of Vel Sarenne," he said. "You will not find it on any map. You will not find it in any chronicle outside the sealed archives. It lay where the Saltmere fen lies now—it *is* the Saltmere fen, its fields, its roads, its nine thousand souls. And it did not fall to plague or war or flood from any natural sea. It fell because the gift, unbound, *answered*. A drought year. A desperate king. A court full of men and women whose wishing had never once been refused, because everything around them wished too, and agreed. They wished for rain." His voice dropped, and even he felt the cold of it. "It rained for forty days. It rained until the rivers stood up out of their beds. It rained until the wish was satisfied, and wishes, Zephyrine Dravenmoor, are never satisfied by halves. Nine thousand people learned to breathe water or died learning. And the first kings of the realm looked at that drowned country and understood something the Garden had never taught them: that power without consent is not power. It is weather. And you cannot reason with weather. You can only build against it."

He stopped walking. He was close to Zephyrine now, close enough to see the pulse beating in their throat, quick as a bird's.

"So they built. The covenant stone. The binding. Not to steal—to *save*. Every gifted soul since has lived because nine thousand died. Every binding the temple has laid, every registry the crown keeps, every pyre Halvard lit on his way here—all of it, all of it, is a wall built over a mass grave, and we are the masons, and I will not apologize for masonry." He straightened. "That is the doctrine. Not hatred. Arithmetic. Grief, done honestly, becomes arithmetic, and arithmetic does not flinch."

* * *

He had expected Zephyrine to argue. They were a scribe; scribes argued with documents. Instead they asked a question, and it was not the question he had prepared for.

"How many?" Zephyrine said. Their voice was hoarse. "Since the binding. How many gifted have the temple burned?"

Cailean considered lying, found it unnecessary, and was disturbed by how unnecessary it felt. "I don't have the number. No one does. The registers of executions are kept separately from the tithes."

"Separately," Zephyrine repeated. "So no one would add them up."

"So that the sum would not be *needed*." Cailean heard his own voice harden and did not soften it. "Yes. Do you imagine I haven't done your arithmetic, scribe? Do you think I copy tithes in my sleep and never once subtract? Nine thousand drowned. Two hundred years of burnings—I will grant you thousands, I will grant you tens of thousands if it eases your conscience to make us monstrous. Now tell me the alternative. Tell me what happens when the stone breaks and every gift in the realm wakes at once, in every frightened heart, in every drought year, in every war. You've seen what one untrained scribe can do by accident. Now multiply." He stepped closer. "I have read the accounts of Vel Sarenne. I have read them until the pages went soft. Children calling their drowned mothers up out of the floodwater because they wished so hard the wish *obliged them*. That is what freedom costs. That is what your third covenant—" he saw them flinch, and knew the guardian had spoken to them, knew everything, "—your arithmetic of kindness would spend. And I have stood in the Saltmere fen, Zephyrine. I have stood hip-deep in it on the anniversary, as the Provost requires, and I have felt under my feet the roofs of houses. I am not cruel. I am *remembering*."

"Then remember all of it," Zephyrine said, and their voice had changed—steadier now, the scribe's voice, the voice that reads a ledger aloud. "Remember who bound the stone. Remember whether the kings asked. Remember that the Garden gave freely and the crown took, and wrote 'salvation' on the receipt." They lifted their chin. "You preach drowning. I've seen the original covenant. The magic answered *wishes* because that was its nature—but the Garden taught consent. Asking. Sharing. The kings didn't bind a danger, Brother Cailean. They bound a *teacher*, and then they burned every school."

For a moment—for one suspended moment—Cailean felt the old vertigo, the one that visited him sometimes at night in the archive, when the sealed scrolls seemed less like locks than like doors. He mastered it. He had always mastered it. Faith was not the absence of doubt; faith was doubt, carried, like a wounded soldier, until the journey ended.

"You believe that," he said quietly. "That is what makes this bearable. If you were a cynic I would despise you. But you believe the Garden's promise, and I believe the fen's proof, and only one of us can be right, and God—" he almost laughed, "—God has arranged matters so that neither of us will live to see the refutation. So we must act on faith alone, both of us. Yours would drown a kingdom, Zephyrine. Mine would merely keep burning individuals, one at a time, carefully, with registries and warrants and folded hands. Weigh them. I have weighed them every night of my life. I know which scale pan touches the ground."

"And that's the horror of you," Zephyrine whispered. "Not that you're wrong. That you'd still be right about the weight if you were."

* * *

It was then that Cailean turned from the stone and addressed the whole sanctuary—Halvard in his shadow, the bound smugglers, his own twelve, the listening ruin itself—because a sermon is not finished until it has been offered to everyone, including the walls.

"Hear me, then, all of you," he said, and his voice rang off the vaulted dark. "I am not the Provost's knife. I am not the crown's hound. I have burned the guilty and the innocent and I have prayed over both, and I tell you the truth that the temple has hidden even from its own priests: we are not protecting the realm *from* magic. We are protecting the realm from *grief*. From the grief of the drowned, wearing our bodies, doing our arithmetic. The binding is a scar, and scars are not sins. And this place—" he gestured at the singing stone, at the light falling through the broken roof, "—this place is not a refuge. It is the wound before the scar. Open it, and the bleeding starts again, and it will not stop at borders or registries or the careful lines we have drawn for two hundred years."

He drew the consecrated blade from his belt—the binding-iron, quenched in the fen-water itself, the only steel permitted to touch the covenant stone—and held it low, point down, a preacher's posture.

"Zephyrine Dravenmoor. You stand at the heart of the Garden with a stolen stone and a theory of mercy. I stand here with nine thousand dead beneath my feet and a duty I did not choose but will not abandon. One of us must yield." He raised the blade. "I have come too far, and buried too many, and remembered too faithfully, to be the one who yields."

Above them, through the broken vault, the light shifted—and the guardian moved in it, vast and slow, a presence like weather deciding—and the covenant stone beneath Zephyrine's palm began, softly, unmistakably, to sing.

Cailean tightened his grip and stepped forward, and found, to his surprise and without any surprise at all, that Halvard had moved between them.
