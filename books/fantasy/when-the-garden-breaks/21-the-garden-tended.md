## 21. The Garden, Tended

Ossa Vane had never been good at lying still.

The wound in her side had other opinions. Brother Cailean's man had put a blade between her ribs—not deep enough to kill, deep enough to argue—and the Garden's magic had done the rest, knitting flesh with a warmth that felt less like healing and more like being argued with by something very old and very patient. Three weeks of bed-rest in a sanctuary that had no beds until she'd demanded them, and Ossa lay on a straw pallet in what used to be the Garden's infirmary, staring at a ceiling where vines had grown through stone and bloomed out of season.

She hated it. She was also, if she was honest with herself—and she tried to be, at least once a day—grateful for it.

"You're scowling at the ceiling again," said the girl who brought her broth.

"I'm negotiating with it."

"It's winning."

"It always does." Ossa pushed herself up on one elbow, hissed, and accepted the bowl. The girl was called Merrin, fourteen years old, gifted with a small talent for making things grow crooked and stubborn, which Ossa privately considered the most useful kind of gift there was. She'd come over the pass three weeks ago with frostbitten toes and a story about soldiers burning a farmstead, and now she brought broth to a smuggler twice her age like it was an ordinary Tuesday.

Nothing here was ordinary yet. That was the whole point of it.

Ossa ate, dressed, and did what she did every morning: took up her charcoal and her roll of treated hide and went back to work. Because the Garden had deep ways—tunnels beneath tunnels, passages older than the covenant itself, some drowned, some collapsed, some that went places she hadn't mapped yet because the walls seemed to rearrange themselves when nobody watched—and someone had to map them before winter came down off the Thornfells and started killing stragglers on the high roads.

Someone had to make sure the ones coming in could find their way.

* * *

The first hour belonged to the lower galleries. Ossa walked them slow, favoring her left side, counting paces out of habit even though the charcoal did the counting for her if you let it—the Garden liked to be drawn; she'd figured that out early, figured out that ink dried faster here, that charcoal lines came out truer than any hand had a right to make them. The place cooperated with cartographers. It wanted to be known, maybe. Or it wanted its refugees to stop getting lost in the dark and waking the things that slept down there.

There were things that slept down there. She'd met two. They were mostly polite.

Today the third gallery was dry, which it hadn't been last week—a spring had rerouted itself overnight, and Ossa stood at the edge of the old flood channel with her hands on her hips and revised her whole western section. "You could've mentioned this," she told the water, which gurgled in a way she chose to interpret as apology.

"Talking to the plumbing again?"

Zephyrine Dravenmoor came around the corner carrying a lantern they didn't need—their own light had gotten stronger since the reforging, a faint gold shimmer under the skin that made shadows behave oddly around them—but carried anyway, because they said people found the glow unsettling and the lantern made it feel more like company than spectacle.

Ossa grunted. "Your plumbing moved."

"The Garden's allowed. It was here first." Zephyrine crouched at the edge of the channel, and Ossa watched them look at the new-cut water with an expression she was still learning to read: not the scribe's careful blankness she'd first met in Grell's Hollow, not the half-dead awe of the reforging, but something quieter. Ownership without possession. The look of a person standing in a house they'd inherited and deciding, room by room, what it would become.

"Third gallery runs east now," Ossa said. "Which means your classroom floods by midwinter unless we divert it, and I can divert it, but I'll need three strong backs and someone who doesn't mind getting wet."

"Merrin won't mind. Merrin will enjoy it."

"Merrin enjoys everything. It's suspicious."

Zephyrine laughed—that was still new too, the laughing. In Grell's Hollow they'd been all economy and watchfulness, a person built like a locked drawer. Now the drawer stood open and there was actually stuff inside, and most of it, Ossa was discovering, was decent.

"I brought bread," Zephyrine said. "And a question. The family from the Ashvale valleys—the ones who came in yesterday with the twins?"

"The loud twins."

"They're not loud, they're *resonant*. Their gift is sound. Everything about them is sound." Zephyrine broke the loaf and handed half over. "Their mother asked me whether the children would have to register. Old habit. She asked it the way you ask if the floor's going to give way—already braced for the answer."

"And what'd you tell her?"

"That the only ledger in this place is the one I'm writing myself, and no name goes into it unwillingly." Zephyrine chewed thoughtfully. "She cried. Then she helped me stack firewood for two hours. I think the crying and the firewood were the same thing, somehow."

Ossa nodded. She knew the type—knew it from decades of moving people through bad country. The ones who'd been hunted longest needed a task before they could accept a kindness. Give them something to carry and they could believe the hand offering it wasn't going to turn into a fist.

"They'll want passage records eventually," she said. "Routes in. Safe houses on the approach. If this place is going to be a refuge instead of a rumor, someone's got to build the road behind every person who arrives, so the next one can follow it."

"That's what you're doing."

"That's what I'm doing." She tapped the charcoal against her hide-roll. "Smuggler's arithmetic, scribe. You don't move cargo once. You move the route. The route outlives everybody."

Zephyrine looked at her for a moment with that gold-lit gaze, and Ossa got the distinct feeling of being read like a document—which she tolerated from exactly one person in the world, and only because she'd once held a knife to that person's throat over a contract, and they'd talked her down instead of dying, and some debts you paid by letting them know you.

"Thank you," Zephyrine said.

"Don't make it weird. Eat your bread."

* * *

Midmorning, Ossa climbed to the upper sanctuary, because the lesson happened at midmorning and she'd stopped pretending she wasn't going to attend.

It wasn't much of a classroom. A stretch of ruined colonnade where the roof had fallen inward centuries ago and the Garden had grown a canopy instead—living boughs woven through broken arches, leaves filtering the mountain light into something green and slow. Twenty-odd seats scavenged or carved from fallen stone. And at the front, Zephyrine, standing in the exact posture of a temple lecturer, hands folded, voice pitched to reach the back row—the posture of the institution that had owned them for twenty years, worn now like a coat turned inside out, showing all the seams.

Ossa sat on the wall at the edge, where she always sat, where the charcoal and the hide-roll lay across her knees, because she'd discovered that watching Zephyrine teach taught her things about the Garden no survey could.

"Yesterday," Zephyrine was saying, "we talked about listening. Today I want to talk about the difference between a gift and a debt."

An old man in the second row—Harrowgate, a former tax clerk whose gift was knowing the weight of anything he touched—raised his hand like he was still asking permission from a magistrate twenty years dead. "Isn't that the same thing? What the crown said. That the gift was a debt owed to the realm."

"The crown said a lot of things." Zephyrine's voice stayed gentle, but something underneath it didn't, and Ossa saw the newer arrivals—there were four this week—sit up straighter. "Here's what the mountain showed me, in the pass. Here's what the guardian showed me, in the heart of this place. Before the first kings, magic wasn't property. It was weather. It fell on everyone equally, and mostly everyone ignored it, the way you ignore weather. Then the kings bound it into the stone. Took the rain and put it in a barrel, and charged admission for the drinking. And the temple—" a pause, and Ossa watched Zephyrine choose honesty over comfort, watched them do it the way she watched climbers commit to a hold, "—the temple kept the barrel. Swept the floor around it. Wrote the ledgers that pretended nothing had ever fallen from the sky."

Silence in the colonnade. Somewhere above, a bird with too much opinion sang three notes.

"A debt is something taken from you that must be repaid," Zephyrine said. "A gift is something given to you that asks only to be passed on. For a thousand years we were taught our own birthrights were debts—owed to the crown, owed to Saint Verane, owed to a God whose silence we mistook for judgment. The third covenant says otherwise. It says the weight is real—we all feel it, don't we?" Nods around the colonnade; Ossa felt it too, faintly, though she wasn't gifted, just adjacent, just contracted. "But a weight shared isn't a debt. It's a load-bearing wall. It holds up a house."

Merrin, in the front row, raised her hand without waiting. "What happens when somebody doesn't consent? The covenant needs consent, right? What if somebody says no?"

"Then they say no," Zephyrine said simply. "And we find another way to help them, or we let them go in peace. Consent that can't be withheld isn't consent. It's just a softer chain." A pause. "I spent twenty years wearing chains soft enough to forget. I'd rather die than hand them to anyone else, even prettier ones."

Ossa looked down at her mapping. Her eyes had gone blurry, which was ridiculous, and she blamed the altitude.

* * *

Afternoon belonged to the deep ways again, and the deep ways today had opinions.

She'd been charting a branch off the fourth gallery—an airless-feeling corridor the Garden had clearly sealed once upon a time and recently unsealed, as if making room. The air in it tasted like the moment before a storm. Forty paces in, her lantern flame bent sideways, toward a wall with no draft, and Ossa stood very still and listened to the professional instinct that had kept her alive through thirty years of smuggling: *when the environment tells you something, believe it.*

The wall was thin. Behind the wall was a hollow, and the hollow was full of—

She pressed her palm flat to the stone, and the stone showed her.

Not visions, exactly. More like memory stored in masonry. A room. Rows of alcoves. Names—hundreds of names, thousands—carved into plaques of white wood, and each plaque humming faintly with the resonance of the person it commemorated. A reliquary of the bound. The place, Ossa understood suddenly, where the first covenant had recorded everyone it had ever chained. Not honored. *Filed.*

And beneath the hum, older, almost subsonic: a second layer of names, scratched out, overwritten, the originals of the ones who had refused. Who had said *no* to the first binding, and been erased from the record entirely, and whose refusal had survived anyway, in the stone, because stone remembers what ink is told to forget.

Ossa sat down on the cold floor with her back against the humming wall and stayed there a long while.

When she finally came out, dusk was pooling in the galleries, and Halvard was waiting at the junction with a lamp and the particular stillness of a man trying not to loom.

He'd changed since the siege. Everyone said so; Ossa preferred evidence. The Inquisitor's coat was gone, replaced by plain wool, but he wore it like a uniform anyway—you couldn't take the structure out of some men, only point it somewhere better. His face was still the face of a man who'd burned Grell's Hollow, and both of them knew it, and neither of them pretended otherwise, and somehow that had become the basis of a working relationship Ossa would have described, six months ago, as impossible.

"You've been down there four hours," he said.

"The wall talks."

His jaw tightened. He'd been a hunter of the gifted long enough that casual talk of living walls still landed wrong, and honest enough now to say so. "What does it say?"

"Names. The old registry. Every gifted soul the first covenant bound, and the ones who refused written underneath." She rolled up her hide-map and slung it across her shoulder. "Your Inquisition kept lists, Halvard. So did the temple. This place kept both, and then kept the truth under both. There's a lesson in that for somebody."

"For me," he said. Not bitterly. Just accurately.

"For you," Ossa agreed. "Also, your warden rounds are late. The eastern gate seal flickers at sundown—it did it yesterday too—and if you keep missing it, one of these days something polite but enormous is going to wander in and eat the goats."

"I'll check it tonight."

"Check it *now*, the goats are already nervous."

He almost smiled. It was a strange thing to witness, like watching a fortress attempt a dance. He turned toward the eastern stairs, then paused.

"Ossa. The names in the wall. The ones who refused." His voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet when he was handling something explosive. "Is Zephyrine's family among them?"

She hadn't checked. She realized, with a small internal jolt, that she'd been avoiding checking, the way you avoid pressing a bruise. "I'll look tomorrow," she said. "If they are—"

"If they are, they should be in the new covenant's record too. Refusal is a contribution." He said it steadily, and meant it, and went up the stairs with his lamp.

Ossa stood alone in the junction a moment, listening to the Garden breathe around her—the drip of rerouted water, the far-off sound of Zephyrine's evening students laughing at something, the deep subterranean hum of ten thousand names keeping their vigil in the dark.

*Refusal is a contribution.* The man who'd executed a dyer in Grell's Hollow for refusing to run had learned to honor refusal. People were capable of it, apparently. Change. She filed the observation under *things that are true even when inconvenient* and went to find supper.

* * *

Evening, and the infirmary she'd been released from, and Merrin sitting cross-legged on Ossa's pallet uninvited, sharpening Ossa's spare knife with the intense concentration of a child doing something forbidden.

"Put that down before you lose a finger."

"I'm helping."

"You're sharpening a smuggler's blade with a growth-gift. The steel's going to sprout."

Merrin looked at the knife with sudden suspicion. Ossa took it away, and the girl scooted over to make room, which was how Ossa had ended up, over three weeks, with a shadow.

"They say you mapped the whole pass," Merrin said. "Before the war. Before the hunt. That you moved forty people through the Thornfells in one winter and lost none."

"Lost one goat. Still bitter about it."

"What was it like? Being free? On the road?"

Ossa considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. Outside the infirmary's broken window, the sun was going down over the sundered Garden, and the whole ruin caught the light the way a cup catches wine—all those broken arches filling up with gold. Down in the colonnade, lamplighters were lighting lamps that didn't need lighting, because the light was nice. Refugees were arriving in ones and twos along the routes she'd rebuilt, following her charcoal lines home. Somewhere in the scriptorium, Zephyrine was writing—she'd seen the stacks of pages, the new covenant going into words, the record that would eventually go out into the realm's official archives, signed with a true name.

"It was cold," Ossa said. "It was terrifying. Half the time I was sure we'd all die, and the other half I was sure we'd be caught, and the overlap was considerable." She leaned back against the warm stone. "And every single morning, I woke up and nobody owned me. You get used to the cold. You never get used to that. Don't let anyone tell you different."

Merrin was quiet a moment. "The lady from Ashvale said Zephyrine's writing it all down. Everything. The covenant, in the realm's own records, in their own hand."

"So I hear."

"Do you think anyone will listen? The chanceries, the courts? After everything?"

Ossa thought about the wall full of refused names. About Halvard climbing the eastern stairs to tend a gate. About a barrel that had been opened, and weather that had remembered how to fall.

"Chanceries listen to documents," she said. "And there's about to be a very good one." She reached over and blew out the lamp, because the sunset was doing fine work on its own. "Now get out of my infirmary and go to bed, sprout. Dawn comes early, and the third gallery won't map itself."

Merrin went. Ossa stayed a while longer in the gold light, side aching pleasantly, hand resting on the roll of maps that was slowly becoming the most important document she'd ever touched—not because of what she'd drawn on it, but because of who was walking it.

First season of something planted, she thought. Frost would come. Frost always came.

But the roots, she was fairly sure, had gone down deep enough.

Tomorrow she'd open the wall of names, and find out whose refusal ran in the Dravenmoor blood—and whether the record wanted one more line added, in a smuggler's honest hand.
