## 1. The Taster's Portion

The bitterness arrived in the fourth course, folded into the honey like a secret told too quietly to be overheard.

Juniper Yarrow caught it the way he caught everything: with his tongue, his patience, and a face that gave away nothing at all. He stood at the left shoulder of Prince Aldric's chair in the great hall of Vellamere Palace, where forty candles burned in honor of the boy's fourteenth birthday and the winter wind worried at the stained glass like a dog at a bone. The prince was laughing at something his fool had said, all elbows and coltish grace, and Juniper — small, gray-suited, unremarkable as a shadow on the wainscoting — lifted the silver tasting spoon from the fourth course and let a single measure of the honey-glazed quince rest upon his tongue.

He held it there. He always held it there.

Ten heartbeats for the first impressions: sweetness, of course, the heavy floral sweetness of mountain honey cut with citron peel. Then the warmth of it, the way good glaze should cling. Then, arriving late and shy, beneath the sweetness like a footstep behind you on a stair —

Bitterness. Faint. Green. Cold at the edges, the way certain roots were cold.

Juniper did not swallow. He turned the morsel over against his palate with the deliberation of a jeweler turning a stone toward the light, and he named what he tasted without naming it aloud, because naming things aloud was how tasters died young. *Wolfsbane root, perhaps. Or its cousin from the southern valleys — the one without a common name, the one Master Corvane kept in the third drawer of the black cabinet, the one whose bitterness was polite enough to wait until you'd finished smiling.*

Not enough to kill. Not yet. Enough to season.

He removed the spoon from his mouth with a soft click and set it down on the salver held by the under-steward, and in the same motion he tipped the entire fourth course — platter, glaze, garnish, and all — into the copper failing-vessel hidden beneath the serving table's linen skirt. It was a maneuver he had performed nine hundred times in eleven years of service, smooth as breath, invisible as breath. A kitchen boy staggered past with the fifth course; Juniper stepped into the gap the platter left behind; by the time anyone looked, the table showed nothing but abundance, and the abundance showed nothing but itself.

"Is the quince not to your liking, master taster?" asked Lord Havistock, who presided over the far end of the high table and enjoyed needling the guild's man the way cats enjoy needling things smaller than themselves.

"The quince is excellent, my lord," Juniper said. His voice was the voice he used for such occasions: pleasant, thin, forgettable as water. "I found one piece over-glazed. I've set it aside."

Havistock snorted and returned to his wine. No one else looked up. That was the craft of it, Juniper thought, watching the fifth course — braised pheasant in cream — travel safely to the prince's plate. The best poisoning is the one no one notices, and so the best *un*poisoning must be quieter still. He had saved three lives this year alone: a merchant magistrate, a visiting duchess, and once, memorably, the entire choir of Saint Ellery's, who had been scheduled to sing at a wedding where the almond cakes carried more than almonds. None of them knew. None of them would ever know. Gratitude required a name, and Juniper Yarrow had spent eleven years making sure his name stuck to nothing.

He was, by design, the smallest man in every room he entered.

* * *

The feast ran two hours longer than it needed to, because feasts always do. Juniper tasted eleven courses in total: the pheasant, clean; the chestnut soup, clean but oversalted, which he noted for the ledger out of professional completeness rather than suspicion; the sugared plums, which nearly undid him with their perfection and which he tasted twice just to be fair. The prince ate everything set before him and lived through all of it, flushed and laughing, and when the cake came — tall as a cathedral spire, white as the first snow — Juniper tasted it crumb by crumb, layer by layer, the frosting separately from the sponge, the candied violets separately from both.

Clean. All of it clean.

Which was, in its own way, the most interesting finding of the night.

Juniper stood through the toasts with his hands folded and his eyes down, the posture of a servant who has made himself furniture. Around him the hall roared and glittered. The old king raised his cup to his grandson with a hand that trembled only slightly, and the courtiers roared louder, and somewhere above them all the banners of Vellamere stirred in the heat of forty candles, and Juniper Yarrow thought, not for the first time and with no particular self-pity, *none of these people could pick me out of a line of three.*

It was a strange thing, being unseen on purpose. He had been fourteen himself once — the same age as the prince, though the resemblance ended there — when Master Corvane had found him half-starved in the fish market and said, in that voice like a door closing gently, *"You have a careful tongue, boy. Careful tongues are worth feeding."* Eleven years since. Eleven years of training: the bitter herbs, the sweet poisons, the ones that numbed and the ones that burned and the one — he had never learned its name — that Master Corvane dosed him with every month, a thimbleful in dark wine, saying only, *"Drink. It builds what you'll need."* And Juniper had drunk, because the guild had fed him and housed him and taught him the only trade at which he excelled, and because a ward does not question the hand that feeds him.

Especially not when the hand feeds him poison.

He felt it now, faintly, the way one feels an old scar in cold weather: the monthly dose sitting in his blood like a tenant who has paid his rent and means to stay. Immunity, Corvane called it. Armor, Corvane called it. Juniper had stopped asking questions about it years ago, the way one stops asking questions about weather.

The last toast was drunk. The prince was carried off to bed by his governess, protesting joyfully. The candles guttered. And Juniper gathered his notes — he kept them in his head, always, never on paper where paper could be found — and slipped out through the servants' corridor into the cold.

* * *

The Guildhouse of Small Mercies sat in the oldest quarter of the city, wedged between a chandler's and a shuttered temple, its facade so deliberately plain that visitors walked past it twice before finding the door. Inside, it was warm and dim and smelled of beeswax and dried herbs, and it was the only place in the world where Juniper's footsteps made any sound worth hearing.

He went first to the scriptorium, because the ledgers waited, and the ledgers always waited.

They lived in a locked cabinet behind the archivist's desk, and they were the true heart of the guild — not the tasters, not the poisoners, not the network of informants threaded through every noble house in the kingdom, but the books. Seven volumes, bound in green leather gone soft with handling, recording thirty years of the guild's commerce. Most of it was ordinary: fees for tastings, retainers from paranoid lords, the going rate for a discreet antidote. But some entries were otherwise. Some entries were the reason the cabinet locked.

Juniper took out Volume Six — his assigned volume, the one he balanced each month — and set it under the lamp, and began.

*One bottled echo,* sold to Lady Merrow of the Marches, who wished to hear her dead son laugh again and had paid a sum Juniper did not permit himself to imagine. Entry verified. Seal intact. The echo, according to the delivery agent, still laughed sometimes at dusk, though it had begun to sound tired.

*A year of borrowed luck,* drawn from a farmer who had sold it willingly and wept while signing, and purchased by a duelist who would fight eleven matches on it and win ten. Entry verified. Interest accruing.

And then, near the bottom of the page, the entry he had read a hundred times and never once understood:

*One soul. Sold. Buyer: sealed by order of the Master.*

The ink was Master Corvane's own — Juniper knew the hand the way he knew his own heartbeat, the narrow letters, the downward slash of the final stroke. The date was thirty years old. The seller's signature beside it was smudged, water-damaged, half-legible — but legible enough, it had always been legible enough, and Juniper had spent eleven years not looking directly at it the way one does not look directly at the sun.

Tonight, for reasons he could not have explained — the politeness of the bitterness in the quince, perhaps, or the sight of the prince alive and fourteen and laughing — he looked.

*Yarrow.*

His own family name, written in a stranger's hand, on a line that recorded the sale of a human soul.

Juniper sat very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked under the night porter's rounds, and the sound seemed to come from very far away, from another country, another life. He read the entry again. And again. The letters did not rearrange themselves into innocence. They sat there, patient as poison, and waited for him to understand them.

He did not understand them. Not yet. But the dread arrived anyway, quiet and complete, settling into his chest like the monthly dose settles into the blood — familiar, unwelcome, paying its rent.

He closed the ledger. He locked the cabinet. He climbed the stairs to his narrow room under the eaves, where he kept his few possessions: three shirts, a whetstone, a tin of willow-bark tea, and — wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of a drawer he never opened — a single recipe card in his mother's handwriting, faded almost to nothing, for a garden preserve she had called *summer, put by.*

He had been seven years old when she died. Or when she went away. The guild had never been precise about which, and he had never been brave enough to press.

Juniper lay awake in the dark for a long time, listening to the winter wind worry the roof tiles, and told himself what he had told himself for eleven years: *Stay small. Stay unseen. Ask nothing. Owe nothing. The smallest man in the room survives the longest.*

But the name on the ledger page followed him down into sleep, and in his dreams it was written in his mother's hand instead of the Master's, and it was not smudged at all.

* * *

Three days later, the fish market.

It was the kind of morning that made the city honest: hard frost, hard light, gulls screaming their grievances over the gutting tables. Juniper moved through the crowd on his weekly errand — salt cod for the Guildhouse kitchens, and no other purpose, and certainly no habit of taking the long route along the harbor wall where the light came off the water in sheets — when a man stumbled out of the alley by the net-menders' shed and fell against him.

Frostbite had gotten to the stranger first. Whatever else had gotten to him second. He was dressed for a long road in a short winter: snow-crusted boots, a courier's satchel frozen stiff across his chest, lips split and gray. His hands clawed at Juniper's coat with the desperate strength of the dying, and his breath came out in rags.

"You're—" The man's eyes, wild and glassy, searched Juniper's face. "Guild colors. You're theirs." A cough, wet and terrible. "Then listen. Before the winter finishes falling—"

"I'll fetch a physicker," Juniper began, already turning, already reaching for the anonymity of the crowd—

The man's grip ironed him in place. Up close, Juniper could see the frost had bloomed *inward*, silvering the inside of the man's collar, the inside of his ears, as if the cold were coming out of him rather than in. He had seen strange things in eleven years of impossible trades. He had never seen this.

"No physicker," the courier rasped. "No time. There's a map. In the satchel. Wax-sealed — don't break the seal, it opens for—" Another cough bent him double. When he straightened, his eyes had cleared in a way that frightened Juniper more than the frost had. "The court beneath the mountain keeps souls, taster. Kept ones. Bought ones. And there's one there — there's a name there you'll want to read."

"What name?" Juniper heard himself ask, and hated how fast it came, how hungry.

The courier smiled. It was a terrible smile, cracked and blue-lipped, and it held something that might have been pity. "Yarrow," he said.

The word went through Juniper like a blade through water — no resistance, all the way down.

"Take it," the man whispered, pressing the satchel's strap into Juniper's hands. "Before the winter finishes falling. She's waiting. She's been waiting thirty years, and the vault keeps what's kept, but doors—" His voice failed. His knees failed. Juniper caught him, or tried to, and lowered him onto the cobbles as gently as he had ever done anything in his life.

Around them the market went on shouting prices. Gulls wheeled. The frost-light lay on the dead man's face like a sheet laid ready.

"Who are you?" Juniper asked the body quietly. "Who sent you?"

There was no answer, and there was no time for one, because down the quay the city watch had seen the crowd gathering — two cloaked figures, truncheons at their belts, moving with the unhurried certainty of men who find bodies for a living and ask questions after. Questions, Juniper knew, that would begin with *who was he speaking to* and end somewhere very bad for a guild man found holding a dead courier's contraband.

He did not think. Thinking was for later, thinking was for the cellar and the lamp and the ledger. Now there was only the strap in his hands and the watch fifty paces off and eleven years of training in the art of disappearing.

Juniper Yarrow slid the satchel beneath his coat, stepped backward into the fish-market crowd, and was gone — small, gray, unremarkable, carrying the weight of a dead man's errand against his ribs like a second heart.

Behind him, unnoticed by everyone, the first flakes of the long winter's snow had begun to fall.
