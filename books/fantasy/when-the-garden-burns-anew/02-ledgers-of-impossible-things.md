## 2. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The counting room of the Guild of Small Mercies smelled of tallow, dried ink, and the faint mineral tang of things that had been kept too long in jars. Juniper Yarrow arrived at his desk an hour before dawn, as he did every quarter-day, and lit the single lamp he was permitted. The flame caught and steadied, throwing a small warm circle across the ledger before him — a circle just large enough for one man, one book, and one lifetime of careful not-looking.

He took off his coat, hung it on the peg behind the door, rolled his sleeves to the elbow, and set out his instruments the way a surgeon sets out knives: three pens of different nibs, a pot of black ink, a pot of red, a blotter worn soft as cloth, and the small brass scale that weighed nothing so much as it weighed intention.

Balancing the guild's accounts was his second profession, though no one paid him for it. It had begun years ago as penance-work, the sort of quiet labor given to members who had failed at something — in his case, failing to die convincingly during his certification trials, which had embarrassed the examiners — and it had become, over time, the architecture of his days. Numbers did not lie to him. Numbers did not ask him to taste anything. Numbers sat still.

The autumn ledger lay open to its final pages. He dipped the black pen and began.

*Item: one bottled echo, sold to Lady Vessamine of the Hill Houses, contents verified, vessel intact, buyer satisfied.* He wrote the entry in his small, even hand, each letter the same height as its neighbors, and beside it entered the price: forty crowns and a favor unnamed, which was how favors were always priced, because named favors could be refused and unnamed ones only waited.

The echo itself sat on the shelf above his desk, in a stoppered jar of green glass, and even now, even muffled, it murmured. If you held the jar close to your ear you could hear a woman laughing in a garden that no longer existed, laughing at something someone had said, the sound going around and around inside the glass like water finding its level. Juniper had catalogued it three years ago and had made the mistake, once, of listening longer than the task required. The laugh had gotten into him somewhere beneath the ribs and stayed there, the way cold gets into stone. He did not listen anymore. But he knew it was there. That was the trouble with this work: you came to know what the guild kept, and knowing was a kind of carrying.

*Item: one year of borrowed luck, drawn from the luck of the Ferrymen's Row dice-houses, lent to Master Corvane against the winter contracts, interest accruing.*

This one he wrote more slowly. Borrowed luck was the guild's steadiest trade and its ugliest. Luck, properly understood, was not a substance but a balance, and every year borrowed from one place arrived somewhere else owing. The dice-men of Ferryman's Row had been losing steadily since midsummer — three suicides among them already, though the ledgers recorded these as *collateral adjustments* — while the guild's contracts had turned golden as honey. Juniper entered the figures without comment. Comment was not his function. His function was the sum at the bottom of the page, and the sum must always come true.

That was the creed he had built for himself, brick by brick, in the years since they'd taken him in: *stay small, keep the sums true, save what lives can be saved quietly, and want nothing large enough to be seen wanting it.* A taster who wanted things died of them. An accountant who wanted things cooked them. He had watched it happen to better men than himself — well, no, he corrected the thought, dipping the pen again; he had never actually watched it happen to better men, because there were very few men in the guild at all, and none of them better, only louder. Loudness was not goodness. He had learned that young.

The lamp guttered as the first grey of morning touched the high window, and Juniper trimmed the wick and turned the page.

And stopped.

The next entry was written in a different hand. Not the archivist's neat slant, not the deputy's cramped scrawl, but a broad, deliberate script with flourishes at the ascenders, ink gone brownish with age — thirty years old, if the binding thread of the page was any guide, and Juniper's eye for bindings was nearly as good as his tongue for poisons. It was the Master's hand. Everyone in the guild knew Master Corvane's handwriting the way children know their father's footsteps on the stair.

The entry read:

*Item: one soul, sold entire, provenance sealed by order of the Master. Buyer: the Court Beneath. Price: charter perpetual, guild protected against season, crown, and grave. Witnessed by none. Sealed by my own hand, and let no man after me break it.*

Below the words, where the seller's signature belonged, the wax seal had been pressed flat and scraped — deliberately defaced, the name lifted off the parchment so thoroughly that only the ghost of it remained, a shadow of letters in the grain of the paper like a bruise under skin.

Juniper sat very still.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above him, the guild house woke in its usual order: the kitchen fire first, then the cistern pump, then boots on the stairs, the day's first apprentices grumbling their way down toward the tasting hall. Ordinary sounds. Sounds he had heard ten thousand mornings. This morning they sounded far away, as if heard through the green glass of a jar.

He should turn the page. That was the discipline of it — the whole point of the work was that the accountant did not linger, did not wonder, did not *listen to the jar*. Every member of the guild learned early which doors stayed shut, and the sealed entries were the deepest of those doors. There were perhaps nine of them in all the ledgers, scattered across sixty years like teeth pulled from a jaw. He had catalogued their locations, because cataloguing was his nature, but he had never once broken one open. Breaking seals was how accountants disappeared. He had seen the empty desks. He had inherited two of them.

But his hand did not move to turn the page.

Instead it moved — slowly, as though the hand belonged to someone else, some braver and more foolish ancestor of his — to the brass scale. He cut a sliver of the sealing wax's residue with his penknife, no larger than a grain of rice, and set it in the pan. Then, from the drawer where he kept his working tools, he took the little vial of reagent he used for testing the prince's food: a clear spirit that bloomed certain colors in the presence of certain substances. It was meant for arsenic and worse. It had never once occurred to him, until this exact moment, that it might be meant for anything else.

Three drops. The wax sliver drank them in.

The color that rose was not the sickly green of poison nor the dull blue of forgery. It was a deep, warm gold, the gold of late afternoon through honey, and Juniper knew it instantly, the way you know your own name spoken in a dark room, because he had tested it a thousand times in a thousand kitchens.

It was the color of a bloodline. Apothecaries called it *the family mark*: blood and wax and certain rare resins carried the signature of the line that made them, and no two lines matched. Tasters used it to verify hostages and marriage-children. Guilds used it to verify heirs.

Juniper stared at the gold bloom fading in the pan, and felt the well-ordered floor of his life give its first small, terrible creak.

Because he knew that signature. Not the defaced wax — the *hand*. He had grown up, in whatever passed for growing up in the Guild of Small Mercies, with a single relic of the woman who had borne him: a recipe card, soft as cloth from handling, written in a gardener's careful script. *For the winter table, when everything else is grey.* He kept it folded inside the back cover of his personal notebook, and he had copied its handwriting so many times in practice, in longing, in the wordless way orphans imitate the dead, that he could have reproduced it blindfolded.

The flourishes at the ascenders matched. The peculiar backward loop on the descenders matched. The way the letters leaned slightly leftward, as if walking into wind, matched.

His mother had signed the sale of her own soul.

Or — the other thought arrived unbidden, cold as the fish-market fog outside — someone had signed it *for* her. Someone with access to her hand, her wax, her trust. Someone whose broad deliberate script now sat above hers on the same yellowed page, sealing the entry, ordering it never broken.

Master Corvane had raised him. Fed him, schooled him, taught him the tongues of plants and the patience of poisons, given him a bed and a trade and a name when the world had offered him none. And Master Corvane had been, thirty years ago, exactly the kind of man who stood to gain a *charter perpetual* from the Court Beneath — protection against season, crown, and grave — and exactly the kind of man who would know what a grieving, desperate, dying gardener might sign away, and what her orphaned son might be worth afterward, kept close, kept grateful, kept *small*.

Juniper looked down at his own hands, steady on the desk, hands trained never to tremble, and hated how steady they were.

The sounds of the waking guild continued overhead. Boots. Voices. The clatter of the tasting hall being laid for breakfast, where he would sit within the hour and sample porridge and tea and preserved plums for the senior members, and find them all clean, and say nothing, and be thanked by no one, and go on being invisible, which had been, until this moment, the whole of his ambition.

He became aware, gradually, of a sound beneath the other sounds — a faint, rhythmic murmur from the shelf above his desk. The bottled echo, stirring in its jar. The woman laughing in a garden that no longer existed. He had told himself for three years that the laugh meant nothing to him, that it was inventory, that it was a number in a column.

Now, with his mother's handwriting drying gold in the pan before him, he let himself hear it fully for the first time.

It was not a stranger's laugh.

He closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child, and sat in the grey light with his hands flat on the cover, feeling the first crack run through the well-ordered life like frost through mortar — not loud, not yet, but deep, and widening, and impossible to unmake.

Above him, unhurried, familiar, came a footstep he knew as well as his own heartbeat: the Master's tread on the stairs, descending toward the counting room, toward the ledgers, toward him.

Juniper blew out the lamp, folded the recipe card back into its hiding place, and put on the face he wore for tastings — blank, modest, forgettable.

The door handle turned.

* * *

"Yarrow." Master Corvane filled the doorway the way winter fills a valley — not entering so much as arriving, bringing his own temperature with him. He was a broad man gone silver at the temples, with the heavy calm of someone who had never once needed to raise his voice, because the world had learned to lower its own around him. His eyes went, as they always did, first to the ledgers, then to Juniper, weighing both. "Quarter-day already. You keep better count of the calendar than the calendar does."

"The sums don't balance themselves, Master."

"No," Corvane said softly. "They don't. That is why I keep you." He crossed the room and stood over the desk, and for a moment — a moment Juniper would take out later and examine the way one examines a wound — the old man's gaze rested on the autumn ledger, on the exact spot where the sealed entry slept beneath the newer pages. Something moved behind his eyes, brief as a fish under ice.

"You've reached the old entries," Corvane said. It was not a question.

"I reach them every quarter-day, Master. They're part of the count."

"They are." Corvane smiled, and it was the smile Juniper had trusted all his life, warm as a hearth, and for the first time Juniper saw the hearth for what it had always also been: a thing that burns. "Some entries are old because they are finished, boy. Finished things are best left counted and closed. You understand closure. You're the most closed man I've ever known." He said it fondly. He laid one heavy hand briefly on Juniper's shoulder, and the weight of it was paternal and proprietary in equal measure, the weight of a man touching something he owned. "Balance the books. Come to supper tonight. I have a new vintage I want your tongue on."

He left. The door closed. The footsteps climbed away.

Juniper sat unmoving for a long time, listening to the echo laugh softly in its jar, and understanding, at last, with the particular clarity of the poisoned, that he had been tasting for the wrong master all along.

Tonight, then. A new vintage. A tongue to try it.

He opened the ledger again, and beneath the sealed entry, in his own small even hand, he wrote a private notation in the margin — a taster's mark, the one he used for dishes he did not yet trust:

*Hold this portion. Watch who serves it.*
