## 3. A Stranger at the Dock Gate

The fish market smelled of brine and betrayal, which was to say it smelled like every honest place in Saltmere, and Juniper Yarrow had learned long ago that honesty was mostly a matter of what you were willing to smell.

He came on the fourth day of the week, as he always did, when the herring came off the boats gray and silver and the gulls argued overhead like aldermen. He bought a pound of smoked mackerel from Marta, who had three teeth and no illusions about anyone. He let her cheat him by two coppers because being cheated was cheaper than being noticed. He kept his hood up against the wind coming off the harbor, and he kept his shoulders rounded, and he kept his face arranged into the particular blankness of a man whose business is nobody's business — the face of a taster, which is to say the face of a ghost with an appetite.

Thirty-one years old, and invisible in a city of forty thousand. It was not a failure of craft. It was the craft.

*Stay small,* his creed went, the one he'd never written down because writing things down was how they got read. *Taste everything. Commit to nothing. Be the man the room forgets before the door closes behind him.* The guild had raised him on that creed the way other orphans were raised on bread and lard, and for thirty years it had fed him, clothed him, kept him alive through poisons that would have felled a bull elephant, and asked nothing in return but his silence, his smallness, and his blood — a vial every month, drawn by the guild physician, poured into doses he was never permitted to see again.

He had never wondered where those doses went. That, too, was part of staying small.

Marta wrapped the mackerel in brown paper and overcharged him, and Juniper paid, and turned toward the dock gate with his collar up and his mind already on the ledgers waiting back at the guildhouse — the third-quarter accounts, the entry for a bottled echo that needed reconciling, the column of borrowed luck that never balanced quite right.

That was when the stranger fell against him.

Later, Juniper would replay the moment the way he replayed every death he'd failed to prevent: frame by frame, taste by taste. But in the moment itself there was only weight — sudden, staggering, a hundred and fifty pounds of frost-burned flesh slamming into his left side and sending the mackerel spinning into the gutter. He caught the man more out of reflex than mercy; a taster learns to catch falling things, cups and goblets and occasionally kings.

"Steady," Juniper said, the word automatic, flavorless. "Steady, friend."

The man was young. That struck Juniper first, even before the cold did — young, maybe twenty-two, with the wind-scoured cheeks of a road-runner and lips gone the blue-gray of old pewter. Frost had gotten into him somewhere far north of here; it sat in the creases of his eyes like salt, and when he exhaled, the breath came out faintly white despite the mild morning. His courier's satchel was crusted with ice. His fingers, when they closed around Juniper's forearm, were so cold they burned.

"You're—" The man's voice cracked like lake ice. "You're the one. The map said—"

"I think you've mistaken me for someone else." Juniper said it gently, automatically, the way he said everything. *Be the man the room forgets.*

"No." The courier shook his head, and something rattled in the motion, deep in his chest, wrong. "No mistake. I carried it eleven days. Through the pass. Through the—" A cough took him, wet and terrible, and when he pulled his glove away from his mouth there was blood on it, frozen mid-drip into tiny garnet beads. "Eleven days, and the cold followed me the whole way. It knows my name now. Do you understand? It *knows my name.*"

Juniper understood fever when he heard it. He also understood that the man's pulse, fluttering under the icy grip on his arm, was running thin as watered wine. Whatever this was, it wasn't only fever.

"You need a physician," Juniper said, and made the mistake of glancing toward the watch-post by the gate.

The courier saw him do it, and something desperate flared in the young man's eyes, and his grip tightened.

"No physicians. No watch. Listen to me." He dragged Juniper closer, close enough that Juniper could smell him beneath the frost — pine smoke, and ink, and underneath both a scent Juniper knew professionally and personally and had hoped never to encounter free in the open air: *winterbane*, the slow poison of the high passes, distilled from a flower that bloomed only in snow. Someone had dosed this man. Someone skilled. "They sent hunters after me. They'll come dressed as watchmen, or worse, they'll come dressed as friends. You have to take it. You have to be the one who—"

"Whoever you are—"

"*Yarrow.*" The name came out ragged, reverent, terrible. "Your name is Yarrow. I checked. Gods forgive me, I checked twice, because I prayed I was wrong."

The market noise seemed to fall away, the way sound does underwater. Gulls still wheeled. Herring still slapped across the scales. Somewhere a fishwife was shouting about the price of cod. And Juniper stood in the middle of all of it, holding a dying stranger who knew his name, feeling the first true crack run through thirty years of careful invisibility.

Nobody knew his name. That was the arrangement. That was the *creed*. The guild paid him in anonymity the way other employers paid in coin, and he had spent his whole life not spending it.

"How do you know my name?" he asked quietly.

"The ledger," the courier whispered. "I was the Undercourt's runner. Eleven days ago I was given a sealed packet and told: deliver it to the son of the gardener, the taster, the one called Yarrow, before the winter finishes falling. Before the winter finishes falling, do you hear me? That's not poetry. That's a *deadline*. When the last snow lands, the court wakes its winter, and everything the vault keeps goes past keeping." His breath fogged and hung between them, unnaturally slow, like smoke deciding whether to rise. "I opened it. On the fourth night, in the pass, I opened it, because a runner who doesn't know what he carries dies stupid, and I didn't want to die stupid."

"And what does it carry?"

The courier smiled then, and it was the worst thing Juniper had seen in a career of terrible sights — a smile of pure relief on a face going gray. "A map. And a name. The name of a soul sold to the court beneath the mountain, and the year it was sold, and the hand that sold it." His eyes, glassy with frost and dying, held Juniper's with terrible gentleness. "It's your mother's soul, taster. Sold thirty years ago. Sealed in the Master's own hand."

The world did not tilt. Juniper noted, distantly, that he was trained too well for the world to tilt. His heartbeat stayed level. His breathing stayed even. Only his hands betrayed him, going cold in a way that had nothing to do with the morning air, and only his mouth, which tasted suddenly of copper and old ash — the taste, he realized, of the garden fire he could barely remember, the one from before, from when he was four and his mother burned and no one would tell him why.

"She's alive?" he heard himself ask. "Down there. She's—"

"Kept." The courier sagged against him, and Juniper took his full weight without deciding to. "Souls don't die, taster. They get *kept*. In jars. In the dark. In summer that never ends, if the stories are true." A shudder ran through him. "Take it. Take it before—"

Footsteps. Boots on cobbles, several pairs, moving with the unhurried purpose of men who are not hurrying because they already know where they're going. Juniper looked up. Three figures in city watch colors were threading through the fish stalls toward them, and the lead one was looking directly at their corner of the market with the flat certainty of a man reading from instructions.

The courier felt Juniper stiffen and understood instantly. His frozen hands moved fast now, faster than a dying man should manage, jamming a packet wrapped in oilcloth into Juniper's coat — inside breast pocket, against the ribs, the way smugglers do it. The wax seal pressed cold through the fabric.

"Before the winter finishes falling," the courier breathed. "Say it back."

"Before the winter finishes falling."

"Don't trust the Master. Don't trust the watch. Don't trust anyone who offers you warmth." His grip was failing now, sliding down Juniper's arm. "And taster — whatever they seasoned you for — spit it out."

Juniper's blood went to ice water. *Seasoned.* The word landed in the place where the monthly vials lived, the place he had never once examined, and something in him — some animal instinct older than the guild, older than the creed — reared up howling.

The watchmen were ten paces away. Eight.

"They'll say I fell," the courier whispered, sinking, letting his knees buckle as though surrendering to his wounds at last. "Let them say it. Walk away. Don't run. Running is a name. Walking is nobody." His eyes found Juniper's one final time, and in them was neither fear nor pain but a runner's last professional satisfaction: *delivered.* "You were the easiest part of the journey, taster. Finding you was like finding a shadow. Whoever taught you to hide taught you well."

"He did," Juniper said, and hated how steady his voice was. "He taught me very well."

"Then hide well now."

And the courier died between one breath and the next, folding down onto the wet cobbles with the gracelessness of the truly finished, his frost-white breath rising once, hanging, and dispersing.

Juniper stepped back. One step. Two. His heart hammered but his face did nothing, his face was a locked room, his face was thirty years of practice. He bent — naturally, casually, a good citizen retrieving his dropped parcel — and lifted the ruined mackerel out of the gutter, and straightened, and turned toward the dock gate.

"Oi." The lead watchman's voice, behind him. "You. Did you see what happened here?"

Juniper turned halfway, the picture of mild inconvenience, a fishmonger's customer delayed by tragedy. "Man collapsed," he said. "Frost-bit, by the look. Came out of the north pass, I'd guess. I caught him, but he was gone before I could shout for help." He shook his head with practiced sorrow. "Bad season for travelers."

The watchman studied him. Juniper let himself be studied, offering nothing — no fear, no guilt, no interest. Inside, beneath the oilcloth packet pressing cold against his ribs, terror sang like a struck wire. *They know my name. They know my name. They know my—*

"You touched him?"

"Caught him, aye. Nothing more."

"Name?"

Here it was. The hinge of his whole life, swinging. Every instinct screamed *stay small, stay small*, and every instinct was wrong, because the courier had known his name already, had carried it eleven days through a killing pass, which meant the name was already loose in the world, already spent, already worthless as currency.

"Yarrow," he said. "Juniper Yarrow. Apothecary's assistant." The lie arrived smooth and warm, worn soft from use.

The watchman wrote it down. Of course he wrote it down. Somewhere in a watch-house ledger, in a clerk's indifferent hand, Juniper Yarrow existed now in ink, and there was nothing in any world that could unwrite it.

"That'll be all. Move along."

Juniper moved along. He did not run. Running is a name; walking is nobody. He walked through the dock gate at the pace of a man thinking about his lunch, past the watch-post, past the tar barrels, past the beggar who never begged him because beggars knew better than to see him, and he did not break stride until the fish market was a memory and the guildhouse hill rose ahead of him, gray stone and dark windows, the building that had raised him, fed him, poisoned him, and — if a dying courier's last words were worth anything — *sold his mother.*

At the top of the lane, sheltered by the wall where no window overlooked, he finally stopped. His hands were shaking now, late and useless. He drew out the packet. Oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, sealed with a stamp he didn't recognize: a mountain split by a stair, descending.

He didn't open it. Not here. Opening it was a threshold, and thresholds deserved walls around them.

But standing there in the cold with the dead man's delivery against his heart, Juniper Yarrow understood with terrible clarity that the life of staying small was over. The courier had known his name. The watch had written it down. Hunters were coming dressed as friends. And somewhere below the mountain, in a jar of preserved summer, a soul named Yarrow had been waiting thirty years for exactly this moment — for her son to stop hiding.

*Before the winter finishes falling.*

He tucked the packet away, picked up his ruined mackerel, and walked home to betray everyone who had ever kept him.

* * *

The guildhouse received him without comment, as it received everything. Kettle-steam in the corridors, the sour bite of tinctures, apprentices scurrying with covered trays. Juniper nodded to the ones who nodded to him, which was none of them, and climbed to his cellar room, and bolted the door, and sat on his narrow bed with the packet in his lap for a long time before his hands stopped shaking enough to work the knot.

Inside the oilcloth: a single sheet of heavy paper, folded twice, and a scrap of something else — a recipe card, brittle with age, written in a woman's slanted hand. *For green fire, burn what loves you most.* He stared at that until his eyes stung, then set it aside with fingers that trembled in a way he refused to examine.

The map he unfolded slowly, the way a man opens a letter he already knows contains his own sentence.

And there, in ink gone brown with thirty years, in a hand he recognized because he had balanced its entries a thousand times — the Master's hand, Corvane's hand, the hand that signed the sealed entries, the hand that had signed for one soul, sold —

There was his mother's name.

* * *

He worked until the candle gutted, because working was the only prayer he knew. He copied the map's visible script into his private cipher. He traced the tunnel-lines beneath the mountain with one finger, following them down and down to a chamber marked with a symbol he had seen only once before in his life — on the brittle recipe card, beside the words *for green fire*: a garden, burning.

Then he heard the footsteps on the cellar stairs.

Heavy. Unhurried. The tread of a man who owns every door he walks through, and had walked through this one a thousand times with warmth in his voice and poison in his pockets.

Master Corvane knocked anyway. He always knocked. That was the worst of him — the courtesy.

"Juniper," came the voice through the wood, gentle as snowfall. "You look pale, boy. Come up to the fire. I've had the cook make your favorite."

And Juniper, sitting in the dark with a dead man's map spread across his knees and his mother's name drying in brown ink above a burning garden, heard himself answer in the small, steady voice of a man who has just decided to become enormous:

"In a moment, Master. I'm balancing the ledgers."
