## 6. An Old Debt at the Apothecary

The apothecary at the end of Candlewick Row did not advertise itself. There was no painted sign, no bundle of dried herbs hung above the door as the other shops kept. It announced itself only by smell — a complicated, layered smell that reached Juniper halfway down the street and told him, before he saw the crooked door or the smoke-stained shutters, exactly what was being ground inside. Wormwood, mostly. And beneath it, faint as a whisper under a sermon: nightshade, valerian, something older he could not name.

He had not been to Wren's shop in four years. He had told himself this was prudence. In truth it had been shame, of a small and manageable kind — the shame of a man who keeps a debt in a drawer because looking at it would mean admitting he still needed things.

He knocked twice, then once more. The old signal. He half expected it to mean nothing anymore.

Footsteps inside, unhurried, uneven — one leg dragging slightly, an old injury Juniper had never asked about. The door opened on a chain, and through the gap came one eye, brown and sharp and ringed with sleeplessness.

"Guild business," said the eye's owner, "went out of fashion here some years ago."

"Not guild business."

The chain slid off anyway.

* * *

Wren's shop was a museum of mislabeled things. Jars lined every wall from floor to ceiling, and every label was wrong on purpose — *flour* on a jar of powdered moonstone, *mending pins* on something that hissed softly when the light touched it. A disgraced archivist's revenge: if the guild ever sent anyone to confiscate her collection, they would spend a week cataloguing lies. Juniper had always found it funny. Tonight it made him feel like a man standing inside someone else's paranoia, which was, he supposed, exactly what he was.

Wren poured tea without asking. Her hands were stained to the wrist with tannin and ink, and she moved through her own shop the way he moved through the palace kitchens — without looking, without wasted motion, entirely at home in territory no one else wanted.

"You look terrible," she said. "You've looked terrible for years, but tonight it's worse. Sit down before you fall down."

He sat. The tea was bitter in a way he catalogued automatically — willowbark, gentian, a trace of something that numbed the tongue — and he caught himself doing it and hated himself a little. Four days since the courier died against him in the fish market, and still his tongue worked before his grief did.

"I need a translation," he said.

Wren set down her cup. "Of what?"

He unwrapped the map from its oilcloth. He had not let it leave his body since the dock gate; he had slept with it against his ribs like a second heartbeat. On her table, under her lamp, it looked smaller than it felt — a square of cured hide, dense with ink, the mountain drawn in cross-section like a dissected animal. Tunnels coiled down its flanks. At the center, where a heart should be, a circle of tiny script surrounded a symbol: a stylized bloom, petals open, drawn with a precision that made his chest hurt to look at.

Wren leaned over it and went very still.

"Where did you get this?"

"A courier. Dying. He gave it to me and died."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." He met her eyes. "You owe me, Wren. You know you do. I'm calling it in."

Something crossed her face — not anger; something wearier and more honest than anger. She turned back to the map and began, slowly, to trace the outer ring of script with one ink-stained finger.

"I do owe you," she said. "Three years ago I falsified a provenance record, and when the Master's auditors came for me, someone lost my file in a drawer for six months. Someone who knew exactly how ledgers behave. I never learned who arranged it. I always suspected it was you."

"It was me."

"Yes. I thought so." She pulled a magnifying lens from a drawer, screwed it into a brass arm, and bent over the script. "So here is your translation, free of charge, as a down payment on a much larger debt I hope you're not about to make worse." Her voice changed as she read — flattened, careful, the voice of a woman handling something that might go off. "It's Old Court Script. Pre-charter. Nobody's written it above ground in two hundred years. It says—"

She stopped.

"Wren."

"It says *the court beneath remembers what the world above forgets.*" She straightened. "And around the seal, in a tighter hand: *kept in trust. Sold in perpetuity.*" She looked up at him. "Juniper. This is a receipt."

"A receipt for what?"

"For whatever's buried in the middle of that mountain." Her finger came down on the garden symbol, gentle as touching a wound. "This mark. Do you know it?"

His throat closed. He nodded instead of speaking, because speaking would have cracked something loose.

It was on the recipe card. His mother's recipe card — the single scrap of her handwriting that had survived her, tucked into a book of household remedies he'd kept since he was nine years old. *For a burning garden, sow in frost,* her card had said, in that looping, impatient hand, and beside the words she had drawn a flower just like this one, petals open, precise as a signature.

"My mother drew it," he managed. "Before she died."

Wren sat back. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the lamp hissed, and somewhere in the walls of the shop something settled with a dry whisper, like paper turning over in its sleep.

"Sit there," Wren said finally. "Don't touch anything. I'm going to check one thing, and if I'm right, you're going to wish very hard that I'm wrong."

She climbed a ladder into the upper shelves and came down with a ledger — one of hers, a private one, bound in gray leather. She flipped through it with increasing speed, lips moving, until she stopped so abruptly that Juniper heard the pages snap.

"Thirty-one years ago," she said quietly. "A sale entry. Sealed. I archived it myself, back when I still had a guild seal and believed things." She laid the ledger open on the table beside the map. Two entries, side by side. The guild's charter transaction — the founding contract that gave the Tasters' Guild its legal existence, its protections, its immunity from prosecution — and beside it, in the margin, a sealed annotation in the Master's hand. Corvane's hand. Juniper knew it intimately; the man had corrected his childhood penmanship with those same strokes.

"The charter wasn't granted," Wren said. "It was *purchased*. Everyone knows the guild paid something for it — the story is gold, land, favors. But the payment line is sealed, and sealed entries don't get opened, and I was young and ambitious and didn't ask." Her finger hovered over the annotation. "I can't unseal it. Only the Master can. But I can tell you what a sealed entry costs to maintain, because I processed the maintenance fees for thirty years. Do you know what kind of seal holds for three decades?"

"No."

"One paid for in advance. Perpetually renewed. Paid to—" she tapped the map, to the circle of script around the heart-symbol, "*them*. To whoever lives under the mountain and writes in dead languages." She looked up. "Your courier didn't bring you a treasure map, Juniper. He brought you the other half of a receipt. Your mother's name is in that sealed entry. I'd bet my shop on it."

"How could you possibly know that?"

"Because the courier was carrying a map *to* the collateral. Receipts travel toward their owners." She closed the ledger gently, like closing an eye. "That's how their contracts work. That's how all of it works, down there. Nothing is given. Everything is held."

The tea had gone cold in his cup. He stared at the map, at the little flower at the mountain's heart, and felt the well-ordered architecture of his life — the ledgers, the routines, the creed of staying small — groan like a hull under ice pressure.

"What do they trade in?" he asked. "Down there. What does a court like that want?"

Wren got up and stood at the window, looking out at Candlewick Row, at the ordinary lamplit street where ordinary people bought ordinary remedies.

"I spent ten years in the guild archive reading their correspondence," she said. "Every sealed contract routes through them. Every impossible trade the guild ever recorded — the bottled echo, the borrowed luck, all of it — traces back to that court as counterparty. And in thirty years of letters I learned exactly one rule about them, and I'll give it to you now, because you're going to need it." She turned. "They trade in what you cannot afford to lose. Not what you value — what you *cannot afford*. The price is always calibrated to the wound. Bring them gold and they'll take the mine it came from. Bring them a favor and they'll take the friendship it grows from. They are scrupulous, Juniper. They are fair. They never take more than the exact weight of what losing it will cost you. And that is why no one who deals with them comes back whole."

"I don't intend to deal with them. I intend to take something back."

Wren laughed — one short, sharp note, entirely without humor.

"Then you intend to rob a court of creditors. Good luck. They keep books too, you know. Better ones than ours. Every soul in that vault has a provenance record longer than your arm, and every record ends in the same place — in a contract signed by someone above ground who couldn't afford to lose what they sold, and sold it anyway." She came back to the table and put both hands flat on the map, pinning it like a specimen. "Which means whoever sold your mother's soul signed their name. And if you go down there, they will show you that signature. They'll be *delighted* to. It's hospitality, to them. So before you take one step toward that mountain, ask yourself whose name you're prepared to see."

Juniper thought of Corvane's hand correcting his penmanship. Thought of the sealed entry, maintained faithfully, punctually, for thirty years — a debt serviced with the same care a gardener gives an orchard. Thought of immunity doses, measured and patient, building in his blood toward something.

"I think I already know," he said.

"Do you?" Wren searched his face. "Say it out loud. If you can't say it in my shop, you won't survive saying it down there."

He couldn't say it. Not yet. The word formed and dissolved behind his teeth like sugar in hot water. Instead he said: "Translate the rest. The tunnels. Which ways in."

Wren held his gaze a moment longer — testing, he realized, whether he'd break — then bent back over the map. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone archivist-flat again, and somehow that was worse.

"The main stair enters through the old quarry on the north face. It's marked *closed* — that's a lie, everything on this map that's marked closed is open, and everything marked open is a grave. Here—" her finger traced a hairline spiral, "—a servant's way, kitchen access. Marked with a knife symbol. And here, at the bottom, the vault itself. No lock drawn on it. Just words." She squinted through the lens. "*Opens to hunger.*"

"To hunger?"

"That's what it says. Doors down there don't open to keys, apparently. They open to want." She straightened, rubbing her eyes. "Which should frighten you, because it means the court has never needed locks. It lets people in based on what they're starving for. And everyone who reaches that door is starving for something."

Juniper looked down at the map and thought, absurdly, of his cellar. Of the ledgers hidden under his floor, balanced nightly, kept immaculately, a life's work of recording other people's impossible trades while committing none of his own. He had always believed staying small would save him. That invisibility was armor. That a man who tasted poison for a living and wrote nothing memorable and wanted nothing loudly would pass through the world untouched.

But the door at the bottom of the mountain opened to hunger. And he was starving.

"Wren," he said. "When I go — and I am going — I'll need someone above ground. Someone who can read the ledgers, cover my absence, and if it goes wrong, make sure the truth outlives me. You owe me a favor. I'm spending it. All of it."

Wren was quiet for a long time. Outside, a cart rattled past on Candlewick Row, and somewhere a drunk was singing badly, and the city went on being the city, ignorant of the receipt lying open on the table between them.

"You understand what you're asking," she said at last. "If I open the sealed entries — if I start pulling threads in the guild archive — that's not a favor anymore. That's treason. Slow, documented, provable treason. The Master doesn't forgive that. The Master doesn't even *punish* it, really. He just... removes the person, and adjusts the records so it's as if they never stood anywhere at all." She flexed her ink-stained fingers. "I built my whole disgrace carefully, Juniper. Small sins, survivable sins. I stayed small the way you did. It worked."

"And now?"

"Now—" She exhaled. Picked up her cold tea. Drank it anyway, grimacing, like a woman taking medicine. "Now some frost-bitten courier walked halfway across the kingdom to die against you in a fish market rather than let this map be lost, and I'd like to know why *you*, specifically. Why a taster? Unless someone chose you. Unless someone has been keeping you for something." She set the cup down. "Yes. Fine. I'll do it. But I'm doing it properly. If we're committing treason, we commit treason with *documentation*. Copies. Witnesses. Redundancy. If the truth is going to outlive us, it needs to be written down in enough hands that no one can burn them all."

"That sounds like an archive philosophy."

"It sounds like the only weapon disgraced people have left." She rolled the map up and pushed it back across the table to him, but kept two fingers on it, holding it still. "One more thing. Free advice, no charge, because I like you and I want you terrified. Whatever you find down there — however bad the truth is — remember that courts like this one don't give refunds and don't accept apologies. The moment you walk through that door, you become an entry in *their* ledger. Everything about you becomes tradable. Your name, your memories, your grief, your—" she paused, choosing, "—your immunity. Especially that. A man who can't be poisoned is worth more than a man who can't be killed. Don't let them learn what you're worth."

Too late, Juniper thought. Someone already had. Someone had been measuring his worth in careful doses for years, seasoning him like meat toward a single feast.

He took the map. It weighed nothing and everything.

"Wren. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Come back and thank me." She turned away, busying herself with jars that didn't need moving, her back a rigid line of fear she would never admit to. "And Juniper — the sealed entry. When you come back, I'm opening it. I want the Master's signature. I want it dated. I want it in my own archive where I can stare at it every morning for the rest of my life."

At the door, he paused. The night outside smelled of coal smoke and coming frost, and the first flakes were starting to come down over Candlewick Row — early, too early, winter finishing its falling ahead of schedule, as though the mountain already knew he was coming.

"Wren," he said. "What was my mother's name? In the entry. Did it say?"

She didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice was very quiet.

"I told you. It's sealed." Her eyes flicked to the map, to the flower at its heart. "But you already know what grows at the bottom of that mountain. Ask yourself who planted it there."

He stepped out into the falling snow, and behind him he heard her turn the lock — twice, then a third time, the way frightened people do — and he walked north, toward the quarry road, toward the mountain, carrying a receipt and a recipe card and a debt he was finally, after thirty years, ready to collect.
