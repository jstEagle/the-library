## 7. The Feast He Was Seasoned For

The winter court's opening banquet was held in the Long Hall of the old palace, where the ceiling was painted with a sky that had not existed for a hundred years — a summer blue, fat with gilded clouds, presiding over three hundred candles and a king who looked like he had been carved from the same pale wood as his throne.

Juniper stood at the taster's station behind the high table, hands folded, spine straight, wearing the gray livery of the Guild of Small Mercies and trying to be furniture. This was the creed made flesh: *stay small, stay dull, stay alive.* A taster who drew the eye drew the knife. For eleven years he had perfected the art of being overlooked, and he was very good at it, so good that even now, with the hall roaring with music and the smell of roasting quail, no one glanced twice at the narrow young man with the ink-stained fingers and the face of a clerk who had never once been promoted.

He should have felt at home here. Instead, every instinct he owned was standing at attention like soldiers before a firing squad.

It began with the wine.

The first pour was a spiced red from the southern valleys, and Juniper took his measured sip from the testing goblet while the cupbearer waited with the king's. Honey, clove, black cherry, the faint iron ghost of a cheap barrel. Clean. He nodded, and the wine went to the royal table, and nothing happened, and this was how the world was supposed to work.

The second course was a soup of white beans and smoked ham, and it was also clean, and Juniper noted it in the small ledger of flavors he kept behind his eyes — *bean, smoke, bay, nothing* — and moved on.

It was the third course where the night tilted.

A pheasant pie arrived under a crust molded into the shape of the mountain itself, all sugar-glazed ridges and candied snow. Juniper cut into the testing portion, lifted a forkful of dark meat and gravy to his mouth, and stopped.

Not because it was poisoned. That was the terrible thing. It wasn't poisoned. It was *familiar.*

Beneath the pheasant and the prunes and the wine-dark gravy there was a bitterness. Faint as a whisper through a wall. Bittercress root, aged in ash — a compound so subtle that any ordinary palate would read it as nothing more than the gamey edge of old bird. But Juniper had tasted that bitterness every month of his life since he was nine years old, dissolved in Master Corvane's "tonics," swallowed dutifully in the guildhouse kitchen while the Master watched him with those warm, patient eyes.

*Your constitution is a gift, boy. We are building something in you. Someday you'll thank me.*

Juniper set down the fork.

His hands were steady. Eleven years of practice saw to that; a taster whose hands shook died of suspicion before he ever died of poison. But inside his chest, something was moving with the slow, grinding inevitability of a glacier calving.

He tasted again. Carefully. Professionally. He let the gravy sit on his tongue and catalogued it the way Wren had taught him, back when Wren still had a desk and a future: base notes, heart notes, the long tail of aftertaste. The bittercress was there, but it was *dressed* — masked by mace, cushioned by fat, dosed so precisely that it would pass beneath any tongue in the kingdom except one. Except the tongue that had been marinating in it, drop by drop, year by year, until the poison and the boy had grown together like a tree around a nail.

This dish was calibrated to him.

And if it was calibrated to him, then it was meant to pass his tasting. And if it was meant to pass his tasting, then whoever built it knew exactly what Corvane had been feeding him. Knew the doses. Knew the schedule. Knew the *purpose.*

*We are building something in you.*

The hall's warmth seemed to thin. Juniper became aware of the candles, hundreds of them, all burning at once, all that open flame above all that dry sugar crust. He became aware of the king — old King Aldous, third of his name, white-bearded and mild, laughing at something the ambassador from the coast had said, lifting his goblet with a hand spotted like a speckled egg.

*Before the winter finishes falling,* the dying courier had said. Juniper had thought it meant the map. The tunnels. The court beneath the mountain. He had carried those words south for weeks like a coal in his pocket, and all the while they had meant *this* — meant tonight, meant this table, meant this pie.

He looked at the pie. The pie looked back, sweet and golden and innocent as a lie told well.

* * *

Service continued around him. Juniper performed his office. He sipped, he swirled, he nodded, and each nod sent another course out to the high table like a small absolution. Roasted quail with juniper berries — *juniper*, someone in the kitchens had a sense of humor or a death wish — clean. Candied chestnuts, clean. A sorbet of frozen pear to clear the palate, clean, though he held it on his tongue longer than he needed to, grateful for the cold.

All the while, the arithmetic ran in the back of his skull, relentless as water dripping into a cistern.

Corvane had raised him. Fed him. Dosed him. Built, molecule by molecule, an immunity with walls around it — immunity to bittercress, to widow's breath, to half a dozen compounds Juniper could name and three he couldn't. Every taster developed resistances; that was the trade. You poisoned yourself slowly so you could save others quickly. Juniper had always believed his tolerances were broad, general, a professional armor.

But armor has a shape. And the shape of his armor, he understood now, tasting the ash-aged bitterness bloom politely across his tongue, was the shape of *one specific dish*. Everything else had been scaffolding. Every tonic, every monthly dose, every grimace swallowed in the guildhouse kitchen — all of it had been load-bearing masonry for this single evening, this single plate, this single old man laughing over his wine.

The question that remained was the question that mattered: *who?*

Corvane himself? The thought arrived and Juniper flinched from it, then made himself look at it straight, because a taster who flinched from a flavor died of it. Could the Master have built his own ward into a weapon? Seasoned him like a ham toward a feast? It would be elegant. It would be *safe* — the guild's own taster passing the dish, the guild's own taster hanging for it when the king died, the guild's charter renewed by a grateful new crown, and Master Corvane weeping beautifully at the funeral of the boy he'd raised. Two birds. One stone. No ledger entry required.

Or someone else. Someone who had read Corvane's sealed entries, who knew the dosing ledger better than Juniper did, who wanted the old king dead and the Guild of Small Mercies broken and buried in the same grave. Wren's voice came back to him from the apothecary's back room, low and urgent: *the court beneath trades in what you cannot afford to lose.* What did Corvane cannot afford to lose? His charter. His ward. His perfect, invisible instrument.

Either way, the trap had the same teeth, and Juniper was standing directly between them.

He looked down at the testing plate. The remaining slice of pie sat there, steaming gently, its gravy gone glossy as it cooled. In the king's portion — already served, already cut into, already halfway to being eaten — the same gravy pooled against golden pastry.

Old King Aldous speared a piece of dark meat on the point of his knife.

Juniper moved.

Later, he would not be able to reconstruct the decision, because there wasn't one. There was only the gap between the fork rising and his body crossing the distance, the way there is only the gap between lightning and thunder. He caught the king's wrist — lightly, a touch so practiced and deferential it read as ceremony, the traditional blessing of the taster's hand — and turned the bite aside, and said, in the flat, bored voice of a man reciting ritual:

"Forgiveness, Majesty. The prunes. A bad batch from the south — I've tasted it in the kitchen stores. Your physicians will thank you to skip the fruit tonight."

Murmur. Pause. The king blinked at him with milky, amiable eyes, and for one suspended heartbeat the whole glittering hall balanced on the edge of whether a gray-clad nobody was worth listening to.

Then Aldous laughed, warm and wheezing. "Prunes! Forty years on the throne and undone by prunes." He waved the fork away. "Take it from me, boy. Take the whole plate. You've earned a king's supper."

Juniper bowed. Smiled the small, forgettable smile. Lifted the king's plate in both hands like an offering.

And walked with it, unhurried, past the candlelight and the music, out through the servants' door into the cold stone corridor beyond, where he set it down on a ledge and stood very still and let his hands finally shake.

* * *

He did not eat it. He did not throw it away. Both impulses rose and both were wrong — eating it would prove nothing except that Corvane's thirty years of seasoning worked, which Juniper already suspected down to his marrow; destroying it would erase evidence he might desperately need. Instead he scraped the pie into the empty wine flagon he'd carried for exactly this purpose, stoppered it, and tucked it inside his livery against his ribs, where it lay like a second heartbeat.

Then he went back to work. That was the discipline. The feast was not over; the king was not safe; whatever hand had built this dish might have built others. Juniper returned to his station through the servants' passage and resumed his post as though he had merely stepped out to spit, and the hall swallowed him back into its noise without noticing the gap.

But everything was different now. He tasted the remaining courses the way a man reads a letter he knows contains a betrayal — hunting between the lines. The cheese course: clean, but he checked it twice. The honeyed figs: clean. The final wassail, drunk from shared cups in the old style: clean, though the nutmeg sat strangely on his tongue, and he could not decide afterward whether that was poison or only fear.

Through all of it, he watched.

He watched the cupbearers, and marked the one who served the pheasant pie — a broad, calm man with a coastal accent and a wedding ring, who wept quietly when the toast was called and showed no other sign of anything. Not him, probably. Poisoners who wept were either innocent or magnificent.

He watched the high table, and marked the empty chair beside the king's — the crown prince's chair, vacant tonight, the birthday feast of chapter one come round again in his memory, the prince who lived because a taster had once caught a bitterness no one thanked him for. The prince was absent from the winter court entirely, "kept abed with a chill," said the heralds. Or kept abed the way one keeps a valuable piece off the board when one intends to overturn it.

And he watched the doors.

Near midnight, when the wassail was gone and the king had retired wheezing and cheerful, when the candles had burned down to stubs and the hall was thick with grease and laughter going sour, the far doors opened and a figure entered whom Juniper had last seen standing in a fish market doorway with frost on his eyelashes.

Not the courier. The courier was dead. This was the man who had been watching from the doorway *when* the courier died — tall, gray-cloaked, with the patient stillness of a heron at the water's edge. He did not approach the dais. He did not approach anyone. He simply stood among the departing guests and turned his head, slowly, the way a man reads a room — until his gaze found the taster's station.

Found Juniper.

Held him.

There was no threat in it. That was the worst part. It was the look of a craftsman inspecting finished work. Of a gardener walking his rows at harvest time, checking the fruit for ripeness, deciding what may hang a little longer and what must come down tonight.

Juniper held the gaze for three seconds — long enough to seem incurious, short enough to seem harmless — then looked away and busied himself with his testing goblets, and when he looked up again the gray cloak was gone.

His heart was slamming. His face was smooth. Inside the livery, against his ribs, the flagon of poisoned pie pressed its cool weight against him like a hand.

*They know I know.* Or worse: *they don't care whether I know, because knowing changes nothing.* Either way, the message was the same. The dish had failed — or rather, the dish had been *made* to fail, by a taster who was never supposed to taste it at all — and somewhere in this palace, in this city, in the warm rooms above the cold tunnels, someone was revising their plans. Plans that had him in them. Plans that had always had him in them, since he was nine years old and swallowed his first tonic and said thank you.

He gathered his goblets. He signed the night's tally with the kitchen steward — *all courses passed, no adverse events*, the lie sitting on the page in his neat clerk's hand — and he walked out of the Long Hall through the servants' stair, down and down, away from the dying candles and the painted summer sky.

In the shadowed vestibule at the bottom, he stopped, and took out the flagon, and looked at it.

One dish. One dose. Thirty years of monthly tonics, all of it masonry for a single plate of pheasant pie, and the mason had watched him build it with warm, patient eyes, and called it a gift.

Resolve did not arrive like fire. It arrived like frost — silently, comprehensively, changing the nature of everything it touched. Juniper Yarrow stood in the dark with a stolen poison against his heart and understood that the small life was over. The ledgers, the map, the name on the sealed entry, the courier's last words falling like snow: *before the winter finishes falling.* The winter was finishing now, tonight, in this palace, and he was standing in its path holding the only proof that it had been planned.

He had two roads out of this vestibule. Up the outer stair, to the guildhouse, to Master Corvane's fire and his warm voice and his monthly tonic — to confess the night's irregularity and watch the old man's face for the flicker that would tell him everything.

Or down.

Down was not on any stair in this palace. Down was a wax-sealed map hidden in the false bottom of his ledger case, and tunnels beneath the mountain, and a court that traded in what you could not afford to lose, and a soul named Yarrow waiting in the dark like a seed waiting for spring.

Somewhere above him, faint through the stone, the banquet's last song ended. The winter court slept. And in the morning, Juniper knew, the gray-cloaked man would report that the taster had passed every course and saved the king with a lie about prunes — and that the taster had then gone home to the guild, where he belonged, where he had always belonged, where he was seasoned and kept and counted.

Which meant that tonight, of all nights, was the night not to go home.

Juniper buttoned his coat over the flagon, settled the ledger case on his shoulder, and turned toward the river gate, where the ice was forming and the map said the mountain's roots began.
