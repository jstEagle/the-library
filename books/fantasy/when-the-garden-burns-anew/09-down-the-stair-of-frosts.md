## 9. Down the Stair of Frosts

The mouth of the mountain was not a cave.

Juniper had expected a cave — a crack in the rock, something honest in its ugliness, something he could brace himself against with the plain physics of stone and dark. Instead, when he parted the frost-killed bracken at the mountain's northern foot, he found a doorway. A proper doorway, arched and linteled, cut from grey stone that had been dressed by hands that cared about proportion, and set into the mountainside as neatly as a door set into a wall of a house. No house stood within ten miles. The door simply opened onto rock on three sides and onto darkness on the fourth, and it had been waiting there, he understood at once, for a very long time.

Snow lay unbroken before it. Not disturbed snow — *unwritten* snow, if such a thing could be said, so smooth and blue-white in the last light that his eye slid off it, unwilling to believe no one had ever crossed it.

He crouched and looked closely, because looking closely was what he did. Taster's habit. You learned early that the difference between a safe dish and a dead prince lived in details too small for anyone else to bother with: the wrong sheen on a sauce, a grain of spice ground a hair too fine, a bitterness folded under sweetness like a knife wrapped in a cloth. He had spent fifteen years reading the small print of other people's intentions. He read it now, in the snow.

There were footprints. They were simply not going toward the door. They went *out* of it — faint impressions pressed up through the snow from beneath, as if the snow remembered feet that had passed before the snow itself had fallen. And every single print pointed outward. None pointed in.

*Everyone leaves,* the prints said. *No one arrives.*

Juniper stood, brushed the knees of his trousers, and stepped over the threshold anyway.

The map — Wren's translation of it, inked out in her cramped archivist's hand across the back of his mother's recipe card's protective wrapping, because Wren had refused to let the original leave her sight until the last possible moment — showed a stair. *The Stair of Frosts,* she'd written, with the underline of someone who wanted him to take the name seriously. *Nine hundred steps, give or take whatever the mountain is feeling. Do not say your name aloud below the fourth landing. Do not eat anything offered. And Juniper — the cold down there isn't weather. It's a record. Everything it touches, it keeps.*

He had nodded at all of this in her cellar, surrounded by ledgers, feeling very brave and very stupid in equal measure, the way he'd felt at every stage since a dying courier had put a wax seal in his palm.

Now, with the first step taken and the daylight behind him thinning to a grey ribbon, brave felt further away than stupid.

* * *

The stair descended in wide spirals, hewn so smoothly his boots made almost no sound. Someone had built this to be walked quietly. That was its own kind of message. The walls wept a fine rime that caught the glow of the pale moss growing in the mortar lines — coldlight, Wren had called it, the kind of illumination that gave no warmth and asked none back. It turned everything the color of old silver: the steps, the walls, his own hands when he raised them to check the map.

He counted the landings the way he counted courses at a feast. One. Two. Three. At each, a niche had been carved into the wall, and in each niche sat something small and waiting — a clay cup, a wooden bird, a child's shoe worn through at the heel. Offerings, or tolls, or leavings. He did not touch them. His mother's voice, thirty years dead in every way that mattered, murmured somewhere behind his sternum: *Never take what grief has placed.* He hadn't heard that voice clearly in a decade. He was surprised how completely it still owned him.

Between the third and fourth landing, the cold changed.

He noticed it the way he noticed poison: not as a sensation but as an absence where a sensation should have been, a wrongness in the texture of things. The cold above had been ordinary cold — dumb, indifferent, the cold of altitude and season. This cold was *attentive*. It moved against the direction of the draft. It gathered at the nape of his neck like a reader leaning over his shoulder.

And then, softly, from everywhere and nowhere — from the rime on the walls, he realized; the frost itself — it spoke his name.

"*Yarrow.*"

Not shouted. Not threatening. Said the way a clerk confirms an entry. The way you'd read a name off a ledger to check it against a face.

Juniper stopped mid-step and did not answer. Wren's warning rang in him: *below the fourth landing, do not say your name aloud.* But this was worse than a rule about speaking. The cold already knew. The cold was *reading* him, the way he read a dish — patiently, thoroughly, cataloguing.

"Yarrow," it said again, and this time there was more in it: a taste of woodsmoke, of dried yarrow flowers hung from a kitchen beam, of hands in garden soil. The frost was pulling the name apart to see what it was made of, and what it was made of was *her*. Her name had become his name. The cold was tasting his inheritance.

He kept walking. He kept his jaw shut. Each step downward, the frost whispered fragments — not his memories, he understood, but other people's, stored here like preserves on a shelf, offered up the way a market-stall keeper calls out wares. *A wedding in a lemon grove. A debt forgiven on a deathbed. A lullaby in a language with no living speakers.* The cold was full of kept things, and it was showing him its inventory to see what he would reach for.

That was the trap, he saw. That was the whole architecture of the place. You didn't pay at the bottom. You paid all the way down, in installments, a coin at a time, and the coin was always something you'd rather have kept.

*Doors that open only to hunger,* Wren had written. He'd assumed she meant literal hunger — that he should descend fasting, empty, ready. Standing now in the whispering cold, he began to suspect she'd meant something less comfortable. Hunger for what?

At the fifth landing he found out.

* * *

The door was set flush into the spiral wall where no door needed to be — the stair continued past it, unbroken — and it was made of a dark wood gone grey with age, banded in iron green with verdigris. No handle. No keyhole. Only a shallow basin, waist-high on a pedestal beside it, filled to the brim with perfectly clear water that did not ripple even though the air moved.

Above the basin, carved into the lintel in letters worn nearly smooth: *PAY WHAT YOU CANNOT SPARE.*

Juniper stood before it for a long time, hands in his armpits, breath silvering.

He thought about the guild ledgers. He thought about the entry sealed in Corvane's hand — *one soul, sold*, and the seller's signature in his family name. He thought about what he was willing to lose, and ran the inventory the way he ran a pantry audit: gold, some. Reputation, little enough to spare. The map, the recipe card, Wren's translated notes. His own life — he had already, without admitting it, priced that in.

But the lintel didn't say *pay what you can afford.* It said *what you cannot spare.* The mountain wanted the thing with hooks in it. The thing that would hurt.

He thought, with a lurch, of the immunity doses. Fifteen years of Corvane's careful spoon-feeding, building his body into a lock keyed to a single poison, a single feast, a single murder that wasn't yet committed. His immunity was the Master's masterpiece and the Master's leash both. If he surrendered that — if the mountain took it — the winter banquet would kill him like anyone else. Every plan he'd made downstream of that banquet depended on being the one man in the hall who could palm the dish's heart and live.

*Pay what you cannot spare.* Was this the price? Was this the door's clever cruelty — to demand exactly the thing his whole descent depended on keeping?

He leaned over the basin. His reflection waited in the water, and the water's cold came up at him like a held breath.

"No," he said aloud, and his voice sounded strange and small in the spiral dark. "Not that. Ask me again."

The water did not move. The door did not move. The frost along the walls whispered, gently, horribly, in a dozen stored voices at once — and among them, threaded thin as wire through the others, a voice he knew. A low voice, roughened by pipe smoke, humming three notes of a tune that had no name, the tune he had been humming to himself in cellars and tasters' closets his entire adult life without ever knowing where he'd learned it.

His father's voice. Kept in the cold like a jar of summer fruit.

Juniper gripped the pedestal until the verdigris bit his palms.

His father had died when Juniper was six — a fall, they'd told him, on the mountain road, though even then he'd registered the particular flatness with which Corvane had delivered the news, the same flatness he would later learn to read as the sound of a ledger being balanced. He had almost nothing left of the man. A tune. An impression of large hands and the smell of tar. The knowledge, unverifiable and therefore precious, that he had been loved before he became useful.

The door did not want his immunity after all. It wanted something he could not spare, and of all the contents of his life, this was the thing he truly could not: the last proof that he had ever been anybody's son, and not merely anybody's taster.

"You knew," he said to the door, and his breath shook. "You all knew."

The frost whispered. Somewhere below, faintly, something vast shifted its weight — the sound a mountain makes when it is paying attention.

Juniper closed his eyes. He did the thing he did before every tasting: he emptied himself, set his fear on a shelf, and became only a mouth and a judgment. Then, deliberately, he brought the memory forward the way you lift a lid off something scalding.

"I'll trade," he said. "One memory. His voice. Take it whole — the tune, the sound of it, all of it. I keep the fact of him. I keep that he loved me. I'll know it the way I know an entry in a ledger — true, recorded, and untasted."

The water shivered. Just once.

He bent and spoke the trade into the basin, formally, the way Wren had taught him the Undercourt liked its bargains — precise terms, no ambiguity, no generosity left lying around for the house to scoop up. "*I, called Yarrow, offer one memory entire: the voice of my father, its sound and its song, kept these twenty-nine years. In exchange: passage past this door, and passage past every door between here and the court below that opens to hunger. Witnessed by the cold.*"

The frost along the walls went utterly silent.

Then the cold came into him like a tide sliding over a footprint on wet sand. It didn't hurt. That was the worst part. It was gentle, professional, thorough — a taster's own technique turned back on him, lifting the memory out with clean fingers, leaving no residue, no crumb. He felt the shape of the loss pass through him: the rough warmth of the voice, the three-note tune, the way *his name* had sounded in it —

— and then he didn't feel it, because there was nothing there to feel. There was a father-shaped space in him, tidy and documented, and around it the fact sat gleaming like a coin rubbed featureless: *he was loved. It is written down somewhere. I cannot hear it anymore.*

Juniper straightened. Tears had frozen on his cheeks; he hadn't noticed them forming, and he broke them off like small crystals and let them fall, and did not look at where they landed.

The door swung inward without a sound.

* * *

Beyond it the stair grew narrower and older, the masonry rougher, the coldlight sparser. The frost here had fewer voices — or perhaps it had simply finished with him, having taken its payment and filed its receipt. He walked for hours. He ate nothing; hunger, he now understood, was currency down here, and he intended to arrive solvent. Twice he passed side passages breathing warmer air, and twice he heard, far off, the small industrious sounds of kitchens — a knife on a board, water boiling — which was somehow more frightening than silence. Dead courts, Wren had said. Kitchens of the dead. But dead things, apparently, still kept their cooks.

On and down, counting landings he no longer bothered to number, until the stair ended.

It ended at a curtain of frost hanging floor-to-ceiling across a corridor wide as a cathedral aisle — a sheet of ice grown dense as glass, and beyond it, blurred and golden, candlelight. Hundreds of candles. The warm amber glow of a great hall seen through a dirty window, and moving shadows within it, elegant and slow, and music — distant, plucked, patient, the music of people who have been playing the same piece for a century and are in no hurry to finish.

Juniper stood before the ice curtain, hollowed out, hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food, one memory lighter and no wiser about whether lighter was better.

Through the frosted barrier, the music paused. Conversations, he realized, were happening just on the other side — a murmur of many refined voices — and then, distinctly, one of them rose above the rest, dry and amused, addressed not to the room but to the door itself, to him, as if the speaker had been waiting with a hand already on the latch:

"Well," said the voice. "The taster's come at last. Do come in, Yarrow. We've been keeping your mother's seat warm."
