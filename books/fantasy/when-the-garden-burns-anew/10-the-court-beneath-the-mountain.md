## 10. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The stair ended where all stairs end, in a door that had been waiting for him.

Juniper had counted his steps down the Stair of Frosts the way he counted everything—out of habit, out of discipline, out of the taster's old creed that a man who knows exactly where he stands is harder to kill. Four hundred and eleven steps. The cold had taken his father's voice three hundred steps ago, and it had taken something else besides, though he could not yet name what. A warmth, maybe. The last of his certainty that he would walk back up.

Now there was only this: a door of black wood banded in tarnished silver, set into stone that sweated frost, and carved into its lintel a single word in no alphabet he knew but which he understood anyway, the way one understands hunger or grief.

*Enter owed.*

He pressed his palm to the wood. It was warm. That was the worst part—that after hours of cold that remembered names, the door itself was warm as a living cheek, and it opened before he could decide whether knocking was wise.

Light spilled out. Candlelight, thousands of candles, and beneath them a sound like a city holding its breath.

* * *

He had imagined the court beneath the mountain a dozen ways on the long descent, and every imagining had been wrong. He had pictured a cavern, crude and dripping, some goblin-hollow of the old stories. What he found instead was a hall so vast the ceiling vanished into darkness above the candle-smoke, hung with chandeliers that might have been gardens once—wrought iron twisted into vines and roses and things that were neither, each bloom cupping a flame. The floor was pale stone veined with silver, polished by centuries of feet into something like water, so that walking across it felt like walking on the skin of a frozen lake with the whole dark sea underneath.

And the people.

They filled the hall in their hundreds, dressed in colors that had gone out of fashion when the mountain was young, and they turned to look at him as one creature turns—not all at once, which would have been merely frightening, but in a slow ripple, like wind moving through wheat, each face finding his as the doors closed behind him with a sound like a book shutting.

Juniper Yarrow had spent thirty years learning how not to be seen. He had made an art of it. He stood at feasts like furniture; he walked markets like weather. He had been invisible so long that invisibility had stopped being a skill and become a shape, the way a corset reshapes the ribs.

Now four hundred ancient faces looked at him, and every one of them *saw* him.

It should have been terror. It was, partly. But underneath the terror, shameful and warm as stolen wine, was something else. Relief. The terrible relief of a man who has been alone in a room for decades and hears, at last, another person breathing.

*They see me,* he thought, and hated himself for the way the thought glowed.

A herald came toward him through the crowd—or Juniper assumed she was a herald, because she carried a staff of white birch hung with small bells that made no sound, and because the crowd parted for her the way flesh parts around a knife. She was tall and gray, gray hair, gray gown, gray eyes, but her mouth was painted red as a wax seal, and when she smiled he saw that her teeth had been filed to points too fine and even to be natural.

"Yarrow," she said.

Not *who are you*. Not *state your name*. She said it the way one reads a label on a jar.

"You know me."

"The map knows you." Her eyes went to his coat, where the courier's map lay against his ribs. "The seal knows you. And the mountain—" she tilted her head, listening to something Juniper could not hear, "—the mountain has been expecting someone hungry for a very long time. Come. You are expected at table."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You haven't. That's what makes you interesting."

* * *

The table was longer than any feast-hall above ground could have held, and it was laid for hundreds, though most of the seats stood empty—the exiled nobles ate, apparently, in shifts, or in eras. Juniper was seated near the head, which he understood immediately as both honor and display, the way a rare vintage is put on the shelf nearest the window.

The food arrived, and his hands began to shake.

Not from fear. From *profession*. Because the dishes that came up from the kitchens below were beautiful beyond anything the winter court above had ever produced—game birds glazed in something amber and smoking faintly, bread dark as peat, fruits he did not recognize that gleamed like lanterns—and every dish, every single one, was poisoned.

Not murdered-poisoned. Not the crude work of assassins. These were seasoned poisons, curated poisons, poisons blended the way a perfumer blends attars: a trace of bitterness under the honey of the bird, a cold metallic note in the bread like a coin held too long in the mouth, a sweetness in the fruit that sat wrong on the tongue's memory of sweetness. Each dish carried a price woven into its flavor, and Juniper, tasting, reading, cataloguing as he had been trained since childhood, felt his fear transmute into something almost like homecoming.

He knew this language. He had never heard it spoken fluently before.

"You taste it," said his neighbor, an old lord whose beard was braided with tiny keys. "Don't pretend otherwise. Your face does arithmetic."

"I'm a taster," Juniper said. "By trade."

"Above?" The old lord's laugh was dry as moth wings. "Above, they pay tasters to die in place of kings. Down here, we keep tasters to tell us *what we're eating*. There's a difference, boy, and it's the difference between our world and yours."

"And what am I eating?"

"Hospitality," said the old lord, and raised his glass. "With hooks in it. But then, everything down here has hooks in it. The wine remembers your name. The bread will want a favor returned. Eat anyway. Refusing hospitality in this court is far more dangerous than accepting it."

So Juniper ate. He chose carefully—he took the bird over the fruit, the bread over the soup, avoiding the two dishes whose flavors spoke of memory-loss and binding—and as he ate, the hall watched him with an attention that felt less like scrutiny than like thirst. He realized, somewhere between the second course and the third, that they were not watching him eat.

They were watching him *taste*. No one down here had seen a true taster work in a century, perhaps. He was not a guest. He was a curiosity, a rare bird blown in through a crack in the mountain, and the court's delight in him was the delight of collectors.

He should have hated it. Instead he poured the wine—it remembered his name, murmuring *Juniper, Juniper* in a voice like his mother's, which nearly undid him—and let himself be seen, and told himself the warmth in his chest was only the alcohol.

* * *

After the meal, the court withdrew to smaller chambers, and the gray herald returned for him. She led him through corridors lit by candles that burned blue, past doors that whispered as he passed—doors that open only to hunger, Wren had warned him, and these doors murmured *what do you lack, what do you lack* in voices like dry leaves, and Juniper kept his eyes forward and his wants clenched behind his teeth.

"You came following a name," the herald said. It was not a question.

"I came following a debt."

"Mm. In this court those are often the same thing." She stopped before a tall window—not a window, he realized, but a sheet of ice grown thick and clear as glass, and beyond it, deep in the black rock, something glowed. A soft gold light, steady as a heartbeat. "Do you know what that is?"

"No."

"That is the vault. Everything this court has ever bought and kept is in there. Souls. Seasons. Names. Thirty years ago a man came down from above and sold us something very fine, and paid with it for a charter, and the charter has kept his guild fat and safe ever since." She watched Juniper's face in the reflection, gray eyes bright. "You know the man."

"I know the guild."

"You know more than the guild." She turned to face him fully, and her red mouth curved. "You smell of him. Old men who take in strays leave their scent on them, like dogs. Corvane's ward, come down my mountain in the dead of winter, carrying a dead courier's map. Do you imagine we don't know whose seal that is? We sealed it ourselves, a lifetime ago, and gave it to a woman who begged us for a copy. She said her son would come one day. She said he'd be a taster." The herald leaned close, and her breath smelled of candle smoke and snow. "She was right about everything except the day."

Juniper's throat had gone tight. "My mother was here."

"Your mother is *here*," the herald corrected softly. "That is rather the point of a vault."

The word landed in him like a blade sliding between ribs—clean, precise, meant. *Is.* Present tense. Thirty years of mourning, and the truth was that he had never once mourned the right thing.

"What does the court want from me?" he asked, when he could speak evenly.

"What courts always want. A trade." She gestured, and the blue candles guttered. "You carry something we want very badly, taster. And we hold something you want very badly. Tomorrow, when you've rested, the court will name its price, and you may accept or refuse, and either way you will learn what things cost down here. Tonight—" she swept her arm toward a side passage, "—you are our guest. Sleep. Dream if you dare. The mountain will watch over you."

"And if I try to leave?"

"Then the mountain will watch you go," she said, "all the way down, and it gets so *cold* at the bottom of a man, Yarrow. Even the ones who are used to swallowing bitter things."

* * *

The chamber they gave him was small and round and warm, with a bed hung in velvet gone soft with age, and a single candle that burned without shrinking. On the table beside it lay a carafe of water and a plate of candied plums, and Juniper stood looking at them for a long time before he touched either.

Plums. His mother had candied plums, in the garden summers, in the years before. His hands checked the fruit automatically, the taster's ritual, reading the sugar-glaze for bitterness, for metal, for the cold note of traded things—and found nothing. Only plums. Sweetness offered plainly, for once, with no hook in it that he could find.

He ate one standing up, and it tasted like being eight years old, and he had to sit down on the edge of the bed until the shaking passed.

*Seen,* he thought. *They saw me at the table. They knew my name at the door. They know whose son I am.* Above ground, thirty years of careful smallness, of ledgers balanced in pencil and erased by morning, of saving lives no one thanked him for—and down here, in one evening, every hidden thing about him had been read aloud by strangers.

It should feel like flaying. It felt like being unwrapped.

That was the hook. He understood it now, sitting in the velvet dark with plum-sweetness still on his tongue. This was how the court caught people—not with chains, not with threats, though he had heard the threat in the herald's voice clearly enough—but with this. With recognition. With the intoxication of mattering, of being known, of walking into a hall and having four hundred faces turn toward you like flowers toward a sun you had spent your whole life believing you were too small to cast.

Corvane had fed him doses and called it care. The court fed him plums and called it welcome. Everyone who had ever fed Juniper Yarrow had wanted something, and the cruelest part was how good it tasted going down.

He lay back on the bed and did not sleep. Through the stone he could feel it, faint and steady, that gold heartbeat from the vault, and he let himself imagine—for just a moment, the way a starving man lets himself imagine bread—what waited inside the jar that held his mother.

Then he rose, took out the map, and studied it again by the deathless candle. And there, in the margin, in handwriting he now recognized with a lurch as his mother's—the same slanted hand as the recipe card he had carried since he was nine—were words he had missed in the cellar, in the fish market, in all the desperate miles since:

*The vault keeps what was sold. But the garden keeps what was given.*

Below it, almost too faint to read, a single instruction:

*Ask them what I grew.*

Juniper sat very still, the map trembling in his hands, while somewhere far below the gold light beat on like a heart in a jar.

In the morning, the court would name its price. But tonight, he finally had a question of his own—and in a court that traded in answers, he suspected the question was worth more than anything he'd brought to barter.
