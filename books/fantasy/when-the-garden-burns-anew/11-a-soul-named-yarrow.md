## 11. A Soul Named Yarrow

The Undercourt kept its hours the way a miser kept coins: counted, hidden, and never spent where anyone could see. Vesperin knew this better than any soul in the candlelit halls, for it was his office to announce those hours, and had been, in one form or another, since before the mountain above them had learned to wear its crown of ice.

He was old. Not in the way of men, who wrinkled and slowed and apologized for it, but in the way of bells—older than the things they tolled for. His body had been remade twice by the court's physicians, his voice tuned like an instrument to carry through stone, and his memory... his memory was the court's third vault, though no one said so aloud. The First Vault held gold. The Second held souls. The Third walked on two legs, wore silver-grey robes stitched with the names of every herald who had come before him, and remembered everything.

Which was why the guest called Juniper Yarrow interested him.

Not because of the map. Maps arrived at the Undercourt the way rain arrived at the surface—frequently, unwelcome, and always from somewhere higher up. Half the exiled houses down here claimed descent from some sealed inheritance or other; a wax seal and a desperate expression were nearly legal tender among the desperate. Vesperin had announced the arrival of forty claimants in the last century alone, and watched thirty-nine of them leave lighter than they came.

But this one had asked a question.

*Ask them what I grew.*

The words had reached Vesperin before the morning bell did, carried by the kitchen-spirits who heard everything and repeated most of it. A guest, given honeyed wine and a chamber hung with moth-wing tapestries, had looked the Steward of Accounts in the eye and asked what the woman Yarrow had grown. And the Steward—a creature who had not blinked in four hundred years—had gone still as a stopped clock.

Vesperin had seen stillness like that only once before, thirty years ago, on the night the charter was signed.

* * *

He went to the records himself, which he almost never did. Announcing was his art; reading was beneath his station, and he guarded his beneath-ness jealously the way other officials guarded their honors. But the Third Vault could be consulted by itself, and so, in the hour before dawn, while the candles burned low and green in their sockets of black glass, Vesperin descended the spiral stair behind the Hall of Names and let himself into the scriptorium where the court's debts were written down.

The ledger for the year of the Charter lay open on a lectern of frozen oak, exactly where it had lain for three decades, because in the Undercourt nothing was ever closed, only paused.

He turned pages that felt like turning sheets of ice over a deep lake. Deeds of season sold by the drowned house of Merrow. A name pawned by a baron whose descendants still dined upstairs, unaware they were eating on credit. An echo, bottled, purchased from a guild of tasters far above—

There.

His finger stopped on the entry, and even after thirty years, the cold of it climbed into his knuckles.

*One soul entire, sound of season, unspoiled by grief. Sold: Corvane, Master of the Guild of Small Mercies, holding the seal of charter above. Bought: the Undercourt, in perpetuity. Kept: the Second Vault, shelf nine, jar of preserved summer.*

And beneath, in the buyer's column, the designation every herald learned to dread:

*Soul named: Yarrow.*

Vesperin read it three times. He had read it many times over the years—it was a famous entry, in the way that wounds are famous to the flesh that carries them—but he had never read it knowing that the seller's ward slept under his own roof tonight, asking questions about gardens.

Because there was more in the margin. There was always more in the margins; the Undercourt wrote small when it wished to be cruel.

*A note appended by the Steward of Accounts, at the hour of sale:* The seller wept. The seller asked that the boy be told nothing, ever, and offered a year of his own luck as surety of silence. Luck accepted. Silence contracted.

So that was the shape of it. Vesperin closed the ledger gently, as one closes a door on a sleeping child. The Master above had not merely traded a rival's soul for a charter. He had paid extra to keep the son blind. And the son had come down anyway, following a dead courier's map and a dead mother's handwriting, and stood now in their guest chambers wearing his blindness like a coat that no longer fit.

Curious, Vesperin thought, and the word tasted like the first sip of a vintage he hadn't been offered in centuries.

* * *

The court woke slowly, as courts do when they have forever. Candles were retrimmed. The moth-wing tapestries were shaken free of dust that glittered like ground frost. In the Hall of Audiences, the exiled nobles arranged themselves in their order of faded precedence—the drowned house of Merrow dripping politely at the edge of the dais, the baron's ghost-descendants yawning behind their fans—and waited to be told what the day would cost them.

Vesperin stood at his brass stand and made his announcements in a voice tuned to reach the roots of the mountain. He proclaimed the hour of petition. He proclaimed the reopening of the Second Vault for the annual inventory of kept things. He proclaimed, with particular relish, that a guest was present bearing a sealed map of the kind the court had not seen surfaced in sixty years, and that the court extended to him the hospitality of hooks, as was proper.

Then, because curiosity was the only appetite left to him that had never been taxed, he added an announcement that appeared on no schedule:

"The court will also receive the guest Juniper Yarrow, privately, in the Gallery of Records, at the hour of the second candle."

A ripple went through the hall. Private audiences were rare. Private audiences with heralds were unheard of. Heralds announced; they did not receive. But Vesperin was very old, and very bored, and the Steward of Accounts had gone still as a stopped clock, and he wanted—oh, how he wanted—to watch this young man's face when the record spoke its name aloud.

* * *

Juniper Yarrow came to the Gallery of Records at the second candle precisely, which told Vesperin something. Guests of the court usually came early, hungry to seem eager, or late, hungry to seem careless. This one came exactly when summoned, the way a servant comes, the way a man comes who has spent his life being punctual for other people's meals. He moved quietly. He looked at everything and touched nothing. And his eyes—grey-green, tired, watchful—went first to the exit, then to the herald, then back to the exit, calculating the distance between them the way a taster calculates the distance between the cup and the door.

"You are wondering," Vesperin said, "why a herald receives instead of announces."

"I'm wondering what it costs," Juniper answered.

Vesperin laughed, a sound like a small bell falling downstairs. "Oh, excellent. Everyone else wonders what it means. Meaning is free down here; meaning is the cheapest thing we mint. Cost is the honest question." He gestured to a chair of pale root-wood. "Sit. Nothing in this room is priced. That is its peculiar cruelty."

The gallery stretched away from them in ranks of shelves and lecterns, lit by candles that burned without shortening. Ledgers everywhere, thousands upon thousands, the spines lettered in silver script: years, houses, seasons, names. Somewhere in these stacks was a book with a young man's whole history folded flat between its pages.

"You asked the Steward a question," Vesperin said. "What she grew. The woman on the map."

Juniper's hands, resting on his knees, went very still. "You heard about that."

"I hear about everything. It is my profession and my punishment." Vesperin lowered himself into the chair opposite, robes settling like snow deciding where to lie. "I could make you ask me properly. Petition me, offer consideration, wait three days while the clerks deliberate. That is the court's way, and the court's way exists to make you pay for your own curiosity until curiosity starves." He folded his long grey hands. "But I am old, and I have announced ten thousand transactions in this mountain, and I find I want to know what you will do with the answer. So I am going to break protocol, guest, and give you something for nothing. Consider it a herald's whim. We are permitted one per century, and mine is overdue."

He rose, crossed to a lectern near the center of the gallery, and opened the ledger of the Charter year to the page he had already read three times in the dark. Then he turned it, so the young man could see.

"Read," Vesperin said.

Juniper read. Vesperin watched him the way he had once watched kings sign away provinces—not the face, which can be schooled, but the hands. The hands went white at the entry of the sale. They trembled at the description: *sound of season, unspoiled by grief.* They clenched at the margin, at the note about weeping, at the year of luck paid for silence—and Vesperin saw the exact moment the young man understood that the father-figure who fed him, dosed him, and raised him had also purchased his ignorance as deliberately as a merchant purchases cloth.

"She grew a garden," Juniper said finally, and his voice was steady in the way that things are steady just before they shatter. "On the roof of the guildhouse. She grew things that shouldn't have lived up there. The Master tore it out after—" He stopped. Started again. "After she died. He told me she died."

"She did not die," Vesperin said, gently, because even cruelty deserves precision. "She was *kept*. There is a difference, in this court, and it is the whole difference. Death ends. Keeping continues. Your mother sits on the ninth shelf of the Second Vault in a jar of preserved summer, entire and unspoiled, exactly as she was on the day she was sold. She has not aged. She has not grieved. She has not—" he hesitated over the word, then chose it deliberately— "*finished.*"

The silence that followed was long enough for a candle to gutter and recover.

"Corvane sold her," Juniper said. "To buy the guild's charter. To keep us—" his jaw worked— "to keep *me* fed, and housed, and trained, and blind. Thirty years. He seasoned me toward a feast and hid her on a shelf."

"He did what he believed necessary," Vesperin said. "They always do. I have announced the transactions of necessary men for six hundred years, guest, and I will tell you what I have learned: necessity is the coin in which monsters prefer to be paid. It spends everywhere and costs the spender nothing." He tilted his ancient head. "But do not mistake me for your ally. I gave you this record because I wished to see what you would do, not because I wish you well. The court bought that soul lawfully. She is ours, in perpetuity. Perpetuity is a word we take seriously below the mountain."

"What would it cost to take her back?"

"Ah." Vesperin smiled, and the smile was old and thin as paper. "Now we arrive at price. You cannot afford it. No one alive can afford it—that is rather the point of a vault. But." He raised one grey finger. "You asked what she grew. And I have been in this court long enough to know that questions, properly asked, are the only currency that appreciates. So before the court names its price tomorrow, before the Steward of Accounts descends on you with his arithmetic, I am going to show you one thing more. Not the vault itself—I cannot, and would not; the doors remember who opens them. But the door. Every kept thing has a door, and every door has a face."

He led Juniper down the long gallery, past ledgers of drowned houses and pawned seasons, past the shelf where bottled echoes murmured to each other in their sleep, until they came to the end of the records and the beginning of the stone. A corridor sloped downward, colder with every step, until the candlelight turned blue and their breath showed silver.

And there it was.

The door of the Second Vault was not wood or iron. It was a slab of winter itself, cut smooth as lake ice, and set within it—suspended like a fly in amber—was a single pressed flower. White-petaled, five-pointed, with a heart of gold so deep it looked like a wound that had healed into light.

Juniper made a sound Vesperin had not heard in six hundred years of announcing: small, involuntary, human.

"You recognize it," Vesperin said. It was not a question.

"It's from her garden." Juniper's breath fogged the cold and hung there, refusing to fall. "Yarrow. She grew it from seed everyone said wouldn't take. She pressed one bloom every summer and put them in her recipe cards. My card has one just like it. The same five petals. The same—" His hand rose toward the flower embedded in the ice, hovered, fell. "It's the same flower."

"No, guest." Vesperin's voice, tuned for proclamation, dropped to something almost private. "Your card holds a flower *like* this one. This flower *is* the garden. Or rather—" he studied the pressed bloom with the detachment of long practice— "the garden is what remains of her that she loved best, and the vault keeps what was sold, and what was sold included everything she grew. The court did not merely purchase a woman. It purchased a *season*. A summer that bloomed impossible things on a rooftop in the cold. It has been keeping that summer fresh for thirty years." He turned to look at the young man, and in the blue light his ancient eyes were bright with something that might, in a younger creature, have been called excitement. "Do you understand what I am telling you? The jar on the ninth shelf is not a memorial. It is a seed."

Juniper stood before the door a long time. The cold pressed against them both like a patient creditor. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed—something had settled in it, the way sediment settles in shaken water, leaving it clearer and heavier all at once.

"She wrote on her card. *The vault keeps what was sold. But the garden keeps what was given.*"

"Yes," Vesperin said softly. "That sounds like her. The gardener's creed. The sellers sell; the garden remembers." He straightened his robes. "Tomorrow the court will name its price, and it will be a price meant to be refused, because the court profits more from wanting than from having. Tonight, you know what I know, which is more than the Steward would like. Go back to your chamber, guest. Eat nothing. Sleep little. And decide what you are willing to become, because the door you are looking at—" he laid one grey hand briefly against the winter-slab, and beneath his palm the pressed flower seemed to glow— "opens for hunger, and closes for regret, and it has been waiting thirty years for someone to knock who actually intends to leave with something."

Juniper looked at the door a moment longer. Then he turned and walked back up the corridor without a word, his footsteps quiet as a servant's, his shadow long as a claimant's.

Vesperin remained alone in the blue cold, listening to the silence behind the ice.

And there—so faint that a younger herald would have missed it, so faint that he would spend the rest of the night deciding whether he had imagined it—he heard the flower press itself a fraction closer to the surface of the winter, as if it had heard footsteps it recognized.

As if, after thirty years of being kept, something on the ninth shelf had finally begun to listen.

* * *

He reported nothing to the Steward of Accounts. This too was a breaking of protocol, his second in one night, and Vesperin found the accumulation agreeable. Instead he climbed to his tower above the Hall of Names, took out the one possession he owned outright—a small brass bell, cracked, silent for six hundred years, retired from service before the mountain had a name—and polished it while the candles burned low.

Tomorrow the court would name its price. Tomorrow the young man would learn what the vault cost, and the Steward's arithmetic would descend like snowfall, patient and burying.

But Vesperin had read the margin notes. Vesperin remembered the weeping seller and the purchased silence and the year of luck spent like a bribe. And Vesperin, who had announced the sale of that soul with his own tuned voice thirty years ago, and had felt the words go through him like nails through a plank—Vesperin had decided, in the blue cold before the vault door, which side of the announcement he wished to stand on when the reckoning came.

He lifted the cracked bell, and though it made no sound, he rang it anyway—once—for the woman Yarrow, kept and unfinished on her shelf.

Somewhere below, in the dark of the Second Vault, the preserved summer stirred in its jar.

And far above, through miles of stone, the long winter on the mountain's crown creaked, and shifted, and began—very slightly, very early—to fall.
