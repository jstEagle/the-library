## 12. The Price of a Name

They did not take him to a throne room.

Juniper had imagined one, in the hours since the herald had led him from the guest chambers — some vast cavern ribbed with stone and candlelight, a seat of antler or bone at its center, the exiled nobility of the Undercourt arranged in tiers like frost flowers on glass. What he got instead was a kitchen.

Not the servants' kitchens he had passed through on his way down — those had been warm, working places, thick with the smell of bone broth and tallow. This was older. A round chamber deep in the mountain's heart where the ceiling sweated ice and the hearth burned with a flame that gave no heat, only light, pale as the memory of noon. A single table of black wood stood beneath it, and around the table sat five figures who might once have been beautiful in the way old coins are beautiful: worn smooth, still valuable, no longer quite what their faces promised.

The herald stood at Juniper's shoulder. "The Court of Kept Things receives you," she said, and her voice had the particular flatness of someone reciting words worn hollow by repetition. "Be seated. Be honest. Be brief."

Juniper sat. The chair was cold through his coat, cold through his shirt, cold in a way that seemed to be reading him as it went, taking inventory of his warmth the way he took inventory of a dish's bitterness. He kept his hands folded on the table where they could see them. Taster's habit. Empty hands meant honest hands, or so the guild taught its novices before it taught them anything worth knowing.

"You carry the seal of the map," said the figure at the table's head. She was the oldest of them, or wore her age most openly — silver hair braided with something that glittered and was not metal, eyes the color of water under ice. "We have not seen it in thirty years. We had begun to hope it was lost."

"And we had begun to hope," said the man to her left, younger, with a mouth made for smiling and currently not using it, "that whoever carried it would be someone more interesting than a poison-taster."

Juniper did not flinch. "You know what I am."

"We know what you are," the woman agreed. "It is written all over you. The careful hands. The way you tasted the air when you came in — yes, we saw. You were checking our hospitality for bitterness." Something moved at the corner of her mouth, not quite approval. "Good. That is the first sensible thing anyone has done in this room in a century."

"I was told," Juniper said, "that the court keeps a soul in its vault. A soul named Yarrow."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and for three full heartbeats nobody spoke. The fireless fire crackled anyway, which should not have been possible, and Juniper filed that away among the many things about this place he would examine later, if there was a later.

"Yarrow," the silver-haired woman repeated softly. "Thirty years ago a man came down the Stair of Frosts carrying a charter and a debt he could not pay. He offered us gold. We do not take gold; gold is only a promise that other people agree to believe in, and promises rot. So he offered us something better. Something *kept*." Her eyes fixed on Juniper with an attention that felt less like being looked at and more like being opened. "He sold us a gardener's soul, and with it the summer she had grown into it, and with *that* the seed of a garden that could burn away any winter ever sworn. Do you understand what we bought, taster? We bought an ending to every long winter. And then we locked it in a jar, because an ending is only valuable while it is still owed."

Juniper's throat was dry. "She was my mother."

"We know," said the smiling man, and now he smiled, and it was worse than not smiling. "Why else do you think we let you past the third door? Doors that open only to hunger, and yours has been starving for thirty years. It echoes down here. Hunger always does."

The herald shifted behind him. Juniper did not turn, but he marked the sound — leather, impatience, perhaps warning.

"The map says I can buy her back," he said. "The courier died getting it to me. I'd like to know the price before I decide whether the death was worth it."

"Practical," murmured the woman. "Your mother was practical too, in the little time we knew her essence. Souls are not people, taster — I will not insult you by pretending otherwise — but they keep the shape of the life that fed them. Yours keeps the shape of a garden. Ours keeps the shape of a ledger." She spread her hands on the black wood. "So let us speak of ledgers. We know you carry one. Not the map — the *book*. The guild's whole account of impossible trades. Bottled echoes. Borrowed luck. Sealed entries in your Master's hand. You balance it every week. You have balanced it since you were twelve years old."

Cold walked up Juniper's spine, and this time it was not the room.

"How—"

"We bought a soul from Corvane," the smiling man said pleasantly. "Do you imagine we sold him a charter and never looked at what secured it? A guild's charter is only as strong as its secrets. We have spent thirty years watching his secrets through the keyhole of the thing we own. And lately—" he tilted his head, birdlike, "—lately we have watched *you*. Balancing. Cataloguing. Staying small. Keeping his book truer than he keeps it himself, because truth is the only religion you have."

The silver-haired woman leaned forward. "Here is the price, taster, and it is the only price we will name, so listen well. Not gold. Not years of your life — we have plenty of those, stacked in jars like salted fish. Not your service; servants betray, and we have been betrayed by professionals before. We want the ledgers. All of them. The guild's whole book of impossible trades, delivered into our keeping, page and seal and signature. Every debt the guild owes, every debt owed *to* it, every entry your Master sealed in his own hand — including ours. Give us the book, and you walk out of here with your mother's jar under your arm before the next candle dies."

The silence afterward had weight. Juniper sat inside it and let himself feel, fully and honestly, how easy it would be.

That was the trap's true teeth, he realized — not the threat, but the ease. He had spent his whole life invisible, keeping accounts no one read, saving lives no one thanked him for. The ledgers were the only proof he had ever existed at all. And the court was offering to trade them back to him for the one thing he had come down a mountain to reclaim. His mother. Thirty years of grief, priced against thirty years of paper.

And yet.

He thought of Wren in her cluttered archive, trading favors because the guild had discarded her and she had never stopped loving it anyway. He thought of the courier dying in the fish market with frost in his beard and a stranger's name in his mouth. He thought of the Master seasoning him, dose by dose, toward a feast Juniper was never meant to survive — and of every entry in that book Corvane had falsified, every impossible trade propped up on the foundation of one stolen gardener's soul.

If he gave them the book, the Undercourt would own the guild entire. Every secret, every debt, every member's name and weakness, delivered to a court that traded in souls and seasons and kept its purchases in jars. Corvane would fall, yes. And everything else would fall with him — Wren, the novices, the whole crooked architecture of small mercies that had raised an orphan boy and taught him a creed worth keeping even when the men who taught it didn't.

And if he refused, he walked out of this kitchen empty-handed, and his mother stayed in a jar for another thirty years, and the courier's death became just one more unrecorded cost.

Two betrayals. That was the shape of it. There was no door out of this room that did not open onto one.

"No," Juniper said.

The word came out quieter than he intended. He said it again, louder. "No. I won't give you the ledgers."

The smiling man laughed — a short, genuinely amused sound. "You came down the whole mountain. You traded your father's voice for passage. You sat in our chairs and ate our bread, and you checked it for poison first, which we respected. And now you refuse the only bargain we will ever offer you?"

"I refuse it because it isn't a bargain," Juniper said. His heart was hammering but his voice held steady, and he was distantly proud of that. "A bargain leaves both parties poorer in something they chose to lose. This is a ransom with my mother as the hostage and my whole world as the fee. You don't want the book because it's valuable. You want it because with it you'd own the guild the way you own her — kept, shelved, owed. And I've spent my life keeping accounts for men who used them as leashes. I won't hand another leash to anyone. Not even to get her back."

He expected anger. What he saw instead, flickering across the silver-haired woman's face, was something almost like disappointment — and beneath it, faintly, respect.

"Corvane would have paid," she said. "That is the difference between you, I think. He understands that everything is for sale; he simply lies to himself about the exchange rate. You understand it too, taster. You just haven't finished lying to yourself yet." She turned to the herald. "Take him to the frost cells. Let him reconsider at leisure. Winter is patient. So are we."

"The map says—"

"The map says nothing," the woman interrupted gently. "Maps are invitations, not contracts. The courier knew that. Perhaps that is why he ran out of winter before he reached you." She rose, and the others rose with her, and for a moment the fireless fire guttered and the whole kitchen smelled unbearably, briefly, of a summer garden — roses and cut stems and sun-warmed earth — and then it was gone, and Juniper's eyes were burning, and he hated them for knowing exactly what that scent would do to him.

"He will not reconsider," the herald said quietly, at the door.

"Everyone reconsiders," said the silver-haired woman. "Some simply require colder rooms."

* * *

The frost cells were not dungeons, exactly. Dungeons were built to hurt. These had been built to *wait*, and the waiting did the hurting on their behalf.

The herald led him down a stair of blue ice into a corridor of cells whose walls were frozen solid, each cell a block of clear ice with a heavy door set into it, and inside each block — Juniper saw with a lurch — shapes. Not bodies. *Impressions.* Things that had waited here so long the ice had learned to remember them: the outline of a cloaked figure, the ghost of a chair, a smear of handwriting suspended mid-word. The cold here did not merely take your warmth. It took your record. It pressed you flat between its pages and kept you.

"Cell nine is vacant," the herald said. She opened the door, and the hinge made a sound like a held breath released. "There is bread twice a day. Check it before you eat it — the kitchens are old, and old kitchens grow careless, and you strike me as a man who checks." For just a moment, something human crossed her carved-stone face. "Your mother's jar is two floors above you. If it helps to know: it is the best-kept thing in the vault. Nothing spoils in there. Not even her."

Then the door closed, and the lock turned, and Juniper Yarrow stood alone in a block of remembering ice.

He checked the bread. Of course he checked the bread. Bitterness along the crust's underside — mild, a sleeping agent, probably accidental, probably. He broke off the clean half and set the rest on the shelf, and sat down on the ice bench, and began, because it was the only prayer he knew, to catalogue.

Assets: one immunity, built over years, possibly useless now. One recipe card, hidden in his boot lining, ink fading. One memory of his father's voice, spent at the third door, already going grey at the edges like a photograph left in sun. One refusal, freshly made, consequences pending.

Debts: one mother, held. One guild, unknowing. One scribe named Wren who would notice his absence within the week and would either report it or follow it, and Juniper could not decide which possibility frightened him more.

Liabilities: the entire Undercourt, patient as winter. The Master's enforcers, presumably already on the Stair. And the cold, which was even now learning his name — he could feel it trying it out against the walls, *Juniper, Juniper*, soft as snowfall, testing how he fit between its pages.

He laughed, once, without much humor. Stay small, the creed went. Stay invisible. He had followed it perfectly his whole life, and it had delivered him precisely here: the smallest room in the smallest cell beneath the smallest mountain, invisible to everyone except the things that wanted to keep him.

Somewhere beyond the ice, footsteps passed — measured, unhurried, going deeper into the mountain rather than up. Enforcers coming down, or prisoners going further in. He couldn't tell. The cold muffled everything except names.

He was still cataloguing when he heard the voice.

"Not the left wall," it said, low and dry, from the cell beside his. "Lean on the right one if you must lean. The left remembers whoever touches it, and after a month you start hearing yourself snore in it. Trust me. I've had time to conduct experiments."

Juniper turned. Through the clear ice of the adjoining wall, dim as a figure seen through fogged glass, someone was sitting up on their bench and regarding him with the frank curiosity of a scholar presented with a new specimen.

"I'm the court scribe," the voice went on. "Formerly. They keep me here because I copy things I shouldn't and forget things I shouldn't. And you—" a pause, and Juniper could hear the smile in it, "—you're the taster who told the Court of Kept Things *no*. Word travels fast in a mountain. So. Before the cold finishes learning your name, taster — would you like to hear about the servant's way into the vault?"
