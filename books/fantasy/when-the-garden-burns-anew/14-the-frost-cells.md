## 14. The Frost Cells

The cold in the Undercourt did not behave like cold. It behaved like a creditor.

Juniper learned this within the first hour of his imprisonment, when the frost began to write on the walls of his cell. Not the random ferning of ice on a windowpane, but words — patient, looping script that appeared as if a finger were tracing it from the far side of the stone. *Yarrow*, said the first line. Then: *son of the gardener*. Then, lower, almost shy: *we know what you came for*.

He pressed his palm against the wall and felt nothing. That was the worst part. The cold had no temperature he could brace against; it lived inside the rock the way rot lived inside fruit, and it was reading him the way he had spent his whole life reading other things — slowly, thoroughly, looking for what could be taken.

His cell was small enough that he could touch both walls with his outstretched arms, and low enough that standing fully upright made his scalp brush the ceiling. A grate near the floor let in air that smelled of candle smoke and old stone and, beneath that, something sweeter — preserved summer, faint as memory. He told himself it was his imagination. He told himself a great many things in those first hours, because telling himself things was how he survived.

*They will not kill you,* he recited silently, settling onto the frozen bench that served as a bed. *You are worth more to them alive. You are a debt being held, not a prisoner being kept.* The distinction mattered. Debts got fed. And if they fed him, then they fed him through hands, and hands could be read.

This was the creed he had carried down the mountain with him, folded small: *everything passes through someone's hands, and everything in someone's hands leaves a trace.* It was the first law of tasting, though he had never called it a law aloud. In the guild they called it diligence. In the palace kitchens they had called it paranoia, until the night it saved a prince's life, and after that they called it nothing at all, because tasters were not thanked, only retained.

Here, in the dark, it was simply the thing keeping him alive.

* * *

The bread arrived on the second day.

It came through a slot in the door — a slot sized for bread and nothing larger, which told him something about what the court feared leaving its prisoners. The loaf was small, dense, gray-brown, and warm. Warmth, in this place, was a message. Everything else down here was cold by design; warmth meant intention.

He did not eat it. He set it on the bench and looked at it for a long time, the way he had once looked at a plate of sugared plums destined for an old king's table, the way he had looked at every dish in twenty years of professional suspicion. Then he broke off a corner the size of a thumbnail and did what he always did: he waited.

Tasting was mostly waiting. Anyone could put a thing in their mouth; the discipline was in the stillness afterward. You held the crumb against your tongue and you counted — one breath, two, five, ten — while your body reported upward like a good servant reporting to a careful master. Heat or coolness where heat or coolness should not be. Numbness blooming at the edges. A sweetness that arrived too fast, like a guest who embraces you before asking your name. Bitterness hiding under bitterness, the way a lie hides under a smaller lie.

The corner of bread tasted of rye and ash and, underneath, a slow green bitterness that crept up from the back of his tongue like frost climbing a window. He knew it. Not by name — names were dangerous currency down here, and he suspected the court preferred its poisons unnamed — but by *behavior*. It was a numbing agent, gentle, almost kind. It would not kill him. It would take the edge off fear, off hunger, off thought. It was the poison they fed to prisoners they wanted docile, and it was dosed lightly enough that a man who ate the whole loaf would simply grow quieter each day until quiet became surrender.

Juniper spat the crumb into his palm and considered it there, a tiny wet ruin of someone's kindness.

*So,* he thought. *They want me soft.*

Soft men signed things. Soft men agreed to prices. Somewhere above him, presumably, the court was waiting for exactly this loaf to do its work — waiting for the son of the gardener to decide that the ledgers were too heavy a price after all, that his mother's soul was better left sleeping in its jar of summer, that a warm dungeon and a numb tongue were easier than truth.

He wrapped the rest of the loaf in a strip torn from his sleeve and tucked it into the corner of the cell, away from the frost-writing on the walls. Evidence. If he ever stood before anyone and accused this place of anything, he wanted to be able to say: *here is the bread they used.*

Then he sat back down and began, methodically, to starve with dignity.

* * *

On the third day, a voice came through the wall.

It was a woman's voice, thin and dry as paper left too long in sun, and it did not introduce itself so much as simply begin, mid-thought, as if their conversation had been going on for years and he had merely stepped out of the room.

"—the trick is the crust," she was saying. "They bake the dose into the crust. The middles are clean. Tear, don't cut, and eat from the center outward, and you'll live long enough to be executed properly."

Juniper went very still. "Who's there?"

"A neighbor." A pause, and then, with the dry precision of someone who has corrected many documents: "Cell nine. You're cell eight, I believe, from the way your door sounds. Iron hinges, badly oiled. Mine squeaks in E-flat. I've had time to learn."

"You can hear my door?"

"I can hear everything in this place. It's the one advantage of being kept." Another pause. "You didn't eat the bread yesterday. Or today. I heard you tear it and wrap it. Very disciplined. Guild-trained?"

"Taster. Twenty years."

"Mm. I thought so. You hold your silences like a man holding a full cup across a crowded room." The voice warmed slightly, which for her seemed to mean only that it became less dry. "I was a scribe here. Forty years. Before that—" she stopped, restarted. "Before that I tasted for the Undercourt. Long ago. When I was young and the court was younger and crueler, if you can imagine such a thing."

"What happened?"

"The usual. I read something I wasn't supposed to read. Wrote something I wasn't supposed to write. They took my name for it — that's the punishment here, did you know? Not death. Names are more useful than deaths. A dead woman is finished; a nameless one can still work." She sighed, a sound like wind through a keyhole. "They keep mine in a drawer somewhere, I expect, filed between a stolen season and a bottled echo. So now I am 'cell nine,' and I copy out other people's debts in a hand no one remembers belongs to anyone."

Juniper sat forward on the frozen bench, his breath fogging despite himself. "You know the vault."

A silence. Long enough that he thought she might have decided he was not worth the risk of answering. Then:

"I know *a* vault. I know the kitchens above it, because the servants' stair runs down past the pantry, and the pantry shares a wall with the cold rooms, and the cold rooms share a door with the vault corridor. I know which of the kitchen staff look the other way, and which ones report, and which ones can be paid in salt." Her voice sharpened. "And I know why you're asking, Yarrow. The frost writes on my walls too. Your business travels. This prison is a sieve of whispers wearing a mask of silence."

"Then you know what I want."

"Your mother. From the jar." She said it flatly, without cruelty, the way one states a ledger entry. "Everyone knows. The Herald made sure of it — he likes his curiosities known. A soul sold thirty years ago by a guildmaster, come back down the mountain to fetch it home. The court finds you *entertaining*, Yarrow. Do you understand that? You are a story to them. Stories get kept alive longer than men."

"Then help me stay alive."

"Why should I?" No malice in it. Genuine inquiry, the question of a woman who had spent forty years watching men promise things through walls. "What does a nameless scribe gain from helping a story escape?"

Juniper thought about it honestly, because lying to her would have been both wrong and useless — she had spent her life detecting falsified entries, and he doubted prison had dulled the skill.

"Nothing," he said finally. "Not yet. Maybe nothing ever. I'm a taster; I don't deal in futures, only in what's in front of me. But I'll tell you what I know, which is this: I spent my life invisible on purpose, and it turns out invisibility is just a smaller cell. You lost your name; I gave mine away by degrees, one unremarkable day at a time. We're both experts in being overlooked." He paused. "Overlooked people know the servant's ways. You said there's a stair past the pantry. Servants' stairs don't open for gold or titles. They open for *hunger* — that's what the doors down here answer to, hunger, need, the true kind. And nobody in this court is hungrier than two prisoners who haven't eaten in three days."

Another silence, but a different quality of silence. He could hear her thinking, hear the small movements of a woman shifting on a bench in a cell he would never see.

"You're stranger than the stories," she said at last. "The stories say you're a coward who counts drops."

"The stories are mostly right. I count drops. Counting drops is how cowards survive long enough to become something else." He leaned his shoulder against the shared wall, and the cold of it bit through his coat, but he stayed anyway, because a wall shared was a conversation held. "Tell me about the crust."

* * *

She told him about the crust. And about the water, which came in clay cups and carried a mineral sweetness that meant sedative on odd days and nothing on even ones — the court alternated, apparently, so that prisoners could never learn the pattern, though of course patterns were the one thing a scribe could never stop seeing. She told him which guard walked the corridor with a lantern trimmed low because he skimmed the lamp oil to sell, and which one counted steps aloud to himself because the silence frightened him, and how the third shift changed at the sound of a bell rung twice from somewhere above, and how the bell-ringer was deaf in one ear and always rang it three times on the first attempt, so that the *true* signal was the fourth ring, alone.

Forty years of observation, poured through six inches of stone, and Juniper drank it the way he had once drunk the details of a feast — course by course, cataloguing, cross-referencing, building in his mind a map of the prison that was probably more accurate than any map the jailers possessed.

In return he gave her what he had. He taught her the waiting discipline — how to hold a crumb on the tongue and count, how to distinguish the green bitterness of the calming draught from the sharper, bluer bitterness underneath it that appeared only in the loaves baked on days when the court expected trouble. "Blue bitter means they're nervous," he said. "Green bitter means they're comfortable. When the blue starts showing up, something's happening above us. Watch for it."

"And what do we do when it shows up?"

"We get ready," he said, "to move."

On the fifth day, the blue bitterness came.

He felt it under his tongue within four breaths of touching the crust-corner he allowed himself — a quarter-loaf's center, per her instruction, the clean heart of the thing. His body had grown lean and precise with hunger, the way a blade grows true when you grind away everything unnecessary, and it reported the poison instantly, unmistakably. Nervous bakers. Something stirring in the courts above.

That evening, the frost on his walls wrote differently. Not accusations now. Questions. *Why did the guild send no one for you? What is Corvane to you, that he lets you sit?* And once, chillingly, in a hand that looped like a signature: *he sold you too, you know. He just hasn't collected yet.*

Juniper sat with that one for a long time, in the dark, in the cold that remembered names. He did not doubt it. He had tasted the Master's seasoning in every immunity dose for years without knowing what he was tasting; he had balanced ledgers whose sealed entries bore his own family name; he had followed a dead courier's map straight into the one place in the world built to hold him. Of course Corvane had priced him. Everything was priced. The only question the frost was really asking was whether Juniper Yarrow intended to remain an entry in someone else's book.

"No," he said aloud, to the wall, to the frost, to the woman behind it. "I don't intend to remain an entry."

Through the stone, softly, came the sound of a woman laughing — dry, brief, real. "Good," said cell nine. "Because I've been thinking. About the pantry. About the stair. About the servant girl who owes me forty years of copied letters and can be bought with a promise instead of salt." A pause. "Eat your bread, Yarrow. Center-outward. Tomorrow we plan properly, and tomorrow you'll need your strength — because I'm going to teach you how a nameless woman walks through a court of names, and the first lesson is that hunger opens doors, but *discipline* keeps them open."

He tore the loaf apart and ate the clean heart of it, and it tasted, for the first time in five days, like something other than a trap. It tasted like rye and ash and the faintest ghost of summer — like a garden he had never seen, burning green somewhere above him in a future he had not yet earned.

The frost wrote nothing more that night. Perhaps it had run out of questions. Perhaps, for the first time since the mountain closed over him, it had begun, cautiously, to hope along with him.

And far above, muffled by stone and distance, a bell rang — once, twice, three times. Juniper counted, holding his breath, waiting for the fourth.
