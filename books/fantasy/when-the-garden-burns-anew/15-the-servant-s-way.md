## 15. The Servant's Way

The scribe's name was Maren. She told him this in the frost cell the night before they ran, whispering it through the gap in the stones as though it were a confession, and perhaps it was.

"You'll need to know it," she said. "For later. For what I'm going to do."

Juniper pressed his palm flat against the ice-rimed wall between their cells. "I don't want your name. I want you to come with me."

"I can't." A pause, and then a sound that might have been laughter, worn thin by cold. "Taster, I've been down here eleven years. Do you know what the court does with scribes who lose their masters? Nothing. That's the cruelty of it. They do nothing at all. You simply stop being written about, and then you stop being."

"You're being written about now," Juniper said. "I'm writing about you. In my head. It's a habit I can't break — I catalogue everything I can't afford to forget."

Another silence, longer. When she spoke again her voice had changed, gone soft and wondering, like someone touching an old scar and finding it warm. "Eleven years," she said, "and no one has ever put me in a ledger."

* * *

They planned it the way he planned everything: in small portions, tasted one at a time.

Maren knew the kitchens. Every servant of the Undercourt did eventually, because the dead court still ate — that was the first thing she'd taught him, back when they were only two voices passing bread-lore through stone. The nobles below had no fields, no flocks, no seasons except the ones they kept bottled in the vault. Everything they consumed came up from the deep pantries or down from the world above, carried on trays by servants whose faces were not permitted to be remembered.

"The kitchens are the one place the court doesn't look," Maren said. "A lord will memorize your face if you pour his wine. But a scullery boy carrying slop? He's furniture. He's weather. The court is built on people it refuses to see, and that refusal is a door."

Juniper had spent his whole life being refused. He'd built a creed around it: stay small, stay unremarkable, stay alive. He had never once thought of it as a door. He thought of it now, lying in the dark with frost blooming on his eyelashes, and felt something turn over in his chest — not hope exactly, but its rough draft.

"The servant's way runs behind the great hall," Maren continued. "Past the cold larders, through the wash-yard, and out by the ash chute. There are three doors between here and there, and each one opens only to hunger."

"Hunger?"

"That's how the court counts its staff. Not by name — names are currency here, didn't you know? — but by appetite. The doors taste you. If you're hungry enough to serve, they open. If you're hungry for something else—" She let it hang. "You'll have to be careful what you want, Taster. The doors can tell."

He thought of the jar in the vault. Of summer in glass. Of his mother's soul kept like preserves against a winter that never ended. *I am hungry for exactly one thing,* he thought, and then caught himself, because even thinking it felt like knocking.

* * *

The bread came at the hour the court called dusk, though no sun had ever reached this deep. Juniper broke his loaf and read it the way he read everything: crust, crumb, scent, the faint bitter ghost beneath.

There it was. Fainter than yesterday. A dusting of something mineral and old, ground fine into the flour — the same compound that had been thinning the prisoners by degrees, keeping them docile, keeping them *slow*. In the guild they'd called it greypeace, and it was banned in three kingdoms for what it did to the will.

He ate half the loaf anyway, and fed the rest to the gap in the wall.

"Yours is dosed too," Maren said, when she understood what he was doing. "Of course it is. Why would they waste good poison on—"

"On people they think are already broken?" Juniper finished. "Because broken people still have hands. And hands can be trained to carry things." He worked the last of his portion loose from his cheek and spat it into his palm, a taster's trick older than any guild: the mouth keeps what the throat refuses. "Eat nothing tomorrow. Chew and keep. Spit when you can. By the time we reach the kitchens you'll want to be hungry enough to open every door we meet."

Maren was quiet a long moment. "You've done this before," she said. "Survived somewhere, by being small."

"I was raised in it," Juniper said. "It's the family trade."

* * *

The lock on his cell was iron and honest, which meant it could be lied to.

He'd spent three days working a sliver of bone — saved from the prison stew, filed smooth against stone — into the mechanism, and on the fourth night, when the corridor torches guttered low in their long slow breathing, the lock gave with a click no louder than a knuckle cracking. He stood in the doorway of his cell and listened to the mountain breathe: the groan of settling ice, the far-off chime of the court's clocks counting hours that meant nothing, and beneath it all, so faint he might have imagined it, a smell. Green things. Growing things. Summer leaking from a vault two floors above, seeping down through stone like light under a door.

He freed Maren next. She came out blinking, small and grey-eyed and shivering inside a stolen guard's cloak, and looked at him with an expression he couldn't name until he realized he'd seen it before — in his own face, in the reflection off the guild's silver tasting spoons. The look of someone who has stopped expecting rescue and doesn't know where to put it.

"This way," she whispered. "And whatever happens — walk. Servants never run. Running is for people who matter enough to chase."

* * *

The first door was oak, black with age, set into a wall that had no business having a door. As they approached, Juniper felt it wake — a pressure against his mind like a thumb testing fruit at market.

*What do you hunger for?*

Not words. Weight. The question arrived whole, without language, and it leaned on him with the patience of something very old.

Juniper did what he had trained his whole life to do. He made himself empty. He thought of nothing but the tray he wasn't carrying, the slop pail he hadn't been given, the shift he was late for. He hungered the way a servant hungers: dully, generally, for warmth and sleep and the end of the day. He let that dullness fill him to the brim.

The door sighed open.

Maren followed his lead, and he heard her breath catch as the pressure passed over her too — and paused, longer on her, because she had lived eleven years in these cells and her hunger had grown teeth. But teeth were still hunger. The door accepted her the way a ledger accepts a debt.

The second door was worse. It stood at the end of a passage lined with hooks, and from the hooks hung aprons, caps, cloaks — the uniforms of the dead court's invisible army, some of them centuries old, some of them still holding the shape of the shoulders that had worn them last. Juniper took a plain grey smock and a scullery cap. On Maren he settled a laundry-maid's apron, and tucked her hair up beneath a kerchief, and stepped back to look at her.

"Furniture," he said quietly. "Weather."

"Weather," she agreed, and something almost like a smile touched her cracked lips.

The second door asked its question, and this time Juniper answered it honestly, because honesty was the only disguise he had left: *I hunger to carry something out of this mountain.* The door considered. Carrying was service. Service was hunger. It opened.

* * *

The kitchens of the dead court were vast and cold and terrible, and they were also, Juniper realized within moments, the most familiar room he had entered since leaving the guild.

Steam rose from copper cauldrons in slow columns. Knives hung in ranks. A hundred servants moved through the gloom in silence, chopping, stirring, plating — and none of them spoke, and none of them looked at one another, and the work went on with the smooth dreadful efficiency of a machine made of people. Above them, through grates in the ceiling, came the murmur of the court at its evening revels: laughter like breaking glass, music played on instruments that sounded wet.

"They cook for ghosts," Maren breathed. "Half those dishes go up and come back untouched. The lords below don't eat anymore, not really. They just like to watch it served."

But the servants ate. That was the thing that caught in Juniper's chest and stayed there. Along one wall stood a long scarred table, and at it sat kitchen folk on their breaks, eating real food — bread with actual butter, stew with actual meat — and talking. Low, quick, in a dozen languages, but *talking*. Laughing, once, briefly, at something. Here in the belly of the court that traded in souls and seasons and names, the invisible people had made a country of their own, population everyone, borders nowhere.

A woman with flour to her elbows looked up as they passed. Her eyes went over Juniper's borrowed smock, Maren's kerchief, and she gave the smallest nod — the nod of one servant acknowledging another, no questions asked, because questions were a luxury of the seen.

Juniper nodded back, and understood suddenly why Maren had said what she'd said in the cell. This was the solidarity of the unseen. It asked nothing and offered everything, and it held only as long as no one demanded a name.

"Chute's past the wash-yard," Maren murmured. "Two more turns. Stay boring."

They were four steps from the wash-yard when the bells began.

* * *

The alarm rolled through the mountain like a stone dropped down a well — one deep toll, then another, then a third that never quite ended, harmonics stacking on harmonics until the steam itself seemed to shiver. In the grates overhead, the court's laughter cut off mid-note.

Every servant in the kitchen stopped moving at once.

Then, as one, they turned and looked at the strangers in their midst.

Juniper's hand found Maren's wrist. His mind, the taster's mind, the cataloguing mind, ran its tables at lightning speed: *hunters will check the cells first, then the gates, then the servant ways; we have minutes, maybe less; the chute is close; the vault is above us and the jar is in it and the whole mountain is waking—*

But the servants didn't raise any cry. They stood in their silence, a hundred faces turning like flowers toward an absent sun, and the flour-woman looked at Juniper again, longer this time, and her gaze dropped pointedly to the floor. To the direction of the vault. To the theft the bells were singing about.

Her expression did not change. But she turned back to her work, and as she turned, she shifted her body — casually, the way a cook shifts to block a draught — so that she stood squarely between the kitchen's main entrance and the two fugitives by the wall.

*Go,* said everything about her posture. *Quickly. We saw nothing, because seeing is not our trade.*

Maren made a small sound beside him. Later, Juniper would understand it: the sound of someone watching the invisible choose, freely, at cost, to protect one of their own. Eleven years underground, and the court had never once managed to teach its servants to inform.

They ran — no. They *walked*, fast, boring, weather moving through weather, while behind them the hunters' boots struck the flags of the great hall and the flour-woman's broad back held the door of the world.

* * *

The third door was the ash chute, and it nearly killed them.

It opened to hunger readily enough — but the chute beyond was a black throat dropping away into cold, and climbing down it meant trusting rusted rungs set into slag, in the dark, with pursuit somewhere above. Juniper went first, testing each rung the way he tested food: weight, give, the honest feel of metal that would hold. Maren descended above him, breathing in small controlled gasps, her kerchief long lost, her grey hair floating loose in the updraft.

Halfway down, light bloomed above them. Torchlight, spilling into the chute's mouth, and a voice — the Herald's voice, silk over iron, calling down into the dark:

"They went below. Seal the upper ways. And find me the scribe who signed the cellar passes — *find her* —"

Boots moved off. The light died. Juniper hung on the rungs with his arms burning and his heart slamming, and above him Maren laughed once, high and shaking.

"He means me," she said. "My passes. I forged them for you, back at the cells — did you think the third door opened by luck? I signed us into the kitchen rotation weeks ago. My hand is on every page between here and the vault." She looked down at him through the dark, and her eyes were bright and calm and absolutely certain. "Which brings us to what I told you. What I owe you a name for."

"Maren—"

"When we reach the bottom, there's a wicket gate. Iron, court-sealed. It opens to a name given freely — that's the toll, that's the oldest law down here, older than the lords. Someone has to pay it." She began to climb down toward him, rung by rung, unhurried. "I've been thinking about which name. And I find I'd rather it weren't mine."

"Then we find another way—"

"There is no other way. There's only the one gate, and the hunters above, and a jar of summer two floors up that won't wait forever." She reached his level and gripped the rungs beside him, close enough that he could see the frost in her lashes and the strange peace beneath it. "Listen to me, Taster. You're going to go up there and take your mother back. And I'm going to stand at this gate and hold it open behind you — because a gate paid in a true name stays true, and stays *open*, and no hunter in this mountain will be able to shut it while I stand willing."

"And what happens to you?"

She smiled, and it transformed her whole grey face. "Nothing happens. That's the mercy of it. I'll simply be *no one* — unwritten, unnamed, unfindable. The court won't be able to punish me. It won't be able to remember me." Her voice dropped. "But you'll remember. You said you catalogue everything you can't afford to forget. So here's my payment, and here's my price: *Maren.* Scribe of the Undercourt, eleven years in the frost, who once tasted for lords and got clever about ledgers. Carry it up with you. Say it sometimes, in places with sunlight. That's all. That's the whole toll."

Juniper's throat was tight as a locked door. "That's not all. That's everything."

"It's the smallest thing I own," Maren said. "And the only thing worth giving."

* * *

The wicket gate waited at the chute's foot, iron-black, humming faintly with old law. Beyond it, a service stair climbed toward the vault levels, toward the sealed court, toward the jar of preserved summer and the dozens of souls beside it — dozens, he remembered, and his chest ached with the arithmetic of one pair of hands.

Maren pressed her palm to the iron.

"Maren," she said clearly, to the gate, to the mountain, to the dark. "Given freely. Hold."

The gate drank the name the way dry ground drinks rain. Somewhere in its depths a bell chimed once, soft and final, and the iron swung inward on darkness that smelled, impossibly, of green growing things.

Maren stepped back and looked at her own hands as though checking they were still there. They were. But her face had gone subtly strange, unfixed, like a word read too many times.

"Well," she said, and her voice sounded farther away already. "Go on, then. Doors don't hold themselves."

Juniper took her hand — the hand of a woman with no name, warm as anyone's — and squeezed it once, the way servants say everything they cannot write down.

Then he went up the stair, toward the vault, toward summer in a jar, while behind him a nameless woman stood in a doorway and held the mountain open.
