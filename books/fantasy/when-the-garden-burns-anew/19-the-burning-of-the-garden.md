## 19. The Burning of the Garden

The mountain let him go the way a hand lets go of something it has held too long — slowly, finger by finger, with a reluctance Juniper could feel in his bones. The last tunnel narrowed, then opened without warning into grey morning light and falling snow, and he stood at the mountain's mouth with a jar of summer pressed against his chest and frost in his eyelashes, breathing air that tasted, for the first time in days, of nothing at all.

No poison. No bitterness under the sweetness. Just cold, and pine, and the faint char-smell of a world that had been burning somewhere out of sight for thirty years.

Behind him, deep in the dark, the court's winter was waking. He had heard it begin — a sound like an enormous door being unbarred, like ice groaning across a lake, like ten thousand candles being snuffed in sequence. Somewhere below, hunters were lacing their boots. Somewhere above, on the switchback road, Corvane would be waiting.

Juniper adjusted his grip on the jar and started up through the snow.

* * *

He did not take the road. The road was where Corvane would be, and Corvane had spent three decades teaching Juniper everything he knew about staying alive, which meant he had also taught him how to be avoided.

Instead Juniper climbed the goat track on the mountain's eastern flank, the one the fishwives used in autumn when they wanted salt air without paying the toll. It was steep and stupid and it made his lungs burn, and twice he had to stop and tuck the jar inside his coat because his hands were shaking too badly to trust them with glass. The snow came down thicker as he climbed, fat flakes that fell without wind, straight down, as though the sky were lowering itself gently over the world like a lid.

The false winter. He could feel it now that Wren had named it for him. It wasn't weather. It was *accounting*. Somewhere beneath the guildhall, in a vault of charter-magic older than the city walls, a ledger entry read: *one winter, extended indefinitely, paid for by one soul, kept.* And every flake that fell was interest accruing on that debt.

His mother had been the principal.

He climbed faster.

The ruined garden sat in a hollow below the summit ridge, a place Juniper had known all his life without ever once being allowed to visit. Corvane had spoken of it exactly four times in twenty years, always the same way, in the flat voice he used for closed accounts: *Your mother kept a garden there. It burned. Do not romanticize it.* Juniper had obeyed, because obedience was the currency of survival in the Guild of Small Mercies, and he had been buying his life one small compliance at a time since he was six years old.

Now he stood at the garden's edge with snow to his knees and understood, finally, why Corvane had never wanted him to look at it.

It hadn't burned down. It had been *burned out* — deliberately, thoroughly, with a precision Juniper recognized from ledgers. Every trunk was charred to the same height. Every stone wall had been toppled inward. Even the soil looked wrong, grey and compacted, as though someone had salted it not just against growth but against memory. Thirty years ago, Corvane had not merely destroyed a rival gardener's work. He had erased the evidence of what she'd grown.

But gardens are patient, and so are sons.

At the center of the ruin, pushing up through the grey crust of snow, was a single green shoot. It should not have existed. Nothing green should have survived this winter, in this place, under this sky. Yet there it stood — thin, absurd, defiant — and around its base the snow had melted back in a perfect circle, and the air within that circle smelled of thyme.

Of her garden. Of the recipe card in his pocket, the only thing of hers he owned, the one that read *for the fever, gather at midsummer, from the bed by the wall.*

Juniper knelt. His knees went through the crust into the melted earth, warm as a living body, and he took out the jar.

* * *

It was smaller than he remembered. In the vault, surrounded by dozens of its siblings — souls kept in jars of preserved season, each one glowing its own stolen weather — his mother's had seemed to blaze. Here, in the open grey morning, it was modest. A jar the size of two fists. Inside it, summer moved the way summer moves behind the eyes when you remember a specific afternoon: golden light, drifting pollen, the sound of bees working a lavender hedge, warmth pressing gently against the glass like a cat asking to be let in.

And within the light, faintly, a shape. Not a face. Nothing so crude. But a *presence*, the way a well-tended bed has a presence, the way a kitchen in use has a presence — an intelligence of tending, of growing, of feeding things that could not feed themselves.

"Hello, Mother," Juniper said.

His voice cracked on the second word. He let it. There was no one here to hear him be weak except her, and she had heard him be weak before, in another life, in a house with a garden by the wall, before Corvane had come with his ledger and his mercy and his fire.

"I don't know if you can hear me. Wren said the kept ones sleep. He said the court keeps them dreaming so they don't wear themselves out against the glass." He turned the jar in his hands. The summer inside tilted with it, slow as honey. "I've been tasting for the guild for eleven years. You'd laugh. Or you wouldn't — you always said I had a careful tongue. I taste for a prince who doesn't know my name, and I keep books for a man who sold you, and I told myself it was survival, and it was, but it was also fear, and I'm done confusing the two."

The snow fell. The little green shoot trembled beside his knee.

"He seasoned me, you know. All those doses. Eleven years of building an immunity, drop by drop, and I thought it was love, or at least usefulness. It was neither. It was preparation. He built me into a weapon pointed at a feast." Juniper laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and short. "You'd have poisoned him yourself years ago. Properly. With thyme. Nobody suspects thyme."

Inside the jar, the summer brightened. Just slightly. Just enough that he could pretend.

"I'm going to open this," he said. "I don't know what happens. Wren thinks the soul will go back into the ground, back into the garden, because that's where she grew it from — your garden, your work, your *self*, rooted in this soil. And when it takes root, it burns. Green flame. The kind that eats lies." He swallowed. "It'll burn the charter magic too. The guild's whole foundation. Corvane's power. Everything."

He looked at the jar. At the shape in the light.

"It might hurt you. Coming back after thirty years asleep. It might hurt a great deal. And I have spent my whole life making certain that nothing harmful ever passed anyone's lips, that no bitter thing went untasted, and now the first gift I ever give you might be pain, and I—" His voice failed entirely. He pressed his forehead against the cool glass. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry it took me so long. I'm sorry I stayed small. I'm sorry I balanced his books instead of burning them. If you can hear any of this — if any part of you is in there — know that I am not asking permission. I'm telling you, so you won't be surprised. You taught me that much. You never surprised the plants."

The summer inside the jar pressed against the glass. Once. Gently. Like a hand on the other side of a door.

Juniper stood.

* * *

He heard the footsteps before he saw the man — heavy, deliberate, crunching through the crusted snow along the ridge path. Of course. Of course Corvane would come himself rather than send enforcers, because Corvane had never trusted anyone to close an account properly, least of all an account this old.

The Master of the Guild of Small Mercies came down into the ruined garden with snow in his white hair and his hands empty and open, and he stopped ten paces away, and he looked at the jar in Juniper's grip the way a man looks at a wound he inflicted long ago and convinced himself had healed.

"Juniper."

"Master Corvane." Juniper did not bow. He had bowed to this man ten thousand times, at doors and tables and thresholds, and he found that the muscle for it had simply died in the night, somewhere in the frost cells, somewhere between the scribe's sacrifice and the vault door. "Wren is coming with the ledgers. The real ones. Your falsifications, all thirty years. By tonight the winter court will have read every entry aloud."

Corvane nodded slowly, as if hearing a weather report. "Then we haven't much time. Give me the jar, boy."

"She has a name."

"She has a *function*." Corvane's voice was calm, reasonable, terribly gentle — the voice he used when correcting a novice's ledger errors. "One soul, traded for a charter that feeds four hundred people. Four hundred orphans and strays and poisoners' children who would have frozen or starved or swung from a gibbet without the guild's protection. I did the arithmetic. You of all people understand arithmetic."

"I understand arithmetic." Juniper's breath smoked between them. "That's how I caught you. Thirty years of entries in your hand, and every one of them balances, and that's how I knew they were false. Real accounts don't balance perfectly. Real accounts have grief in them somewhere."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes — not remorse, nothing so useful, but the flicker of a man watching a carefully tended fire catch a wind he hadn't priced in. "I was merciful," he said. "I could have sold her to the flesh-markets. I could have sold *you*. Instead I raised you. Fed you. Taught you a craft. I gave your mother's death meaning and your motherless life a purpose, and this—"

"This is the interest," Juniper said. "Coming due."

Down the mountain, faint but unmistakable, a bell began to ring. Then a second bell. Then many, arrhythmic, urgent — the sound of a city noticing that the snow had begun to fall upward.

Juniper looked at the sky. The flakes were rising now, slowly, pulled back toward the clouds as though the whole false winter were being retracted, unspent, returned to sender. And beneath his feet, in the warm circle of melted earth, he felt a pulse. Faint. Patient. The garden, waiting.

"You're going to do it regardless," Corvane said. It was not a question.

"You seasoned me for a feast," Juniper said. "Consider this the meal."

He broke the seal.

* * *

There was no explosion. There was no roar. What there was, was *midsummer*.

Light poured out of the jar like water finding its level, gold and thick and smelling of lavender and cut grass and hot stone, and it went into the ground the way rain goes into drought-cracked earth — eagerly, deeply, with a sound almost like drinking. Juniper staggered back. The green shoot at his knee shivered, doubled, tripled, became a dozen shoots, a hedge, a young tree. Frost fled outward from the garden in a widening ring, and everywhere it retreated, things grew — moss over the toppled stones, grass through the grey soil, buds swelling black and tight on the charred trunks like fists unclenching.

And then the garden caught fire.

Green flame — there was no other word for it, though it burned nothing it touched. It rose through the new growth in silent columns, emerald and gold, and where it rose, the *air itself* changed. Juniper felt it pass through him and it did not burn; it *read* him, the way he read a dish, cataloguing bitterness, naming what was true — and then it went past him, down the mountain, into the city, into the foundations of the world.

He heard the guild's charter break. Not with his ears. With something older. A sound like a great ledger slamming shut, like a debt being torn in half, like thirty years of impossible entries being struck through in a single stroke of emerald ink. Far below, he knew, the false accounting was guttering out — the bottled echoes evaporating, the borrowed luck returning to its lenders, the sealed contracts dissolving into blank paper. Everything the guild had built on his mother's stolen soul was coming down at once, gently, the way snow comes down.

Corvane cried out.

Not in pain. Worse than pain. He cried out the way a man cries out when the floor becomes transparent and he sees, for the first time, exactly how far the fall goes. The green light washed over him and through him, reading him, naming him — *one soul, sold; one winter, falsified; thirty years, stolen* — and where it passed, the power went out of him like heat leaving a dead coal. He did not fall. He simply became *smaller*. A man. Only a man. Standing in a burning garden that would not burn him, because even the fire had more mercy than he had ever shown.

"The account," Corvane whispered. His hands were trembling. "The account is—"

"Closed," Juniper said.

And in the heart of the green fire, at the center of the garden, where the single shoot had been, a woman made of midsummer stood with her back to him, tending.

She was translucent. She was brief. She was bending over a bed by a wall, gathering something at midsummer, humming — and Juniper knew the tune, had known it all his life without knowing its source, had hummed it over poison vials and ledger pages in the loneliest hours of the smallest years. She straightened. She turned.

She had his eyes.

She looked at him the way she must have looked at seedlings: measuring, proud, already planning what he'd become. Her mouth moved, and though no sound crossed the distance, he heard it anyway, in the place where names live:

*You taste everything, my love. Taste this. This is joy.*

Then the green flame rose through her like sunrise through a window, and she was everywhere, and she was gone, and the garden blazed with a fire that warmed without consuming, and the last of the false winter lifted off the mountain like a cloth being drawn back from a finished meal.

Juniper Yarrow stood in the ashes of the garden that had never really died, holding an empty jar, laughing and weeping in exactly equal measure, tasting the air on his careful tongue and finding, at last, nothing bitter in it at all.

Behind him, boots crunched in the slush. He turned.

Wren stood at the garden's edge, snow-dusted, wild-eyed, clutching three ledgers to his chest like a man carrying infants out of a fire — and behind Wren, climbing the ridge path with the unhurried confidence of people who own the cold, came figures in the pale livery of the winter court, their herald bearing a scroll sealed with wax the color of deep ice.

"Taster," the herald said, unrolling it. "The Undercourt acknowledges the theft of one soul, the breaking of one seal, and the opening of one door. The court remembers. The court requires an accounting." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "You are summoned to testify."
