## 20. Testimony of a Taster

The hall where the winter court held its judgments had once been a granary. Juniper knew this because Wren told him, in the low urgent voice she used for things that mattered, on the walk from the mountain's mouth to the city gates. *They built the court above the grain stores so the old kings could watch the people starve politely.* Now the great room stood empty of grain and full of people instead — nobles in furs gone gray at the cuffs, guildmasters in their sober blacks, the young king on his plain oak throne with frost still melting from the windows behind him, because the long winter had broken only three days ago and the world had not yet learned to believe it.

Juniper stood at the center of the floor with two ledgers under his arm and his hands, for the first time in his life, trembling where everyone could see.

He had spent nineteen years learning not to be seen. He knew every trick of it: how to stand at the edge of a room so the eye slid past him; how to keep his face as blank as an unsalted broth; how to make himself, in the language of his profession, *small*. A taster who was noticed was a taster who was dead, or worse, a taster who was useful to someone's ambition. His whole life had been built on the creed Master Corvane had fed him with his morning immunity doses, drop by patient drop: *stay small, boy. Small things survive.*

Corvane stood now at the edge of that same floor, flanked by two guards who were not there to protect him. They had taken his seal ring. His robes were the plain black of a guildmaster under accusation rather than above it, and he looked, for the first time since Juniper had known him, like an old man who had been caught standing in a garden he had burned.

Their eyes met across the hall. Corvane did not look away. That, more than anything, steadied Juniper's hands.

"Name yourself," said the Herald of the winter court — a different Herald than the one below the mountain, this one tall and gray-robed, her voice carrying the particular dryness of someone who had heard a thousand confessions and expected to be bored by most of them.

And here it was. The door he had chosen, the one that locked behind him.

"Juniper Yarrow," he said, and heard his own name come back off the stone walls, multiplied, given to a hundred ears at once. It sounded strange out loud. It sounded like someone else's name, someone who mattered. "I am — I was — a taster of the Guild of Small Mercies. For nineteen years I tasted the food of princes and merchants and magistrates, and no one knows my face, and that was the point of me." He let that sit. Let them hear what the guild had made him. "Today I am here to be known."

He opened the first ledger.

* * *

Wren had spent three sleepless nights preparing them, and it showed in the margins — her cramped annotations in green ink, cross-referencing dates, flagging the entries that had been altered, the ones written in Corvane's hand over older, honest entries. But the reading itself was Juniper's work. He had insisted on that. Wren could prove the falsification; only he could give the numbers their weight, because he had grown up inside them, had learned arithmetic by counting the guild's impossible inventory, had believed, until a month ago, that all of it was simply how the world worked.

"A bottled echo," he read. "Purchased from the drowned choir of Saltmere, year one of the old king's reign. Sold to Lady Vessamine Hale, who wished to hear her dead son's voice at dinner. Price: her memory of his face. She is alive. She does not know why she cannot picture him."

A murmur went through the hall. Somewhere in the third row, a woman in a gray fur began quietly to weep.

"A year of borrowed luck. Drawn from the common luck-pool of the fisher-families of the Dock Ward — drawn without their knowledge or consent. Sold to the shipping concern of Aldous Crane. In the year following, Crane's ships never sank. Eleven fisher boats did. Forty-three souls lost, none of whom ever signed anything."

He kept reading. His voice did not shake anymore; it had found the flat, even cadence of his trade, the voice he used when cataloguing bitterness in a birthday feast, and there was something in that which made the hall quieter than any orator could have. This was not performance. This was a man doing his job.

"The tears of a grief-struck widow, harvested over seven years and sold as an ingredient in love philtres. The last warm day of a summer, bottled and sold to a southern lord who wished to feel it twice. The name of a midwife, purchased and worn by a fraud who delivered children badly under it for a decade before the real midwife's own hands forgot their trade."

Each entry, he named the buyer and the seller and the price, and each price, he noticed, was always the same kind of thing: something the seller could not afford to lose, and therefore something they would pay. Wren had taught him the pattern in one sentence, hunched over candlelight in her cellar: *the court beneath trades in what you cannot afford to lose, and the guild has been its fence for thirty years.*

At the edge of the hall, Corvane watched him with an expression Juniper could not catalogue. Not rage. Not fear. Something almost like pride, which was the most obscene thing in the room.

* * *

It took two hours to reach the sealed entry.

He had known it would. He had rehearsed this part alone, in the ruined garden, speaking to ash and the first stubborn green shoots pushing through it, until the words stopped tasting like blood. But rehearsal, he learned now, was nothing like the moment. The hall had grown restless. The king had leaned forward twice. And the entry itself sat on the page in Corvane's unmistakable hand, the wax seal beside it stamped with the guild's sigil — a small mercy, the open palm — pressed hard enough to crack the paper.

Juniper set his palm flat on the page, the way a taster sets a hand over a dish to feel its heat, and read.

"One soul. Sold to the Court Beneath the Mountain, thirty-one years ago, in exchange for the guild's charter and the charter's protections in perpetuity." His voice held. He made it hold. "The soul of a gardener. The seller's signature—" and here he paused, because here was the thing he had discovered in chapter and margin and sleepless night, the thing that had cracked his well-ordered life down the middle like river ice, "—is my own family name. Yarrow."

Silence, total, the kind of silence he had spent his life creating around poisoned dishes.

"The gardener's name was Maren Yarrow," he said. "She kept a garden on the south slope of the mountain. The guild's records list her as a rival claimant to a spring that fed both her garden and the guild's cellars. The charter required a soul of true growing things. The Master chose hers." He looked up, and found Corvane's eyes, and did not look away this time either. "She was my mother. I was four years old. I do not remember her face. I remember her hands, and the smell of her recipe cards, and I remember being told she had died of fever. The fever was a ledger entry. Everything else was a lie seasoned into me one dose at a time."

He turned the page toward the Herald, toward the king, toward the hall.

"I have brought the ledgers entire," he said. "Every impossible trade. Every name. I will read them all if this court requires it, though I warn you it will take until spring, and some of you will find your own signatures in them, and I would ask you — I would ask all of you — to remember, when you do, that the guild's whole method was to make sure no one was ever asked to sign in daylight. Small trades. Quiet trades. Trades made by people trained their whole lives to stay small." He closed the ledger. "I was one of those people. I am choosing not to be, in front of all of you, permanently, and I understand exactly what it costs, because I have read the price lists."

The Herald descended from her dais and took the ledgers from his hands with the gravity of a priest receiving relics. "These will be copied under seal," she said. "Every entry verified. Every signature weighed." Then, softer, only for him: "You understand you can never taste anonymously again. Every kitchen in this city will know your face. Every enemy of every house you've saved will know it too."

"Yes," Juniper said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They gave Corvane the floor afterward, because the law of the winter court was old and insisted on balance, and because — Juniper suspected — the king wanted to watch him try.

Corvane walked to the center of the hall the way he had walked through the guildhouse for thirty years, unhurried, certain of his ground. Up close, Juniper could see what the accusation had done to him: the fine tremor in his hands, the way his eyes moved to Juniper and stayed there, hungry and grieving at once, as though Juniper were a dish that had been spoiled at the last moment.

"I traded one soul," Corvane said, and his voice filled the hall the way it had filled a hundred lessons. "One. To save a guild of two hundred, whose work — whose *actual* work, whatever these ledgers say about what we fenced along the way — has saved ten thousand lives. Tasters die so that princes don't. Poisons are caught so that cities don't burn. I made one terrible trade to keep that machine running, and yes—" his gaze cut to Juniper, and there it was, the paternal menace laid bare at last, "—I took her son. As penance. As insurance. As mercy, if any of you can stomach the word. What was I to do with a gardener's orphan? Leave him to the streets? I raised him. I fed him. I made him immune to half the poisons of the known world, and if that immunity was aimed, in the end, at a single feast — then ask yourselves what I was protecting him *from*, and whether a man who plans murder seasons his instrument with love."

"You doctored the ledgers for thirty years," Wren said from the gallery, her voice cracking across the hall like a whip. She came down the steps with the falsification folios hugged to her chest, ink-stained and furious and magnificent. "You rewrote entries in your own hand. You sold a soul and called it a fever. You fed a child poison in measured doses and called it fatherhood. Read your own confession, Master. It's in your handwriting too."

"It is," Corvane said simply. And then, to the hall, to the king, to Juniper: "I regret nothing except that the boy learned to read the margins."

The king spoke then, for the first time — a young voice, still finding its weight. "The Guild of Small Mercies is suspended. Its charter is void; the court beneath will learn that its protections have burned. Master Corvane will stand trial. The ledgers will be copied, verified, and read aloud in public session until every name in them is answered for." His eyes found Juniper. "Including yours, taster. You handled their goods for nineteen years. You'll answer for it too — openly, under your own name, like everyone else."

"That," said Juniper, "is the fairest thing anyone has offered me in my life. I accept."

* * *

Afterward, in the corridor outside the hall, with the winter light coming through high windows in long pale blades and the sound of the city thawing beyond the walls — dripping eaves, cart wheels in slush, someone laughing — Wren caught his sleeve.

"You did it," she said. "You absolute madman, you actually did it. You're going to be famous. Do you understand that? Tasters aren't supposed to have names. You've broken the whole craft."

"I know." He looked at his hands. They had finally stopped trembling. "Wren — when they copy the ledgers. When they read them aloud, session after session, all the way through." He hesitated, and hated himself for hesitating, and said it anyway. "There's a page near the end. The last entries. Entries the guild recorded but never sold. Souls the Undercourt still holds. Names, and vault locations, and the terms of return."

Wren went very still. "Juniper. Those pages describe dozens of people."

"People," he agreed. "Not entries. My mother was in a jar for thirty years while I learned to call a prison a home. There are others." He turned toward the window, toward the mountain, white and clean in the new light, its ruined garden a dark scar on the south slope where green fire had burned away a false winter. "And three days ago, a letter arrived from the court beneath. Sealed in wax I recognized. It said: *the vault remembers who opened the door.*"

Wren stared at him. "What does that mean?"

"I don't know yet," Juniper said. "But I kept the map. And I'm going to find out."
