## 1. Charms for a Magicless City

The trick to selling magic in a city that has outlawed it is to sell something else entirely and let the customer imagine the rest.

I learned this from my mother, though she never said it outright. She said other things — *keep your hands moving, keep your mouth humble, never let them see you listen* — and by the time I was old enough to understand what she'd been teaching me, she was gone, and I was standing behind a stall on Ashrow Lane with a tray of river-stones wrapped in red thread, watching a baker's widow pay three coppers for love.

"Three coppers," said the widow, Maren Ostell, whose bread I had eaten every week since her husband drowned in the canal two springs past. She held the little bundle up to the light as if she could see through the cloth into whatever lived inside it. Nothing lived inside it. It was a river-stone, a thread, and a prayer-shaped silence.

"You'll want to keep it near the bed," I told her. "Warmth wakes it."

"Wakes what?"

"Whatever's sleeping in there." I smiled the smile I kept for this — soft at one corner, apologetic at the other, the smile of a man who deals in small wonders and won't oversell them. "I can't promise it'll make him love you more than he did. That kind of thing doesn't come back. But it holds what's left of it close. Keeps the cold out of the corners."

Maren's eyes went wet, which was not the charm's doing either, unless you counted me as the charm. She paid. She always paid. And I always felt the specific shame of a man who has just sold a stone for three coppers to a grieving woman — and beneath the shame, quieter, the worse knowledge: *if I wanted to, it would work.*

Not the stone. The wanting. There is a word in the old tongue for what my family carried, and the word has been scrubbed from the crier's lists and the schoolroom primers and the gravestones, but it lives in my sword-hand the way a scar lives in skin. Swordspeaker. One who speaks and the steel answers. My mother used to say our gift was older than crowns, which sounds like flattery until you learn it was meant as an accusation.

I wrapped Maren's change, wished her warm nights, and turned to rearranging my stock so I wouldn't have to watch her go.

* * *

Ashrow Lane opens its stalls an hour after the bells, because Ashrow Lane is the part of Corvane where ambition goes to die quietly. We sell charms and mended pots, secondhand boots, candles stubbed down to honesty. My stall sits between Tamsin Vetch's herb-table and a cooper who is mostly wine by noon. On a good day I take in twelve coppers. On a bad day I take in conversation, which pays worse and costs more.

Tamsin arrived while I was counting nothing, carrying her basket the way she carries everything: like a grievance with handles.

"You're out of feverfew," she said, setting the basket down hard enough to bruise the yarrow.

"I'm never out of feverfew. You're out of feverfew. Mine's under the table, where yours would be if you didn't give half your stock away to people who can't pay."

"People who can't pay are most of my stock," she said. "Give it here."

I passed her the paper twist through the gap between our stalls, the way we'd done a thousand mornings, and she counted leaves with her thumb before trusting me, the way she'd done a thousand mornings, and neither of us called it anything but trade. That was Tamsin. A hedge-healer with cracked knuckles and a dead sister she mentioned the way other people mention weather — often, briefly, and without expecting an answer.

"Crier's coming up the lane," said the cooper, who heard everything first because he did nothing second.

We all heard it then. The bells had gone strange that morning — I'd noticed it at dawn, a rhythm off by half a beat, and had told myself it was a new bell-ringer with a limp in his wrist. But now came footsteps in the matched cadence of city guardsmen, and ahead of them a voice pitched to carry over rooftops.

"Hear ye! By decree of the Crown Regent, acting in the name of His Majesty the King, long may he sleep—" everyone knew what *long may he sleep* meant; the King had been sleeping eleven years, and the Regent ruled in his name the way a thief rules in the name of a locked door— "a purge of unlawful arts is declared throughout Corvane and the demesnes thereof. All practitioners of hedge-craft, blood-reading, word-binding, sword-speaking, and other gifts of the old corruption shall surrender themselves to the Crown's mercy or be taken by the Crown's justice. Informants will be compensated. Shelterers will be charged as accomplices. Hear ye!"

The lane went quiet in the way a field goes quiet when hawks pass over. Not silent — hawks don't stop the sparrows' hearts — but everything small went still and waited to be counted.

I kept my hands moving. Bundle, wrap, tie. Bundle, wrap, tie. My mother's voice in my skull: *keep your hands moving.* Across the way, Tamsin had gone white around the mouth, and I watched her do arithmetic with her eyes — feverfew, birch-bark tinctures, the poultices she brewed by feel and whispered over, the whole honest gray edge of her craft sliding now onto the wrong side of a list read aloud by men with spears.

*Word-binding,* the crier had said. *Sword-speaking.*

Twelve years since anyone had spoken those words in daylight in Corvane. Twelve years since my mother pressed my hand flat against a blade and made me swear an oath I still carry like a swallowed hook, and I had spent those twelve years being exactly what the crier's list could not find: a charm-seller who sold stones and thread and the shape of a silence, harmless as rain.

"Hear ye," said the crier, further off now, the words thinning. "Informants will be compensated."

The cooper whistled low. "Compensated," he repeated, tasting it. "That's new. Last purge they only promised forgiveness."

"Forgiveness is free," Tamsin said bitterly. "Copper makes people look at their neighbors." And then, lower, not looking at me: "Paloma. Your stall. Some of what you sell—"

"Is river-stones and red thread," I said. "Worthless. Always has been."

She looked at me then, and there was a moment — I felt it like a draft under a door — where I thought she might say something else, might ask something else. Tamsin Vetch is many things, but slow about people is not one of them. Three years of mornings, and I have never once seen her fooled by anything except her own generosity.

But the guards were walking the lane now, unhurried, reading faces the way tax men read ledgers, and whatever she saw in mine she folded away unasked. "Feverfew's short-weighted," she said instead. "You owe me a leaf."

"I owe you a garden."

"A leaf will do." She turned back to her basket, and we both pretended very hard to work while the law strolled past us, close enough that I could smell the lamp-oil they burn in their lanterns even at midmorning, because witch-hunters, my mother once told me, never trust the sun.

One of them paused at my stall. Young, jaw like a shovel, spear butt planted with the confidence of someone who has never been hit with his own weapon.

"What's your trade?"

"Charms," I said. "Harmless ones. Love-luck, hearth-blessing, safe travel. Superstition, mostly. No craft in it."

He picked up one of the bundles — Maren Ostell's twin, stone and thread — and turned it over. "These do anything?"

"They do whatever the buyer needs them to," I said, which was true, and which was the closest I have ever come to confessing my gift in public, because that, too, is true of every charm I've ever sold: the magic was never in the stone. It was in the woman holding it, believing. I only sold the container. The faith was hers, free of charge, and no decree yet written has outlawed that.

The guard grunted, put it down, moved along. My heart resumed operations at roughly triple rate.

* * *

Midday, and the lane emptied early. Fear empties streets faster than rain does, and it lingers longer. I ate bread and hard cheese behind the stall and listened to Ashrow Lane talk to itself in the lowered voices of people checking whether their neighbors are listening.

Old Ferren from the lamplighters' row had already stopped speaking to his sister-in-law — she reads tea-leaves, badly, more comfort than craft, but *badly* is a distinction the crier's list does not honor. The cooper was telling anyone who'd stand still that he'd reported a fortune-teller on Coppersmith Street last spring, back when reporting was merely pious, and was visibly recalculating whether piety might now be made to pay. Tamsin packed her basket early and stood a moment at my shoulder, looking at the tray of little wrapped stones.

"If they come asking," she said quietly, "you tell them I buy herbs from you and nothing else. Give them something dull to hold. Men like that relax when they think they've caught you being boring."

"And if they come asking about you?"

"Then you tell them I'm tedious and bad with money." Almost a smile. "Which is half true."

She went, and I sat with my useless beautiful stones, and I did the thing I do every night and had done every morning and would do every day until the day it failed: I checked the leash.

Every oath has a weight. Most people never feel theirs — they swear loyalty to a guild or a marriage or a crown and carry it the way you carry your own name, unnoticed because it's everywhere you are. But a swordspeaker feels oaths the way a hound smells weather. The oath my mother bound into me at nine years old, hand flat on the blade, her voice steady and her heartbeat hammering through her palm into mine — that oath runs from my spine down my sword-arm and anchors somewhere I've never been able to see. To the crown. To whoever wears the crown. To the thing the crown serves, though I didn't know then that crowns serve anything.

It had never pulled before. Twelve years, and it lay quiet as a scar, and I had learned to call it part of my body.

Today, when I reached for it with that inner listening my mother warned me never to let anyone see, it was — awake. Not pulling. Not yet. Just awake, the way a chain is awake when the hand on its far end shifts its grip.

Somewhere above us, in the pale winter light, the Regent's clerks were drawing up lists. And somewhere below — I didn't know below yet, wouldn't know for days — something older than the Regent's office leaned against its end of the chain to see if I was still attached.

I sold four charms that afternoon. A girl with chapped hands bought a luck-knot for her brother going down the mines. A man bought protection-from-theft, which is a stone and a lie, and lied to himself so thoroughly about it that he left lighter-footed than he came. Harmless. All of it harmless. All of it a hiding place built out of other people's hope, mortared with sleight of hand, roofed with my mother's instructions, and I had lived in it so long I had mistaken it for a life.

At dusk I closed the shutters and counted the take: nineteen coppers and a button. I remember the button. I remember thinking, absurdly, that if the purge took me, the inventory of my life would come to nineteen coppers and a button, and that some clerk would write it down in a ledger, and that would be the whole of Paloma Galloway, last swordspeaker in Corvane: *charms, worthless; man, harmless; soul — *

The thought snagged there. *Soul.* I don't know why. Grief does odd bookkeeping, and I was grieving the lane already, grieving the mornings with Tamsin and the cooper's wine-warmed gossip and the particular safety of being nobody. But the word turned over in me like a stone in a streambed, showing a shine underneath.

My mother, the last night I ever saw her, had said something strange. She'd kissed my forehead and held my face in both hands and said, *Whatever they tell you about me, Paloma, remember I paid full price.* I'd asked her what she meant. She'd smiled my own smile back at me — soft at one corner, apologetic at the other — and said, *Nothing, love. Keep your hands moving.*

Nineteen coppers and a button. Full price for what?

I was still holding the button when the knocking started — not at my door, but up the lane, urgent, arrhythmic, the knocking of someone who has run out of gentler ways to ask for help. And under the knock, faint as a bell through fog, I felt the oath in my arm shift its grip again, and this time it pulled.

Someone was coming down Ashrow Lane who should not have been able to walk.
