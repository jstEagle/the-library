## 2. The Dying Man at the Door

The trouble with selling charms that do nothing is that you have to be very good at doing nothing convincingly.

I'd had twelve years of practice. My stall on Ashrow Lane sold love knots woven from river reed, luck coins polished until they lied about their age, and small glass bottles of "bottled courage" that were, in point of fact, cheap wine with a dramatic label. The wine was the honest part. People bought it because they wanted to believe, and belief is the only magic I've ever seen work reliably in a city that banned the real thing.

It was a grey morning in the month of Frostmarch, the kind where the fog comes off the canal and sits on the cobbles like something owed money. I was arranging my luck coins — largest on top, they sell better when they look abundant — when the bell over my door did something it hadn't done in years.

It rang twice.

Once for the door. Once, a heartbeat later, for the weight that fell against it.

I looked up from my coins and there was a man in my doorway, holding himself upright by the frame the way a drowning man holds a spar. He was tall, or he would have been if his spine had been cooperating, and he was dressed in the plain grey wool of a courier, except couriers don't usually bleed through theirs.

"Shut the door," he said.

Now. Here is what a sensible shopkeeper does when a bleeding stranger tells him to shut the door. A sensible shopkeeper says *I'll fetch the watch*, which is both true and useful, and then does exactly that.

I shut the door.

I want to be clear that this was not heroism. It was arithmetic. If the watch found a bleeding man in my shop, they would ask him questions, and then they would ask me questions, and I have spent twelve years making sure no one asks me questions. Shutting the door was self-interest wearing a brave coat. I've found most virtue is.

He came off the doorframe in stages, like a building deciding to collapse, and made it as far as my counter before his legs filed their resignation. I caught him under the arms — he weighed less than he should have, the way men do when they've been bleeding a while — and lowered him behind the counter among the crates of reed.

"You're Galloway," he said. "Paloma Galloway."

"That's the name on the sign."

"Don't play clever with me." His eyes were the problem. Not wild, the way I expected hunted men's eyes to be. Focused. He looked at me the way an auditor looks at a ledger he already knows is cooked. "You're the last one. You're the last swordspeaker."

My hands went still on his shoulders. Outside, the fog pressed against the window like a listener.

"I think you've mistaken me for someone interesting," I said. "I sell love charms. They don't even work."

"I know they don't work." Something that might have been a laugh moved through him and cost him dearly; he coughed, and the cough was wet. "That's how I knew. Only a man hiding something sells charms that don't work. Honest frauds make them work. You're too good a liar to be a fraud."

There's a compliment in there somewhere. I've decided not to look for it.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about the court beneath the mountain, since apparently I'm going to spend this whole account dancing around it otherwise.

Every child in Vael Corren grows up on the story. Under the mountain at the city's back — the one we call the Crown's Tooth, because flattery is cheaper than fear — there is a court older than our crown. The old kings didn't conquer it. They *contracted* with it. That's the word the histories use, all smooth and mercantile: contracted. As though you could put a signature under something with teeth that old and walk away owning anything but your own funeral.

What the court buys, the stories disagree on. Souls, mostly. Years. Names. Firstborn children, in the versions mothers use to get sons to bed early. What the court *sells* is never disputed: peace. Prosperity. A decade here, a generation there, purchased at rates no honest bank would post.

And every story agrees on one more thing, the thing mothers whisper and priests deny and the crown has spent a great deal of ink denying harder: the court keeps what is sold. Forever. There is no buyback. There is no appeal. The mountain does not do refunds.

I'd always assumed it was a story the way most founding stories are — a lie with architecture. Useful for explaining why the rich are rich and why you shouldn't go out after dark.

I am telling you this now so you understand that when the dying man in my shop started talking about the court beneath the mountain, my first instinct was not awe. It was the specific irritation of a man who has built a careful life being handed a reason it might end.

* * *

He'd propped himself against the counter, and I'd done what little I could — pressed a rag to the wound under his ribs, which was deep and neat and made by someone who knew blades. His blood was darker than I expected. Everything about him was slightly wrong for a courier: the calluses were in the wrong places, the boots too good, and under the grey wool, when he shifted, I caught the glint of a chain.

"Who cut you?" I asked.

"The crown," he said, as if reporting weather. "Inquisitor Verel's people. I carried something they wanted back."

"And you brought it to a charm-seller on Ashrow Lane."

"I brought it to the last swordspeaker in Vael Corren, because in three days there won't be anywhere left to bring it." He gripped my wrist. His hand was cold in a way that had nothing to do with the fog. "Listen to me. The hunt isn't random. It's not fear, it's not piety, whatever they're saying from the pulpits. Verel is sweeping the city for the gifted because the crown needs them. Needs them *specifically*. Do you understand? Oath-bound gifts. Speakers, binders, oath-keepers. The crown is collecting its own chains before—"

He coughed again, longer this time, and I held the rag harder against his ribs and felt, absurdly, my own heart doing something it hadn't done in years. Reaching. The gift doesn't live in the sword alone; it lives in the wanting-to-help, the old reflex that got my mother killed and got me renamed and got me a stall full of useless glass bottles. I pushed it down. Twelve years of practice. I'm very good at nothing.

"There's no one coming," I told him. "Whatever you think I am, I can't help you."

"You already helped me. You shut the door." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, gray around the edges. "That's more than anyone's done since the bells started."

Outside, distantly, a crier was calling the morning proclamation. Even through the walls I could hear the new words threaded into the old list of prices and grain tariffs. *By order of the Regent's Justice: any person found practicing the gifted arts...* The sentence ended the way those sentences always end now, in a word that used to mean *confiscation* and lately means something with more ash in it.

"They'll search the lane today," the man said. "They're working outward from the canal districts. This street is tomorrow's sweep, maybe tonight's. Which means you have a choice to make faster than you'd like."

"I really don't. My choice is that you leave and I never met you."

"With three broken ribs and a hole in you?"

"With whatever you have," I said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it, and said it anyway, because cowardice honestly stated is at least a kind of honesty. "I have a life here. It's small, but it's mine, and it's the only version of it I get."

He nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something on a form. Then he took the chain from around his neck.

On it hung a token. Bone, I thought at first — pale, dense, worn smooth as a river stone — but bone doesn't have that faint warmth, and it doesn't hum against your skin like a struck tuning fork remembering its note. It was the size of a coin and twice as thick, and carved into its face was a spiral that hurt to follow with the eye, the kind of line that seems to keep turning after you stop looking.

"A debt-token," he said. "From below. From the court."

"Then it's worthless. The court doesn't honor debts. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what the crown teaches them." He pressed it into my palm and closed my fingers over it, and the warmth of the thing sank straight through my skin into the meat of my hand, into the wrist, up the arm, along paths I had spent twelve years pretending weren't there. My sword-hand. Of course it sang loudest there. "This one's different. This one's a claim, not a payment. Whoever holds it holds a lien on a soul kept below. One soul. Named. Held in the garden of oaths, in the Keeper's ledger, under seal."

"Garden of oaths," I repeated. "You're delirious."

"I'm dying. It's different. Delirious men invent things. Dying men finally say what's true." His grip tightened. "Twelve years ago, a bargain was struck beneath the Crown's Tooth. A life for a life. A woman bought her son back from a fever that should have taken him, and the price was—" he tapped my closed fist, once, gently, "—that. The token names the debt. And the debt has a name, Galloway. I read it. I was a clerk down there, before I ran. Eleven years reading the ledger. Your name is written in it in your mother's hand."

The shop went very quiet. The fog outside, the crier, the canal — all of it receded, and there was just the hum of the token in my fist and this stranger's cold fingers and the sound of my own breathing, which had stopped being a thing I did automatically and become a thing I had to remember.

"My mother died twelve years ago," I said.

"Yes," he said. "That was rather the point of the transaction."

* * *

I want to tell you I threw him out then. That some part of me — the sensible part, the part that had arranged those luck coins largest-on-top — stood up and said *no, take your bone trinket and your fever story and bleed on someone else's floor.*

What I actually did was sit down on a crate next to him, because my legs had opinions of their own, and ask, in a voice I barely recognized:

"Why give it to me? If it's my soul, why not keep it? Sell it? Use it?"

"Because a claim is only worth what it can buy," he said, "and I need it to buy one thing." He leaned close, and his breath smelled of copper and of something older, mineral, like the air in a cave. "The court keeps what is sold. But a claim-holder can enter. Can *speak* for the soul. No one else can. Not the crown — the crown trades, it doesn't redeem. Not the priests — they won't come near it. Only the named, or the one who holds the named's token." His eyes were burning now, fever-bright, and I understood that everything he'd done since the door — the bleeding, the begging, the dying — had been in service of getting these sentences out. "Verel can't let that token exist. Because if the garden can be spoken to, the garden can be *questioned*. And the garden remembers every bargain the crown ever broke."

"Broke," I said. "Not kept?"

His smile again. Gray. Terrible.

"What do you think fuels a court that old, Galloway? What do you think burns under the mountain, decade after decade, bright enough to light a kingdom's peace?" He laughed, softly, horribly. "Oaths. Every oath sworn to the crown goes into the fire. Yours too. Especially yours."

And that was when the knocking started.

Three blows on the door, spaced like a heartbeat. Then a voice, official and bored, the worst combination the crown produces: "Open in the name of the Regent's Justice. Street inspection."

* * *

Here is a thing about fear that nobody puts in the songs: it clarifies. All those years I thought my caution was a fog, a soft gray blur I lived inside. It wasn't. It was a lens. In the space between the second knock and the third, I saw my whole life with terrible sharpness — the stall, the fake charms, the sword wrapped in oilcloth under my floorboards, the name Galloway worn thin as a coin from disuse — and I saw exactly how much of it was scaffolding around one secret, and how quickly scaffolding burns.

"In the back," I whispered. "There's a cellar. The hatch is under the reed—"

"No." He was already shaking his head, and the motion cost him; blood came up with his breath. "I'm done running. Look at me. I've been running for nine days. The token moves faster without me." He pressed my fist tighter around it. "Hear the warning before they come in. The court beneath the mountain *keeps what is sold* — but it lies about what was paid. Whatever you find below, whatever they show you, whatever your mother's voice says to you — the ledger is true and the stories are not. Find the garden. Read the fire. And Galloway—" his hand slipped from mine, and he sagged against the counter, and still he kept his eyes on me, furious and alive, "—don't swear anything down there. Not one word. Not to save your skin. Not to save hers. An oath is a chain, and that court collects chains."

The knocking became kicking.

I did the coward's arithmetic one last time, fast: cellar for him, impossible, no time; cellar for me, possible, five seconds, maybe six. Out the back and over the wall into Tamsin's yard, where the hedge-healer would hide me and curse me and hide me anyway.

I looked at the man. He looked back, and mouthed one word — *garden* — and then the door came in.

They were quick, I'll grant them that. Two in grey-and-gold, Verel's colors, crossbows level, and behind them a clerk with a slate, because the crown kills you and files you in the same motion. They saw the blood. They saw the man. One of them said something procedural, and the man in my shop — whose name I never learned, whose body I left cooling behind my counter among the harmless charms — laughed at them.

He laughed, and said, "Read the ledger before you burn it," which meant nothing to them and everything to me, and the crossbow went off, and then it was very quiet, the way it is after.

I was already through the back hatch. I heard the clerk's boots overhead, the crash of my luck coins scattering across the floorboards, someone saying *check the cellar* — and then the wall, and Tamsin's yard, and the hedge, and the long grey fall of Frostmarch fog swallowing me lane by lane.

I ran. I'm not proud of it. I ran with a dead man's warm token clenched in my fist, humming against my palm like a second pulse, like something patient, like something that had waited twelve years and could wait a while longer.

By the time I reached the canal, I had stopped shaking. That frightened me more than the guards had. Fear that passes leaves resolve behind, the way a flood leaves silt — and standing there in the fog with the criers' voices moving through the streets like searchlights, I understood that the small careful life I'd built was already ash behind me, and that ahead of me there was only one road, and it went down.

The court beneath the mountain keeps what is sold.

My name was in the ledger.

I turned toward the mountain, and the token burned in my hand like a promise, and I began — God help me — to plan.
