## 3. What the Token Says

The bone token sat on my workbench like a thing that had always lived there, patient as a spider in a corner you'd swear you'd looked at yesterday.

I hadn't slept. I want to be honest about that up front, because everything I did that night — the tea I forgot to drink, the candle I let gutter down to a puddle of tallow, the three times I picked the token up and put it down again like it might bite — everything makes more sense if you know I was running on no sleep and the particular adrenaline that comes from watching a man die on your doorstep while guards kicked in your neighbor's shutters.

The token was small, no bigger than a knucklebone, carved from something dense and yellowed that I wanted very much to believe was cow bone. It had a hole through its center, worn smooth, as if it had hung on a cord against someone's skin for years. And it was warm. Not body-warm. Warm the way a stone is warm at dusk, long after the sun has gone — warm with a heat that had nowhere to come from.

I turned it in my fingers. There were markings, fine as scratches from a cat's claw, and I'd spent a good hour telling myself they were decorative. A charm-seller learns to read marks the way a gambler learns to read faces: well enough to spot the dangerous ones, and to pretend not to see anything else.

But this was my shop, my table, my candle, and no one was watching.

I held it up to the flame.

The scratches drank the light, and then — slowly, the way frost creeps across a window — they moved. Rearranged. Became letters. Not the flowing court-script they teach to clerks and priests, but an older hand, angular and certain, the kind of writing that looks like it was carved rather than written, like the words had always been in the bone and the bone had simply decided to admit it.

I read it. Then I read it again, because the first time my eyes had clearly lied to me.

*One soul, whole and unspent, sold in the year of the Ash Frost.*

Below that, a name.

My name. *Paloma Galloway.*

Below that, the seller's mark — a small, looping signature that I would have known anywhere, that I had watched sign my school letters, my father's burial papers, the back of every receipt that ever crossed our kitchen table.

*Merren Galloway.*

My mother.

* * *

Here is what I knew about my mother, in the order I had always known it:

Merren Galloway was a practical woman. She mended nets in the spring, took in washing in the summer, and in the winter she sat by the stove and told me stories with the particular flatness of a woman who had decided that wonder was a luxury her household could not afford. She was not unkind. She was *careful*, the way people are careful who have been broken once and have studied the break.

My father died when I was five. Fever, they said. The winter after, I got the same fever, the same wet rattle in the chest, the same burning that had taken him, and my mother sat by my bed for three days doing nothing — nothing but cold cloths and broth and a stillness so complete it frightened me more than the sickness did.

On the third night, I remember, she leaned close and made me swear something.

I was five. I was burning. I would have sworn anything — I swore I'd never eat the crusts off another child's bread, I swore I'd be good, I swore I loved her more than the moon, which at five is the largest thing one can conceive of. But she held my face in her cracked hands and said, "No. Swear me this, and mean it, because I will hold you to it when you've forgotten why."

And she said: *Swear that you will never speak to a sword. Swear that if a blade ever answers you, you will not answer back. Swear it, Paloma. Swear it and live.*

I swore it. The fever broke the next morning. I always assumed the two things were unrelated — a child's oath and a child's recovery, coincidence dressed up in ceremony because my mother liked ceremony the way some people like salt: sparingly, and only where it was needed.

I did not know then what a swordspeaker was. I do not think most people in Ashrow knew. The gift is rare, and it hides well, because its first symptom is silence — a child who looks at the swords hanging in the guardhouse and feels them *looking back* learns quickly not to mention it. I was eleven the first time I answered one. Just once, just a whisper, a practice blade in a friend's father's shop that hummed when I passed and said, in a voice like a struck bell heard underwater, *you*.

I did not answer. I had sworn.

I went home and told my mother, because I was eleven and terrified and she was the only architecture in my world. I expected her to be frightened. I expected her to hold me. Instead she went very still, the way she'd gone still over my fever bed, and then she said, "Good. You kept it. Now keep it forever," and she made me swear the oath again, older, with my hand on our kitchen knife, and this time she wept while I said the words, which confused me more than anything, because my mother did not weep. My mother mended nets. My mother took in washing. My mother did not weep over kitchen knives.

I kept the oath. Twelve years of keeping it. Twelve years of selling love charms that did nothing and luck knots that did less, of letting the gift sit in me like a swallowed stone, of feeling every blade in every guardhouse turn its attention toward me like a field of flowers toward the sun — and never, not once, turning toward it.

I thought the oath was protection. I thought my mother, in her practical, careful way, had understood that the gift was dangerous — that the crown burned the gifted, and a swordspeaker was a bonfire wearing a boy's skin — and had built me a wall instead of a cradle.

I had built an entire life on that understanding. The stall. The sleight of hand. The harmless charms. The small gray shape of a man who was no one, who wanted nothing, who was safe because he was nothing at all.

The bone token sat on my workbench and called that understanding a lie.

* * *

Because here is what I knew now, in the order I was learning it:

One soul, whole and unspent. Sold in the year of the Ash Frost. The winter of my fever. The winter my father died.

Sold by Merren Galloway.

The dying man's words came back to me the way blood comes back into a slept-on limb, painful and slow and impossible to ignore. *The court beneath the mountain keeps what is sold.* He'd pressed the token into my hand like it was the last coin in his purse, like it was the last truth in his body, and he'd said — what had he said exactly? My memory of those minutes was a shattered window; I kept finding new shards. He'd said *it's yours*. He'd said *they'll come for what they're owed*. He'd said something else, something I'd been refusing to hear since the door closed behind the guards.

He'd said, "You don't even know what you're missing, do you? Gods. They never tell the children."

I sat with that for a long time.

A soul, sold. I had heard of it the way everyone in Ashrow had heard of it — in the stories you told at midwinter to make the dark feel intentional. The court beneath the mountain. The old bargain-place. A thing older than the crown, older than the city, that bought and sold in the only currency it valued. Most people treated it as a story with the serial numbers filed off. The crown treated it as heresy to speak of, which — I was beginning to understand — is what power says about the things it profits from.

And my mother, practical Merren, mender of nets, had sold mine.

I tried the kind alternatives first. It's what I do; I am a man who has made a career of harmless charms, and harmless charm begins at home. Perhaps the token was forged. Perhaps it was bait — the inquisitors luring the gifted with documents that named them. Perhaps *Paloma Galloway* was a common name, though I had never met another and my mother had chosen it from a saint nobody in Ashrow honored, and the seller's mark was a coincidence, and the year was a coincidence, and the fever, and the oath, and the weeping over the kitchen knife —

No. I ran out of kind alternatives somewhere around the kitchen knife.

The truth assembled itself with the ugly efficiency of all true things: my father died in the Ash Frost. I caught his fever. And my mother, watching her second beloved person burn from the inside in a single winter, went looking for a door, and found one, and it had a price, and the price was me.

But — and here is where I kept catching myself, turning the thought over like the token, looking for the trick in the carving — she'd bought something with it. The fever broke. The token said *sold*, not *sold and delivered*, and the dying man had said the court *keeps* what is sold, and a debt-token is a claim, not a possession. Whatever she'd bought with my soul, the court had been holding my soul in escrow for twelve years, and this token was the receipt.

Which meant I was a man walking around with a hole in him shaped exactly like himself, and I had never once noticed, because how would you? You can't feel the absence of a thing you've never had. You can only feel the strange weather where it should be — the way I'd always felt slightly unreal in crowds, slightly unanchored in my own bed, slightly like a man reading his own life over someone's shoulder.

I had called that feeling modesty. I had called it caution. I had built a whole personality on it — the harmless charm-seller, the gray man, the one who wants nothing because wanting requires a self to want *from*.

Gods. They never tell the children.

No. They don't. They make them swear oaths instead, and the children grow up thinking the oath is a wall, when all along it was a fence around a field that had already been sold.

* * *

The candle had burned down to almost nothing. Somewhere outside, Ashrow Lane was quiet in the way that is worse than noise — the quiet of a street holding its breath, shutters closed, everyone listening for boots.

I picked up the token one more time. I told myself I was looking for forgery-marks, for the tells of a con, because that was my trade and I knew every one of them. There were none. The bone was old. The warmth was old. The name was mine, and the signature was my mother's, and there was a date, and the date was the winter I burned.

And there was one more line, smaller than the rest, tucked at the bottom like a clerk's afterthought, and it took me several readings to accept it:

*Redeemable by bearer. Claim runs with the blood. The court beneath the mountain keeps what is sold — and what is kept may be reclaimed, by the sold or by the sold-from, at the court's pleasure.*

At the court's pleasure. Not mine. Not hers. The court would decide when and whether I could walk into the dark under the mountain and ask for the piece of myself that had been held as collateral since I was five years old.

I laughed. It came out wrong, a dry cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man who has just read the terms of his own mortgage and found the lender is a legend.

My mother had sold my soul to save my life, and the crown — the crown whose oath I carried in my sword, whose colors I'd sworn to, whose hunt was even now flushing the gifted into the streets like rats — the crown's own court, its own bargain, its own buried partner, held the deed. The oath I'd kept for twelve years, the wall I'd lived behind, and the soul that had been taken to buy my fever's end — all of it, all of it, threaded through the same dark needle.

I thought about the oath. I thought about how it had never once felt like mine — how it had felt, all these years, like wearing my mother's coat, too big in the shoulders, smelling of someone else's fear. I had thought that was inheritance. Love, in its clumsy practical way.

It was not inheritance. It was *terms and conditions*.

I set the token down. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me; I'd always prided myself on steady hands, the hands of a man who does close work, fine knots, delicate sleight. But they shook, and I let them, because it seemed suddenly important not to pretend, even alone, even to myself. Twelve years of pretending seemed like quite enough for one lifetime.

"Mother," I said to the empty shop, and my voice sounded like a stranger's. "What did you *do*?"

The candle went out. Not guttered — went out, all at once, the way a candle goes out when something in the room has decided the light was a courtesy it no longer wishes to extend.

And in the dark, from the street below, came the sound I had been dreading since the dying man's blood dried on my doorstep: boots. Many boots. Moving up Ashrow Lane with the unhurried rhythm of people who are not searching, because they already know.

I was at the back window with the token in my fist and my mother's oath in my throat before I heard the first shutter splinter — two doors down, where Tamsin the hedge-healer lived, the woman who bought my fake fever-tinctures and sold me real ones, and who was about to learn exactly what kind of man her neighbor was.

Because here is the thing about a wall you've kept for twelve years: it holds until the moment it doesn't. And the moment had just come up my street wearing the crown's livery.
