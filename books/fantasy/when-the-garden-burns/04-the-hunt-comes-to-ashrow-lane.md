## 4. The Hunt Comes to Ashrow Lane

The first thing I heard was the splintering of Tamsin's door, and the second thing I heard was my own heartbeat, which is a strange thing to hear after twelve years of not listening to it.

I want to tell you I moved at once. That the dying man's token burned in my pocket like a coal and I flung open my shutters and strode out into the lane with my gift blazing like a drawn sword. That's the version that would sell books, if anyone ever wrote a book about this, which no one should, because the truth is less flattering and involves considerably more standing frozen behind a curtain while an old woman screamed.

There were six of them. Crown inquisitors wear grey — not the grey of soldiers or the grey of priests but a particular grey that seems to drink the color out of whatever stands near it, so that Tamsin's cheerful blue door looked like something drowned by the time they'd finished knocking it in. Their captain had the white sash of the Office of Unsworn Gifts, and under his arm he carried the ledger. Everyone on Ashrow Lane knew what the ledger was. It was the book where your name went from being a name to being a number.

"Tamsin Wicker," the captain read aloud, in the tone of a man reading a laundry list. "Hedge-practitioner. Unlicensed remedies. Unsworn gift of mending." He looked up at her doorway, at the herbs still hanging in bundles from her lintel — feverfew, yarrow, that stuff she swore cured hangovers and I never once saw work. "By order of the Regent's Court, you are taken for questioning."

"I mend broken bones," Tamsin said. She wasn't screaming anymore. She'd gone very still and very clear, the way she did when a patient came in bleeding and panicking — she used to say fear was just another injury, and you treated it first. "Children's bones. Farmers' bones. Whose bones have I broken, captain?"

"That isn't the question before the court."

"No," she said. "It's the question before me."

One of the younger inquisitors shifted, and I saw him glance at the captain, and I understood with a cold drop in my stomach that they were enjoying this. Not the cruelty of it — maybe not even that — but the *certainty*. There is nothing so satisfying to a frightened man as a list, and the crown had given them a list, and every name crossed off it made the world feel a little more ordered, a little more survivable. I knew the feeling intimately. I'd been crossing myself off lists for twelve years.

They put the irons on her. Cold-iron, the kind that doesn't hurt the ungifted at all and hurts everyone else like pressing your hand against winter itself. Tamsin didn't cry out. She looked down the lane instead — past them, past her ruined door, past the neighbors pressed to their windows pretending not to watch — and I would swear on anything you like that her eyes stopped on my shutter for half a heartbeat.

*Don't,* I told her silently. *Don't you dare look at me. Don't you dare make me real.*

She looked away. And that was worse, somehow. Because it meant she understood. It meant the woman who sold me real fever-tinctures and bought my fake ones, who had probably known for years exactly what I was and never said a word — she understood that looking at me would cost us both, and she chose, in the last three seconds of her old life, to protect me.

I have been given many gifts in my time. Most of them were curses wearing nice clothes. But nobody had ever done anything like that for me before, and it sat in my chest like a swallowed stone all through what happened next.

* * *

What happened next took four minutes. I know because I counted, the way you count when you're trying not to act, each second a rung on a ladder leading down somewhere you don't want to go.

Minute one: they walked her up the lane toward the cart waiting at the corner. The cart already had two people in it — the cooper's son from Bellows Street, who was sixteen and had once made a barrel sing, whatever that means; and a woman I didn't know, who was weeping without any sound at all, which is its own kind of terrible.

Minute two: Mrs. Aldery from the corner house opened her door. I will think about this for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be. Mrs. Aldery, seventy years old, bad hip, dead husband, no gift that anyone had ever found — opened her door and stood in the inquisitors' path and said, "You'll not take that girl through my street like a criminal. She set my husband's leg when the bone came through the skin. Where's your license for decency?"

And the captain — patient, grey, tired in the way of men who have done this forty times — said, "Madam, go inside."

"She set my husband's *leg*—"

"Madam. Go inside, or be entered in the ledger yourself."

Mrs. Aldery went inside. Of course she did. She was brave, not stupid, and the difference between those things is usually about ten seconds long.

Minute three: they reached the cart. The cooper's son was hauled up. Tamsin stepped onto the wheel-rim, one hand on the rail, and the cold iron on her wrists must have been biting deep because I saw her jaw set the way mine sets when I burn my hand on a charm-pot and refuse to say so.

Minute four: the driver gathered the reins.

And here is the thing about a wall you've kept for twelve years. I said that already, didn't I. Here is the other thing about it: it doesn't come down all at once. It comes down the way a dam goes — a crack, a trickle, a sound too small to hear, and then everything you've held back your whole life arrives at once and asks you, politely, where you'd like it delivered.

The crack, for me, was the moment the cart lurched forward and Tamsin's bundle of feverfew — knocked loose from her lintel, trampled, forgotten — lay in the mud of the lane, and I thought: *she'll need that.* Stupid thought. Senseless thought. She wouldn't need feverfew where they were taking her; she'd need a miracle, and the crown had recently made miracles illegal.

But the thought cracked the wall, and through the crack came everything else: the dying man's blood on my floorboards. The token in my pocket with my own name on it. My mother's face, twelve years younger, saying *swear it, Paloma, swear you'll never speak.* The oath in my throat like a fishhook turned sideways. Twelve years of selling love charms that did nothing to people whose love needed help, and calling it prudence, and calling it survival, and knowing — knowing in the place where I kept the truth about the token, the place I'd only just discovered and already hated — that it had never been prudence at all. It had been obedience. A good dog keeps the rules. A good dog gets to keep living.

The cart reached the corner.

I went through my window.

Not the door — the door has a bell over it, and I have spent twelve years making sure that bell announces nothing but commerce. Through the window, over the herb pots, into the lane, and I was moving before I'd decided to move, which is how my gift always worked and why I'd buried it, because a swordspeaker's body answers the sword faster than the man can answer for it.

"Captain," I said.

My voice came out wrong. Not shaking — steady, level, pitched low, and I realized with distant horror that it was the voice. The speaking voice. The voice that makes steel listen. Six heads turned, and every one of them felt it, even if none of them could name it: that small animal prickle at the back of the neck when something older than crowns addresses you.

"You've got the wrong woman," I said, walking toward them, and my hands were empty and my heart was going like a drum in a procession and some traitor part of me — the part that had starved for twelve years — was *singing.*

"The ledger says otherwise," the captain said.

"The ledger says she mends bones." I stopped six feet away. Close enough to talk. Far enough that what I was about to do would have room. "I'm telling you it's wrong. Take me instead. I'm the one you want."

"And who are you?"

And there it was. The question. The whole hunt in miniature, the whole twelve years folded into five words, because there was only one true answer and the true answer was a death sentence twice over — once for the gift, once for the token, once for the name the token carried, which was my mother's crime wearing my face.

"Paloma Galloway," I said. "Charm-seller."

Which was true. Which was also, as of that morning, the smaller half of the truth, and I watched the captain weigh it and find it light, and I watched his hand drift toward the ledger, and I understood that in about four seconds my name was going in the book regardless of what I said next.

So I did the thing a swordspeaker cannot do quietly.

I spoke.

Not words. Not a command. Just — the voice, unhooded, the full register of it, the thing my mother spent my childhood teaching me to hide and my oath spent twelve years holding down. I spoke the way a struck bell speaks, and the sound went down the lane and into the stones of Ashrow Lane themselves, and every blade in reach answered me.

Six swords left six scabbards.

Not drawn — *left*. Risen. They came out of their sheaths like trout rising to a fly, smooth and silent and wrong, and hung in the air in a ring around the captain, points outward, trembling with eagerness. Steel wants things. Nobody tells you that unless you're born able to hear it. Steel wants to be swung, wants to be true, wants desperately to matter — and for twelve years I had refused to listen to all that wanting, and now I let it pour through me, and gods forgive me, it felt like setting down a weight I'd forgotten I was carrying.

The lane went silent. Even the weeping woman in the cart stopped.

The captain stood very still in the middle of his own disarmed escort, and to his credit — I will give him this much and no more — he didn't reach for a weapon, since he no longer had one. He looked at the floating swords, and then at me, and I watched him do arithmetic. Six blades. One speaker. A street full of witnesses.

"Swordspeaker," he said. Quietly. Almost reverently. "They said your kind was gone."

"They say a lot of things." My voice was still doing the thing, resonant, and I hated how good it felt. "Here's what happens now. You take the irons off her. You take the boy off the cart. You write in your ledger that Ashrow Lane was swept and found clean. And you forget my face, because if you don't, the last thing those swords hear from me won't be polite."

It was a bluff. Half of it. The irons and the boy and the ledger — that part I could enforce, more or less, though enforcing it would cost me things I hadn't finished counting. But the threat at the end was wind. Swordspeakers don't kill the unarmed. It's not a rule; it's a physics. The voice moves steel, and steel moves flesh, and a man with empty hands stands outside that chain entirely. He might even have known it. His eyes flicked to my hands, empty, and stayed there a moment too long.

But he was tired, and his escort was white-faced, and a street full of windows had suddenly grown very full of faces, and there is no version of the crown's arithmetic in which fighting a swordspeaker on a public street ends well for the crown's story. Stories are what the hunt was really made of. So he did the math and hated the answer.

"Release the hedge-witch," he said.

They cut the irons off right there in the lane. Tamsin rubbed her wrists and didn't look at me — smart woman, even now — and the cooper's son scrambled down from the cart, and the captain took out his ledger and wrote, in a hand far too neat for a man whose morning had gone this badly: *Ashrow Lane. Nothing found.*

Then he looked up at me one last time.

"You understand," he said, conversationally, sheathing nothing because there was nothing to sheathe, "that this changes nothing. The ledger is copied. Your description travels tonight. By week's end there will be men here who don't do sums the way I do."

"I'd expect nothing less of the crown."

"No," he said. "You wouldn't. That's rather the point of you." He clicked the ledger shut. "For what it's worth, charm-seller — the woman you saved today was entry four hundred and eleven. There are nine hundred more names behind her. No voice holds that many swords."

He was right. That was the worst part. He was entirely right, and I stood in the middle of Ashrow Lane with my gift blazing openly for the first time in twelve years, six swords slowly sinking back into their scabbards around me like tired birds settling, and I felt the exhilaration start to curdle into the other thing, the colder thing, the thing that had been waiting under the exhilaration all along:

*I can't do this again. Whatever this was, I can't do it twice.*

Tamsin touched my elbow. When had she gotten beside me? "Walk with me," she murmured, "before your knees figure out what you did."

"My knees are fine."

"Your knees are lying. It runs in the family of people who just did something magnificent and idiotic." She steered me toward her ruined doorway, past the trampled feverfew, and paused there, looking back down the lane where the cart rattled away with one passenger fewer and the inquisitors regrouped like grey water closing over a dropped stone.

"Paloma," she said, very low. "That thing you did with the swords. How long can you keep doing it before the oath finds you?"

I hadn't told her about the oath. I hadn't told her about the token, or the dying man, or the word *sold*, or any of it. But she was a hedge-healer, and hedge-healers know how bodies carry secrets — the tightness in the chest, the flinch in the hands — and apparently I'd been carrying mine where she could read it for years.

"I don't know," I said. Which was true. And then, because the wall was down anyway and there seemed little point in rebuilding it overnight: "I think it's already looking for me."

She nodded slowly, the way she nodded over a wound that was worse than the patient wanted it to be. "Then we'd best get you off this street," said Tamsin Wicker, "before it finds you. I know someone who knows the way down."

"*Down?*" I said.

She looked at me, and for the first time since the irons came off, she smiled — a thin, fierce, terrified smile, the smile of a woman choosing a side.

"Where else does a soul go," she said, "when it's been sold?"
