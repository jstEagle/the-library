## 5. An Oath Is a Chain

The token sat on my workbench all night, and I did not sleep, and the candle burned down to a coin of wax, and still I could not stop looking at it.

Bone doesn't hold light. That's what I'd always believed. Bone is dead matter — it takes engraving the way a stone takes rain, patiently and without meaning anything. But this token held something. When I turned it in my fingers, the letters of my own name seemed to move, the way fish move under ice. *Paloma Galloway. Sold at age nine. Price: one life, whole and breathing, against one soul, freely given by the mother.* And beneath that, in smaller script, like a clerk's afterthought: *Held in trust by the Court Beneath the Mountain. Redeemable only by the bearer or his blood.*

My mother's blood ran in me. So did hers, apparently, in the ledger of some buried court I had never known existed until two nights ago.

I set the token down. Picked it up again. Set it down. This is what fear looks like when it has nowhere to go — a man alone in a locked shop at three in the morning, fidgeting with a piece of bone like a rosary he doesn't believe in.

There was a knock at the door.

I went very still. Ashrow Lane was quiet in the way that meant *listening*, not sleeping. The knock came again — three raps, soft, deliberate. Not the fist of the watch. The watch knocked like they wanted the door to know it was guilty.

"Paloma." Tamsin's voice, low through the wood. "Open up. I saw your candle."

I let her in because the alternative was standing there being a man who didn't open doors for Tamsin, which was not a man I intended to become. She slipped inside smelling of rain and comfrey, her healer's satchel bumping her hip, and she took one look at me and one look at the token and said nothing, which from Tamsin was a whole conversation.

"You haven't slept," she said finally.

"I've been reading."

"At that bone thing?"

"It has a lot to say."

She lowered herself onto my stool, uninvited, the way she always did, and held out her hand. I gave her the token and watched her face do what mine must have done — the small collapse, the reassembly. She was a hedge-healer. She knew about debts. Half of Ashrow Lane owed somebody something; that was the local currency, along with gossip and secondhand boots.

"This says you," she said. "This actually says *you*."

"My mother sold me. Or — sold part of me. The part without a body." I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin as tin. "Twelve years ago. When I was sick. The fever that nearly took me — I remember it. I remember her sitting by the bed all night, singing that song she never sang any other time. I thought it was just a song."

Tamsin turned the token over twice more, then set it down between us like it might bite. "And the dying man?"

"Gave it to me. Said the court keeps what's sold. Said—" I stopped. Saying it aloud made it realer than the bone did. "Said I should come collect."

"And are you going to?"

"I don't know what I'm going to do. I keep waiting to feel like the person who decides things, and he hasn't shown up yet."

That got half a smile out of her, gone quickly. Then her eyes went past my shoulder, to the corner where my sword stood leaning against the wall, wrapped in its plain oilcloth the way it had been wrapped for twelve years, the way it had been wrapped when I bought this stall and started selling charms that did nothing.

"That's not wrapped for hiding," she said slowly. "Is it. That's wrapped for *keeping quiet*."

"Tamsin—"

"A swordspeaker. You're a *swordspeaker*." Her voice climbed and she strangled it down to a whisper, glancing at the shutters as if the crown could hear through wood. Maybe it could. I was starting to suspect it could hear through almost anything. "Paloma, they're hanging people for hedge-craft. For *fever charms*. You've been sitting on the one gift that makes witch-hunters weep with envy, in a city running a hunt—"

"I know what I've been sitting on."

"Do you? Because I don't think you do. I think you've been sitting on it so long you've forgotten it has weight."

She wasn't wrong, and I hated her a little for it, the way you hate anyone who describes the room you've been living in accurately.

Here is what I had never told anyone, not even Tamsin, not even in the years when we shared a wall and a winter and most of our secrets: a swordspeaker does not simply *have* a gift. A swordspeaker is bound. My sword and I are joined by an oath — sworn when I was nine years old, feverish, half-delirious, my mother's hand gripping mine around the hilt while a man in grey made me repeat words I barely understood. Words about service. About the crown. About how the blade would speak truth through me and I would speak truth through the blade, and both of us would belong, always, to whoever wore the ring of Ash.

I hadn't understood. I'd been nine and burning with fever. I'd thought I was promising to be good.

* * *

After Tamsin left — she made me eat something first, bread and hard cheese, the medicine of people who love you practically — I unwrapped the sword.

It had been a long time. The oilcloth came away in a whisper and there it was, the same as ever: a plain longsword, honest steel, a grip worn to the shape of my hand because it had been shaping itself to my hand since I was a child. No jewels. No maker's mark. Just a faint line of script down the fuller, so old and fine you'd take it for a scratch unless you knew to look.

I knew to look. I'd spent twelve years not looking.

When I touched the grip, the oath woke.

It always wakes. That's the thing nobody tells you about oaths — they don't sleep, they wait. It rose up through my palm like cold water finding a crack, and I felt it settle into place along my arm, my shoulder, my throat, exactly where it always settled, familiar as a scar. And then, as it always did, it *spoke*. Not in words. In certainty. A vast, patient certainty that lived somewhere behind my sternum and said, without language: *you are bound, and the binding holds, and the binding is good.*

Twelve years I had mistaken that voice for my own conscience.

I sat down on the floor across from it, because standing near it suddenly felt like standing near a well you'd just realized was bottomless.

"Show me," I said quietly. "Show me what you are."

The sword cannot lie. That is the heart of the gift and the trap of it — a swordspeaker's blade speaks true, always, whether the speaker wills it or no. And I had learned, in the few desperate times I'd drawn it, that if I asked the blade a question and listened past my own pulse, it answered. Not in words. In knowing. Things simply became *true in my mouth*: I would draw steel and say a sentence and the sentence would be undeniable, weighted, a truth with edges.

So I asked it what it was bound to.

And the knowing came, and it was worse than I feared, because I had been fearing the wrong shape entirely.

I had imagined the oath as a rope — a line from me to the crown, something that could be cut if I found the knife. What the blade showed me instead was a chain of a different order. Every link forged from a word spoken by someone else. My mother's word, first: *let him live.* The grey man's word, receiving it. The crown's word, sealing it. And then my own small feverish word, *yes*, threaded through all of them like wire through links. The chain did not run from me to the palace. It ran *through* me, back into the ground, back down — down toward the mountain, down toward heat, down toward—

Oh.

Down toward a garden.

I didn't have a name for it yet. I wouldn't for days. But I felt it the way you feel a draft from a door you can't see: the oath drew power from somewhere beneath everything, and fed it upward, and the crown drank, and the court below kept the books. My soul wasn't just *held* by the court beneath the mountain. It was *earning interest*.

And here was the part that closed my hands into fists until the nails bit: the oath was also a leash. A live one. Somewhere in the palace archives there was a record of a swordspeaker named Paloma Galloway, bound and accounted for, and the men running the hunt for the gifted had access to those records. They didn't need informants. They didn't need informants. All they needed was to call the roll.

An oath, once invoked, must be answered. That's the law beneath all laws, older than crowns. If the crown summoned its bound blades, the blades would come — or the binding would begin to *pull*, and a pulled oath feels like drowning from the inside. I had heard of a swordspeaker in my mother's time, before my time really, who tried to refuse a summons. They found him three days later at the bottom of a ravine with his own sword through him. The official story was bandits. The story the gifted told each other in whispers was that the oath had simply *collected* him.

Could I break it? I asked the blade that too.

The answer arrived with the gentleness of a landslide. The chain could be broken in exactly two ways. The first: death of the bearer. Clean, final, the links dissolving. The second: renunciation — but renunciation required the consent of every party who had ever spoken a word into the chain, and two of those parties were dead, and one of them was a crown, and crowns do not consent.

Unless.

The knowing flickered there, at the edge, like a lamp behind fogged glass. *Unless the terms themselves were destroyed.* Unless whatever the oath drew from — whatever garden, whatever furnace, whatever ledger-room beneath the mountain — unless *that* burned. An oath is a chain, yes. But a chain is only as strong as the anchor it's bolted to.

I sat on the floor of my shop until the window went grey, holding a sword I had loved the way you love a limb, and understanding for the first time that the limb had been someone else's all along.

* * *

By the time the sun cleared the rooftops I had done three things. I had rewrapped the sword, though my hands shook doing it. I had opened the shutters, because hiding in the dark felt too much like agreeing with them. And I had taken stock of myself the way you take stock before a flood: what can be carried, what must be left, what will be lost regardless.

The stall could be rebuilt. The charms were nothing — I could carve new ones in a week. The shop had belonged to the woman I bought it from, and before that to nobody important, and walls are just walls.

But the list of things I could not carry or rebuild was longer. Tamsin next door, with her satchel and her terrible honesty. The widow Fenner who paid in pies. The children who bought luck-knots with buttons. Twelve years of a small, ordinary, deliberately dull life, built plank by plank over a cellar full of fire.

And underneath all of it, the bone token in my pocket, warm now as if it had been next to my skin long enough to learn my temperature. *Redeemable only by the bearer or his blood.* I was the blood. I was also, apparently, the goods.

A crier passed down Ashrow Lane, hoarse already though the day was young: *"By decree of the Crown! All practitioners of hidden arts shall present themselves for examination! Shelterers of the gifted shall be judged as the gifted! The Crown thanks its loyal subjects!"*

*Shelterers judged as the gifted.* Which meant Tamsin. Which meant the widow Fenner, if they decided pies were evidence. Which meant everyone whose door had ever opened for anyone.

I looked at the wrapped sword in the corner. It looked back, in the way of oaths — patiently, without malice, absolutely certain of itself.

*You are bound,* it said, in the voice behind my sternum. *And the binding holds.*

"Not for much longer," I said out loud, to an empty shop, like a madman. But saying it made something in my chest stand up straighter, so I said it again. "Not for much longer. Whatever it costs."

Because here was the arithmetic I'd finished sometime in the grey hours: the hunt would find me anyway. The leash would pull eventually — a summons, a roll-call, a name read from a ledger. I could wait for the chain to yank, or I could walk toward the anchor and put my hands on it. The court beneath the mountain had my soul in a drawer somewhere, and my oath bolted to its floor, and apparently my mother's signature in its books.

Everything I was had been traded, signed, and stored by other people.

Fine. Then I would go and be a problem for the bookkeepers.

I was reaching for my coat when the shouting started — out on Ashrow Lane, several voices, and under them the flat metallic clank of watch-helmets, and above it all a woman's voice I knew as well as my own, raised not in fear but in fury, telling someone exactly what she thought of them.

Tamsin.

I was out the door before my coat was on.
