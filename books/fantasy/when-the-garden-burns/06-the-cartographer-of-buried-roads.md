## 6. The Cartographer of Buried Roads

I want it on the record, whoever keeps records down there in whatever ledger they use for people like me: I did not want to be rescued.

I was doing fine. I had told the inquisitor — a damp little man with a voice like a wet boot — precisely and at length what he could do with his writ of seizure, and I had my elbow planted in the ribs of the second guard, and I was about to demonstrate a technique my grandmother called *the widow's opinion* when the whole street went strange.

You've never heard a swordspeaker work. Most people haven't; that's rather the point of them. It isn't shouting. It's quieter than quiet — like the moment before a storm decides to be rude about it. The air on Ashrow Lane went taut as a drumhead, and every blade on the street — the guards' swords, the fishmonger's cleaver, the letter opener some fool clerk was carrying in his satchel three doors down — turned toward Paloma Galloway like flowers finding the sun.

Then they stopped being anyone's swords at all.

The guards' scabbards emptied themselves with a sound like startled birds. Steel clattered across cobbles, rang off doorposts, lay down in the gutter as if exhausted by its own career. One sword — the inquisitor's own, a pretty thing with a gilded hilt he clearly hadn't earned — rose into the air, hung there considering its options, and then drove itself point-first into the muck between his boots, standing at attention like a soldier who'd just heard something worth deserting for.

"Go home," Paloma said, and his voice carried the way a bell carries, all through you.

They went home. Mostly. The inquisitor stood there a moment longer, doing sums behind his eyes, and then he too decided that discretion was the better part of not being made to eat his own sword, and withdrew with what dignity remains to a man whose weapon has publicly disowned him.

Which left me, rubbing my bruised ribs, looking at the last person in Ashrow Lane who could afford to do what he'd just done.

"Well," I said. "That was stupid."

"I know," said Paloma.

"No, I mean genuinely, historically stupid. You've been hiding for twelve years and you blew your cover in broad daylight over *me*. I'm not worth it. Ask anyone."

"You were worth it," he said, in that flat stubborn way of his, and handed me back my herb basket, which one of the guards had kicked over. My feverfew was everywhere. He knelt in the street picking it up, this man who had just unmade six soldiers with a word, gathering scattered flowers like an apologetic schoolboy.

And that — if you're wondering, and someone always wonders — is the moment I decided to follow him under a mountain. Not heroism. Not gratitude, exactly. It was watching him pick up the feverfew. People show you who they are in the small motions. Everything else is performance.

* * *

We couldn't go back to his shop, obviously. By nightfall there would be a writ nailed to his door and a description circulating that read, in essence, *tall, tired-looking, dangerous, sells fake love charms.* We couldn't go to mine either; the inquisitors had already turned my rooms inside out and left them looking like a hedge-healer's idea of a bad harvest. So we went where anyone goes in this city when they need something impossible and can't afford the church's price.

We went to see Odd Wren.

Her shop sat at the crooked end of Candlemaker's Row, wedged between a chandler who'd gone blind and a house that had been condemned so long it had achieved a kind of architectural tenure. The sign above the door read *WREN — SURVEYOR & MAPMAKER*, and beneath it, in smaller letters someone had scratched long ago: *she will find you a way or she will find you out.*

"She's disgraced," Paloma said as we climbed the stairs. "I remember. She used to do charts for the Crown survey. Then—"

"Then she mapped something she wasn't supposed to," I said. "Ask her yourself. And let me talk first. She likes me."

"Everyone likes you."

"It's a burden." I knocked twice, paused, then knocked three more times, because Wren answered patterns, not knocks, and the pattern meant *hedge-healer, no watch, bring tea next time.*

The door opened on a chain, then wider, onto a small round woman of about sixty with ink-stained fingers, spectacles pushed up into grey hair, and the expression of someone who has been interrupted during the most interesting part of something.

"Tamsin Vetch," she said. "You look like a woman who's been arrested."

"I got better. This is Paloma. Can we come in before you say anything else accurate?"

Wren looked past me at Paloma for a long moment. Cartographers look at people the way they look at terrain — measuring, noting the drop-offs. Whatever she charted in him, she must have found passable, because she unhooked the chain and stood aside.

Inside, her shop was less a room than a landslide of paper. Maps hung from every surface — the city in cross-section, the river country, trade roads gone fallow, coastlines that predated the current crown by centuries. And along one whole wall, behind a curtain she pretended we couldn't see through, maps of a different character entirely: hand-drawn, obsessive, corrected and re-corrected until the paper had gone soft as cloth. Maps of passages going *down.*

Paloma saw them. Of course he saw them. He was holding a bone token that named a court beneath the mountain; he'd have seen a map of the underneath painted on the inside of his eyelids.

"Wren," I said, "we need a road."

"Roads are easy. Try the chandler." She began shooing us back toward the door with the brisk economy of a woman who had learned that visitors cost ink.

"He needs a road *down,*" I said. "Into the court."

The shooing stopped.

For a moment the only sound was the rain starting against the window, and Wren's breathing, which had gone very careful, the way breathing does when someone is keeping it on a leash.

"Sit down," she said finally, in a completely different voice. "Both of you. Tamsin, put the kettle on, you know where everything is, and don't touch the red tin, that's pigment."

* * *

While I fussed with the kettle — mostly to give them privacy, partly because I genuinely wanted tea — Wren pulled one of the soft-worn maps from behind the curtain and spread it on her table, weighting the corners with an inkpot, a skull of indeterminate species, a cold pipe, and a stone that hummed faintly when Paloma leaned near it, which interested both of them far more than it interested me.

"This is the old stair," Wren said, tapping a line that descended through the paper like a root through soil. "There are eleven ways into the court beneath the mountain that I have verified, and four more I suspect. Nine of the eleven are sealed from the other side. One is watched by the Crown — has been watched for years, though the Crown pretends it doesn't know why it's watching. That leaves one." Her finger came to rest on a mark near the river cliffs, north of the city. "A stair that isn't there."

"That's helpful," Paloma said dryly.

"It's precise. The stair is *unmapped* — that's different from unmappable. It exists only when it wants to. The old surveyors called it the courtesy door. The court opens it for those who carry a claim." Her eyes dropped to his hand, where the bone token sat in his palm like a knuckle of moonlight. "And you're carrying one. May I?"

He hesitated — I watched him hesitate, watched the whole war of it cross his face in the space of a breath — and then set the token in her ink-stained fingers.

Wren held it up to her lamp. Whatever she read there, she read quickly; she'd read such things before, or things like them. I saw her jaw tighten.

"Twelve years sold," she said quietly. "Named bearer. Court-sealed. This is a live debt, swordspeaker. Do you understand what that means? It means somewhere below, there is a shelf with your name on it, and your name is still warm."

I heard him swallow. "Can you take us to the courtesy door?"

"*Us?*" Wren set the token down and looked at me over her spectacles. "Since when does the healer descend?"

"Since the healer got dragged into it by association," I said. "Also since the healer is the only one of you who knows how to keep a man alive underground, and you're both going to need keeping. Look at him. He hasn't slept in two days, he's been living on burnt coffee and guilt, and he's about to walk into a place that eats souls. He'll need someone who can brew willowbark in the dark."

"And you?" Wren turned to Paloma. "Why bring her? A man on a private errand travels lighter alone."

It was a test, and a fair one. I braced for him to say something noble and useless.

"Because she didn't leave when the swords came out," he said. "People who stay when the swords come out are the only ones I trust."

Well. I busied myself with the kettle and pretended my eyes weren't doing anything embarrassing.

Wren studied him a moment longer, then nodded slowly, as if confirming a measurement. "All right. Here is my price, and hear it fully before you agree, because I don't bargain twice."

She rolled up the map of the eleven ways and tapped the table.

"I was born down there."

The kettle chose that moment to begin its low whistle, and nobody moved to stop it.

"The court doesn't only keep souls," Wren said. "It keeps children. Or it did, in my day — children born to the sold, raised in the galleries, and when they came of age, sold *upward* to families on the surface who wanted heirs without the mess of birth. I was sold at seven years old to a surveyor's household that needed a clever girl and got one. I learned maps because maps were the only thing I owned that they couldn't inventory. Every chart I've ever drawn — the Crown surveys, the disgrace, all of it — was practice. I have spent fifty years learning the way home." She laid her hand flat on the wall of hidden maps. "These aren't cartography. They're letters I never sent."

"Gods," I said softly. "Wren."

"Don't 'gods' me yet. Here's the price." Her voice stayed level, but her hands, resting on the table, had gone white-knuckled. "There were nine of us sold up that year. I've found graves for two and lives for three. Four are unaccounted for. If there are children below now — and there will be children below, there are *always* children below — then I go down to map the way out for every one of them, and none of you leaves without them, and if the court burns —" her eyes flicked to Paloma, sharp as a stylus — "and I can see by your face you intend it to burn, swordspeaker, then we burn it *after* the children are clear, or not at all. That is my price. Not coin. Not favor. A promise."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere below us, the city went on believing itself safe.

Paloma picked up the token and closed his fist around it, and I watched him weigh his soul against a promise, and I knew — I knew before he opened his mouth — which way it would tip, because that was simply the shape of the man.

"They come out first," he said. "All of them. On my oath."

"Careful," Wren said, almost gently. "Oaths are currency down there. Spend yours wisely."

"My oaths have been spent for me my whole life," Paloma said. "I'd like to try spending one myself."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something I had never once seen her do in fifteen years of acquaintance: she laughed — short, rusty, surprised out of her like a bird flushed from cover.

"Oh, you'll do," she said. "You'll absolutely do. Right. Tamsin, turn off that kettle before it wakes the row. Swordspeaker, sit. We leave tomorrow at dark tide, and between now and then you're going to learn enough of the deep roads to not die in the first gallery." She was already pulling fresh paper toward her, already sketching, ink flying. "And Tamsin —"

"Yes?"

"The red tin. Bring it. What's in it may save all our lives, and if it doesn't, we'll at least die beautifully."

I got the tin down. It was, admittedly, mostly pigment. But a good healer lies about her supplies the way a good mapmaker lies about her distances: sparingly, and only to get everyone home.

Outside, the rain kept falling on a city that had outlawed magic and forgotten why. Inside, three liars bent over a table full of true maps, planning a burglary of the underworld.

It was, I thought, filling the kettle again after all, the beginning of something. I didn't yet know what it would cost. I only knew that for the first time since they took my sister's name off the census rolls, I was exactly where I intended to be.

Above the crooked shop, the storm gathered. Below it, something ancient felt three small debts coming down the stair that wasn't there, and — I am certain of this, though I couldn't tell you how — began, in the dark, to smile.
