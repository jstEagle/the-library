## 7. Down the Stair That Wasn't There

The stair was behind a wall in the cellar of a house that had burned down forty years ago, which is the sort of sentence I'd have laughed at a month ago and now simply nodded at, because my life had become a collection of sentences like that.

"Stand here," Wren said, pressing my palm flat against the soot-black brick. "And don't think about what you want."

"That's the worst instruction anyone's ever given me."

"It's the only one that matters. The mountain smells wanting. It opens for want. And then—" she made a small gesture, fingers closing like a trap, "—it keeps you."

Tamsin shifted her pack on her shoulders and said nothing. She'd been quiet since Odd Wren's map room, quiet in the way of someone counting coins she wasn't sure she had enough of. I'd asked her twice if she was certain. Twice she'd said yes in a voice that meant *stop asking*.

I pressed my hand to the brick and tried to think about nothing, which is like trying to hold water in a fist. I thought about the debt-token in my breast pocket, bone worn smooth as river stone, with my own name cut into it in letters I hadn't been able to stop reading. *Paloma Galloway. Sold, not lost.* Twelve years old when my mother pressed her lips to my forehead and swore an oath over my fevered body, and I had spent twelve years believing that oath had saved me.

Maybe it had. That was the worst part. Maybe it had saved me and sold me in the same breath.

The brick warmed under my palm. Then it moved — not swinging open, nothing so honest as that. It simply stopped being a wall, the way a word stops meaning anything if you say it long enough, and there was a stair going down into dark, narrow, older than any masonry I knew, its steps hollowed in the middle as if by centuries of feet.

Nobody had feet anymore. That should have told us something.

"Ladies first," I said, because gallows humor is free and everything else costs.

Wren went first anyway. She carried her maps rolled tight against her spine like a sword she didn't know how to draw, and she descended without touching the walls, without looking back, with the rigid economy of a woman walking through a room where she'd once been hurt. Tamsin followed. I went last, and pulled the darkness shut behind me, or tried to — the wall reassembled itself above our heads with a sound like a held breath released, and then there was no Ashrow Lane, no witch-hunt, no crown. Just stone, and three people, and whatever waited below.

* * *

We walked for hours. Or the mountain let us believe we walked for hours; time down here had the texture of a rumor. The stair spiraled, widened, narrowed, passed landings where doors stood sealed with wax stamped by kings two hundred years dead. Wren counted turns under her breath and marked them on her maps with a stub of charcoal, and every mark she made seemed to cost her something.

"You've been down here before," I said, somewhere around the point where my legs stopped complaining and started simply obeying.

"I was born down here." She didn't break stride. "Chapter eleven of my life. We'll get there or we won't."

Fair enough. Everyone in this expedition had a locked drawer. Mine contained a bone token and a mother's kiss. Tamsin's, I suspected, contained a sister. The mountain, I was beginning to understand, specialized in locked drawers. It collected them.

It was Tamsin who noticed it first. "Do you hear singing?"

I did. Faint, from far below — not words, just a melody, patient as dripping water. And threaded through it, almost too soft to catch, a smell: bread baking. Woodsmoke. Rain on hot stone in summer. The smell of the apartment above my mother's old shop, the year before she died.

"Stop," Wren snapped, and grabbed Tamsin's arm. "Don't follow the smell. Don't follow the song. It's testing us."

"Testing us how?"

"By showing us the door out." Wren's face was gray in the dim light of the moss-lamps that grew along the walls, pale green things that gave light the way memory gives comfort — real, but not quite warm enough. "Everyone who comes down the stair meets it. The temptation. It learns what you love fastest, and it wears that love like a coat. My grandmother called them the *kind lies*. She said the mountain isn't cruel. Cruelty would be simpler. It's kind, the way a spider is generous."

"And if we see one?" Tamsin asked. "One of these kind lies?"

"Then you remember it isn't real, and you keep walking, and you don't say goodbye." Wren looked at each of us in turn, and her eyes were the flattest I'd ever seen them. "Because goodbye is how it gets its hooks in. You speak to it, you answer it, you let it finish a sentence — and part of you stays behind to finish the conversation forever. Understood?"

Understood. We said it. And I want it noted, for the record, that I understood perfectly, completely, in every particular.

Understanding, as it turned out, was not the problem.

* * *

The landing came without warning. One moment stair, the next a wide gallery floored in smooth black stone, and standing at the far end of it, framed in moss-light, was my mother.

She was alive. Not the gray ghost of grief, not the fever-dream version I'd conjured a thousand times in the years since the coughing took her — alive, solid, wearing the blue apron with the scorched hem she never replaced because replacing it would have meant admitting she'd ruined it. Her hair was coming loose from its braid the way it always did by evening. She was holding a wooden spoon.

"Paloma," she said. "You're standing in a draft. Close the door."

Twelve years. Twelve years, and the first thing she says is the thing she always said, and God help me, my mouth opened.

"Don't," Wren breathed, somewhere behind me. "Paloma. *Don't.*"

But she crossed the gallery toward me, spoon still in hand, and she smelled like thyme and woodsmoke and the specific soap she bought from the chandler on Copper Street, and every rational thought I owned fell off a cliff.

"You're dead," I managed. "You died. I buried— I was sixteen, I—"

"Hush." She reached up — she had to reach up, I'd outgrown her at fourteen and she'd never forgiven the indignity — and touched my cheek. Her hand was warm. That was the unforgivable part. Not the sight of her, not the voice. The warmth. "You've been carrying something heavy, my love. Let me carry it a while."

My hand was already moving toward my breast pocket. Toward the token. Toward the truth I'd been chewing on for days like a man chewing a nail: *she sold me, she sold me, she sold me.*

"Did you do it?" The words came out cracked. "Mother. The token. Did you sell my soul?"

And here is what I learned about the mountain's kindness: it doesn't flinch. It gave her a sad smile, exactly the smile she wore when she was about to tell me something I wouldn't like, and it said, "Come downstairs and I'll explain everything. The kettle's on. You look half-starved. Come home, Paloma."

*Come home.* Two words, and they weighed more than the whole mountain above us.

Behind me, Tamsin made a sound — small, strangled. I heard her, distantly, the way you hear rain when you're dreaming. Because across the gallery, in the corner of my eye, Tamsin was seeing something too. A girl, maybe, dark-haired, laughing at something. And Wren stood rigid as a fence post, staring at nothing, jaw clenched hard enough to crack teeth, refusing even to look at whatever the mountain had dressed up for her.

"Paloma," my mother said again, gently, "the kettle's on."

"She's dead," I said. To myself. To the mountain. To the thing wearing my mother's warmth like a stolen coat. "You're dead. I sat with you. I held your hand. This isn't—"

"Isn't it?" she said. "You've wanted me back every day for twelve years. Is wanting such a crime? Stay. Ask me your questions. I'll answer all of them. Isn't that what you came down here for? Answers?" Her head tilted. "Or did you come for the token? For the court? Then go, and leave your mother standing in a draft."

It was masterful. I'll give the mountain that. It didn't threaten. It offered, and it made refusal feel like the cruelty.

Wren's hand closed on my shoulder. Hard. "It can't answer anything," she said quietly. "It can only repeat what you already fear and hope. Everything it tells you, you're telling yourself. Ask yourself, swordspeaker: does your mother's ghost know anything you don't?"

And that — that was the knife that cut the rope. Because she was right. The thing in front of me was offering explanations, but explanations weren't what I wanted from my mother. What I wanted — what I'd wanted since the day I read my name on that bone — was the truth, and the truth was the one thing the mountain couldn't give, because the mountain only had what I brought down with me.

"You're not her," I said. My voice shook. I let it shake. "And worse — you're not even lying well. She'd have made me tea first. She never once sent me anywhere without feeding me."

Something flickered across the false face. Not hurt. Recalculation.

"I'm sorry," I told it, and I meant it, which frightened me more than anything else that day. "I'm sorry I ever wanted this badly enough that you could use it. But I have to go find out what actually happened. From the thing that actually knows."

I stepped past her. It was the hardest step I have ever taken, harder than any I took in the burning garden later, though I didn't know that yet. My shoulder passed through hers — or hers through mine — and there was no cold, no chill, no dissolution. She simply wasn't there anymore, the way the wall hadn't been there, the way the door in the burnt house hadn't been there. The mountain's magic was all subtraction. It never showed you a lie. It just removed the truth and left the shape of it standing.

Behind me, Tamsin was crying silently, fists clenched, staring at the empty air where her dark-haired girl had been. She didn't need Wren's hand on her shoulder. She turned away on her own, wiped her face with her sleeve, and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"The sister. It was my sister." A breath. "She's been dead nine years and she looked *happy*, Paloma. It made her happy just to gut me."

"Now you know what it has of yours," Wren said. "Good. Knowing is armor. Come on. The stair continues."

* * *

It continued. Down, and down, and the singing changed key as we descended, the melody losing its sweetness by degrees until it sounded less like a lullaby and more like a ledger being read aloud. The moss-lamps thickened. The air warmed. Somewhere below us, vast and patient, something kept accounts.

Wren walked faster now, checking her marks against her maps with increasing frequency, and I noticed her hands weren't steady. I noticed, too, that she never once said what she'd seen on that landing. Whatever the mountain had shown the cartographer of buried roads, she'd folded it flat and filed it away in the locked drawer with the rest of her, and I decided — generously, I thought — not to ask.

That generosity lasted maybe an hour.

"What did it show you?" I finally said. "On the landing. You looked at nothing. You refused to even turn your head."

"I looked at nothing," Wren said, "because I already live with what it would show me. I see it every time I close my eyes. The mountain doesn't get to charge me twice." She rolled her maps tighter. "Ask me again when we're out. If we're out."

Tamsin caught my eye and shook her head slightly. *Let it go.* So I let it go, the way you let go of a rope you suspect is attached to something heavy on the other end.

We ate cold provisions at a landing where the walls were carved with names — thousands of them, tens of thousands, cut in scripts spanning centuries, climbing toward the ceiling and vanishing into shadow. I found myself reading them without meaning to. Names, and beside each name, in smaller letters, a price.

Not all prices were coin.

"There you are," Wren said softly, running her finger along a column. "Every soul the court ever bought. Every soul it ever sold onward. It keeps records the way other courts keep portraits." Her finger stopped. She went very still.

"Wren?"

"Nothing." But her finger had stopped beside a name, and I saw enough of it before she pulled away — a child's name, a girl's name, cut shallow, the way names are cut when the cutter hopes they'll be missed. "Nothing. Keep moving."

The singing below swelled. And underneath it now, faint but unmistakable, came another sound: footsteps. Many. Rhythmic. Coming up the stair toward us.

Wren blew out her breath slowly. "Court hunters," she said. "They've smelled us. Three travelers, one of them carrying a debt-token that sings louder than a cathedral bell." She looked at me, and for the first time since I'd met her, Odd Wren smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "Congratulations, Galloway. Your soul wants to come home to its owner. Unfortunately, so does everyone else."

The footsteps grew louder. Around the spiral below us, torchlight bloomed — and with it voices, and with the voices, something worse: the clear, carrying tone of a herald reading a proclamation, and the proclamation had my name in it.

Not *Paloma Galloway, charm-seller of Ashrow Lane.*

*Paloma Galloway, asset of the buried court. Return him intact.*

"Well," said Tamsin, loosening the knife at her belt. "At least we know we're wanted."

The stair ahead of us ended at a door — a real one this time, iron-banded, ancient, and glowing faintly at its edges with the same pale fire I'd seen in the garden illustrations on Wren's maps. Beyond it, I could feel it the way you feel a storm through a window: the court beneath the mountain, waiting, keeping, burning.

Behind us, the hunters climbed.

Wren put her shoulder to the door. "Choose fast," she said.
