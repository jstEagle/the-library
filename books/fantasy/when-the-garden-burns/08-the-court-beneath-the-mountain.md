## 8. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The stair ended the way all good lies end: suddenly, and with the floor missing.

One moment I was walking down stone that had been stone for a thousand years, and the next I stepped into air that smelled like a garden in high summer — which should have been my first warning, because nothing down here had any business smelling alive. My boot found ground that wasn't there a heartbeat before. Tamsin stumbled against my back. Wren didn't stumble at all; she walked through the threshold like a woman coming home to a house she'd been evicted from.

"Keep your eyes forward," Wren said. "And your mouth shut. And whatever you do, don't compliment anything."

"Why not?"

"Because it'll hear you."

I had questions. I'd had questions for three days of descending, and Wren had answered roughly four of them, all with maps drawn on scraps of leather that she consulted by touch rather than sight, because — as she explained with the patience one reserves for children and idiots — *the dark down here isn't empty, it's listening.* So I kept my mouth shut and looked forward, and that is how I first saw the court beneath the mountain.

I want to tell you it was ugly. It would be tidier if it were ugly. Monsters are easy when they're ugly; you can hate them without homework.

It was beautiful.

We came out onto a gallery of black stone that curved like the inside of a cupped hand, and below us spread a cavern so vast the ceiling was a rumor. Rivers of pale light ran along channels cut into the floor — not water, though it moved like water, slow and silver and patient. Terraces descended in rings, each one hung with gardens, and every garden burned. Not consumed — *burning*, the way a lamp burns, steady and golden, flames rising off leaves and petals and vines without blackening them. The whole cavern was lit by ten thousand small fires that never went out, and the light they gave wasn't firelight at all. It was warmer than that. It was the color of a promise being kept.

"Oh," Tamsin said softly beside me. "Oh, that's not fair."

That's exactly what I thought, though I'd have phrased it less honestly. Beauty is a weapon the way a debt-token is a gift: it only works if you reach for it.

People moved along the terraces. That was the second wrongness, the one that took longer to land. They weren't shambling horrors or chained wretches. They wore fine clothes — or clothes that had *been* fine, decades ago, gone soft and strange with time — and they moved like people who have nowhere to be but also nowhere else to go. A woman stood at a balustrade looking out over the burning gardens with her hands folded, and she had the posture of someone waiting for a coach that stopped running forty years ago. A man crossed a bridge of white stone carrying a tray of cups no one would ever drink from. An old soldier sat sharpening a sword that had no edge left to find, stroke after patient stroke, the sound carrying up to us like a heartbeat.

"The sold," Wren said quietly. "And some of the sold-from. The ones who couldn't stop looking for what they gave away." She tapped her temple. "The court doesn't keep them in cages. Cages are for things that might leave. These don't leave. Why would they? Everything they lost is right there." She pointed at the gardens. "Burning pretty."

"And us?" Tamsin asked. "What are we?"

"Guests." Wren's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "For now. Down here, guest is just the word for *not yet sold*."

* * *

We went down. Of course we went down. You don't climb three days through the dark bones of a mountain to admire the view from the cheap seats.

The terraces were warm underfoot, which unsettled me more than cold would have. Cold says *danger*. Warmth says *stay*. We passed close to one of the burning gardens, close enough to feel it on my face, and I made the mistake of looking directly at the flames.

They weren't ordinary fire. Each flame had a shape inside it — a suggestion of a shape, the way smoke suggests shapes if you stare too long. And each flame had a voice, if voice is the right word for something you hear behind your teeth instead of in your ears. Whispering. Hundreds of whispers braided together, and underneath the braid, words. Oaths. I caught fragments as we passed: *—so help me—* and *—my crown, my life, my—* and *—until my dying day, until my dying day, until my dying day—*

I knew that cadence. Every child in Ashrow swore it at fourteen, hand on the crown's iron seal, when the clerk came around with his ledger and his kind smile. *Until my dying day.* I'd said it myself, twelve years ago, with my mother's hand on my shoulder and her nails digging in just hard enough to mean something.

My oath was in there. Somewhere in those ten thousand fires, mine was burning too, keeping the lights on.

"Paloma." Tamsin's hand on my arm. "You've gone gray."

"I'm always gray. It's called a complexion."

"You've gone grayer."

Wren glanced back at me, and whatever she saw made her slow her pace. "Don't listen to them," she said. "The garden talks. It wants you to lean in. Everyone leans in, eventually. That's how the court feeds."

"Feeds on what? Fire doesn't eat."

"Fire eats what you feed it." She nodded at the flames. "Oaths are fuel. Promises burn hotter than wood. Ask any marriage."

* * *

The heart of the court announced itself the way wealth does: by not announcing itself at all. No gates, no guards, no trumpets. Just a gradual change in the quality of the silence, and then a hall so large our footsteps stopped echoing, swallowed whole, and pillars rose around us carved from the living rock into shapes that took me too long to identify.

Hands. They were hands. Thousands of them, palm-up, carved reaching out of the stone, and set into each open palm was a small flame.

Between the pillars stood shelves.

That's the only word I have. Shelves — but built to hold things no carpenter ever planned for. Some held jars of smoked glass with light moving inside them, slow as honey. Some held rings, crowns, wedding bands, a child's milk tooth on a cushion of velvet gone gray. Some held names written on strips of skin-pale vellum, and the vellum breathed. In and out. In and out.

Kept things. The court's inventory.

Tamsin had gone very quiet, which for Tamsin is louder than screaming. Wren walked the aisles like a woman reading a familiar street, and once — only once — she stopped, reached toward a jar, and pulled her hand back before touching it. She didn't explain. I didn't ask. There's a debt between the three of us now, and part of the interest is knowing which silences to honor.

I was looking at a shelf of small brass bells, each one silent, each one trembling faintly as if rung forever just below the range of hearing, when I felt it.

You have to understand what my gift feels like from the inside. Most days it feels like nothing, which is the point of hiding it — a swordspeaker's oath-sense sits quiet unless an oath is spoken nearby, and then it rings like a struck bell behind my sternum. Twelve years of practice had taught me to keep it muffled, dimmed, wrapped in wool. But down here, in a cavern lit by burning promises, the wool caught fire.

Every shelf rang. Every jar, every bell, every breathing name — they all rang at once, a chord so vast I gripped a pillar to stay upright, and the pillar was warm, and I swear on nothing at all that it pulsed.

And beneath the chord, one note. One clear, single note, close, insistent, singing a frequency I knew the way you know your own name.

I followed it. I don't remember deciding to. Tamsin called after me and I heard her the way you hear rain on a roof — real, distant, irrelevant. Three aisles down, past a case of folded shadows, past a bowl of years (actual years, I could see them, little glinting grains like salt), to a shelf at the height of my heart.

On it sat a flame in a lantern of black glass.

Small. Modest. The kind of thing you'd pass in a market stall — and I would know, I've sold enough of them. The flame inside didn't flicker. It stood perfectly still, and it was the exact gold of the garden-light, and when I got within arm's reach, it turned.

Not the lantern. The flame. It leaned toward me the way a flower leans toward sun, and the whispering chord in my chest resolved into one word, spoken in a voice I hadn't heard since I was eleven years old and feverish, a voice that used to say *there's my brave boy* over burnt porridge and unpaid rent:

*Paloma.*

The lantern was warm. I picked it up and it fit my two hands the way a loaf fits, the way a sleeping cat fits, the way things fit when they were made for you, and behind the black glass the flame pressed itself against the pane like a face at a window.

"That's not possible," Tamsin said behind me. She'd followed. Her voice had gone thin. "Paloma, that's not — souls aren't *kept in jars*, that's a story mothers tell—"

"It's not a jar." My own voice sounded far away. "It's a lantern. Jars are for preserving." I turned it over. On the base, etched so fine you'd miss it if you weren't looking with a swordspeaker's eyes — with *my* eyes, the only ones down here tuned to read oaths — was a line of script in the old court-hand:

*Galloway, Paloma. Sold by Maren Galloway, mother, in the year of the Fever Winter. Price: one life, whole and unspent. Paid in full.*

Twelve years ago. The year I nearly died of the lung-rot. The year my mother started locking her door at night and counting coins that weren't there. The year she made me swear, hand on her breastbone, *never ask where the medicine money came from, never ask, swear it, Paloma, swear it on your sword-arm* — and I swore it, because I was eleven and dying and she was crying, and what kind of monster makes a dying boy swear anything?

The kind who's already paid the price. The kind who knows exactly what the medicine cost, and can't bear to be asked.

The flame pressed against the glass again. *Paloma,* it whispered, and this time there was impatience in it, almost humor, the tone my mother used when I was being deliberately dense. *Well? Don't just stand there holding yourself. Say something.*

I set the lantern down very carefully, the way you put down something that might go off, and discovered my hands were shaking.

"Paloma?" Tamsin again, closer now, her healer's instincts reading my pulse from six feet away the way she reads everything. "What is it? What did you find?"

I opened my mouth to lie. I'm good at lying; I've done it professionally for a decade, charming customers about love charms that do nothing. But the garden-light was in my eyes and my soul was sitting on a shelf three inches from my elbow, humming my own name, and the lie died somewhere on the way out.

"It's mine," I said. "Tamsin. It's *me*. My soul. She sold it. Twelve years ago, to buy the medicine, and it's been down here the whole time, in a *lantern*, and it knows me, and it—" My voice cracked like a boy's. "It sounds happy to see me. How does it sound *happy*?"

Tamsin didn't flinch. That's why I'd follow her into a mountain. She just looked at the lantern, then at me, and said, "Because it's yours. Of course it's glad. Now pick it up, because I don't think we're leaving without it, and I don't think—" She stopped.

She'd gone still in the way that means someone has come up behind you.

I turned.

At the end of the aisle stood a figure I hadn't heard approach, which should have been impossible, because the floor was stone and my ears work fine. It was tall and narrow and dressed in robes the color of ledger-paper, and its face was pleasant the way a locked door is pleasant — smooth, symmetrical, offering nothing. Around its neck, on a chain of black iron, hung a ring of keys. Dozens of them. Hundreds. They didn't clink when it moved.

"Forgive the interruption," it said, and its voice was the voice of a clerk reading terms aloud: courteous, complete, and utterly indifferent to whether you survive the signing. "But guests are not permitted to handle the inventory."

The flame in the lantern flared. Behind the glass, it pressed harder against the pane, and for the first time the whisper wasn't gentle.

*Run, sweetheart,* my soul said, in my mother's voice. *Run.*

The Keeper smiled with a face that had learned smiling from a book, and produced, from the folds of its robe, a ledger bound in something I refused to look at too closely.

"Now then," it said. "Which of you is Galloway?"
