## 9. The Keeper of Sold Souls

I had imagined the thing that kept souls would be a monster.

Twelve years of nightmares will do that to you. In my worst versions it was a spider the size of a cathedral, or a faceless judge with my mother's voice, or — once, memorably, after too much plum wine at Tamsin's midwinter table — a giant version of myself, wearing my soul like a coat and complaining that it didn't fit across the shoulders. I had braced myself for teeth, for chains, for a throne of bones with a name like a landslide.

What I got instead was a small elderly gentleman pouring tea.

He sat behind a desk of black wood in a room that should not have fit inside the mountain — long, low-ceilinged, lined floor to ceiling with drawers. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands, maybe, each no bigger than a bread box, each fitted with a little bone pull and a paper label written in a hand so fine it looked embroidered. The air smelled of cedar and candle smoke and something underneath both, something older, like the smell of a book that has been closed for a century.

The Keeper himself looked like someone's grandfather, if someone's grandfather had been carved out of patience. He was slight, silver-haired, dressed in a robe the color of unlit candles, and his hands — I could not stop looking at his hands — moved with the unhurried grace of a man who had never once in his existence been interrupted.

"Paloma Galloway," he said, and his voice was warm as a hearth. "You're earlier than expected. Do sit down. The tea is oolong; I find surface folk distrust anything stranger."

Behind me, Wren made a small sound in her throat. I didn't turn to look at her. I had the distinct sense that if I took my eyes off him, the room might rearrange itself around my absence.

"You were expecting me," I said.

"I keep ledgers." He set a second cup on the desk, then a third, then paused with the fourth teacup hovering and glanced past my shoulder with mild arithmetic. "Three of you arrived at the gate. One of you is not on my books." His eyes — pale grey, the grey of old pewter — settled on me with an expression I can only describe as *apologetic*. "That will matter later, I'm afraid. Everything here matters later. It's rather our defining feature."

I sat down because my knees made the decision without consulting me. Tamsin stood at my shoulder like a drawn knife, which was sweet of her, given that we were, at that moment, two people and a cartographer in a room full of drawers labeled by souls.

"Which one am I?" I asked. Stupid question. Obvious question. But my mouth has always gotten ahead of my courage.

"Drawer four thousand and eleven, alcove nine." He said it the way a librarian gives a call number. No cruelty in it. That was the worst part. Cruelty I knew how to fight. This was just... filing. "Would you like to see it?"

Every instinct I owned said *no*. My hand said yes before the rest of me caught up, and the Keeper smiled — gently, the way you'd smile at a child reaching for something hot — and rose, and led us three steps down the endless wall of drawers to a small bone-handled drawer labeled in that embroidered script: *GALLOWAY, P. SOLD BY MOTHER, PRICE ONE LIFE.*

He opened it.

Inside, on a bed of grey silk, lay a coin-sized disc of light. Not metal. Not glass. It moved when I breathed. When I leaned closer, it flared — bright, startled, *recognizing* — and I felt it like a hook behind my ribs, like a fish feeling the line go taut.

"That's—" My voice came out wrong. Thin. Twelve years old. "That's me."

"A portion of you," the Keeper corrected, closing the drawer with a click that went through me like a tooth cracking. "Souls are not whole things, Master Galloway, whatever your priests tell you. They are more like... gardens. Sections. This one was sold twelve years ago this spring, payment rendered against a debt owed by the crown of Ashrow to this court. Your mother signed in blood and grief, which is traditional, and the crown countersigned in ink and relief, which is also traditional." He folded his hands. "She got what she purchased. You lived through a fever that killed four children on your street. The court got its interest. Everyone was satisfied, in the way that contracts satisfy, which is to say nobody was happy but everything was legal."

"The crown," I said. "You're saying the crown owes *you*."

"I'm saying the crown pays *us*," the Keeper said mildly, "and has done since before there were crowns, back when it was only a warlord with a good memory for grudges. But that is above your weight class, and mine, frankly. Shall we return to tea?"

We returned to tea. It felt absurd, sitting in the belly of the mountain discussing my soul over oolong, but there was a quality to the Keeper's courtesy that made refusal feel like rudeness, and rudeness feel dangerous, the way it's dangerous to snap at a snake for being polite.

Tamsin didn't sit. She stood with her arms crossed and her jaw set in the particular angle I'd learned meant she was cataloguing exits. Wren had drifted toward the drawers, eyes moving over labels with the hunger of a woman reading names she'd been forbidden to know. I noted both facts and filed them under *later*, because the Keeper was watching me again with that terrible gentle attention.

"Now," he said. "You've come about the token."

"It says the debt is transferable," I said. "It says the holder can claim what's owed."

"It does." He poured my second cup himself. "And you hold it. Which means, technically, legally, in the oldest and most binding sense of the word, you may claim your soul back."

My heart did something acrobatic and unhelpful. "Then I claim it. Give it here. We'll be on our way—"

"There is a fee," the Keeper said.

Of course there was a fee. There is always a fee. I have run a charm stall for twelve years; I know how the sentence *there is a fee* works. What I wasn't prepared for was how kindly he said it.

"The court runs on oath," he continued, folding his hands again. "Not gold — gold is for surface kingdoms, who mistake weight for worth. We run on sworn things. Promises. Every oath sworn to your crown burns in our garden and heats our halls, lights our lamps, keeps the mountain breathing. And gifts—" his gaze flickered, briefly, to my sword hip, where Paloma-the-shopkeeper supposedly carried nothing sharper than a letter opener, "—gifts are simply oaths that live in the body. A swordspeaker's gift is an oath the blade swore to the crown, and the crown's line swore down to the blade, and so on, a chain of promises running all the way back to the warlord and all the way forward to a man selling fake love charms on Ashrow Lane."

I went very still. "How do you know about the stall?"

"I keep ledgers," he said again, with the faintest smile. "Yours is a thick one, for a man who pretends to be ordinary. Now. Here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, because I am courteous and not cruel, whatever you've decided to think of me."

He turned his teacup a quarter-turn, aligning the handle with the desk edge, and something in the precision of it made my skin crawl worse than any threat could have.

"You may have your soul back. Today. Freely walked out of this mountain, no further debt, no interest accruing against your children or your children's children — the contract dies clean. In exchange, you give me your gift. Not taken. *Given.* You kneel in my garden, you speak the renunciation yourself, willingly, and the power leaves you and becomes ours, and the ledger balances."

The room got very quiet. Even Wren stopped reading labels.

"And if I don't?" I asked.

"Then you walk out with your gift intact and your soul still in drawer four thousand and eleven," he said pleasantly, "and every year on the anniversary, the court collects its tithe from the crown, and the crown, being short, collects it from wherever it can — which lately means witch-hunts, Master Galloway, bonfires, hedge-healers dragged through the streets. Your soul is collateral on a debt the crown cannot pay. So long as it sits in my drawer, the hunt continues. So long as it sits in my drawer, girls like your friend Tamsin's neighbor get taken in carts." He spread his hands, all apology. "I don't make the world this way. I merely keep the accounts of the world as it is. But you should understand what refusing costs, and it isn't you who pays."

There it was. Mercy, if you squinted. Mercy shaped exactly like a trap.

Because that was the elegance of it, wasn't it. He wasn't threatening me. He was *offering*, warmly, reasonably, a trade that any sensible man would take — my gift, which I'd spent twelve years smothering anyway, buried under sleight of hand and harmless charms, versus my soul, versus everyone the crown would hurt while I dithered. He'd priced it so that walking away felt like murder and accepting felt like housekeeping. Give up the thing you hate having, keep the thing you've missed, everybody wins.

Except.

"Freely given," I said slowly. "Those words are doing a lot of work."

"They are the entire mechanism," the Keeper agreed, and there — just for a heartbeat, just at the edges — something moved behind the polite grandfather face. Something patient. Something very, very old, watching a lock approach its key. "Coerced sacrifice feeds us poorly. Willing surrender is the finest fuel there is. Why do you think your crown makes everyone *swear* their loyalty rather than merely demand obedience? An oath freely spoken burns brighter than a prisoner ever could."

"And if I give you my gift freely," I said, "what happens to the oath on my sword? The chain you mentioned?"

The pause was small. Anyone else would have missed it. But I'd spent twelve years reading customers — the hesitation before a lie, the half-second where the price gets calculated — and the Keeper hesitated.

"The oath dissolves with the gift," he said. "Naturally."

"Naturally," I repeated.

"Master Galloway." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into something softer, almost tender, and gods help me, some starved corner of me *leaned toward it*. "You have spent twelve years hiding. Hiding the gift, hiding the grief, hiding behind a stall of lies you sell to lonely people. You are tired. I can see that you are tired. I am offering you rest. Walk out of here whole — truly whole, your soul home where it belongs — and never again flinch when a guard looks at your hands. Never again feel the sword whisper to you at night. Is that not mercy? Name one person in your life who has offered you anything half so kind."

And the awful, shameful truth was that I couldn't. Not right then, not with the hook of that little light still pulling behind my ribs, not with the smell of cedar and the warmth of the tea and twelve years of exhaustion sitting on my chest like a stone. He'd found the exact shape of my wanting and poured himself into it.

I looked down at my teacup. My hand was shaking.

*Take it,* whispered something in me that sounded like my mother. *Take the deal. Live. That's all I ever wanted for you, just live.*

But my mother had said *just live* twelve years ago, and look what living had bought: a city of hunted neighbors, a girl in a cart, a drawer with my name on it. *Just live* was how they got you. *Just live* was the bait on every hook the powerful ever cast, because a frightened man doesn't read the fine print, and a tired one doesn't ask what dissolves along with the gift.

"Not yet," I heard myself say.

The Keeper's smile didn't move, but the temperature of it changed, the way a room changes when a door opens somewhere far away. "The offer stands until you leave my hall."

"I know," I said. "That's what worries me. Nothing free holds forever. I want to see the garden first. I want to see what my mother's oath looks like when it's burning. I want—" I stood, and my legs held, which surprised me, "—to read the rest of the ledger before I sign anything. Where I come from, sir, that's called due diligence."

For the first time, the Keeper looked at me the way a shopkeeper looks at a customer who has actually read the contract. Respectful. Recalculating.

"Very well," he said, rising, setting his cup precisely on its saucer. "The garden is open to visitors. It likes visitors." He gestured toward a door I was quite sure hadn't been there when we entered. "Though I caution you — most who see it burning find the trade much easier to accept afterward. Hope is heavy, Master Galloway. I offer to carry yours."

I didn't answer. Behind me, I heard Wren's breath catch at something on a label, and Tamsin's boots shift — and then, when I glanced back, only Tamsin was standing by the door. Wren was gone, three drawers down, her finger resting on a slip of paper, her face the color of ash, reading a name over and over like a prayer she'd forgotten she knew.

"Wren?" I said.

She didn't hear me. Or she heard me and couldn't afford to answer, because whatever name was on that label, it had her by the throat — and the Keeper, watching her with his patient grey eyes, made a small note in a ledger I hadn't seen him pick up, and smiled.

* * *

They gave us a guide to the garden — a thin young clerk with ink-stained fingers and a smile that never quite reached compliance, who walked ahead of us through corridors of black stone murmuring courtesies like a litany. Tamsin fell into step beside me.

"He offered you a trade," she murmured. It wasn't a question. She'd heard. Of course she'd heard.

"He offered me mercy," I said.

"I know that tone," Tamsin said grimly. "Hedge-healers use it. 'This won't hurt a bit, dearie.' It's the tone you use when the thing you're about to do would horrify them if they understood it." Her hand found my sleeve, brief and hard. "Whatever he's offering, Paloma — the price isn't the price he named. It never is. So before you sign anything, before you kneel anywhere — we need to know how that garden actually works. And I think I know how we find out."

She nodded, subtle, toward the ink-stained clerk walking ahead of us — toward the ledger tucked under his arm, and the ring of keys at his belt, and the way his eyes kept flicking to her with something that was not fear and was not respect but was, unmistakably, *interest*.

"The under-clerk," she breathed. "Leave him to me."
