## 10. Tamsin's Bargain

The dead keep better company than the living, my mother used to say. She meant it as a comfort, I think, though she said it while drinking, so who knows.

I'd been thinking about my sister a lot since we came down the stair that wasn't there. Which is to say: more than usual. Which is to say: constantly. In the living world I had learned to fold her memory up small and tuck it into the pocket behind my ribs where I kept all the things too heavy to carry openly — the smell of her hair (woodsmoke and mint), the way she sang off-key on purpose to make me laugh, the last thing she ever said to me, which was *don't wait up*, because she thought she was going somewhere she'd come back from.

Down here, in the court beneath the mountain, folding things small didn't work. The court had a way of unfolding you. The lanterns burned without flame. The corridors curved when you weren't looking at them. And every sold thing in this place seemed to know what you were carrying in your pockets.

* * *

I found the under-clerk on the third day, or maybe the fourth — time down here moved like syrup off a cold spoon — in a gallery of ledgers so tall the shelves disappeared into dark overhead. He was a thin, grey, precise creature with ink-stained fingers and spectacles he didn't need, because his eyes were not eyes at all but two small polished buttons of jet, and they saw everything anyway.

He was cataloguing. That's what he did, I came to understand. Every soul in this court had been entered into a ledger once, priced and shelved like jars of preserves, and the under-clerk maintained the shelves.

"You're the hedge-healer," he said without looking up. "The one who wasn't sold."

"The one who walked in voluntarily," I corrected. "There's a difference. Voluntary means stupid."

That earned me a pause in the pen's scratching. A long one. Down here, I would learn, surprise was a currency too, and I'd just paid him some.

"Paloma Galloway's companion," he said. "The swordspeaker." He said it the way a fishmonger says *the good haddock*. "And Wren, the mapmaker. Born here, sold upward. Interesting inventory your little expedition carries."

"I'm nobody's inventory."

"Everyone is someone's inventory, healer. The only question is whose ledger holds your line." He finally looked up, and those button-eyes caught the ledger-light and held it. "You want something. People don't wander into the archive wing for their health. There isn't any health down here."

I hadn't planned to say anything yet. I'd planned to browse, to seem idle, to let him make the first offer the way you let a horse come to your hand. But there was something about being seen so completely by those jet buttons that made pretense feel like wearing wet wool.

"The garden," I said. "The burning garden. Paloma needs to reach its heart, and we don't know the way."

"No one knows the way. That's rather the point of a heart." He set down his pen with the delicacy of a man laying down a winning card. "The Keeper tends it. The paths through it shift with every oath that burns. What was true an hour ago is a lie now. You'd need knowledge that updates itself."

"And you can get it."

"I can *buy* it," he said, with the first thing resembling warmth I'd seen on him, and it was worse than his coldness. "Everything in this court is bought or sold, healer. Even information about the fire. Especially information about the fire."

"Then name your price."

"Oh, no." He folded his hands. "You misunderstand the transaction. I don't set prices. The *market* sets prices, and the market for what you have—" the button-eyes did something almost like blinking— "is very high indeed."

"What do I have?"

"Grief," he said simply. "Fresh grief. Grief is the rarest stock in this place, hedge-healer. The sold souls down here, their grief was taken at purchase — standard procedure, makes them easier to shelve. The court runs on oaths, but it *trades* in grief. And yours—" he tilted his head, reading me like a ledger page— "yours has a sister in it. Dead above ground. Recent enough to still be wet."

The pocket behind my ribs went cold.

"No," I said.

"I haven't named a price."

"You named it. You just dressed it up in market language."

He smiled. It was a clerk's smile, apologetic, procedural, and entirely without malice, which somehow made it infinitely worse. "Then let us speak plainly, since plainly seems to distress you. I can give you what Paloma Galloway needs: the way to the garden's heart, updated hourly, delivered by whatever means survives down here. In exchange, I require memories. Not all of her. The market doesn't deal in wholesale. A few entries. Withdrawn cleanly. She won't feel the loss — you will, briefly, and then you won't, because you won't remember what you lost."

"That's monstrous."

"That's *commerce*," he said, with genuine puzzlement, as if I'd called arithmetic monstrous. "You are asking me to help unmake this court. Do you imagine I'm unaware? Of course I know. I've known for centuries. I am the under-clerk; I file the ledgers of everything the Keeper buys and sells, and I have read every entry, and I will tell you a secret the Keeper does not know I know: the ledgers balance wrong. They've balanced wrong for twelve years. This court is owed something it never received, and debts that go unpaid make institutions unstable." He unfolded his hands. "I am not loyal to the Keeper, healer. I am loyal to the *ledger*. Help me balance it, and I'll help you burn it."

* * *

I didn't answer him that day. Or what passed for a day. I went back to the room Wren had found us — a hollowed-out alcove behind a wall of shelved names, beds of grey moss that breathed faintly, like the mountain itself was sleeping next to us — and I lay there listening to Paloma breathe across the alcove, and I hated myself in advance.

Because here is the ugly truth, the truth I'd tell no one for a long time: I knew before I left the archive that I would do it. I sat with the horror of it for exactly as long as it took to look at Paloma sleeping — this man who'd broken his oath in public to save me from the inquisitors, who'd used his gift for the first time in years with his whole body shaking, who carried his own soul's debt-token like a stone in his chest — and I understood that I would pay anything the clerk asked, because he needed the way to the heart, and I could give it to him, and what was a few memories against a man's soul?

Grief spent as currency. My mother would have laughed herself sick.

Mara was my older sister by four years. She was the one who taught me the hedge-craft — not the formal stuff, the real stuff, the bruised-leaf-and-whispered-word kind. When the crown started hunting the gifted, she sent me away first. Said one healer in the family was already enough of a target. Said she'd follow. Said *don't wait up.*

They burned her on Ashrow Green on a Tuesday. I know it was Tuesday because the town crier announced the executions right after announcing the grain prices, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that has never left me: *they filed my sister between barley and oats.*

So yes. I understood ledgers. I understood what it was to have someone you loved become an entry.

The question was how many entries of her I was willing to sell.

* * *

"How many?" I asked the under-clerk.

He looked genuinely pleased to see me, in the way of a merchant greeting a returning customer. "Ah. The healer returns. I took the liberty of preparing an itemized proposal." From somewhere inside his grey coat he produced a slate, and on it, in beautiful copperplate, was written:

**ITEM ONE: The day of her death. Complete withdrawal.**
**ITEM TWO: Her voice. All memories containing the sound of it.**
**ITEM THREE: Her name.**

I read it three times. My hands were steady, which surprised me. "Item three. You want her *name*?"

"Names anchor memory, healer. If you keep her name, you keep a hook, and hooks drag the rest back. A clean withdrawal requires removing the anchor. Otherwise the sale... regrows." He tapped the slate. "Like cutting ivy above the root."

"If I sell her name, I won't know I had a sister."

"You'll know you have a wound," he said gently. "Wounds persist. That's what makes grief such honest currency — it outlives its own accounting."

I should have walked away. I want that on the record, wherever records go when gardens burn: I stood in that gallery of ledgers, in the syrup-slow light, and I knew walking away was possible, and I weighed it honestly, and I chose to stay. Not because I was brave. Because Paloma was worth more than my remembering, and I had decided that the day I broke Tamsin out of an inquisitor's cart, and I have never once regretted the deciding.

Only the paying.

"Items one and two," I said. "Not three."

"Healer—"

"Not. Three. Take the day she died and take her voice, and leave me the name. I'll live with the ivy. I'll live with the hook dragging. Give me the route to the garden's heart, updating, surviving, and I'll walk out of here not knowing why my chest hurts when I hear singing, but knowing *her name*, and you will never touch her again."

The button-eyes regarded me for a long, long moment. Then the under-clerk did something extraordinary: he crossed out item three on the slate. Physically crossed it out, with his pen, in front of me.

"A counter-offer accepted against my better judgment," he said. "Do you know why I accept?"

"Enlighten me."

"Because the Keeper always takes everything. It is the nature of the institution. And I find—" the jet buttons gleamed— "that I have developed a small, unsanctioned fondness for transactions in which someone refuses. Refusal is data, healer. Refusal tells me the ledgers can still balance differently." He extended his ink-stained hand. "Shall we conclude?"

I shook it. His hand was cold and dry as old paper, and the moment our skin touched, he said, in a voice suddenly formal as a marriage rite:

"*Withdrawn: the day of her death. Withdrawn: the sound of her voice.*"

And it was like two pages tearing out of the book of me.

No pain. That was the obscene part — no pain at all, just a smoothness, a gap where texture used to be. I knew Mara had died; I still knew it, the fact remained like a nail in a wall. But the day around the nail was gone. Burned clean. And her voice — I reached for it instantly, panicking, grasping — and found I could remember every word she'd ever said to me and none of the sound. *Don't wait up*, the words floating silent as text on a page. My sister had become literature.

"There," the under-clerk said, and pressed a small object into my palm: a sliver of pale stone, veined with slow-moving light. "A waystone. It holds the route to the garden's heart and rewrites itself as the paths shift. Follow the vein of light. When the light forks, the way has changed; the stone will know before you do." He closed my fingers over it. "One caution, free of charge, because I liked your refusal: the heart of the garden is not a place. It is a *moment*. The first oath ever sworn to the crown, still burning after all these years. Everything else is kindling around it. Your swordspeaker must renounce at the root, not the branches. And healer—"

"Yes?"

"When you see what's growing at the heart — the oaths, the people inside them — do not stop to grieve. The garden feeds on grief freely given. That is how it got your friend's mother, and that is how it will get you." He stepped back into the shadow of the shelves. "Now go. And if anyone asks, you stole this from me while I slept, and I put up a tremendous fight."

* * *

I walked back through the galleries holding the waystone like a coal, and I tested the wound as I went, prodding it the way you prod a pulled tooth. *Mara.* The name worked. The name rose up whole and warm and mine. *My sister Mara, who taught me hedge-craft, who sent me away, who died on a Tuesday between the barley prices and the oats.*

But when I tried to remember her face saying goodbye — nothing. A silent film missing its final reel. And when I tried to remember her voice at all — any voice, laughing, angry, singing off-key on purpose — smooth blank paper, page after page.

I stopped in a corridor of shelved names and I cried, quietly, efficiently, the way you cry when you've budgeted for it, and then I wiped my face and went to find Paloma, because grief is grief but the debt comes due either way, and I have always believed in paying what you owe.

He was awake. Sitting up in the moss-bed with the debt-token in his hands, turning it over and over like a man trying to read his fortune in a bone.

"Tamsin." He looked up, and his face did something complicated. "Where have you—"

"I bought us a map," I said, and held up the waystone, its vein of light pulsing like a slow heartbeat. "To the garden's heart. Don't ask what it cost."

He looked at me too long. Paloma had spent twelve years hiding what he was, so he knew hiding when he saw it, and I watched him decide — visibly, kindly — not to press. Not yet.

"Tamsin," he said again, softly. "What did it cost?"

And because I couldn't lie to him outright — because he'd broken an oath for me and was about to break another for all of us — I told him the only true thing I had left:

"It cost the sound of my sister's voice. So when we get to that garden, Galloway, you'd better make sure it was worth hearing."
