## 11. Wren's Map Ends Here

I have been called many things in my fifty-one years: fraud, heretic, the Cartographer of Buried Roads, and once — by a very drunk harbormaster — "the only honest woman in Ashrow," which was the most damning of all. But the thing I have been longest is a liar by omission, and tonight the omission has grown too heavy to carry up another stair.

So when Paloma Galloway asked me, in that careful voice of his, how a disgraced mapmaker came to know the roads beneath the mountain at all, I did what I always do before telling the truth. I made tea. There is no problem so large that it cannot be postponed for the length of time it takes water to boil, and some problems — the ones involving your own birth — can be postponed indefinitely if you keep a full kettle.

Tamsin sat across from me with her arms folded, watching me the way she watches everything: like a hedge-healer assessing a wound she already knows is worse than I'll admit. She'd seen my hands shake on the stair down from Odd Wren's garret. She'd said nothing. Healers are like that. They let you bleed until you're ready to say where it hurts.

Paloma turned his cup without drinking. The debt-token lay between us on the table, bone-pale, patient as a debt always is.

"You said you'd mapped the court," he said. "Mapped it. Not guessed at it. You knew which galleries flood in spring. You knew the Keeper keeps a ledger of names." His eyes came up. They were kind eyes, which was the worst part. Kind eyes make lying feel like kicking a dog. "Nobody maps a place they've never been."

"No," I said. "They don't."

The kettle began its low complaint toward boiling. I watched it instead of him for a moment, gathering myself the way one gathers a hem before stepping into cold water.

"I was born there," I said.

* * *

You should understand something about the buried court before I go on, because everyone above ground gets it wrong. They think of it as a prison, or a vault, or a hell with good manners. It is none of those things, or not only those things. It is a *household*. It has nurseries. It has a kitchen that smells of mushrooms and slow-simmered bones. It has a schoolroom where children learn their letters off slate because paper burns too easily, and the Keeper is very particular about what burns.

I was born in the nursery wing, third daughter of a woman whose name I never learned to spell, because she was sold-from before I could hold a chalk. That's the phrase we used below — sold-from. Every child in the court had two categories available to them: sold, and sold-from. Sold meant your soul sat in the Keeper's ledger, a line item, an asset. Sold-from meant someone above had traded you away, and whether they'd done it for gold or bread or a decade of peace, the result was identical: you belonged to the mountain now, and the mountain kept what was sold.

I was sold at six years old. Not downward — upward. That's the part nobody believes. The trade runs both ways. The court sells things to the surface too: luck, longevity, small mercies, and occasionally a child with steady hands and a gift for lines. A surveyor in the crown's service wanted an apprentice who could draw passages he'd never walked, and the crown asked the court, and the court named its price, and the price was me. Or rather, the price was the remainder of my soul, which even at six I understood was a way of saying *me* while letting everyone involved pretend otherwise.

My new master was not cruel. I want that on the record, because cruelty would be simpler. He taught me instruments, projection, scale, the mathematics of depth. He praised my work. He fed me well and beat me never and called me *his find*, the way a man calls a horse *his find*, and I lived in his house for eleven years drawing maps of places I remembered with a child's imperfect memory and a child's terrible clarity, both at once.

Because here is the thing about being sold upward out of the buried court: you remember it. The soul they take isn't the memory. The memory stays, lodged in you like a splinter worked too deep to dig out, and every map I ever drew was me pressing on the edges of that splinter, trying to make it ache in useful shapes.

"Your maps," Paloma said quietly. "They weren't commissions."

"They were letters," I said. "Home. Written in the only language I had left."

* * *

Let me tell you what the leaving was like, since I've started. It costs nothing now; the coin's already spent.

The day they brought me up, I was carried in a surveyor's crate with air holes drilled in the top, because the passages between the court and the surface are not for walking, not for the sold. The court does not permit its property to stroll out the front door. I lay in the dark among brass instruments and packing straw and I sang to myself, very quietly, the counting song the nursery used, the one where you count down from ten and each number is a door closing. At one, the crate opened, and there was sky.

Do you know what sky looks like to a child who has never seen it? It looks like a mistake. It looks like a ceiling that someone forgot to finish, vast and unfinished and going on forever, and I screamed, and the surveyor laughed and told his wife I was frightened of open spaces, and in a way he was right. I have spent forty-five years frightened of open spaces. A map has borders. The sky has none. Nothing that vast can be trusted to hold anything.

But I learned. Children learn anything; it's the one cruelty-proof talent we have. I learned the surface, its roads and rivers, its little towns stacked along the mountain's skirt like cups on a shelf. And every night I drew the below from memory, over and over, refining it as I grew and my memory grew with me, correcting a child's errors with an adult's rigor. The gallery of lamps. The flooded stair. The long market of the sold, where souls hang in glass like lanterns. The garden.

I drew the garden last, always last, the way you save the hardest passage of a piece of music for when your hands are warm.

"And the other children?" Tamsin asked. Her voice was rough. She'd lost a sister; I'd heard it in the way she spoke of grief earlier, like a woman handling a broken bowl, careful of the sharp edges. "In the nursery. What happened to them?"

"Some were bought," I said. "Like me. Trades upward. The court likes those trades — they bring fresh eyes, fresh hands, and the surface never asks where the clever apprentice came from." I turned my cup again. The tea had gone cold; I'd made it for nothing, which felt appropriate. "Most stayed. Most are still there. The sold don't age the way we do, not while their souls sit in the ledger. Some of them are still six years old. Still singing the counting song. Still waiting for a door that opens outward."

Paloma had gone very still. He does that — goes still the way water goes still before it freezes, and you understand that the stillness is not calm but concentration.

"The token," he said. "It names me. My mother sold my soul twelve years ago. Which means—"

"Which means somewhere below, there is a boy who is still you," I said. "Or the shape of you, the part the ledger holds. Twelve years old forever, or near enough. Yes. I know how it works. I've had forty-five years to study the mechanics of my own theft."

* * *

Here is the confession under the confession, the one I've circled twice now and must finally land on.

I didn't agree to guide them out of charity. Charity is for people who can afford it. I agreed because Paloma Galloway was the first person in forty-five years to stand in my garret holding proof that the road below could be walked — that it wasn't just ink and longing, that the doors I'd drawn from memory actually opened. And I agreed because I had already decided, months before he ever knocked, that if the chance came I would go home, and I would not come back alone.

"You're wondering why I helped," I said. "A disgraced cartographer, living on forged charts and bad tea, risking the gallows for two strangers. Everyone wonders. Tamsin wondered loudest, with her eyebrows."

"My eyebrows are very articulate," Tamsin said, and for a moment the room almost smiled.

"It wasn't for you," I told Paloma. "I'm sorry, but you deserve the truth before you walk into that place: I am not your rescuer. I'm a woman going home, and you happen to be traveling the same direction. When I was six years old, a door closed behind me, and I have spent my whole life drawing that door. Every commission, every chart, every scrap of parchment I ever put my hand to — underneath, it was always the same drawing. The door. The stair. The way back." My voice did something I didn't authorize; it cracked, and I let it, because I am old enough to know that a crack honestly placed is worth more than a smooth lie. "My maps end at the garden, Paloma. Do you understand? Every single one. I could never draw past it. My hand wouldn't. Because past the garden is the ledger, and in the ledger, on a page I could recite in my sleep, is my name, and next to my name is a price, and the price was paid, and paid things stay paid. Until someone burns the book."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below us, Ashrow Lane went about its evening, unaware that three people in a garret were planning arson against eternity.

"So no," I said. "I will not lead you down and lead you out and wave goodbye at the top of the stair while the mountain keeps its household. I was sold upward. There are others who were sold nowhere at all — born below, kept below, children who have never seen the sky and think it's a story we tell to frighten them. If the garden burns, the ledger burns, and everything in it comes free. Every soul. Every name. Every child." I looked at Paloma, and I found I was not asking. Fifty-one years teaches you the difference between asking and announcing. "So whatever your plan is, whatever bargain you mean to strike with that courteous horror in the deep, amend it. We go for the garden, and we do not leave until every name in that book walks out under its own power. Including mine. *Especially* mine."

* * *

Paloma was quiet a long time. I gave him the silence; men carrying oaths need silence the way mules need rest.

"You were a child," he said finally. "And they sold you like a bolt of cloth."

"They sold me like an investment," I corrected. "Cloth doesn't appreciate. I did. Forty-five years of mapping, and every map I made made the crown richer, made the trades smoother, made the whole rotten exchange between the surface and the deep run easier. That's the joke of it, Paloma. That's the wound wearing a gift's clothing. They took me out of the dark, gave me a splendid talent, and set me to work improving the machinery of my own captivity. Every accurate chart I ever drew was a brick in the wall." I laughed, and it came out with edges on it. "My master used to say I had a gift for finding what was hidden. He never once asked why I was so good at it. Nobody asks the sold why they know the way back. They just use the knowledge and call it loyalty."

Tamsin reached across the table and put her hand flat on mine. She didn't say anything. Hedge-healers know when touch says more than words, and hers said: *I have a dead sister and a live anger and I understand the arithmetic of loss.* I turned my hand over and gripped hers, briefly, like two survivors of the same shipwreck acknowledging the same water.

"The children," Paloma said at last. "How many?"

"In the nursery wing? When I left, thirty-one. Some will be gone — traded, spent, whatever the court does with inventory it tires of. Some will be grown, or as grown as the un-souled get. Call it twenty. Call it thirty. Enough that you cannot carry them and I cannot leave them, and so we are agreed whether we like it or not."

"We're agreed," he said.

He said it simply, without heroics, and I want it noted that this is the mark of the man: he does not perform his decency, he merely pays it, like a debt he considers his own. He picked up the bone token and turned it in the lamplight, and I saw him look at it differently than he had an hour ago — not as his own stolen property anymore, but as one entry in a very long column.

"There's a problem," he said. "The garden. The Keeper said — well. You'll see what the Keeper says. But fire needs a speaker, and I'm the only swordspeaker this side of the mountain, and breaking an oath kills the man who swore it. So whatever we're building, we're building around that."

"Then we'd better build fast," I said, and stood, and went to the great chest under my bed where I keep the maps I've never shown anyone — the true ones, the ones drawn from the inside of a six-year-old's memory, the ones that end at the garden because I could never bear to draw what comes after.

I unlocked it. I lifted out the oldest sheet, the one with the nursery wing marked in a child's uncertain hand, thirty-one small beds drawn in a row, each bed with a name beside it in chalk-faded ink.

Mine was the third from the window.

"Learn these," I said, and spread the map across the table over the top of everything else — the tea, the token, the oath, the whole fragile evening. "Because tomorrow we go down through the flooded stair, and after that, my maps end."

Outside, far off toward the mountain, something answered — a sound like a bell, if a bell could grieve, rolling up through the stone of Ashrow Lane and into the soles of my feet, where I recognized it the way you recognize your mother's voice after forty-five years.

The court knew we were coming. It had known all along.

It was ringing the nursery bell for supper.
