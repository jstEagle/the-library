## 12. The Crown's Envoy

The stair took us down the way a throat takes food — slowly, reluctantly, and with every intention of keeping us.

I don't remember most of it. That's not false modesty or dramatic flourish; I genuinely lost hours. There was a stretch where the walls went from cut stone to something older, veined with pale roots that pulsed faintly when Tamsin brushed them, like the mountain had a pulse and we were traveling down its gullet toward the heart. There was another stretch where Wren stopped walking entirely and stood with her hand flat against the rock, listening, her lips moving as though she were reading aloud from a book no one else could see.

"What is she doing?" Tamsin whispered.

"Mapping," I said. "In her head. She doesn't need paper anymore."

Wren opened her eyes. "The flooded stair was the last of my work," she said. "Everything below this belongs to them." And she said *them* the way you'd say *the weather*, flat and inevitable, a fact you build your life around without ever expecting it to apologize.

We slept in a gallery where the ceiling hung low enough that I could touch it, which I chose not to do, on the principle that some invitations are better left unanswered. Tamsin kept first watch. Somewhere in the dark, water dripped in a rhythm too regular to be accidental — three drops, pause, three drops — and after an hour I realized Wren was counting them under her breath, and after two hours I realized they'd been counting the same pattern since before we arrived, and I decided firmly that this was a thing I would think about never.

The bell woke us.

Not the grieving nursery bell from Ashrow Lane — this one lived down here, and it didn't grieve so much as *announce*. It came through the stone itself, through our boots, through the bones of our hands where they pressed against the floor, a vibration more than a sound, and Wren sat up already dressed, which told me she hadn't really been sleeping at all.

"It knows we're here," she said.

"The court?"

"The whole mountain. It's been counting us since the stair." She shouldered her pack. "Down here, being known isn't the same as being caught. But it's a start."

* * *

The passage widened by degrees, the way a lie widens — each step plausible on its own, until suddenly you're somewhere you never agreed to go. The rough gallery gave way to worked stone, the worked stone to tile, and the tile to something I had no name for: floors inlaid with lines of silver that glowed faintly underfoot and rearranged themselves, very slowly, as we walked. Wren watched them for a long moment and then laughed, one short, ugly note.

"They're redrawing the map," she said. "The floor. We're standing on their map of us."

Tamsin looked down. The silver line beneath her feet had just curved gently around mine, the way a shepherd's path curves around a sheep.

"Then let's give them something worth charting," she said, and stepped off the line entirely.

I loved her a little, right then. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept down here — which, given what I've since learned about ledgers, is probably everywhere.

The corridor opened without warning into the court.

I've tried, in the years since, to describe it to people who weren't there, and I've failed every time, because the honest description sounds like madness: it was beautiful the way a trap is beautiful. A cavern vast enough to hold weather, roofed with stone carved into branches and leaves so dense and high that the eye gave up trying to find where tree ended and sky began. Light fell from somewhere — warm, gold, sourceless — and fell on terraces of white stone descending in tiers, and on figures moving between them: men and women in fine clothes, going about the business of a court. Some wore crowns of black iron. Some wore chains of the same iron, delicate as jewelry, and wore them with the same pride.

And at the center of everything, filling the middle distance the way a cathedral fills a town, stood the garden.

I will tell you about the garden properly later. For now I'll say only this: I saw it burning, and the fire was wrong — green-white, cold-edged, silent — and every flame rose out of nothing visible, out of the air itself, row upon row upon row of them, thousands, tens of thousands, planted in beds of dark earth as far as the light reached. And even from that distance, even before I understood anything, some animal part of me knew what burned there. You can't spend twelve years hiding a gift behind sleight of hand without learning to feel oaths the way you feel weather. The garden was made of promises. It was a field of vows on fire, and the court warmed its hands at the blaze.

"There," Wren said quietly. She nodded toward a tier near the base of the garden, where a long table stood attended by servants who moved slightly too smoothly, like figures in a dream who have practiced being people. "That's where they'll receive us. We're expected."

"How can you possibly—"

"Because I was born here." She said it the way you'd confess to a crime, or claim an inheritance; with her, I've learned, those are often the same gesture. "And because nobody walks into this court uninvited, Paloma. Not even us. Especially not us."

* * *

They received us at the long table with wine I refused and bread Tamsin ate anyway, because Tamsin holds that refusing bread is an insult no situation can justify, and I love that about her too, though in that moment I mostly wanted to shake her.

The servants did not speak. They poured, they bowed, they withdrew, and their smoothness resolved under close inspection into something worse than smoothness: a faint translucence at the edges, as though each of them had been thinned by use, the way a coin wears down from passing through too many hands.

"Kept things," Wren murmured. "Sold souls wearing bodies that don't quite fit anymore. Don't look long. Looking long is how they get permission."

"Permission for what?"

"To be looked at."

I looked at the table instead. It was set for four, which was interesting, because we were three.

The fourth chair filled while we watched.

He came up out of the floor — not dramatically, no rising platform, no theater; the tiles simply parted and closed behind him the way water closes over a dropped stone — and he was, I will admit, exactly what a chief inquisitor of the crown ought to look like, which is to say he looked like nothing at all. Middle height, middle years, a face arranged for forgetting. Grey cloak, plain at a distance. Only up close did the embroidery show: thread-of-gold sigils stitched across the shoulders and down the sleeves, hundreds of them, and I knew the stitchwork instantly because I'd spent half my life copying lesser versions onto charm-cards for copper coins.

Oaths. He was wearing oaths. Hundreds of sworn bindings, embroidered into his very clothing, and he carried them lightly, the way a rich man carries money.

"Paloma Galloway," he said. His voice was pleasant. That was the worst thing about him, though I didn't know it yet — I still thought the worst thing was his office, his rank, the witch-hunt writ large in his presence. "Swordspeaker. Last of the line. You've caused the crown a great deal of anxiety, and I've come a very long way to relieve it."

"You came down here," Tamsin said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the healer's voice, the one she used over wounds. "You're *crown*. What are you doing in the court beneath the mountain?"

He smiled at her with genuine warmth, and I hated how good he was at it.

"Madam, where do you imagine the crown's authority comes from?" He spread his hands, and the golden sigils caught the green-white light of the distant garden. "Every coronation oath ever sworn feeds that fire. Every soldier's enlistment vow. Every magistrate's affirmation, every witness's testimony sworn on the book, every marriage solemnized in the king's name. The crown does not merely know this place exists." He leaned forward slightly, confiding, a man sharing a family secret. "The crown is *furnished* by it. We trade. Souls sold below rise as power above; oaths sworn above burn as fuel below. It has been thus for nine hundred years, and it will be thus long after all of us are ash, unless someone does something regrettable."

My mouth was dry. "Someone like me."

"Someone exactly like you." He turned to me fully then, and for the first time his pleasant face became precise, focused, the face of a man reading a document he'd waited years to open. "Twelve years ago, a woman named Maren Galloway came to this court with a dying boy in her arms and a bargain in her mouth. She sold your soul, Paloma — the whole of it, root and branch, in perpetuity. In exchange, the court granted her son twelve years of life. And in exchange for *that*—" he paused, savoring it, a man arriving at the best clause of the contract, "—your mother required the crown to stand surety. Which meant the crown countersigned. Which means—" he tapped the table once, gently, "—that your soul is not merely the court's property. It is an *asset of the realm*. I am here," he said, "to conduct an audit."

Tamsin had gone white. Wren had gone very still, in the way I was learning preceded either maps or knives.

"And if I refuse the audit?"

"Then you are a debtor in default, and default has remedies." He said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "But I haven't come to seize. I've come to offer." From within his grey cloak he drew a scroll, sealed twice with wax I recognized — the crown's red, and beneath it, a second seal, black, that I did not. "A full pardon. For you and your companions. The hunt ends tonight; the inquisitors withdraw from Ashrow Lane by morning. Your neighbor — the hedge-healer taken Tuesday — returned to her street alive and unharmed. Every gifted soul in the city walks free under your name."

The words landed in my chest like warm stones. I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Tamsin's face — free. Wren's maps — legal. The fear I'd carried since the crier's bell first rang over the rooftops — lifted. He watched me feel all of it, watched it move across my face, and I understood with sick clarity that he was very, very good at his work.

"In exchange for," I said.

"A small thing. A formality, truly." He slid the scroll across the table until it touched my hand. "Your companions descended with you under a misunderstanding, which the pardon will correct. You will attest — publicly, sworn on your sword, which the crown accepts as the highest form of attestation — that you coerced them. That the hedge-healer was taken in your service against her will, and the cartographer acted under duress. One sentence, spoken in the garden's light, and they walk free, and you walk free, and the old arrangement continues as it has for nine hundred years." He smiled again. "Loyalty, Master Galloway. The crown asks only loyalty. It seems little enough, for so much."

I looked at the scroll. Under the seals, in fine clerk's hand, the sentence was already written, waiting only for my voice to make it true. *They followed me deceived.*

And here is the thing I learned about myself at that table, in that terrible beautiful place: I looked at Tamsin, and I looked at Wren, and I thought — *one sentence.* One sentence, and Tamsin goes home to her herbs and her stubborn kindness. One sentence, and Wren goes back to her maps and her griefs. One sentence, and I keep my gift, my pardon, my head, and everyone I've come to love in the space of five wretched days gets to live.

It would have been so easy. That's what I want anyone reading this to understand. It wasn't a devil's bargain with obvious horns. It was a kind man offering a reasonable price for a worthless word, and the word cost me nothing except everything it would mean afterward.

I picked up the scroll.

Tamsin's breath caught. Wren said, very quietly, "Paloma," in the voice you use to stop a horse.

"I'm not signing," I said. "I'm reading."

And I read it aloud, every clause, in a clear voice, while the inquisitor's smile held and the translucent servants poured wine no one drank. Because I am a swordspeaker, and words are my craft, and I wanted to hear exactly what shape of lie I'd be asked to swear.

Clause seven stopped me.

Clause seven was small, tucked between provisions about jurisdiction and forfeiture, and it read: *The party of the first part further conveys unto the crown all claims, present and future, upon the soul herein described, to be held in trust and renewed annually at the crown's discretion.*

"He's not pardoning you," Wren said softly. "He's refinancing you. Twelve years bought you once. This buys you forever — and puts the deed in the crown's vault instead of the court's."

The inquisitor's smile did not waver. That was the moment I finally understood that the smile had never been real, that it was embroidery, thread-of-gold, an oath worn lightly — and that underneath it was the same thin translucence I'd seen in the servants. He'd been down here a long time. Perhaps he'd been down here always. Perhaps the crown's chief inquisitor was simply the oldest kept thing in the collection, wearing a body that fit him better than theirs because he'd stopped minding.

"You knew," I said to him. "About my mother. About the token. You've known for twelve years, and you let me sell charms on a street corner like a tame little fraud, because a swordspeaker in hiding is a sword in a scabbard, and now the crown wants it drawn."

"I knew," he agreed pleasantly, "everything."

I pushed the scroll back across the table.

"No."

Just that. No speech, no defiance, no ringing declaration. I was too tired for theater, and besides — I'd spent my life learning that the truest words are the smallest ones.

The inquisitor regarded me for a long moment. Then he rolled the scroll closed with neat, unhurried fingers, and tucked it away, and stood, and the temperature of the entire cavern changed. The green-white fire of the garden, distant as it was, seemed to lean toward us. The silver lines in the floor began, very softly, to glow.

"That," he said, and all the warmth had drained out of his voice, leaving something with the texture of ledger-paper, "is a default."

Around the tiers of the court, figures were turning toward our table. Crowns of black iron. Chains worn like jewelry. Faces I couldn't read at any distance, all of them angled our way, all of them attentive, and beneath my feet the silver lines drew together into a single bright cord running from our table toward the garden, toward the burning rows, like a path being prepared for something to be walked along it whether it consented or not.

"Tamsin," Wren said, in her knife-voice. "Left archway. Now."

The inquisitor raised one hand — not commanding, merely *noting*, a clerk recording a delinquency — and spoke a word I felt in my teeth.

Every flame in the distant garden flared at once.

And down among the burning rows of oaths, something that had been lying still for twelve years sat up, and looked at me, and began — patiently, inevitably, the way debt always travels — to walk.
