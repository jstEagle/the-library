## 13. A Sword Speaks True

The inquisitor's name was Corvane. I learned it the way you learn the name of a disease — from the way everyone in the room flinched when he said it himself.

"High Inquisitor Aldous Corvane," he announced, to a court of sold souls who could not have cared less. He had come down into the buried court with six oath-sworn blades and a face like a man arriving at his own coronation. His boots were clean. That was the detail that frightened me most. He had walked through the mountain's dark and the mountain's dark had not dared touch him.

He stood at the far end of the Keeper's hall, beneath arches of pale stone that wept slow beads of light, and he looked at me the way a man looks at a horse he has already paid for.

"Swordspeaker Galloway," he said. "You've been difficult to find."

"You should try the charm stalls," I said. "We're everywhere."

Tamsin's elbow found my ribs. Wren, beside her, had gone the color of old paper, her fingers working the hem of her coat like she was trying to unpick a seam that held the world together. Behind us, the under-clerk who had taken Tamsin's memories was nowhere to be seen. Cowards, even here. Especially here.

Corvane smiled. It was a good smile. Practiced on crowds, no doubt. "I'm not here for the hedge-witch or the mapmaker. The crown has no quarrel with them." He let that hang in the air like smoke. "The crown has never had a quarrel with *you*, Paloma. You are crown property. Your sword is sworn. Your oath is registered in the garden below us as we speak, burning away nicely, keeping the lights on."

The garden. Somewhere beyond these halls, past galleries where kept things murmured in their cases, every promise ever sworn to the crown burned as fuel. Including mine. Including, I now knew, my mother's.

"I've read your file," Corvane went on, strolling forward as though this were his court and not the Keeper's. "Twelve years ago, a woman named Maren Galloway came to the crown's brokers with a problem: her son was dying of a fever that kills in three days, and she had nothing to trade but the one thing brokers want. She signed. The court took its payment. The crown took its cut. And you lived." He spread his hands. "Your mother saved your life, swordspeaker. The least you can do is honor the paperwork."

"You left out the part," I said, "where the crown broke the bargain. Where the peace she bought lasted nine years instead of ten because someone in a nice coat decided a decade of quiet wasn't worth what they'd promised her."

Something flickered across his face. Not guilt. Inquisitors don't keep guilt where it can be found. But interest — the interest of a man discovering a ledger error.

"That," he said, "is above both our stations."

"It's above yours. It was never above mine. It's been sitting on my soul this whole time."

He sighed, and for a moment the performance dropped, and what was underneath was worse: a tired man doing arithmetic. "Paloma. I am offering you a pardon. Full restoration. Walk out of here at my side, testify against the witch and the cartographer, and the hunt ends tomorrow. No more Ashrow Lane raids. No more neighbors dragged into wagons. You could reopen your little stall and sell your little lies until you die warm in your bed." He glanced at Tamsin. "They will be processed regardless. Their fates don't need to weigh on you."

*Processed.* As if Tamsin were wool. As if Wren were grain.

I looked at Tamsin. She looked back at me with the flat, steady gaze of a woman who had already given this court her sister's face and would give it nothing else. She shook her head once — small, almost invisible. *Don't.*

Wren didn't shake her head. Wren just said, very quietly, "There are children here, Corvane. Sold down before they could walk. Does your file mention them?"

"The court's inventory is the court's business."

"There it is," Wren said. "That's the whole crown, in one sentence."

Corvane ignored her, which was the first wise thing he'd done. He turned back to me, and his hand drifted to the hilt of his own sword — an oath-blade, I realized, same as mine, the metal carrying that faint wrongness of steel that has been made to listen.

"I hoped it wouldn't come to force," he said. "But I confess I prepared for it. Because I know something about oaths, swordspeaker, that you have spent twelve years pretending not to know." He drew a breath, and when he spoke again, his voice changed — dropped into the formal cadence, the liturgy-voice, the voice that makes stone sit up straight.

"*By the oath sworn in the year of the frost fair, by blood and by blade, by the word given over the anvil at Vell — Paloma Galloway, I invoke your bond.*"

The world went white at the edges.

*

You need to understand what an invoked oath feels like, from the inside. It isn't a suggestion. It isn't loyalty. It is a hand closing around the base of your skull and a voice saying *mine*, and every part of you that ever swore anything answering *yes* before the rest of you can object.

My sword came out of its sheath without my permission.

I felt my arm do it — felt the muscles fire, felt the familiar sweet hum of the gift rising up my wrist like water finding a channel. Twelve years I'd kept this thing corked, and now it poured out of me all at once, obedient, joyful, *hungry*. My sword spoke. Not words. Swordspeaker steel doesn't use words. It uses certainty. And the certainty pouring off my blade said: *the witch. The traitor. Cut her down.*

Tamsin saw it happen. Saw my eyes go somewhere else, saw the point swing toward her. To her credit — and Tamsin's credit is a deep well — she didn't run. She planted her feet and lifted the little bone-handled knife she'd carried since Ashrow Lane, knowing exactly how that story ended.

"Paloma," she said. Just that. My name, in her voice, the way she'd said it a hundred times over cards and bad tea.

The blade sang louder. *Cut her down.* And the worst part, the part I will carry longer than the scars, is that some animal corner of me wanted to obey. The oath felt *good*. It felt like coming home to a house I'd forgotten I loved. That's what they never tell you about chains — after enough years, the weight starts to feel like being held.

Corvane watched from ten paces, arms folded, patient as winter. "Resist if you like," he said. "It only hurts more. The oath always wins, swordspeaker. It has your name in it."

And there it was. The trap inside the trap. A swordspeaker's blade cannot be refused. Every text, every fireside story, every whispered lesson my mother beat into me before she died: *the sword speaks true, and the speaker speaks with it, and there is no third voice.* That was the whole architecture of the gift. That was why kings loved us and feared us in equal measure. We were honest weapons. We could not lie with our steel even if we lied with our lives.

Which meant there was exactly one move left on the board, and it was the move that ends every swordspeaker's story badly.

Because there *is* a third voice. There has always been a third voice. It's just that no one who used it lived long enough to write about it.

You speak against your own blade.

*

Here is what the old texts say happens. Here is what my mother told me, once, drunk on festival wine, in the only honest conversation of our complicated life: *"If you ever speak against the steel, lad, the oath will tear itself out of you like a hook from a fish's jaw. It won't kill you quick. Oaths don't waste what they're owed. They'll take the gift first, then the years, then whatever's left. No one does it twice."*

She'd laughed when she said it. I hadn't understood why until this moment, standing in a dead court beneath a dead mountain, my sword pointed at the only friend I had left.

*Tamsin,* I thought. *Wren. The children in the galleries. The souls in the cases. My mother's signature still burning in some garden bed like a candle nobody mourned.*

*Cut her down,* sang the blade.

"No," I said.

Small word. Four letters, one breath. I have written entire novels' worth of sentences in my head that meant less.

The blade shuddered. Actually shuddered, like a struck bell, and the hum up my arm turned to a snarl. Corvane's head tilted, curious. "What was that?"

"I said no." Louder now, and I reached — gods, it was like reaching into fire — I reached for the gift itself, the deep bright river that had been my secret and my shame since I was nine years old, and I did the unthinkable thing. I spoke *through* it, against its current, upstream, backwards. I put my whole self into the words the way I'd never dared put my whole self into anything:

"*I unswear. I refuse. Blade of mine, oath of theirs — I speak against you, and let the bond break where it breaks.*"

The sound that came out of my sword was not metal.

It was a scream. A long, high, human scream, the sound of twelve years of swallowed truth tearing loose all at once, and light burst up the length of the blade like a crack running through ice — white, then gold, then a filthy bruised red, the colors of the oath dying in reverse order of how they'd been sworn. My knees hit the stone. Something was leaving me. Many things were leaving me. I could feel them go, each one named: the sureness in my wrist. The hearing-that-heard-lies. The way steel had always, always answered me. Gone, gone, gone, ripped out root and branch, and underneath the loss was a pain so total it looped back around into silence, the way the loudest thunder goes deaf.

Somewhere very far away, Corvane was shouting. "*Impossible — the invocation holds, the invocation HOLDS—*"

It didn't hold.

My sword fell from my hand and rang on the stone, and the note it struck was just — a note. Metal on rock. Nothing more. For the first time since I was a child holding my mother's practice blade, the steel said nothing to me at all.

And the oath — the great registered, catalogued, garden-burning oath of Paloma Galloway — cracked.

I know it cracked because I heard it. Everyone heard it. Across the halls of the buried court, through galleries full of murmuring kept things, down in the burning garden itself, there came a sound like a glacier calving, like a cathedral beam giving way, and along the horizon of my mind where the oath had sat for twelve years — a smooth, hateful, familiar pressure I had stopped noticing the way you stop noticing a stone in your boot — there opened a fissure. Thin. Hairline. Leaking cold air.

But not broken. Not yet. Cracked oaths, I understood dimly, from somewhere outside my body, are worse than whole ones. A whole oath just sits there. A cracked one *pulls*.

"Tamsin," I managed. My voice sounded like someone else's, scraped hollow. "Run. Both of you. While he's — while it's—"

Corvane had stopped shouting. That was bad. He stood staring at my fallen sword with an expression I took a moment to identify, because I'd never seen it on an inquisitor's face before.

Fear.

Not of me. Of the arithmetic. If a swordspeaker could refuse the invocation — if the inviolable bond had a hairline fracture in it — then every oath in the garden was suddenly a question, and Corvane had built his whole life on the answers.

"What did you do?" he whispered.

"Nothing," I said, and started, painfully, to laugh, because it was true and it was the funniest thing I'd ever said. "That's rather the point."

Behind him, down the long gallery, the light of the distant garden flickered. Once. Twice. Like a lamp running low on oil.

Or like a fire deciding what to eat next.

Wren's hand closed on my collar and hauled, and Tamsin's shoulder went under my arm, and between them they got me upright and moving while Corvane stood frozen in the mathematics of his collapsing certainty. But as they dragged me toward the shadowed arches, I felt it — faint, insistent, pulling at the crack in me like tide on a sandbar.

The garden had noticed its broken candle.

And it was hungry for the rest.
