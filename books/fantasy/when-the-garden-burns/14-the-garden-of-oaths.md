## 14. The Garden of Oaths

Tamsin's bought knowledge came to her in fragments, the way a fever breaks in patches — one moment she was walking steady beside me, the next she'd stop mid-stride with her eyes gone distant and say something like *the third arch lies* or *count the pillars, not the doors*, and then she'd shake herself like a wet dog and keep moving.

She didn't tell us what each memory had cost. I didn't ask. Some prices you can see on a person's face, and hers was getting more expensive by the hour.

"The garden's real," she said, somewhere past the fourth turning. Her voice had a scraped quality, like a word used too many times. "The under-clerk wasn't lying about that. He said—" She stopped. Pressed two fingers to her temple as though holding a door shut. "He said it's the only thing down here that isn't sold. Everything else in this court is collateral. The garden is the *engine*."

"An engine of what?" Wren asked. She walked with her map held open against her forearm like a waiter's cloth, marking our route in ink that glowed faintly blue in the dark. "Heat? Light? They've got both."

"Of *keeping*," Tamsin said. And then, quieter: "That's all he gave me before the memory ran out. The rest I paid for separately."

I didn't want to know what *separately* meant. I was already carrying enough debts that weren't mine.

We moved through galleries that grew warmer as we descended — which was wrong, everything down here was wrong, heat should rise and this heat rose from below like breath from a sleeping mouth. The walls changed too. Upstairs, the buried passages had been rough-hewn, practical, the architecture of people digging. Here the stone was dressed and polished, veined with gold thread that caught Wren's lamp and threw it back in long slow pulses, like the mountain itself had a heartbeat and we were walking down its throat toward the heart.

"You feel that?" Wren said. She'd stopped marking her map. "It's louder down here. The pulse."

"It's not a pulse," I said, and my voice came out flatter than I intended. "It's an oath. Mine. It's been getting louder since we passed the second gate."

Both of them looked at me. I kept walking, because if I stopped I'd have to explain, and explaining meant admitting what I'd been feeling for the last hour: that the oath binding my sword to the crown had stopped being a quiet weight at the base of my skull and started being a *voice*. Not words. Something older than words. A rhythm, patient and enormous, like surf against a cliff that has never once lost.

*You swore. You swore. You swore.*

"I think," I said carefully, "that we're walking toward the thing my leash is tied to."

* * *

The last corridor opened without warning.

One moment we were squeezing through a seam in the rock barely wide enough for Wren's shoulders; the next, the stone simply gave up being a wall, and light poured over us — real light, golden and warm, the kind of light that makes you think of kitchens and harvest afternoons and every good thing you've ever been promised by someone who meant it.

I put my arm across my eyes. When I could see again, I understood why the Keeper's court had seemed beautiful and wrong in equal measure.

This was just beautiful. That was worse.

Terraces of white stone descended in curves ahead of us, and on every terrace grew roses. Not rose-colored. Rose-*shaped* — blooms the size of dinner plates, petals folded in whorls so perfect they looked machined, and every one of them burning. Not consumed by fire. Made of fire. Each blossom burned with a steady, low flame the color of candlelight through honey, and none of them diminished, and none of them spread, and the whole hillside of them threw off warmth and light and a sound —

A sound. That was the pulse. Thousands of small voices, layered so deep they'd become a hum, and underneath the hum, if you let yourself listen the way I'd spent twelve years teaching myself not to listen, you could pick out individual threads.

I picked one out. I wish I hadn't.

*"—so help me, so help me, so help me—"*

A woman's voice. Young. Frantic with love, the way only a frightened mother's voice is frantic.

"That's the engine," Tamsin whispered. She had her hand over her mouth. "Paloma. That's what he meant. Every oath ever sworn to the crown — they're all still burning. They never stop burning. That's what keeps this place lit. That's what keeps the Keeper *fed*."

Wren had gone very still beside me. Cartographer-still, the stillness of someone memorizing. But when she spoke, her voice wasn't professional at all. "How many?"

"How many what?"

"Oaths. How many oaths are burning right now?"

I looked out over the terraces. Row upon row upon row, descending into gold-lit distance, and beyond the visible gardens, more gardens, tiered away into the dark until the eye surrendered. Every soldier who'd ever sworn service. Every magistrate. Every witness in every courtroom. Every child in every schoolroom reciting the loyalty verse because the teacher said they had to. Every knight. Every swordspeaker.

Every mother bargaining with anything that would listen.

"I don't know," I said. "More than there are people alive. Oaths outlive the ones who swear them. That's rather the point of an oath."

"And they're all feeding *this*." Wren's jaw worked. "All those people upstairs, lighting lamps, paying taxes, tucking their kids in — and every promise they made to the crown comes down here and burns for the benefit of a court most of them don't know exists."

"Not don't know," I heard myself say. My voice sounded far away. "Were never allowed to know. There's a difference. The first is ignorance. The second is design."

My sword hand had gone cold. That was new. In twelve years of hiding, through inspections and witch-hunts and one memorable night when an inquisitor's hound had sniffed my stall for three full minutes, my gift had always sat quiet, banked like coals under ash. Now it was awake and clawing at the inside of my skin, and I realized with a lurch that it wasn't waking up on its own.

It was being *called*. The way a dog is called. The way a debt is called.

Because I was standing in front of the thing my oath fed, and the oath knew it, and whatever bound my blade to the crown ran down through the mountain's roots and ended here, in this garden, in one of these burning flowers — and I could feel which one. Third terrace. Left of center. A bloom slightly larger than its neighbors, burning slightly brighter, the way a rich man's house burns more candles than a poor man's.

"There," I said. I couldn't stop myself from pointing. "That one's mine."

* * *

We went down the terraces single file, and the closer we got, the more I could hear.

Not just my mother anymore — though her voice was everywhere now, woven through the hum like a gold thread through grey cloth, *so help me, so help me, keep him breathing, take what you need, so help me* — but others. A young man swearing fealty with his whole heart and no idea what hearts were used for down here. An old judge intoning the coronation oath for the ninth time, bored and reverent at once. Children. Gods help me, children, their small voices bright as struck flint, promising to be loyal to a crown they couldn't yet lift.

"They're not suffering," Tamsin said. She'd stopped again, and her face was wet. "That's the horrible part. Listen — they don't know. They're just... saying it. Forever. Saying it into the fire."

"Is that better or worse?" Wren asked.

"I don't know. I genuinely don't know."

I knew. I knew with the terrible clarity of a man standing in front of his own grave: it was worse. Pain would at least mean they were *here*, aware, able to hate what was happening to them. This was pain with the awareness scooped out. This was being used so thoroughly that even your resistance had been harvested along with everything else.

We reached the third terrace.

My flower stood in its plot of black soil, taller than the rest, and up close I could see what I couldn't see from above: the petals weren't uniform. They were layered, strata of flame, and each layer was a different voice. At the core, oldest and hottest, burned a woman's desperate whisper — my mother, twelve years younger, trading the only thing she had left. Around her, added year by year like rings in a tree, my own renewals: the oath at my knighting, the oath at my commissioning, the small daily affirmations a swordspeaker made without thinking, *on my honor, by my blade, so help me* — every one of them a petal, every petal a link in the chain, and the chain ran up through the mountain, through the crown, through the hunt that was even now flushing the gifted into the streets, and it all ended here, in fire, feeding *this*.

"She didn't sell me to a monster," I said. I was surprised how steady my voice came out. "She sold me to a *system*. To a machine that grinds promises into fuel. She thought she was making a bargain with the crown. She was making a bargain with the furnace the crown keeps in its cellar."

"Paloma—" Tamsin started.

"No. Let me finish. I need to say this while it's true." I crouched in front of the flower. Its heat washed over my face, gentle as a hearth. Somewhere in that fire, my soul turned on its spit. "Twelve years I built my life around hating her for it. Twelve years of *she chose the city over me, she chose her fear over my face*. And she did. That's real, and I'm not taking it back. But she wasn't the architect. She was a woman in a burning house, handing her child out a window to whoever reached up. And the person who reached up was wearing the crown, and nobody told her the crown eats what it catches."

The flower flickered. Just once. Just slightly — a stutter in the flame, like a candle in a draft.

And every other flower on the terrace flickered with it.

Wren saw it too. "Did you—"

"I felt it." I stood slowly. My gift was roaring now, fully awake, and it wasn't roaring at the flower. It was roaring at *me*, at the shape of a thought forming in the back of my mind with the terrible inevitability of dawn. "When I spoke truth near it. When I named what actually happened instead of the story I'd been telling myself. The whole garden *heard*."

"So the garden responds to truth," Tamsin said slowly.

"The garden is *made* of oaths. And an oath is just a promise wearing armor. Strip the armor off — say what's actually true underneath the words — and..." I trailed off, staring at the terraces below us, thousands of burning blooms, an entire civilization's worth of sworn words, all of it balanced on a foundation of stories people told themselves about duty and honor and love.

Stories that could be *corrected*.

The terror arrived first, cold and total. Because I understood what I was looking at, and I understood what it meant, and I understood that the thought forming in my head was not a thought a sane person should be having. If truth could make the garden flicker, then enough truth might make it do more than flicker. And if the garden failed, everything it powered failed — the court, the Keeper, the hunt upstairs, the leash on my own neck, all of it, all at once, like pulling a thread that turned out to be holding up the sky.

And I also understood — with the same cold clarity — that I had no idea whether the sky would land on the people upstairs or on the things below.

"We need to go," Wren said quietly. "Now. Before whatever you're thinking becomes whatever you're doing."

But I couldn't move. Because beneath the terror, beneath the horror, beneath the grief for my mother and the fury at the crown, something else had kindled — small and hot and unmistakable, the same feeling I'd gotten at fourteen when I first spoke to a blade and it answered:

Liberation.

For twelve years I'd thought my chains were the gift, the hiding, the lie of the charm stall. They weren't. They were *here*. All of them. Every chain I'd ever worn had been forged in this garden, and I was standing in the forge, and forges burn.

"Paloma." Tamsin's hand on my arm. "Your face is doing something frightening."

"My mother's voice is in that flower," I said. "So is mine. So is every oath anyone ever swore to the crown, and the crown has been spending them like coin to run a prison underneath its own kingdom." I turned to look at them, and I felt my own expression do the frightening thing again. "I don't know how yet. But I'm going to burn this garden down."

Above us, at the head of the terraces, a door I hadn't noticed opened — tall, black, silent — and the temperature of the garden dropped ten degrees in a heartbeat.

Someone was coming to see who was standing too close to the fire.

And from the look on Wren's face, and the way Tamsin's hand tightened on my arm, and the way my own oath suddenly went quiet as a held breath —

Whoever it was, they'd heard every word.
