## 15. What My Mother Sold

The garden did not want me to find her.

I understood that the way you understand a locked door — not by reasoning about it, but by pressing your hand against it and feeling the refusal in your palm. The rows of burning oaths stretched away into the dark in perfect order, each flame a different color, each one humming with somebody's promise, and between me and the center of the garden the paths kept rearranging themselves. Subtly. Politely. The way a host steers a guest away from the room where the family argues.

"Charming," I muttered. "I've done this trick myself. Behind a stall. With charms that do nothing."

The garden had better material to work with, though. My sleight of hand was flour and misdirection; this was memory, and memory is the one commodity I'd never learned to counterfeit.

I walked anyway. You learn that, growing up hiding something dangerous inside you: when a thing tries to steer you, walk straighter.

* * *

My mother's hands smelled of tallow and rosemary. That's the detail that comes back first, always, before her face, before her voice — the smell of her hands after a day of dipping candles for the chandler on Bellows Street, rosemary oil rubbed in to keep the wax from cracking her skin. She'd hold my face between those hands at night and make me recite the oath, and the smell of tallow and rosemary would settle over me like a second blanket.

*Swear it again, little wren.*

*I swear my sword speaks only for the crown.*

*Again.*

*I swear my sword speaks only for the crown.*

*Good boy.* And she would press her forehead to mine, and her eyes would be wet, and twelve years later I would stand in a garden of fire beneath a mountain and finally understand why.

I was seven when she made me swear it the first time. Seven years old, swearing an oath whose words I could barely shape, binding a gift I didn't know I had to a crown I couldn't point to on a map. Children will swear anything for a parent's approval. That's not innocence. That's just an unlocked door waiting for someone with a reason to walk through it.

I thought, for most of my life, that she'd been afraid of my gift. That the oath was a muzzle she fitted on me out of love — keep it leashed, keep it hidden, keep my swordspeaker son small and safe in a city that burns what it can't tax. It was a tidy story. It explained her tears. It explained the way she flinched when I practiced, the way she burned my practice-swords in the stove and told the neighbors my father had been a cooper.

Tidy stories are how survivors sleep at night. I should know. I sold them by the handful from a stall on Ashrow Lane.

* * *

The garden stopped pretending to redirect me somewhere around the two hundredth row of flames. Or maybe I stopped noticing. The heat here wasn't heat, exactly — it was attention. Every oath I passed felt me walking and leaned toward me like wheat toward sun, and I caught fragments as I went: *I swear to serve until my debt is paid, I swear my firstborn's service, I swear the truth of my ledger, I swear, I swear, I swear.* A thousand years of people promising things they couldn't take back, all of it burning steadily, quietly, feeding down through the roots of the mountain into whatever engine kept this court lit and lovely and wrong.

And there, at the center, where every path converged whether it wanted to or not: a flame the color of candlelight. Tallow-colored. Rosemary-oil warm.

"Oh," I said, to no one. "Oh, you absolute —"

I didn't finish. Some words don't survive contact with their target.

She stood up out of the flame the way smoke stands up out of a snuffed wick. Not my mother whole — I knew that immediately, the way you know a portrait from a person — but everything the garden had been fed of her. Every word she'd spoken into her oath, every thought she'd poured into the bargain, rendered here in fire and kept burning because the court never let a fuel go cold while it still had heat to give.

Her face was my face, older. Her hands were cupped around something invisible, the way she used to cup them around a match.

"Paloma," said the memory of my mother.

"Not her," I told myself, out loud, because I'm a liar by trade and I know when I'm about to sell myself something. "Not her. A receipt wearing her face."

"Is that what you think receipts look like?" she asked, and her mouth did the thing at the corner that meant she'd caught me in a half-truth, and my chest hurt so badly I nearly sat down in the ash.

* * *

Here is what I remembered on the walk down the mountain, in the dark, with Tamsin's hand on my elbow and Wren counting stairs under her breath: I remembered being nine, and feverish, and dying.

That's not melodrama. The sweating sickness came through Ashrow Lane the winter I was nine, and it took the cobbler's twin girls and old Master Ferrow and half the street besides, and it took me down to the place where the fever stops being heat and becomes a voice, and the voice says *you may put it down now, whenever you like, nobody would blame you.* I remember my mother sitting beside me for three days without sleeping. I remember her going out into the snow on the third night. I remember waking on the fourth morning alive, and her coming back with snow in her hair and a smile like a cracked plate, saying the fever had broken, saying God or luck or the rosemary tea had turned it, and me too sick and too relieved to notice that she wouldn't let me kiss her cheek, that she washed her hands before touching me, that her hands smelled of nothing at all.

Twelve years ago. The token named the date. The token named the seller. *Sold by Maren Galloway, of sound mind and desperate heart, one soul entire, male child, age nine, in exchange for —*

In exchange for what? I'd read that line forty times on the road down and the ink — bone doesn't hold ink, the words were more like grooves, like scars — the grooves went smooth right there. As if the price had been paid somewhere other than paper. As if the contract's other half lived in a different ledger entirely.

Now I stood in front of the flame that had been fed on my mother's oath for twelve years, and I asked it.

"What did you buy?"

* * *

"You were dying," she said. No preamble. No comfort. The memory gave me her the way memory actually works — skipping the soft parts, going straight to the load-bearing grief. "The fever had you. I'd tried everything. Hedge-remedies, church candles, the charlatan on Bellows Street who charged me a month's wages for boiled river water and a prayer to a saint who has never once answered anyone." Her hands tightened around the invisible match. "And then a man came to the door. Crown robes. Inquisitor's seal. He said the crown knew what I'd been hiding in my house since you were born. He said he could have us both on the pyre by morning."

"He offered you a trade instead."

"He offered me a trade." Her laugh was terrible. It was exactly her laugh. "He said the crown keeps accounts older than the city. He said there was a court beneath the mountain that bought souls, and the crown brokered such sales, and took its commission, and everyone went home happy except the soul. He said a child's soul, freely sold by a loving parent, was worth — " she stopped. Started again. "It was worth ten years, Paloma. Ten years of peace. The crown wanted a decade free of plague, free of famine, free of the border war bleeding the east counties white. One soul, ten years. He made it sound like arithmetic."

"And you did the sums."

"I did the sums." She looked at her hands. "You were nine. Ten years was a fortune. I'd have paid it for one more winter with you. I'd have paid it for one more *day*, and I knew that even then, and I hated myself for knowing it, because it meant I wasn't buying your life, I was buying mine. A life with you in it. There's a difference. I've had twelve years of fire to work out the difference."

The garden hummed around us. Somewhere far off, Tamsin and Wren were doing whatever brave foolish things brave foolish people do while I stood having the worst conversation of my life with a candle.

"The crown broke it," I said. "Didn't they. The ten years."

She was quiet for long enough that I almost repeated the question.

"The peace lasted six," she said. "Six years, and then the famine came anyway, and the border war started up again anyway, and last spring the witch-hunts began anyway. I went back to the man with the seal. I demanded the balance. Four years owed, Paloma. Four years of peace still outstanding on a contract signed in my blood and your name." Her voice did something I'd never heard it do — it went flat, the way voices go when the feeling underneath is too large to carry up intact. "He laughed at me. He said the crown's obligations to the court below were *spiritual*. He said the ten years had been delivered in the currency heaven values. He said — "

She stopped.

"Say it," I said. "I've come a very long way to be lied to by professionals. At least be honest at the end."

"He said the crown had already spent the four remaining years," she said. "*Invested* them. In the hunt. In the inquisition. In keeping the city afraid enough to stay obedient." Her eyes came up and found mine, and they were fire, actual fire, and behind the fire was my mother, tired past the end of tiredness. "They took your soul, and they took the payment, and then they spent the payment making the world cruel enough that a mother selling her son's soul seemed reasonable. That's the machine, Paloma. That's what feeds this garden. Not evil. Arithmetic. Fear doing sums."

* * *

I want to tell you I raged. That I cursed her, or forgave her, that I did something clean and dramatic worthy of the ballads they'll probably get wrong about all this.

What I did was sit down in the ash, because my legs quit, and laugh. Ugly laughter. The kind that scares dogs.

Ten years of peace for one soul. Six delivered, four stolen. My mother had been robbed by the same institution that owned the deed to me, and the robbery had been dressed up as piety, and she had died — three winters ago, coughing, in a room I paid for with counterfeit charms — believing herself damned for a bargain that the other side had defaulted on before her body was cold.

"They'll tell you forgiveness is yours to give," she said. She crouched in front of me, this woman made of everything my mother had sworn, and her hands hovered near my face without landing, because memories can't touch and neither, anymore, could she. "It isn't. Forgiveness is a debt, same as any other, and I'm the one who owes it. What you give me — if you give me anything — is something else. Something with no ledger under it."

"I know what it's called," I said.

"Do you?"

"It's called understanding that you were cornered by monsters who taught you the price of the door." I wiped my face. My sleeve came away gritty with ash. "It's called hating what you did and knowing I'd have done worse. It's called — " and here my voice failed me, the way it does, the way it always does, on the truest sentence available. I tried again. "You saved my life with the only coin you had. They're the ones who set the exchange rate. I forgive *them* nothing. But you —"

"Don't," she whispered.

"— you, I'm carrying home," I finished. "Whatever's left of you when this place burns. Whatever the garden hasn't eaten. You come up with me, Ma. Out of the ledger. Into the light. Somebody's going to plant a real garden over the stair down to this hellhole someday, and you're going to be in the soil of it, and nobody is ever going to burn you for fuel again."

She made a sound I'd heard once before — the night my fever broke, muffled through the wall, the sound of a woman alone in a kitchen letting herself feel the thing she'd been holding off for three days. Then the flame steadied, and she was memory again, composed, and the garden's attention shifted around us like a crowd sensing trouble.

"You shouldn't have come to the center," she said. "The Keeper knows you're here now. And the crown's man is close — closer than you think. He's been below since before you crossed the threshold. The crown doesn't send hunters after strays, Paloma. It sends them to collect on investments."

"I know. We've met his work."

"No." Her image flickered, guttered, the way a candle gutters when a door opens somewhere in the house. "You don't understand. Your oath — the one I fed into this garden when you were seven — it isn't just a leash. It's a *key*. The inquisitor doesn't need to find you. He needs only to speak the words of your own vow aloud, and your sword will answer him through you. Through the garden. Everything sworn here is connected here." Her fire was thinning, stretching, being pulled back into the row of flames behind her. "Paloma. When he comes — and he is coming — you cannot let him finish the invocation. Whatever it costs. Whatever you have to say against your own blade. Because if he turns your sword —"

The flame snapped sideways, and she was gone, and the garden exhaled a breath of hot ash that smelled, for one impossible moment, of tallow and rosemary.

* * *

I got up. My legs worked, mostly. Somewhere behind me, down the black throat of the path, I could hear running footsteps — Tamsin's, I'd have bet the shop on it, with Wren's uneven stride behind — and ahead, deeper in, a light that wasn't fire was approaching with the patient, well-lamped confidence of a man who has never once been lost.

My mother had sold my soul to buy a decade of peace, and the crown had pocketed the change and spent it on chains.

Fine. I'd spent twelve years selling fake magic to people who needed real hope. I knew exactly what a thing was worth versus what it cost.

And if the inquisitor wanted to invoke my oath — well. He was about to learn what happens when a swordspeaker answers his own blade.

I turned toward the footsteps and the light, and I drew my sword, and for the first time since I was seven years old, I spoke to it first.
