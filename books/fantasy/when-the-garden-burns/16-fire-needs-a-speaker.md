## 16. Fire Needs a Speaker

I have made maps of things that do not exist. I have made maps of things that exist but shouldn't — the drowned quarter under the harbor, the king's second treasury, the stair in my grandmother's cellar that went down farther than any cellar has a right to go. I once drew a map of a dream I had three times, and sold it to a merchant who swore it saved his caravan, so perhaps even my lies have a way of becoming true.

But I had never mapped an escape from a place that was actively trying to keep me until now.

The under-clerk's information sat in Tamsin's head like a coal wrapped in linen — she wouldn't say what it cost her, and I knew better than to ask, because I knew what the under-clerks took as payment, and I knew what Tamsin carried that anyone would want. She'd gone pale in that particular way people go pale when they've traded something they can't get back and are pretending otherwise. I recognized it because I'd seen it in mirrors.

So: the garden's heart. A burning bed of oaths at the center of the buried court, every vow ever sworn to the crown fed into it like kindling, and the Keeper sitting beside it like a man warming his hands at a fire he'd been tending since before fire had a name. That was where Paloma's soul was kept. That was what we'd come to steal.

The problem — there is always a problem; if there isn't one, you haven't looked closely enough — was that knowing where a thing is and knowing how to take it are two different maps entirely.

* * *

We holed up in what the court called a guesting gallery, which meant a hollow carved out of the mountain and furnished with the memory of comfort. Benches that looked soft and weren't. Lanterns that burned without oil, giving light the color of old honey. Everything down here was like that — almost right, right-adjacent, a court built by things that had studied comfort the way a crow studies a scarecrow.

Paloma sat on the not-soft bench with the debt-token in his palm, turning it over and over. Bone, worn smooth, with his name cut into it in a hand I recognized, because I'd spent my childhood reading the ledgers this place wrote its thefts in. He'd been turning it for an hour. If he turned it much longer it was going to wear through to the truth underneath, which was that no amount of turning would change what it said.

Tamsin sat across from him, watching, her healer's hands folded in her lap like she was waiting for a wound to present itself. Maybe she was. Wounds were the only currency down here that couldn't be counterfeited.

"So," I said, unrolling my papers on the floor between us. "Let's talk about arson."

Paloma looked up. Tamsin did too. There's something about a cartographer announcing arson that focuses a room.

"The under-clerk told you the heart of the garden is unguarded," I said. "That's true the way most things said by clerks are true — technically, and only if you don't ask follow-up questions. It's unguarded because it doesn't need guarding. You can't cut it, you can't carry it, you can't smother it. Fire that runs on oaths doesn't go out for lack of air. It goes out when the oaths stop being true." I tapped the paper. "And nobody down here stops swearing. That's the whole engine. The crown swears fealty, the court swears silence, the sold swear to wait, the sold-from swear to forget. Every oath feeds it. It's the most reliable fire in the world."

"Then how do we put it out?" Tamsin asked.

"We don't put it out." I let that sit. "We make it eat itself."

Paloma set the token down. Slowly, the way you set down something that might be listening. "Explain."

"You're a swordspeaker." I said it plainly because dancing around it was wasting time we didn't have, and because I'd watched him flinch from the word for six days now and he needed to stop. "Your gift isn't swinging a sword. Anyone can swing a sword. Your gift is that oaths obey you. When you speak, promises hold their shape or break their shape. Your whole life, you've used it to keep your own oath — binding your blade to the crown, keeping the chain quiet. Yes?"

He didn't answer, but his jaw did something that qualified as an answer.

"An oath sworn by a swordspeaker isn't just words," I went on. "It's fuel of a higher grade. The garden burns common vows the way a house burns straw. But a swordspeaker's oath? Sworn inside the garden, spoken into the fire itself?" I spread my hands. "That's pitch. That's the fat at the bottom of the candle. And here's the beautiful part, the part that makes me think the universe occasionally has a sense of humor—"

"Don't," Tamsin said, but she was almost smiling.

"—an oath only burns clean if it's *kept*," I finished. "A broken oath is ash before it hits the flame. So you can't just walk in and swear a lie. The garden will taste it and spit it out. You have to swear true. Whole heart, whole soul — well. Whatever's left of yours. You have to mean it."

"And then?" Paloma asked. His voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet before he did something reckless. I'd known him four days and I already had his tells catalogued. Cartographer's habit. People are just terrain that walks.

"And then you renounce it," I said. "Same breath, if you can manage it. You swear, and then you unsay. The oath enters the fire true and dies false, and the fire can't tell which half of it to believe, and—" I mimed a small explosion with my fingers, which I will admit lacked gravitas, "—the whole garden hiccups. Badly. Permanently, if we're lucky. Oaths are chained to each other down here, all feeding the same root. You light one wrong and the wrong spreads."

Silence. The honey-colored lanterns buzzed faintly, like they were eavesdropping, which for all I knew they were.

"That's suicide," Tamsin said flatly.

"It's *arson*," I corrected. "Suicide is incidental."

"Renouncing a swordspeaker's oath while standing in a garden made of oaths," Tamsin pressed, leaning forward, "while the Keeper watches, while hunters are already sweeping the galleries looking for us — what does that do to *him*? To his gift? To whatever's left holding him together?"

I looked at Paloma. He was looking at the token again.

"I don't know," I said. Honest, at least. "Nobody's ever tried it. That's either a terrible sign or a wonderful one. All my maps end somewhere unmapped. This one ends in fire."

* * *

I worked through the night, or what passed for night down below — the lanterns dimmed on a schedule the court pretended was natural, and everyone politely ignored that the schedule was chosen by someone. I laid out the route on the floor of the gallery in chalk and charcoal and, when I ran out of those, crushed brick from a wall I chipped at when I thought the gallery wasn't paying attention.

Here is what I knew, assembled from scraps, which is the only way anything worth knowing is ever assembled:

One. The garden lay at the heart of the court, past the Hall of Kept Things, past the long gallery where the sold souls stood in niches like candles in a shrine. My map of the public ways was good. I'd drawn half of it from memory before I could write properly, back when mapping home was the only game a sold child could play alone.

Two. The under-clerk had sold Tamsin the location of the garden's heart, but not the way to it — the direct approaches were warded against exactly the kind of theft we intended. Which meant we needed the old ways. The service corridors. The veins of the mountain, dug before the court grew beautiful, when it was just hungry. I knew some of them. Not enough. The rest I was reconstructing from the logic of the diggers — and diggers, whether they're digging mines or tombs or escape routes, always follow the same grammar. Water finds water. Shaft meets shaft. Nobody digs a corridor that dead-ends without leaving a door, because nobody who digs wants to be entombed with their own work.

Three. The hunters were closing. We'd heard them — the court's own, pale and courteous, walking the galleries in pairs, asking questions in voices like warm milk. They weren't hunting us the way surface hunters hunt, with dogs and noise. They hunted the way this whole place hunted: slowly, patiently, by making the walls feel smaller every hour. By morning — court-morning — they'd have narrowed us to two or three possible galleries, and by the following morning they'd have narrowed us to one, and after that there would be a knock at our door and a smile and the end of everything.

Which meant we moved tomorrow. No, I decided, drawing the line that mattered most: we moved at dimming, when the court's attention pooled around the Keeper like moths. Tomorrow at dimming, down the vein, to the garden.

Four — and this was the scrap I kept turning over, the splinter in my thumb —

I looked at Paloma. He'd finally stopped handling the token. He was asleep, or nearly, sitting upright with his sword across his knees, because even exhausted, even hunted, he slept like a man guarding a door. Twelve years of that. Twelve years of an oath he didn't know was a leash, worn so long it had grown into the bone.

He was going to have to swear a new one and kill it in the same breath, and none of us knew what that would leave behind.

* * *

Tamsin found me at the far end of the gallery, where I'd pinned my working sheets to the stone with slivers of brick. She crouched next to the largest one — the vein route, all black lines and question marks.

"This section here," she said, touching a stretch of corridor I'd marked with a jagged stroke. "You've redrawn it four times."

"Diggers' logic says it should connect to the gallery above the Hall of Kept Things. My memory says it might not exist anymore. Courts renovate. They paper over the ugly parts." I shrugged. "If it's sealed, we lose an hour finding another way, and an hour is the difference between escape and exhibit."

"What's your confidence?"

"Fifty-five percent," I said. "Cartographers deal in honest numbers. It's why nobody invites us to parties."

She laughed — short, surprised at herself, quickly swallowed. Then she sat down on the cold floor next to me, which no one had done in longer than I wanted to calculate.

"You were born here," she said. Not a question. Paloma had told her; of course he had. He was constitutionally incapable of keeping a secret from someone who'd bled for him.

"I was born here and sold upward at seven," I said. "Standard arrangement. The court breeds a few of us true-born — children with both feet in the world, no debts attached — and sells them to families on the surface who want a child and can pay. It's a courtesy. A trade mission." I kept my eyes on the map. "My mother got a daughter. The court got a line of credit. I got out."

"Do you remember it? Before?"

"Everything," I said. "That's the trick of this place. It makes sure the sold remember, so the longing stays sharp, so the sold-from stay reachable. Memory is collateral here." I tapped the vein-route. "I've been drawing my way home since I was six. Every map I've ever made — the harbor, the treasury, the stupid dream-map — every one of them was practice. For this one." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Twenty years of professional misdirection, and it turns out I was just a homesick child drawing the same picture over and over."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. Then: "There are other children. Sold ones. You said."

"There are always other children. That's the business model."

"And your plan gets us out. Three of us. Four counting the sword."

"My plan gets us to the garden," I said. "What happens after the garden is Paloma's plan. What happens after *that*—" I gestured at the jagged stroke, the fifty-five percent corridor, all the honest question marks bleeding off the edge of the paper, "—is improvisation with excellent documentation."

She nodded slowly. Then she said, in the tone of someone laying cards face-up: "When we run, I'm not leaving without them either. The sold. Whoever can walk."

I looked at her. She was thinking of her sister — the dead sister whose memories she'd been spending down here like a woman spending a dead man's coins, and I will tell you what I understood then, sitting on the cold floor of a court that ate oaths: grief doesn't shrink when you spend it. It changes shape. Hers had become a door held open, and she was going to hold it open for strangers, because the alternative was letting it close on nothing.

"Then we'd better make the corridor wider," I said.

* * *

Paloma woke at dimming-minus-two — I'd started measuring time that way, court-time inverted, a small private rebellion — and came to stand over my maps with the token in his fist and his jaw set in the configuration that preceded recklessness.

"Walk me through it," he said.

I walked him through it. The vein route, the fifty-five percent corridor, the gallery above the Hall of Kept Things, the drop into the garden's rim. Where the hunters would be, which was everywhere we'd been. Where the Keeper would be, which was where the Keeper always was: beside the fire, tending it, patient as arithmetic.

"And then I swear," Paloma said.

"You swear. Something true. Something that costs. The garden has to swallow it whole before it knows what you're doing — the moment it realizes the oath comes with a renunciation attached, it'll reject the whole mouthful. So you speak it like you mean to keep it forever." I met his eyes. "Can you do that? Mean it and murder it in the same breath?"

He was silent for a long moment. Down the gallery, Tamsin pretended to check her kit and listened with her entire body.

"I swore an oath to my mother when I was nine," he said finally. "That I'd survive. That I'd live no matter what it cost anyone else. She made me swear it while she was dying, and I've kept it for twenty years, and it turns out she bought that survival with my soul before I ever opened my mouth." His hand closed around the token. "So yes. I know how to mean an oath. I've had practice meaning oaths that were knives."

"That's the spirit," I said, because someone had to be wry about it or we'd all lie down.

"And if it works," he said. "If the garden burns. What happens to the oaths it's been feeding on? The ones still — live. On the surface."

I'd thought about this. I'd thought about it more than I'd admitted, lying awake during the fake night, staring at the honey-colored dark.

"They break," I said. "Every oath sworn to the crown, every vow the court holds — they come undone. Soldiers wake up from fealty like waking from a fever. Hunters forget what they were hunting. Debts dissolve. The whole architecture of 'must' comes down at once." I paused. "Including mine."

Tamsin looked up sharply. Paloma frowned. "Yours?"

"I was sold," I said. "Sold means contracted. Contracted means oath-bound — the sale itself is a vow, mine, sworn when I was seven, that I belonged to the buyers and would never return home. When the garden burns, that breaks too." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like wearing someone else's hat. "I'll be free. Actually free. First time since I had teeth."

"You don't sound happy about it," Tamsin said.

"I sound like a map with the edges torn off," I said. "Ask me again after."

Because here was the scrap I hadn't shown them, the splinter I'd been turning all night: freedom, real freedom, was a blank space on the page. And I had spent my whole life filling blank spaces. I genuinely did not know what I was if I wasn't drawing my way back to a home I'd been stolen from. When the garden burned, I'd have to find out. Maps end somewhere unmapped. I'd written that myself, smug as anything, and now the unmapped country was me.

* * *

The hunters found the edge of our scent an hour before we moved.

We heard them the way you hear weather — a change in pressure, a hush in the galleries where there had been murmur. Two of the pale courteous things stood at the mouth of the vein corridor we'd scouted, conversing in warm-milk voices about inventory, and the word "inventory" had a weight to it that made my skin crawl. We were inventory. We had always been inventory; the court had simply misplaced us temporarily.

I redrew the route on the spot, chalk moving fast, cutting the fifty-five percent corridor and replacing it with a shaft I liked even less — narrower, older, stinking faintly of the deep water that ran beneath the court. Forty percent. Maybe forty-five. I am an honest woman; I wrote the number down.

"No," Paloma said, reading over my shoulder.

"It's the only way that doesn't walk us past them."

"It's a throat," Tamsin said, peering at it. "You're proposing we crawl down a throat."

"I'm proposing we crawl down a throat *quietly*," I said. "The alternative is walking down a hallway loudly, into a pair of gentlemen who collect people. Choose your anatomy."

They chose the throat. Good soldiers, both of them. Paloma rolled his maps — my maps, our maps — and tucked them inside his coat next to the token, and Tamsin checked her kit with the economy of someone who'd packed for emergencies her whole life, and I took one last look at the gallery that had briefly been ours.

On the wall, in chalk, small, where only someone crawling past at eye-level would see it, I drew my mark. The little wren, wing raised. I'd left it on a hundred walls across two countries — a signature, a joke, a claim: *I was here, I saw this, I made it onto the page.* But this was the first wall I'd ever marked that could be burned down around the mark.

"Ready?" Paloma asked.

"No," I said honestly. "But ready isn't a place, it's a direction. Come on."

We went down the throat of the mountain, three hearts and one borrowed sword, toward a garden that burned on everything people had promised and never meant to keep. Behind us, the hunters turned from their conversation, slow as weather, and began to walk.

Ahead of us, forty-five percent chance, lay the fire that was going to eat the world's oldest lie.

I'd drawn the map. Now all we had to do was survive the territory — and give Paloma Galloway one true sentence to swear, and one breath to take it back.
