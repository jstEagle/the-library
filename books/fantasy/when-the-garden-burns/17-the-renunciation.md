## 17. The Renunciation

There is a particular quality to the silence before you do something unforgivable. I had heard it once before — the moment before my mother pressed a bone token into a stranger's hand and sold me, though I didn't know it then, though I was too small to understand that the hush in our kitchen was the sound of a ledger opening somewhere beneath the world. I heard it again now, in the burning garden of oaths, with the Keeper's courteous face turned toward me and the inquisitor's hand resting on his sword like a man who has already decided how this ends.

The garden burned around us. That was the horror of it, the thing I kept having to re-learn every few heartbeats: it was *supposed* to burn. Every oath ever sworn to the crown hung here as a flame — ten thousand small fires, each one a promise someone had made and could never take back. My mother's oath. My oath. The oath of the baker on Cordwainer Street who swore his firstborn's loyalty for a license. The oath of Tamsin's grandmother, probably, sworn for nothing more than permission to gather herbs off crown land without being called a witch for it. All of it fuel. All of it feeding the court beneath the mountain, which fed the crown above, which hunted people like me through streets I'd grown up in, and none of them — not one soul up there shivering by their stoves — knew what their promises were warming.

"You understand what is asked of you," the Keeper said. It wasn't quite a question. The Keeper did not ask questions; it made statements and waited for you to agree, the way a trap makes a statement of its jaws.

"I understand," I said. "You want me to swear an oath. A true one. Freely given."

"Freely given," the Keeper repeated, savoring the words the way a miser savors coin. "That is the price of fire, swordspeaker. An oath freely given burns brightest. And then—" its voice curled, almost gentle, almost kind, the way kindness sounds when it comes from something that has eaten ten thousand years of other people's promises— "and then you will break it. Renounce it. Unmake with your own mouth what your own mouth made. No one can do that for you. No one can force it. Forced oaths are ash already; we have plenty of ash. We need flame."

"And when I break it," I said, "the garden takes it personally."

The Keeper smiled. I want to be clear that it was a beautiful smile, in the way a landslide is beautiful from a safe distance. "The garden will take everything. Personally."

* * *

Let me tell you what it costs to stand in that place and mean what you're about to say.

Tamsin stood at my left shoulder. She had spent her sister's memories to buy us the way here — three memories, the under-clerk had told her afterward, three and she'd haggled him down from five, which meant she'd walked into that office with grief in her purse and haggled anyway, because Tamsin haggles with death itself if death quotes her a bad price. She didn't know yet exactly which memories she'd lost. She knew she couldn't remember the smell of her sister's hair. She'd told me that in the dark corridor outside the garden, very quietly, the way you tell someone a wound when you're still deciding whether it hurts.

Wren stood behind me, maps rolled tight in her fists like weapons. Her maps ended here — she'd said so, said it like a confession, said it like a woman laying down a tool she'd carried since childhood. But Wren had also found us the last passage, the one even her stolen childhood hadn't remembered, because she'd drawn it fresh, from watching the court's own hunters move. Born here. Sold upward. And she'd come back down anyway, for children she'd never met, because some inheritances are wounds and some wounds you choose to carry.

And across the burning rows, in armor polished so bright it threw back the oath-fire in shards, stood Inquisitor Halvard Vane. Chief huntsman of the crown. The man who had taken Tamsin off Ashrow Lane in irons, who had offered me pardon for betraying my friends not six hours past, who had watched me refuse with the mild disappointment of a man watching weather turn. He had come below because the crown had always known the court existed. Had always traded with it. His warrant, his authority, his whole shining career — all of it warmed by this same fire, drawn from this same ledger of souls.

He thought he was here to stop me.

Here is what he had not understood, though I told him plainly, though the truth of it sat in the air between us like a fishmonger's catch gone off in the sun: you cannot stop a swordspeaker from speaking. You can kill me. You can bind my sword — he'd done that, invoked my oath, forced my blade to turn against Tamsin, and I had spoken against my own steel, the one act forbidden to my kind, and the oath had cracked like river ice in spring. But you cannot stop the words. The gift is not the sword. The sword is just where the words go to become real.

"The crown offers clemency one final time," Vane said. His voice carried beautifully in the garden's acoustics; I suspected the court had designed it that way, so that bargains struck here would echo forever. "Kneel. Swear renewed fealty. Walk out of this mountain with your gift intact and your companions' fates reduced to a line in a ledger no one reads."

"My companions are standing right here," I said. "They can hear you reducing them."

"They are irrelevant."

"There it is." I turned to Tamsin. "Did you hear? Irrelevant. Twelve years I hid my gift from men like this, and the whole time they were going to say it out loud eventually. Irrelevant. That's what the oath was for, Tam. Not protection. Inventory."

She snorted. Even now, even here, Tamsin snorted at the crown. I have loved few things in this life as steadily as I love that snort.

* * *

I want to describe the garden properly, because I don't think I'll have the gift much longer and someone should remember it accurately.

Imagine an orchard planted underground, in cavernous dark, and imagine that instead of trees there are flames — but flames shaped like living wood, like hedges, like rosebushes with thorns of light. Each plant is an oath. The old ones have grown tall and gnarled, rooted deep in the stone: coronation oaths, war oaths, the oath of some king four hundred years dead swearing to protect a harbor that no longer exists. The new ones are saplings, thin and trembling — a soldier's enlistment vow, a magistrate's promise of justice, my mother's desperate bargain, twelve years old, barely taller than my knee and burning green at the tip.

And mine. Mine stood near the center, transplanted there the day I took up my sword and spoke the words they put in my mouth. *I swear my blade to the crown, my voice to its service, my life between its enemies and its body.* I had been nineteen. I had thought I was becoming something. I had actually been becoming fuel.

Somewhere among the kept things — the shelves, the reliquaries, the glass cases where the court stored what it had purchased — my soul sat in its little vessel, doing whatever souls do when they're inventory. I had seen it an hour ago. It looked like a candle flame that had learned to be homesick.

The Keeper drifted ahead of me through the rows, robes trailing sparks that did not fall so much as *wait*. "Choose your words carefully," it said. "A weak oath burns briefly. We require a conflagration."

"Oh, I intend to over-deliver," I said.

"That is not reassuring."

"It isn't meant to be."

Vane moved to flank me, and I felt his purpose the way you feel weather change — the oath in my cracked-and-mending sword pulling toward him like iron filings toward a magnet. He still held the leash. My oath to the crown was broken but not dead; a cracked oath leaks, and a leaking oath can be *poured*, and he meant to pour what remained of my binding into one final command: *strike down your friends.*

So. Sequence mattered. If I swore the new oath while the old leash still tugged, the Keeper might claim both — might drink my renunciation and my binding together and call it a fair trade, might keep the garden alive on my wreckage. If I broke the old oath first, openly, before witnesses, then whatever I swore next would be *mine*, unencumbered, freely given — the only currency the court truly honored, the only fire hot enough to do what needed doing.

Which meant I had to renounce the crown first. Here. Now. In front of its chief inquisitor. While standing inside the engine room of its power.

I have made harder choices. I cannot, at this moment, name one.

* * *

"Inquisitor," I said. "Before I swear anything, I'm going to settle an account."

His eyes narrowed. Good. Let him narrow them. Let him spend his attention wondering what trick I had, because the truth was worse than any trick: I had no trick. I had only the thing they had spent twelve years trying to hunt out of existence, the gift itself, the plain unhindered word.

I drew my sword.

The blade came up singing — it always sang, that was the curse and the glory of it, a swordspeaker's steel remembers every promise spoken through it — and the song came out wrong, cracked, the note of ice giving way. The remnant of my oath strained along the edge, Vane's will hauling on it, whispering *turn, turn, strike the hedge-witch down.* I felt my wrist try to obey. God help me, I felt my own arm begin to curve toward Tamsin, and I understood in that instant why the crown bred swordspeakers like hunting hawks: not for the gift, but for exactly this. For the moment a leash matters more than a person.

"No," I said.

Not to Vane. To the sword. To the oath. To the nineteen-year-old who'd knelt on cold flagstones and believed a promise was a form of safety.

*I, Paloma Galloway, last oath-bound swordspeaker of the crown of Ashrow, speak against my own blade. I unsay the words. I unbind the steel. What was sworn in fear I renounce in freedom. The crown has no further claim on my voice, my sword, or my life. Let the oath be broken, and let the breaking be witnessed by fire.*

The words went through the blade like lightning through a mast.

Every swordspeaker knows the law: we may speak *through* the sword, never *against* it. Speaking against your own steel unmakes the gift — that was the doctrine, the terror, the reason I'd hidden for twelve years. They taught us it was death. They taught us wrong, or rather, they taught us a half-truth the way liars teach arithmetic. Speaking against your own blade doesn't kill you. It kills the *oath*. It kills the lie the oath was wrapped in. What survives the burning is whatever you actually are, standing in the ashes with your hands empty and your name suddenly, terrifyingly, your own.

My sword screamed. There is no dignified way to put this; the steel shrieked like a scalded cat, and the crack that had run through it since the garden corridor split wide, and the remnant of the crown's leash — the poured-out dregs of my binding — tore free and streamed upward as smoke, and the smoke went into the nearest oath-flame, my own sapling, and my sapling *roared*.

Vane lunged.

Of course he lunged. A man whose entire selfhood is authority does not watch authority burn; he tries to arrest it. His blade met mine and found — this is the part I'll treasure into whatever ordinary life waits for me above — found nothing to grip. No oath to seize. No binding to pour. Just steel meeting steel, skill meeting skill, one tired shopkeeper who'd kept his edge sharp in secret against one polished instrument of a rotting crown. Our blades rang twice, three times, and on the third ring his sword spoke — *his* oath, his binding, the crown's voice riding his arm — and mine did not answer, because mine was free, and a free sword fights better than a leashed one. Ask any hawk.

I broke his guard and laid my flat against his breastplate, hard enough to sit him down hard on the ash-strewn path, and I stood over him breathing like a bellows and laughing, actually laughing, because twelve years of dread had just ended in two exchanges and the dread had been the heavy part, not the fighting. The fighting was almost a relief.

"You're finished," Vane said from the ground, and there was something almost pitiable in how certain he sounded. "The court will not permit this. The Keeper keeps what is sold. Your soul is still in that case, Galloway. You've gained nothing."

"Not yet," I agreed. "Watch."

* * *

I turned to the Keeper, and the garden turned with me — I swear the flames leaned, the way flowers lean at sun, except the sun was me and the leaning was hunger.

"Now," I said. "The trade. As discussed. One oath, freely given, and then renounced. You get your conflagration. I get my soul out of its case before the fire reaches it. Agreed?"

The Keeper's smile widened by a degree I chose not to measure. "Agreed. Speak."

So I did. And I want this recorded precisely, because the Keeper would record it precisely, in its dying ledger, and ledgers should balance.

I knelt — not to the crown, not to the court, not to the Keeper. I knelt to the garden itself, to the ten thousand small betrayed promises burning around me, and I placed my palm flat on the warm stone, and I swore:

*I, Paloma Galloway, of my own free will, with no coercion and no fear, swear a new oath: that I will protect this court beneath the mountain, that I will serve its Keeper faithfully, that my sword and my voice belong to this place and its keeping, now and for as long as I live.*

The garden drank it in. I felt the oath leave me the way blood leaves a wound — a warmth, a pull, a strange lightness, and then the nearest flames flared emerald-bright, fed, delighted. New growth. Fresh fuel. Somewhere in the dark, the court's roots thickened.

The Keeper made a sound I had not heard from it before: pleasure. Pure, unguarded, ancient pleasure. "Freely given," it murmured. "Oh, well given. And now, swordspeaker. The second half. Renounce."

"Yes," I said. "Now."

And I stood up, and I looked it in its courteous face, and I said:

*I, Paloma Galloway, renounce the oath I swore one breath ago. It was freely given and it is freely broken. I owe this court nothing. I never owed this court anything. Everything it holds, it holds by theft dressed as trade. Let every oath in this garden hear me: a promise made to a thief is not a promise. It is a chain. And chains burn.*

Then I spoke the words through my sword one final time — the last speech my gift would ever make, though I didn't let myself think that yet, couldn't afford to, grief is a luxury for survivors — and I pointed the blade not at the Keeper, not at Vane, but at the heart of the garden, at the oldest tree, the coronation-oak of some king four centuries dead, the first lie this place was built on.

"*Burn,*" I said.

And the garden obeyed me.

* * *

You have never seen fire until you have seen an oath take offense.

It did not spread the way fire spreads. It *remembered*. Every flame in that garden suddenly recalled what had been done to it — every soldier's vow spent on a butcher's war, every magistrate's justice sold by the pound, every mother's bargain broken by the crown that brokered it — and the remembering was the spreading. The fire went from row to row like a rumor through a market crowd, and each oath it touched flared white, then gold, then a color I don't have a name for, a color like freedom looks if freedom were briefly visible, and the heat of it didn't burn so much as *unmake*.

The Keeper's composure lasted four full seconds. I counted. Four seconds of perfect courtesy, and then the most ancient thing in the mountain made a noise like a library falling downstairs.

"This is not the bargain—" it began.

"It is exactly the bargain," I said. "You wanted a conflagration. Read your own terms, Keeper. You should have hired a lawyer."

Behind me, Tamsin was already moving — of course she was, Tamsin moves *toward* trouble the way water moves downhill, it's not bravery, it's hydrology — wrenching open the reliquary cases, shouting names she didn't have, calling souls out of their vessels the way you'd call cats in from rain. And they came. Gods, they came. Little flames, homesick candle-flames, one after another after another, rising out of their cases like sparks off a bonfire, drifting toward the breach Wren had mapped, thousands of them, the sold and the stolen and the bargained-away, stirring free for the first time in decades, in centuries.

Wren stood at the corridor mouth with her maps pinned open and her voice steady as a mason's line, calling directions — *left at the fallen pillar, mind the flooding gallery, children in the middle, adults on the ends* — and the freed souls streamed past her like migrating birds who had finally remembered south.

Vane got up. I'll give him that; he got up, and he drew, and he came at me again through the burning rows, and I fought him the ordinary way, the human way, footwork and temper and a plain steel edge, and it was enough, it was only just enough, because the garden was coming down around us and courage was the only weapon either of us had left that still worked.

The ceiling groaned. The first gallery collapsed somewhere east of us with a boom I felt in my teeth. The court beneath the mountain — beautiful, wrong, lit for ten thousand years by other people's promises — was dying, and it died loudly, the way institutions do.

The Keeper turned to me one last time amid the ruin, robes guttering, ledger clutched to its chest like a drowning man's plank, and its smile was gone, and what was underneath the smile was older and sadder and somehow more honest than anything it had shown me since I arrived.

"Swordspeaker," it said. "Your soul. The case. Before the fire reaches it. Choose."

And I understood, in the roaring and the falling and the terrible bright beauty of it, that the choice was not what I'd thought. The choice was not the Keeper offering mercy. The choice was the last clause of the last contract, the fine print finally coming due: the gift that had ignited the garden could not survive the garden's burning. Save the flame in the case, and lose the flame in my sword. Keep the power, and stay sold. Take myself back, and walk out of this mountain ordinary.

Tamsin was shouting my name. Wren was holding the door. The souls were rising like snow in reverse.

I ran for the case.

Behind me, the last of the coronation-oak came down, and the dark rushed in where ten thousand lies had been, and somewhere above us — I would swear to this — somewhere above, winter cracked a little, early, like ice before thaw.
