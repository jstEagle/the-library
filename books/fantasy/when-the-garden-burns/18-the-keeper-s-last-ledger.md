## 18. The Keeper's Last Ledger

The case was smaller than my whole life had been.

That's the thought that arrived first, absurdly, as I knelt in the ruin of the garden with ash sifting down like gray snow and the mountain groaning around us like an old man getting out of a chair he'd sat in for ten thousand years. The case was small. A hand's length of black wood, banded in silver gone green with age, sitting on a shelf that had held it for twelve years while I sold fake love charms two hundred feet of stone and half a kingdom above.

Twelve years of me, folded into something you could post through a letter slot.

"Paloma!" Tamsin again, from the doorway Wren was holding — holding with her whole body, arms braced against stone that wanted very much to be somewhere else. "Whatever you're doing, do it *faster*!"

"I'm having a moment," I called back.

"You can have your moment *upstairs!*"

She had a point. Tamsin usually did, which was one of the more irritating things about her, right after her habit of being right. I reached for the case.

And the Keeper said, "Ah. There you are."

I don't know how to describe what it's like when something that large speaks quietly. The Keeper had been courteous since the moment we'd walked into its court — courteous the way a cathedral is courteous, the way winter is courteous. It stood — if standing was the word for a thing that had been furniture, then architecture, then a presence, and was now mostly a wound wearing the shape of all three — amid the burning wreckage of its garden, and it looked at me with eyes that were not eyes, and it said *there you are* the way you'd say it to a book you'd misplaced for a decade.

"The garden is dying," it said. "You've killed my garden."

"Funny," I said. "From where I'm kneeling, it looks like the garden killed itself. All I did was stop lying to it."

The Keeper considered that. Around us, the last embers of the oaths guttered in their beds of ash — every promise ever sworn to the crown, burning out one by one, and each one going out made the court a little less real. The pillars were softening at the edges. The beautiful wrong proportions were drifting toward merely beautiful, which in this place meant toward nothing at all.

"Yes," the Keeper said at last. "That is rather the trouble with fuel. It does not get a vote." It moved — flowed, really, the way smoke flows when it's pretending to be dignified — and settled across from me, folding itself down until we were nearly eye to eye. Its courtesy never flickered. I'll give it that. It was dying, and it knew it was dying, and it kept its manners about it like a coat it refused to take off even in the fire.

"In twelve thousand years," it said, "no one has burned my garden."

"Well." I wiped ash off my face and only succeeded in grinding it in. "I've been told I have a talent for saying no."

"That," said the Keeper, "is precisely why I am here, and not simply letting the ceiling finish the work."

Behind me, the mountain shifted its weight. Somewhere in the dark, a gallery came down with a sound like the world clearing its throat. Tamsin swore with the fluency of a woman who'd grown up around soldiers, and Wren shouted something about *load-bearing lies*, which I intended to ask her about later, if there was a later, which the general acoustics suggested was an open question.

"The ledger," the Keeper said. "Before the end. You deserve to see the final entry. I keep proper accounts. It is the one virtue I was given."

"I thought you weren't given virtues. I thought you were given a job."

A pause. If a thing without a face can look tired, it looked tired.

"Child," it said, "the job *was* the virtue. That is what they told me, anyway, when they set me here. Someone must hold what is sold, or the selling means nothing. Someone must keep the accounts, or the bargains unravel, and then what was the point of any of it?" It extended one long, gray hand, and the ledger appeared in it — the actual ledger, the master book, the one I'd seen it consulting in the hall of kept things, except now its cover was scorched and its spine was broken and pages were shedding off it like feathers off a plucked bird. "I would show you the entry myself, but my hands—" It looked at them. They were coming apart at the fingertips, dissolving into the same ash that was eating everything else. "My hands are no longer reliable."

So I took the ledger. It was heavier than a book that size had any right to be, because of course it was; everything in this place weighed what it actually meant.

I found the entry by feel before I found it by reading. My fingers knew where to go, the way they knew where to go on a sword hilt. Twelve years ago. A woman's name — my mother's name, written in a hand too steady for someone signing away her son's soul, or perhaps exactly steady enough, the steadiness of someone who has decided that shaking will accomplish nothing. And beneath it, the terms, in the court's own careful script:

*One soul, entire, unspent. Received in good order. Held against the debt of the crown.*

And beneath that, in fresher ink, an addendum I hadn't seen before:

*Balance outstanding: one gift, freely given, to close the account.*

I read it twice. Three times. The mountain rumbled. Tamsin shouted something that was mostly my name and mostly profanity.

"I don't understand," I said, which was true, and which I was beginning to suspect was the most honest sentence I'd said in years.

"It is simple." The Keeper drew itself up as straight as a dying thing could. "Your mother sold your soul to buy the city peace. The crown took the peace and broke the bargain — you know this now; the garden showed you; I am sorry for it, though apology is a poor coin. But the account was never closed. A sale requires two signatures, and the crown's signature was a lie, and so the debt rolls forward. Year upon year. Interest accruing." It gestured at the burning dark around us with something that might have been bitterness, in a thing that had probably invented patience. "Do you know what fed this court, swordspeaker? Not souls. Souls are only collateral. What fed it was the *unpaid*. Every bargain the crown broke. Every oath sworn in bad faith. The garden grew fat on debts the crown never meant to honor, and I grew fat keeping them, and the crown grew fat on us both, and everyone below paid the interest."

"And now the garden's dead."

"Now the garden is dead," the Keeper agreed. "Which brings us to the matter of you. Because the account must be closed properly, child. Even now. *Especially* now. A ledger that burns with entries unresolved leaves ghosts, and I have kept enough ghosts." Its not-eyes fixed on me. "Two ways remain. Hear them both before you choose. It is the least I owe you, and I am aware I owe you a great deal."

The mountain screamed. A crack ran across the ceiling overhead, wide as a road, and through it I could see — impossibly, wonderfully — a thread of gray daylight. Dawn, maybe. Or just the sky remembering it existed.

"Way the first," the Keeper said. "Take the gift. Your swordspeaker's gift — the oath-gift, the voice in the blade. It was never yours, you understand. It was issued to you with the oath, the way a soldier is issued boots. The crown minted it; the garden fueled it; the oath carried it to you. But the crown is burning, and the gift is falling loose from the harness, and it must go *somewhere*. Take it, and it is yours outright — no oath, no leash, the strongest gift in living memory, unbound, free." Something moved behind its courtesy then, something old and sad. "And your soul stays here. Collateral on a debt no one alive remembers taking out. Kept. Forever. In whatever comes after courts."

"And way the second?"

"Take the soul." The Keeper's voice dropped, and for the first time it sounded less like a cathedral and more like a person — a small, tired person at the end of a very long shift. "Walk out of here whole. Entire. Unspent, as the entry says. Belonging to yourself, owing nothing, bound to nothing." A pause. "And the gift goes out with the fire. No one carries it. It dies with the garden. There will be no more swordspeakers. Whatever you are afterward, you will be ordinary."

Ordinary.

I knelt there with ash in my hair and my friends holding a door against the end of the world, and I thought about ordinary the way a drowning man thinks about air.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about a gift: it doesn't feel like a gift from the inside. It feels like a job you never applied for. It felt like being nine years old and hearing my sword hum when the reeve rode past, and knowing — *knowing*, the way you know hunger — that someday it would speak through me and I would not get to choose the words. It felt like my mother teaching me to hide it before she taught me to read, wrapping me in sleight of hand and harmless charms like armor made of shame. It felt like twelve years of selling nothing to people who wanted something, because the something I had was the thing that would get me killed.

They called it a gift. The crown issued it. Like boots.

"Paloma!" Tamsin's voice cracked this time, fear finally getting past the fury. "*Now!*"

"Give me a moment!" I shouted back, and then, quieter, to the Keeper: "Why are you offering? Why does a dying jailer care how the books balance?"

The Keeper was quiet for a long moment. Around us the last oaths went out — I felt them go, little cold spots in the warm dark, promises ending — and with each one the Keeper dimmed a little more, thinned a little more, became more suggestion than substance.

"Because I was sold too," it said.

I stopped breathing. Just for a second.

"Long ago. Before your kind built cities. I was made to keep the accounts, and I was *told* it was a virtue, and I believed it, because believing was easier than looking at the shelves." It spread its dissolving hands, indicating the ruined garden, the collapsing court, the twelve thousand years of it. "No one ever asked me what I wanted. Do you know what I wanted?" The not-eyes found mine. "To be done. To close the book. To put down the pen. And I could not, because the accounts were never clean, because the crown always broke something, because there was *always* another entry." Its voice frayed. "You are offering me the only thing anyone ever offered me, swordspeaker. An ending with the books balanced. Let me have it. Let me do this one last thing correctly."

And there it was. The inheritance. The whole rotten, beautiful inheritance of my life, laid out on a dying creature's palm: my mother's bargain, the crown's betrayal, the oath in my blood, the gift in my hand, the soul in a box — all of it passed down hand to hand like a debt nobody chose, everybody serviced, and nobody questioned. My mother sold me to save me. The crown saved me to spend me. The court spent me to feed itself. And now the court, in its last hour, was handing the choice back to me like a hot coal, saying: *you decide where the chain ends.*

Every inheritance I'd ever received had come with strings. Every single one. The name, the gift, the oath, the guilt — all of it freighted, all of it owed, all of it somebody else's bargain wearing my face.

So I did the one thing nobody in twelve thousand years had apparently thought to do.

I refused the framing.

"No," I said.

The Keeper went still. Even the mountain seemed to lean in.

"Not either-or," I said. "That's the trick, isn't it? That's always the trick. Two doors, both locked from the outside, and me grateful for the privilege of picking. My mother got offered two doors. The crown got offered two doors. Everyone in this ledger got handed a choice between things other people decided, and called it freedom." I stood up. My legs shook. I let them. "I'm not choosing a door, Keeper. I'm choosing *me*. Both halves or nothing. Soul and self, together, or you can explain to whatever comes next why the last entry in your precious ledger is a lie."

For a moment — one long, ringing moment — nothing moved but the ash.

Then the Keeper laughed.

I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever survives of this place: the Keeper laughed. It was a terrible sound, rusty, like a gate opening for the first time in centuries, and it shook loose dust from the ceiling, and it went on far longer than anything that size should have found funny.

"Oh," it said, wiping at a face that wasn't there. "Oh, *well done.* Twelve thousand years, and the answer was refusal. Not door one. Not door two. *Neither.*" It looked at me with something I could only call delight. "Do you know why no one ever tried it? Because everyone who came here had already been taught, from birth, that they were a thing to be divided. Sold-from, sold, spent, spent-on. No one ever arrived whole enough to say no to the arithmetic." It raised one fading hand. "But you — you learned to say no in a market stall, over charms that did nothing, for twelve years. All that hiding. All that small, stubborn, daily dishonesty." The laugh again, softer. "It was practice. You've been rehearsing this refusal your entire life."

Behind me, the crack of daylight widened. Cold air poured in — real air, mountain-cold, tasting of snowmelt and pine and the world. Tamsin was screaming my name in a tone that had abandoned persuasion entirely.

"Here is what I can do," the Keeper said quickly, and its voice was going thin now, going with the light. "The account closes tonight whether we like it or not. The garden is dead; the crown's claim died with it; the debt has no holder. Which means — hear me — which means the choice was always false. There is nothing left to trade *against*. The gift falls to whoever holds it when the fire ends. The soul belongs to whoever claims it. And you, swordspeaker—" the not-eyes blazed, the last bright thing in a drowning world— "you are holding both."

I looked down. My right hand was closed around the case. My left was closed around the hilt of my sword, which was singing — softly, desperately, a note like a struck glass, the gift pouring out of the dying garden and into the only vessel left that could carry it.

Both hands full. Both halves mine.

"Then I'll keep them," I said. "Both. Not because I earned them. Not because I was issued them. Because they're *mine* — the soul my mother bought with her worst day, and the gift I'm choosing to carry instead of being carried by. And if the gift tries to make me its boot-wearer again, I'll burn it myself. That's the entry. Write it down."

The Keeper smiled. It had never smiled before. It should not have been able to smile. It did it anyway, beautifully, terribly, and began to come apart into light instead of ash.

"Entered," it said. "In my own hand. The last entry in the last ledger: *account closed by refusal. The debtor walks out owning himself.*" Its voice was almost gone, and it spent the last of it like a miser spending his hoard on one final extravagance. "Go *up*, Paloma Galloway. Go up, and be ordinary, and be extraordinary, and be neither, and be yours. And when they ask you what you bought down here—"

The light took it. Where it had been, there was only a book — the ledger, whole again, unburnt, closed — resting on the ash like a headstone.

"—tell them the price was refusing to pay."

I picked up the ledger. I don't know why. It seemed rude to leave it.

Then I turned and ran for the door, for the cold, for the voices of the two people on earth who had followed me into hell and were currently holding it open — and behind me the court came down like a held breath finally released, and ahead of me the crack in the world widened, and through it I saw snow.

Falling upward.

Or — no. Falling normally. We were just running fast enough to meet it.

"WREN!" I bellowed, case in one hand, ledger under my arm, sword singing at my hip like a thing newly born. "HOW MUCH FARTHER TO YOUR STAIR?"

Her voice floated back down the collapsing dark, ragged and laughing and utterly certain:

"MAP SAYS LEFT!"

* * *

The map, it turned out, said left, then right, then *down* — "don't ask," Wren gasped, hauling a child over a fallen pillar, "the stair was built by people who hated cartographers" — and then up, up, up, through galleries that shed their ceilings behind us like a snake shedding skin, the freed souls streaming past us through the stone as if the mountain had finally opened all its windows at once.

Tamsin ran beside me with her hand fisted in my sleeve, and when the floor of the long gallery tilted and began to pour itself into the dark below, she didn't scream. She just gripped harder and said, conversationally, through her teeth:

"If we die down here, I want you to know your charms never worked."

"I *know* they never worked," I said. "That was the *point*—"

"Good," she said. "Just so we're honest. At the end."

We crested the stair as the last gallery fell, and the cold hit us like a blessing, and above us — impossibly, wonderfully, *early* — the sky was doing something it hadn't done in four months.

Breaking.
