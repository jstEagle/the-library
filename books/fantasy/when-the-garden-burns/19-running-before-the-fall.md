## 19. Running Before the Fall

I want it on the record, wherever records go when courts burn, that I said we should leave *before* the garden caught fire. I said it clearly, in plain Ashrow dialect, with appropriate volume. And Paloma — bless his stubborn, self-immolating heart — looked at me with that expression he gets when he's about to do something noble and ruinous and said, "It'll hold."

The garden did not hold.

Nothing down here holds. That's rather the point of the place.

* * *

When the Renunciation went up — and I don't have a better word for what Paloma did, swearing an oath and snapping it like a green branch in the same breath, which is apparently the one thing a swordspeaker is never supposed to do, which is of course exactly why he did it — the whole buried court shuddered like a dog shaking off water. Except the water was fire, and the dog was a mountain, and we were standing on its spine.

The garden of oaths burned the way I imagine a library burns: not all at once but in waves, each shelf of sworn words catching and flaring and feeding the next. Only instead of books it was promises. Centuries of them. Every "I swear by the crown" ever spoken to keep the peace, to seal a bargain, to buy a decade of quiet for a city that never knew what the quiet cost. All of it fuel. All of it going up at once because one man from Ashrow Lane decided he'd rather be nobody's sword than somebody's soul.

"Run," Wren said, and her voice had a quality I'd only heard from her once before, back on the surface, when she talked about the stair that wasn't there. It was the voice of a woman who has already seen the map of what happens next and does not like the route. "Everyone run. Children first. Tamsin, you're with me at the rear."

"I'm always at the rear," I said. "It's my best feature."

"Tamsin."

"Yes. Running now."

There were eleven children. I counted them twice, then a third time, because counting is the thing I do when my hands want to shake and I can't afford the shaking. Eleven freed souls, blinking and stumbling out of the alcoves where the court kept them — some of them no older than six, some of them old enough to be gray, because time moves strangely when you're a kept thing, and a child sold forty years ago might still wear a child's face. They moved toward Wren like iron filings toward a lodestone. She was born here. Sold upward as a babe, mapped her whole disgraced life back toward this place, and now she stood in the burning heart of it calling names she'd learned from ledgers, and every single one of those children answered.

If you'd told me a month ago that the most frightening thing I'd ever witness would be a cartographer being loved, I'd have laughed you into the street.

* * *

The galleries began to fail in earnest about two hundred paces from the garden.

I don't mean they crumbled. Crumbling would have been merciful. What they did was *forget*. The court beneath the mountain runs on oaths the way a mill runs on water, and with the garden burning, the architecture was losing the promises that held it up. Walls went soft at the edges, stone turning vague, like a word said too many times. Ceilings sagged into shapes that suggested ceilings had been a suggestion all along. One corridor simply stopped agreeing to exist while we were in it, and Wren grabbed my arm and hauled me sideways through a doorway that hadn't been there a heartbeat earlier and wasn't there a heartbeat later.

"How are you doing that?" I gasped.

"I'm not," she said. "The map is. Maps remember roads even when roads forget themselves." She had the great rolled chart of her final work slung across her back, inked with passages she'd drawn from childhood nightmares, and I swear the paper glowed faintly where our feet needed to fall next. "Keep counting your children."

"Eleven."

"Then count again after the next gallery. Things get lost here. That's how the court likes it."

We ran. The children ran better than I did, which stung my pride less than you'd think, since my pride had been taking a beating all week. Behind us the light changed — the gold of the burning garden deepening to a color I don't have a name for, a color like the inside of a closed eye, like the last moment before you admit you're crying. The Keeper's court was dying around us, and it died beautifully, which felt obscene.

Paloma fell in beside me somewhere in the third gallery. He looked terrible. He looked wonderful. His hands were empty — the sword gone, left behind in the garden or taken by it, I didn't ask — and without the blade he seemed somehow more armed than he'd ever been, if that makes sense. A man who has finally put down the thing everyone told him he was.

"You're bleeding," I said.

"You're limping."

"We're a matched set. Where's the inquisitor?"

His jaw did something complicated. "Still below. He wouldn't come." Which meant, though neither of us said it, that Paloma had offered, and been refused, and had walked away from a man standing in fire because the man chose the fire. There's a grief in that shape. I recognized it. I've worn it myself, sized for a sister.

* * *

The trouble came at the Long Stair.

Wren's map ended at a door — a real door, oak and iron, older than anything else down there, set into rock at the base of the ascent to the surface. Beyond it, the sealed stair she'd found us years of routes around. Through it, daylight, eventually. Cold air. Ashrow Lane and its terrible bread and its neighbors who'd need a very long explanation.

The door was shut, and the mechanism that should have opened it was made of oath-work, and the oath-work was burning out faster than everything else.

"It needs a vow to open," Wren said, flat and fast, running her hands over the iron bands. "That's how they built it. Nothing leaves the court unsworn. You speak a promise at the lock and it opens — and the promise becomes part of the garden, part of the crown's ledger, part of—" She stopped. We all heard what she stopped herself from saying. Part of the machine we'd just set fire to. Speak a vow into a dying lock and you fed the lock your promise at the exact moment promises had stopped meaning anything, and the door would drink and drink and never open, or worse, open onto nothing.

"So we break it down," Paloma said, and hit it with his shoulder, and the door took the blow the way a mountain takes weather.

"The under-clerk said there was another way," I said. Everyone looked at me. Yes, fine, I'd been conducting side negotiations with a bureaucratic horror for days; a girl plans ahead. "He told me the door answers to payment. Not vows. Payment. The court's oldest law — everything sold can be bought back, and everything bought can be spent."

"What does a door cost?" Paloma asked.

And here is where I have to be honest, because if I'm honest nowhere else, let me be honest here, in the smoke, with eleven children watching me decide.

I knew what it cost. I'd known since the under-clerk named his price for the garden's heart, back when I thought I was buying information cheap. Memories. Grief rendered into coin. I'd paid him three memories of my sister Merrin already — the day she taught me to swim in the flooded quarry, the taste of her terrible honeycakes, the sound of her laughing so hard at my first healing attempt that she snort-laughed and I never let her forget it. Paid them away and felt the holes close over smooth, like river stones, and told myself it was worth it, because it bought the knowledge that saved us all.

One memory left. The last one. The one I'd been saving the way you save the last of someone's handwriting, the last letter you'll never answer.

Merrin dying. Me, nineteen, holding her hand in the fever-dark, promising her — swearing, actually swearing, and I am not a swearing person, which is why it mattered — promising her I would live a life big enough for both of us. That I would love people loudly and heal them badly and never, ever make myself small to stay safe. Her last smile. Her hand going quiet in mine.

That one.

"Tamsin," Paloma said, and he'd read my face, curse him, he'd read it the way he reads everything, gently and completely. "No. Find another way."

"There isn't one, and you know it, and anyway it's my memory." I was already walking toward the door. "You gave up your gift. Wren gave up her whole life to come home. Don't you dare make this a competition you win."

"That's not—"

"Merrin would have liked you," I said, which stopped him cold, because I never talk about her, because talking about her costs. "She'd have liked you enormously and lectured you about the sword. So let me spend her properly. Let me spend her on eleven children and a mapmaker and a man who belongs to himself now. That's a life big enough for both of us, Paloma. That's the biggest one I could have bought."

* * *

The under-clerk was waiting when I pressed my palm to the iron.

Of course it was waiting. Everything in that court waited; patience was the house style. It unfolded out of the shadow beside the door like a ledger opening, all gray courtesy and ink-stained fingers, and its voice was the voice of every clerk who has ever told you a fee is non-negotiable.

"The door requires tender," it said. "You have tender."

"You know what I'm offering."

"The court knows all its accounts." Something in the gray face shifted — not quite sympathy; clerks don't have sympathy, but they have *recognition*, the look of one who handles grief professionally. "This unit notes that the item is of high quality. Final acquisition. Such memories appreciate."

"Spend it," I said. "Open the door."

"Tamsin—" Paloma again, and Wren's hand on his arm, holding him back, because Wren understood. Wren, who'd been sold as a child and had spent her whole life paying down a debt she never incurred, knew better than anyone that some prices you pay not because they're fair but because the people behind you are worth more than the thing you're losing.

"It's all right," I told him, and here's the strange thing, the thing I keep turning over like a stone in my pocket: it *was* all right. Even as the clerk drew the memory out of me — and it draws, there's no other word, a long slow pull like thread off a spool, like breath leaving a body — even then, some part of me stood apart and watched and thought: *this is what love is for.* Not keeping. Spending. Merrin gave me her deathbed promise and I hoarded it twelve years like a miser, and it turns out a promise isn't a keepsake. It's seed. You put it in the ground or it's just a dead thing in a drawer.

I felt the memory go. I felt the shape of it go — I could tell you that I lost something warm, something that smelled of fever-herbs and tallow candle, something with a hand in it, a small strong hand — but the content was already gone, already poured into the lock, and the door drank it the way dry earth drinks rain.

The iron bands glowed. The lock turned with a sound like a sigh.

"Paid in full," said the under-clerk, and for one moment — I'd swear this on any altar you like, though I suppose altars are out of fashion now — for one moment it bowed. Low. Longer than commerce required. Then it folded itself back into the shadow, and the door swung open onto cold air and the smell of stone and, faintly, impossibly, pine.

* * *

I don't remember most of the climb. That's not the memory-loss talking; that's just what terror does to bookkeeping. I remember Wren going first with the smallest children strapped to her back and chest, her map glowing like a lantern against the dark of the stair. I remember Paloma carrying two at once, one on each hip, a man who'd spent his whole life learning to carry exactly one thing — a sword, an oath, a secret — discovering he had two arms and a spine and a heart with room in it. I remember counting. Eleven. Eleven. Eleven. At every landing, eleven, and once ten, and the ten seconds between ten and eleven were the longest of my life until a boy named Corvin — sold sixty years ago, still eight years old, poor lamb — came toddling out of a side passage chasing a moth that shouldn't have existed underground, and I nearly hugged him into paste.

Light above. Real light, gray-gold, thin as winter broth but *real*, falling down the stairwell like something poured.

And then we were through, and the mountain closed its mouth behind us, and the sound that came up through the rock was the sound of a court finishing its dying — a long groan, a settling, a silence deeper than the silence that had been there before. Somewhere below, a garden of oaths burned itself to ash, and every chain forged from those oaths, every leash and lock and ledger-line, went slack.

The snow was melting. That's what undid me. Not the escape, not the loss — the snow, lying filthy and slushy on the mountainside with little streams running through it, the first thaw coming early, coming *now*, as if the world had been waiting for permission. The children stood in it blinking, eleven kept things seeing open sky, and one of them started to cry, and then several of them, and then Wren, who had not cried once in all the weeks I'd known her, sat down right there in the wet snow with a child in each arm and wept like a woman setting down a weight carried across a lifetime.

Paloma found me at the edge of the group. He didn't say anything clever. He just stood next to me, shoulder to shoulder, looking down at the city smoking gently in its winter-thaw valley, and after a while he said, "What was it? If you don't mind my asking. What you paid."

And I reached for the answer and found the hole where it should be, smooth-sided, river-stone round. I knew its shape. I knew it had been warm. I knew it was hers, and mine, and that it had bought a door.

"I don't know," I said, and found I was smiling. "But I know what it was *for*. That's more than most people get."

He put his arm around my shoulders, and we stood there, two people stitched together by shared catastrophe, watching eleven children learn what wind feels like.

Then Wren stood up, wiped her face, and pointed down-valley with the grim authority of a woman consulting a map only she could see.

"There's a road," she said, "and at the end of it, a city that's about to find out its crown has been trading souls for peace — and a ban on magic that just lost its fuel. We should be there when it happens. Someone will have to explain." She glanced at Paloma, and something almost like mischief crossed her exhausted face. "Preferably before the inquisitors start improvising."

Paloma sighed the sigh of a man who had hoped, briefly and foolishly, that saving the world entitled him to a nap.

"All right," he said. "Let's go tell them the truth."

Down in the valley, the bells of Ashrow began to ring — wrong, urgent, the alarm pattern — and we started walking.
