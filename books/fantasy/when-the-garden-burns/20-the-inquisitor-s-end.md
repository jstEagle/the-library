## 20. The Inquisitor's End

I had spent twelve years learning how to be small, and it turns out all that practice was for this: standing in a burning garden with no magic left in me at all, holding a sword I could no longer speak to, facing down the most dangerous man the crown had ever sent below the mountain.

The fire was everywhere now. Not ordinary fire — oath-fire, the kind that burns what binds rather than what breathes. The garden of oaths blazed around us in columns of white and gold, and every flame was a promise being kept against its will for the last time. Ten thousand vows to the crown, going up like festival lanterns. Somewhere in that blaze was my mother's bargain. Somewhere in it was mine.

And somewhere behind us — Tamsin, Wren, a dozen freed children running for a stair that might not exist anymore.

Which left me here, doing the one thing I'd sworn never to do since I was nine years old: drawing attention.

"Inquisitor Hale," I said. "You should run."

He did not run. Of course he didn't. Men like Corvus Hale don't run from fires; they appoint the fire to a position and expect it to file reports.

He stood at the far end of the garden's central aisle, where the Keeper's great ledger-hall opened into the burning beds, and he looked exactly as he had on Ashrow Lane three days ago: tall, gray-cloaked, immaculate, as though ash itself had signed a contract agreeing not to touch him. Only his eyes were different. On the surface they had been cold. Down here, lit by ten thousand dying oaths, they were *hungry*.

"Galloway." He said my name the way a man names a debt he intends to collect personally. "You've made a remarkable mess."

"I do try."

"You burned a garden that has fed this court — fed the *crown* — for four hundred years." He began walking toward me, unhurried, his boots finding the stone path between the blazing oath-beds as if he'd walked it a hundred times. Which, I realized with a lurch, he probably had. "Do you understand what you've done? Every ward on every border. Every binding on every witch-hunter's badge. Every oath your precious neighbors swore at their coronations and their weddings and their trials. It's all fuel, Galloway. It was always fuel. And you've set the whole storehouse alight."

"Then I'd say the crown should have invested in something less flammable than other people's promises."

Something crossed his face then — fast, gone before I could name it. Not anger. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing a joke he'd once told himself, back before he stopped telling jokes.

"You're giftless," he said. He had stopped ten paces away, and now he looked at my sword hand, at the blade I held low and plain, no runes crawling up it, no voice whispering along its edge. My swordspeaker's gift had gone with the renunciation, traded up in smoke along with everything else. "I felt it happen. Do you know what that was like, from my side? Like a lamp going out in a house I've been watching for years." His head tilted. "Twelve years I've hunted the last swordspeaker. Twelve years, and the crown finally tells me the truth — that you're not to be killed at all. You're to be *retrieved*. Your soul is an asset, Galloway. Line item four hundred and eleven in the court's ledger, cross-referenced to the treasury. Did you know that? Your mother's price was paid out of the royal accounts. You've been crown property since you were six years old."

"I read the ledger," I said. "It was a long night."

"And still you burned it."

"It seemed rude not to. It had my name in it."

The garden roared around us. An oath-bed collapsed nearby in a shower of white sparks, and I felt the heat press against my face like a living thing — felt, but couldn't answer it. That was the strangest part, the part nobody warns you about. For twelve years the world had whispered to me. Steel spoke; iron remembered; the oath in my blood hummed its single note, crown-and-crown-and-crown, day and night. Now there was nothing. Just heat, and noise, and a man walking toward me who wanted to put me back in a ledger.

I had never felt more like myself in my life.

"Hale," I said. "The court is coming down. The Keeper is dead or dying. Whatever the crown promised you for bringing me home — the coin won't exist by morning. The men who wrote the contract are going to be very busy explaining to a city why its wards just went dark. Run. Take your people. Go."

"We are not leaving without you."

"There's nothing left to take. That was sort of the point."

"The order," he said, and now his voice changed, dropped into the register men use when they recite things they've built their lives on, "is to retrieve asset 411 intact. Alive. Bound. The oath-fire may consume the garden, but the crown's claim travels with the man, Galloway. A soul sold to the crown belongs to the crown wherever it goes." He drew his sword.

And that's when I heard it.

Or rather — didn't hear it. Because Hale's blade came up gleaming in the oath-light, and every instinct I had left reached out for it the way a tongue finds a missing tooth, and found nothing. No voice. No hum. Just steel, honest and mute, the same as any blacksmith makes.

But Hale's blade — Hale's blade was singing. Quietly. Under the fire-roar, under the groan of the mountain beginning its long collapse, there was a thread of sound coming off that sword like heat-shimmer, and I knew it because I'd been bound to its twin my entire adult life.

Oath-steel. Crown-forged. A hunter's blade speaks one word only, and the word is *obey*, and it says it in the voice of whoever holds the leash.

"You feel that?" I asked him. "Your sword?"

His grip tightened. "The blade serves."

"Does it." I took a step backward, and another, giving myself room, buying seconds, because I am a shopkeeper first and a hero a very distant second and both professions teach you the same lesson: never stand still while a dangerous man decides what he thinks of you. "Because here's what I know about oath-steel, Hale, and I knew it even before I could hear it, back when I was just a boy selling fake love charms and pretending the humming in my head was normal. Oath-steel doesn't serve the hand. It serves the chain. And chains have two ends."

"Save your sermons."

"Oh, I'm not preaching. I'm appraising." Another step. The heat at my back was enormous now; sweat ran down my spine, and the plain steel in my hand was almost too hot to hold comfortably, which is a problem you solve later or never. "You said it yourself. The crown trades with this court. Has traded for four hundred years. Wards and witch-hunts and burnings on the surface — theater, all of it, harvest season. They flush the gifted down the mountain like grain into a mill. And you—" I pointed my plain, mute, honest sword at him, "—you are the finest harvesting tool they ever forged. Oath-bound. Leashed. Line item yourself, aren't you? Somewhere in that ledger, before I burned it. Asset Hale. Cross-referenced."

"Enough."

"Was it in the ledger, Hale? Did you look yourself up? I would have. Gods know I did."

"*Enough!*" 

He came at me.

Here is what I can tell you about fighting without a gift after a lifetime of having one: it is like being deaf in a room full of musicians. My body remembered things my blood no longer carried. My feet knew the angles. My wrists knew the weight. But the conversation was gone — the half-second warning, the whisper of *he feints high*, the old intimacy between a speaker and the speaking steel — and so what remained was just a man with a sword, which is to say, finally, fairly, honestly: just two men with swords.

He was better than me. I'll say that plainly, because I nearly died twice in the first ten passes and only the burning ground saved me — an oath-bed gave way beneath his lead foot and he had to dance back through white fire, and I used the heartbeat to put a tomb-raised planter between us. Sparks sheeted off it. He came around the left, low, fast, textbook crown-form, and I caught his blade on mine and the shock of it went up my arm and into teeth I no longer had magic to cushion.

"You were *good*," he snarled, pressing, our blades locked, his face a hand's width from mine. Firelight turned his eyes to coins. "They said the last swordspeaker was wasted on charms. They said if we ever flushed him, he'd be worth ten hunters. And you can't even—"

"I'm not trying to beat you," I told him.

That confused him. Good. Confusion is free; use it whenever offered.

Because I wasn't. Look — even giftless, I'd have given most hunters a hard afternoon. But Hale wasn't most hunters, and I was tired, and burned, and my soul was three hours old and didn't fit yet, like new boots. What I was trying to do was keep him talking. Keep him here. Keep him looking at me instead of the stair.

Keep him, in short, exactly where Tamsin had gambled everything he would be: at the heart of the garden, when it fell.

"The crown broke your leash, Hale," I said, backing along the path, giving ground, giving words. "Not me. The garden. Every oath feeding this place was sworn *to* them — and it's all burning. Including yours. Listen." 

And this time he listened, because he couldn't help it. The mountain was screaming now, deep and structural, stone settling into stone the way a house settles when it decides to become rubble. And threading through it, thinning with every passing moment, came the sound of Hale's own sword —

— going quiet.

Oath-steel sings the crown's voice, and the crown's voice lived in the garden, and the garden was dying, and so the song was guttering like a candle in a draught. I watched him hear it. I watched a man listen to his own purpose go silent. His blade dipped an inch. Two. His lips moved around a syllable — some command, some invocation, *obey, obey* — and got nothing back.

"No," he said. To the sword. To the mountain. To four hundred years.

"There it is," I said softly. "The hunt ends, Hale. Look at what's left when it does."

He looked at me. And here is the thing I will carry out of this mountain if I carry nothing else: he wasn't angry. He was *afraid*. The cold collector, the crown's best knife, standing in the ashes of the machine that had made him — and underneath all that armor of office and certainty, a man discovering that he had been harvested too, all along, as thoroughly as any hedge-healer dragged off Ashrow Lane, and that no one, in all his years of faithful hunting, had ever once come to hunt for *him*.

"They'll rebuild it," he whispered. "They always—"

"Not this one. Not this way. The fuel's awake now, Hale. You can't re-ledger souls that remember being free."

He raised his sword again. Slower, this time. Almost ritual. And I understood — with the terrible clarity that comes to a man with no magic left, only eyes — that this was not a fight anymore. It was an argument, and he intended to win it the only way left to him: by proving the old order still worked, one last time, on the last target it would ever have.

I raised my plain blade to meet him.

We came together one final time, steel on steel with no voices in either weapon, just weight and skill and desperation, and it was ugly and graceless and utterly human, and I lost my footing on a cracking flagstone, and his point came for my throat —

— and stopped.

An inch away. Trembling. Because his sword had finally, fully, gone silent — and a hunter's blade with no oath in it is just a knife, and knives do not decide anything on their own. The choice fell back into the man's hands, alone, for perhaps the first time since he was a boy.

Corvus Hale stood over me with a dead sword and a burning sky, and he wept. Once. A single sound, torn out of him like a tooth.

Then he stepped back. Sheathed the blade — sheathed it, gods help me, the muscle of a lifetime overriding everything — and turned to face the great burning heart of the garden, where the oldest oaths still blazed in their beds: coronation-vows, dynasty-oaths, the crown's own founding promises, burning white and enormous.

"I was seven," he said, conversationally, terribly, to the fire. "When they took my oath. It was the price of my father's pardon. One son, bound to the crown, and the family name goes clean." He laughed, and it was the worst sound I heard all day. "Line item. You were right, Galloway. I never looked myself up. I was afraid of the number."

"Hale." I got to my feet. "There's a stair. There's time. Come *up*. Come up and see what a city looks like when it stops lying."

He shook his head slowly. "A man who hunts the gifted for thirty years doesn't get to walk into the thaw, Galloway. Whatever waits up there — it shouldn't wait for me. And besides." He touched his silent sword, gently, like a sick friend. "This is the only home I have left. Best not to die homeless."

The garden's heart chose that moment to fall.

A column of oath-fire went up with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat, and the heat drove me back three staggering steps, arm across my face. Through the white blaze I saw him one last time — standing straight, gray cloak lifting in the firewind, unbound at last and refusing to move — and I swear on nothing, because I owned nothing left worth swearing on, that he looked *relieved*.

"Tell the city," Hale said, and the fire took the rest.

* * *

I ran.

No dignity in it — I want that recorded somewhere. I ran through a collapsing paradise with my arms over my head and my lungs full of ash and my soul rattling in my chest like a coin in an empty purse, and I did not look back, because the books all say the hero looks back, and the books have clearly never watched a friend choose his own funeral pyre.

The galleries were coming down in sheets. Wren's marked route — chalk arrows, bless her and her disgraced cartographer's heart — zigzagged up through passages that existed this hour and might not the next. I caught up with her at a junction, hauling two of the younger children over a fallen lintel, and she took one look at my face and asked no questions, which is the highest form of kindness I know.

"Tamsin?" I managed.

"Ahead. Holding the door." She jerked her chin upward. "Move, Galloway. The mountain's not done with us."

So I moved. We all moved — Wren's sold children, quiet as ghosts, quick as mice, climbing through the dark behind a woman who had mapped her way home for twenty years and was only now, finally, taking it.

Behind us, the garden burned everything it had ever been fed.

And ahead — I could smell it before I saw it, filtering down through cracked stone, impossible and unmistakable — ahead was cold air. Clean air. Thaw-air.

Winter, breaking overhead, like a promise someone had finally decided to keep.
