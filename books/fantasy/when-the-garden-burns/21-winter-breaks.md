## 21. Winter Breaks

The last stair gave out beneath Wren's boots with a sound like a bone snapping, and we came up into daylight the way drowned men come up into air — too fast, too loud, all of us gasping and clutching at each other like the sky might be a lie.

It wasn't a lie. It was snowmelt.

I remember that most clearly, of everything from those first minutes on the surface: not the ache in my legs, not Tamsin's hand locked around my wrist hard enough to bruise, not the eleven children blinking in the light like moles dragged out of their burrows. I remember water. The whole city was dripping. Eaves running, gutters chuckling, icicles letting go of the rooftops in long silver slides that shattered on the cobbles below. The great winter that had gripped Ashrow for three years — the winter everyone said was just weather, just bad luck, just the crown's taxes making everything colder somehow — was breaking apart above our heads while we stood there in the mud of Odd Wren's hidden stair, filthy and half-starved and alive.

"Thaw," Tamsin said, as if reporting a symptom. She tipped her face up and let a drop land on her cheek. "Actual thaw."

"Don't get sentimental," Wren said. "You'll rust."

But she was crying. She'd deny it to this day, but she was crying, standing there with her maps ruined and her birth-city collapsed behind us and eleven sold children arranged around her skirts like ducklings who'd imprinted on the wrong bird. She cried the way she drew — precisely, without wasted motion — and then she wiped her face with her sleeve and started counting heads, because that was the only language she had for love.

I looked down at my hands.

They were just hands. I'd spent twelve years pretending they were just hands, and now they actually were, and I want to tell you it felt like freedom. It didn't, not right away. It felt like an amputation healed over — the ghost-ache still there, the fingers flexing around something that wasn't. My gift had gone into the garden with everything else. Every oath ever sworn to the crown had burned as fuel, and mine had burned loudest, and what was left when the fire went out was a man holding a sword he could no longer speak to.

The sword was still on my hip. Old habit. It weighed nothing and everything.

* * *

We came into the city by the river road, which was the only road anyone thought to watch, because nobody watches the road the flood takes. The guards at the gate weren't checking for magic. They were checking for looters, and even that with the weary half-heartedness of men who hadn't been paid in two months. The crown's coin had stopped meaning anything around midwinter; now its writ was following.

"You feel that?" Tamsin murmured as we passed under the arch. "The ban?"

I did. Or rather, I felt its absence — a pressure I'd lived under so long I'd mistaken it for gravity, suddenly gone. For three years, since the witch-hunt began, every working in the city had been like shouting underwater: muffled, distorted, dangerous. The inquisitors' wards had sat over the districts like lids on pots. Now the pots were open. I could see it in small things — a hedge-charm glowing openly in a window box where before it would have been hidden under dried flowers, a washerwoman muttering a blessing over her line without glancing over her shoulder, two children playing at sparks in an alley and no one dragging them away by the collar.

"It'll take time," I said. "Habits like fear don't thaw fast."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "But look." She nodded toward the gatehouse, where someone had torn down the proclamation boards. The notices were still there underneath — HUNT FOR THE GIFTED, REWARD FOR INFORMATION, the crown's seal stamped in red wax — but someone had pasted new paper over them, crude and handwritten:

**THE GARDEN IS BURNED. THE CROWN'S WORDS ARE ONLY WORDS. COME OUT.**

Underneath, in smaller letters, someone with a hedge-healer's careful hand had added: *Tamsin of Ashrow Lane lives. She sends her love to her street.*

She stared at it for a long moment. "That's Marta's handwriting," she said finally. "My neighbor. The one who pretended not to know what I was for three years."

"And?"

"And she spelled 'gifted' wrong." Tamsin laughed — a wet, cracked sound, the first real laugh I'd heard out of her since the court. "Three years of pretending, and the first thing she does when the lid comes off is misspell 'gifted' on a public notice."

"That's the bravest thing I've heard all week," Wren said, "and this week I watched a man argue with his own sword."

* * *

Ashrow Lane looked smaller than I remembered. That's what absence does — it inflates things, and then returning deflates them, and you have to decide whether you love the thing or the inflation. I decided, walking up the lane through the meltwater, that I loved the lane itself: the crooked doorways, the smell of woodsmoke and wet stone, old Pell's shutters hanging at their permanent drunken angle. The dread I'd carried here for twelve years — the flinch at every guard's boot, the charm-stall built like a mask over my own face — that had inflated the place into a prison. Without the dread, it was just a street. A street could be home.

My shop was exactly as I'd left it, which meant the door hung open and the interior had been picked over. They'd taken the copper bangles and the good ribbon. They'd left the charms.

Of course they'd left the charms. The charms were worthless. That had always been the joke only I was in on: a stall full of love-luck and traveler's-blessing, each one crafted with genuine care and absolutely no magic whatsoever, sold to a frightened city by a man whose hands could do more real work than any amulet ever struck — if he'd dared. Sleight of hand hiding a swordspeaker's gift. The greatest con in Ashrow, and the mark I'd conned was myself.

I swept the floor. I restocked the shelf. I set the little brass bell back on its hook over the door, the one that had gone silent when the inquisitors' wards went up, because the wards ate small magics and a bell-charm is a small magic. I didn't re-charm it. I hung it plain, and when the wind moved it, it rang anyway, because that's what bells do when you don't enchant them into silence.

Then I opened the shutters, set out the stall, and waited to see who'd come.

* * *

The first customer was a child, maybe seven, who slapped a bent copper on the counter and demanded a charm "so my da stops coughing."

I looked at the copper. I looked at the child. Somewhere behind my ribs, the ghost of the gift stirred — the old reflex, the reach toward power that wasn't there anymore, like a tongue finding the gap where a tooth was.

"I can't charm your da better," I said. "No charm can. But—" I dug under the counter and came up with a jar of honey-and-thyme paste, the kind Tamsin used to make before the hunt made her hide her craft. "This will help the cough. Costs the same as the charm. And this—" I took a scrap of red ribbon, tied it in a knot, and handed it over. "This is just a ribbon. But if you tie it somewhere you'll see it every day, it'll remind you that somebody in this city wants your da well. That's not magic. It works better than magic, sometimes."

The child considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Is it a trick?"

"It's the opposite of a trick. Tricks are when things seem true and aren't. This is a thing that seems like nothing and is true."

She took the ribbon and the paste and left the bent copper, and I sat there after she'd gone feeling something I couldn't immediately name. It took me until the third customer — a widow wanting luck for her son's job search, getting a pressed four-leaf clover and ten minutes of honest advice about which foremen paid fair — to name it.

Lightness. Not the lightness of having less. The lightness of carrying nothing false.

For twelve years every sale had been a double transaction: the charm changing hands, and the lie changing hands alongside it. *Here's your love-luck, madam, and here's my silence, and here's my neck kept out of the noose.* Now there was just the charm, and the charm was honest, and honest worthless trinkets turned out to sell fine when the person selling them had stopped being afraid.

Word got around, the way word does on a street. By the fourth day I had a queue. By the sixth, someone had chalked on the cobbles outside my stall: **THE CHARM SELLER WAS DOWN THE MOUNTAIN AND CAME BACK. HIS GOODS ARE PLAIN BUT HIS PRICES ARE FAIR.**

I never found out who wrote it. I left it there until the rain took it.

* * *

Tamsin reopened her practice the same week, and she did not hide, and the first thing she did was hang a sign that said HEALING, HEDGE-CRAFT, HONEST WORK in letters visible from the far end of the lane. The second thing she did was come to my stall at closing time, sit on my counter, and eat the supper I'd been looking forward to.

"So," she said, through a mouthful of my bread. "Ordinary."

"Completely. I tried to light the stove yesterday with the old way — just reaching for it, you know, the reflex — and nothing. Burned my thumb on the flint like any fool."

"Poor lamb."

"I'm grieving. Mock me gently."

She swallowed and was quiet a moment, and I knew what was coming, because I'd been avoiding the same thought for six days. "Do you regret it?" she asked. "The choice. You could've kept the gift. The Keeper offered—"

"The Keeper offered me my soul or my gift, and told me most people take the gift, because most people would rather be powerful than be whole." I turned the bent copper over in my fingers — I'd kept it, don't ask me why, or do, and I'll say it's because it was the first honest coin I ever earned. "And I stood there doing the arithmetic, Tamsin. That's the shameful part. I stood there weighing it. Twelve years of hiding against a lifetime of power. And then I thought about my mother, who traded my soul for a decade of peace and got a decade of war instead, because the crown broke faith with her the moment it was convenient. And I thought about the inquisitor, who had every gift the crown could grant and chose the fire rather than live unbound." I shrugged. "I chose me. It felt strange for about a day. Then it felt like putting down something heavy I'd forgotten I was carrying."

"You don't miss it? Even a little?"

"Every morning. Around dawn, when the gift used to wake first, before the rest of me. I reach for it and it's not there and for about four breaths I mourn." I met her eyes. "And then I get up and make breakfast, and nobody owns me. Not the crown, not the mountain, not my mother's bargain, not the oath. Four breaths of grief in exchange for that is the best trade I've ever made, and I once sold a man a 'storm-proof' hat during a drought."

Tamsin laughed, then sobered. "Marta asked about you. The whole street's asking. What happened down there. Whether it's true the crown knew — really knew — about the court. Whether there'll be reckoning."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her the truth: that the crown's oaths fed something hungry, and the oaths are burned now, and what grows next depends on what we plant." She hopped off the counter. "Regent's council is in shambles, by the way. Half of them ran. The other half are claiming they always opposed the hunt, which is the political equivalent of a love charm — technically harmless, fundamentally false."

"And the army? The inquisitors?"

"Gone quiet. An oath-bound army without oaths is just men with weapons and no reason to use them. Some went home. Some are asking the questions Marta's asking." She paused at the door. "Wren sent word, incidentally. From the stair camp. The children are settling. And she says—" Tamsin's mouth twitched, "—she says the deep roads didn't all collapse. There are survivors below, in the outer galleries. They're signaling. They want to talk trade."

I looked at her. "Trade."

"Wren's words exactly. She says the freed court has silk, deep-metal, and grudges, and that a city with a broken crown might want two of the three." Tamsin grinned. "She's drafting a map of the negotiable passages. She wanted me to ask whether you'd stand witness at the parley. Not as a swordspeaker." Her voice gentled. "As a man who's been sold and bought himself free. They trust that story, she says. Down there, it's the only story that counts."

The bell over my door rang — plain, unenchanted, honest — in the evening wind.

I looked at my hands on the counter. Just hands. Good enough.

"Tell Wren," I said, "that I'll bring the charms."

Tamsin raised an eyebrow. "The ones that do nothing?"

"The ones that do nothing," I agreed, "and mean it. Somebody down there should learn what an honest trinket looks like. Consider it diplomacy."

She left, laughing, and I banked the stove and stood a while in the doorway watching the lane run with meltwater, bright in the last light, everything dripping and loosening and beginning. Three winters of fear, going soft at the edges. A city learning to speak without whispering.

Tomorrow I'd dig out the good ribbon stock. Tomorrow there'd be a parley to plan and a map to read and a street that needed reminding, knot by knot, that somebody wanted it well.

Tonight, the thaw sang in every gutter in Ashrow, and for the first time in twelve years, every word of it was addressed to no one's master — least of all mine.
