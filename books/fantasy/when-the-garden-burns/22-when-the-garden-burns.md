## 22. When the Garden Burns

The first thing I did with my ordinary hands was plant a charm stall that actually cheated people.

Not in the way you're thinking. Hear me out. The love charms still did nothing — that hadn't changed, and never would, because there is no charm in this world or the buried one beneath it that can make a heart do anything it doesn't already want to do. But now, when a nervous boy with straw-colored hair bought one for a girl who sold bread on Ashrow Lane, I told him so. "This is dried lavender and wishful thinking," I said, and took his copper anyway. "But she laughs at your jokes when you're not even being funny, which is worth more than any charm I could sell you."

He went red as a beet and bought two.

That's the trade I run now: honesty at a markup. Business has never been better. It turns out that when a city spends ten years terrified of its own shadow, what it wants most isn't magic. It's permission to stop flinching.

* * *

Winter broke the way a fever breaks — all at once, in the middle of the night, leaving everyone blinking and damp and unsure whether to trust it. I woke on the third of Thawmonth to water dripping off the eaves of Ashrow Lane in a rhythm I'd forgotten existed, and by noon the whole street had turned out like it was a festival, pointing at icicles falling off the chandler's roof and shattering on the cobbles like glass bells.

Tamsin stood in the middle of it all with her face tipped up to the sun and her sleeves rolled past her elbows, and I thought — not for the first time, but for the first time without flinching — *I would follow that woman back down the mountain if she asked.*

She didn't ask. She asked for something much worse.

"Spade," she said, holding out her hand. "And don't give me that look. You owe me a garden."

"I owe you exactly nothing," I said. "You spent your sister's memories buying our way through a collapsing court. If anyone owes anyone—"

"Then consider it repayment." Her hand stayed out. Her eyes didn't, though; they'd gone somewhere else, somewhere below the frost line, where they'd been going more often since we came up. "I want dirt under my hands, Paloma. Real dirt, over real ground, that grows things because I tended them and not because some ancient ledger-keeping horror decided they should grow. Is that so much?"

It wasn't. That was the thing about Tamsin — she asked for almost nothing and meant all of it.

So we dug. Or rather, she dug, and I hauled, and Wren stood at the edge of the plot with her maps spread over a barrel, telling us we were doing it wrong.

"You can't put a garden there," Wren said, tapping the old stair. We'd sealed it after the collapse — good Ashrow masonry, Tamsin's cousin's work, a capstone with no name carved on it, because names are how doors remember. But the ground above it had a particular quality I couldn't shake, a faint warmth underfoot even in frost, like a banked fire. "There's a gallery underneath. Partially intact. The collapse didn't take everything."

"Good," said Tamsin, driving her spade in. "Let it hold up my roses."

Wren opened her mouth to argue — Wren always opened her mouth to argue; it was her way of saying *I love you, please be careful* — and then closed it again, because she looked at Tamsin's set jaw and understood something I took another week to understand myself.

Tamsin wasn't building over the stair despite what lay beneath it. She was building over it *because*. You bury grief by planting things on top of it. Anyone who tells you different has never tried either.

* * *

The trade with the freed court started, as most dangerous things do, with a letter.

It arrived in the second week of Thawmonth, delivered by a child — an actual child, maybe nine years old, with the grey-veined eyes of the born-below and a courier's satchel nearly as big as she was. She marched into my stall between customers, set a folded paper on the counter, and said, in the flat recitation of someone who'd memorized it: "From the Court Beneath the Mountain, greetings to the Speaker Who Burned the Garden, who is also the Charm-Seller Who Cheats Honestly. Reply may be left at the fourth lamp on Cordwainer Street, under the loose brick. Do not follow me. Merrow says if you follow me she'll know, and she'll be very disappointed, and also she'll have Wren's head examined for teaching surface-folk the lamp-brick system."

"Merrow," I repeated. "One of Wren's children?"

"One of Wren's *people*," the girl said, with the precise indignation of someone correcting a category error. "We're not children anymore. We're a diplomatic delegation." And she marched out before I could point out that a diplomatic delegation of one nine-year-old was still, definitionally, a child.

The letter was written in Wren's cartographer's hand — of course it was; Wren had been going down twice a week since the thaw began, mapping the surviving galleries, and apparently moonlighting as a scribe for the new government of the formerly damned.

*Paloma,* it read. *They want trade. Not coin — they've seen what coin does to a court, thank you very much. They want light. Seeds. Salt. Books. In exchange they're offering worked stone, deep-metal, and the craft of things grown without sun. Merrow's people have been keeping a fungus-garden in the eastern galleries that will make you weep. Also they want you specifically to come to a meeting because they say a bargain sworn in the ashes of the old oath needs a witness who knows what oaths cost, and apparently that's you now. Congratulations on your promotion from coward to institution. — W.*

P.S. Tamsin says bring the lavender seeds. The big ones. Don't ask why, just bring them.

I brought the lavender seeds.

* * *

The fourth lamp on Cordwainer Street turned out to mark the entrance to a stair that hadn't existed three months ago. That's the thing about the freed court: once the garden stopped burning, once the great engine of sworn oaths choked and died, all that stored power didn't vanish. It settled. It pooled in the low places like meltwater. And the born-below, who had spent generations learning every trick of their prison, discovered they could shape it — carefully, a little at a time, the way you'd carry hot coals in a shovel.

Wren met me at the top of the new stair. She looked different. I kept trying to identify how, and it took me half the descent to figure it out: she looked *rested*. The woman who'd led us down through the dark with her maps and her secrets had carried her birth-court like a stone in her chest. Now the stone was still there, but she'd set it down long enough to sleep.

"You came," she said.

"You asked."

"I asked expecting you to invent an excuse. You've gotten predictable in your old age."

"I'm thirty-one."

"Ancient," she agreed, and led me down.

The court beneath the mountain was still beautiful and wrong, but wrongness fades with familiarity, the way a strange house becomes home one unnoticed morning at a time. The galleries where the Keeper's ledger-hall had stood were rubble now, deliberately unrestored — the born-below had voted, Wren told me, to leave the ruin visible. "So no one forgets what was done here," she said, "and no one pretends it couldn't be done again. Ruins are honest. Monuments lie."

Merrow received me in a chamber lit by lamps of cultivated glow-moss — soft green light, nothing like the hungry gold of the burning garden — and conducted the negotiation with the gravity of a woman three times her age, which, given that she'd spent her childhood as property, she probably felt she'd earned. The terms were simple. Seeds and salt and stories from above; stone-work and deep-grown food and, when they mastered it, healing arts from below. No crowns. No oaths. Every exchange witnessed and recorded in a plain book, in plain ink, by rotating hands.

"No oaths," Merrow said, fixing me with those grey-veined eyes. "That's the condition. Your people keep wanting to swear things. Swearing is how the last place got built. So: no oaths. Promises only. A promise can be broken and survived. An oath eats the breaker."

"I've noticed," I said.

She almost smiled. Underneath the gravity she was nine years old, and I found I liked her enormously.

At the end of it she pushed a small object across the table toward me. A bone token — but whole, unburned, clean. New-carved. On it, in careful strokes: *PAID IN FULL.*

"What's this?"

"Your receipt," Merrow said. "The ledger burned, but debts don't die with the book that records them — we learned that the hard way. So we re-cut the accounts from memory while the memories were fresh. Every soul that was taken, every soul that came free. Yours is marked settled." She paused. "Your mother's debt too. She paid twelve years of peace for it, and then the crown stole the peace back. The court remembers that. We wanted you to know that the ones who lived down here remembered it, even when nobody up there did."

I sat with that for a while. The glow-moss hummed. Somewhere far off, water moved through stone, patient as forgiveness.

"Thank you," I said finally, and it came out rougher than I intended.

"Don't thank us," said Merrow. "Bring the lavender seeds next time. Wren won't say why they matter and it's driving everyone mad."

* * *

It was Tamsin who explained the lavender, later that month, kneeling in the garden she was raising over the old stair.

The soil there was strange — warm, dark, richer than any soil on Ashrow Lane, fed by whatever slow power still seeped up through the broken galleries. Things planted there grew fast and slightly wrong: carrots like fists, beans that climbed a trellis in a single week. Tamsin had spent the season learning what the ground would accept and what it wouldn't. It wouldn't accept wheat. It wouldn't accept anything that grew straight and tall and uniform.

It accepted lavender. Enormously. The plants came up silver-grey and sprawling, and their scent drifted down through the cracks of the sealed stair, and — this was the part Tamsin wouldn't explain until she was ready, and Tamsin was never ready until she was ready — something down below had begun to change because of it.

"The children born below," she said one evening, her hands black to the wrist with good dirt, "have never smelled a living flower. Did you know that? Everything down there is stone and moss and mushroom. Wren told them about flowers the way you'd tell stories about dragons. And now—" she gestured at the plot, the purple spikes nodding in the evening air, the faint sweetness sinking into the earth — "now the smell leaks down. Through the cracks. They come and stand on the stairs — the intact parts, the parts we didn't seal — and they just breathe."

She wiped her eyes with her wrist, briskly, as if brushing away a fly.

"It's not enough," she said. "A smell isn't a flower. So I'm going to start cuttings. Hardy ones. Things that can grow in moss-light. And when they take root down there, I'm going to walk them down myself and plant them, and if anyone has a problem with hedge-healer dirt under the mountain, they can take it up with my spade."

"Tamsin Galloway," I said — because yes, that had happened too, quietly, in the spring, with no oaths, just a promise made in a kitchen over burnt bread, the kind that can be broken and survived, which is the only kind I'll make now — "you are planning to garden your way through the border between worlds."

"Somebody has to," she said. "The diplomats are busy."

* * *

On the last night of Thawmonth, I went alone to the ruins of the old garden.

Not the buried one. There was another — smaller, older, easy to miss. A crown garden, once: a walled plot behind the inquisitors' hall where the oaths of office had been administered for six generations, where every swordspeaker before me had knelt on flagstones and sworn away pieces of themselves to feed the thing beneath the mountain. When the buried garden burned, this one burned with it. Sympathetic fuel. Every oath ever sworn to the crown, igniting wherever its twin stood.

The walls still stood. Everything inside them was ash.

I climbed through the gap where the gate had been and walked on ground that crunched softly under my boots, grey and fine, six inches deep in places. The flagstones where I had knelt at seventeen — where my mother had stood beside me, her hand on my shoulder, her voice steady, saying *swear it, Paloma, swear it and live* — those stones had split in the burning. A crack ran right through the kneeling-place, wide enough to plant something in, if a person were inclined to plant things in ash, which I was.

I knelt. My knees found the crack. Of course they did.

I'd brought paper and ink, because some things need writing to become real, and a stick of charcoal, because some things need erasing to let go, and I'd planned some elaborate ceremony involving both. What I actually did was simpler. I dipped my finger in the cold wet ash — it had rained the week before; the ash held the memory of rain — and I wrote on a flat stone, in letters that gleamed grey-on-grey, nearly invisible:

*MAREN GALLOWAY*

My mother's name. The woman who sold my soul to buy a decade of peace, and whose bargain the crown broke within the year. The woman who taught me sleight of hand and swore me to silence and died believing she'd saved me. The woman whose memory I'd confronted in the burning garden, and forgiven — forgiven, but not absolved, because forgiveness is mine to give and absolution was never mine to grant.

I looked at the name for a long time. The wind came through the broken wall and stirred it, letter by letter — M blurring, A softening, R smudging into the grain of the stone — until the wind had finished the work and the stone was blank, and the name was nowhere, and therefore everywhere, which is where the dead belong.

"There," I said out loud, to no one, to her, to the ash. "Letting go. As promised."

The wind gusted once, hard, and was still. Draw your own conclusions. I've drawn mine, and mine involve a mother who always did have impeccable timing.

* * *

I reopened the stall on the first market day of spring, in the same spot on Ashrow Lane where I'd hidden for twelve years behind harmless charms.

The stall looked the same. That was deliberate. Same awning, same crooked table leg, same jars of dried this and powdered that. The city needed some things to stay the same; it had had enough of things transforming. But the sign was new, painted by Wren in her precise cartographer's hand, and it read:

**GALLOWAY'S CHARMS — HONESTLY USELESS. NO OATHS ACCEPTED.**

Business, as I said, is booming. This morning I sold a courage charm (dried thyme, absolutely inert) to a young woman heading down the new stair for the first time, taking a clerk's position in Merrow's delegation. I sold a protection charm (chalk dust) to a grandmother whose granddaughter was planting a cutting-garden in the eastern galleries. And last, near closing, a boy came — straw-haired, red-faced, the same one from the winter — and bought a wedding charm for a baker's daughter, and when he asked me if it would work, I told him the truth:

"Not even slightly. But you don't need it. Look at your hands. They're already shaking. That's better than any charm."

He grinned like sunrise and ran.

I was stacking the jar lids when Tamsin came up the lane with dirt on her knees and lavender in her hair and a letter in her hand — a new one, fresh ink, the seal unbroken.

"From below," she said. "Merrow's hand. She says the first cutting took root."

I took the letter. I read it twice. Down in the dark, in moss-light, in ground that had never known a living bloom, a lavender cutting had put out its first white rootlet, thin as a thread, stubborn as hope, reaching.

"Well?" said Tamsin.

I folded the letter and tucked it inside my coat, over the pocket where a bone token used to hang — *PAID IN FULL*, filed away among the things I keep — and I looked up the lane toward the mountain that no longer owned anything of mine, and down the lane toward the sea of spring mud and market stalls and ordinary miraculous life, and I answered the only way a man answers when belonging is finally something he chose instead of something that was chosen for him.

"Well," I said, and rolled up my sleeves. "Get the spade."
