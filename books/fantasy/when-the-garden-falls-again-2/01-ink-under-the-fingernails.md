## 1. Ink Under the Fingernails

The trouble with ink, Mara Nakamura had decided by their nineteenth winter, was that it never fully came out. It lived under the fingernails, in the creases of knuckles, in the fine whorls of fingerprints, so that even after a day of scrubbing at the washbasin, a scribe's hands told on them. Anyone who looked closely could read a life in those stains. Thornfell brown for the ledger work. The violet-black of the abbot's correspondence. And once—only once—a smear of smuggler's red that Mara had scrubbed until their skin went raw and still hadn't entirely forgiven them.

They sat now in the copying room beneath the bell tower, where the light came slantwise through horn-paned windows and turned every dust mote into a small slow comet. Before them lay the tithe ledgers of the Thornfell valley, forty years of grain accounts in a hand so cramped it might have been written by a spider falling downstairs. Their task was to copy it all into fresh books, because Abbot Corren believed that a debt not written down twice was a debt only half-believed in.

Mara believed in debts. They had been raised on one.

*Seven years, four months, eleven days.* The arithmetic lived in their chest the way other people carried prayers. That was what remained of an indenture contracted before they could walk, signed by a mother who had signed many things in her life, most of them false names, and this one—this one true and ruinous. Forty years of service to the monastery in exchange for a debt no one would name aloud. Mara's mother had served nine of them before she died, and the contract, being a patient and hungry document, had passed to her child like a fever or a blessing, depending on which monk you asked.

Mara asked no one. Mara counted.

Their quill scratched. Outside, the first snow of the season came down over the garden in fat, unhurried flakes, softening the herb beds where Brother Ansel grew his medicinal thyme and pretending, as snow always did, that the world was kind.

"You're pressing too hard," said Brother Fenwick from the next table, without looking up. He was seventy if he was a day, half-blind, and could nonetheless detect the weight of a quill stroke from across a room, the way bats hear things that aren't sounds. "You'll wear through the vellum, and then it'll be your wages buying the replacement, and we both know how much those come to."

"Nothing," Mara said. "My wages come to nothing."

"They come to gratitude," Fenwick said comfortably. "Gratitude and roof and board."

"And board," Mara agreed, "which is mostly barley."

"Barley builds character."

"So does a rock. I'd rather have the bread."

Fenwick chuckled, a dry rustling sound like pages turning, and returned to his own copying, which was a psalter he had been working through for six years and would likely outlive. There were worse companions than Fenwick. He was one of perhaps three souls in Thornfell who had never once called Mara *the debt-child*, and one of two who knew about the counting, because he had caught them once, years ago, whispering numbers into their collar during evening prayer and had the decency to pretend he hadn't.

The morning wore on. Mara copied. Grain in, grain out. Tithes owed, tithes paid, tithes *deferred*—that word appearing with increasing frequency as the valley's harvests thinned, year over year, like a man going bald. The ledger didn't editorialize. Ledgers never did. But Mara had learned long ago to read between columns, and what lived between these columns was hunger, arriving on schedule, patient as the abbot himself.

It was near midday when the summons came.

Brother Tull appeared in the doorway with the particular expression of a man delivering news he hopes will reflect well on him regardless of how badly it goes over. "The Nakamura scribe," he announced, though there was plainly only one, "is wanted in the abbot's study. Immediately." Then, because Tull had never met a silence he didn't want to fill: "He has the new contracts."

Mara's quill stopped.

For a moment the copying room was very quiet. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere below, the kitchen boy was singing a song about a miller's wife that he absolutely should not have known, and someone was shouting at him to stop.

"The contracts," Mara repeated.

"For the spring allotments," Tull said. "And—" he hesitated, savoring it, this small power of being the messenger, "—I believe yours is among them. For renewal review."

Renewal review. Three words that should have meant nothing. Contracts of indenture at Thornfell ran in ten-year terms, and Mara's current term had four years left on it; there was nothing to renew, nothing to review, nothing to sign. Unless.

Unless the abbot had found a reason.

Mara set down the quill with great care, aligning it parallel to the ledger's edge, because a person's hands will do something while the rest of them is screaming, and better the something be tidy. "How kind of him," they said, "to think of me."

"He thinks of everyone," said Tull, with the serene confidence of a man who had never been thought of in his life. "That's what makes him an abbot."

* * *

The abbot's study occupied the warmest corner of the monastery, a fact Corren defended as practicality—he received visitors, he said, and shivering hosts insulted guests—and which everyone else understood as exactly what it was. A fire burned behind the iron grate. A map of the valley hung on one wall, its borders marked in the Crown's colors, blue and gold, the sigil of the realm stamped in wax at each corner like a seal on a jar. On the desk, square and waiting, lay three documents.

Two belonged to the valley. The third bore Mara's name.

"Nakamura." Corren did not look up from the paper he was reading. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a voice built for preaching and a face built for disapproval, though he deployed the latter sparingly, believing—as he often said—that fear was a tool best kept sharp by infrequent use. "Sit."

Mara sat. The chair was deliberately lower than the abbot's, an architectural sermon Mara had long since stopped resenting and simply noted, the way one notes weather.

"Do you know what this is?" Corren held up a page. It was a shipping manifest, or part of one, salvaged from a wrecked trader's cart that had come through the valley in autumn. "Found it in the toll records. Interesting thing about it—the freight list is written in cipher. Not merchant cipher. The other kind."

Mara kept their face pleasant and empty, the face of a person thinking about barley. "The other kind."

"Smugglers' cant," Corren said. "Which is why it came to me, and why it's been sitting useless in my drawer for two months. No one at Thornfell can read it." His eyes came up at last, pale and unhurried. "No one official, that is."

The fire popped. Snow fell. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse conducted its own small economy.

"I don't read cant," Mara said.

"No," Corren agreed mildly. "Of course not. Your mother certainly didn't, either. A woman who traveled the salt roads for eleven years, carrying goods whose origins she never once thought to ask about, and who never learned so much as a word of the trade language." He folded his hands. "It's remarkable, really. The whole family must have been very devout."

There it was. Mara had wondered for years when the abbot would finally reach for the thread, and here it was, dangling from his soft white fingers. He had never had proof. He had suspicion, and suspicion was a crop he farmed expertly, planting it in autumn and harvesting obedience all year.

"If you say so, Abbot."

"I do say so." He slid the manifest aside and drew the third document toward him, but did not push it across. "Here is my difficulty, Nakamura. The valley's tithes are short again. The Crown's assessors come in spring, and if the accounts are wrong—if anything in them is *obscured*—Thornfell pays penalties, and penalties mean I extend labor obligations across the valley, and no one thanks me for that, least of all the monks who'll be hauling their own water." He tapped the contract. "So I find myself needing a scribe I can trust absolutely. One whose interests are bound to mine. And I find myself looking at your file, and noticing that your term ends in four years, and thinking: four years is not very long. Four years is barely time to train a replacement. And your mother's original debt—well. The original sum was never discharged, was it? Only serviced."

The words landed the way cold water lands, all at once and everywhere. Mara had rehearsed this moment for nineteen years without knowing it. Every night of counting had been rehearsal. *Seven years, four months, eleven days.* A number that had become almost a friend, almost a horizon. And now the horizon was being moved, casually, the way a cruel child moves a finish line, and the cruelest part—the part Mara would carry to whatever grave they got—was that Corren wasn't even doing it out of malice. He was doing it out of bookkeeping.

"How much longer?" Mara asked. Their voice came out level. They were proud of that, later, in the way one is proud of surviving a fall.

"Ten years," Corren said. "A decade extension, in exchange for which I ask one small service. You learn to read that manifest. You tell me what's moving through my valley. And in return, when your term finally ends, you leave Thornfell with a character letter that will open any scriptorium in the province." He smiled, and it was nearly a kind smile, which made it worse. "Or refuse, and I send the question of your mother's old associations to the diocesan review, and we discover together whether the church's mercy extends to hereditary suspicion. I should warn you—it's been my experience that it extends precisely as far as it must, and not one inch further."

Outside the window, the snow kept falling on the garden, on the herb beds, on the low stone wall where Mara had sat as a child counting years instead of sheep. *Seven years, four months, eleven days.* Now: seventeen years, four months, and some number of days that Mara refused, right then, in front of God and the abbot and the mouse in the wall, to calculate.

Because that was the trap of it. If they refused to count, the debt couldn't grow. Some stubborn, childish corner of them insisted on this arithmetic of refusal, though they knew it for nonsense. Debts did not care whether you watched them.

"I'll need the manifest tonight," Mara said.

Corren's eyebrows rose, genuinely pleased. "Eager. Good. Diligence is its own reward."

"It isn't," Mara said. "But I'll take the manifest."

He laughed—a real laugh, brief and startling, the laugh of a man who occasionally surprised himself with humanity—and pushed the papers across. "Go on, then. And Nakamura? Whatever your mother taught you, I'm glad she taught you *something*. A mind shouldn't go to waste minding grain."

Mara rose, bowed the precise degree required, and walked out of the warm room into the cold corridor, the contract feeling heavier than paper had any right to feel, as though the ink itself had weight, as though the words were small stones sewn into the lining.

* * *

They did not go straight to their cell. They went, instead, up the stair to the bell tower's base, where a narrow window overlooked the garden, and stood with the manifest rolled inside their sleeve and let themselves, for exactly sixty heartbeats, hate everything.

Hate was a discipline too. Fenwick would have said so. You rationed it, took it in measured doses, or it spoiled on you and curdled into the kind of bitterness that showed in a scribe's hand, in the downstrokes, in the way letters leaned. Mara stood at the window and hated thoroughly and professionally: the abbot, the contract, the church that held the paper, the mother who had signed it, the grandfather who had presumably started it, the whole long chain of Nakamuras passing obligation down the line like a family heirloom nobody wanted and everybody inherited.

Sixty heartbeats. Then they unrolled the manifest against the windowsill and looked at it properly for the first time.

And felt the bottom drop out of the evening.

Because the cipher was not difficult. That was the first shock—that Corren had sat on this for two months believing it unreadable, when any salt-road courier could have cracked it between two cups of tea. Mara's mother had taught them the cant in fragments, disguised as games, as lullabies, as the sorts of nonsense rhymes mothers sing while braiding hair: *when the moon wears a ring, the toll-man knows nothing; when the crow flies low, take the river, not the road.* It had seemed like folklore. It had been a trade language, and Mara spoke it fluently, and had spent nineteen years making sure no one at Thornfell ever heard a syllable of it slip.

The second shock was the freight list itself.

Most of it was ordinary. Salt, yes, but also tin, dyewoods, candle-wax, the usual bloodless inventory of things people want and pay to have moved quietly. Mara decoded it line by line, translating in a shorthand only they could read, and was halfway down the page composing the report that would buy their freedom—or rather, that would fail to buy it, that would purchase instead another decade of barley and bell-calls—when they reached the final entry.

It was short. Three words in cant, followed by a symbol.

The three words translated as: *for the old debt.*

The symbol was a crown. Not drawn grandly—not the proud blue-and-gold stamp that hung on the abbot's map. This was quick, furtive, scratched by a hurried hand: a circlet with a hollow center. A crown with nothing inside it.

Mara stared at it for a long time. The snow thickened outside. Down in the garden, the last light failed, and the herb beds went blue and indistinct, and somewhere below a bell began to call the monks to evening prayer, and Mara Nakamura, keeper of forty years of other people's accounts, stood in the tower window with a dead smuggler's manifest in their hands and felt—for the first time in a life built on knowing exactly what was owed, to whom, and by when—the sensation of reading an entry in a ledger whose other party they could not name.

*The old debt.*

Whose? Owed by whom? Paid to what?

They copied the symbol into the margin of their own private notebook—the small greasy-leaved book where they kept their mother's rhymes, hidden beneath a floorboard, the one possession they owned outright—and told themselves it was nothing. A superstition. Smugglers were pious in odd ways; they chalked signs on doors, kissed coins, feared the dark under bridges. Every trade had its ghosts.

But that night, lying awake in their cell listening to the wind worry the shutters, Mara found the counting wouldn't come. Seven years, four months, eleven days—the number that had rocked them to sleep for nineteen years—slipped away like water, and in its place, over and over, unbidden, came three words in their mother's voice:

*The crown remembers.*

They did not know yet that the words were not theirs. They did not know that somewhere south, in the teeth of a storm, a stranger was already walking toward Thornfell with those same words dying in his mouth and a sealed map pressed against his ribs. They knew only that the ledger of their life had just shown them a column they had never seen before, and that the figures in it were large, and old, and written in a hand that looked unsettlingly like their own.
