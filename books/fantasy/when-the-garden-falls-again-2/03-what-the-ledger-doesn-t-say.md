## 3. What the Ledger Doesn't Say

The dead man's satchel smelled of tallow and wet wool, and it was heavier than a sleeping cat, which is how Mara measured most things.

They had taken it from the gatehouse the night before, when Brother Anselm — who was ninety if he was a day and blind in one eye, which made him the only person at Thornfell whose suspicions moved slower than Mara's hands — had asked them to carry the poor soul's belongings to the scriptorium for cataloguing. *Cataloguing* was the word Abbot Corren had used, spoken in that particular tone he reserved for words like *tithe* and *obedience*, which meant: bring me anything of value, and I will decide what God requires of the rest.

Mara carried it to the scriptorium, all right. They simply did not arrive there immediately.

Instead they sat in the drying loft above the stables, where the heat of the ovens below rose through the floorboards and where no one came looking for a scribe, because scribes were furniture. Useful furniture. Furniture with ink-stained fingers and a debt balance posted quarterly on the refectory door like a price tag on a cow.

Mara set the satchel on their knees and did not open it.

This was the thing about terror that no one told you: it looked exactly like procrastination. You sat very still. You told yourself you were being careful, methodical, prudent. You counted the stitches on your cuff. And underneath all that calm, your heart hammered out the only honest sentence in the room: *they will hang me for what I am about to do.*

The courier had died whispering to *them*. Not to the abbot, not to Brother Anselm, not to God. To Mara, a debt-bound copyist nobody would miss until supper. He had pressed a wax-sealed map into their hands with the last strength in his frostbitten fingers and said, *the crown remembers,* and then he had done the single most inconvenient thing a dying man could do.

He had stopped breathing before he could explain.

"Right," Mara said aloud, to the loft, to the mice, to no one. "Let's see what got you killed."

* * *

The satchel's contents, inventoried in Mara's neat copying hand on a scrap of blotting paper (habit was habit):

One wool cloak, ruined by blood, worse by snow. One knife, good steel, unremarkable. One purse containing eleven copper bits and a button. One button, brass, stamped with a sigil Mara did not recognize and then, after a moment, recognized so hard their stomach dropped through the floorboards into the stable yard.

A crown. Drawn hollow — an outline only, no jewels, no cross, no band across the brow. Just the shape of a crown with nothing inside it.

Their mother had worn that shape on a cord around her neck, back before the fever took her, back when she'd been alive enough to teach Mara the smugglers' cant under the blankets at night, giggling like a girl, saying *never let them hear you speak it, little bird, never let them know what we are.* Their mother had kissed that pendant every morning the way other mothers kissed prayer books.

Mara had thought it a keepsake. A pretty trinket from her wandering years.

They turned the button over three times, as though the fourth turn might reveal it was something innocent. It did not.

Beneath the knife and the purse: papers. Letters of passage, two of them, in names that were plainly false because both men were described as having *a countenance of honest bearing*, which no honest man has ever been accused of. A travel ration stub. And then — wrapped in oilcloth with the care people give to scripture or to money —

A ledger.

Not the map. The map was safe, rolled tight inside the false seam of Mara's own mattress pallet, hidden with the kind of cunning that would have made their mother proud and possibly a little tearful. This was something else: a small book, bound in grey leather gone soft as ears, its spine cracked, its pages crowded with a hand that changed across decades. Different writers. Many different writers. The same book passed along like a relay baton, or a hot coal.

The earliest entries were three hundred years old. Mara knew this the way they knew everything about paper — by the color of it, by the way old iron-gall ink went brown and ate slightly into the page, by the smell, which was dust and vinegar and time. Three centuries of handwriting. And Mara, who copied ledgers six days a week and dreamed in columns, felt the particular thrill of their trade rise up in them despite everything, the way a musician cannot help tapping a rhythm.

Then they read the first entry, and the thrill curdled.

*In the year of the Long Hunger's end, the Crown was sealed to the king's brow by consent of the Five Houses, that the realm might be fed and the winter answered. Let this record keep what the histories may not: the Crown eats. That is its purpose. That was always its price.*

Below it, five names. Five family names, written in a formal column, each followed by a mark like a tally stroke.

Nakamura was the third name down.

Mara read it four times. Their eyes kept sliding off it, the way eyes slide off a word you've misread, insisting on correcting it to something sensible. *Nakamura.* Their own name. Their mother's name, and her father's, and the name Mara had been told — gently, vaguely, over the years — was *just a foreign thing, little bird, from her grandmother's people, far away, nothing to fuss over.*

Three hundred years ago, someone named Nakamura had stood in a cold room with four other families and helped seal a hungry crown to a king's head.

And then, apparently, had written down the truth about it, in a book that had been smuggled and hidden and handed on ever since — which meant that somewhere behind Mara, up the long line of quiet, evasive, cant-speaking ancestors, someone had spent three hundred years making sure this ledger stayed moving, stayed secret, stayed *out of the hands of anyone who might ask questions.*

Questions like: why does our family talk in smugglers' cant? Why did Mother flinch at crown imagery? Why, when Mara once drew a crown on a practice sheet at age eight, had their mother gone white as whey and burned the page in the stove and held Mara very tightly and said *never draw that again*?

Mara had assumed, at eight, that mother had merely disliked royalty. Children assume things. Children assume their parents are ordinary.

They sat in the drying loft while the ovens ticked below, and the ground shifted under everything they believed about their inheritance. Not dramatically. Ground never shifts dramatically; that's why it kills you. It just quietly stops being where you put your foot.

"So," Mara said to the mice, who had gathered with the frank interest of creditors. "We're not the victims of an old injustice. We're the *co-signers*."

The mice declined to comment.

They read on. The entries grew sparser, then denser, generations of hands recording in cipher and cant — and here Mara's other inheritance, the forbidden language under the blankets, came up off the page and shook their hand like an old acquaintance. Smugglers' marks for *safe road*, *paid in full*, *do not trust the church*, *the bearer owes nothing*. And threaded through it all, recurring every few pages like a refrain, the hollow crown drawn small in the margin, sometimes crossed out, sometimes not.

The last entry was recent. Fresh enough that the ink hadn't fully browned. In a hurried, slanting hand:

*I have it. I have where it sleeps. If I don't come back, find a Nakamura. There's always a Nakamura. Tell them the debt comes due.*

Mara closed the ledger very carefully, the way you close a door on a room where something is crying.

"There's always a Nakamura," they repeated. Their voice sounded strange in the loft — smaller than usual, and rather more awake. "That's not a family. That's a *standing appointment*."

Somewhere below, the monastery bell rang terce, and Mara realized they had been sitting there for two hours, and that at some point the abbot would want his catalogue, and that Mara now possessed exactly none of the innocence required to produce one.

They wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth. They did not hide it with the map — no, their mother's lessons ran deeper than panic, and panic makes poor hiding spots. Instead they slid it into the binding of the great psalter awaiting repair on the workbench, flat against the boards beneath the loose leather, where it became, to any searching eye, part of a book too boring to steal. Mara had learned that trick from watching Brother Anselm hide his brandy for thirty years without detection.

Then they went down to face the day wearing the expression they did best: mild, dutiful, faintly bored.

It was getting harder to wear. It fit worse every year. Today it fit like a borrowed coat two sizes too small.

* * *

Abbot Corren burned the courier at noon, without rites.

Mara stood in the yard with the other indentured souls, hands folded, head bowed, watching the pyre take. Corren had made his reasoning plain that morning, in the chapter house, in the tone he used for decisions already made: a stranger, dead at their gate, carrying nothing of value (Mara had seen to the inventory personally, and reported it with a straight face), of no order, no household, no proof of baptism — *the Church does not waste prayers on strays, children, and neither should we.* Someone had murmured about charity. Corren had looked at him until the murmuring stopped.

So there was no psalm. No censer. No earth, even — ash to the wind, because a grave cost labor and the courier had earned nothing.

What Corren had not counted on — what Corren never counted on, because he believed furniture had no interior life — was that among the bowed heads stood the finest copyist in three counties, fluent in two scripts and one forbidden cant, who had spent the whole service composing, in perfect liturgical Latin, the funeral office the man deserved. *Requiem aeternam dona ei, Domine.* Mara said it silently, completely, all seven movements, timing each line to the fall of sparks. It was the only rite available to them, and they gave it lavishly. Somewhere in the smoke, Mara liked to think, a dead smuggler who spoke cant was laughing at the joke of receiving full Catholic honors from a thief of a scribe, delivered free of charge.

Yara — no. Not Yara. The sister hadn't come. But word of her had: a woman asking after her brother at the tollhouses east of the pass, described by a peddler at breakfast as *armed, furious, and asking particularly after anyone from Thornfell.* Mara had gone very still over their porridge and decided, in the way one decides these things, not to think about that today either.

Today's list of things not to think about was growing impressively long. Mara reviewed it: the map under the pallet. The ledger in the psalter. The button in their sleeve, because they could not explain why they'd kept it, except that their mother had kissed that shape every morning of her life and Mara was apparently sentimental at the worst possible times. The sister hunting them. The words *the crown remembers*, which had stopped sounding like a dying man's delirium sometime around dawn and started sounding like a warning label.

And the newest entry, added just now, standing in the smoke: *my family helped forge the thing that killed this man. My family has been running from the receipt for three hundred years.*

"You're frowning, Nakamura." Brother Anselm, beside them, squinting at the fire with his one good eye. He was the closest thing to a friend the monastery permitted, which is to say he occasionally shared his brandy and never asked questions, mostly because he forgot them. "Bad business, burning a man like driftwood. Even a stray deserves a word or two."

"Yes," Mara said. "I was just thinking that."

Anselm patted their shoulder with a hand like a dried cod. "You're a decent one. Don't let the abbot grind that out of you. Debt or no debt." He shuffled off toward the warmth of the kitchen, and Mara watched him go and felt the familiar arithmetic rise in them — nine more years on the indenture, maybe ten if Corren had his way, a lifetime of copying other people's accounts while their own family's true account sat hidden in a psalter, accruing interest.

Except.

Except the ledger's last line had said otherwise, hadn't it. *The debt comes due.* Debts due were debts payable. Debts payable meant an end — one way or another, an end. For the first time in Mara's adult life, the arithmetic had a bottom to it. It was a frightening bottom. It might be a gallows-shaped bottom. But it was *theirs*, in a way the indenture had never been, and there was something almost giddy in that, the way standing at a cliff edge is almost flying.

The pyre collapsed inward with a sigh of sparks. The abbot turned back toward the chapter house, satisfied, a man who had tidied a problem away. Behind him, at the gate, the snow had drifted high against the posts, and Mara noticed — because noticing was the trade they'd actually been born to — fresh hoofprints cutting through it. Several horses. Arrived within the hour. Riders who had not yet shown themselves.

Riders who arrived the morning after a stranger died at a remote mountain gate, in a season when no honest traveler rode at all.

Brother Anselm reappeared at the gate, squinting, then turned and bustled back across the yard with unusual speed, catching Mara's sleeve. "Visitors," he breathed. "Church colors. Grey and silver, and the leader's got a look like a bill collector." He lowered his voice further, which for Anselm was a heroic act. "Asking for the abbot by name. Asking, my lad, about *you*."

The ground shifted again. Mara kept their face mild, dutiful, faintly bored — but inside, somewhere beneath the terror, a small clear voice that sounded remarkably like their mother said: *little bird. Time to fly.*

"I expect they want help with their correspondence," Mara said pleasantly. "Everyone does. I'm very good."

But they were already, in their mind, counting the hours until dark, and measuring the passes, and remembering — with a gratitude that felt almost like grief — every secret road their mother had ever taught them.

There was always a Nakamura. And this one, it seemed, was about to become very difficult to find.
