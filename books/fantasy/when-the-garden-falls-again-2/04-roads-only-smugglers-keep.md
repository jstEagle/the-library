## 4. Roads Only Smugglers Keep

The thing about leaving a place you've lived for eleven years, Mara discovered in the hour before dawn, was that it took almost no time at all.

They had imagined this departure a hundred times—usually during Abbot Corren's sermons, usually with dramatic music that existed only in their head. In the imagining there was always a moment of standing at the gate, looking back at the monastery's crooked bell tower, feeling something twist. What they had never imagined was the packing. Eleven years of indenture fit into one canvas satchel: two shirts, a spare pair of socks, the good penknife with the bone handle, a stub of tallow candle, and three books lifted from the scriptorium that would be missed by absolutely no one because they were inventories of dried fish from 211.

And the map. Wrapped in oilcloth, sewn flat against their ribs where a satchel-strap could plausibly explain any lump.

Mara paused once at the dormitory door, hand on the latch, and listened to Brother Anselm snore in the particular wet rhythm that meant he'd been at the plum wine again. Somewhere below, the kitchen fire was banked for the night. Sister Odile would rise in an hour and find her flour bin undisturbed, which was the only apology Mara could afford to leave her: they had not stolen so much as a crust on the way out. It seemed important, somehow, to leave owing nothing but the debt everyone already knew about.

*Eleven more years,* Corren had said yesterday, smiling his smile that was all ledger and no face. *Perhaps fourteen, given the irregularities.*

Irregularities meaning the courier. Meaning the questions Mara hadn't answered quickly enough. Meaning, though Corren didn't know it yet, the map sewn against Mara's ribs that made every extra year of indenture not just cruel but dangerous—for everyone who stayed.

So. The window, then the wall, then the goat path behind the tannery sheds, which smelled exactly as bad as it always had and which Mara had climbed perhaps forty times in secret and never once in daylight.

Their mother had taught them this path. That was the thought that caught in their chest like a fishbone as they dropped from the wall into the snow. Not the abbot, not the brothers. Their mother, who had come to Thornfell to deliver candles and stayed three days too long, walking the walls with a small child on her hip, pointing out loose stones and dead ground and saying, in the low sing-song cant of the road families, *remember this one, little ledger, this is how you get out when you need to.*

Mara had assumed she meant smuggling. They had been eleven when she died; they had spent eleven years telling themselves she'd meant something harmless.

The snow took their footprints behind them almost faster than they could make them. Good. That was the first lesson of the road, before locks and lies and knots: let the weather work for you.

* * *

By midmorning Mara had reached the treeline above the monastery and allowed themselves the luxury of looking back.

Thornfell sat in its bowl of white like something abandoned—a scatter of grey roofs, smoke rising thin and dutiful from the kitchen chimney, the bell tower leaning east as it had leaned for as long as anyone remembered. From here you couldn't see the scriptorium windows where Mara had spent eleven years copying other people's numbers. You couldn't see the debt ledgers with their neat columns of years owed. You couldn't see anything of them at all, really. Just one more building in the snow.

"Good," Mara said out loud, because the wind was loud enough that saying things out loud felt permitted, and because if they didn't say it they might feel something else instead.

The map wanted them south and west, toward the Salt Road and the passes beyond it—the smugglers' roads, the ones that weren't on any survey the crown had ever paid for. Mara knew the first leg without consulting it, which frightened them more than the inquisitors did. They knew the way a person knows their own handwriting. Left at the split oak. Down through the hollow where the ice never thickened because a spring kept the stone weeping. Across the frozen stream at the flat rock their mother had called the Ferryman's Table, because he ate there, she'd said, and you should never interrupt a god at dinner.

Mara crossed the stream and did not look down at the black water sliding under the ice, because their mother had also taught them that some superstitions were load-bearing.

It should have been terrifying. It was, in fact, terrifying. But underneath the terror, in a small locked room at the bottom of their chest, something else was happening—something that felt appallingly like joy. Every mile put another ledger between them and Corren's ink-stained arithmetic. Every familiar landmark rose up out of memory exactly where it belonged, and each one was a small vindication: *you kept it, you kept all of it, the maps your mother drew in your head are still accurate.* Mara had spent eleven years being told they were slow, dutiful, unremarkable. It turned out they were none of those things. It turned out they were fluent in a language the monastery had never known they spoke, carrying a treasure worth killing for, moving through country they loved with the specific competence of someone finally using both hands.

Mara laughed once, breathless, halfway up the switchback above the Ferryman's Table—a real laugh, startled out of them—and then immediately clapped a mitten over their own mouth like a fool, because laughing loudly in open country was how people got killed, according to their mother, rule four, right after *never trust a man whose boots are newer than his coat.*

Rule five, Mara remembered, cresting the ridge, was *the roads remember you even when nobody else does.*

They were about to learn what she'd meant by that.

* * *

The Salt Stair was less a stair than a rumor of one: a seam of old quarry steps cut into the cliffside above the salt flats, half-collapsed, entirely invisible unless you already knew to look for the iron ring bolted into the third landing. Caravans had used it for two hundred years to move salt without paying the toll at Grayford Bridge. The crown had surveyed the cliff twice and found nothing both times, which told you everything you needed to know about surveys.

Mara reached the top landing by late afternoon, legs burning, and stopped to eat the heel of bread they'd brought, sitting with their back against the cliff where the wind couldn't find them. Below, the salt flats stretched white and pink in the failing light, beautiful in the way that places are beautiful when they want to kill you. The crust crackled and groaned as the cold deepened, a sound like distant applause.

That was when they saw the riders.

Three of them, far off across the flats, coming up the old toll road at a pace that said *not lost*. Grey cloaks. The lead rider wore something pale at the shoulder—a badge or a baldric—and even at that distance Mara's stomach performed a maneuver they hadn't known a stomach could do.

Inquisitors. Already. A day and a half ahead of any reasonable estimate, which meant someone at the monastery had sent word ahead, which meant Corren, which meant Mara owed that man one final entry in the mental ledger: *betrayal, premeditated, one unit.*

They stuffed the bread in their mouth and moved.

Down the Stair went Mara, fast and quiet, testing each step with their weight before committing it, exactly as taught. The iron ring on the third landing opened a shelf-tunnel behind the fallen slab—two hundred years of smuggler maintenance had kept it clear, and apparently the smugglers' ghosts were still doing upkeep—and Mara slipped inside, dragging the slab shut behind them with a soft grinding kiss of stone on stone.

Darkness. Cold that bit through wool. And through a crack in the slab, a knife-thin line of the flats below, where the three riders were now close enough to resolve into detail.

The lead rider dismounted at the base of the Stair. She was tall, narrow, moved like someone who had studied how bodies worked mostly in order to predict their failures. She stood at the foot of the old steps and looked up at them for a long time—not searching, exactly. Reading. The way Mara read a column of figures and knew which number had been fudged.

Then she said, clearly enough to carry up the cliff:

"I know you're not here yet. But you'll come down this way. Roads like this are habits, and habits are just debts the body pays." She turned her gloved hand over, examining it idly. "My name is Serel Vane. When you're ready to stop running, I pay better than the people chasing you."

Mara, flattened in the dark with the map pressing cold against their ribs, held their breath until their vision sparked.

The woman waited a while longer. Then she remounted, and the three riders continued along the flats—not away, but along, paralleling the Stair's descent, patient as bookkeeping.

Mara exhaled very slowly and did not move for the better part of an hour, by which time the dark was total and the temperature inside the tunnel had dropped to the category of *philosophical problem.*

Here is what Mara noticed, huddled in that cold, and here is why it mattered: Serel Vane had spoken to the empty air with complete confidence, the way you speak to someone you have already met. Not *I know someone came this way.* *You'll come down this way.* As if Mara were a figure in a ledger she'd already balanced.

Their mother's voice, unhelpfully, chose that moment: *when someone reads you that well, little ledger, it's either love or hunting, and you can tell which by whether they bring food.*

Serel Vane had brought no food. Noted.

* * *

Mara came off the Stair after midnight, wide loops north of the riders' campfire, and put six miles between herself and them before dawn, mostly by trusting the ice over the fen where a heavier body would have gone through. Twice they doubled back along their own trail and broke it with detours over bare stone. Once they waded a quarter-mile up a frozen creek bed for no reason except that their mother had once done it while telling a story about a tax collector, and the story had ended badly for the tax collector, and Mara had absorbed the moral through sheer repetition.

It worked. By sunrise the inquisitors' fire was a smudge behind them, and the map agreed with the route their feet had chosen, and for one whole hour Mara felt invincible.

Then the hour passed, as hours do, and the feeling curdled.

Because here was the thing about being good at disappearing that nobody warned you: disappearing works. Mara stood on a ridge overlooking the valley where the road forked toward the Gullet pass, and they looked at the empty white miles ahead—no monastery, no abbot, no inquisitors in sight, no witnesses, no one who knew their name—and they felt the exhilaration drain out of them like heat from a cooling stone, and what pooled in its place was this:

Nobody in the world knew where they were.

Not the abbot, who had only ever known *where* to find them, never *who*. Not Sister Odile, who liked them but would grieve them briefly and practically. Not Brother Anselm, asleep and snoring and irrelevant. Their mother was nine years dead. Their father had never been located, which their mother had always said with such relish that Mara suspected it was a compliment. There was Yara Ferro, the courier's sister, somewhere behind them with grief and a grudge—but she didn't know them either. Nobody did. Mara Nakamura, twenty-three years old, was a set of skills wrapped around a secret, moving through snow, and if they lay down in the next drift and did not get up, the sum total of the world's inconvenience would be one missing scribe, easily written off.

"That's the trade," Mara said to the wind, conversationally. "That's what the road charges."

Their mother had never said that part. Or maybe she had, and eleven-year-old Mara had filed it under *things mothers say*, along with *never sleep in a room with one exit* and *always know which way the river runs.*

The river ran east, Mara noted, out of habit, and followed the fork toward the pass.

* * *

Two days later, hungry and smelling powerfully of woodsmoke and desperation, Mara traded three pages of forged travel writs to a carter named Doss in exchange for passage, hot stew, and the use of his cart's blind spot among the salt barrels.

Doss was a road-family man himself—third generation, by the knot he tied on his left rein, which their mother had named the Liar's Bowline—and he asked no questions, which was its own kind of courtesy. He did talk constantly, which was a different kind of courtesy, the kind that fills silence so you don't have to.

"—so the inquisitor's been asking at every wayhouse from Grayford to the flats," he said, around a mouthful of stew, as the cart jolted south. "Grey cloak, young, polite in a way that makes your skin crawl. Asking after a scribe." He glanced sideways. "Said the scribe was 'competent.' Which from that sort means *dangerous*, I'd think."

"What did you tell her?" Mara asked, in a voice pitched to sound bored.

"Told her I don't read, wouldn't know a scribe from a scarecrow." Doss grinned, gap-toothed. "Road families don't turn each other out. Even strangers wearing the right knots." He tapped the Liar's Bowline on his reins, then nodded at Mara's sleeve, where—Mara realized with a lurch—they had absently tied the same knot around a button thread, hours ago, without noticing. Muscle memory. Inheritance. "Your ma teach you that?"

"My mother taught me a lot of things."

"Mm." Doss looked ahead at the road, and his grin softened into something older. "Mine too. Funny, isn't it. Everything they gave us fits in a pocket, and it weighs more than the cart."

They traveled together through the afternoon, and it helped—it genuinely helped, the warmth of shared stew and shared grammar, the relief of being *read* correctly by a stranger. For a few miles Mara almost forgot the cold thing that had opened in their chest on the ridge. Almost forgot that belonging-by-knotwork wasn't the same as being known.

At the crossroads below the Gullet's mouth, Doss turned east with his salt and left Mara standing alone with the pass yawning ahead of them like a throat.

"Hey," he called back, one last gift. "The inquisitor. Vane. Word on the road is she doesn't lie. Whatever she offers you, it'll be real. That's what makes her worse than the ones who lie."

Then the cart creaked away, and the snow began again, and Mara turned to face the mountain.

The Gullet Pass swallowed travelers—that much even monastery gossip had confirmed. It was two days of narrows and hanging slopes, and the map's margin promised a shrine partway in, marked in faded brown ink with a sigil Mara now recognized from records three centuries old. The sigil beside the name *Nakamura.*

Family, then. Ghosts, then. Company of a sort.

Mara pulled their collar up, checked the map's seal one last time against their ribs, and started walking.

Behind them, far back down the white road, a single grey rider crested the hill and did not follow—only watched, unhurried, the way a creditor watches a debtor walk toward the last of their assets.

Mara didn't see her. But the road remembered, and it passed the message along, and by the time the pass closed around them like a fist, the feeling of being watched had settled over their shoulders like a second cloak—one that fit perfectly, and weighed nothing, and could not be taken off.
