## 5. The Sister with the Broken Compass

Yara Ferro had been waiting three days in the Hollow Stag waystation, and she had learned three things about the place: the stew was mostly turnip, the innkeeper watered the ale twice, and nobody who came down from the Salt Stair looked happy about it.

She sat in the corner with her back to the wall and her broken compass on the table where she could see it. The needle spun when it wanted to, drifted when it didn't, and pointed true only once a day at dawn, as if even the compass needed to sleep before it could be honest. Tam had won it off a river pilot in a card game neither of them should have been playing, and he'd carried it for six years, and now it was hers because everything he'd owned was hers, and she would have traded all of it — the compass, his coat, the stupid lucky dice in his pocket — to have him back complaining about turnip stew.

The courier's satchel had come back to her instead of him. That was how she'd known. The monastery sent effects home with a formality note, *died of exposure on the road, no rites available*, and Yara had read the note four times and then burned it, because *no rites available* was what you wrote when you didn't want to say what actually happened. Tam hadn't died of exposure. Tam had grown up sleeping in snowdrifts for fun. Tam had once walked forty miles through a whiteout to deliver a wedding invitation because the bride paid double.

He'd died carrying something, and someone had taken it from him, and that someone had come down off the mountain two days behind him wearing a scribe's cloak and looking like a startled cat.

Yara knew this because the innkeeper talked. Innkeepers always talked, especially to a woman who bought ale and asked friendly questions and left coins on the table like she wasn't counting them. A scribe from Thornfell, traveling alone, heading south on the smuggler roads — not the pilgrim road, not the king's road, but the roads Yara's family had run for three generations. Scribes did not travel smuggler roads. Scribes did not know smuggler roads existed.

So she waited, and she sharpened her knife, and she watched the door.

* * *

Mara Nakamura came in an hour past dusk, shaking snow from their hood, and Yara felt something twist in her chest that she refused to name, because the last time she'd seen that face it had been pressed against Tam's shoulder in a memory she'd invented and never lived — the two of them laughing over some joke about ink, some future she'd built out of nothing because grief builds houses out of anything.

They looked worse than a startled cat. They looked like someone who hadn't slept in four days and had recently discovered their entire life was a lie, which, as it happened, they were.

Yara stood up. The chair scraped. The room went quiet the way rooms do when a knife comes into the conversation.

"Scribe," she said.

Mara turned. Their eyes went to the knife, then to her face, and Yara watched recognition arrive like cold water — watched them understand exactly who she was, because she had Tam's jaw and Tam's stubborn chin and, apparently, Tam's way of making people want to be somewhere else.

"You're—" Mara started.

"The sister. Yes." She crossed the room slowly, letting them count her steps. "Yara Ferro. You have my brother's map."

"I don't—"

"Don't." She said it softly, which was worse than shouting. "I've been sitting here for three days eating turnips and rehearsing this, and I'll tell you what I've learned: I don't have the patience for the part where you pretend. So we skip it. You have the map. My brother bled out at your gate getting it to you, and you took it, and now you're running down roads only my family knows, which means either you're the luckiest fool alive or somebody taught you, and I'd very much like to hear which."

Mara's hand had drifted toward their belt, toward nothing, because scribes didn't carry knives — or so Yara had assumed until she saw the small blade there, well-used, held the way you hold a tool you've actually worked with. Interesting. Everything about this scribe was interesting, and Yara hated that, because interesting was the first step toward forgiving, and she was not ready to forgive anyone.

"He chose to give it to me," Mara said quietly. "I told him not to. I said take it to the abbot, take it to anyone else. He grabbed my wrist hard enough to bruise and he said—" their voice caught, just slightly, "—he said *the crown remembers*. And then he died. I didn't kill him. Whatever killed him was already inside him when he got to the gate."

"Convenient."

"Yes," Mara said. "It is convenient. It's also true, and I notice you haven't asked what killed him, which tells me you already suspect, which tells me he told you more than he told me, which means you know enough to be scared and you're standing here anyway." They tilted their head. "That's brave or stupid. In my experience it's usually both."

Yara's knife hand tightened. This was the problem with scribes — they *talked*, they took your anger and turned it into a conversation, and conversations were how you ended up agreeing to things.

"Give me the map," she said, "and I'll let you walk away."

"No."

The word landed in the quiet room like a dropped plate. Even the innkeeper, pretending very hard to wipe a clean mug, stopped wiping.

"No?" Yara laughed, and it came out ugly. "You're a scribe. You copy other people's words for a living. You think you can outrun me on my own roads?"

"I think," Mara said, "that your brother gave his life to put that map in specific hands, and I think those hands were mine for a reason I don't understand yet, and I think if I give it to you, you'll go after the Crown alone, out of grief, and die the same way he did — except nobody will find you, because you won't be dying at a gate with witnesses. You'll be dying in a ditch." They paused. "And I think you know all of that, and it's why you're talking to me instead of just taking it, because you could have taken it. You've had three days and a knife. You waited because some part of you wants what I want."

"And what's that?"

"To know why."

The worst part — the part Yara would replay for weeks, turning it over like a stone with something underneath — was that the scribe was right. She could have robbed them in their sleep. She'd considered it. But Tam had pressed that map into a stranger's hands with his last breath, and Yara Ferro did not throw away her brother's last breath just because it hurt.

She threw the punch instead.

It was, she'd admit later, a fair fight for about ten seconds. Mara blocked the first swing — actually blocked it, elbow up, weight low, someone had taught them — and got a grip on her wrist, and for one absurd moment they were locked together in the middle of the waystation like dancers who hated the music. Then Yara got her knee into the exchange and Mara went over a stool, and the stool went over the table, and the innkeeper shouted something about the deposit, and then the window exploded.

Not exploded, exactly. It burst inward with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, and the night poured in full of white noise and moving stone, and Yara had half a second to think *rockslide, the thaw, the scree above the stable roof* before the world decided the argument was over.

* * *

They dug themselves out of the wreckage of the common room's east wall at nearly midnight, coughing, gray with dust, side by side in the rubble like two dolls dropped by the same careless child.

The rockslide had missed killing them by the width of a prayer. It had not missed the stable. Half the waystation's roof was buried; the scree slope above glinted in the moonlight, still settling, still deciding whether it was finished. Somewhere under the rubble, the innkeeper's dog was barking with the indignant rhythm of a creature whose house had betrayed it.

"Your fault," Yara croaked.

"Excuse me?"

"You. If you hadn't made me angry enough to hit you, we'd have been at the table. Away from the window."

"If I hadn't made you angry, you'd have stabbed me at the table," Mara said, spitting dust. "Statistically, the window saved one of us. Possibly both. I'd have to check the ledgers."

Despite everything — despite the bruise blooming on her knuckles and her brother dead in a stranger's grave and three days of turnip stew — Yara felt something crack open in her chest that might, under different circumstances, have been a laugh. She smothered it immediately. There would be time for laughing later, or there wouldn't, but not now, not yet, not while the wound was still sharp enough to cut anyone who touched it.

They worked in silence after that, hauling beams, digging out the innkeeper (alive, furious, already calculating damages), freeing the dog (unharmed, immediately forgiving). The road south was buried. The road north was buried. The pass itself groaned overhead every few minutes like an old man shifting in bed, dropping pebbles as warnings.

"We can't travel tonight," Mara said finally. "Maybe not tomorrow. That slope needs a day to finish falling."

"So we wait. Separately."

"In a building with half a roof. In winter." Mara gestured at the ruined common room, where the fire had survived, absurdly, in its stone hearth, throwing light across the wreckage. "Or we wait together, near the fire, and you keep the knife where I can see it, and we talk. Not about forgiveness. About Tam."

Hearing his name in a stranger's mouth did something to her that she hadn't prepared for. She'd braced for the fight. She hadn't braced for the *name*.

"What do you want to know?"

"Everything. He was my responsibility for eleven minutes and I never even learned his favorite color."

"Green," Yara said, before she could stop herself. "Stupid answer for a courier. Green like spring, he said, so people remembered couriers brought spring with them." Her throat closed. She sat down hard on an overturned bench, and after a moment, Mara sat too, at the far end, careful as a cat near a strange dog.

"He talked the whole time," she said. "Even dying, I bet. Did he talk?"

"He said the crown remembers. He said I had to be the one. He apologized." Mara stared into the fire. "To me. As if it were his fault he was dying at my feet."

"That sounds like him." Yara pulled the broken compass from her pocket and set it on the bench between them, because her hands needed something to do and because — though she'd have denied it under torture — she wanted to see what the scribe made of it. "He won this cheating at cards. Needle's cracked. Points true once a day, at dawn, and lies the rest of the time. He used to say it was honest the same way people are — mostly wrong, right when it matters, if you're patient enough to catch it."

Mara looked at the compass for a long moment. "My mother taught me these roads," they said quietly. "The ones you were surprised I knew. She taught me the cant, the passes, the signs. She never said why a temple scribe's wife would need smuggler roads. I thought she was just... careful. Now I think she was preparing me for something, and I hate her a little for it, and I miss her completely, both at once. Is that what it's like? Having a dead person in your chest?"

Yara stared at the fire. The dog had come over and put its head on her knee, traitorously affectionate, warm as a coal.

"Yeah," she said. "That's what it's like."

Above them, the mountain settled with a long grinding sigh, and snow began to sift down through the broken roof, catching the firelight, falling slow and almost gentle onto the wreckage of the room. Neither of them moved to fix the roof. Neither of them spoke for a while.

When Yara finally did speak, her voice was rougher than she meant it to be.

"Dawn's in five hours. The compass points true at dawn. Where are you going, scribe?"

Mara reached into their coat and drew out the wax-sealed map — Tam's map, Tam's seal, the thing Yara had crossed a kingdom to take — and set it on the bench between them, next to the compass, without letting go of it entirely.

"South," they said. "Through the Gullet. There's a shrine on the way. I found our name in the courier's records, Yara. *Nakamura.* Inked beside the Crown's sigil, three hundred years old. Your brother didn't die carrying a secret. He died carrying my family's debt. And I need to know what it was."

Yara looked at the map. At the compass beside it, needle drifting, waiting for morning to be honest. At the scribe who had her brother's last breath in their keeping and her family's roads in their blood.

"One condition," she said. "When we find whoever killed him — and we will find them — I get to be in the room."

Mara nodded slowly. "Agreed. One condition of mine. When I do something reckless and probably fatal, you talk me out of it."

"That's going to be a full-time position."

"I pay in stolen monastery bread."

Against every plan she'd made and every vow she'd sworn over Tam's empty grave, Yara laughed — a short, cracked, unwilling sound, gone as fast as it came, but real. She picked up the compass and closed her fist around it.

"Dawn," she said. "Then we walk."

And high above the ruined waystation, on the scree slope, something shifted in the dark — a figure where no figure should be, watching the firelight through the broken roof, patient as winter, before withdrawing silently into the pass that ate travelers whole.
