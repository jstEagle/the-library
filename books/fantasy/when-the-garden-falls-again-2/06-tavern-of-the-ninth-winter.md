## 6. Tavern of the Ninth Winter

The den had no name on any map worth trusting, which was how Mara knew it would be safe. Their mother had taught them that much before the debt took her: *the places with signs are for people who want to be found.*

It sat in a fold of the Greymantle hills like something the landscape had swallowed and half-digested — a low stone building with a turf roof gone to seed, its door hidden behind a woodpile that swung aside when you knocked in the rhythm of three short, one long, one short. Yara had known it. Of course she had. Tam had drunk here, smuggled through here, probably been born in the back room if his sister was to be believed.

"Rule one," Yara muttered as the door swung open onto warmth and lamplight and the smell of stewed mutton and pipe smoke. "Don't stare at anyone."

"Rule two," said a voice from inside, "is don't bring strangers who stare at everyone."

Mara blinked. The room beyond was long and low, lit by oil lamps hung from antlers, crowded with perhaps thirty people who all stopped talking at once in the way of people who were very practiced at stopping talking. A woman behind the bar — broad, gray-streaked, with forearms like mooring rope — looked them over with the unhurried appraisal of someone pricing a horse.

"He's not staring," Yara said. "He's counting exits. It's a scribe thing."

"It's a survivor thing," Mara said quietly, and the woman behind the bar laughed, a great rolling sound, and jerked her thumb toward two stools at the end of the bar.

"Sit, then, counter-of-exits. You'll take stew whether you want it or not. Nobody argues with Bess about stew. It's the only law left in these hills that works."

They sat. The talking resumed around them, lower now, watchful. The stew arrived in clay bowls without being ordered, along with bread that was dark and dense and honest — nothing like monastery bread, which was white and soft and tasted of submission. Mara ate too fast and was embarrassed by it and kept eating anyway. Three days of hard walking since the waystation. Four days since a dying man had pressed a sealed map into their hands and died saying *the crown remembers*.

Yara ate like someone performing an obligation to her body while her mind did other work. She'd barely spoken on the road. When she did speak, it was mostly corrections — *you're holding the compass wrong, walk where I walk, stop humming.* But she'd also, without comment, shifted her bedroll between Mara and the open pass both nights, and Mara had noticed, and had wisely said nothing about noticing.

"The map," Mara said, under the noise. "I want to ask about the seal."

"I know. You've been doing that thing with your fingers since the Salt Stair. Like you're copying invisible words." Yara tore her bread. "Ask Old Wren. He's the one who'd know. Corner table, the one asleep in his cup."

"Is he actually asleep?"

"No idea. Nobody is ever sure with Wren. That's rather the point of him."

***

Old Wren turned out to be a small man folded into a large chair like a letter crammed into the wrong envelope, with a beard that had gone past white into some fourth color entirely, and hands spotted and knuckled like the roots of an old tree. He smelled of lamp oil and dried fish. His eyes, when they opened, were quick and yellowish and missed nothing, which contradicted the sleeping entirely.

"Seal," he repeated, turning the broken wax fragment over in his palm. Mara had pried it loose from the map's wrapping at the waystation, keeping the map itself hidden — but the seal they'd carried separately, wrapped in a sock, because their instincts told them the seal mattered more than the map. Instincts trained by a mother who'd taught them cant under the covers with a candle stub.

Wren held the fragment up to the lamp. His eyebrows climbed. Then, slowly, they came back down, and something in his face closed like a shop shutter.

"Where did you get this?"

"A dead man gave it to me."

"Dead men give away a lot," Wren said. "Mostly regrets." He turned the fragment again. The wax showed a circle broken by a vertical line — a crown seen edge-on, or a keyhole, depending on the light. "You know what this is?"

"A sigil. Old. Pre-dynastic, maybe. It's not in any heraldic index I've studied."

"You've studied heraldic indexes." He sounded delighted. "A scribe. Thornfell, by the ink stains. They still indenture children up there? Still call it education?" He didn't wait for an answer. "This isn't a sigil, boy. It's a signature. There's a difference. A sigil says *this house stands*. A signature says *this hand was here*. And this hand signed something three hundred years ago that nobody's supposed to remember signing."

Around them the tavern roared on — dice games, arguments about tolls, a fiddler tuning and retuning without ever committing to a song. Wren leaned close, and his smell intensified, and Mara found they didn't mind.

"Buy me a drink," he said, "and I'll tell you a story. Not the story they tell in the capital with trumpets. The true one. It costs a drink because truth should cost something, or everyone would carry it."

Mara signaled Bess. Wren got a dark spirit in a glass so cloudy it might have been a relic. He drank half of it in one go, sighed like a man setting down a heavy load, and began.

***

"Three hundred years ago there was a famine. Not a bad harvest — a famine. Nine years of it. The kind where the histories get embarrassed and start using words like 'the Troubles' so they don't have to write down what people ate." He swirled his drink. "The kingdom then wasn't this kingdom. Smaller. Colder. Ruled from a hall in the mountains by a king named Aldous — first of that name, though he didn't know he'd be starting a tradition of them.

"And Aldous had a problem bigger than hunger. Because hungry people don't just die, boy. Hungry people *decide things*. They decide the nobles have too full a larder. They decide the gods are deaf. They decide, sometimes, that crowns come off heads easier than heads come off shoulders."

"So he went looking for a solution," Mara said.

"He went looking for a *smith*." Wren held up a spotted finger. "And here's where your family comes in, though you haven't asked yet, and I can see you dying to ask, and I'm going to make you wait, because I'm old and this is my only remaining pleasure."

Yara snorted into her own drink. She'd drifted over during the story and now stood with her arms crossed, pretending indifference, fooling no one.

"The smith he found was a woman of the Nakamura line. Binders, they were called, back then — a trade, like cooper or chandler. People who could bind a thing to a thing. A spirit to a stone. A promise to a bloodline. Mostly small work. Love-charms that actually worked, which is why binders were always either wealthy or dead." Wren smiled with too few teeth. "She was both, eventually.

"Aldous came to her and said: make me a crown that will end the famine. And she said — and this part is written nowhere, but we keep it, we smugglers, we fence-sitters, we people who live off what falls off carts — she said, *a crown cannot make wheat grow.* And he said, *then make me one that makes people endure until it does.*"

The fiddler finally committed to a song, something slow and mournful. Wren waited out the first verse, drinking.

"So she forged it. Hollow, they called it afterward, though it wasn't hollow in the way you're thinking. Hollow like a bell is hollow. Made to be rung. She bound into it every enduring thing she knew — patience, endurance, the long memory of stone, the stubbornness of roots under snow. And it worked. Oh, it worked. The famine broke two years later — weather, mostly, though the priests credited the crown, and the crown never corrected them, because crowns can't talk.

"But here's the price, boy, and lean in, because this is the whole of it." Wren's voice dropped, and Mara found themselves leaning anyway. "A binding needs feeding. Everything bound wants to unbind; that's the first law. So she built the feed into the wearing. The crown draws from the head that bears it. Memory. Years. Self. Slowly, gently, so gently the wearer never feels it happening — it takes what it needs to stay bound, and it gives back endurance, and the king endures, and the kingdom endures, and everyone agrees it's a fair trade right up until the king forgets his mother's face and starts mouthing at walls."

Mara's skin prickled. *Mouthing at walls.* Where had— they hadn't heard that phrase before. They were almost sure.

"One king per generation," Wren went on. "That was the design. Each wears it twenty, thirty years, gives it what it asks, dies a little early and a little empty, and passes it on. A creditor, see? The crown lends the kingdom its endurance, and collects from the throne. Every reign is a payment plan." He drained his glass. "Three hundred years of payments. You do the arithmetic, scribe. What's left of the first Aldous in the ninth one?"

The tavern noise seemed very far away. Mara thought of the ledger logic their mother had drilled into them: *every debt has a debtor, every loan has a lender, and the dangerous ones are the loans nobody remembers taking.* 

"Who holds the note?" Mara asked. "The binders?"

"The binders held the *renewal*. Every fifty years or so, the binding frays — everything frays — and a binder of the forging line has to renew it, tighten the chains, remind the crown what it owes and what it's owed. Otherwise it gets... hungry. Takes more than memory. Starts taking from around the king. From near the king." Wren tapped the table. "From the countryside. From winters that don't end."

Yara had gone very still. "Eight months," she said. "Winter's lasted eight months in the capital this year."

"Mm," said Wren, noncommittal, which from him was a shout.

"And the last renewal?" Mara asked. "When was it?"

Wren looked at them for a long moment, those yellow eyes going soft and sorry, and Mara understood, with a lurch, that he'd known who they were from the moment he'd seen their face. Or their name. Or maybe just the way they held their hands, like someone waiting to copy something down.

"Sixty-one years ago," he said gently. "By a binder named Sana Nakamura. Who looked at the crown, and looked at the chains, and looked at her granddaughter playing in the garden — and packed a bag in the night and was never seen again."

The tavern tilted, very slightly. Mara gripped the table's edge. Somewhere behind them the fiddler sawed on, oblivious.

"She left it fraying," Mara said. Their voice came out flat and strange. "She left the whole kingdom holding a debt she refused to pay, and she ran, and she—" *taught me letters in candlelight, taught me cant under blankets, taught me every way to disappear* — "and she taught her family how to run too. For three generations. We thought it was a gift. She was teaching us to be ready to leave."

"Or she was teaching you to be ready to finish it," Yara said quietly. "Depends how you read the will."

"That's not fair."

"No," Yara agreed. "Neither is anything else in this story. Your grandmother chained a monster to a throne and then handed your family the bill. My brother carried the proof of it in his coat until it killed him in a snowdrift. Fair left this conversation a long time ago." But she said it tiredly, not cruelly, and she put her hand flat on the table next to Mara's, not quite touching, which from Yara was practically an embrace.

Wren watched them both with the expression of a man watching accounts settle.

"Now you understand the seal," he said. "Her mark. The binder's signature. Your dead courier wasn't carrying a treasure map, boy. He was carrying a *notice of default*. Someone in the capital — someone who knows what's coming — sent that map out to find the heir of the forging line. To call the debt home." He pushed the wax fragment back across the table. "Question is whether the heir intends to pay."

Mara looked at the little broken circle. A crown seen edge-on. A keyhole. A signature.

Every kingdom in arrears. Every reign a payment plan. And somewhere north, a winter eight months deep, collecting.

"I intend," Mara said slowly, "to read the whole contract first. Before anyone pays anything."

Wren grinned his too-few teeth. "Spoken like a Nakamura."

***

They took a room above the tavern — one room, because Yara insisted, and Mara suspected the insistence had less to do with distrust than with the fact that neither of them slept well alone anymore. Mara lay awake listening to the wind worry the turf roof, turning Wren's arithmetic over and over.

*A creditor.* All their life they'd thought of debt as the shape of their cage — the monastery's ledger, the abbot's smug arithmetic, decades owed for a mother's borrowing. But debts ran both directions. If the crown borrowed, then someone had lent. If the kingdom paid, then someone had profited. And if the payments had stopped sixty-one years ago—

Then something up north had been extending credit. Patiently. Generously. Waiting for the debtor to come home.

Mara was still following that cold thread toward sleep when Yara spoke from her bedroll, in the dark.

"The figure. On the scree slope, after the rockslide. You saw it too."

Mara hadn't, actually. But they'd felt watched for four days, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue.

"Yes," they lied, or half-lied.

"Inquisitor grey," Yara said. "Moving like a soldier. Whoever it is, they're good." A pause. "Better than Tam was."

"We'll lose them in the Greymantles tomorrow."

"Mm." Yara rolled over. "Get some sleep, scribe. Tomorrow we start lying to professionals."

But sleep, when it finally took Mara, brought the same dream it had brought every night since the gate: snow, a dying man's lips moving, and the words arriving at last, clear as ink on fresh paper —

*the crown remembers you.*
