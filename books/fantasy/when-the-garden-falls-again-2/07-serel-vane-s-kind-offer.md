## 7. Serel Vane's Kind Offer

The trouble with winter inquisitorial work, Serel Vane reflected, was that it was mostly paperwork conducted while cold.

She sat in the customs office at Greyhollow Ford, which the Crown's church had requisitioned three days ago and which still smelled of pickled herring. Her junior clerk, Brother Tam, had arranged the evidence on the desk with the fussy precision of a man who alphabetized his prayers: one dead courier's travel papers, one inventory of said courier's effects (a knife, a broken compass, forty feet of good rope, and a prayer book with all the pages about forgiveness torn out—Serel chose to find this funny rather than ominous), and one report from the monastery at Thornfell stating that a scribe of the house had been seen leaving by the smugglers' gate two nights after the stranger died.

"A scribe," Tam repeated, for the fourth time, as though the word itself were suspicious. "Fled through the *smugglers' gate*. What kind of scribe knows where that is?"

"The dangerous kind," Serel said, "or the desperate kind, or both. Bring me the indenture records."

The records took an hour to surface, because the Thornfell monastery's archivist apparently believed that making information difficult to retrieve was a form of devotion. When they finally arrived—a single leather ledger, water-stained, smelling of tallow—Serel ran her finger down the column of names until she found it.

**NAKAMURA, M. — indentured, term indefinite, debt carried from maternal line. Original sum unpayable. Renewals: 4.**

"Unpayable," she murmured.

"That's common enough," Tam said. "Half the temple-bound are."

"Yes." She tapped the line. "But look how they write it. Not *deferred*, not *under review*. Unpayable. Somebody did the arithmetic generations ago and decided the answer was *forever*." She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom. "Find out everything about the mother. And the grandmother. Quietly."

Tam went off to be quietly thorough, and Serel sat alone with the herring smell and her own thoughts, which were not quiet at all.

Here was the thing about Serel Vane that nobody in the Order ever quite believed when she told them: she had joined the inquisitors because she thought they were the good ones.

Not the knights—the knights were for wars, and wars were for lords who'd grown bored. Not the parish clergy, who meant well the way a flood means well. The inquisitors. The people whose actual job, stripped of the frightening title, was *keeping accounts*. Who kept the realm's ledgers balanced against the long dark. Every child learned the catechism of it: the Hollow Crown holds back the Hungering Cold; the Crown requires a king; the king requires our vigilance; therefore we keep vigil. Three hundred years of winters that ended when they were supposed to end. Somewhere north of here, past the Gullet pass, whole valleys remembered what winters used to do before the Crown—valleys that were now, technically, memories themselves.

Her own family had been on the wrong side of an account once. Her father had borrowed against their farm from a grain-factor in Vell's Landing, and when the arithmetic turned against him he hadn't run, hadn't lied—he'd walked into the factor's office, put the deed on the desk, and said, "I owe what I owe." Serel had been nine years old and had understood, watching him say it, that there was a kind of dignity in honest debt, and a kind of slavery in dishonest debt, and that most of the world's cruelty lived in the space between the two.

The Church had bought the farm back for them. That was the first mercy anyone had ever shown the Vanes, and it had come with an inquisitor's seal on the deed.

So yes. She believed. She believed the way you believe in a roof over your head—not passionately, but absolutely, and with a very clear sense of what happens if someone takes it away.

And now some temple scribe with smuggler's boots and a family name three hundred years deep was running into the mountains carrying a map that could unroof everyone.

* * *

They caught up with her—or rather, Serel positioned herself to be caught—at the Salt Stair, which was less a road than an argument between a cliff and gravity that the cliff had been winning for ten thousand years.

She'd read the terrain maps twice. Anyone fleeing south from Thornfell toward the low country would either take the King's Road, where Serel's riders waited at every bridge like patient spiders, or cut down the Salt Stair, where the switchbacks funneled through a defile narrow enough that a woman with a crossbow and a warrant could become, briefly, geography.

Mara Nakamura came down the Stair at mid-morning on the fourth day, and Serel watched them come and felt something complicated loosen in her chest.

Because she'd built a picture of the fugitive in her head—had to, that was the craft—and the picture had been of a hardened courier's accomplice, some cutpurse in scribe's clothing. What came picking its way down the ice-glazed steps instead was a thin, dark-eyed person of perhaps twenty-five years wearing a monastery cloak that fit like an apology, moving with the peculiar competence of someone who had done something exactly once, long ago, under supervision, and was now doing it again with no supervision and considerable prayer.

They saw her at thirty paces. She watched them do the arithmetic—the defile walls, the snow, her crossbow, their total absence of weapons—and arrive, visibly, at surrender. It was almost touching. They raised their empty hands with the weary grace of a person who had been expecting this since the moment a dying man ruined their life at the monastery gate.

"Scribe Nakamura," Serel called. "I'm Inquisitor Vane. I'd like to talk. You'll notice I haven't loosed anything at you."

"You've got a crossbow pointed at my knees."

"My knees point is generous. It's your ankles. Knees suggest I'm worried about you running." She let that sit, then lowered the crossbow entirely, which cost her something, because Tam would have had opinions. "Sit. There's a flat rock. It's even marginally warmer than standing."

Mara didn't sit. They stood there in the wind with their hands half-lowered, and studied her the way they probably studied ledgers—looking for the error in the column.

"You're alone," Mara said.

"I'm alone."

"Inquisitors don't travel alone."

"This one does, when she wants a conversation instead of a confession. Those aren't the same thing, whatever the training manuals say." She gestured again at the rock. This time, slowly, Mara sat, perching on its edge like a bird deciding whether the hand feeding it also intended to close.

"I know why you ran," Serel said. "You don't have to perform innocence for me; I've read the same reports you're imagining I've read. A man dies at your gate with a sealed map in his hand and a strange sentence in his mouth. Two nights later you're gone through a door you were never taught existed. That's either guilt or fear, and honestly, scribe, it doesn't matter which, because my offer is the same."

"Offer," Mara repeated. Flat. But their eyes had sharpened, and Serel recognized the look—it was the look of someone doing sums, and she had spent eleven years learning never to interrupt a person doing sums.

"Full pardon," she said. "Written, sealed, countersigned by the Chapter at Vell's Landing. Not a pardon-with-asterisks. The courier's death wasn't yours; no one sane believes a temple scribe killed a Crown courier in a snowstorm, and the one or two insane people who might believe it can be managed. The map is Church property, taken from a dead courier by the nearest lawful hand—that's you, congratulations, your theft was actually custody. Hand it over, come down to Greyhollow, answer some questions comfortably by a fire, and walk free. Your debts—" and here she paused, because she'd rehearsed this part and knew its weight, "—your debts are part of the negotiation. An unpayable indenture renewed four times is a scandal waiting for a clerk brave enough to file it. I'm that clerk. There are funds. There are precedents. Walk with me and I will make the arithmetic work."

The wind came up the Stair and pushed at them both. Below, far below, the valley smoke rose straight as plumb lines.

"Why," Mara said.

"Because I asked nicely?"

"You're an inquisitor. You have riders on the King's Road and a warrant that presumably lets you do worse than point crossbows at ankles. Why negotiate with someone who's already cornered?"

Serel considered giving the official answer. *The Crown is merciful. The Church prefers restoration to punishment. Etcetera.* She had given that answer many times and had never once watched it land.

Instead she said: "Because I grew up on a farm that the Church bought back, and I've spent my whole career trying to be the kind of institution that does that on purpose rather than by accident. And because whatever you think the map leads to, the truth is simpler and worse than anything you've imagined, and I'd rather you hear it from me than learn it the hard way at the end of a chase."

Something flickered across Mara's face—too quick to name. Interest? Recognition? The particular flinch of a person who has already heard the truth from someone else and is recalculating everything?

"And what truth is that?" they asked carefully.

"That the Crown protects us." Serel heard her own voice go quiet, the way it did when she spoke of things she actually believed, which was inconvenient of it. "That the long winters—the famines, the Hungering Cold, the centuries when whole provinces simply stopped existing—all of it ended three hundred years ago, and it ended because someone forged a thing strong enough to stand in the door against the dark, and every king since has held that door, and the price has been paid so that farmers like my father never have to know there's a price at all. That's the truth, scribe. The Crown is a wall. Walls aren't cruel. They're just heavy, and somebody has to hold them up."

Mara was silent for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was careful in a new way.

"And the kings? What happens to the men holding it up?"

"They're honored," Serel said. "Buried in state. Remembered forever." Even as she said it, she heard the dodge in it—the question had been *what happens to them*, present tense, and she'd answered a different question, and Mara's face said plainly that they'd noticed. She covered it with briskness. "That's not the point. The point is the offer. Map for pardon, cooperation for freedom. I'll even throw in the rope from the courier's effects; it's good rope and you clearly intend to keep climbing things."

"I need to think."

"You have until the count of fifty. After that my riders come up the Stair from the bottom, and the conversation acquires witnesses and worse posture."

Mara looked past her, up the switchbacks, toward the high country. Toward the Gullet. And Serel—who was good at faces, better than she was at anything except maybe filing—saw the fugitive weigh her offer and find it genuinely, painfully tempting. Of course it was tempting. It was *designed* to be tempting; she'd built it herself, mercy-shaped, load-bearing. Freedom, forgiveness, an end to the running, and all it cost was handing over a piece of wax-sealed paper to the nice lady with the crossbow.

Then Mara's gaze came back down from the heights and settled on Serel, and something in it made Serel feel suddenly like the one being audited.

"When you say the kings are honored," Mara said, "you mean the dead ones. Have you ever actually *seen* King Aldric?"

"No," Serel admitted. "No one has. He hasn't appeared publicly in—"

"A year," Mara said softly. "I know. I've read the court circulars; copying them was one of my duties before the abbot decided I was worth more as a warning to others." They stood, brushing snow from the grey cloak. "Inquisitor Vane, I believe you. That's the worst part. I believe every word you said, including the parts you didn't say on purpose. Which means one of us is wrong about what's behind that door the Crown is holding, and I have recently discovered reason to suspect it's you."

"The map," Serel said, and her voice came out harder than she'd intended. "Fifty counts, scribe."

"Forty-nine of them just passed." Mara took a step backward, toward the cliff edge, and Serel's heart performed an unscheduled maneuver. "Thank you for the offer. Truly. It's the kindest thing anyone's tried to do to me since a man died at my door. But I've been owned my whole life by institutions that described their chains as care, and I find I've developed a professional eye for it."

"Mara Nakamura—"

"—is going to climb that ridge," they said, pointing up, "where your riders can't follow and your crossbow range ends at rockfall. You seem decent, Inquisitor. For an instrument. If you ever want to find out what your masters are actually keeping behind that wall—" they tapped their own temple, right where a crown would sit, and the gesture was so precisely unsettling that Serel forgot, for a full second, to shout for the riders, "—come find me. Before the solstice. After that, I suspect none of this will matter."

And then they went up the cliff face like a rumor, finding holds that Serel's eyes could not confirm existed, and were gone into the white noise of the high passes before her breath finished fogging.

* * *

Brother Tam found her an hour later at the base of the Stair, stamping her feet and pretending to study a contour map.

"The riders lost them above the third switchback," he reported. "Rockfall country. Should I send word to the garrison at the Gullet's mouth? We can seal the pass. Nobody crosses."

"No."

"No?" Tam blinked. Tam's face did most of its thinking out loud. "Ma'am, the fugitive is heading directly into the Gullet. It's the only route to the capital that isn't watched. If we seal it—"

"If we seal it, they die up there, or they turn around and die down here, and either way we learn nothing and lose the map's trail." Serel folded the contour map along its worn creases. "We leave the pass open, Tam. We let them run where they're running. Because our scribe isn't fleeing the capital—they're walking straight toward it, and toward whatever answer they think is waiting there. And I want to know what it is before I decide whether to arrest them or—" she stopped.

"Or what, ma'am?"

She looked up at the Gullet's jagged teeth against the sky, at the pass that swallowed weather and travelers and, according to the old stories, occasionally entire mule trains, and felt the strangest sensation: that for the first time in her orderly, accounted-for life, she was curious about something the ledger couldn't tell her.

"Or follow," she said. "Ready the mules, Tam. We're going into the Gullet."

"*Into* it? Ma'am, they call it the pass that eats travelers."

"Yes," Serel Vane said, shouldering her pack, and discovering that some traitorous corner of her was almost smiling. "Doesn't that strike you as exactly where the interesting answers would be?"
