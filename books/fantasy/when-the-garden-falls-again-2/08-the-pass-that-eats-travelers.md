## 8. The Pass That Eats Travelers

**Mara**

The Gullet did not look like a pass. It looked like a mistake the mountains had made and then tried to hide.

Mara stood at its mouth in the gray hour before dawn, breath steaming, pack straps biting into shoulders that had not yet forgiven yesterday's climb, and understood why no honest road went this way. The walls of rock rose on either side so close together that in places Yara could have touched both with her elbows outstretched. Above them, the sky was a thin blue seam stitched between black stone. Somewhere up there, snow waited with the patience of a creditor.

"It's called the Gullet," Mara said, "because it swallows people. I feel like we should acknowledge that before we go in."

"We're acknowledging it by walking into it." Yara adjusted the broken compass hanging at her throat—a habit, Mara had noticed, like a woman touching a wound to check it still hurt. "My brother's notes say smugglers used it for two hundred years."

"Your brother's notes also say you can tell a lie from the truth by how fast a candle gutters, and we tested that at the waystation, and the candle said your stew was safe to eat."

"The candle was right. You're alive, aren't you?"

"Barely. Because of the stew."

It was their rhythm now, three days out from the rockslide that had buried the waystation's eastern wall and, in the process, buried whatever chance either of them had of walking away from the other. Mara had stopped pretending they were traveling separately. Yara had stopped holding a knife at every rest stop, though she kept it close enough that the pretense would be brief if needed.

They went into the Gullet.

The dark came quickly, the blue seam overhead narrowing to a thread and then vanishing altogether. Mara lit the lantern low, cupping the flame the way their mother had taught them—*a light held high says here I am; a light held low says I'm only passing through.* Their mother had taught them an unreasonable number of things about passing through places without being seen there.

"You know these roads," Yara said behind her. Not accusing. Just noticing, the way she noticed everything, with that ferret-bright attention she usually aimed at exits.

"My mother taught me maps the way other mothers teach prayers."

"Was she a scribe?"

"She was a lot of things the monastery ledger doesn't mention." Mara stepped over a frozen trickle of water, glassy and treacherous. "The Nakamuras didn't exactly leave clean records."

"No," Yara said quietly. "You left records. We just never got to read them."

That landed somewhere under Mara's ribs and stayed there. They walked on.

* * *

**Yara**

Yara counted her brother in small things now. It was a habit she hated and could not stop. Toma had whistled off-key when he was nervous, and she caught herself listening for it in the wind. Toma had sworn the Gullet could be crossed in nine hours if you kept moving, and she found herself counting hours against his voice. Toma had trusted strangers too easily, and one stranger had watched him die in the snow while she'd been three hundred miles away learning about it from a merchant's rumor.

Except the stranger hadn't watched him die. She'd dug him out of the drift, held his head, listened to his last words. Yara knew this because she'd made the woman repeat it four times, hunting for the crack in the story, and there wasn't one. There was just Mara Nakamura, tired and stubborn and annoyingly literate, carrying her dead brother's map like it weighed something more than paper.

"Stop," Mara said suddenly.

Yara froze. Ahead, the lantern showed nothing but more corridor of stone. Then she heard it: a sound like a giant clearing its throat far above them. A soft, grinding shift.

"Avalanche?" she whispered.

"Not yet." Mara turned, face half-lit, eyes going up the walls as if she could see through them. "But the snowpack's been talking all morning. Little slips. We need to get past the narrows before midday or we want to be standing somewhere the mountain can't bury us sideways."

"You know this too. From your mother."

"I know it from a smuggler named Halfmap Henk who drank my mother's plum wine and paid for it in geography." Mara almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile in two days, and Yara hated that she'd been counting those too. "Come on. The narrows are ahead, and then there should be a shelf—an old shelter cut into the east wall. Smugglers called it the Inn."

"There's an inn in the pass?"

"An inn with no wine, no beds, and no roof. Henk's humor ran dry."

The narrows were worse than anything yet—the rock pinched down until the lantern light touched both walls at once and the sky was a memory. Yara's shoulders brushed stone on both sides. Her breath came back at her off the ice. And above them, faint but constant now, the mountain murmured: settling, shifting, weighing whether today was the day.

Halfway through, the murmuring stopped.

The silence was worse. Yara had grown up in hill country; she knew what silence in a mountain meant. It meant the thing above them had finished deciding.

"Run," she said, and shoved Mara forward, and they both ran, packs banging, boots skidding on glassy ice, the lantern swinging wild shadows—and then the world behind them roared.

Not thunder. Thunder is loud and then done. This was a long, rolling crush, the sound of a thousand tons of snow deciding to become one object. The passage behind filled with white smoke and pressure. Something struck Yara between the shoulder blades—not the mass itself but the wind of it, a fist of displaced air that picked her up and threw her against the north wall.

Then it was over, and the Gullet was a different place, and the way back was gone.

* * *

**Mara**

Mara came up coughing in a fog of snow-dust that the lantern turned into a small glowing universe. Everything was white. Everything hurt. She took inventory the way Abbot Corren had taught her to take inventory of damaged goods—quickly, without sentiment. Ribs: bruised, not cracked. Left ankle: complaining, would keep working. Lantern: alive. Map:—

Her hands flew to her chest, to the oilskin packet sewn inside her coat, and found it intact, and let out a breath that shook more than she liked.

"Yara?"

A cough answered from the white. Then: "If you say 'the candle warned us,' I'm leaving you here."

"You can't leave me here. The exit is behind us and the exit is currently forty feet of snow."

"Then I'll leave you here *and* feel better about it."

Yara emerged from the fog like something being carved out of marble, gray-faced, hair full of snow, blood at her temple where she'd hit the wall. She was limping. So was Mara. They regarded each other across three feet of fresh debris, two women who had very recently been trying to reach the same destination for entirely different reasons, and who now had, formally, no way back at all.

"The Inn," Mara said. "If it exists. If it isn't buried."

"And if it is?"

"Then we dig ourselves an inn."

It took another hour, ankle screaming, to find it—and it existed, barely: a shelf of natural rock overhung by a slab, with a low mouth of a cave behind it, old soot on the ceiling proving that Halfmap Henk had not invented it after all. Someone had even stacked firewood in the back generations ago, dry as prayer. Mara got a flame going while Yara saw to her temple, and they sat in the small orange world of the shelter while outside the light died and the temperature dropped toward the kind of cold that has opinions.

Yara broke the silence first. "Toma crossed this pass eleven times. He told me once that the Gullet takes someone every few years, and the smugglers leave a mark for them. A little cairn with a token." She stared into the fire. "I always thought he was making it up to seem dangerous."

"Smugglers do love their rituals. My mother said it's because they spend their lives being forgotten on purpose. The marks are how they remember each other." Mara hesitated. Then, because the fire was warm and the alternative to talking was thinking about the avalanche: "She used to sing a work song about the Gullet. Terrible song. The refrain is just the word 'swallowed' repeated in increasingly despairing harmony."

Yara laughed—one short, startled bark, as if the laugh had escaped custody. She looked almost embarrassed by it. Then quieter: "Tell me about her. Your mother. The real version."

So Mara did. Not everything—there were rooms in the family history Mara wasn't ready to open, doors marked with names and sigils—but some. How Sana Nakamura had come to Thornfell with a debt nobody would name, trading her literacy for a roof, teaching her child two alphabets and one alphabet's worth of caution. How she'd gone up the mountain one winter to "settle accounts" and come back thinner, quieter, and dead within the year. How the abbot had recorded it as *fever.*

"Fever," Yara repeated flatly.

"He was fond of tidy words. He burned our courier without rites for the same reason. Tidy words are cheaper than true ones."

Yara fed a stick to the fire and said nothing for a while. When she spoke again, the knife-edge had gone out of her voice, replaced by something rawer. "I spent three weeks wanting to kill you. Did you know that? I practiced speeches. I had a whole—" she gestured vaguely at the air— "*oration*. About justice. About how you stole his dying from me."

"You didn't give the speech."

"You were copying ledgers when I found you. Ink on your fingers, apologizing to a book you'd smudged." Yara's mouth twisted. "It's hard to deliver an oration to someone who apologizes to books."

"I'm glad the attempt failed. Your orations are terrible. You lead with the accusation and bury the grief."

"Grief isn't supposed to lead. It follows you around like—" She stopped. Rubbed her face. "Like a debt," she finished, and both of them heard the word land, because debt was the whole architecture of both their lives: Mara indentured, Yara orphaned twice over, Toma dead running an errand neither of them fully understood yet.

Outside, the wind found the pass and began its long argument with the stone.

* * *

**Mara**

Morning came gray and cautious. They climbed out of the shelter into a world remade—the avalanche had stripped the pass floor and piled it high behind them, and ahead, where the Gullet began its final climb toward the saddle, the rock opened into a wider bowl.

Which is where Mara saw the shrine.

It stood against the east wall, tucked beneath an overhang that had sheltered it for what looked like centuries: a shallow alcove of fitted stone, older than any smuggler's cairn, older in its bones than the monastery at Thornfell. Inside it, tiers of niches held offerings. Some were recent—a strip of dyed cloth, a clay bead, a candle stub melted onto the ledge. Some were ancient beyond guessing: coins green with age, a rusted knife, a child's wooden bird worn smooth by hundreds of years of hands.

And carved above the alcove, deep in the stone, was a sigil.

Mara knew it the way you know your own handwriting. Not from study—from inheritance. A circle broken by seven points, and within it, a crown rendered hollow: drawn as outline only, empty at its heart. The Hollow Crown. The same mark that sat beside the name *Nakamura* in the courier's three-hundred-year-old records. The same mark, Mara realized with a lurch, that their mother had inked tiny and private on the inside cover of every ledger she ever kept, where the abbot never looked.

"Yara," Mara said. Their own voice sounded far away. "Come look at this."

Yara came. She read the sigil, read the offerings, read Mara's face. "Your family built this."

"My family *kept* it. Look at the dates scratched under the niches." Mara knelt, brushing frost from the stone. Names and years, dozens of them, in a hand that changed century by century but stayed recognizably the same household: *Nakamura, for the road. Nakamura, for the debt remembered. Nakamura, that the Crown stay sleeping.* The most recent was thirty-two years old, in a slanting, confident script Mara had spent their whole childhood trying to imitate.

Their mother's hand.

"She came here," Mara whispered. "Before she died. She climbed the Gullet in winter to—to pay something. Or ask something. Or apologize." The cold seemed to move inside Mara's coat then, under the wool, against the skin. Every story they had built about Sana Nakamura—the quiet scribe, the fevered widow—was a tidy word, and here was the untidy truth chiseled in a mountainside: their mother had been a servant of the Hollow Crown, and had never told them.

Yara crouched beside her. She didn't touch the shrine. She touched Mara instead—one hand, briefly, on the shoulder, the first unprompted kindness between them since the rockslide. "My brother carried this map knowing your name was on it," she said. "He must have known what it meant. Maybe he was coming here too."

"To settle accounts," Mara said. The phrase felt heavy, ancestral, almost funny in its awfulness. Their whole family line, generation after generation, trudging up a frozen gullet to negotiate with a piece of metal.

Something glinted at the base of the newest niche. Mara reached in—heart hammering, some old instinct whispering *offerings are given, not taken*—and found instead that it was not an offering but a message: a slim tube of oilcloth wedged behind the candle stub, sealed with wax stamped by that same hollow sigil. Addressed, in the mother's slanting confident hand, to no one—to whoever came next, which had apparently always been the plan.

Inside the tube: a single page, dense with cipher, the margins crowded with annotations in three different inks. Mara recognized the cipher instantly. It was the smugglers' cant, the second alphabet, the one their mother had sung to them over mending and plum wine. But the page was long, and the light was short, and the wind was rising again with a new note in it—deeper, hungrier, the sound of the season itself leaning in.

"What does it say?" Yara asked.

Mara looked at the first deciphered line, and the bowl of the pass seemed to tilt.

*"To the one who finds this: the Crown is not a treasure and not a weapon. It is a contract, and our family wrote it. Read every word before you decide what you owe."*

Mara folded the page away with hands that were not quite steady, and looked up at the saddle where the pass finally opened toward the north—and the capital, and the king, and the winter that had forgotten how to end.

"We read every word," Mara said. "Starting tonight. All of it."

Above them, far up the seam of sky, the first flakes of the next storm had already begun to fall.
