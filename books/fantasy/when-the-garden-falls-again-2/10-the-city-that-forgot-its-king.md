## 10. The City That Forgot Its King

The road to Corvale flattened out the way grief does—gradually, then all at once. One morning there were hills behind them and a gray line ahead that Yara took for cloud, and by afternoon it was walls, and beyond the walls a city wearing eight months of winter like a coat it had stopped trying to take off.

"Eight months," Mara said, reading from a tinker's bill they'd picked up at the last waystation. "Snow in the capital since spring. The tinker writes that the river froze twice."

"Rivers don't freeze twice," Yara said.

"That's what I said to him. He said, *tell that to the river.*"

Yara had been to Corvale exactly once, at nineteen, following her brother Tomas on a run he swore was simple: silk in, salt out, home by the thaw. She remembered lanterns strung across the Weavers' Bridge like a necklace someone had dropped and couldn't bear to pick up. She remembered the fish market shouting before dawn. She remembered thinking the city was too big to be sad about anything.

She wanted very much to remember those things now, because the city in front of her was doing its best to make remembering difficult.

* * *

They came in through the Salt Gate with the wool-cart traffic, hooded, unremarkable, which was Yara's favorite way to be anywhere. The gate guards didn't look at faces. They looked at hands—turning them over, checking for the ink-stain that marked registered guild labor—and waved through anyone whose palms said *peasant* loudly enough.

Inside, the cold had a texture Yara hadn't felt before. Mountain cold was honest; it wanted you dead and said so. This cold was patient. It sat in the streets like a landlord who had decided the rent was due forever.

And the people—

Yara had grown up around hunger. Smuggler camps ran lean more winters than not, and she knew the look of a hungry town: short tempers, long silences, doors barred early. This wasn't that. The people of Corvale weren't hungry for bread. Or they were, but bread was the smaller want, and they'd found a way to manage it.

In the Fishmarket Square, where she'd once haggled over smoked eel with a woman who could swear for ten minutes without repeating herself, there was now a stall with no fish. There was bread—gray, dense loaves stacked like masonry—and beside the bread there was a ledger, open, and beside the ledger there sat a clerk with a warm scarf and cold eyes, and above the whole arrangement hung a sign:

**THE KING'S BOUNTY — FAIR EXCHANGE FOR LOYAL SUBJECTS**

"What's fair exchange?" Mara asked a woman in line, all innocence, in the flat accent of the northern valleys they'd been practicing for two days.

The woman looked at Mara the way you look at someone who has just asked how much air costs.

"A memory," she said. "One memory per loaf. Small ones go further. You remember your mother's face? That's three loaves right there. Your wedding day, five." She paused. "You don't want to know what a child's name goes for."

Yara stood at the edge of the square and watched the clerk work. He was efficient. He'd ask, *what will you give today*, and the person would say something small—"the smell of my grandmother's kitchen"—and he'd write it down, and they'd hand him something else too, some token, a ring or a button or a lock of hair in wax paper, because apparently the memory needed an anchor or it wouldn't take properly, and then a man in a gray hood would touch the giver's temple with two fingers and say a word Yara couldn't hear, and the giver would blink, and smile, and walk away lighter.

Lighter. That was the word everyone used. *It doesn't hurt. You feel lighter.*

Yara watched a young man come out of the gray-hooded touch and turn immediately to his companion and ask, cheerfully, "Wait—why are we here? What were we doing?"

His companion laughed. "You always forget. It's why we bring rope."

"You bring rope?"

"I bring rope so you can find your way back to the things you've sold. Well. To where they used to be."

The young man laughed too, easily, the laugh of a man who had misplaced his own wedding and found it funny. And Yara understood, with a lurch, that this was the horror of it—not cruelty, not famine, not even tyranny. Nobody was being beaten. Nobody was starving in the streets. The city had simply discovered that memory was edible, and it was eating itself one pleasant afternoon at a time, and calling the taste *lightness*.

"Tomas would have hated this," she said quietly.

Mara glanced at her. It was the first time she'd said her brother's name aloud in days, and both of them knew it, and neither of them made anything of it, which was its own kind of agreement.

"He smuggled memories?" Mara asked.

"He smuggled everything. But he used to say—" Yara fumbled for it, because grief kept rearranging her brother's words when she reached for them, moving the furniture in the rooms of her head. "He used to say a courier's whole trade is other people's secrets, and the only thing you never sell is what someone told you for free. Because free means it mattered."

Mara nodded slowly. "Then we're in the right city to break something."

* * *

They found lodging above a chandler's shop in the Tallow District, paid for with coin and no questions, from a landlady named Odile who had the gift—common among the very old—of appearing deaf until money changed hands.

Odile's shop smelled of tallow and rosemary, and she talked while she trimmed wicks, which Yara suspected was how she did most of her thinking.

"You'll want to see the palace," Odile said. "Everyone does. Everyone wants to see if the king's real."

"Is he?"

"Haven't seen him." She snipped a wick with great precision. "My daughter saw him. Three years ago, at the Founding Day parade. Said he looked 'tired but kind.' Then last year, no parade. No king. Just the Lord Protector reading a proclamation off a balcony to a crowd of clerks, saying His Majesty 'continues his devotions.' Devotions." She snorted. "A year of devotions. My husband had devotions once. Lasted an afternoon and cost me a new roof."

"Do people believe it?" Yara asked.

"People believe what lets them keep their bread." Odile held up a candle to the light, checking it for bubbles. "There's a woman comes by Tuesdays, sells me beeswax. Her husband works the palace kitchens. He says the king's table is still laid every night. Full setting. Roast capon, wine, the gold plates. Nobody eats it. In the morning they clear it away and lay it again." She set the candle down. "What do you make of that?"

Yara made of it a picture she didn't like: a table set for a man who couldn't come, every night, by servants who either believed he was coming or had stopped believing anything at all, and between those two kinds of servants she couldn't have said which was worse.

"Maybe he's ill," Mara offered.

"Maybe." Odile's eyes were shrewd and unhurried. "Or maybe the crown fits better than the head these days. Old story. My mother had a version of it." She leaned closer, voice dropping into the register of women who have outlived everyone who could punish them for gossip. "In my mother's version, the kings of Corvale stopped being men a long time ago. They became *hats*. You put the hat on whoever's next in line, and the hat does the ruling, and the man inside just... waits. Gets quiet. Gets smooth, like a stone in a stream." She straightened up. "Course, my mother also believed eels were the souls of tax collectors, so weigh it accordingly."

Yara weighed it. It weighed exactly as much as the map sewn into Mara's coat lining, and the word *remembers*, and a dying man's hand pressing wax into hers.

"Weighing it accordingly," she said.

* * *

The trouble with a city that trades memories is that it keeps imperfect records of everything else.

That was Mara's theory, anyway, delivered at midnight over the chandler's table with a stub of candle and the map unrolled between them, weighted down with two candlesticks and a bowl of Odile's stew that nobody was eating.

"The archives," Mara said, tapping the map's margin, where their grandmother's handwriting looped and faded. "Every rite, every binding, every debt the Crown ever called in—it'll be written down somewhere. Bureaucracies can't help themselves. Even magic likes paperwork."

"You sound fond of that idea."

"I'm a scribe. Of course I'm fond of it. It's the only religion I have." Mara traced a street with one finger. "Palace archive, here—the map marks a door. A courier's door. Servants' passage, coal delivery, the routes nobody watches because nobody important walks them."

"And nobody important includes us."

"For once, yes."

Yara should have felt relief. Instead she felt the specific unease of a plan going *well*, which in her experience was just failure arriving by a slower road. She'd spent four days and one rockslide learning to trust this scribe, and the trust was still new enough that it squeaked, like boots not yet broken in.

"Tomorrow," she said. "We sleep tonight. And tomorrow we watch the palace first. I don't walk into any building I haven't circled twice. House rule. Tomas's rule, actually." She caught herself using his name again and let it stand. "He got us out of nine customs houses alive with that rule. The tenth killed him, and not because he broke it. Because somebody else did."

Mara looked at her across the candle. "I'm sorry about Tomas."

"Don't be sorry yet. Be sorry after. If it turns out he died carrying a map to save a king nobody's seen in a year, I intend to be *furious* first and sorry later, and I need you to help me stay furious, because fury travels lighter than grief and I've got a mountain range of it left to cross."

Mara considered this. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me since the monastery."

"It's the Ferro family motto. *Fury Travels Lighter.* We had it embroidered on nothing, because embroidering takes time and time is money."

For the first time in days, Mara laughed—a real one, startled out of them, quickly smothered because the walls were thin and Odile was theoretically asleep. Yara found she liked the sound, and hated that she liked it, and decided not to examine that particular feeling until a safer season.

* * *

They circled the palace twice the next day, per house rule, and the circling told Yara more than any archive could have.

The palace of Corvale was enormous and immaculate and *empty*. Not abandoned—empty the way a stage is empty between acts. Guards at every gate, polished and bored. Lamplighters working the grounds at dusk with religious punctuality. Laundry flapping: sheets, tablecloths, shirts in the king's size, though the king's size had gone unmeasured for a year. Everything maintained. Nothing inhabited. A body with all its organs and no pulse.

And the people of the city walked past it daily and did not look up.

That was the part Yara couldn't shake. At the Fishmarket, at the Bounty stall, in the taverns, she'd started asking—casually, in her valley accent, playing the ignorant northerner, a role she wore comfortably because it required no acting—when people had last seen their king.

The baker's boy: "Seen him? Why would I see him?"

The ferryman: "My father saw King Aldric crowned. Lovely day. Wasn't it? I feel like it was lovely. I can't quite—" and then he frowned at the middle distance like a man looking for keys, and gave up, and rowed on.

An old woman selling chestnuts, the only person all day who answered plainly: "Nobody's seen him, dear. Nobody says so either. First rule of Corvale: the king reigns, therefore the king exists, therefore asking where he *is* is treason, therefore nobody asks." She cracked a chestnut with her thumb. "Second rule: buy a memory before you start needing one. Questions use them up."

Yara bought chestnuts she didn't want, because the old woman had earned them, and because her hands needed something to hold that wasn't a knife.

No tyrant, she thought. That was the thing she'd come expecting—some monster on a throne, some visible evil she could aim her fury at like an arrow at a target. But there was no target. There was ceremony performing itself for an audience of clerks, a table laid nightly for a ghost, guards guarding a door that presumably opened onto a man who might not be a man anymore, and outside it all, a city politely forgetting, one loaf at a time, that it had ever known anything different.

Absence wearing ceremony. She turned the phrase over. It fit like a key.

Tomas had died carrying proof of this. Had he known? Had he read far enough into whatever he carried to understand what the Crown was, or had he just seen a job—a sealed packet, a fat purse, a route through the Gullet—and taken it the way Ferros took everything, fast and grinning and trusting the road to sort itself out?

She hoped he hadn't known. She hoped it desperately, and then immediately hated hoping it, because hoping he hadn't known meant hoping he'd died stupid, and Tomas Ferro had never been stupid a day in his life except on purpose, for charm.

She hoped he'd known, then. She hoped he'd read it and understood and run anyway, because running toward a thing that scared you was also a Ferro family tradition, less embroidered than the motto but older.

* * *

Dusk came down over the Tallow District like ash settling, and in the chandler's window the candles did their small stubborn work against it.

Yara sat sharpening her knife, which was what she did instead of praying, and Mara sat hunched over the map, copying the marginalia into a pocket notebook by candlelight, lips moving, translating their grandmother's looping hand from cipher to plain script one word at a time.

"There's a phrase here," Mara said suddenly. "Repeated. In the margin near the palace door-mark." Their finger stopped on the page. "It says *the host must be willing*. And underneath, in different ink, older, pressed hard enough to tear the vellum—" They went quiet.

"Underneath what?"

"*The host must be willing,*" Mara repeated, "and then it says, *or the binding holds the wrong one.*"

Yara stopped sharpening. Outside, somewhere down the street, a bell rang the hour, and the sound crossed the frozen city the way all sounds did here—thinly, as if the cold were charging a toll.

"The wrong one," she said.

"The wrong one." Mara looked up, and in the candlelight their face had gone the color of the snow on the rooftops. "Yara. Someone wrote that warning three hundred years ago. Someone in my family. Which means someone in my family already knew what happens when the Crown takes a king who isn't willing." They swallowed. "And I think—I think we're about to walk in and meet him."

Through the frosted window, far off across the dark roofs, the palace showed its lights: orderly, golden, unmanned, a table set every night for a king who might be nothing but a hat, waiting for whoever came to take it off—or to put it on.

Yara slid her knife back into its sheath and stood.

"Then we'd best not keep him waiting," she said.
