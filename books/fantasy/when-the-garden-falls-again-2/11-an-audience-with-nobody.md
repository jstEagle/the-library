## 11. An Audience with Nobody

The credentials took three days to forge, and the hardest part was not the seal.

Anyone who has ever watched a scribe work assumes the writing is the craft. It isn't. The writing is the easy part—Mara could reproduce a chancery hand in their sleep, had done so often enough under Abbot Corren's whip-crack deadlines that their wrist ached in its shape even now. No. The hard part was the *wear*. A fresh letter of passage looks like a fresh letter of passage: ink too black, vellum too white, folds too crisp. Real documents live lives. They get rained on. They get carried against a sweating ribs for a week. They get read by bored clerks who leave thumbprints of tallow and impatience.

So Mara aged the letter. They rubbed it with ash from Yara's pipe—she smoked something foul called grave-moss and defended it like a firstborn child—and they creased it along lines a courier's satchel would make, and they dripped a little candle grease near the corner because clerks are slobs and documents know it.

"You're enjoying this," Yara said, watching from the doorway of their rented room above the cooper's yard.

"I'm working."

"You're smiling at a piece of paper like it told you a joke."

Mara held the letter up to the grey light. The name on it was *Scribe Ferren Odo, envoy of the Chantry of Saint Vess, come to audit the winter alms ledgers.* The seal was good enough to pass at arm's length and bad enough to be plausible—perfect seals were for people with something to hide, and everyone knew that, which meant perfect seals were themselves suspicious. Forgery, Mara had learned from their mother's stories of the smuggler roads, was mostly an exercise in being exactly as imperfect as expected.

"I'm smiling," Mara said, "because this is the first useful thing my education has ever produced. Ten years copying tithe ledgers, and it turns out I was training to lie to a palace."

"Ten years," Yara said, "and you still count everything. You counted the stairs coming up here. I saw your lips move."

"There were forty-two. That's not counting, that's *noticing*."

"That's counting." But she said it almost fondly, which from Yara Ferro was practically a sonnet. She'd been softer since the Gullet, since the shrine with her brother's compass laid among the Nakamura offerings—no, not softer, exactly. Aimed differently. Grief was still in her, but it had stopped swinging wild and started pointing somewhere, and the somewhere happened to be the same direction Mara was walking.

"The audit angle is clever," she admitted. "Nobody wants to look at alms ledgers. Boredom is better than a bribe."

"Boredom *is* a bribe," Mara said. "It's the only one palaces can always afford."

* * *

Getting into the palace grounds proved insultingly easy, which was the first horror of it.

The outer gate guards looked at the letter, looked at Mara's borrowed clerk's coat—one size too big, sleeves rolled twice, smelling faintly of someone else's onions—and waved them through with the enthusiasm of men waving through weather. Inside, the courtyard was swept clean and utterly empty except for two gardeners pruning a hedge that had already given up growing in the eight-month winter. Nobody asked where the Chantry of Saint Vess was headquartered. Nobody wondered why an audit arrived in the ninth month of a famine. The machinery of ceremony turned and turned, feeding itself paper, and no one had checked in years whether anything came out the other end.

A chamberlain's underling—a boy of perhaps fifteen with the haunted eyes of someone who had once cared about his work—escorted Mara down corridors of white marble toward the records offices. Their footsteps echoed. The tapestries on the walls depicted harvests. Plump wheat, fat grapes, peasants with the serene smiles of people who have never existed. Someone had dusted them faithfully all through the famine.

"Is the king well?" Mara asked lightly, the way one asks about weather.

The boy flinched so hard he nearly walked into a suit of armor. "His Majesty keeps his own hours," he said, in the tone of a man reciting a rope he'd been thrown. "The court attends to the regency council's business. His Majesty's health is—" He stopped. Started again. "The ledgers are this way."

He left Mara in a records room stacked floor to ceiling with boxes labeled in a fading hand: *ALMS, WINTER, YEARS 1–3; ALMS, WINTER, YEARS 4–6; ALMS, WINTER, YEARS 7–8 (INCOMPLETE).* The last box was nearly empty. There were no alms left to record. There was barely a city left to receive them.

Mara gave the empty box ten honest minutes, because if anyone looked in, a forger caught actually working was a forger with a future. Then they listened at the door, heard nothing but the building's long cold breathing, and slipped out into the corridor going the wrong way.

Up. Everything interesting was up. Servants' stairs wound through the palace's bones, and Mara climbed them with the particular silence their mother had taught them—weight on the outside of the foot, breath through the nose, hands ready to catch a rail before it creaked. On the third landing a laundress passed carrying linens; Mara became furniture, became shadow, became a person who obviously belonged and therefore required no seeing. The laundress nodded at them. People nod at what they assume. It is the great mercy and the great flaw of the human animal.

The throne room announced itself by temperature. Colder than the rest of the palace, somehow—the doors at the end of the gallery stood open, and air moved out of them like water out of a cracked jar, carrying a smell Mara knew from the shrine in the Gullet, from the map's old wax: stone and snow and something underneath, older, like the inside of a bell.

Mara should have turned back. They noted this clearly, the way they noted stair-counts. Every sensible instinct said *you have seen enough, take the ledger box, go, write the report, sell the story to someone with an army.* Sensible instinct had been overruled all winter, though, ever since a dying man had pressed wax and blood into their hands and spent his last breath on four words.

*The crown remembers.*

Mara wanted to know what it remembered. And some colder, quieter part of them suspected the answer was: *us. All of us. Our family most of all.*

They went in.

* * *

The throne room of Aldric III was built to hold a thousand courtiers and was holding one king.

He sat on the throne the way a coat sits on a hook. That was the image that arrived whole and unwelcome in Mara's mind: not a man seated but a garment hung up, shoulders still shaped, sleeves still filled, everything arranged correctly and nothing inside. He wore the crown—of course he wore it, he never took it off, the servants whispered that much even here—but it sat on him strangely, low, as though it had settled into him over years the way a ring settles into a finger gone thin.

He was mouthing words.

No sound came out. His lips moved in steady, patient rhythm, like a man praying, or dictating, or—Mara stepped closer, hating themselves, unable to stop—like a man *counting*. His eyes were open and fixed on the middle distance, on nothing, on the far wall where another harvest tapestry hung with its lying wheat. His hands lay on the arms of the throne, and they were the worst part, because they moved too, fingers tapping out some silent arithmetic against the gilded wood, patient as dripping water.

"Your Majesty?" Mara said, before they could decide not to.

Nothing. The lips went on. The fingers tapped. And then—Mara would swear this later, in the small hours, to Yara, who believed them, which frightened Mara more than anything else that winter—the tapping *changed*. It stuttered. Skipped. As if whatever was counting had lost its place because someone had spoken aloud in a room that hadn't held a voice in months.

The king's head turned. Slowly, joint by joint, like a door unused for years. His eyes found Mara and there was—

There was a moment. Mara would spend the rest of their life trying to describe it honestly and would keep failing, but the attempt went roughly thus: imagine looking through a frozen window into a lit house. You can see the fire inside. You can see someone moving behind the glass. You cannot reach them, and they cannot see you—or rather, they see you the way the drowning see the surface, upside-down and impossibly far—and every second the ice thickens. What looked out of King Aldric's eyes was a man behind very thick ice, and he was mouthing, and now that his face was turned Mara could almost read it, could almost catch the shapes:

*—cold, it's so cold, tell her I kept it, tell her I kept it as long as—*

Her. Tell *her.*

Mara's grandmother had fled the capital thirty years ago and died in a smuggler's loft with her debts unspoken. But standing in that freezing hall, watching a king beg a stranger through glass, Mara felt the word *her* land somewhere old and personal, like a key turning in a lock they hadn't known was theirs.

"Aldric," Mara said—not *Your Majesty*, just the name, plain, the way you speak to a person and not a throne.

The king's mouth stopped.

For three heartbeats the hall was absolutely silent, and in that silence Mara heard—felt, more than heard, the way you feel a note below hearing—a vast slow attention turn toward them from somewhere behind the king's eyes. Not the man. The *other* thing. The tenant. It regarded Mara the way a ledger regards a new entry: without malice, without interest, with only the terrible patience of arithmetic.

Then the doors opened at the far end of the gallery, and boots rang on marble, and the moment shattered like the ice it resembled.

* * *

Serel Vane did not shout. Mara had expected shouting—inquisitors shouted, surely, it was half the job—but Serel simply crossed the hall at a walk, unhurried, her grey cloak whispering, and positioned herself between Mara and the throne with the ease of someone arranging furniture.

"Scribe Odo," she said. "Of Saint Vess."

"Envoy," Mara corrected, and hated how thin their voice came out. "Auditing the winter alms."

"Mm." Serel glanced past them at the king, who had resumed his silent counting as though the interruption had been weather. Something crossed her face—quickly, professionally suppressed, but Mara had spent ten years reading faces across ledger desks, faces of people hiding debts, and they knew what they saw. It wasn't triumph. It wasn't suspicion, even. It was closer to *shame*, worn the way you wear a coat you can't afford to replace.

"The alms ledgers are in the east wing," Serel said. "You've wandered."

"The stairs were confusing."

"The stairs were confusing," Serel repeated, tasting it. She looked at Mara properly then, and Mara braced for the recognition—for the Salt Stair, for the pass, for the waystation where a woman with a broken compass had tried to take their throat. Surely Serel had described them by now. Surely there were sketches. Surely the whole machine of the Crown's church was grinding toward them at this very moment.

But Serel only said, quietly, "You spoke to him."

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't an accusation either. Her eyes went again to the throne, and stayed there longer than an inquisitor's eyes should.

"He answered," Mara said, before wisdom could intervene. "Did you know that? Did any of you know he's still *in there*?"

"Enough." The word came out sharp, and Serel seemed surprised by her own sharpness. She drew a breath, and when she continued her voice had been smoothed flat, official. "You will return to the east wing, complete your audit, and depart by the south gate before evening bells. You will not enter this hall again. You will not speak of what you saw. These are the courtesies owed to a Chantry envoy." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "They are also the limits of my courtesy. They do not extend far."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I write a report." She said it simply, without threat, and that simplicity was worse than any threat. "I am very good at reports, Scribe Odo. I have written many. They are how I pay my own debts." For one unguarded instant her composure flickered, and Mara saw beneath it a woman doing sums of her own—sums that had recently stopped balancing. "Go east. Now."

Mara went. At the doors they paused, because some stubborn Nakamura instinct refused to leave a room without the last word, even a room containing a hollow king and an inquisitor.

"He said *tell her I kept it*," Mara said. "Whoever she is. Whoever he is, under it. Somebody should tell her."

And they walked out before Serel could answer, down the servants' stairs, past the laundress who nodded at them again, out through gates that opened onto a city buried to its eaves in snow and forgetting, and it was only in the cooper's yard, safe, shaking, Yara's hand on their shoulder, that Mara understood what had actually happened in that hall.

Serel Vane had recognized them. Had to have. And she had written no report, raised no alarm, summoned no guard.

She had hesitated. And a woman like Serel Vane did not hesitate over a smuggler and a forged letter.

She hesitated over a king, mouthing words to nobody, in a palace full of people who had stopped listening—and Mara, who had come to steal a look and leave, sat down heavily on Yara's cot and heard themselves say, in a voice that had already decided:

"We're not leaving the capital. We're going back in. Tonight, tomorrow—I don't care. Because he's counting, Yara. He's been counting for years, and I think I know what he's counting *down to*."

Outside, the solstice dark gathered early over the roofs, and somewhere beneath the palace, patient as arithmetic, something turned another page.
