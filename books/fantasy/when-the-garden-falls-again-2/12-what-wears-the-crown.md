## 12. What Wears the Crown

The king had once been a man who liked the smell of rain.

He was almost sure of this. It surfaced some mornings—mornings being a courtesy the Crown extended to him, a candle lit in a windowless room—along with other small treasures: the taste of plums, a dog named Bristle who had died when he was nine, his mother's laugh, which had been more cough than laugh and all the lovelier for it. These fragments drifted up through the dark like bubbles in a frozen pond, and he learned to cup them quickly, before the cold below noticed and drank them.

Because the cold below noticed. The cold below noticed everything.

*Aldric.*

It said his name the way a creditor says it. Not cruelly. Never cruelly. The Crown had no cruelty in it, only appetite, and appetite is patient and polite and always, always hungry.

*I'm here,* he answered, because he had learned that silence made it reach deeper. If he spoke first, it only took what he offered. If he went quiet, it went looking, and its looking was like having his pockets turned out in a market square while the whole city watched—everything he owned spread on a cloth and appraised, and then, one by one, taken.

*The harvest is short this season,* the Crown said. *The city forgets quickly. Fear forgets fastest of all. Fear is a poor soil.*

"Then let them be afraid," Aldric said, or thought, or was. There was no difference anymore between the words he spoke and the words he merely carried, because he had not spoken aloud in— he counted— he could not count. The counting was one of the things it had taken. He had once known numbers the way a farmer knows weather. Now he knew one, and two, and *many*, and *too many*.

*You know why we cannot,* the Crown said, in the tone of a parent explaining, again, why the child may not have the knife. *The cold beneath. The older winter. You remember.*

He did not remember. That was the joke of it, the joke only he was left to laugh at: the Crown consumed his memories to hold back a cold that lived, as far as he could tell, entirely inside the Crown's own telling of it. The older winter. The deep hunger. The thing the Crown had been forged to starve. He had no proof any of it existed except that the Crown was so very certain, and the Crown was never wrong about arithmetic. Debts, sums, balances, what was owed and what remained—those it knew to the last copper. Everything else it ate.

He had been a king for eleven years. He had been a hostage for nine.

* * *

In the beginning—*beginning* was a word that still meant something, barely—he had fought it the way men fight in stories. He had built walls in his mind, recited his name, scratched tallies into the arm of his chair with a hidden knife until the guards took the knife and the chair both. He had tried to starve it in return, hoarding his memories like a miser, giving it nothing, and for three days he had felt triumphant, and on the fourth day the winter had come down off the mountains eight weeks early and forty people in the river districts had frozen in their beds, and the Crown had said, very gently, *Do you see now,* and he had seen, and he had eaten his own hoard like a man breaking a fast, and it had tasted of his mother's laugh.

That was the trick of it. That was the whole architecture of his captivity, and he could admire it the way one admires a well-made lock even while locked inside it: the Crown did not take his memories for itself. It took them for the kingdom. Every recollection he surrendered became warmth somewhere—a hearth that burned a week longer, a granary that didn't rot, a child in the low town who did not cough through the night. He had checked, in the early years, through the one window of himself that remained: he could still, sometimes, push a feeling out through his fingertips, into the stones of the palace, and feel where it landed. Once he had pushed out the memory of Bristle—warm dog, wet nose, the particular shame of a boy whose dog has caught a chicken—and felt it arrive in a guardroom, and heard a young guard laugh at nothing and say to his fellows, *I don't know, I just suddenly remembered being happy.*

So he paid. He was, when it came down to it, a king who paid his debts, and the Crown had chosen him for exactly that, the way a moneylender chooses a borrower with good land and bad luck.

*You were chosen for your honesty,* the Crown told him once, and he had laughed—inside, where laughter cost nothing—and said, *I was chosen because my father died and you were already on his head.*

*That too,* the Crown had said, and he could have sworn it was amused.

* * *

There was a woman he remembered, and he guarded her the way he guarded nothing else.

Not a lover. Not his mother. A scribe—no, not a scribe, something more, something with a title the Crown flinched from whenever he reached for it, and its flinching was the most information he'd gotten in years. A woman with ink under her fingernails and a way of looking at him that was not the look everyone else gave the crown. Everyone else looked *at* the crown, at the hollow silver band that never warmed and never tarnished, as if the man beneath were a chair it happened to be sitting on. She looked at *him*. She looked at him the way you look at a debtor—no. The way you look at a hostage.

She had come to the palace in his fourth year. Winter had been bad. He had been thin in the way of men being eaten slowly, and the court had learned not to notice, and she had noticed, standing in the receiving line with her ink-stained hands folded, and she had looked at him and then at the Crown, and her face had done something terrible and wonderful: it had *recognized* it.

*Binder,* the Crown had said, low, the way you say the name of a disease. *Nakamura.*

He had held on to that word through everything that followed. Binder. Nakamura. He repeated it to himself in the dark like a prayer, and the Crown would reach for it, and he would feed it other memories instead—sacrificing the taste of plums, the color of his father's eyes, the name of the village where he'd learned to swim—to keep that one word safe. A binder. Someone who bound the Crown. Someone who could, presumably, unbind it.

She had spoken with him once, alone, under pretense of delivering documents. Her voice had been dry and quick, a voice used to being ignored in rooms full of important men. "Your Majesty. Does it speak to you?"

He had wanted, so badly, to say yes. He had looked at the door, at the guards, at the hair-thin silver threads he alone could see running from the Crown to the walls, to the stones, to the deep cold under everything, and he had said, "It says the winter must be fed."

"I know what it says." Her jaw had tightened. "My family wrote what it says. Three hundred years ago. We wrote the terms." And then, quieter, in the voice of a woman setting down a weight she had carried too far: "We were supposed to keep watch. Not feed it. There's a difference, and my grandmother—" She had stopped herself. Looked at him with those eyes that took inventory. "Can you hold on? Five years, perhaps less. There is a rite. There was always a rite. My grandmother knew it, and she—"

She had not finished. The Crown had been listening—it was always listening—and something in the room had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather, and she had felt it, and he had watched her understand that she had said too much, and he had watched her decide, with a speed he would envy for the rest of his eaten life, to run.

She had run. The Crown had sent nothing after her. It didn't need to. It had simply stopped feeding the low town for two days, and forty more people had frozen, and the court had spoken of the traitor scribe Nakamura who had abandoned her king, and the story had done the Crown's hunting for it, as stories always did.

But he had kept the word. *Binder.* He kept it the way the starving keep a seed under their tongue—not to eat, but to plant, someday, if there was ever a someday.

And then, last spring, the Crown had grown quiet in a way it never was, and he had felt it reach far away, farther than the mountains, farther than he could follow, and when it came back it was satisfied, and it said, *The last binder is dead. The line is ended. There will be no rite.*

And he had grieved, alone, in the dark, for a woman he had spoken to once.

* * *

*You are sulking,* the Crown observed, on what he chose to believe was a morning.

"I am resting. It's different. Sulking has more dignity."

*You make jokes because you are afraid.*

"I make jokes because I am *funny*, and because you can't eat humor. I've tested it. You've tried. It goes down like gravel."

A pause. The Crown's pauses were its closest approach to laughter. *It is true that we have never digested one of your jokes. They sit strangely. Perhaps humor is the one thing a debt cannot be levied against.*

"There's a sermon in that," Aldric said. "Write it down. Oh—wait."

The Crown did not laugh. But something in the vast cold arithmetic of it shifted, briefly, the way a ledger shifts when the accountant, against all policy, enjoys a line item.

This was the other thing he had learned, in nine years: the Crown was not evil. He had wanted it to be evil. Evil could be fought. Evil had a shape you could hate. But the Crown was a *creditor*, and creditors were worse than monsters, because a creditor believes it is the reasonable party in every room. The Crown had been forged—by his own kingdom, by the Nakamuras and the other forging families, by desperate people in a desperate winter—to end a famine, and it had ended the famine, and it had never once pretended the price would be anything other than what it was. It had told his great-great-grandfather the terms. It had told his father. It had told him, on the night of his coronation, with his father's body barely cold and the silver band descending toward his brow like a lid: *You will feed me, and the kingdom will live, and you will be remembered as a good king, and the remembering will cost you the memory.*

And he had said yes. That was the part he could not forgive, on the rare nights he had enough self left to do any forgiving. Not the Crown. Himself. Nine years old with Bristle, and he'd have said yes then too. Anyone would. That was the horror of it. That was the design. It was built to be sayable-yes-to, the way a loan is built to be signable, the way the fine print is built to be unread.

*The solstice comes,* the Crown said now, and its voice had changed, and the change went through him like ice water through a crack in a dam. *The long winter finishes falling. We have spoken of this.*

"We have." His heart—did he still have a heart, or only the memory of one, filed somewhere in the deep cold?—his heart did something anyway. *We have spoken of this* was what the Crown said before taking something large.

*The harvests of memory have thinned. The city hoards. Fear forgets fastest, and the city has run out of fear to sell. We must complete the feeding, or the deep cold rises, and everything ends—your kingdom, your people, the debt entire. You understand. You have always understood.*

"Complete it how?" He knew. He had always known; he had simply kept the knowing in a box at the bottom of himself, and now the Crown was opening the box with his own hands, as it did.

*All of it,* the Crown said, gently, reasonably, the way it said everything. *The city's remembering. Eight months of winter have made them soft in the mind. At the solstice, when the veil of the year is thinnest, we draw the rest through you. Every memory in the capital. Their names for their children. Their griefs. Their loves. It will keep us a century, and the deep cold will stay beneath the ice where it belongs, and the city will wake blank and peaceful and begin again, and you, Aldric—*

"And me."

*You will have nothing left. You will be a warm room with no one in it. But the kingdom will live, and you will have paid your debt entire, and there is a kind of peace in that. You have told me yourself there is a kind of peace in that.*

He had. He had told it that, in the third year, when the alternative was despair, and it had filed his words away the way it filed everything, against the day it would need them back as collateral.

He sat—was he sitting? he assembled the sensation of sitting, from spare parts—and he looked out through the one window of himself, down at the white city, at the bread lines and the quiet streets and the people trading their childhoods for loaves, and he thought about a woman with ink under her fingernails saying *there was always a rite*, and he thought: she said *my grandmother*. Which meant there had been others. Which meant the line had gone somewhere. Lines, like debts, do not end. They only change hands.

*Aldric,* the Crown said. *You are hiding something.*

"I am always hiding something. You've eaten most of it. There's very little left to hide, and I've become terribly possessive of the remainder."

*Not a memory. A hope.* It said the word the way a moneylender says *charity*. *Hopes are expensive, Aldric. I will have to collect.*

And far away, at the edge of the white city, at the edge of his one small window, he felt it—faint as a bubble under ice, faint as a dog's wet nose—a mind approaching the palace that smelled of ink.

The Crown felt it too. It went very still, the way the deep cold was still.

*No,* Aldric thought, and for the first time in nine years, he lied to it, wrapping the lie in every scrap of himself he had left: *It's nothing. A clerk. A petitioner. No one.*

The Crown considered. Somewhere below the throne room, in the dark under the dark, something old and patient began, very quietly, to count the days until the solstice.

* * *

That night—if it was night—he took out the word he had kept under his tongue for nine years and held it to what light remained.

*Binder.*

*Nakamura.*

*Hold on,* he whispered, to no one, to whoever was coming, to the seed under the ice. *Hold on. I am still in here. I am still, whatever is left of me, in here.*
