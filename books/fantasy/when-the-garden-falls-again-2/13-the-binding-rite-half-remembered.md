## 13. The Binding Rite, Half-Remembered

The palace archives smelled of cold candlewax and older paper, the way a tomb smells of stone and older bones.

Mara had told the archivist they were sent by the Chancellery of Winter Provisions to verify grain ledgers against the old tithe rolls, and the archivist—a small man named Pell with ink-stained cuffs and the resigned posture of someone who had not been asked an interesting question in forty years—had waved them through without looking up from his soup. Forgery, Mara was learning, was mostly a matter of knowing what bored people wanted to hear. Their mother would have been proud. Or appalled. With their mother it had always been both at once.

Three days now since the audience chamber. Three nights of lying awake in the rented room above the cooper's shop, seeing Aldric's face: the king mouthing words no one heard, his eyes like windows into a house where all the furniture had been sold off one piece at a time. *A clerk. A petitioner. No one.* He had lied for them. Mara was almost certain of it—certain the way you were certain of weather, which is to say without proof and with your whole body. The Crown had looked at them, that vast slow attention like a lighthouse beam dragging across a rowboat, and the king had spent whatever he had left to say: *nothing here.*

So. They owed him. And Mara Nakamura had grown up learning that debts were the only honest language in the world.

*Find out what he needs,* Yara had said, when Mara told her. *Then find out how much it costs.* Yara said everything that way now, flat and forward-leaning, a woman walking into wind. Since the shrine in the Gullet she had stopped threatening to take the map back, which was its own kind of frightening. She had started saying *we* instead of *I*, as if the word were a rope bridge she was testing with her weight before trusting it.

The archives occupied three floors under the west wing, and the third floor was the problem. The first two held ledgers, tax rolls, correspondence—the ordinary sediment of ruling. But the binding rites of kings, if such things existed—and after the throne room, Mara no longer permitted themselves the luxury of doubt—would be kept where the church kept them, catalogued under theology or law or, most likely, hidden inside something dull enough that no curious clerk ever pulled the box down.

Mara pulled down boxes. For six hours, they pulled down boxes.

It was nearly midnight by the archive's own dark candles when they found it—not a book about the rite but a book pretending not to be. *Regulations Concerning the Maintenance of Royal Vestments,* bound in grey calfskin, stamped with the seal of some long-defunct office of robes. Every page was exactly as boring as advertised. Except that every fourth leaf had been sewn in upside down, and when Mara took a knife to the binding—flinching, because destroying books went against thirty years of training and three hundred years of family instinct—the reversed leaves turned out to be written in a hand they knew.

Not knew. *Knew.*

The handwriting was their grandmother's.

Mara sat very still for a long time while the candle guttered. They had seen her hand only once, on the flyleaf of a smugglers' cant primer their mother kept wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of a chest: *For Ren, who reads everything twice.* The same narrow letters, the same way the descenders hooked left like little scythes. Here there was no flyleaf dedication. Here there were diagrams, marginalia, corrections in a second hand that had clearly annoyed her—*no, Sef, the circle must be drawn sunwise or it binds the binder too*—and instructions for a thing called, in a language Mara half recognized from the cant, the Rite of Unmaking.

She had come here. That was the first fact, and it landed in Mara's stomach like swallowed ice. Their grandmother—the binder who fled, the debt-dodger, the woman whose name was a curse word in Mara's private vocabulary—had stood in this same room, by this same light, writing down how to destroy the Crown. And then she had run anyway.

"Right," Mara said aloud, to the empty archive, because saying things aloud made them real, and they needed this real. "Let's see what you were so afraid of, Gran."

* * *

The rite took shape over the next day and a half, assembled from the vestments regulations, a hymnal whose last psalm had been scraped away and rewritten, and a scrap of vellum tucked inside a box labeled *Miscellaneous Petitions, Rejected, 201–214* that contained nothing else at all. Mara copied everything onto plain paper disguised as grain arithmetic. Pell wandered past twice and nodded at them with the deep approval of one bureaucrat observing another doing pointless work correctly.

By the second evening, Mara understood three things, and each one was worse than the last.

First: the rite worked. There were notes in the margins—test results, essentially, though "tested" apparently meant "performed on the Crown while it was still young and hungry." The Crown could be unbound from its host. It could be shattered. The method required no army, no miracle, only precision, preparation, and access to the coronation chapel on the right night. The solstice, the hymnal noted, was traditional. The solstice was nine days away.

Second: it required a willing heir of the forging families. Not present, not consenting—*willing*, in the old sense, the sense the cant still preserved: one who gives freely what cannot be taken. The forging families. Mara read the list of names twice, then a third time, hoping they'd misread. Ferro. Vane. Oyelaran. And Nakamura, set apart from the others, marked with a symbol Mara had seen only once before: pressed into the wax seal of a dying courier's map.

Their own blood. Of course it was their own blood. Debts ran downhill in families; that was practically the definition of a family.

But it was the third thing—the note in the margin, squeezed sideways in their grandmother's scythe-hooked hand, as if she'd hoped smaller writing might make it less true—that stopped Mara's breath.

*Unbinding alone saves neither. The Crown does not sit ON the king. By now it is root and branch of him. Break the vessel and you break what it holds. If we unmake the Crown, Aldric dies with it. No exceptions found in 3 attempts. I will not pay this. Let my children be angry with me; anger keeps better than grief.*

Mara put down the pen. They stood up. They walked, very calmly, to the far end of the archive room, stood facing a shelf of defunct customs tariffs, and screamed into their own cupped hands until their throat was raw.

Because here was the joke, the great cosmic ledger joke, the punchline three centuries in the making: the Crown fed on kings to hold back the cold, and the only way to free the kingdom was to kill the king, and their grandmother had known, had *written it down in her own tidy furious hand*, and had chosen instead to run and leave the bill to her descendants. Anger keeps better than grief. Well. Anger was keeping beautifully.

"You knew," Mara whispered at the shelf of tariffs. "You knew what it would cost, and you decided it wouldn't be yours."

And then, quieter, because rage burns down to something more stubborn underneath:

"But it's mine. Fine. It's mine. I'm the heir. I'm willing." They laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Gods help me, I'm already willing. I just wish somebody had asked me before I got attached to the idea of surviving this."

* * *

Yara was waiting in the cooper's loft, sitting on a flour barrel with her boots off and her knife across her knees, sharpening it in slow strokes. She'd taken to sleeping with it even here. Mara understood. In the capital, trust was a currency, and everyone was broke.

"You've got the look," Yara said, not looking up. "The scribe look. Like you've read something and now the rest of us have to live in it."

"I found her," Mara said. "My grandmother. Her notes. The whole rite."

The whetstone stopped. Yara looked up, and in the lamplight her face did the thing it had been doing more often lately—grief and attention fighting for the same ground. "And?"

"And it works. Sunwise circle, four names spoken, blood of a forging heir given freely, at the solstice, in the chapel where the Crown was first bound." Mara sat down across from her. "There's more."

"There's always more."

"The unbinding kills him." Mara made themselves say it plainly; kindness deferred was cruelty compounded. "The Crown isn't a hat, Yara. After nine years it's grown into him—root and branch, her words. You can't pull a root out of a tree and expect the tree to stand. We break the Crown, King Aldric dies. It's not a risk. It's a certainty. Three attempts, she wrote. Three attempts, three dead hosts."

Yara was silent for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the cooper's dog dreamed audibly about something worth barking at.

"Nine years," Yara said finally. "He's been drowning for nine years, and nobody threw him a rope because throwing the rope meant—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. When she spoke again her voice was low and terribly steady. "Tomas knew."

"What?"

"Tomas. My brother. Your courier." She was standing now, pacing the length of the loft, four strides and turn. "The last month, before he took this job—he was different. He'd been somewhere. He came back thin, and quiet, and he burned papers in our mother's stove, and he told me—" her voice caught, and she pushed through it like a woman forcing a door in a flood, "he told me that if anything happened to him, I should find the person carrying the map and stay close to them. Stay *close*. Not take the map. Not sell it. He said the map would find the right hands because it was made to, and that whoever carried it would need someone beside them who wasn't afraid to die angry."

"Mara," she said, and now she did stop, facing them, tears running freely down a face that refused to make any other concession to feeling. "He knew it would cost a life. He knew, and he carried it anyway, straight into the teeth of the inquisitors, because he thought a king's life—even a stranger's, even a hollowed-out stranger's—was worth his." She laughed, wet and furious. "And I spent a month trying to rob you. I put a knife to the throat of the person my brother died to protect."

"He didn't die to protect me," Mara said gently. "He died to deliver this." They held up the pages of grain arithmetic. "I'm just the wrapping."

"No." Yara wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way you'd dry dishes. "No, don't do that. Don't do the humble-scribe thing. He chose you. He saw the seal light up for you—I was there, at the gate, before I followed you. The wax glowed when it touched your hands. It never glowed for anyone else. Whatever your family did to that Crown, it's been waiting three hundred years for a Nakamura to come collect." She sat back down on the barrel. "So. We kill a king to save a kingdom. Tell me the rest. All of it. I want the price written down like a proper invoice."

Mara told her. The solstice. The chapel. The four names—Ferro, Vane, Oyelaran, Nakamura—spoken by a willing heir. The blood, freely given, which the old texts distinguished carefully from blood taken, coerced, or traded, and which Mara suspected was the entire hinge of the whole machine: the Crown ran on unwilling payment, and only an unforced gift could undo it. Yara listened without interrupting, sharpening nothing, holding the knife like a rosary.

When Mara finished, Yara was quiet again. Then: "Vane."

"The inquisitor? She's one of the forging families?"

"Serel Vane." Yara said the name like spitting a seed. "Her order's been hunting Tomas's map for months. If the rite needs a Vane's blessing—if it needs any of the families—" She shook her head. "That's not a rope, Mara. That's a noose with good manners."

"It doesn't need her blessing. It needs the names spoken. One heir speaking all four lines would do it—my grandmother's notes say the families' powers were merged into the Crown at the forging, so one voice can carry all four." Mara hesitated. "But it has to be willing. Freely given. And I keep thinking—" They stopped.

"Say it."

"I keep thinking about her eyes. When she caught me in the gallery. She didn't report me. She stood there with her hand on her sword hilt and she *didn't report me*, and she looked at the king through that grille like—" Mara searched for the word. "Like a woman reading a ledger she suspects was cooked. She's debt-bound herself; the fence told us the church indentures its inquisitors. Maybe she's not the enemy. Maybe she's just another debtor who hasn't read her own contract."

"Or maybe she's exactly what she looks like," Yara said, "which is a crown's dog with a clever face. Tomas trusted the map, not people. I'll trust people when they've earned it the expensive way." She slid the knife into her belt and stood. "Nine days. What do you need from me?"

"Two things. First—help me copy these notes. Everything, three copies, hidden separately. If we're taken, the rite survives us."

"That's the scribe answer. What's the second thing?"

Mara looked at her. "Be ready to hate me. On the solstice night, whatever happens, whatever I have to say or do or promise to get us into that chapel—be ready to hate me for it, and follow anyway."

Yara studied them for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—a small, crooked, exhausted smile, the first Mara had seen from her since the rockslide.

"Scribe," she said, "my brother carried a map that glows for you across half a kingdom with inquisitors on his heels. I think he knew exactly what kind of person would need to say terrible things to finish this. Be ready to be forgiven." She turned toward the ladder, then paused. "One more thing. This heir. Willing blood, freely given. Does the rite say what happens to *them*?"

Mara had read those lines a dozen times. *The giver stands within the circle and is not harmed; the gift passes through and is spent.* Their grandmother's margin note beneath it: *This part is true. I tested everything else; believe me on this.*

"It says the giver lives," Mara said. "It's the only generous line in the whole document. Which is either the truth or the bait."

"Gran's notes," Yara said, climbing down. "Which gran? The one who wrote the rite, or the one who ran?"

Below, the street door opened and closed. Footsteps on the stairs—heavy, unhurried, two sets, one of them pausing at each step with the particular rhythm of someone counting doors.

Yara's hand went to her knife. Mara blew out the lamp.

In the sudden dark, through the gap in the shutters, lamplight moved in the alley below—and with it, catching the snowfall, the pale gleam of a church tabard. Two voices murmured. One of them, low and level and unmistakable even through wood and winter, said:

"This is the one. Serel, are you certain? Because once we knock, there's no unknocking."

And Serel Vane's voice answered, and Mara could not tell, from tone or silence or anything human, whether it was mercy or arithmetic when she said:

"I'm certain. Knock."
