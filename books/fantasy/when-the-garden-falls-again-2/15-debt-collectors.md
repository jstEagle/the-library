## 15. Debt Collectors

They took Mara in the third hour past midnight, in the archive corridor where the candles burned green and the dust tasted of old vellum.

Mara had known it was coming. You could not spend three days reconstructing a forbidden rite in the palace archives without someone noticing, and Serel Vane noticed everything. So when the shadows at the end of the corridor resolved into four figures in grey-and-silver, Mara did the only sensible thing: set down the candle, folded their hands, and waited.

"Master Nakamura." Serel stepped forward, her lantern held high. Up close, in the green light, she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "You've been busy."

"I'm a scribe," Mara said. "Busy is what I am."

"You've been copying from the sealed folios. The ones that require my seal to open."

"Your seal was on the desk. I assumed it was an invitation."

One of the guards made a sound that might have been a laugh before remembering himself. Serel did not smile, but something at the corner of her mouth twitched—the ghost of a smile, or the memory of one.

"You understand," she said, "that I have to take you in."

"I understand that you *get* to take me in. There's a difference. One of those is duty. The other is opportunity wearing duty's coat."

"And which are you offering me?"

"The rite," Mara said quietly. "The whole of it. The binding, the unbinding, what it costs, who can perform it. Everything I found in those folios. In exchange for Yara Ferro's release."

The corridor went very still. Even the candles seemed to lean in.

Serel studied them for a long moment. "She betrayed you."

"She did what starving people do. She traded the only thing she had left—me—for the only thing she wanted—her brother's name cleared. I'd have done the same. Probably faster, and with more swearing." Mara shrugged. "I want her out of your cells. That's the price. No Yara, no rite. And you'll find, Inquisitor Vane, that I am the only person alive who can give you the rite complete. My grandmother saw to that when she scattered it across six documents in six different hands. She was thorough. It runs in the family."

Serel's lantern dipped slightly, as if her arm had grown heavy. "And if I simply have you flogged until you talk?"

"Then you'll get the rite with one piece missing, and you won't know which piece until solstice night, when it fails, and the Crown finishes eating this city." Mara met her eyes. "You believe the Crown protects the realm. I know. I've read your letters—you keep drafts, did you know that? You draft everything twice. But whatever you think it protects, it needs a binding renewed by solstice or it takes the city instead. Ask yourself what your superiors were doing last week, when they couldn't remember their own mothers' names. Ask yourself why Chancellor Ives signed an order with the wrong hand."

Something flickered behind Serel's eyes—doubt, or its close cousin, fear.

"Yara Ferro walks free," Mara said. "Tonight. Before I say another word."

* * *

They kept Mara waiting in a small room with one window and no chair, which struck them as a particular kind of pettiness. Whoever designed interrogation rooms, Mara thought, clearly resented chairs.

The window overlooked the inner courtyard, where snow fell without wind, straight down, like ash. Eight months of winter. The drifts against the far wall came up to a guard's shoulder, and nobody shoveled them anymore because there was nowhere left to put the snow. The city had stopped fighting winter sometime around the fourth month and started merely enduring it, the way a debtor stops arguing with a ledger and starts just reading it.

Mara counted the things they knew, the way their mother had taught them. *When you're afraid, count what you actually hold. Fear lies about the inventory.*

They held: the rite, complete, in their head. A name—Nakamura—that meant both debt and key. A grandmother they had never met who had apparently been either a hero or a coward, depending on which document you believed. And one bargain, half-struck, resting on the word of an inquisitor.

Not much. But more than nothing. Their mother would have said it was a fortune.

The door opened near dawn. Serel stood in it alone, no guards, which was itself a message.

"She's released," Serel said. "Out the river gate, with coin for passage and a pardon under seal. Her brother's record is expunged as of this morning. He died in service, officially. Carrying dispatches."

Mara let out a breath they hadn't known they were holding. It shook a little on the way out. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Come with me. Someone wants to meet you."

"Someone?"

Serel's jaw worked. "I don't know how else to say it. Come and see. And Master Nakamura—" she hesitated, and for a moment she looked less like an inquisitor and more like a woman standing at the edge of something she'd spent her whole life being told was solid ground, "—whatever happens in that room, remember that I gave you Ferro first. Remember the order of things."

"I will," Mara said, and meant it, which frightened them more than anything else that morning.

* * *

The king's private chapel sat at the heart of the palace like a swallowed stone. Mara had expected grandeur; what they got was emptiness. The pews were bare, the saints' niches vacant, the great windows shuttered against a cold that had long since stopped caring about glass. Someone had once loved this room. Now it was a place where love had been, the way a scar is a place where skin was.

Aldric sat alone beneath the empty coronation canopy.

He was smaller than his portraits. The portraits showed a broad-shouldered man with a beard like hammered silver; the thing in the chair was thin as a winter branch, wrapped in a fur robe that seemed to wear him rather than the other way around. His eyes were open. They were the worst part—not dead, not mad, but *waiting*, the way a well waits, deep and dark and patient.

Serel stopped at the door. "I'll be outside," she said, and her voice was strange, and then she was gone, and the door closed with a sound like a book shutting.

Mara stood in the aisle, uncertain whether to bow. Bowing felt obscene. Not bowing felt dangerous. They settled for a scribe's greeting, hand flat over the heart, the gesture used for records and their keepers.

"Your Majesty."

For a long moment, nothing. Then Aldric's lips moved.

"*Sssss.*"

The sound was wrong. Too many voices in one throat, layered, like a choir heard through a wall. Aldric's head turned—not the way a man turns his head, but the way a compass needle finds north, smooth and inevitable—and fixed on Mara.

"*Little scribe.*"

Mara's skin prickled. Every instinct they owned, every smuggler's reflex their mother had drilled into them, screamed the same word: *run*. They stayed. Partly from courage, mostly from the realization that their knees had locked.

"You're the Crown," Mara said. "Not the king."

"*The king sleeps,*" the voices said, gentle as snowfall. "*He dreams. I keep him safe in the dreaming. As I have kept all of them. Three hundred years, little scribe. Nine kings. Ask them if they suffered.*"

"They can't answer. You ate their answers."

"*I ate their winters.*" Something moved behind Aldric's face—a ripple, like a reflection disturbed. "*Come closer. Let me look at you. Nakamura. Yes. I know the name. I have been waiting a long time for a Nakamura to walk up this aisle.*"

Mara did not come closer. But they did not leave, and after a moment the Crown continued, and its voice changed—it softened, warmed, took on a texture almost human, almost kind.

"*Your grandmother sat where you stand. Sana Nakamura. Last of the binders. She had your hands—I remember her hands. Ink in every crease. She wrote so fast when she was frightened.*"

"I've read her letters."

"*Have you? Then you know she broke her oath. The binding must be renewed each generation by a willing heir of the forging families, and when her time came, she ran. She hid the rite in pieces and fled into the mountains and told herself she was protecting the world, when the truth is simpler and sadder: she was afraid. As you are afraid now. As everyone is afraid. It is the one tax I cannot forgive.*"

"Fear isn't a debt."

"*Everything is a debt.*" The Crown's voice curled around the words like smoke. "*That is the first law, little scribe, older than your monastery, older than your kingdom. Everything is owed to something. The question is only ever: to whom, and on what terms. Your abbot holds your papers. I could hold them instead—or burn them entirely.*"

Mara went very still. "What do you mean?"

"*I mean,*" the Crown said, and now Aldric's body leaned forward in the chair, slow as a drawbridge lowering, "*that I know what you want. Do not insult us both by pretending otherwise. You want the paper that says Isandro's child owes Thornfell nothing. You want the ledger line closed. You have wanted it since you were small enough to hide under your mother's copying table, listening to her count years she didn't have.*"

"How do you—"

"*I am the Crown. I have worn nine kings, and kings see everything written in their realm, and your family's indenture is written very deeply indeed. Here is my offer.*" Aldric's right hand rose, trembling, and pointed at a table beside the chair. On it lay a single document, sealed in wax the color of dried blood. "*Your freedom papers. Signed, witnessed, lawful, final. And beneath them, a royal writ restoring the name Nakamura to the rolls of honor—your grandmother's shame struck out, her service recorded, her heirs ennobled. No more hiding. No more smugglers' cant whispered in cellars. Your mother's debt: paid. Your grandmother's name: clean. Your own hands: free to write anything, anywhere, forever.*"

The silence rang.

Mara stared at the document. It was exactly what they had dreamed of—literally dreamed, in the bad nights, in the good ones, the fantasy that kept them copying ledgers through Abbot Corren's sneers: the paper that ended it. Freedom, certified. Ancestry, restored. Everything stolen, handed back with interest.

"That's it?" Mara said, and hated how their voice cracked. "No price?"

"*There is always a price. But mine is honest, which is more than your abbot's was. Renew the binding. One heir, willing, of the forging lines. Chain me to the next king, as your grandmother should have chained me to this one, and go free. That is all. That is what your family has always done—one generation pays, the next goes free, and the world turns warm.*"

"One generation pays," Mara repeated slowly. "And the one after that. And the one after that. Forever. That's not a debt, that's a *lineage of debts*. You're not offering to close my ledger. You're offering to make me the one who writes it onto someone else's children."

"*Is that so terrible?*" For the first time, something raw surfaced beneath the honeyed voices—something that sounded, horribly, like pleading. "*The alternative is the cold. Little scribe, you have not seen what I hold back. Beneath this kingdom there is a hunger older than hunger, and I am the lid on that pot, and every memory I take, every winter I stretch, every king I hollow—I take them so that children in Thornfell can learn their letters without freezing. I am not cruel. I am *arithmetic*. Someone must pay. I am merely the collector who keeps the collection from becoming slaughter.*"

"Until this winter," Mara said. "Eight months. Your arithmetic is failing. You need more than a renewal now—you need the solstice feeding, the city's memories, all of it. Your own collectors told me. So this offer isn't mercy. It's a drowning man selling rope."

Aldric's face did something terrible then: it smiled. All the voices at once, harmonized, warm as a hearth.

"*Yes,*" the Crown said. "*I am drowning. And you are the rope. And here is the truth that makes my offer what it is, little scribe: you will say yes anyway. Not today. Perhaps not tomorrow. But I have watched nine kings convince themselves, and I have learned that everyone, given enough cold and enough fear, chooses the paper over the people. Your grandmother did. She meant to renew the binding. She came all this way, stood where you stand. And then she thought of her daughter—your mother, a baby in a mountain village—and she chose the paper. She chose to run and live. I do not blame her. I have never blamed her. Blame is for those who have never been offered the choice.*"

The words landed like stones in still water, and the ripples went out and out.

Because that was the poison in it, Mara realized. Not the threat. The *understanding*. The Crown wasn't snarling. It was sympathizing. It knew exactly what Mara's freedom was worth because it had priced it against a grandmother's love and watched the scales tip. It had already won this argument once, three hundred years ago, in this same aisle, with better blood than Mara's.

"I need time," Mara heard themselves say.

"*Of course.*" The warmth never wavered. "*Take until solstice. Three days. Consider it a grace period—every creditor grants one; it makes the eventual payment feel like a choice.*" Aldric's head tilted, and for one flickering instant—half a heartbeat, no more—the hollowness in the king's eyes shifted, and something small and drowned looked out through them. The lips moved again, and the voice was one voice, cracked and human and barely there:

"*Don't—*" 

Then the tide came back in, and the many voices returned, mild as milk: "*Go home, little scribe. Rest. Eat something. You'll think better fed.*"

Mara walked backward up the aisle, unwilling to turn their back on the thing in the chair, and their heel found the door, and Serel's hand caught their shoulder and steered them out into the grey corridor, and the door shut behind them, and only then did Mara discover they were shaking so hard their teeth chattered.

Serel said nothing. She walked them back through the frozen halls, past guards who did not look up, and at the door of the guest room where Mara would be kept—comfortably, courteously, imprisoned—she finally spoke.

"Well?"

"He offered me everything I've ever wanted," Mara said. "That's how I know he's lying. Or worse—" they laughed, a thin, awful sound, "—that's how I know he isn't."

"Three days," Serel said. "Solstice is in three days."

"I know."

"And Nakamura—" Serel glanced up and down the corridor, then leaned close, her voice dropping to almost nothing. "Whatever you're going to do. Whatever you're *actually* going to do. The Crown watches this palace like it watches its kings. But it doesn't watch the bread ovens. Nobody watches the bread ovens. Everyone thinks the baker's boy is simple, and he isn't, and he owes Ferro's sister a debt from the old days, and she's asking for you by name."

Mara stared at her.

"I gave you Ferro first," Serel said. "Remember the order of things." And she walked away down the corridor, her boots ringing on stone, leaving Mara alone with three days, a choice shaped like a trap, and a message hidden in a bakery—while somewhere beneath the chapel, patient as winter, the Crown began to count down the hours until solstice, when the long winter would finish falling, one way or another.
