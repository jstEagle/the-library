## 16. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

### Mara

They gave Mara a room with a window, which she understood at once to be an insult rather than a kindness. The window looked out over the palace's inner garden—a courtyard of black hedges and a dry fountain, buried in snow that had stopped pretending to be temporary—and the message was plain as a ledger line: *look what we let you see.*

She had three days until solstice. She had ink, paper, a fire that never quite caught, and a guard outside her door who had introduced himself as Pell and then fallen asleep sitting up within the hour, which told her either that she was considered harmless or that the palace guard was running on fumes and bread-bought memories like everyone else in the city.

Probably both.

Mara sat on the bed with her back to the wall and did what she did best, which was bookkeeping. Assets: one rite half-reconstructed from memory and archive scraps; one grandmother's marginalia decoded down to its final instruction—*a willing heir of the forging lines must hold the name open while the Crown is unmade*—and a working theory, still untested, about how to make the Crown believe she was accepting its bargain right up until the moment she wasn't. Liabilities: everything else.

Yara was in a cell somewhere below the east wing. Serel had said so without saying so, the way inquisitors do—"your associate is being kept comfortably"—and Mara had spent the first night mapping the palace by sound. Boots on stone. A kitchen two floors down, exhaling the smell of boiled grain at fixed hours. A chapel bell that rang wrong, flat and hungry, every time the Crown beneath it stirred.

And the Crown was stirring more each hour. Mara could feel it through the walls now, a pressure behind the eyes like the moment before snowmelt gives way and takes the road with it. It knew what she was. Her grandmother's blood sang in it the way a debtor's name sings in a creditor's ledger.

On the morning of the second day—the day before solstice—a servant brought her bread and, folded inside the napkin, nothing at all. That was the message. *Nothing.* Which meant the bakery drop had been found, or had never been checked, or had been found by someone who couldn't read smuggler's cant and had rewrapped the napkin out of pure bureaucratic habit.

Mara ate the bread and laughed until she cried a little, quietly, so the sleeping guard wouldn't stir.

Then she got to work. If no one was coming to her, she would go to the plan itself. There was a loose stone under the window—there is always a loose stone under a window; palaces are built by the same careless optimism as everything else—and behind it, a service channel wide enough for a thin person carrying very little regret.

She left the room tidy. Whatever else happened tomorrow, she refused to owe the Crown the indignity of a mess.

### Yara

The cell had a straw mattress, a bucket, and a slot in the door through which bread arrived twice a day from hands Yara was beginning to recognize by their hesitancy. The young guard—Pell, though he'd only said his name once and then blushed about it—had started leaving the bread slightly angled so she could pull the napkin through too.

"You're going to get in trouble," she told him on the second day.

"For a napkin?"

"For caring."

He went red as the wax seals on inquisitor writs and left. Yara sat down on the straw and hated herself with the thoroughness she usually reserved for broken compasses.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not stop doing: Tomaso had died in a snowdrift with a map in his fists, and she had spent a month chasing the map instead of the reason he'd carried it. He'd known. He must have known what the Crown was, what it cost—he'd been courier to people who remembered—and instead of telling his own sister, he'd run the errand, because the errand paid, because paying meant eating, because everyone sold something to live and he'd sold the truth one delivery at a time until the truth killed him on the monastery steps.

And when they'd finally given her a chance to sell something herself—to trade Mara Nakamura for Tomaso's name spoken kindly over a grave—she had wavered. Not long. Long enough. Long enough that she would carry the length of that wavering to her own grave, however far off, however cold.

"They'll kill you anyway," she told the ceiling, in case Tomaso was taking messages up there. "That's the thing about them. They always need one more delivery."

The ceiling declined to answer, which was more honesty than most people managed.

That night—the last night before solstice—the bells began ringing early. Wrong-early, flat, hungry, and the whole palace seemed to lean toward the sound like wheat toward sun. Yara heard boots in the corridor, lots of them, moving toward the chapel levels. She heard Pell's voice arguing with someone about whether prisoners were to be moved or left, and the other voice winning, because the other voice outranked caring.

Which meant: empty corridor. One lock, old iron, older than the Crown and therefore possibly less corrupt.

Yara had grown up picking her brother's locks for fun, back when fun was free. She'd forgotten that until this exact moment, kneeling in the dark with a bread-knife and a hairpin, hands steady for the first time in a month.

The lock took four minutes. The corridor took none. And the guilt came along, of course—it always came along, it was family too—but it came along *walking*, and walking was better than sitting.

### Aldric

*(the part of him that was still Aldric)*

There had been a garden once. He was nearly sure. Roses, or something rose-shaped, and a woman who deadheaded them in the rain because she said rain was when the pruning mattered. His mother? His wife? The memory was soft-edged now, eaten to lace, and he had learned not to press on the lacy parts because pressing made them crumble faster.

Beneath him—or around him, or *through* him—the Crown counted. He could feel it counting, the way you feel your own pulse when you're frightened. Three centuries of winters fed to it and it was still hungry, and tomorrow was solstice, and tomorrow it intended to eat not just its king but the city entire: every name, every grief, every small warm fact that made forty thousand people into persons. It would store them against the cold like a miser stores grain, and it would call it survival, and in a hundred years no one would know anything had ever been taken, because no one would remember there had ever been anything to take.

It showed him the bargain it had shown the scribe. Freedom papers. Restored names. An easy life in a warm room. It thought these were the weights that moved people. It had worn kings so long it had forgotten what moved anyone else.

But it had made one error, and Aldric hugged the error to himself like a coal in both hands: it had let him keep *one* memory intact, the oldest one, the load-bearing one. The woman in the garden. Not his mother. Not his wife.

A binder. Dark-eyed, impatient, ink-stained to the elbow. Nakamura, the name stitched into him like a label into a coat. She had chained the Crown to his line and wept while she did it, and she had told him—told the boy he had been, the boy who was the only part of himself the Crown couldn't digest—

*"When one of mine comes to unmake you, king, let them in."*

He had held that sentence for thirty years in the drowning dark, holding his breath, essentially, all this time.

Tomorrow, if any scrap of him remained to notice, he intended to be hospitable.

### Serel

Serel Vane stood in the coronation chapel at midnight and watched workmen she didn't recognize arrange candles in a pattern she did not authorize, and felt the specific vertigo of a career civil servant realizing the org chart has rooms she was never shown.

"Who ordered the candlework?" she asked the nearest workman.

"The steward," he said, and did not look at her, and his eyes had the flat shine of a man who has recently sold something and is trying not to count the change.

She found the steward in his office, or found a man wearing the steward's posture, sorting ledgers with fingers that moved too smoothly, like water deciding to be a hand. The office smelled faintly of frost.

"Chancellor Vane." The voice was the steward's voice played through the steward's mouth by something patient underneath. "You've served the Crown faithfully for nineteen years."

"I've served the church," Serel said. "The church serves the Crown. I've never actually met the Crown before. It's a pleasure, I'm sure."

"It will simplify things if you think of tonight as an audit." The smooth fingers turned a page. "At solstice, arrears come due across the city. Debts of memory, mostly. Small ones. No one will miss what no one remembers missing. You will keep the guard cooperative and the gates orderly, and in spring you will find your own debts—forgiven. All of them. Your mother's debts too. Wasn't that the word? Forgiven?"

Serel's mother had died in a debtors' rowhouse when Serel was eleven, and the church had buried her in an unmarked grave because marked graves were an expense, and Serel had spent her whole life since being *reasonable* about it, being *pragmatic*, being the kind of person who understood that systems were systems.

"Yes," she heard herself say. "That was the word."

"Then we understand each other."

She walked out of the office on legs that belonged to someone calmer. In the corridor she stopped, put one hand against the cold stone, and did the thing she had trained herself never to do in front of subordinates, which was to feel something all the way through.

Nineteen years. Nineteen years of believing the Crown held back the winter, that the feeding was regrettable but necessary, that mercy was a budget line you funded when you could. And the thing in the steward's office hadn't mentioned holding back winter at all. It had talked like a landlord. Like the famine years had never ended and never would, because famines were profitable if you owned the grain.

*You've seen what it does,* Mara Nakamura had said to her, handcuffed, furious, *to your own superiors' minds.* And Serel had filed the sentence away as rhetoric.

Unfiled now.

She went to the guard barracks, woke the night sergeant, and gave orders in her best chancellor's voice: double watch on the prisoner cells, no movements authorized below the chapel level, and anyone carrying candles after midnight was to be detained for questioning.

"And if the steward countermands?" the sergeant asked.

"Detain the steward," Serel said, "for questioning," and heard, distantly, from the direction of the east wing, the particular clangor of a cell door opening that no one had authorized either.

### Yara

Getting out of the cell was the easy part. Getting *anywhere* was the hard part, because the palace at pre-dawn on solstice eve was a maze wearing a costume of order, and every corridor had either boots or candles in it, and the candles were worse. Nobody carried candles that color. Nobody carried candles arranged in threes.

She was pressed into an alcove behind a suit of armor—moldy, moth-eaten armor, some king's idea of heritage—when she heard footsteps stop directly beside her head, and a voice, low and familiar and entirely unwelcome:

"If you breathe on me, Ferro, I'll have you arrested on principle."

"Pell?" she whispered.

"The guard," he corrected, miserably. "Guard Pell. Who is currently committing about four crimes." He slid a bundle through the alcove's shadow: her knife, her coat, and a folded square of cheap bakery paper. "Chancellor Vane's orders. Detain all unauthorized movement. I am detaining you *very* thoroughly, this way, toward the kitchens, where there's a coal chute nobody guards because nobody remembers it exists."

Yara stared at him. "Why?"

"Because you pulled my napkin through the slot," Pell said, "and thanked me. Nobody thanks anybody anymore. The whole city's gone strange. Everyone's polite and nobody's *kind*, if that makes sense, and yesterday I caught myself unable to remember my sister's face, only that I have a sister, and I think—I think whatever's happening tomorrow, it's already starting, and I'd rather be on the side that says thank you." He was quiet a moment. "Also the chancellor gave me a direct order and I've decided to interpret it creatively. Don't tell her the creative part."

"I wouldn't dream of it," Yara said, and meant it, and discovered that meaning things again felt like a muscle coming back after a long illness.

The coal chute was exactly as forgettable as advertised. She came out into the alley behind the kitchens, into air that tasted of iron and ending, and unfolded the bakery paper by the light of the wrong-colored candles bleeding through the windows above.

It was written in cant—the smugglers' cant, her mother's cant, *Mara's* cant—and it was short, because good plans are short and bad ones are essays:

*Solstice noon. Chapel undercroft. The rite needs three: one of the forging blood to hold the name open, one to hold the circle, one blade willing to turn. I have the first. Come be the second. Bring your grief — it's the only honest weapon left. — M.*

*P.S. If you're reading this angry, good. Anger keeps. Use it on the Crown, not on yourself. It's had enough of our families' payments. Let's close the account.*

Yara read it twice. Then she read the postscript a third time and had to stand with her forehead against the frozen brick of the bakery wall for a while, breathing, because the woman whose freedom she had tried to sell had left her, in a coal-chute message, the exact absolution she had no right to expect and less right to accept.

*It's had enough of our families' payments.*

Tomaso's face, finally, instead of Tomaso's death. Him at fourteen, teaching her to pick locks for fun. *Everyone sells something to live, Yara. Just don't sell the same thing twice.*

"Well," she said aloud, to the alley, to the city, to the hungry bells. "I'm done selling."

Noon was six hours away. She had a knife, a coat, a compass that still pointed wrong but pointed *somewhere*, and a grief that had finally been issued orders.

Yara Ferro went to war with the only army she had, which was one person, and for the first time since the snowdrift, marching felt better than standing still.

### Mara

The undercroft beneath the coronation chapel was older than the palace, older than the kingdom's manners, a round stone room where the forging families had once done their worst arithmetic. Mara reached it an hour before dawn through the service channel and a locked door her grandmother's marginalia had described with insulting accuracy (*third hinge, tap twice, the masons owed us favors*), and set about preparing the circle while the Crown's attention pressed down through the ceiling like weather.

It knew. Of course it knew. It had offered her the world and she had smiled and said *let me consider the terms*, and it had believed her, because three hundred years of kings had taught it that everyone sells something, and it could not imagine a person who lied about the price only to pay a different one.

*You will fail,* it said through the stones, conversational, almost kind. *Not because you are weak. Because you are owed, and the owed always come to collect, and collectors can be bought. Your grandmother learned this. Your mother. You are simply the last entry in a very old ledger, child, and ledgers end.*

"My grandmother wrote in margins," Mara said, laying the chalk-line with steady hands. "Margins are where the corrections go."

Above her, the bells began to ring in the new day—solstice, the shortest one, the one the Crown had waited three hundred years to finish spending—and the light through the grate turned the color of old wax, and somewhere in the palace a coal chute banged open, and a chapel stair filled with boots, some friendly, some not, and Mara Nakamura picked up the chalk and wrote her family name into the circle, not as a debt this time.

As a signature.

The doors at the top of the stair boomed—once, twice—and then a voice she knew, hoarse and furious and alive, shouting her name down through the dark, and Mara smiled at the circle, at the Crown, at the whole rotten magnificent account of the world.

"Right on time," she said. "Let's close the books."
