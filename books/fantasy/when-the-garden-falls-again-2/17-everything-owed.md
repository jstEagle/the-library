## 17. Everything Owed

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that only liars and bookkeepers learn well: the sum that must appear correct on the page while being wrong underneath it. I had spent eleven years copying ledgers at Thornfell, and so when I knelt before the thing wearing King Aldric's face and said, in a voice I made sure sounded properly broken, "I accept your bargain," I confess I was thinking about column alignment.

The Crown seemed pleased. It is difficult to describe how a crown can seem pleased, but imagine a smile made entirely of interest accruing.

"Then it is settled," Aldric said—or the mouth of him said, while somewhere far behind his eyes something small and drowning watched through the windows. "Your freedom papers by week's end. Your grandmother's name restored to the rolls. And in exchange, you will renew the binding at the solstice, and the long winter will end, and the realm will be spared."

"Yes," I said. "All of it. Every debt paid."

Every debt paid—except the one I intended to collect.

I bowed low enough that my hair fell forward and hid my face, which was fortunate, because my face at that moment was doing something no negotiator's face should do. Somewhere between terror and glee lies a grin that would get a smuggler hanged, and I wore it all the way down the long gallery, past guards who did not see me, past servants who had sold so many memories for bread that they no longer remembered why they were carrying the trays they carried.

Yara was waiting in the coal cellar, which had become our headquarters on the grounds that nobody audits a coal cellar. She looked up from sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen—she has six knives, and sharpens whichever one is closest when she is nervous, which is all of them, always—and read my expression in about half a second.

"He said yes," she said flatly.

"I said yes."

"You said yes." She put down the knife. Picked up another one. "Say it again, slowly, so I can decide whether to hit you."

"It's called a ruse, Yara. Smugglers use them. Your brother used one to get a shipment of Frostfall brandy past the Salt Stair tolls, according to his own letters, which I have now memorized out of guilt."

"Do not talk about Tam's letters like you knew him."

"I'm sorry." And I meant it, mostly, though there is a kind of apology that is also a bookkeeping entry—a line written so the account looks balanced—and I could not tell anymore which kind mine was. That was the trouble with the whole plan. To deceive the Crown, I had to mean my lie so thoroughly that even I half-believed it, and every day the border between the Mara who had accepted the bargain and the Mara preparing treason grew fainter, like ink washed twice.

"The solstice is four days off," Yara said. "Serel?"

"Serel comes tonight. She says the archive rotation changes at moonrise and after that the binding chamber will be watched."

"And if she's lying?"

"Then we're caught, and I get my freedom papers early, in the form of a very short life."

Yara almost smiled. Almost. With her it counted double.

* * *

Serel Vane arrived at the coal cellar door looking, for the first time since I'd known her, like a woman whose ledger had been audited by someone honest.

She had told me what she'd seen—I'll come back to that, because I still cannot think of it without my hands going cold—but the practical version was this: High Inquisitor Dornave, her superior for nine years, the man who had taught her the catechism of the Crown and how to hold a questioner's silence like an instrument, had sat down to supper three nights ago and asked her, pleasantly, what her mother's name was. He had known her mother. He had buried her mother. He had wept at the funeral and paid for the candles.

Dornave had traded that memory. Not under duress. Not for bread, as the citizens in the lines below did. He had traded his grief for his daughter's advancement—the Crown had offered to ease a rival out of his path, quietly, and he had paid with the memory of the woman whose loss had made him kind in the first place. Serel had found the transaction record in the church ledgers, filed in her own hand from two years ago, which she did not remember writing.

"I've been keeping accounts for them," she said now, sitting on a crate of coal dust with her sword across her knees like a cat she no longer trusted. "Nine years. I thought I was protecting the realm through winter. What I was doing was collections."

"You believed," I said gently.

"Belief is just a debt you don't know you owe yet." She said it bitterly, then paused, hearing herself. "That's rather good. Write that down."

"I intend to steal it for the novel I'll never have time to write."

"Grief later," Yara said, in her customary role as the world's least patient chaperone. "The rite. Walk me through it again, scribe. All of it. Every cost. If I'm going to die holding a circle in a chapel, I want to know precisely what I'm dying of."

So I laid it out, the way I had learned to lay out a monastery ledger: plainly, in order, hiding nothing, because the whole point of honest bookkeeping is that the numbers are allowed to be terrible.

"The unbinding requires three things. First, a willing heir of the forging families—that's me; the Nakamura blood is the lock the Crown was built around. Second, the true name of the host spoken by someone outside the binding—" I nodded at Yara, who had extracted Aldric's birth name from a palace laundry registry, of all places, three days of digging through linen inventories, and had been insufferably proud of it ever since. "Third, a circle held by someone bound to the Crown by oath, who must turn that oath inside out at the moment of unbinding. Serel."

"Turning an oath inside out," Serel said. "Is that a technical term or poetry?"

"Both. The rite doesn't break oaths—it reverses their direction. Whatever you swore to give the Crown, you take back. Which means whatever protection your order's vows grant you will be withdrawn at exactly the moment the Crown fights hardest. It will know you're turning. It will try to collect everything you've ever promised it. At once."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning you should spend tomorrow remembering every promise you've ever made, and deciding which ones you can live without."

Serel was quiet for a while. Coal dust settled on her shoulders like premature snow. "And the host," she said finally. "Aldric. When the Crown shatters—"

"The binding is what's keeping him alive. Has been keeping him alive for thirty years. The Crown eats his memories to fuel itself, and burns what it eats to hold back—" I stopped. I had felt it, in the audience chamber, that deeper cold beneath the Crown's hunger, the thing the Crown was actually bracing against, and I did not have words for it that didn't sound like madness. "Something older. Something the first kings bargained with during the famine. The Crown isn't the monster. The Crown is the wall. And walls are heavy, and the king is the mortar."

"So destroying the wall kills the mortar."

"Kills the man. Frees—whatever else there is to free." I looked at my hands. Ink under the fingernails, still, after everything. Some stains you carry out of habit until you realize they're identity. "Which brings us to the last entry in the ledger, and I want it read aloud, because we agreed we'd count honestly. If we do this, Aldric dies. There is no version where he lives. The Crown offered me his life as part of the bargain—renew the binding, keep the wall standing, keep the man alive in his prison another era. Walking away from the deal kills him. Doing nothing kills everyone's memories instead, slowly, city by city, until the long winter finishes falling. Those are the options. I've checked the margins. There are no others."

"Then we pay it," Yara said, simply, the way she said most terrible things. "Tam carried that truth to Thornfell and died of it. Least I can do is spend it well."

"There's one more cost," I said. "Mine. The rite needs the heir's inheritance—the binder's gift, the thing my grandmother used to chain the Crown to Aldric's line. I have it. I've had it all along; it's why the courier chose me, why the Crown wants me specifically, why my grandmother fled rather than pass it on and passed it to me anyway in the only way she could. Once I use it, it's gone. Spent. And with it goes—" I took a breath, because I had practiced this sentence in front of a mirror like a vain girl and it still came out crooked. "With it goes any chance of using the same gift to unbind myself from anything else. Ever. Debts, oaths, indentures. The tool that could have freed me from Thornfell forever gets destroyed along with the Crown."

Silence. Even Yara, who considered patience a form of weakness, let that sit.

"So you're choosing," Serel said slowly, "to burn the one thing that could buy your freedom. In order to free a king who will die anyway, in a city that will forget you by spring."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"When I say it like that," Serel said, "it sounds like the first honest entry I've seen in nine years."

* * *

We worked through the night, and I will tell you that treason, done properly, looks remarkably like clerical work.

I copied the binding rite out three times—one to study, one to destroy, one to leave hidden in the palace archives addressed to whoever came after us, because if we failed, someone deserved to know what had actually happened here. My grandmother's marginalia turned out to be not merely notes but a full commentary, decades of it, written in the smugglers' cant our family used so the church couldn't read it. Deciphering her hand was like arguing with her across three hundred years. She was sarcastic. She was furious. On one page she had written, beside the instruction for holding the circle: *the holder will want to run. Let her want to. Wanting is fine. Staying is the work.*

"Your grandmother sounds like fun," Yara said, reading over my shoulder.

"She abandoned her granddaughter to a monastery's debt to keep this exact knowledge out of church hands."

"Yes, but apart from that. Fun."

"...Yes. Apart from that. Fun."

Serel spent the night, as instructed, taking inventory of her soul, which she did the way she did everything: aloud, methodically, in a voice like a filing clerk. "Sworn at ordination: obedience, silence, service, the protection of the realm through winter. Sworn at Dornave's elevation: to bear witness against his enemies. Sworn—" and here she stopped for a long time, staring at the coal cellar wall. "Sworn once, privately, years ago, that I would never become my father. He sold promises too. Smaller ones. To smaller people."

"Keep that one," I said. "That's the one worth reversing last."

At some hour past midnight Yara fell asleep sitting up, knife in hand, and woke instantly when Serel moved, and the two of them regarded each other with the wary respect of cats sharing a warm stove. I kept copying. And somewhere in the small hours, copying my grandmother's furious handwriting by candlelight, I understood what courage actually was, and it disappointed me completely.

It is not fire. It is not the charge, the vow, the burning certainty. Courage is a ledger. It is sitting down before the disaster and entering every cost in its own column—Aldric's life, my inheritance, Serel's vows, Yara's safety, the chance of failure, the chance of success, which carries costs of its own—and refusing to round anything down. The brave thing and the honest thing turn out to be the same thing, done with a steady hand. Everyone I had ever resented—my grandmother fleeing, my mother teaching me cant instead of comfort, the abbot extending my bond, even the Crown itself—had all been doing arithmetic too. They had just been cheating on the entries. Rounding other people's losses down to zero. Writing freedom in the income column when it belonged in debts.

I would not cheat. Whatever else happened at the solstice, the books would balance.

* * *

On the third night, the Crown summoned me.

Not through Aldric—through the map. I had kept it wrapped in oilcloth in my coat, and at vespers it grew warm against my ribs, and when I unwrapped it the sigil at its heart had begun to move, slowly, the way ice moves over a lake if you watch long enough. Words surfaced in the wax like things floating up from deep water.

*You waver, heir of Nakamura.*

"I don't," I said aloud, alone in the scriptorium they had assigned me, which was a foolish way to conduct a conversation and exactly the kind of thing my grandmother would have enjoyed. "I'm tired. There's a difference."

*The papers are drawn. Your name restored. Thornfell's debt cancelled entire—you need never copy another ledger.*

"That's the interesting part," I said. "Everyone keeps offering me freedom from work. Nobody offers me work worth doing."

*We offer you an era. Peace. The wall holds; the cold stays out; children grow up who would otherwise never be born. You would be the savior of the realm, comfortably, anonymously, alive.* A pause, and then, with the terrible gentleness of a creditor who knows your weakest column: *Your grandmother refused us too. Look how her refusal served her. Fugitive. Dead in a ditch somewhere with her secrets. Refusal is expensive, little scribe. We only ask you to check the price before you pay it.*

And that, of all things, was the moment I stopped being afraid. Because it had made my argument for me—every cost counted, refusal priced honestly—and honest accounting was now, officially, mine.

"I have checked the price," I said. "Line by line. You should try it sometime. You've been rounding down the king for thirty years."

The warmth in the map went very still. And then, faintly, from nowhere and everywhere, I heard—not the Crown, but something behind it, beneath it, older and colder and patient beyond all sense—turn its attention toward me like a lighthouse swinging.

Four days, I reminded myself. Three, now. Hold the line, write the columns, keep the grin hidden.

When I got back to the coal cellar, Yara took one look at my face and reached for a knife.

"What happened?"

"We're on schedule," I said. "And Yara—when this is over, remind me to teach you the cant. My grandmother left jokes in the margins and somebody ought to laugh at them besides me."

She almost smiled. Almost. With her, it counted double.

Above us, through the coal chute, the false winter went on falling, gentle as ash, patient as debt. Two days to the solstice. One rite. Three liars with open books.

It was time to go make the numbers true.
