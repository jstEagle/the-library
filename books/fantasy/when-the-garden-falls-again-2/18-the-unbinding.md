## 18. The Unbinding

The coronation chapel smelled of candlewax and old snow, and beneath both—faint as a lie told so often it had become true—the smell of hunger.

Mara knelt at the center of the circle they had chalked into the ancient mosaic floor, the one the palace custodians had covered with a carpet three hundred years ago and never once moved since. Underneath, the old binders' marks still gleamed faintly, silver in the mortar, waiting like a door someone had locked and then politely pretended was a wall.

"Your chalk is crooked," Yara said from her place at the eastern point of the circle.

"It's not chalk. It's consecrated lime mixed with ash from my grandmother's letters." Mara blew a strand of hair out of her eyes. "And it's crooked because you made me redraw the northern arc twice."

"Because you got it wrong twice."

"I got it *creative* twice. There's a difference."

Yara snorted, but her hand didn't leave the iron compass-spike she'd planted in the floor, the one that anchored her side of the circle. She'd stopped arguing about whether this would work somewhere around midnight. Now she only argued about details, which Mara understood was how Yara Ferro said *I'm frightened and I love you, you impossible scribe.*

Serel Vane stood at the western point, in full inquisitor's regalia, which she had put on that morning for the first time in a week. "If I'm going to commit treason," she'd said, fastening the silver clasp at her throat with steady hands, "I'll do it in uniform. Let them know exactly who did it."

Now she said, quietly, "They're coming. I can hear them in the ambulatory. Six of them, maybe seven. High Cantor Doss is among them—he walks like a man counting his own footsteps."

"Then we're past the part where anyone talks us out of this," Mara said.

"We were past that part when you forged the Lord Chamberlain's seal," Serel said. "We are now merely past the part where anyone *pretends*."

* * *

The rite, as Mara had reconstructed it from fragments scattered across four archives and one very drunk fence's memory, was simple in the way that drowning is simple. You opened the binding. You called the debt due. And you named yourself surety.

That last part was written in her grandmother's hand on the last surviving page of the binder's manual, pressed so hard the pen had torn the paper: *A crown may be unmade only by an heir of its forging, who must stand surety for every year of its rule. The Crown will offer to be paid otherwise. It always offers. Refuse it. Refuse it. REFUSE IT.*

Three refusals. Her grandmother had underlined the third one hard enough to emboss the next page.

Mara had read that page perhaps forty times. She thought of it now as the first bell began to toll—not the chapel's bell, but something deeper, a sound that came up through the mosaic floor and through her knees and settled in her teeth.

"That's early," Yara said. "Solstice isn't till dusk."

"The Crown doesn't keep our calendar anymore." Mara rose, unfolded the map—the true map, the courier's dying gift—and laid it in the center of the circle. The wax seal, broken now, caught the candlelight. "It keeps its own."

She began the opening words.

* * *

The old language of the forging families wasn't Nakamura cant, exactly, but it shared bones with it—the way smugglers' speech always shares bones with the languages of people who needed to hide things. Their mother had taught them cant as lullabies. Mara had never imagined those lullabies were also keys.

The words went into the air and the air took them the way deep water takes stones.

The candles guttered. The silver mortar-marks began to glow, each one waking in sequence around the circle, a slow ring of small cold fires. Yara gripped her spike. Serel drew her blade—not against Mara, but flat across her forearm in the old gesture of witness, the one inquisitors used when they certified a rite as lawful.

"You're certain this is lawful?" Yara muttered.

"No," Serel said. "But it's *witnessed*, which in my order was always the more important thing."

Mara spoke the final phrase of the opening. *I stand surety. I am heir of the forging. Come and be counted.*

The Crown answered.

It didn't come as a sound or a light. It came as a *remembering*—hers, but rearranged. Suddenly she was eight years old again, in the monastery scriptorium, except the abbot was smiling, actually smiling, telling her the debt had been forgiven, all of it, some benefactor had paid everything, and here were her freedom papers, signed, warm from the press, and wasn't it wonderful, wasn't she free—

"No," Mara said, and kept speaking the rite.

The vision folded and dealt again. Now she was older, published, celebrated; her name on a book spine, her mother alive to see it, their little house above the harbor bought outright, no ledgers anywhere in any drawer. A life with nothing owed in it. A life where nobody had ever died at a gate pressing a map into her hands.

*You could have this,* said the Crown, in a voice like Aldric's voice worn down to the grain. *You could have all of it. Walk away. Leave me my king. Leave me my winter. I have fed this city gently, comparatively speaking. I can feed you nothing at all. That is my best offer, scribe. It is a generous one.*

"It really isn't," Mara said, and her voice shook, and she let it shake, because her grandmother's note hadn't said *refuse it calmly.* "You ate a king. You ate a city's memories until people traded their own mothers' faces for bread. You're not a creditor. You're a fire that learned to call itself a hearth."

*I kept the famine away for three hundred years.*

"You started the next one."

The vision turned cruel then. It showed her Yara dead on the Gullet pass. It showed her Serel kneeling before High Cantor Doss, restored, promoted, forgetting. It showed Thornfell burning, the garden falling, everything she loved priced out and sold—and underneath it all, soft as a lullaby, the same offer. *Walk away. Sign nothing. Be free.*

Mara thought of her grandmother's three underlinings, and added a fourth with her whole heart.

"No," she said. "Come and be counted."

* * *

The Crown came.

Not the visions now—the thing itself, pouring up through the mosaic like black water finding a crack, and the circle flared white, and Yara shouted the holding-words in smugglers' cant, the ones their mother had sung them, and the ring held. Serel braced against the western mark, blade driven into the seam between two tiles, jaw set, whispering the inquisitorial confession over and over—"I saw what it made of my superiors, I saw what it made of me, I withdraw my oath, I withdraw my oath"—and each repetition struck sparks off the silver marks.

"Hold!" Yara roared at her. "Hold the line, Vane!"

"I am *holding*, Ferro, what does it look like—"

"It looks like praying! Pray faster!"

Mara laughed—a wild, cracked sound, entirely inappropriate, exactly right—and spoke the counting.

This was the heart of it. Every year of the Crown's rule, named aloud. Every king it had worn. Every debt it had taken on when it promised to end a famine, and every debt it had quietly transferred to the realm instead. She had spent weeks assembling the ledger. It ran to eleven pages. She had memorized it because she couldn't risk carrying it.

"Year one," she said, and the chapel darkened. "Year two. Year three..."

The Crown fought her on every number. It showed her the good years—the harvests, the wars ended quickly, the plague turned aside—as if those canceled the rest. And Mara, who had spent her whole life copying other people's accounts, knew precisely how that trick worked. You show the creditor only the columns that favor you.

"Both columns," she said. "Or none. Year forty-one. Year forty-two..."

Somewhere beyond the chapel doors, boots. Voices raised in High Cantor Doss's clipped cadence. The doors boomed once, twice.

"They're breaking through!" Yara called.

"Then count faster!" Serel snapped.

Mara counted faster. Years spilled out of her like water from a cut sack—years of quiet theft, memories harvested, winters extended, kings hollowed one by one until Aldric, the last, the gentlest, the one who had *volunteered*, believing he was saving his people, believing the binding would hold him safe inside himself.

At the name *Aldric* the Crown screamed.

The doors gave way. Inquisitors poured in, seven of them, blades drawn, and High Cantor Doss stood among them with his hands raised, chanting a counter-rite, trying to drown her out—

Serel Vane turned, lifted her blade from the mosaic, and pointed it at her own order.

"Stand down," she said. "All of you. I have witnessed what wears that crown. I have seen the king's mind from the inside of a report I was ordered to burn. If you take one more step toward this circle, I will certify each of you as corrupted, and I will enjoy it."

Doss stared at her. "Vane. Your career—"

"My career," Serel said, "was a column in *its* ledger. I'm closing the account."

For one heartbeat, nobody moved. Then the youngest inquisitor—Tallis, nineteen, who had once shyly asked Serel to check his handwriting—lowered his blade. Then the woman beside him. Then, one by one, all but Doss, who stood alone in the wreckage of his certainty, chanting to a room that had stopped listening.

* * *

Mara reached the last page of the ledger in her mind.

Here was the price, and she looked at it with open eyes, the way you're supposed to look at anything you sign. Unbinding the Crown meant unmaking it, and unmaking it meant the host fell with it. Three hundred years of rule, and the surety clause, and the king who had volunteered, and the grandmother who fled rather than pay, passing the bill down like an heirloom.

Mara was done inheriting bills.

"I close the account," she said, in the old tongue, in her mother's lullaby-language, in plain honest trade-cant, in all three at once. "Debt acknowledged. Debt counted. Debt *paid*. By the heir of the forging—I release the host. I break the instrument. I burn the record of your claim forever."

The Crown made its last offer then, and it was the cruelest one, because it was true.

*Without me,* it said, as the silver marks blazed and the black water of it thrashed against the circle, *the winter has no keeper. Something else will come. Something worse. You are not ending the cold, little scribe. You are only ending the arrangement.*

"I know," Mara said. "We'll write a new one. Ourselves. Honestly. That's the whole point of keeping records."

She raised her hand. Yara raised hers opposite, holding the circle's far arc. Serel drove her blade back into the seam and bore down with her whole weight, her whole faith, her whole renounced career.

"Now," Mara said—to Yara, to Serel, to the king she had never spoken to and was about to free in the only way left—to the garden, wherever gardens fall. "Now, and not one breath later."

She brought her palm down onto the map, onto the broken seal, onto the center of everything owed.

The silver marks went white.

The Crown went up like a sun going out—light without warmth, a silent, total flare that swallowed the chapel, the inquisitors, the winter, the world—

and somewhere inside the blaze, Mara heard a man's voice, ragged and human and *relieved*, begin to say something—
