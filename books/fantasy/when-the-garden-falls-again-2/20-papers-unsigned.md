## 20. Papers, Unsigned

The regency council met in the Hall of Provisional Voices, which had been the throne room for two hundred years and was now, by order of a committee, a place where no one sat higher than anyone else. They had sawed the dais down to floor level. The scar in the stone where the throne's feet had rested was still visible, pale as an old wound, and every person who entered the hall stepped over it without looking down—except Mara, who looked at it each morning on their way to testify.

Twelve days since the Crown shattered. Twelve days of testimony, ledgers, and men in good wool coats asking Mara to repeat things they had already written down. The council had seven members: three merchants, two generals, a bishop of the Reformed Church—which was to say, the old church with its Crown sigils scraped off the doors—and Lady Ossine Vael, who presided because she owned the granaries and therefore, that winter, owned the city's patience.

Mara stood before them again on the twelfth day, hands folded, ink still under the fingernails from copying out depositions the night before. Some habits were not debts. They were just the shape a life took.

"Mara Nakamura." Lady Vael consulted a paper she did not need. "The council wishes to express its gratitude. The realm owes you a debt it cannot easily name."

"It can name it," Mara said. "I've drafted the wording myself. Three versions."

A dry cough from one of the merchants—the one named Corvath, who traded in salt and had wept openly during his own deposition about the bread lines, though Mara had noticed his warehouses had never once run empty. "The council proposes compensation," he said. "Land, perhaps. A pension. The monastery at Thornfell has, I understand, a claim on your labor—"

"Which I intend to discuss," Mara said, "in Thornfell, with the town council, not here."

"You would refuse a reward?"

"I'd refuse to be *paid*, Lord Corvath. There's a difference." Mara let the silence sit a moment, the way Abbot Corren used to let silence sit over a novice who had made an error in a ledger—though where Corren used silence as a weight, Mara had learned to use it as a blade. "What I came to ask about is the papers."

Lady Vael's quill stopped moving.

"The freedom papers," Mara said. "Signed under seal, releasing my indenture to Thornfell monastery. Your predecessor's administration promised them. Through Inquisitor Serel Vane, who is currently—" Mara chose the word carefully, having watched Serel choose her own words carefully for twelve days, "—assisting this council with matters of internal review."

"Those papers were issued," said Bishop Halloway, "under the authority of the Crown."

"The Crown is ash. I carried some of it out of the chapel myself. It's in an urn somewhere, if you want to check."

"The *authority*," Halloway repeated, with the patience of a man explaining weather to a child, "was the Crown's. The seal was the Crown's. The writ ran from the Crown's justice. That justice has been dissolved. We cannot sign documents under an authority that no longer exists."

"Then sign them under your own."

"We are a provisional council," said General Ashe flatly. She had said little in twelve days, but what she said had weight, like stones dropped in wells. "We have no authority to bind our successors. Whatever we certify today may be struck tomorrow by whatever government follows. It would be worthless paper."

"Worthless," Mara echoed. "Interesting word. My grandmother's name was struck from your archives by worthless paper. My family's debt was recorded in worthless paper for three hundred years. It seems to me that worthless paper runs this kingdom. It's only ever a question of whose hand holds the pen."

The hall went quiet. Somewhere above, snow slid off the roof—a soft sound, an ordinary sound, winter doing nothing more than being winter, and Mara still flinched at it, and suspected they always would.

Lady Vael set down her quill. "You understand our position."

"I understand it perfectly," Mara said. "I've spent eleven years understanding other people's positions while copying them into ledgers. Here is mine: I unbound a king. I broke a thing older than every family in this room. I watched a man die grateful, and I watched a city wake up starving and free, and I did it holding a piece of parchment in my head that could have bought me anything I wanted if I'd only handed it over." They paused. "And I'm not asking you to be grateful. Gratitude rots fast in cold weather. I'm asking you to do the one thing councils exist to do, which is put ink on vellum and mean it."

"We will consider," said Lady Vael, "alternative forms of recognition."

"Consider quickly," Mara said, and bowed—not deeply—and left the hall before any of them could see how much the refusal cost, or how little.

---

Serel found them that evening in the scriptorium of the palace's lower archive, where Mara had taken temporary lodging because it was warm, quiet, and full of paper—the three luxuries Mara trusted most. Serel entered the way she entered everything: like someone who expected the room to object and had prepared arguments in advance.

"They're going to offer you a house," she said.

"A house."

"In the Salt Quarter. Confiscated from a Crown loyalist who fled. Stone walls, a garden. The garden will need work." Serel pulled up a stool and sat, uninvited, in the manner of someone who had spent years interrogating people across tables and had lost the instinct for permission. "Corvath wants you settled and visible. A hero in a house with a garden makes a better story than a hero demanding paperwork."

"And what do you want?"

Serel looked at her hands. They were bandaged at the knuckles—from the chapel, from turning her blade against her own order, from the moment Mara had seen her choose, and Mara thought: *that choice cost her more than the Crown cost me, though no one will ever write it down.* Someone should write it down. Perhaps that was becoming Mara's whole trade.

"I want to tell you something about those papers," Serel said. "Before they come. Because they'll come, and I want you to know exactly what you're holding when they do." She drew a breath. "I was debt-bound too. You knew that."

"To the church."

"To the church's *debt office*, which is worse. My father borrowed against my service before I was born. Same as yours, near enough—your abbot held your paper, my bishop held mine. When I was nineteen I petitioned for release. Standard procedure. I filled out the forms in triplicate, because I was very good at procedure." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "Do you know what happened?"

"They lost them."

"They didn't lose them. They *filed* them. Filed means buried. There's a drawer in the debt office—I saw it, after, when we burned the records—with forty years of petitions filed and forgotten, all from people like us. People who asked politely." She flexed her bandaged hand. "The Crown didn't keep us in bondage with chains, Nakamura. It kept us with *shelves*. And now the shelves have fallen over, and everyone who was owed and everyone who owed is standing in the wreckage asking who signs what."

Mara was quiet a moment. Outside the high window, the city glowed with its new ration-fires; the false winter had broken, but the true one remained, and hunger had not ended merely because a king had died human. Freedom, Mara was learning, fed no one.

"So the papers will come," Serel said. "Unsigned. Or signed by people whose signatures won't outlive the season. And you'll have to decide what that's worth." She stood. "For what it's worth—my opinion, which you didn't ask for—you were never freed by paper anyway. Paper's just how the powerful agree to remember things. And we both know what happens when they get to decide what's remembered."

She left. Mara sat alone among the shelves and thought about drawers full of forgotten petitions, and about Yara Ferro, who was somewhere north by now with her brother's bones and a compass that pointed at nothing, and about how strange it was that the person sent to hunt them had become the closest thing they had to a witness.

---

The papers arrived on the fifteenth day, delivered by a clerk who would not meet Mara's eyes.

They were beautiful. That was the first cruelty of them—someone had spent real effort. Fine vellum, edges trimmed and gilded. At the top, where a seal should have been, there was instead an embossed device of the provisional council: a wheat sheaf and an open hand, new-made, hopeful, meaningless. The text ran in careful formal script:

*Be it known to all persons, present and future, that the laborer Mara Nakamura, indentured to the monastery of Thornfell, is released from said indenture in perpetuity, this release being granted in gratitude for services rendered to the realm.*

At the bottom, where the signature belonged, there was a line.

An actual line, ruled in ink, waiting. Empty.

Mara read the document four times. Then they laughed—one short, ugly sound that startled the clerk into taking a step backward—and sat down on the archive steps with the beautiful worthless thing spread across their knees.

"They couldn't even lie properly," Mara said to him. "Someone told them to leave it blank. Someone decided an honest blank was better than a dishonest name."

"The council felt," the clerk recited, in the voice of a man carrying words he did not believe, "that no living member possessed sufficient authority to—"

"Yes. Yes, I know. Sit down, Master Clerk. You look like a rabbit being asked to explain arithmetic."

He sat. He was young—nineteen, maybe twenty, with ink stains on his fingers that Mara recognized the way you recognize a scar you once had. Mara found themselves softening, which was dangerous, and did it anyway.

"Tell me your name."

"Tomas Ellery, scribe second class."

"Scribe second class Tomas Ellery. Do you know what an unsigned paper is worth?"

"Nothing, sir. Ma'am." He faltered. "Nakamura."

"It's worth exactly as much as people decide it's worth. Which is also what a signed one is worth, but signed ones come with a man attached—a name, a rank, someone to hang if the promise breaks. An unsigned one is a promise with nobody behind it. It's a ghost's IOU." Mara traced the empty line with one finger. "Except. Except that it says something a signed one can't say. Do you see it?"

Tomas looked, frowning, the frown of someone genuinely trying, and Mara felt an unexpected twist of affection. This was what teaching would feel like, some part of them noted. Remember that.

"It admits it," Tomas said slowly. "That it can't make you free. Only... record that you are?"

"*Record*," Mara breathed. "Oh, you're wasted on second class." They stood, folding the vellum with exaggerated care. "Here's what I know, Tomas Ellery, and you may carry it back up the hill: my grandmother bound a hungry thing to a bloodline rather than destroy it, because destroying it would have cost something she wasn't willing to pay, and she wrote the debt down and left it for her grandchildren to settle. Everyone in this kingdom does that. Every debt gets written down and passed along, and the writing is supposed to make it *real*, and the realness is supposed to make it *honorable*, and it's all just paper pretending to be stone." They tapped the folded document against their palm. "But paper cuts both ways. If a blank line can't free me, then no line ever bound me either. Not really. The binding was always just everyone agreeing to pretend. And I am *done* agreeing."

Tomas blinked. "Then... what will you do with it?"

"What I do with everything." Mara smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile, but it was an honest one, and honesty had become the rarest currency in the city. "File it."

---

That night, by lamplight, Mara opened the plain ledger they had begun keeping—the one with no monastery mark, no council seal, no authority behind it at all. Just Mara's own hand, recording true things, which was the only inheritance they had ever actually received from the Nakamura line, though it had arrived disguised as a burden.

On the left page, they copied the council's document in facsimile, including the empty line, rendered faithfully, honestly blank.

On the right page, beneath it, they wrote:

*Fifteenth day of thaw-month. Received today the realm's gratitude in the form of a promise no one would stand behind. The line where a name should go is empty, and the emptiness is the most truthful sentence any official has written in three hundred years.*

*Note for whoever reads this: I was never released. I walked out. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. Papers certify what the powerful permit. Freedom is what you do the morning after, when no one has certified anything and you go to work anyway.*

*My mother taught me letters so I could read other people's accounts. I am keeping this one so that someday a child reads mine and understands: no one is coming with a stamp. No one is coming with a signature. The line stays empty until you draw it yourself.*

Mara sat back. The lamp guttered. Somewhere in the palace above, the regency council was drafting its next compromise, and somewhere north, Yara was walking through melting snow toward a grave, and somewhere south, in Thornfell, Abbot Corren was no doubt telling the brothers that his scribe would return when the excitement faded, because debtors always returned—they always had, for three hundred years.

Mara closed the ledger.

Three hundred years of returned debtors. Well. The record was about to show an exception, and records, done properly, outlasted everyone who tried to argue with them.

In the morning, Mara would buy passage north. There was a monastery at Thornfell that needed its books audited, and a town council that had never been shown the monastery's hidden ledgers, and an abbot who believed that paper was stone. Mara intended to bring him a document too.

It would be unsigned as well.

But it would not be blank.
