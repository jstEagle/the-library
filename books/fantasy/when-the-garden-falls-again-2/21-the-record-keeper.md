## 21. The Record Keeper

The road into Thornfell smelled the way it always had in late thaw: wet stone, woodsmoke, and the particular green rot of the monastery garden waking up under its blanket of last year's leaves. Mara had walked this road a thousand times—carrying ink to the chandlery, carrying tallow back, carrying other people's numbers in their head like stones—and it struck them now, walking it with Yara Ferro at their shoulder and three hundred years of buried truth in their satchel, that they had never once looked up at the monastery from below.

From below, Thornfell Abbey did not look holy. It looked heavy.

"There it is," Yara said. "Your prison."

"My workplace," Mara corrected. "There's a difference. Prisoners get fed."

"You're telling me they didn't feed you?"

"They fed me exactly enough to keep copying ledgers for forty years past my death." Mara adjusted the strap of the satchel. Inside it lay the map with its broken seal, Grandmother Sae's letters, and a new document, written in Mara's own careful hand over nine nights of bad sleep in capital taverns: an accounting. *The Debts of Thornfell Monastery, Compiled by One Who Kept Them.* Every indenture on the books. Every interest rate disguised as a "voluntary donation." Every year added quietly to a servant's term when the abbot judged them insufficiently grateful.

Yara squinted at the building. Her brother Tam had died at its gate, and she had spent the first mile of their journey glaring at it and the next fifty asking questions about it, because grief for her had never been content to sit still. "And you lived here how long?"

"Eleven years."

"Eleven years," Yara repeated, "and you're going to walk in there and burn it down."

"I'm not going to burn anything down." Mara smiled despite themselves. They were tired down to the marrow, still thin from the winter in the city, still hearing Aldric's last whisper when the wind came off the snowfields. *Thank you.* A king, dying human, thanking a scribe. Some nights that whisper was a comfort. Most nights it was a debt of its own. "I'm going to do something much worse. I'm going to read aloud."

* * *

Abbot Corren received them in his study, which was the warmest room in the abbey and always had been, though Mara had only been invited into it twice in eleven years. He was older than Mara remembered—or perhaps Mara had simply never bothered to remember him accurately before. His beard had gone fully white. His hands, folded on the desk, were soft as a clerk's.

"Mara Nakamura," he said, and there was something almost gentle in it. "We mourned you. I said a mass."

"You sold my funeral candle to the chandler at cost, Abbot. I checked the ledger before I left. You marked it 'charitable loss.'" Mara set the satchel on the desk between them. "It's good to see you too."

Corren's eyes went to Yara, who had planted herself against the wall with her arms crossed and the expression of a woman prepared to wait out a siege. "And your companion?"

"The sister of the courier who died at your gate. The one whose body you burned without rites because rites cost money and dead men don't pay interest."

"We could not confirm he was baptized," Corren said stiffly. "The church requires—"

"He was carrying your salvation in his coat, Abbot, and you burned him like kindling. But we're not here about Tam Ferro." Though they would be, later; Mara had promised Yara that much. "We're here about the ledger."

Something flickered across the abbot's face—the small, quick tightening of a man whose accounts have been mentioned unexpectedly. Eleven years, and Mara knew every one of those flickers the way a sailor knows weather. "If this is about your indenture, child, the terms are unchanged. Your family's debt stands at—"

"Fourteen years, eight months, and eleven days," Mara said. "I kept count. That's what you hired me for, after all. Keeping count." They opened the satchel and began laying papers on the desk, one by one, each face-up, each dated. "Here is the original contract, 312 years old, signed by a Nakamura grandmother who owed the abbey two sacks of seed grain after a blight year. Here is the same contract renewed four times without the debtor's signature—only the abbot's. Here is where the interest was converted from grain to labor in a year the abbey had a surplus. And here—" Mara tapped the thinnest page, the one they had copied out with the steadiest hand they owned, "is the current register. Forty-one indentured souls in Thornfell parish. The youngest is six. The miller's daughter, Wren. She sweeps your refectory floor for a debt her father incurred buying medicine for her mother. The medicine didn't work. The mother died anyway. The debt lives on."

Corren had gone very still. Stillness, in Mara's experience, was what powerful men did instead of apologizing.

"Where," he asked quietly, "did you get these?"

"From your own archive, Abbot. Shelf nine, behind the tax rolls nobody reads because reading them is my job and you assumed I'd never turn the skill around." Mara leaned forward. "You taught me to copy. You taught me to verify. You taught me that a record is a sacred thing—that an honest ledger honors the truth even when the truth is ugly. You gave me sermons on it. So let us both be honest now: you believed in honest ledgers for everything except the one that paid for your roof."

"This is sedition," Corren said. "This is ingratitude dressed up as piety. The abbey has fed this parish through famine and—"

"Through famine, yes. And through harvest. Through good years and bad, the debts only grew. Funny, that. Like a certain crown I recently helped destroy, the abbey's ledger feeds whether or not anyone eats." Mara heard their own voice sharpen and made themselves soften it—not because Corren deserved gentleness, but because Yara had told them once, on the Gullet pass with frost in their eyelashes, that anger was a fire best banked until the moment it was needed. "I'm not here to punish you. I'm not even here to shame you. Shame is cheap and you've clearly built a tolerance. I'm here to do the only thing I know how to do."

"Which is?"

"Keep the record."

* * *

The town council met in the wool-house, because Thornfell had no proper hall and the wool merchants' benches were the longest. Mara had stood outside that door many times, delivering copied documents, and had never once been invited in. Today they walked in through the front, with Yara at their side and a stack of papers under their arm, and the councilmen—who knew Mara as the quiet scribe, the one with ink permanently under the fingernails—took a full minute to understand that the quiet scribe intended to speak.

Master Halden, the wool factor who chaired the council, recovered first. "Nakamura? We thought you dead. There was talk of inquisitors, of—"

"There was talk of many things. Most of them are over." Mara set the stack on the long table and began distributing copies—they had made thirty by hand, working nights, because if this document existed in only one place it could be burned, and burning things had rather lost its charm this winter. "Gentlemen. I've brought you an account of the monastery's finances. Not the ones the abbey publishes at harvest festival. The real ones."

For two hours, Mara read.

They read the hidden interest clauses. They read the names—Wren the sweeper, Old Tamasin the laundress, the Cooper family bound across three generations for a barrel of nails. They read the entry, dated nineteen years back, recording the purchase of a debt from a dying farmer at half its value, "the discount reflecting the urgency of the seller's circumstances"—which was the driest sentence ever written about a man being fleeced on his deathbed. They read it all in a clear, level voice, the voice of a scribe reading numbers that were true regardless of who winced at them.

When they finished, the wool-house was silent except for the wind worrying the eaves.

Then Master Halden cleared his throat. "These figures... you're certain of them?"

"I verified every page twice. I invite any of you to do the same. The archive shelf is open." Mara paused. "That's rather the point."

A councilman named Brannigan—a stonemason with a voice like gravel and, Mara happened to know from the ledgers, a brother-in-law currently indentured—spoke up. "What is it you want, then? The debts voided? Just like that? The abbey lent in good faith, some might say—"

"Some might say," Mara agreed. "Some also said the king protected us through his Crown, right up until we learned what the Crown ate for breakfast. I'm not asking you to void anything. I'm asking you to witness. There's a difference. Voiding is forgetting with better manners." They laid out the final sheet, the one they were proudest of and most frightened by, because it was not an accusation but a proposal. "I propose a parish registry. Public. Kept here in the wool-house where anyone can read it, updated monthly, listing every debt, bond, and indenture in Thornfell—abbey-owned or otherwise—with its origin, its terms, and its running total. No more private ledgers. No more debts that grow in the dark like mold. If a debt is fair, sunlight will prove it. If it isn't, sunlight will starve it."

"And the abbey will simply agree to this?" Halden asked skeptically.

"No," said a dry voice from the doorway.

Every head turned. Abbot Corren stood there, flanked by two of his monks, and for a heartbeat Mara felt the old fear rise—the fear of the study, of the extended indenture, of the pen that wrote your life into someone else's column.

But Corren looked smaller in the wool-house doorway than he ever had in his study. He looked, in fact, like a man standing in someone else's house.

"May I?" he asked, and without waiting, crossed to the table and picked up one of Mara's distributed pages. He read it for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its pulpit weight. "The entry about the cooper's nails. My predecessor's hand. I found that page myself, twelve years ago, when I took office." He set it down carefully. "I meant to address it. I told myself I would, once the harvest improved. Once the roof was mended. Once—" He stopped, and something passed over his face that Mara recognized, having seen it on a king and an inquisitor and, if they were honest, in their own reflection: the look of a person meeting the arithmetic of their own excuses. "Once is a word that eats decades, doesn't it, scribe?"

"Ask any creditor," Mara said. "Or any debtor. They'll tell you the same thing."

Corren turned to the council. "I cannot undo three centuries in an afternoon. But I can sign your registry. I can open the archive. And I can tell you that the six-year-old goes home tonight—her debt transfers to the abbey's own account, where I will carry it personally until it's discharged." He glanced back at Mara, and there was something almost wry in it, tired and human. "You learned that trick from me, I think. Putting a name beside a number so it can't be ignored."

"You taught me that a record is a sacred thing," Mara said. "I'm only returning the lesson. With interest."

Someone—Brannigan, gravel-voiced—laughed, and the tension broke like ice at thaw, all at once and noisily. Hands reached for the papers. Questions flew. Halden began arguing terms of the registry with the enthusiasm of a merchant who has just discovered a whole new category of goods, and Yara caught Mara's eye across the crowded room and lifted two fingers to her temple in a lazy salute.

*Well?* the gesture asked.

Mara looked down at their ink-stained fingers, resting on the edge of the table, on top of the first honest public record Thornfell had kept in living memory.

*Good enough,* they signed back.

* * *

That evening, the monastery kitchen—for the first time in eleven years—fed Mara as a guest. It was a strange meal. The food was identical to the food of their servitude (the abbey cook had exactly one setting: austere), but it tasted different, the way a word tastes different when you finally understand its second meaning.

Yara sat beside them, eating with the unselfconscious ferocity of a woman who had crossed the Gullet twice in one lifetime and intended to celebrate every meal henceforth as a minor miracle. Across the table, Corren ate in silence, occasionally glancing at Mara as if checking whether the food had been properly recorded.

"You could stay," he said at last, into his bowl. "As archivist. Paid. Free."

Mara considered it honestly, because they had promised themselves honesty above all things—it was, after all, the whole platform. "No. But I'll come back. Every season, to audit. Whether you invite me or not."

"That sounds less like auditing and more like haunting."

"In Thornfell," Mara said, "we call it bookkeeping."

Corren huffed—almost a laugh—and returned to his soup, and Mara understood that this was as close to peace as either of them was likely to get, and that it would hold.

Later, walking the cloister with Yara under a sky rinsed clean by rain, Mara stopped at the gate where a stranger had died in the snow a season and a lifetime ago. Someone had set a small stone there. Not a marker—just a stone, placed deliberately, the kind of thing travelers do without being asked. Mara crouched and added a second stone beside it.

"For Tam?" Yara asked quietly.

"For everyone the ledgers forgot." Mara stood. Their legs ached. Everything ached. They found they didn't mind. "He carried the map so the truth could arrive somewhere. I'm just the next courier. So are you. So is the abbot, gods help him."

Yara looked at the stones, then at the dark road leading away from Thornfell—south, toward the coast, toward whatever a person did with herself after avenging her brother and saving a kingdom. "So what happens now? The Crown's ash. The king's grave. The council squabbling over a regency. Somebody has to write all of it down, you know. Properly. Before the courtiers start inventing prettier versions."

Mara smiled and pulled from their satchel a fresh ledger, plain-covered, blank, bought with their last coin in the capital. They held it up between two fingers.

"I was hoping you'd ask."

* * *

They left the next morning at dawn, when the mist still lay in the valley like spilled milk. At the gate, Corren waited with a bundle: bread, cheese, and—wrapped separately, with visible reluctance—a jar of the abbey's good ink.

"For the record keeper," he said gruffly. "Try to make the entries legible."

"I learned from the best hypocrite in the diocese."

Behind them, Thornfell Abbey sat heavy on its hill—but lighter than it had been, Mara thought, by exactly the weight of forty-one names. Ahead, the road ran south into a world with no Hollow Crown in it for the first time in three hundred years, full of debts and truths and stories nobody had yet written down.

Mara opened the ledger to the first clean page, balanced it on their forearm, and wrote, in a steady hand:

*Entry the first. On this day, the road out of Thornfell. Weather: clearing.*

*Debts outstanding: all of them. Intentions: likewise.*
