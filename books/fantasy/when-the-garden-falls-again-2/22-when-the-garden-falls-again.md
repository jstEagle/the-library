## 22. When the Garden Falls Again

The garden came back before anything else did.

That was the first thing Mara had learned about Thornfell in the years after the winter broke: gardens are stubborn in a way that institutions never manage to be. The monastery's walls still bore the scars of the long cold—stones cracked where ice had wedged them apart, the bell tower leaning at an angle that made visiting clergy nervous—but the garden had simply decided to grow again the moment the frost let go, as if it had been waiting politely all along with its hands folded.

Mara liked that about it. Gardens didn't pretend. They didn't hold ceremonies or claim ancient rights or issue papers. They either grew or they didn't.

These days it was a teaching garden, which everyone agreed was a strange thing for a monastery to allow and which Abbot Corren—who was no longer abbot, having been quietly reassigned to a monastery so remote that even his letters took two months to arrive—would have found intolerable. Children from the village sat among the raised beds now, learning their letters on slates while bees droned over the bean rows. The council had voted on it. There were minutes. Mara kept copies.

"You're smiling at the beans again," said Yara from the gate.

"I'm smiling at Pip," Mara said. "He's spelled his own name wrong for the fourth time this morning."

"Persistent."

"Wrong in a *new way* each time. That takes talent."

Yara came through the gate with her usual walk—that rolling, watchful gait of someone who'd spent too many years checking the road behind her—and settled onto the bench by the well, where she always sat because it gave her a view of both the path and the sky. Some habits outlived their reasons. Mara understood. They still counted exits in every room, still slept with boots within reach, and probably would until they were old enough that running anywhere would be more dangerous than whatever was chasing them.

"How many today?" Yara asked.

"Nine. Two of them can read actual words now. One of them is Tam, who has informed me that letters are 'a scam invented by people who want you to sign things.'"

"He's not wrong."

"He's seven, and he's not wrong, which is worse." Mara set down the slate she'd been mending. "His mother works the tannery. She signed a contract she couldn't read last spring. He heard about it."

Yara nodded slowly. Down in the valley, the tannery had renegotiated its contracts since then—all of them rewritten in plain language, posted on the door where anyone could stand and puzzle through them. It had taken the council three years and one spectacular public argument, but it had happened. Mara kept those minutes too.

"And your ledger?" Yara asked, tilting her chin at Mara's satchel.

Mara patted it. "Safe."

"Still true things only?"

"Only true things. Which is why it's so short."

* * *

The ledger lived in Mara's cottage—a small stone house near the garden wall that the council had deeded to them outright, no signature required, after a great deal of argument about whether anyone could legally *give* property to someone whose freedom papers had never been issued. In the end the council had simply declared Mara a free person of Thornfell by unanimous vote, and when a lawyer pointed out that this wasn't how such things were normally done, the council's head woman had said, "Neither was burning a crown off a king's head, but here we are," and that had settled the matter.

The ledger itself was nothing grand. Plain paper, stitched binding, the kind of book Mara could have made a hundred of in their indenture days without thinking. But every page held only what had actually happened. No embellishment. No convenient silences.

*Fourth day of Thawmonth. Nine children at lessons. Tam wrote TAM backwards and defended it with vigor.*

*Seventh day. Yara fixed the well rope. The well did not deserve her.*

*Nineteenth day. Letter arrived from Serel Vane. She is still in the capital. She writes that the regency council has finally opened the palace archives to the public, though "opened" apparently means "guarded by four men who ask why you're looking." She says the king's memorial stone reads ALDRIC, WHO WAS A MAN. She says she stood there a long time.*

Serel wrote twice a year, always in the same dry hand, always ending with some variation of *I am watching them, so you don't have to.* Mara believed her. It was strange to be grateful to someone you'd once held at knifepoint, but gratitude turned out to be like the garden—it grew where it grew, and arguing with it got you nowhere.

There were other entries. The one Mara reread most often was short:

*Winter solstice, third year. Snow fell. Only snow fell. We lit candles anyway. Yara said a toast to Tam's brother—the courier, Ferro, who carried the truth further than he knew. We drank to him with plum wine he would have called too sweet. It was too sweet. We drank it all.*

Ferro's grave was up the hill now, under a rowan tree, with a stone that said HE DIED CARRYING SOMETHING TRUE. Yara had carved it herself and refused help, and the F leaned slightly, and she refused to fix that too. "It leans," she'd said. "Everything true leans a little."

* * *

The children came back from their midday bread, and lessons resumed, and Mara moved among the rows answering questions with the particular patience of someone who remembered hating these same questions.

"Why do we have to learn the old script if nobody uses it?" demanded Wren, who was eleven and built entirely of objections.

"Because somebody will use it," Mara said. "Someday, somewhere, there'll be a document in the old script, and whoever reads it first decides what everyone else gets told it says. Wouldn't you rather that person was you?"

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Could I lie?"

"You could," Mara said. "People have. That's sort of the whole lesson."

"What happened to them?"

Mara sat down on the low wall beside her. "One of them helped forge a crown that ate a kingdom for three hundred years. Another one ran away instead of fixing it, and her granddaughter paid for it. And one of them—" Mara tapped their own chest— "spent ten years copying ledgers for a man who owned their debt, and learned exactly how much damage a person can do just by deciding what the record says."

"That's not fair."

"No," Mara agreed. "It isn't. So we keep honest records, and we teach everyone to read them, and we make fairness out of small boring things instead of waiting for big dramatic ones. Fairness is mostly bookkeeping."

Wren wrinkled her nose. "You say that like it's good."

"It's the best thing I know," Mara said, and meant it so plainly that Wren blinked and, for once, didn't argue.

Pip, seizing the opening, announced that he could now spell his name correctly, and demonstrated: PIP. Correct. The class applauded with the unreserved generosity of children who had witnessed the struggle. Pip glowed like a lantern.

"There," Mara said quietly to Yara, who had drifted close. "New record. Ledger-worthy."

"Write it down," Yara said. "True things are rare."

* * *

Evening came early, as it does in the mountains even in high summer, and Mara walked the garden's edge as they did every night—not checking, exactly, but *noticing*. The wall. The gate. The rowan on the hill, dark against the last light. The habit of vigilance had softened into something else over the years, something closer to greeting. You watched the road the way you watched an old acquaintance: warily, but with fondness.

The Crown was dead. Mara had seen it shatter; had felt the moment the hunger stopped, like a headache ending after years. And in the seasons since, they'd learned the difference between fear and attention. Fear made you hide things. Attention made you write them down.

That was the whole inheritance, really. Not the debt—their grandmother's debts had died with the Crown, unpaid and unforgiven, and Mara had made peace with that. The inheritance was the duty to witness. Their family had bound a monster in secret and told no one, and the telling-no-one had cost three centuries. Mara intended to spend the rest of their life doing the opposite, one plain sentence at a time.

Snow began to fall as Mara stood at the wall—real snow, ordinary snow, the kind that melted by noon and meant nothing except that the year was turning. Somewhere beyond the mountains, in the frozen capital, people were lighting the memorial candles for Aldric. In the palace archives, students copied documents under guard, complaining about the fees, unaware that the oldest shelf held a gap where one map had once rested, and that its absence was the most important fact about the collection.

Mara went inside, lit the lamp, and opened the ledger to the last clean page.

They had known for a while that this page was coming. Every ledger ends. This one had run six years, and it held everything: the garden, the children, the contracts, Serel's letters, the plum wine, the snows that fell only in winter. It was, Mara thought, a good record—as far as it went.

But it didn't go far enough back. And there were people who should read it who couldn't yet.

So Mara dipped the pen, and began at the beginning, in plain language, in the old careful hand their grandmother would have recognized:

*I was born indentured in the Thornfell monastery, and I was taught letters by my mother, who taught me smugglers' cant in secret, because she believed that a person should always have one language their owners can't read.*

And Mara kept writing. The storm at the gate. The courier who wouldn't die quietly. The name Nakamura inked beside the Crown's sigil. The Salt Stair, the broken compass, the ninth winter, the pass that ate travelers. The king mouthing words no one heard. The bargain, the betrayal, the chapel, the ash on the snow. All of it, honestly counted, every cost entered in its column—including the freedom papers that never came signed, and the freedom claimed anyway.

Outside, the snow fell gently on the rebuilt garden, on the sleeping village, on the whole patient world, and it was only winter, and it meant only winter, and that was enough.

When the pen finally stilled, Mara read the pages back once, closed the cover, and set the ledger on the shelf beside the others—ready for the morning, ready for the children, ready for whoever came asking true things next.

Then Mara blew out the lamp and slept, unafraid, keeping watch even in dreams.
