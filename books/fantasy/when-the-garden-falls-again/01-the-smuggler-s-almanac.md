## 1. The Smuggler's Almanac

The snow came down in the Thornfell passes the way it always did in late autumn—not falling so much as arriving, sideways and sudden, as if the mountains themselves had decided to change their minds about winter. Ansel Amberdale stood at the head of the mule train with her collar up and her hood back, because hoods were for people who needed to see less than she did.

"Left fork," she said, without turning around. "Not the one that looks like the left fork. The actual one."

Behind her, Marisol made a sound like a kettle deciding whether to boil. "They look identical."

"They're not. The real one has a birch stump at the mouth, split by lightning, with a crow's nest wedged in the crotch of it. The false one has a boulder shaped like a sleeping dog." Ansel adjusted her grip on the lead rope. "The boulder's been there forty years. The stump was alive when my grandmother was young. Trust the thing that dies."

"That's either wisdom or a threat," said Pip from somewhere near the rear, where Pip was always near the rear, ostensibly watching the ore crates and actually eating the honey-cakes that were supposed to last them to Hollowfen.

"It's an almanac," Ansel said. "Everything I know is just pages nobody else bothered to read."

It wasn't entirely true, and she knew it wasn't, and she let it stand anyway because true things about herself were currency she had stopped spending three years ago. The truth was that she carried the Thornfell passes in her head the way other people carried grief—constantly, intimately, in a place too deep to set down. Every switchback above Greywater Ford. Every cave with a dry floor and every cave with a floor that flooded when the moon pulled hard on the high snowmelt. Every shrine, every ruin, every collapsed mine shaft where the rock still hummed faintly at dusk like a struck bell that wouldn't finish its note.

She had never told anyone why she'd memorized all of it. She had never told anyone most things.

The mules picked their way along the shelf road, hooves finding stone by instinct and prayer, and the moon-ore sang softly in its crates behind them. That was the trouble with moon-ore—the reason it was contraband, the reason it paid enough to make a winter's risk worthwhile. It sang. Raw from the deep seams under Thornfell, it caught starlight and held it, and if you listened long enough you started hearing words in the ringing, which was how half the mad prospectors in the lowlands had gotten that way. Crated and warded, it only murmured. But on a quiet night, with the wind down, the whole train of it hummed together like a choir rehearsing in another room.

Ansel hated it and loved it in equal measure. It sounded, faintly, like being remembered.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the hollow beneath Cragwatch Tor—a fold of stone that blocked the wind from three directions, which Ansel knew because she had slept in it eleven times before, in four different years, running four different cargoes for three different captains who had all eventually betrayed someone, though never her. She chose the site without consulting the map. Odell noticed. Odell always noticed, and never said anything, which was the closest thing to affection the captain permitted herself.

Odell was fifty-some, built like a door, with a face that had been weathered past the point where weather could do further damage. She ran her crew the way she ran her mule trains: tight, honest by necessity, and with a standing rule that no one asked questions whose answers might get everyone killed. Ansel had signed on two winters ago with a name that wasn't hers and a knife that was, and Odell had looked at her for a long moment and said, "Can you get cargo through Thornfell in the white season?" and Ansel had said yes, and that had been the whole interview.

Three years ago—no. Two and a half. She kept the count carefully, the way you keep count of anything you're trying to survive. Two and a half years since she had stopped using her family name. Since she had paid off the last debt attached to it and walked out of the last room where anyone had a claim on her. No surname spoken. No debts owed. No loyalties kept. She had written it out once, like a creed, and then burned the paper, because even creeds were ties if you kept them.

And yet here she was, keeping a crew alive through the white season, which was loyalty wearing a workman's coat.

Hesk had the fire going small and disciplined, the way he did everything. He was the largest man Ansel had ever seen outside of festival strongmen—six and a half feet of gentle ex-stonemason with hands like paving slabs and a voice pitched for soothing animals—and he cooked the way he laid foundations: slowly, exactly, with total commitment. Tonight it was barley and salt pork and the dried mountain berries Pip hadn't eaten yet, somehow, against all precedent.

"Extra bowl," Hesk said, ladling it out. "You barely touched midday rations."

"I ate."

"You ate standing up, walking, facing away from us." He handed her the bowl anyway, and his enormous hand managed to make the gesture delicate. "That's not eating. That's fueling."

"Fueling is what eating is for."

Marisol snorted into her own bowl. Marisol was the younger twin by nine minutes and had spent those nine minutes and every minute since establishing that she would not be overlooked. She was sharp-faced and quicker-tongued, with a scar through one eyebrow she'd gotten falling out of a Hollowfen window she still insisted she'd exited gracefully. "She does this every time," Marisol told the fire. "Watch. She'll eat the whole bowl and then claim she wasn't hungry."

"I'm right here," Ansel said.

"You're always right here," Pip said, appearing at the fire's edge with the specific innocence of someone who had definitely eaten the honey-cakes. "That's the whole mystery of you. You're here, you save our hides monthly, you know these mountains better than the mountain knows itself, and we don't know a single true thing about you. Not your favorite color. Not your home village. Not whether Ansel's even your real name."

"It's a name. It answers when called. That's the entire job description."

"But—" Pip sat down cross-legged, undeterred, the way water was undeterred. "Don't you ever want somebody to know you? Properly?"

The question landed in the space between the fire-crackle and the wind, and Ansel felt it the way you feel a draft from a door you thought was shut. She ate her barley. She gave it the full three seconds of silence it deserved, and then one more second, to be sure.

"I want us all to live through the white season," she said. "After that, wants are luxury goods. You know the tariff on those."

Pip opened his mouth. Marisol, remarkably, put a hand on his arm, and he closed it again.

Hesk refilled bowls. Odell chewed and watched the dark and said nothing, and her saying nothing was its own kind of commentary, and Ansel met it across the fire with her chin level, and neither of them blinked, and that was the whole conversation.

Later, when the others had rolled into their bedrolls, Ansel took first watch, because she always volunteered for first watch and Odell always let her, because Ansel on watch was worth two of anyone else and they both knew it. She sat with her back to the tor and her face to the pass and let the cold find the gaps in her coat, and she counted the things she could hear: wind in the scree, the mules shifting, the faint choral murmur of the moon-ore, Hesk breathing in his sleep like a bellows worked slow.

And underneath it, far off, the storm building in the high country. She could feel it coming the way she felt most things—through the pressure in her ears, the ache in old scars, the particular quality of the dark. A big one. Three days out, maybe four, rolling down off the Throat of the World and burying the passes until spring thaw unless you were already through or already sheltered.

She knew a shelter. Of course she knew a shelter. There was a ruined garden halfway down the eastern face of the pass, an old estate swallowed by the mountain a century ago—greenhouses gone to lace, terraces cracked and heaved by frost, a fountain with a dry basin full of drifted leaves. The locals called it the Verdant Garden and wouldn't go near it after dark, because ruins in Thornfell collected stories the way ditches collected snowmelt. Ansel had sheltered there twice. Nothing had bothered her either time except the wind, which moaned through the broken glasshouse ribs in a way that got into your chest and stayed.

She thought about the storm, and the garden, and the route ahead, and she did not think about Pip's question. She was very good at not thinking about things. It was, if she was honest, her single greatest skill—better than the routes, better than the memory. Anyone could remember. Forgetting on purpose, holding the door shut against your own history night after night—that took discipline.

Two and a half years of practice. She was excellent now.

* * *

They lost half a day to a rockfall on the second morning—an avalanche scar above the Serpent's Road spur that had finally finished letting go, dumping a fan of shattered granite across the lower switchback. Any other guide would have turned back to the waystation and waited out the clearing crews, losing the season's margin. Ansel stood at the edge of the rubble for perhaps a minute, eyes moving, and then led them off the road entirely, down through a pine break and along a streambed that didn't appear on any chart Odell owned.

"This isn't a trail," Odell said, not as a complaint. As a fact, offered for confirmation.

"It's a deer path that becomes a goat path that becomes a trail if you know where the washout bridge used to be and aren't afraid to ford twice." Ansel stepped from stone to stone without looking at her feet. "There's a ford below the falls where the river braids shallow. In summer it's nothing. In late autumn it's brim-full and freezing and it will take a mule if the mule argues. So the mules don't argue."

"How do you stop a mule arguing?"

"You walk it in first, on the upstream side, so the current pushes it toward the bank instead of away. And you don't flinch. They can feel flinching through the lead rope."

She walked the first mule in first, of course. The water came up over her knees and tried its best to fold her into the current, and she leaned into it with her whole weight and her breath held in the top of her chest, and the cold went through her boots and into her bones and she did not flinch, and the mule followed her like a penitent.

By the time the last crate crossed, her legs had gone from pain to a distant buzzing, and Marisol pressed a flask into her hands without a word, and Ansel drank, because refusing warmth on the grounds of principle was a stupidity she reserved for larger principles.

"You've done this crossing before," Marisol said. It wasn't a question.

"Once. Years ago. With a man named Corvin, who fell in, lost two crates of emberwine, and cried about it for six miles." She handed back the flask. "I don't cry about things. It wastes heat."

"Is that in the almanac too?"

"Page one."

But something in the joke snagged on the way down, the way a fishhook snags on a sweater. Because the almanac—that was the truest thing she'd said all trip, and the saddest, and she'd said it lightly precisely so no one would weigh it. Everything she knew was pages nobody else had bothered to read. Routes learned from dead men. Fords remembered from crossings she'd made alone. Shrines and ruins and humming mineshafts catalogued across years of solitary travel, filed away in a head that no one else would ever need to open, because she had arranged her life so that no one else would ever need anything from her that she couldn't replace.

A person could be an almanac. Useful, consulted, essential to the journey, and closed again and shelved at day's end, and no one grieved the almanac when the year turned and a new one was printed.

She had thought, for two and a half years, that this was safety. Standing in the freezing shallows of an unnamed river with her found-not-family strung out behind her on a deer path, it occurred to her, briefly and unwelcome, that it might also be a kind of death—one you survived daily.

She put the thought away. She was excellent at that.

* * *

The storm arrived a day early, because storms in Thornfell had no respect for schedules, and they made the Verdant Garden with maybe twenty minutes of light left and the first flakes already coming down thick as moth wings.

"Through here," Ansel said, and took them off the track and up through a breach in a fallen wall, into the ruin.

Even wrecked, even snow-blind, the place had presence. The greenhouses stood in skeleton rows, their glass long gone, their iron ribs arched like the carcasses of enormous fish. Terraces descended the hillside in worn steps, and the fountain sat in the middle court with its dry basin holding a hundred years of leaves, and everywhere—everywhere—the hedge. It was the hedge people whispered about. It grew wrong, the villagers said; it grew in the shape of intention. Walls of it still stood where walls made no sense, arching over paths that led nowhere, and even in deep autumn it held a color that wasn't quite green, dark and glossy, like something remembering summer rather than living it.

"Creepy," Pip announced, with relish. "I love it. Does it have ghosts?"

"It has drafts," Ansel said. "Glasshouse on the east terrace has an intact corner room. Dry floor, working chimney gap. We'll be warm enough."

"How do you even know this place? It's not on any of the charts."

"No," Ansel agreed, and said nothing else, and began unloading the nearest mule.

They got the animals under the greenhouse ribs and the crates stacked and Hesk's small disciplined fire lit in the corner room, and the storm came down on the mountain like a verdict. Wind screamed through the glasshouse skeleton and made the whole structure sing, a low organ-note that rose and fell, and Pip declared it haunted, delightedly, and Marisol threw a pinecone at him, and Odell checked the lashings twice, and Ansel stood at the broken window-wall looking out at the hedge rows filling with white, and felt the strange double sensation she always felt in this place: that she had been here before, and that she had been here before in some way deeper than the two prior visits accounted for.

Déjà vu, probably. Everyone got it in old places. The mind misfiling the present into the past.

Probably.

Dinner was stew, because Hesk believed stew solved morale problems at the root, and he was largely correct. And it was during the stew, with the storm hammering the glassless panes and the moon-ore murmuring in its crates and Pip on his third bowl, that Odell set down her spoon and said, in the tone she used for business:

"Hearth-night's in nine days. Winter solstice eve. We'll be down in the foothills by then if the weather gives us two clear days, and it will, because our guide says so." A nod at Ansel, which was as close as Odell came to praise in public. "There's a barn above Millbrook with a floor and a hearth that doesn't smoke. We do it proper every year. Mutton, mulled everything, Pip sings, which is a trial we all endure, and Hesk makes that spiced honey cake that's worth the whole winter." She paused. "You're invited."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the hedge creaked under the weight of new snow.

Ansel kept her spoon moving. "Thank you. I'll be in Hollowfen by then. I've got business."

"Business," Marisol repeated flatly.

"Errands."

"Errands," said Pip, "that require leaving before the free mutton."

"I don't celebrate hearth-night."

"Nobody doesn't celebrate hearth-night," Pip said. "That's like saying you don't celebrate existing."

"Some people don't," Ansel said, "have anything to celebrate it with."

The words came out with more edge than she'd intended, more truth than she'd intended, and the table went quiet in the specific way tables go quiet when someone has accidentally shown the underside of a thing. Hesk looked at her with those patient stonemason's eyes. Marisol studied her stew. Odell watched Ansel the way she watched weather—assessing, unhurried, prepared for whatever came.

"We're not asking you to have something," Odell said at last. "We're asking you to come stand near ours. There's a difference. First one costs you. Second one costs nothing but an evening."

Ansel looked at the fire. The fire offered no tactical advice.

"I appreciate it," she said, and meant it, which was the worst part. "I'll be in Hollowfen."

Odell held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded once, and picked up her spoon, and that was that—because Odell understood doors better than anyone Ansel had ever met, including which ones you couldn't push.

Later, watches traded, the crew asleep in their bedrolls in the greenhouse corner, Ansel sat alone in the dark of the ruined garden with her cloak buttoned and the storm roaring overhead, and admitted, to the hedge and the snow and no one else, that she had lied. She had no errands in Hollowfen. She had nothing in Hollowfen. She had turned down the hearth-night for the same reason she turned down everything: because the invitation had fit her, and things that fit could hold you, and she had spent two and a half years making herself a shape nothing could hold.

The moon-ore murmured in its crates. The hedge creaked. And Ansel, unable to sleep, did what she always did in this ruin when the wind got into her chest: she walked.

She followed the terrace steps down through the snowfall, past the fountain, along a hedge row that arched overhead like a ribcage, her boots finding the old flagstones by memory. At the end of the row stood a small stone outbuilding she'd never entered—too collapsed, she'd assumed, too exposed—but tonight the door stood open, and tonight, for the first time in three visits, there was light inside.

Faint. Cold. Green-white, like moonlight strained through leaves.

Ansel's hand went to her knife. The storm howled. The hedge creaked its slow intentional creak. And from inside the little outbuilding came a sound she knew absolutely, in her bones, from a hundred nights of crated cargo—

Singing. Moon-ore singing. But there was no ore here. There was only a book, lying open on a fallen slab, its pages glowing faintly, turning themselves in the draft as if someone invisible were reading.

Ansel Amberdale stood in the doorway of her grandmother's buried past, three years of careful invisibility at her back, and the storm sealed the mountain behind her, and the book turned one more page, and waited.
