## 2. A Date That Hasn't Happened

The book did not look up at her. Books never did, and Ansel had spent three years cultivating the belief that nothing in the world ever would—that if she moved quietly enough, owed little enough, loved no one loudly enough, the whole machinery of fate would grind past her without once checking its ledgers.

She stood in the doorway of the outbuilding with rain streaming off her jaw and told herself this was cargo. Contraband, maybe. Smuggled prophecy had to be worth something in Hollowfen; she could name three brokers who'd pay silver for a book that glowed. That was a route forward. Routes were how she thought. Give her a pass through the mountains and she could hold it in her head for years, every switchback, every scree slope, every place the ice grew teeth in winter—but give her a book turning its own pages, singing like moon-ore, waiting for her like a dog that knew her name, and her mind went white as the storm outside.

*It's not waiting for you,* she said to herself. *It's waiting for anyone.*

She crossed the threshold anyway.

The outbuilding had been a gardener's shed once, before the garden above had died and fallen into the mountain's keeping. Tools hung on the walls—rusted trowels, a pair of pruning shears fused into one green mass by a century of damp—and moss had claimed everything soft, so the whole room wore a carpet of it, muffling her boots. The slab the book lay open on was a workbench, or had been. Someone had cleared a space on it recently. Recently as in: the moss there was broken, the stone wiped clean, a hollow worn into it shaped exactly like a closed book resting in the same spot for a very long time, until tonight, when it had opened itself.

Ansel did not touch it. She was not stupid. She crouched instead, close enough to read by the pages' pale light, and kept her knife drawn low against her thigh where the blade wouldn't flash.

The left-hand page showed a drawing of a storm.

Not a dramatic storm—not the kind a cartographer's apprentice might sketch in a margin, all swirling lines and a fat-cheeked wind-face. This was precise. Rendered in fine brown ink gone the color of old tea, it depicted a specific front breaking against a specific ridge, and Ansel's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution as she recognized the ridge, because she had climbed under that ridge an hour ago, and the artist had drawn the exact shape of the cloud-break she'd watched swallow the last of the daylight—the notch in the western shoulder where the wind came through like water through a cracked dam, the low scud dragging east over the fell in ragged banners.

She knew that sky. She had memorized that sky forty minutes ago, standing on the trail, calculating whether she'd make the far pass before dark.

The caption beneath the drawing, in a cramped, slanted hand, read:

*On the eve of the thirteenth turn, the sky will break over the Shoulder of the Shepherd, and the rain will come down like a debt called in.*

Ansel read it twice. Then a third time, because her eyes kept sliding off the words the way they slide off a word you've stared at too long, refusing to make sense.

The right-hand page showed the shed.

Her shed. Her shelter for tonight. There was the doorway she stood in—the artist had even caught the sag of the lintel on its broken side, the way the frame leaned a finger's width out of true, which was why she'd chosen this structure over the collapsed chapel further down. There were the tools on the wall, the moss, the slab. And there, small but unmistakable, inked with the same patient precision as the storm: a figure in the doorway, hood up, rain running off her, a knife held low against her thigh.

The figure had scarred hands.

Ansel looked down at her own hands, still curled around the knife's grip, and turned them over in the page-light. The burn scars ran across both palms and up the left wrist—a childhood accident she'd been told about but didn't remember, a fire in a kitchen in a house in a town whose name she'd paid a broker to strike from two separate registries. She never let anyone see the scars. She wore gloves in company, gloves in summer, gloves until her crewmates had privately concluded she was either disfigured or vain, and she had preferred them wondering to their knowing.

No one alive had seen those hands. She was nearly sure of it. She was *paid* to be sure of it.

And here they were, drawn a hundred years ago, in a book that sang.

* * *

The storm chose that moment to find its voice. Wind came down off the Shoulder of the Shepherd and hit the buried garden like a fist, and somewhere overhead—through however many yards of soil and root and fallen masonry—something vast groaned and shifted. Dust sifted from the shed's ceiling beams. The hedge outside, that impossible living wall ringing the sunken ruin, creaked its slow intentional creak, and Ansel could have sworn the sound was rhythmic now. Almost like breathing. Almost like a sleeper rolling over.

She should leave. Every instinct she'd honed for three years said *leave*: unknown object, unknown provenance, glowing, singing, watching. Leave, put a valley between yourself and it, come back in daylight if you must, come back with Odell's crossbow and Hesk's shoulders and a plan.

But the book had turned another page while she wasn't looking.

She hadn't seen it move. That was the worst part—that it could do things in the blind spots of her attention, that it treated her vigilance as weather, irrelevant. The new spread showed no drawings at all. Only text, dense and slanted, filling both pages edge to edge, and written across the top of the left page in letters slightly larger than the rest, underlined twice:

*A girl will come in from the rain. She will stand where you are standing. She will not believe you.*

Ansel's breath went out of her in a long thin stream.

*Okay,* she said aloud, and her voice sounded strange in the small moss-hushed room, too loud and too young. *Okay. Trick. Some kind of—scrying trick, a lure. Somebody knows I take this pass. Somebody set this up.* Even as she said it, she heard the flaws in it. Nobody knew she took this pass; she varied her routes the way other people varied their breathing, and the Verdant Garden was a ruin so thoroughly dead on every map that smugglers used its name as slang for *nowhere*. To set this trap, someone would have needed to know her face, her scars, her habits, tonight's weather, and a century of patience besides.

Unless the trap hadn't been set for her. Unless it had been set for whoever came, and the book simply... adjusted.

That thought was worse. She sat down on the workbench's edge without deciding to, knees folding, and made herself read.

The hand was the same throughout—the cramped slanted script of someone who wrote fast and often, a working hand, a diarist's hand. But the voice changed from page to page in ways that unsettled her more than the drawings did. Some passages were clinical, almost botanical: measurements of the prison below, notes on the hedge's growth rates, careful tallies of what the seal consumed. Others were raw, human, addressed to no one—or to herself, or to the reader, and Ansel could not decide which possibility frightened her more.

*I have counted the roots again. Four hundred and six living, nine dead. I have named the dead ones after my failures. It helps. It does not help.*

*I dreamed of fire again. In the dream I am young and my hands are unburned and I am happy, and then I wake in the dark under the garden and remember that I gave the dream away on purpose, and I cannot recall why it seemed a fair price. This is the cruelty of it. The seal takes the memory AND the reason it mattered. You are left grieving a door you cannot find.*

Ansel stopped reading. Her hands had gone cold around the knife hilt.

*Gave the dream away on purpose.* Given *what* away? What kind of seal ran on—she turned the phrase over, testing it against everything she knew about hedge-magic, ward-work, the old rural craft that city wizards dismissed and that Bramble had always insisted was older and stranger than any of them guessed—

Bramble. The thought of him landed like a stone dropped down a well. She hadn't let herself think of Bramble in months—another discipline, another erasure, because he was the last thread tying her to a life with her real name stitched to it. If anyone would know what kind of book sang like ore and drew tomorrow's weather, it would be him. If she got out of this storm alive, she'd go downriver to Hollowfen. She'd risk it. Whatever the book was, it was worth more than her invisibility now, because her invisibility was clearly already spent.

The book turned another page.

This time she saw it—the movement at the edge of her vision, a soft whisper of paper, unhurried, deliberate, a hand turning pages though there was no hand. The new spread bore a single line of text, centered, surrounded by generous empty space as if the writer had wanted it isolated. Wanted it *read first*.

*If you are the one I think you are, your name is not the name you are using.*

The dust in Ansel's throat turned to glass.

Three years. Three years of *Winter*, which was barely a name at all, just a season she'd picked off a shipping manifest because nobody looks twice at a woman called Winter in a trade where everyone lies about colder things. Three years of paying strangers to forget her, of burning letters, of leaving crews before hearth-night invitations could become habit, of Odell's gruff "suit yourself" when she'd declined the winter gathering just last week—three years of building a person so thin the wind could blow through her, and some dead hand in a buried garden had cut through all of it with eleven words.

"No," she said. Her voice cracked on it. "No. You don't get—I don't know who you are, but you don't get to say that."

The book waited. Of course it waited. It had waited a hundred years; it could wait through her panic.

She made herself breathe the way she breathed before a hard climb: four counts in, seven counts out, until the shaking slowed. Then, because she could not help herself—because the same memory that terrified her also fed her, the terrible gift of a mind that kept everything—she read on.

The next pages were dated. Not by any calendar she recognized at first, and then she recognized it: the old gardeners' reckoning, moons rather than months, thirteen turns to the year. The entries marched forward turn by turn, and the writer recorded, with mounting precision, the approach of a date. *The thirteenth turn.* Again and again the thirteenth turn, circling closer like a hawk, and always the same phrases braided through the entries: *the Key. the thirteenth Key. she opens or she closes. God forgive me, I only ever see two endings.*

Two endings. Ansel frowned at the phrase. Two endings of what? The entries never said outright—they assumed knowledge she didn't have, the way diaries assume the diarist's whole life—but fragments surfaced between the lines like rocks at low tide. A prison. Something beneath Thornfell that the garden grew *over*, deliberately, root by root, year by year, a cage planted rather than built. A war-god, mentioned only once, in a hand that shook so badly the letters doubled: *Vaskar stirs in his sleep and I felt it in my TEETH.*

Vaskar. Even the name had weight. Ansel had heard it exactly once before, years ago, muttered by a drunk carter on the Hollowfen docks—*sleep, Vaskar, sleep, we buried you fair*—and she'd filed it away as dockside superstition and thought no more of it. Now the word sat on the page in shaking brown ink, and the hedge outside creaked its sleeper's creak, and Ansel understood, with the slow vertiginous certainty of a floor giving way, that the superstition had been the true thing all along, and everything solid in her life was the story.

* * *

Somewhere in the next hour—the storm sealed her in, and time went strange in the moss-light—she found the page with the hands.

It was near the back. The drawings returned here, but different: quick sketches instead of careful studies, the hand moving faster, urgent, as if the writer were racing something. A sketch of the hedge in bloom, impossibly, flowers on a dead garden. A sketch of a tunnel beneath the roots, marked with measurements. A sketch of a doorway of woven branches, half-open, light spilling through—

And then a sketch of a girl, full-figure this time, standing at the mouth of that woven door. Young. Hooded. Knife at her thigh. And rendered with loving, terrible accuracy: the scarred hands, both palms turned outward, fingers spread, as if showing them to someone. As if proving something.

Beneath it, the slanted hand had written:

*She has her mother's stubbornness and my hands. I burned myself at seven and lied about it for a week, so perhaps she gets the lying honestly too. If these pages are ever found, and if the girl reading them is the girl I have drawn, then listen to me, child:*

*I wrote this book so you would not be alone the way I was alone.*

*But I have to tell you the truth, which is that being alone may be the safer road. Everything I love, the seal asks me to feed it. I have begun to wonder if that is the design and not the cost—if the prison was grown from a lonely heart on purpose, because a lonely heart holds nothing back.*

*Find out if I'm wrong. Please. Find out if I'm wrong, and write it down where I can't reach the page.*

Ansel read the passage four times. On the fourth reading, something happened that she would later be unable to explain and unwilling to discuss: her eyes filled, and two tears dropped onto the mossy floor, and she had absolutely no idea which part of the page had done it. The mention of the mother she barely remembered? The burned liar at seven? The loneliness described so precisely it might have been transcribed from her own chest?

Or the signature, which she reached at last, at the bottom of the page, small and almost shy after a hundred years of boldness elsewhere:

*Wren.*

Just Wren. No surname. A hedge-witch's name, a commoner's name, a name that meant nothing to anyone except—

Except that Ansel Amberdale had grown up on stories of a great-aunt Wren who'd walked out of Nettlebed village a hundred years ago and never come back, and whose name her own grandmother had spoken only in winter, quietly, the way you speak of the dead who died wrong.

"No," Ansel whispered again, and this time the word had no force behind it at all. It was just a fact wearing a word.

The book turned its final page for the night. She watched it happen this time, watched the paper lift and fall with a gentleness no draft could manage, and the last spread of the evening held a single entry, fresh-inked-looking despite its age, the letters crisp as if written an hour ago:

*Tonight, the eve of the thirteenth turn, a girl shelters from the storm in the gardener's shed. She reads this page. She is afraid. She is thinking of leaving, and of going downriver, and of a man named Bramble whose shop smells of cedar and lamp oil.*

*Bramble is dead, child. I am sorry. He has been dead for nine years, and you are the reason, and you do not know it yet.*

*Turn the page anyway.*

Ansel did not turn the page.

She stood very still for a long time while the storm hammered the mountain and the hedge breathed its slow breaths and the book lay open, patient as a grave. Then she closed it—slammed it, actually, both hands, the way you slam a door on a thing following you—and the singing cut off mid-note, and the glow guttered down to nothing, and the shed went dark except for the grey seep of stormlight through the broken lintel.

She wrapped the book in her oilcloth. She told herself it was evidence. She told herself she was confiscating contraband, standard procedure, Odell would want it logged. She told herself she'd decide in the morning whether it went to a broker in Hollowfen or into the deepest hole she could dig.

She did not tell herself the truth, which was that her hands were trembling too badly to lie convincingly even to herself, and that the name *Bramble* had been sitting unexamined in her chest for nine years like a splinter she'd refused to feel, and that somewhere beneath the garden a god slept inside a cage grown from a lonely heart, and the book believed—with a century of patience and a dead woman's handwriting—that she was the key to it.

Outside, the storm broke against the Shoulder of the Shepherd exactly as the drawing promised, and Ansel Winterbourne—who was not Winterbourne, who was not anything, who was supposed to be no one—sat down in the dark with her back against the workbench and the wrapped book against her chest and began, very quietly, to plan a run to Hollowfen.

Downriver. To a burned shop and a dead man's ghost and whatever waited at the bottom of page thirteen.

The book, through its oilcloth wrapping, hummed one low note, like a struck bell remembering its sound.
