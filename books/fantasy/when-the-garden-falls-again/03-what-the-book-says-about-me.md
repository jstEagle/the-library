## 3. What the Book Says About Me

The storm had spent itself by the time Ansel dared to touch the book again.

It lay where she had dropped it—on the flat shelf of stone where the garden's ruined fountain had once poured water over carved lilies, now dry as old bone. Around it, the Verdant Garden kept its strange half-life: hedges grown wild and enormous through the broken colonnade, their leaves still green after a century of neglect, as if something below the roots refused to let them die. Rain dripped from a hundred thousand leaves in slow, irregular percussion. The cave mouth behind her breathed cold air that smelled of wet stone and, faintly, of roses.

She had not slept. She had sat with her back against a fallen pillar for six hours, watching the book the way one watches a sleeping snake, and in all that time it had not moved, had not glowed, had not done anything a book ought not to do. It was simply *there*, bound in leather gone soft and dark with age, its pages swollen from centuries of damp but somehow unrotted.

*The pages described tonight's storm,* she reminded herself. *Before tonight's storm.*

Ansel Amberdale had built her life on a simple architecture: know everything, owe nothing, be no one. Three years since she'd last spoken her surname aloud. Three years since she'd let anyone close enough to learn the shape of her silences. She knew every smuggler's route through the Thornfell passes better than the cartographers who drew them; she could recite the guard rotations at four border towns; she remembered, perfectly and uselessly, the exact words Odell had used to hire her, because remembering was what she did instead of trusting.

And none of it—not the routes, not the discipline, not the careful erasure of herself—had prepared her for opening a dead woman's book and finding her own hands drawn on the page.

Her hands were wrapped around her knees now, scarred knuckles white. The scars ran across both palms like river deltas—the burn from the Kettlebridge job, the knife slip outside Hollowfen, the rope burn from the year she didn't talk about. Small marks. Forgettable marks. She had never shown them to anyone deliberately.

The book showed them anyway.

*Get up,* she told herself. *Walk away. Leave it in the rain and let the Garden have it back.*

But her legs unfolded, and her boots crunched over shattered tile mosaic—she noticed, distantly, that the tiles had once formed a sunburst, and that someone had deliberately defaced the face at its center—and she picked the book up again.

The leather was warm. Not sun-warm; there was no sun under this cloud cover. Warm like a held hand.

"Right," she said aloud, because the sound of her own voice made the cave feel less like a throat closing around her. "Let's see what you want."

* * *

The first pages were history, or something like it.

The handwriting changed as she turned them—at least five different hands across the years, some cramped and hurried, some flowing and patient, one so large and angular it looked like it had been written with a chisel. A record, then. Passed down. Added to. The earliest entries spoke of the Thornfell mountains in language she almost couldn't parse: *the god beneath*, *the war that walked*, *the hedge that drinks*. There were diagrams—a mountain cross-section rendered in faded ink, showing caverns beneath the Verdant Garden itself, winding down past the reach of any miner's map. At the bottom of the deepest chamber, drawn small and precise, was a shape like a man folded into himself, bound in thorns.

Beneath it, in that chisel-cut hand: **VASKAR SLEEPS. LET HIM.**

Ansel's mouth had gone dry an hour ago. It went drier.

She knew the name. Everyone raised within a hundred miles of Thornfell knew the name, the way children know the names of things under beds—not as history but as furniture of fear. Vaskar, the war-god, the Unchained, who had burned the lowlands before the founding of any kingdom that still stood. Who had been put down—not killed, the stories always whispered, never killed, gods don't die, only sleep—by means no story agreed upon. Her grandmother's village had a rhyme about him. She'd sung it skipping rope without ever thinking about what she sang.

*Under hill and under hedge,*
*under root and under stone,*
*sleeps the dog that bit the world—*
*tread soft, child, and walk home.*

She had walked into his kennel to get out of the rain.

She turned the page, and the handwriting changed again—smaller now, neater, a hand that took its time. And the ink was different too. Not faded brown like the rest. Dark. Recent-looking, though the page crackled with age when she touched it.

**On the thirteenth moon-turn of the Year of the Falling Leaf,** it read, **the Key comes to the Garden.**

Ansel stopped breathing.

**She comes wet from the road, with a wound on her left arm that she stitched herself and stitched badly. She comes alone, because she has made alone into a country and learned its whole map. She carries nothing that belongs to her.**

Slowly, without wanting to, Ansel looked down at her left forearm, where the gash from Tuesday's rockfall showed through her torn sleeve, closed with three crooked stitches she'd done by firelight with shaking hands.

The entry continued:

**I have seen her face. I will not write how. The Garden shows things to those who tend it, and I have tended it longer than I wished to. She is young. She is frightened. She is trying very hard to be no one, which is how I know she is someone.**

The handwriting here wavered, just slightly, as if the writer's hand had trembled.

**She is my granddaughter.**

The book nearly fell from Ansel's hands.

She caught it against her chest and stood very still in the dripping ruin, listening to her own heartbeat slam against the leather cover, once, twice, a third time. The word seemed to echo off the wet stone. *Granddaughter.* She hadn't heard anyone say it in eleven years. She hadn't earned the right to hear it in eleven years, not since—

No. She shut that door. That door stayed shut.

Except the book had already opened it. Except the book was written in a hand she suddenly, horribly recognized—not the letters themselves, she'd never seen her grandmother write—but the *rhythm* of it, the way certain loops leaned left like they were flinching. Wren. Her grandmother Wren, the hedge-witch of Nettlebed, who had taught her which mushrooms lied and which stars told the truth, who had died—or vanished; the village had never agreed—when Ansel was nine years old.

A century ago, according to the dates in the earlier entries.

Which was impossible.

Which was exactly as impossible as a book describing a storm hours before it broke.

*Keep reading,* said the part of her that had memorized every smuggler route out of self-preservation. *You can't run from what you haven't read yet.*

* * *

The next pages were the prophecy proper.

She read standing up, because sitting felt too much like surrender. The rain had stopped entirely now; water dripped from the colonnade in long silver threads, and somewhere above, wind moved through the great hedges with a sound like slow breathing.

**The Thirteenth Key,** the heading read, in letters larger than the rest.

**Twelve times the seal has been renewed, twelve times by blood freely given, and each renewal buys the world a hundred years. The hundred years are ending. On the thirteenth moon-turn of the Year of the Falling Leaf, the Key must stand at the prison's heart and choose.**

**Two endings I have seen.**

**In the first, the Key refuses. The seal fails. Vaskar wakes, and the waking is fire, and the mountains come down, and the lowlands drown in smoke, and everything I love ends screaming. In this ending the Key lives longest of anyone, and wishes she did not.**

**In the second, the Key accepts. She gives her life to the hedge as the twelve before her gave theirs, and the seal holds another hundred years, and the world goes on not knowing her name. In this ending the Key dies on the thirteenth moon-turn, in the Garden, with the last light of the moon.**

Ansel turned the page with fingers that did not feel like her own.

**There is a date beneath this, and I am sorry for it, girl, but you deserve to know the shape of your cage even if I cannot build you a door.**

Beneath, in numerals older than any kingdom's coinage, was a date.

Ansel read it three times. Her mind—the famous memory, the uncanny recall that Odell called "a bloody ledger with legs"—did what it always did with numbers: fixed them instantly, permanently, helplessly. She converted it to the common reckoning without meaning to. Counted the moons. Did the arithmetic of the almanac she carried in her head the way other people carried knives.

Thirteen moon-turns from tonight.

One year. Give or take a week.

The date of her death, written a hundred years before her birth, in her grandmother's flinching hand.

She became aware, distantly, that she had sunk down onto the edge of the dry fountain, that the book was open in her lap, that her breath was coming fast and shallow in the way it did before panic properly arrived. The cave behind her exhaled its cold rose-smelling breath. The hedges rustled. Somewhere far below—so far below she might have imagined it—something shifted, like a sleeper turning over.

"No," she said. It came out as a whisper. She said it louder. "No. You don't get to do this. You don't get to—" She laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "I spent three years becoming nobody. Do you understand? Nobody doesn't get prophecies. Nobody doesn't get a *death date*. Nobody is *safe*."

The book said nothing, which was somehow worse.

Because here was the thing—the thing her mind kept circling back to with the pitiless precision of a carrion bird: the first ending. *The Key refuses.* Refusal was a choice. Which meant the second was a choice too. Which meant the date wasn't a sentence.

It was a deadline.

And Ansel Amberdale—who had spent three years proving to herself that she owed nothing to anyone, that every debt could be walked away from, that the only freedom was the kind you built out of leaving—had never once in her life met a deadline she intended to miss.

That was the moment the fear finished hardening. She felt it happen, felt the raw terror cool and set into something with edges, something she could hold. Fear was familiar. Fear she could work with. Fear was just information arriving early.

"Fine," she said to the book, to the Garden, to the dead woman whose loops leaned left like they were flinching. "You've got me for thirteen moons. But I'll tell you what nobody's getting: I'm not dying in a hole to feed a hedge, and I'm not burning the world either. Whatever you saw, old woman—you only saw two endings because you weren't creative enough."

The hedges rustled. It sounded almost like laughter. Or agreement. Or wind.

She didn't care which.

* * *

She should have left the book. Every instinct honed across three years of staying invisible screamed it: leave it, bury it, walk away, become no one again, let the Order or the Garden or the god sort out their own paperwork. The smart move. The safe move. The move that had kept her alive and unclaimed since she was nineteen years old.

Instead she found herself doing something she hadn't done since childhood: searching the ruin.

Not frantically. Methodically. Because if the book mattered—if a century-old hedge-witch had written her granddaughter's death into its pages—then whoever had kept this book safe for a hundred years might have left more. And because, underneath the method, there was a feeling she refused to name: the feeling of a girl who had lost her grandmother twice, once to vanishing and once to time, standing in the last place that woman had been.

She found three things.

First, in the cavity beneath the fountain's basin, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark: a pruning knife, its blade blackened with age but still keen, its handle worn smooth by decades of a grip that was not hers but fit her hand anyway. She stared at it for a long moment. Then she belted it on beside her own blade, because sentiment was a luxury and steel was not, and if the knife happened to make her feel less alone in a way she'd deny under torture, that was between her and the oilcloth.

Second, tucked inside the book's back cover, pressed flat as a leaf: a sprig of dried flowers she didn't recognize—small white stars on a stem, gone gray with age but still holding their shape. Nettlebed grew them along the churchyard wall. She had picked them for her grandmother once. She put the sprig back exactly where it had been, and her hands shook while she did it, and she pretended they didn't.

Third—and this was the thing that turned her blood from cold to arctic—scratched into the underside of the fountain's stone lip, in letters small enough to miss entirely, in a hand she now knew as well as her own:

***They will come for the book. Trust the Bloom's knights less than the god's dreams. Run south, little wren. Run south and find your flock.***

Ansel straightened slowly.

*They will come for the book.*

She thought about the Order of the Quiet Bloom—the quiet, courteous, ubiquitous Order whose waystation shrines dotted every pass she'd ever smuggled through, whose knights asked gentle questions and wrote gentle notes and whose gentleness had always struck her, without her ever examining why, as the gentleness of a hand over a mouth. She thought about how the Garden, ruinous and remote, had nevertheless felt *watched*. She thought about the fact that a book containing the operational details of a god's prison had survived a hundred years in an accessible ruin, and asked herself the question she should have asked hours ago:

Who else knew it was here?

The answer arrived not as a thought but as a sensation: the prickle at the nape, the animal certainty of being observed. She turned. The colonnade stretched away into green shadow, empty. The cave mouth breathed. Nothing moved.

Nothing except, perhaps, the future, coming for her at the speed of rumor.

"Run south," she murmured. South was Hollowfen. South was Bramble—old Bramble, who dealt in rare books and rarer secrets, who had sold her her first forged papers and never once asked her real name, which in Ansel's private taxonomy of trust placed him somewhere just below family and just above God. If anyone in the lowlands knew what a Thirteenth Key was worth and to whom, it was Bramble.

If he was still alive. If the Order hadn't already—

She stopped the thought, packed the book deep into her pack wrapped in her spare shirt, checked her gear with the automatic thoroughness of ten thousand departures, and paused only once—at the edge of the colonnade—to look back at the ruin.

The hedges stood motionless in the gray morning light. The dry fountain waited. Somewhere beneath it all, a god slept on a bed of thorns, and a dead woman's magic tended the roots, and a date ticked closer with every heartbeat.

"I'm coming back," she told the Garden quietly. "Not because you said so. Because I don't keep deadlines I intend to miss—and I'm not planning to die on yours."

Then Ansel Amberdale stole a hundred-year-old prophecy, took the south trail at a smuggler's trot, and ran—out of the Garden, down through the dripping passes, toward Hollowfen and whatever waited there—with the weight of two endings on her back and, though she didn't know it yet, a third one folded small in the dark behind her grandmother's final page.

Behind her, deep beneath the roots, something that had waited a hundred years began, very softly, to count.
