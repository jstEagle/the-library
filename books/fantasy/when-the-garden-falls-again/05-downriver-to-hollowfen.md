## 5. Downriver to Hollowfen

The river barge *Kindly Stranger* smelled of fish guts and tar, and Ansel loved it the way you love a thing that asks nothing of you.

She'd paid her passage with three days of route-work—reading the Thornfell waterways from memory the way other people read their own palms—and now she stood at the rail in the grey hour before dawn, watching Hollowfen assemble itself out of the mist. The city came in layers. First the sound: a low industrial murmur, winches and hammers and the argumentative gulls. Then the smell: brine, coal smoke, frying onions, the sweet rot of the tanneries downriver. Then the shape of it at last, stacked and leaning against itself like a drunk old friend—rooftops shingled with everything from slate to ship's hull, stairways stitched up the cliffside, lanterns still burning yellow against the last of the dark.

Hollowfen had no walls because Hollowfen had no enemies it couldn't buy. It was a city built on the honest principle that everything could be smuggled if you were friendly enough about it, and it had grown rich and crooked and strangely kind.

Three years since she'd last seen it. She counted without meaning to—the way she counted everything—and made it three years, two months, eleven days. The night she'd walked out of Bramble's shop for the last time, she'd told herself she was cutting loose a small life. A hedge-witch's apprentice turned cargo-runner, nothing anchored anywhere. That was the story she'd built, plank by plank, until it held her weight.

The book rode against her ribs inside her coat, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a sleeping animal.

*Thirteenth Key.* She hadn't opened it since the Garden. She didn't need to. The page was printed on the inside of her eyelids now, the way routes were, the way faces never were.

"First time to Hollowfen?" asked the bargemaster, coming up beside her with two cups of something hot and terrible. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a beard he clearly trimmed by feel in the dark.

"No," Ansel said, and took the cup anyway, because refusing kindness cost more than accepting it.

"Didn't think so. You've got the look of someone coming home." He sipped, grimaced with pleasure. "Or leaving one. Hard to tell, on this river."

Ansel said nothing. The bargemaster, being a bargemaster and therefore fluent in silence, nodded as if she'd said something wise and went back to his wheel.

Home. The word sat in her chest like a stone dropped into a well—one splash, then nothing, no bottom reached. She tried to imagine Bramble's face and found she could summon the shop more easily than the man: the smell of dried nettle and lamp oil, the almanacs stacked in towers that defied several laws of physics, the bell over the door that rang flat because he'd never fixed it, because, he said, a flat bell kept away customers who needed too much conversation.

Bramble, who had taught her letters with a stick in river mud when ink was too expensive. Bramble, who had never once asked where she came from or what she was running from, only what she wanted to learn next.

Her hands, scarred across both palms—she'd stopped asking herself how, years ago; some questions were doors she'd nailed shut—curled around the cup until the heat hurt.

The book said she would die on the ninth of Deepfrost. The book said she was a key. The book said, in her grandmother's careful hand, that the world would end or be saved depending on what a girl did on a night thirteen moon-turns from the storm.

And the book had been sitting in a ruin an hour's walk from the passes she ran every season, which meant the Order had known about it and left it there, which meant either they didn't know what it was—or they did, and they were waiting.

Either way, Hollowfen was the only place she knew how to disappear into. And Bramble was the only person alive who might know why a dead woman had written her granddaughter's death warrant a hundred years before she was born.

The barge slid under the great leaning arch of the Tollgate, where the harbor-warden's men swung lanterns over the deck, and Ansel pulled her hood forward and became, with the ease of long practice, nobody at all.

* * *

Hollowfen's streets changed character the way weather did. In the Scales—the merchant terraces above the waterline—you got cobbles swept by hired brooms and doorways wide enough for vanity. Down here in the Bilge, the streets were mud and memory, and the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators sharing one secret after another.

Ansel moved through them on feet that remembered without being asked. Left at the drowned chapel. Through Cutpurse Court, where a boy of perhaps nine watched her pass with the professional blankness of a child deciding whether she was worth following. Up the Ropewalk, where lines of hemp hung between buildings like the strings of some enormous drowned instrument, dripping in the morning damp.

It was on the Ropewalk that she first felt it: the way conversations died a half-step ahead of her.

Not the usual thing. Hollowfen talked constantly, loudly, about everything—it was a city that conducted its crimes in full voice because everyone already knew. But as she climbed toward Tanner's Stair, a knot of dockhands by a wine-shop door went quiet mid-sentence, and when she passed close enough to hear them resume, the word that rose out of the murmur was not a word she expected.

"—Bramble."

Another knot, further up. Two women mending nets, heads together. "—burned him out, they say. Burned the whole—"

Burned.

The word went through her like a hook through soft flesh.

She stopped walking. The nets-menders looked up, saw her face under the hood, and something in it—she never learned what—made them gather their work and move off without finishing the sentence. Cowards, she thought, and then, immediately, unfairly: tell me. Tell me the rest.

Tanner's Stair. Forty-one steps; she'd carried crates up them at fourteen, hating Bramble cheerfully the whole way. She took them now two at a time, and the book knocked against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and at the top—

At the top there was no shop.

There was a hole in the street.

That was her first, absurd impression: that the building had simply been deleted, scooped out of the row of shops like a bad tooth. The neighboring structures bore scorch marks fanning outward from the gap, their shutters blackened, their plaster crazed. Where Bramble's Almanac & Curiosities had stood for longer than anyone could remember—not a fine building, not a beloved one, just a stubborn one, all crooked beams and bulging windows—there remained a field of ash and char, cordoned off with rope that had already sagged, already been stepped over so many times it lay in the muck like a shed snakeskin.

Rain had gotten into the ruins. Everything was the color of wet iron.

Ansel stood on the rope.

She was aware, distantly, of doing things wrong—of standing in plain sight, hood back, staring at wreckage with the expression of someone who has just been told the world is larger and crueler than advertised. But her body had gone somewhere else, the way it sometimes did, leaving behind a girl-shaped instrument that could walk and breathe but couldn't feel yet. Later. Later the feeling would come, with interest.

She crossed the rope.

The ash took her boots to the ankle. Here the base of the counter, recognizable by the burn pattern where decades of spilled lamp oil had soaked in—Bramble had called it the counter's soul, said it gave the wood character. There the corner post where the flat bell used to hang. The little brass bracket was fused to the beam, still hanging, holding nothing.

Against the far wall, half-collapsed, the chimney stood alone. On its bricks, someone had written something in charcoal. She waded toward it through the ash, and her heart performed a complicated maneuver—hope and dread braided into a single sick knot—because if Bramble had survived, if he'd come back and marked the wall, then—

The charcoal read: **ASK WHO SET IT AND WHY NO ONE ANSWERS.**

Not Bramble's hand. Someone else's. Someone angry.

Ansel stood in the ruin of the only place that had ever let her be small and unremarkable and safe, and understood, slowly, the way ice understands the spring: her past had never been what she thought it was.

She had spent three years believing she was hiding from nothing. A debtless, nameless drifter, erasing herself out of simple preference, the way some people shave their heads or take vows. She had told herself Bramble's shop was a footnote—a kindly old man, some books, a few seasons of apprenticeship before she grew restless.

But the Order of the Quiet Bloom had burned a bookshop in the middle of Hollowfen, in a city that bought its way out of trouble, and the whole street knew about it and no one would say who did it, which meant whoever did it frightened even Hollowfen.

They had burned a bookshop to send a message. And the message was about her.

"You shouldn't stand there," said a voice.

She turned. The speaker was an old woman in a fishmonger's apron, standing in the doorway of the chandlery next door, arms folded against the cold. Her eyes were red-rimmed, whether from age or weeping Ansel couldn't tell.

"Why not?" Ansel asked. Her voice came out steady. Good. The instrument worked.

"Bad luck." The woman spat, ceremonially, sideways. "Bad luck and worse neighbors. Since the fire, folk cross the street rather than walk past. They say the Bloom marked it. They say anyone who traded there is marked too."

"Traded there how?"

The old woman looked at her for a long moment. Rain dripped off the chandlery's scorched awning between them.

"You're one of his," she said at last. Not a question. "I can see it. You've got the look. He used to feed strays, that man. Fed every stray in the Bilge, near enough. Half this district learned their letters in that shop, and now they cross themselves when they walk past it." She shook her head. "You want to know what happened?"

"Yes."

"So does everyone. Nobody knows. Night before last, maybe—no one heard anything, which is its own answer, isn't it? Fire that starts quiet. Fire that burns a whole shop down to wet ash and doesn't touch the roofs on either side." She tapped her temple. "That's not fire, girl. That's craft. And the people who do that kind of craft came asking after him first—I saw them myself, two nights before, gray cloaks, flower pinned at the collar, polite as tax collectors. Asked had he seen anyone passing through. Asked it gentle. And old Bramble, daft loyal fool, he smiled at them and said—" her voice caught, "—he said, 'I see no one. I sell books.'"

The rain fell softly on the ash. Somewhere below, a ship's horn moaned across the water, long and low, like the city grieving on schedule.

"They came back," Ansel said.

"They came back." The woman wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, roughly, as though annoyed at them. "And the shop burned quiet, and Bramble—" She stopped. Looked at Ansel properly. "No body, if that's what you're standing there waiting to hear. No body found. Whether that means he ran, or means they took him, or means—" she gestured at the ash with her chin, "—well. Wet ash doesn't argue. You'll have to decide for yourself what hope costs."

Ansel looked down at her scarred hands. They had gone very still, which was how she knew, underneath the numbness, how bad it was. When she was truly afraid, her hands never shook. They waited.

"Did he leave anything?" she asked. "Papers. Books. Anything saved from the fire."

"Now you're asking me to hang myself next to him." But the old woman's voice had softened. She glanced up the street, down the street, a habit older than caution. "There's a boy. Ferry-brat, name of Wick. Bramble paid him a copper a week to sleep in the cellar loft, keep rats off the stock. He wasn't there that night—he's the one wrote the message on your chimney, the little wretch, bold as brass before the ash was cold. If anything got out of that shop, it went out in Wick's coat."

"Where do I find him?"

"Where do any of us go when the sky falls?" The fishmonger almost smiled. "To the people we swore we wouldn't bother. Try the Gull's Rest, on the floating tier. He sleeps under the third table when the ferry money runs short. Tell him Netta sent you, and mind you feed him, he's skin and spite."

Ansel pressed two coins into the woman's floury palm before she could refuse—payment, not charity; she'd learned the difference from Bramble—and turned back toward the stair.

"Girl." Netta's voice stopped her at the rope. "Whoever you are, whatever he hid you from—" she nodded at the ruin, at the flat bell's empty bracket, "—he thought you were worth that. Old mean bugger liked maybe six people in his whole life, and I'd have bet my stall you were one. Don't waste it."

Ansel didn't trust her voice. She nodded once and went down the stairs, and the feeling arrived on the fourth step from the bottom, exactly on schedule, with interest: a grief so large and so specific that it had no room for tears yet, only pressure, like deep water.

She had thought she'd cut herself free cleanly. She had thought the past was small.

The past had been burning quietly in the night for her sake, and she hadn't even known enough to mourn it.

* * *

The Gull's Rest sat on the floating tier like a barnacle with pretensions—a tavern built on pontoons and stubbornness, listing gently starboard, its windows fogged with pipe smoke and the steam of a hundred bodies keeping out the rain. Inside it was all low beams and lower conversation. Smugglers, ferrymen, a customs officer trying very hard to look invisible in the corner and failing, because customs officers always chose the same corner and everyone knew it.

Ansel bought a cup of small beer she didn't want, established sightlines, and found the third table.

Under it, curled like a comma, was a boy asleep in a nest of stolen sacking. Maybe twelve, sharp-boned, with the permanent squint of someone raised on water-glare. His coat was enormous—clearly a man's coat cut down badly—and it bulged in ways that coats containing only a boy should not.

She crouched. "Wick."

He woke the way harbor children wake: instantly, silently, with a knife already halfway out of his boot. She saw it happen and didn't move, just held still and let him assess her—hood, hands, no visible weapon, no visible anything.

"Netta sent me," she said quietly. "From the chandlery."

The knife paused. "Netta don't send nobody."

"She does when they pay her." Ansel set a copper on the table above his head, where he could see it through the gap. "And I'll buy you breakfast. Real breakfast. Eggs."

A long pause. Then, from beneath the table, in a voice roughened by sleep and suspicion: "Eggs and bread. And you sit facing the door."

"Eggs and bread, and I'll face the door."

They took a bench by the window. Wick ate like a small wolf—watchful between bites, always tracking the room—but eat he did, and somewhere in the middle of the second egg, his shoulders came down from around his ears.

"Bramble," Ansel said.

The boy's chewing slowed. "Everyone asks about Bramble now." He said it the way you'd say *everyone looks at the gallows now*. "Used to be nobody gave two coppers for the old man. Now it's all anyone whispers."

"What do they whisper?"

"That he was Bloom. That he was running from the Bloom. That he owed the Bloom. That he was the Bloom." Wick shrugged elaborately, a gesture he'd learned from men twice his size. "Nobody knows nothing. That's the whisper. *Nobody knows nothing*, and everybody's scared of the not-knowing." He looked up at her, and beneath the harbor-hard squint his eyes were young and furious. "They burned the best man in the Bilge, miss. Whatever else is true, that's true."

Ansel kept her gaze on the door, as promised. "Netta says things left the shop in your coat."

The boy went still. She watched him weigh it—copper against copper, eggs against eggs, the arithmetic of survival—and then, unexpectedly, choose something other than the arithmetic.

"He paid me a copper a week," Wick said. "And taught me figures. And when the fever took my sister he didn't charge me for the physic-book." He pushed his plate aside and pulled the big coat around, opening it with the care of a man disarming a trap. Sewn into the lining, in a dozen places, were flat shapes wrapped in waxed cloth. "Night before the fire, he comes to me, wakes me up, puts these in my coat himself. Says, *Wick, if ever a girl comes asking—with scarred hands, mind, you'll know her by the hands—you give her the lot and you ask her nothing, because the less you know the longer you float.*" The boy looked at her hands, then, deliberately, at her face. "Didn't say nothing about no book."

"I don't have a book," Ansel said, which was technically true, since the book was currently a rumor-shaped weight against her ribs.

"Good," said Wick, with the gravity of a twelve-year-old dispensing state secrets. "'Cause people asking about the book are getting followed. Gray cloaks. There's one been in here twice today already, drinking one cup for an hour, watching the door." He slid the bundle of waxed packets across the table, all of them, and closed her fingers over them with small grimy hands. "You're the girl with the hands. I'd know you anywhere. He described you like he was describing weather he loved."

Ansel's throat closed. The instrument, the calm walking girl-shape, cracked straight down the middle, and what showed through was just a person, sitting in a smoky tavern, holding a dead man's last errand.

"Wick," she managed. "Get up. Get your coat. Now."

Because the door had opened, and the draft that came through it smelled of rain and cold incense, and the man who stepped in out of the wet wore a gray traveling cloak with the rain still beading on it—and at his collar, discreet as a wedding ring, a small white flower gone brown at the edges.

He scanned the room with the unhurried patience of a fisherman mending nets. His gaze crossed the window bench, crossed Ansel, crossed the boy—and stopped. Came back. Settled.

He began, politely, to remove his gloves.

Ansel stood, slow and unhurried, heart slamming, mind already running the map of Hollowfen's rooftops like a finger down a ledger. Behind her, Wick's chair scraped. Around the room, conversations faltered, resumed, faltered again—the whole tavern sensing weather change.

The Bloom knight smiled at her, courteous as a tax collector.

"Don't run," he said pleasantly, to her, to the room, to no one. "We only want to talk about the old man's books."

And Ansel Amberdale—who was no one, who was nobody, whose name was a debt she'd stopped paying—reached beneath her cloak, gripped the stolen prophecy through its oilcloth wrapping, and ran.
