## 6. The Crew You Don't Choose

The rain found Ansel before Hollowfen did.

It came down the river valley in a grey wall, hammering the water flat, and she let it. Better to arrive soaked and forgettable than dry and remembered. Three years of practice had taught her that weather was the best disguise there was—no one looks twice at a drowned rat trudging through a gate at dusk, hood down, shoulders hunched, just another body the season was trying to kill.

Hollowfen didn't care who you were. That was its whole religion. A city built on stilts and secrets over a slow brown river, it smelled of tar, fish guts, wet rope, and the sweet rot of fruit nobody asked where it came from. The stilt-districts leaned against each other like drunkards; the lantern-light turned everything the color of old brass. Somewhere above her, gulls argued over something dead. Somewhere below, the river carried whatever the city wanted gone.

Ansel moved through it with her collar up and her eyes down and her mind working the way it always worked—cataloguing exits, noting faces, filing the layout of every alley into the vast silent warehouse of her memory. She could have drawn Hollowfen from inside out, blindfolded, in the dark. She'd spent one winter here, three years ago, running cargo for a crew whose names she had made a point of never learning too well.

*Odell*, she allowed herself. *Captain. Chews blackroot, hates the tax barges, pays fair.* *Pip and Marisol—the twins. Pip talks enough for four people; Marisol counts everything twice.* *Hesk. Big as a door, gentle as Sunday morning.*

That was as far as she'd ever let herself go. First names and habits. Nothing that could be used to hurt them, nothing that could be used to hurt *her*. You don't grieve people you never learned properly. She'd learned that lesson young, and she'd learned it so well it had grown into her bones like marrow.

Bramble's shop was in Tanners' Row, on the east bank, past the glue-houses. She told herself she wasn't hurrying. She told herself he might not even be there—that hedge-witches kept their own hours, that an old man could simply be away gathering moss or selling charms upriver. She told herself a great many things as her feet carried her faster and faster through the rain, and none of them survived the moment she rounded the corner and saw what was left of the shop.

*Burned.*

Not damaged. Not scorched. *Burned*—burned the way things are burned when someone wants them erased from the world's memory entirely. The roof was gone. The walls were charcoaled ribs. Rain hissed and steamed off the black timbers, filling the alley with a ghost-smell of burnt sage and melted wax and something else underneath, something chemical and wrong that made her think of the lamps soldiers use. The little sign that had read *BRAMBLE — REMEDIES, CHARMWORK, HONEST WEIGHING* lay in the mud, split down the middle, the letters blistered beyond reading.

Ansel stood in the rain and looked at it, and felt something crack quietly somewhere behind her sternum.

She went in anyway. Of course she did. She climbed over the fallen doorframe with her heart slamming and picked her way through the wreckage of the only place in the world that had ever been almost-kind to her. His workbench was a slab of wet charcoal. His jars had burst in the heat, their contents fused into colored glass tears. His cot in the back room was a frame of springs and ash.

And on the floor, half-buried under a collapsed shelf, someone had chalked a mark on the hearthstone before the fire took everything else: a circle, crossed by thirteen lines.

She knew that mark. She'd seen it last night, inked on the flyleaf of a stolen book that said she would die.

Ansel crouched there in the ruins of Bramble's life, rain dripping off her nose onto the blackened stone, and understood two things with terrible clarity. The first was that Bramble was dead, or worse than dead, and that it was because of her—because he had sheltered her once, taught her routes and herb-lore and how to move unseen, because he had been the single thread connecting her careful new invisibility to the girl she'd been before.

The second thing was that she couldn't stay here crouching in the ashes like a mourner, because mourners got remembered, and being remembered right now meant dying.

She was turning to go when the voices came.

"—told you, I saw her come up Tanners' Row not twenty minutes past. Hooded, moving fast, headed straight for the witch's place." A pause. "Well. What's left of it."

That voice she knew. Pip. One of the twins—the talker, the wiry one, the one who could sell a lie to its own mother. And answering him, low and gravelled as a cart over stones:

"Then we've got maybe a minute before whoever burned this place sends someone back to check the ashes. Move."

Odell.

Ansel did the arithmetic of survival in half a heartbeat. Two options: slip out the back through the gap where the garden fence had burned away, and vanish alone into Hollowfen's ten thousand hiding places—or step out into the open, in front of two people she had spent three years keeping at arm's length, and ask for help.

Every instinct she owned screamed the first option. Her whole architecture of safety was built on the first option. *No debts owed, no loyalties kept.* If she vanished now, they'd shrug, say *that Amberdale girl, always strange*, and forget her by morning—

But they knew she was here. Someone had seen her. Which meant the Order's hunters, whoever they were, could know too, and a lone runner with a stolen prophecy book and a death date was worth exactly nothing in a fight.

And beneath all the cold arithmetic, in a place she didn't want to look at, something smaller and warmer and more frightened whispered: *they came looking for you.*

She stepped out of the ruin.

Pip saw her first and actually yelped—a short, undignified sound that the rain swallowed. He was soaked to the skin, hair plastered flat, a knife already half-drawn from his belt. Marisol stood behind him in the alley mouth, and Marisol's hand wasn't anywhere near a weapon; it was pressed over her mouth.

"Gods' teeth," Pip breathed. "It *is* you. We thought—" He stopped himself, which was unlike him, and started again more carefully. "We heard the Order was asking after you in Thornfell. Asking *hard*. And then Bramble's shop burns the same week, and you walk out of the ashes like something out of a ballad, and I'm supposed to just stand here in the rain?"

"Ansel." Odell hadn't moved from the alley mouth. The captain was a broad, grey-stubbled woman built like a barge, with a face that had been carved by weather and disappointment. Her eyes went over Ansel the way they went over cargo—weighing, assessing, finding the damage. "You're hurt?"

"No."

"You're sure? Because you're standing in a burned building holding your arm like it's hurt."

Ansel looked down. She was gripping her own forearm hard enough to leave marks. She hadn't noticed. She made herself let go.

"I need to leave Hollowfen," she said. Her voice came out flat and steady, and she hated how much effort the steadiness cost. "Tonight. Downriver. I can pay."

"Can you," Odell said.

"I have coin."

"I didn't ask about coin." The captain stepped closer, rain streaming off her oilskin, and studied Ansel with those flat grey eyes. "I asked if you're hurt. There's a difference between a person who's fine and a person who says they're fine, and I've hauled enough of both across this river to know the smell of each. So I'll say it plain, since you seem to have forgotten how we do things aboard my boat. Nobody pays us to help family. Family doesn't pay."

"We're not family," Ansel said. It came out faster than she intended. Sharper. "I ran cargo for you one winter. That's all."

Something flickered across Odell's face—not anger. Something older and tireder. Behind her, Marisol made a small sound, and Pip looked down at his boots.

"One winter," Odell agreed. "One winter in which you slept on our deck, ate at our table, pulled Marisol out of the Greywater when the ford took her under, and taught Pip half the passes in Thornfell because you couldn't stand watching him chart them wrong. One winter, girl. And then you vanished without a word, and we spent two years wondering if we'd done something to drive you off." She jerked her chin at the smoking ruin. "Now the Quiet Bloom is hunting you, our friend the hedge-witch is dead, and you want to hire passage like a stranger with a purse." She shook her head slowly. "You can pay me if it makes you feel safer. But you should know that's not why the *Kestrel* is leaving tonight, and it's not why we were standing in this alley getting soaked."

"Why were you?" Ansel asked. The question escaped before she could stop it.

"Because Pip saw you coming and we've been watching the ashes for two days waiting for you to show up," Marisol said quietly. She had a low, precise voice, the twin mirror of her brother's chatter. "Bramble was ours too, Ansel. We figured if anyone came back to grieve, it'd be you."

The rain filled the silence. Ansel stood in it and felt the first real crack run through three years of careful masonry—not a collapse, nothing so dramatic, just a thin bright line of light where there had been solid wall. They had been *waiting*. Watching a dead man's ashes for two days on the chance that a girl who never let them close would come home to them.

"I can't tell you what's happening," she said finally. "If I tell you, you'll be in danger. Worse than you are."

"We're smugglers," Pip said. "Danger is the job description. It's literally the first line."

"Pip."

"He's not wrong, Captain."

Odell ignored them both. "Here's how it is. The *Kestrel* casts off within the hour, downriver, no questions at the toll-gates because I own the man who runs them. You can be on her or not. If you're on her, you eat at our table, you sleep behind our lock, and when the Bloom comes asking—and they will come asking—we never heard of any Ansel. No surname. No debts owed." Her mouth twisted, almost a smile, and not a kind one. "Sound familiar? It should. It's your creed, girl. We're just better at it than you are, because we practice it *together*."

Ansel looked at the burned shop. At the chalk circle on the hearthstone, dissolving now under the rain, thirteen lines blurring into nothing. At these three people standing in a flooded alley offering her the one thing she had spent three years making sure no one could ever take from her again—and offering it knowing full well what it might cost.

Her grandmother's face floated up unbidden, and she pushed it down. The book in her pack seemed to weigh as much as a coffin.

"If they come for me," she said, "you give me up. You hear me? You open the hatch and you hand me over and you keep your crew. Promise me that, or I find another boat."

"No," said Hesk.

The voice came from behind them, deep and mild as a church bell heard through a wall, and Ansel turned to find the big man filling the far end of the alley, holding an umbrella that was comically inadequate for his size. He must have followed the twins from the docks. Water dripped off his enormous shoulders. His round, sad, kind face was perfectly serene.

"Hesk," Odell sighed.

"She says promise," Hesk said, in the tone of a man explaining the tide to a child. "Can't promise a thing that's wrong. So. No." He extended the umbrella over Ansel's head, which required him to bend nearly double, and smiled at her with the simple, unbearable warmth of a hearth-fire. "Hello, Ansel. You look tired. Come eat something."

And that—gods help her—that was the crack widening. Because it would have been easy to refuse Odell's bargain, easy to refuse anything offered with terms and strategy and captain's logic. But Hesk wasn't offering a bargain. Hesk was offering soup. And Ansel Winterbourne—no, not Winterbourne, never that name aloud, *Amberdale*, the name she'd buried, the name the book knew—stood under a too-small umbrella in the rain beside a dead man's ashes and felt her eyes sting and could not for the life of her have said whether it was grief or relief.

"Fine," she said, roughly. "The *Kestrel*. Tonight. But I keep my own watch and my own cabin corner and—"

"And you'll sit at the table," Marisol finished, taking her arm, "because Marisol feeds strays and doesn't argue about it. Walk. Talk later. Pip, scout the Row."

They moved. And the strange thing, the thing she would turn over in her mind for nights afterward, was how easily her feet fell into step with theirs—how her body, which had spent three years walking alone, seemed to remember this formation, this rhythm, flanked and covered and *accompanied*, the way a hand remembers a tool.

* * *

The *Kestrel* was a river-brigantine, low and mean and fast, black-hulled, with a figurehead of a bird that some previous owner had painted scowling. She smelled of pitch, tea, pipe-smoke, and the particular dog-and-wool warmth of people who lived close together by necessity. Ansel sat at the long table in the low cabin with a bowl of barley stew in front of her and five people around her, and realized she had forgotten what it was like to eat while other people talked.

Pip talked. Pip always talked. He narrated the departure from the dock, the bribes, the near-miss with a Bloom skiff moored at the customs stair ("grey cloaks, grey faces, grey *souls*, probably"), and the entire saga of a goat he'd once smuggled in a flour barrel, which Ansel was fairly certain ended differently every time he told it. Marisol corrected the details without looking up from her ledger. Odell smoked by the lamp and pretended not to listen. Hesk ate four bowls and hummed.

And Ansel sat among them with the stolen book locked in the false bottom of her pack beneath her bunk, and listened, and hated how much she didn't hate it.

"So what's in the pack?" Pip asked, around a heel of bread. "The one you haven't let out of arm's reach since the alley. I'm not prying. I'm professionally curious. Different trade entirely."

"Pip," said three voices at once.

"What! We're all thinking it."

Ansel set down her spoon. Around the table, conversation died. She looked at their faces—Odell's guarded patience, Marisol's quiet attention, Pip's irrepressible curiosity, Hesk's bottomless gentleness—and felt the truth pressing against her teeth like a fever. *A book wrote today's storm before it happened. A book drew my hands. A book knows the day I die.* She could not say it. Saying it aloud would make it real, and would make it theirs, and they had already paid enough for her.

"It belonged to Bramble," she said instead. Each word chosen like a footfall on ice. "It's why his shop burned. I'm going to find out who did it."

Silence. Then Odell knocked the ash from her pipe against the table edge, deliberate as a gavel.

"Bramble's killer," she said. "Now *that's* cargo worth carrying." She looked around the table, and Ansel watched something pass between them, wordless, the way it does among crews—among families—who have sailed together long enough. "All in favor of changing our route to suit?"

"Aye," said Marisol.

"Aye," said Hesk.

"Aye," said Pip, and then, softer, to Ansel: "Told you. Family doesn't pay."

Ansel looked down at her stew so no one would see her face. *You don't choose the crew you travel with,* Bramble had told her once, years ago, weighing herbs in his crooked little shop with the good-lamp burning. *Life puts you in the boat. All you choose is whether you row.* She had thought it a sentimental old man's nonsense. She had built three years of walls on top of the belief that it was nonsense.

Later, when the others had turned in and the river murmured against the hull, she sat on her bunk with the book open on her knees by shuttered lamplight, telling herself she wouldn't read further. She read further. Past the storm, past the cave, past her scarred hands. To the page where the cramped, familiar handwriting—the handwriting she now knew in her gut was her grandmother's—described the Thirteenth Key, and the prison beneath Thornfell, and the choice that would crack the world one way or the other.

And at the bottom of the page, in fresher ink, in a different hand—one she recognized with a lurch as Bramble's—someone had written a single line, as if adding a marginal note to scripture:

*If you are reading this, little wren's-child, look in the hollowed Almanac of Years, third shelf of my cellar, Nettlebed house. Forgive an old man his secrets. Your grandmother left you more than a curse.*

Nettlebed. A name she'd never heard, that nonetheless pulled at her like a hook behind the breastbone.

Above her, through the deck planks, she heard Hesk humming to himself at the night-watch, and Pip's muffled laughter, and the creak of the *Kestrel* running easy downriver toward whatever came next. Ansel closed the book, held it against her chest, and let herself—for one unguarded breath—believe that she might not have to carry it alone.

Then she blew out the lamp and began, quietly, to plan how to reach Nettlebed before the thirteenth moon.
