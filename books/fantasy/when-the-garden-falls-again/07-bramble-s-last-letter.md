## 7. Bramble's Last Letter

The river took three days to carry them to Hollowfen.

Ansel spent them in the Kestrel's belly, curled in the narrow space between crates of smuggled moon-ore, the prophecy book pressed flat beneath her pillow where she could feel its spine through the thin canvas. She did not sleep well. The book's pages seemed to breathe against her waking mind, whispering numbers she almost recognized, dates that circled like carrion birds waiting for their feast.

Twice she climbed topside to watch the banks slide past. The first time, Pip found her standing at the rail, her hair whipping in the wind off the water, and offered her a tin cup of something hot that smelled of cinnamon and bark. She took it. She did not drink it. Pip stood beside her for a while, not speaking, and when he finally wandered back to help Marisol with the rigging, she realized her hands had stopped shaking.

The second time, Odell himself came to find her. He was a thick-armed man with a face like weathered oak, and he did not pretend at gentleness.

"You're jumpy as a cat in a currier's shop," he said, leaning against the mast. "I don't like jumpy passengers. Makes the crew nervous."

"I won't be passengers long." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I have business in Hollowfen. An old teacher. I need to find him."

"Bramble?" Odell's eyes narrowed. "Hedge-merchant Bramble?"

She had never told him the name. She had never told anyone on this crew the name. "You know him?"

"I know his shop burned three months ago." Odell watched her face, and whatever he saw there made him soften, just slightly, around the eyes. "You didn't know."

"No."

"He was a friend of yours."

It wasn't a question. Ansel turned back to the river, to the grey-green water sliding past carrying leaves and foam and the reflected clouds of a sky that could not decide whether to rain. "He was the closest thing I had."

Odell was quiet for a long moment. Then he reached into his coat and produced a flat bottle, pulled the cork, and took a swig before offering it to her. She shook her head.

"Hollowfen's not kind to people asking questions about burned shops," he said. "The Order keeps a heavy presence there since the fire. You'd do better to let me ask around first. Safer."

"I don't know how to be safe."

"Most people don't." He corked the bottle and tucked it away. "But most people also don't carry a book that makes my compass spin wrong when they open it near iron. Whatever you've got wrapped up in, girl, it's bigger than one old hedge-merchant's ghost. You'd do well to remember that."

He left her at the rail. Ansel watched the river until her eyes burned, and then she went back below, and she did not come topside again until the Kestrel tied up at Hollowfen's rotting quay and the city's smell—brine and smoke and the sweet rot of the marshlands—washed over her like a memory she couldn't place.

*

Hollowfen had been beautiful once.

Ansel remembered that, distantly, the way one remembers a dream told by someone else. She had come here first at fourteen, half-starved and running from a village that had decided her grandmother's blood was reason enough to burn her out. Bramble had found her crouched in the shadow of his shop, picking through his refuse for something she could sell or trade or eat. He had not asked her name. He had not asked where she came from. He had simply held out a hand, and when she took it, he had led her inside and given her a bowl of soup and a bed for the night, and then, when she had stayed, and stayed, and stayed—for three years he had given her a home without ever asking her to stay.

She had left without saying goodbye. That was the kind of girl she had been. The kind who took what was given and ran before it could be taken back.

The shop still stood, though standing was a generous word for it.

The fire had gutted the upper floors entirely. The blackened timbers jutted toward the grey sky like broken fingers, and the lower windows gaped empty, stuffed with rags and ash. The sign—the one that had read *Bramble's Curiosities & Hedge-Medicines* in gold paint that Bramble had applied himself, very carefully, over the course of an afternoon—had burned too, leaving only a charred loop of iron that hung from one remaining hinge, creaking in the wind.

Ansel stood across the street from the ruin for a long time.

She had imagined—she did not know what she had imagined. That he might still be here. That the book might have been wrong, or the dates wrong, or her own fear wrong. That she might walk through his door and find him bent over his workbench, grinding something smelly and green, and that he might look up and see her and say, *Well, girl, took you long enough*, and that everything since might be revealed as some long, confused dream.

But the door was gone. The windows were gone. The shop was a mouth full of black teeth, and no one was coming home.

"Ma'am." A voice behind her, polite and firm. "I'm going to have to ask you to move along. This area's restricted."

The Order agent was young, perhaps her own age, with the pale green cloak and the silver badge that marked him as a Quiet Bloom tracker. He held his hands open at his sides, the posture of someone trained to appear non-threatening, and his eyes swept over her—her worn coat, her scarred hands, the way she stood too carefully, too quietly, like prey that knew it was being watched.

"I knew him," Ansel said. Her voice didn't shake. She had learned, in three years of running, how to make her voice not shake. "The merchant. Bramble. He was my teacher."

The young agent's expression flickered. Something complicated moved behind his eyes—pity, maybe, or the careful blankness of someone who had been taught not to feel. "I'm sorry for your loss. But the area's still restricted. There was... an incident. Involving forbidden texts. The Order has an ongoing investigation."

"Forbidden texts." The words tasted like copper in her mouth. "What kind of texts?"

"That's not public information." He gestured toward the street behind her. "Please, ma'am. I don't want to have to ask you twice."

Ansel went.

She went because fighting would have been stupid, and because there were three more agents visible at the far end of the street, and because she was very good at running when she needed to. She went because Bramble was dead and the Order had killed him and there was nothing left here but ash and questions and the memory of a man who had never once asked her to stay.

She went, and she did not cry, because she had learned that too.

*

The crew had taken a room at a inn called the Drowned Anchor, set back from the main streets where the Order's eyes couldn't reach. Marisol was still weak from the ambush, sleeping in the narrow bed by the window, and Pip sat beside her with a knife in his hand, sharpening it in slow, absent strokes. Hesk was somewhere below, getting them food and information. Odell was speaking in low tones with the innkeeper, a woman with grey hair and knowing eyes who nodded at whatever he said and then slid a key across the counter.

"Room's in the back," Odell said when he found Ansel. "Away from the street. Safer."

"Thank you." The words felt strange in her mouth. She had not thanked anyone for anything in a long time. "For all of this. I don't—I don't know how to—"

"You don't have to." He took her arm, not roughly, and steered her toward the stairs. "You don't have to know anything right now. You just have to survive long enough to figure it out."

The room was small and smelled of mildew and old salt. There was a bed, a chair, a washstand with a cracked mirror, and not much else. Ansel sat on the edge of the mattress and pressed her palms flat against her thighs and breathed, slowly, in and out, the way Bramble had taught her when the panic rose.

*You're alive*, she told herself. *He died for you to be alive. Don't waste it.*

She did not know how long she sat there before she noticed the books.

They were stacked in the corner, old volumes left behind by previous tenants or perhaps placed there deliberately, a cache waiting for someone who might need them. Most were water-damaged, their spines curled and pages stuck together, unreadable. But one sat apart from the others, smaller, bound in green leather that had faded to the color of a bruise.

Ansel's hands found it without her permission.

The title was embossed in faded gold: *The Smuggler's Almanac, Vol. XII*. Hollowfen's tides, shipping routes, harbor codes. She had seen copies of this book a dozen times; it was common enough among river-runners, a tool for those who moved goods they didn't want counted.

She opened it, and her breath stopped.

The pages had been cut away. Not torn—cut, carefully, with a blade thin enough to preserve the surrounding text. A hollow space had been carved into the center of the book, big enough to hold a folded sheaf of papers, and the papers were there, yellowed and soft with age, covered in handwriting she knew.

Bramble's handwriting. The same cramped, slanting script he had used to label his herb jars, to inventory his stock, to scrawl notes in the margins of books he lent her.

*My dearest Ansel*, the first line began.

*If you're reading this, then I'm gone and you're still running, and I'm sorry for both of those things. I'm sorry I couldn't be there to tell you what you needed to know before the running started. But I suppose that's the trouble with prophecies—they don't wait for us to be ready.*

She read the letter three times before she understood it.

He had known. Not just known about her grandmother, not just known about the prophecy—but known about *her*, specifically, from the moment she had shown up starving in his alley. Known what she was. Known what she would become. Known that the Order would come for him, eventually, when they realized how much he had seen.

*I found the book in a place I shouldn't have been looking*, he had written. *Your grandmother's work, every page of it. She was the Thirteenth Key a hundred years ago—the one who sealed the war-god Vaskar beneath Thornfell. She wrote the prophecy that named her, and then she erased herself from the world so thoroughly that it took the Order a century to find the trail. But she left breadcrumbs. She always left breadcrumbs for the people she loved.*

*I think she knew, in the end, what the prophecy would cost. Not just her life—her memory, her name, everything that made her who she was. She saw two endings and she chose the one that kept the world safe, even if it meant losing herself entirely. I don't know if she was right. I don't know if there's another way. But I know that you, girl, are the one who comes next, and I know that the Order will kill to keep you from reaching that garden.*

*They killed me yesterday, or they will kill me soon, by the time you're reading this. I can feel them watching the shop. I can feel the net closing. But I had three years with you, and that was enough. That was more than enough. You were the best thing I ever sheltered, even if you never stayed long enough to believe it.*

*Go to Nettlebed. Find the cottage with the blue door. She left something there for whoever came next—something the Order doesn't know about, something they couldn't find. I don't know what it is. But she was a clever woman, your grandmother, and she knew that the girl who came after her would need more than two choices.*

*I'm sorry I couldn't teach you how to stay. I'm sorry I couldn't be the kind of teacher who gives you answers instead of questions. But I was always proud of you, Ansel. Even when you ran. Especially when you ran. Because running meant you were still alive, and being alive meant there was still a chance.*

*Don't let them make you small. Don't let them make you silent. You were born to open doors, girl. Even the ones that shouldn't be opened.*

*With everything I have,*
*Bramble*

The letter ended there. No signature, no date, no closing benediction. Just the words, and the empty space where more pages should have been, and the smell of old paper and old grief and the particular silence of a room where someone should have been breathing but wasn't.

Ansel set the letter down on the bed beside her.

She did not cry. That was what she told herself—that she was past crying, that she had used up all her tears somewhere on the road, that the girl who wept had been left behind in another village, another fire, another name she no longer answered to.

But her hands were shaking again, and her chest hurt in a way that had nothing to do with the cold, and when she pressed her palms flat against her thighs to stop them, she found that she was gripping the fabric hard enough to leave marks.

He had known. All along, he had known.

He had known what she was, and he had sheltered her anyway. He had known the Order would come, and he had kept her secret anyway. He had known he would die, and he had written her this letter, this careful cache of information hidden in a book he knew she would never read because he knew her too well to believe she would stay.

*I'm sorry I couldn't teach you how to stay.*

She had stayed with him for three years. Three years was longer than she had stayed anywhere else, longer than she had ever trusted anyone, longer than she had ever let herself believe that she might belong somewhere. And she had left without a word, without a note, without even a goodbye, because that was who she was. That was who she had made herself into.

A girl who ran.

A girl who erased herself so completely that even the people who loved her had to hide their love in hollowed-out books, knowing she would never find it unless she came back on her own.

But she had come back. Too late, too empty-handed, too late to save him—but she had come back. And he had known she would. He had counted on it. He had built this trap of information and hope, this breadcrumb trail leading to Nettlebed, to the cottage with the blue door, to whatever Wren had left for the girl who came next.

*I don't know if there's another way.*

He had written that. He had admitted, in his careful hedge-merchant's script, that the two endings in the prophecy might not be the only ones. That there might be a third path he couldn't see, a door that hadn't been opened yet.

And he had died believing she might find it.

Ansel picked up the letter again. She folded it, carefully, along the creases that Bramble had made, and she tucked it into the inside pocket of her coat, next to the prophecy book, next to the words of a grandmother she had never met.

*Don't let them make you small. Don't let them make you silent.*

She stood up.

Her legs were steadier than she expected. Her hands had stopped shaking. Something was different in her chest—not softer, not healed, but harder, the way a blade is harder than the ore it was forged from. She had been running for three years. She had made herself small and silent and invisible, a ghost with scarred hands and no name, and she had told herself it was survival, told herself it was safety, told herself that the only way to stay alive was to become no one at all.

But Bramble had died for her to be alive. Her grandmother had erased herself to keep the world safe. The crew had hidden her, trusted her, offered her a place by their fire even though she had given them nothing, not even her real name.

Running was no longer neutral. Running was a choice—and she had been making the wrong one.

She went to the window and looked out over the grey rooftops of Hollowfen, at the smoke rising from a hundred chimneys, at the river gleaming in the distance like a promise she had broken a hundred times.

Nettlebed. A village she had never heard of, a cottage with a blue door, a grandmother's ghost waiting to hand her a choice she never asked for.

She was going to find it.

She was going to find it, and she was going to read whatever Wren had left, and she was going to find that third path if it existed, because Bramble had died believing it did, and she would not—she *would not*—let his faith be wasted.

"Psst." Pip's voice came from the doorway. "You still awake?"

She turned. He was leaning against the frame, knife still in hand, his face unreadable in the darkness. "I'm awake."

"Hesk says the Order's moving. Something big. They're pulling agents from the river posts, sending them toward the northern passes." He paused. "That's where Nettlebed is, according to the maps. Whatever you're planning, you might want to plan faster."

"They're going to Nettlebed."

"Looks like."

Ansel looked at the letter in her pocket. At the prophecy book against her chest. At the grey sky beyond the window, where the clouds were building toward another storm.

"Then we leave tonight," she said. "Before they seal the roads."

Pip's eyebrows rose. "We?"

The word hung in the air between them, strange and heavy. She had never said *we*. She had never included anyone in her plans, never let anyone share her road, never trusted that the people she walked with would still be walking when the sun came up.

But Bramble had sheltered her when she was no one. The crew had hidden her when she had nothing to offer. And she was tired—bone-tired, soul-tired, tired of running alone and calling it strength.

"You came back for me," she said. "At the ford. You didn't have to."

"No," Pip agreed. "We didn't."

"So. We."

He was quiet for a moment. Then, slowly, a smile cracked across his face—not mocking, not guarded, but something closer to wonder. "Yeah," he said. "Okay. We."

He vanished back into the hallway, and Ansel heard him calling down to the others, his voice carrying through the thin walls like a bell, and somewhere in the inn a chair scraped, and a door opened, and the sound of people preparing to move filled the silence like a heartbeat.

She turned back to the window. The clouds were thick, the wind rising, the first drops of rain beginning to fall on the ruined rooftops of Hollowfen.

*Don't let them make you small.*

She would not. She could not. Not anymore.

The prophecy had given her two choices: seal the prison and lose everyone she loved, or refuse and watch the world burn. Her grandmother had faced those same two walls, and she had chosen the first, and she had erased herself rather than live with what it cost.

But Bramble had written the words she needed to hear.

*I don't know if there's another way.*

He didn't know. He couldn't know. But he had left her the question, and the question was enough.

There was another way. There had to be.

And she was going to find it.

*

Serah Ivo stood at the window of the Order's Hollowfen outpost, watching the rain fall on a city she had once called home. The informant's report was still clutched in her hand: a girl with scarred hands, traveling with smugglers, carrying a book that made compasses lie.

She had let Ansel Amberdale escape once. She had filed a false report, risked her commission, committed treason for a stranger who hadn't even thanked her.

She would not make that mistake again.

But not for the reasons her superiors believed.

"The trail leads north," said the agent behind her. "To Nettlebed."

"I know." Serah folded the report and tucked it into her cloak. "Assemble a tracking team. No more than four. I want to reach the village before she does."

"And if we find her?"

Serah did not answer. Outside, the rain drummed against the glass, and somewhere in the distance, lightning flickered over the mountains where the Verdant Garden lay in ruins, waiting.

She had questions now. Questions the Order did not want answered. Questions about the prophecy, about the grandmother who had erased herself, about the thirteenth page that someone had torn from the book and hidden for a hundred years.

Ansel Amberdale might be the end of the world.

Or she might be the only one who could save it.

Serah intended to find out which.
