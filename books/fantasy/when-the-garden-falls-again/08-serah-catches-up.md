## 8. Serah Catches Up

The cliff road above Greywater was the kind of road that made Serah Ivo question her faith, and she had been trained never to do that.

It clung to the mountain's shoulder like a scar, barely wide enough for a cart, with a drop on her right hand that went down and down into fog until the sea below was less a thing seen than a sound—a long grey breathing against stone. On her left rose the cliff itself, black rock veined with white quartz, furred with gorse that the wind combed flat. The wind came off the water in long shoves, smelling of salt and iron, and it had been pushing at her back for three hours as if the mountain itself wanted her off this path.

She did not take its advice. She had taken no one's advice in eleven years.

Serah walked the way she had been taught to walk: heel first, weight low, eyes moving in the rhythm the Order of the Quiet Bloom drilled into every knight from their first day in white—*left, ahead, right, ahead, down, ahead*. She counted her steps without meaning to. She always counted. Four hundred and eleven steps since the last bend, four hundred and twelve, and on four hundred and thirteen the road curved around a spur of rock and she saw, forty paces ahead, a girl sitting on the edge of the world with her boots dangling over the fog.

Ansel Amberdale—if that was even the name; Bramble had died before confirming it—did not startle. That was the first thing Serah noticed, and the thing that would trouble her longest afterward. Most people, hearing footsteps behind them on an empty cliff road, flinched. This girl simply turned her head, looked back with eyes the flat grey of storm-water, and turned forward again, as though Serah were weather. As though hunters came by every day and sat down beside her.

"You walk loudly," Ansel said. "For someone who's supposed to be quiet."

"We are the Quiet Bloom," Serah said. "Not the Silent Bloom. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"The flower is quiet," Serah said. "The gardeners are not." She stopped ten paces away—not too close, not so far as to seem afraid—and let her hands hang loose at her sides, well clear of the sword at her hip. Everything about her posture said *I am not here to fight*, which was true, which was also the lie she had been ordered to tell.

The girl didn't look at her again. She was doing something with her hands—turning a strip of dried meat over, tearing it with her teeth, chewing with the unhurried patience of someone who had eaten in worse places. Her hands were scarred across the knuckles and palms, rope-burned and knife-nicked, the hands of someone who worked for a living, and Serah found her gaze snagging there, on those hands, because the sketches in the stolen book had shown those same hands, those same scars, rendered in ink a century old.

"The fog will thicken before dusk," Serah said. "You don't want to be on this stretch when it does. There's a false ledge half a mile on. Three people have gone over it that I know of, and I only know because we pulled two of them up."

"I know about the ledge." Ansel tore another strip of meat. "I've walked this road six times."

"Smuggling moon-ore through the passes."

That got her a look—quick, assessing, gone again. "You've been reading my file."

"There is no file. There's a book." Serah took one step closer, then another, and when the girl still didn't tense, she sat down on the rock beside her, three feet apart, both of them facing the fog and the breathing sea. It was not how a hunter cornered prey. It was, Serah realized with a strange lurch, how two travelers rested. "I was sent to retrieve it. And you."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the orders change."

Ansel chewed. Swallowed. Below them the fog moved like something alive, rolling in slow banks against the cliff face, and somewhere under it a bell buoy tolled, patient and idiotic, marking a channel no ship could see.

"Let me guess," the girl said at last. "The orders are very sad about it. The Order takes no pleasure in violence. The Bloom must remain quiet. Someone writes it all down in a nice book afterward with nice margins."

Something hot flickered in Serah's chest—indignation, or maybe recognition. "You think we enjoy this?"

"I think you *organize* it." Ansel finally turned to face her fully, and Serah saw what the fog-light did to her face: made her young, younger than Serah had expected, younger than the wanted-notices implied, a girl who had spent three years trying to be no one and had gotten alarmingly good at it. "There's a difference between enjoying a thing and building a whole religion around making sure it happens tidily. Enjoyment I could forgive. Organization frightens me."

"I'm not here to frighten you."

"No," Ansel said. "You're here to kill me if I can't be persuaded. That's what 'persuaded' means where you come from, isn't it? I met a man in Hollowfen who used the same word. He persuaded a debtor of his. They found what was left of him in the river."

Serah said nothing. The wind filled the silence, shoving at their backs, and far below the buoy tolled again.

Because here was the truth she carried up this mountain like a stone in her boot: she had read her orders four times on the ride north, and each time the word *persuaded* had sat differently on the page. The first time, it meant *convince her to surrender the book and come willingly*. By the fourth reading, alone in a cold inn room with the seal of the High Bloom pressed into red wax at the bottom, it had come to mean something else entirely—the way a word can rot overnight, quietly, from the inside. Knight-Captain Vess, her superior, had smiled when he gave her the sealed addendum. Vess had a smile like a well-kept blade, and he had said, *"If persuasion fails, Captain, remember that the Key unchosen is more dangerous than the Key dead."* And Serah had bowed, because that is what knights do, and had ridden out at dawn with a sentence in her ears that she could not stop turning over, the way you turn over a coin and find the face worn smooth.

*"Unchosen."* As if the choice were not the entire point. As if a prophecy that named her a key—one who would *open or close*—could be fulfilled by removing the hand that turns it. Unless. Unless the Order did not want the door closed. Unless the Order feared the closing more than the opening.

Unless, said a small cold voice in Serah that sounded uncomfortably like honesty, your superiors are hiding what they actually want, and they sent you because you have never once asked.

"I'm going to offer you something," Serah said. "Before the fog comes in. Before my patrol finds us—they're a mile back at the shrine, and they'll sweep this road at dusk whether I report success or failure. So listen."

"I'm listening."

"Give me the book. Come south with me, voluntarily. You'll be treated fairly. You'll be given quarters, food, time—" *time,* she almost said, *time to be persuaded further,* and hated herself for the smoothness of it. "No one will touch you."

"And if I refuse?"

Serah looked out at the fog. "Then I'm instructed to ensure you never make the choice at all."

Silence. Then Ansel laughed—a short, hard, disbelieving sound with nothing in it of humor. "You hear yourself? You're offering me the choice to give up choosing. That's not persuasion. That's just killing with paperwork."

"I didn't write the paperwork."

"No. But you're carrying it." The girl stood, brushing grit from her trousers, and Serah rose too, hands open, and for a moment they faced each other on the narrow road with the whole grey world falling away behind them, and Serah thought, absurdly, *she has her grandmother's chin.* She had never seen Wren Amberdale; no portrait existed; the Order had erased the woman so thoroughly that even her grave was a rumor. But the books described her. Stubborn chin. Storm-grey eyes. A hedge-witch who looked at gods and did not blink. And standing here now, three feet away, was the same look on a younger face, and it made Serah feel like she was meeting someone twice.

"Why did you steal it?" Serah asked suddenly. Not the scripted question. Her own.

Ansel's jaw tightened. "Because it has my death date in it."

"It has your *purpose* in it—"

"It has my death date," Ansel repeated, louder, and the wind took her voice and shredded it seaward. "Written a hundred years ago, by someone who knew my face before my mother was born, down to the scars. Do you know what that does to a person? Every bad night I've ever had, every road I've run, some old woman sat in a chair a century ago and wrote it all down and called it holy. I stole it because I wanted to burn it. I couldn't. I read it instead, and it got worse, and now I'd like everyone to leave me alone so I can decide what to do about it without an armed church following me around the coastline."

"The book is not yours to decide about."

"Neither is my life," Ansel snapped, "but here we are."

And Serah—Ivo, Knight-Captain of the Quiet Bloom, eleven years of perfect service, who had never filed a late report, never questioned an order aloud, never once in her disciplined life stepped off the marked path—heard herself say:

"What line?"

Ansel blinked. "What?"

"You said the book got worse. What did it say?" Serah stepped closer, and her voice dropped, urgent, stripped of rank. "The Order's copy is incomplete. Certain pages were removed before binding—removed by the First Circle, centuries ago, and re-copied only in fragments that circulate among the High Bloom alone. I have served eleven years and I have seen perhaps a third of the full text. If you have the original—" She stopped herself. Started again, quieter. "What line made it worse?"

For a long moment the girl just studied her, and Serah watched suspicion war with something else in her face—the loneliness, maybe, of carrying a terrible book with no one to read it to. Then Ansel reached into her coat, slowly, telegraphing every movement, and drew out a slim volume bound in green leather gone brown with age, its spine cracked, its pages furred at the edges like something grown rather than made. She held it against her chest but opened it, thumbed to a ribbon-marker, and read aloud into the wind:

"'On the thirteenth moon-turn the Thirteenth Key shall stand before the sleeping god, and hers is the only hand that opens or closes. Neither army, neither faith, neither blood nor blade may choose in her stead. Whosoever takes the choice from the Key breaks the seal entire, and the god wakes unopposed.'"

The wind dropped. Just for a moment, as though the sea itself had leaned in to listen.

Serah stood very still.

She knew the Order's version. Every knight knew the Order's version, recited at investiture, stitched into banners: *the Key shall open or close, and the Bloom shall guide her hand.* Eleven years she had believed *guide* meant counsel. Instruction. The gentle pressure of wisdom on a frightened girl's shoulder.

But the line Ansel had just read said something else. Said it plainly, in words old enough to predate the Order's own founding: *whosoever takes the choice from the Key breaks the seal entire.*

Not guides. Not guards. Anyone who interferes—any knight, any captain, any well-meaning order with tidy paperwork—doesn't protect the world. They end it.

"That's not in our text," Serah said. Her voice came out strange, thin.

"No," Ansel agreed. "I wondered why."

*Because it forbids everything we were sent to do.* Because Vess's sealed addendum, the *unchosen is more dangerous than the dead*—because if the Order killed her, if anyone killed her, if anyone *forced* her—

"Who else knows this?" Serah demanded. "Does the First Circle know? Does the High Bloom—"

"You tell me," Ansel said. "You're the one with the tidy organization. I'm just the girl with the book." She closed it, hugged it to her chest, and took a step backward along the road, toward the fog. "Here's what I figure, knight. Either your masters don't know this line, in which case they're blind. Or they know it and cut it out anyway, in which case they're not blind—they're *busy*. And either way, you should ask yourself why they sent you after me with orders to remove my choice from the world, when your own missing page says that's exactly how you wake the god."

She took another step back. The fog was coming in now, thick and fast, swallowing the road behind her in soft grey rags.

"They gave me a month," Serah heard herself say. "Vess gave me a month to bring you in. After that, they send the others. The ones who don't sit down and talk."

"Then you'd better decide whose side the paperwork's on," Ansel said, "before they send them." And she stepped backward off the road—onto the false ledge, the killer ledge Serah had warned her about—but she stepped onto it like she owned it, sideways, confident, a smuggler's walk, and the fog folded around her and took her the way the sea takes a dropped coin, and she was gone.

Serah stood alone on the cliff road for a long time.

The bell buoy tolled. The wind resumed. Somewhere behind her, a mile back at the shrine, her patrol was lighting cook-fires and waiting for the captain's report.

She took out her journal—good vellum, official issue, the Quiet Bloom's sigil embossed on the cover—and uncapped her pen, and sat down on the cold rock where the girl had been sitting, and began to write.

*Day nine of pursuit. Contact made with the subject on the coast road above Greywater. Subject declined surrender. Subject is resourceful, mobile, and familiar with the terrain beyond my prior estimates. Lost her in fog near the false ledge. Recommend widening the search eastward, toward the Thornfell high passes.*

All of it true. Every word defensible. And every word arranged, like furniture in a room, to point the searching eyes east while the girl ran west, or south, or wherever she was going.

Serah looked at what she had written for a long moment. Then she added one more line, small, in the margin, in letters so faint they might have been an accident of the pen:

*I have read the forbidden page. Ask me why the First Circle removed it.*

She stared at that line until her eyes burned. Then she scraped it out with her knife, carefully, thoroughly, until the vellum was blank there again, and blew away the little curls of paper, and watched them go over the cliff into the fog.

*Treason,* she thought. The word arrived without drama, the way most enormous things do. *That was treason. You committed treason on a cliff in the fog, and it felt like writing a letter home.*

Eleven years of perfect service. One afternoon to undo it. And the worst part—the part she would carry up many mountains after this—was that it had not felt like falling. It had felt like setting down something heavy she had forgotten she was holding.

She capped her pen. Stood. Turned back toward the shrine, toward the cook-fires, toward Vess's smile and the sealed addendum burning in her breast pocket like a coal.

Behind her, far below in the fog, the bell buoy tolled once more—patient, idiotic, faithful—marking a channel no one could see, for ships that hadn't yet come.

* * *

Knight-Captain Vess met her at the shrine with two men-at-arms and the expression of a man doing arithmetic.

"Well?" he said.

"She went east," Serah said. "Toward the high passes. She's good—better than the reports suggested. I lost her in the fog at the false ledge."

Vess studied her for a moment longer than was comfortable. His smile, when it came, was the well-kept-blade smile she knew so well. "A month, Captain. Don't waste it."

"I won't," Serah said, and bowed, and felt the false report settle into place inside her like a swallowed stone—cold, smooth, and permanent—and already, somewhere beneath the fog below, a girl with a stolen book was running west with a head start that Serah Ivo had just given her, and the first treason of her life fit her hand like a sword she hadn't known she'd been carrying.

She turned toward the fire, and began planning how to lose them again tomorrow.
