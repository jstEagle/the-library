## 10. Hollowfen Burns

The safehouse in Hollowfen had always been a place of quiet corners and half-remembered rhythms—the kind of place where Pip could almost convince herself that a person could disappear and no one would notice. But tonight, the walls felt too thin, the air too close, and the prophecy book tucked beneath Pip’s shirt felt less like a gift and more like a fuse.

She was Marisol’s twin, but if you asked her which of them was the more careful liar, she’d point to herself without hesitation. Marisol saw through people like glass; Pip was the one who learned to bend light until it became invisible. They’d grown up in the spaces between cities, in the smuggler’s clefts where the law looked the other way and the sky was a promise rather than a boundary. Now, with the Order’s gray cloaks already staining the horizon, invisibility felt less like a skill and more like a death sentence.

The first sign came as a faint vibration through the floorboards, a sound too low for the waking ears but familiar to Pip’s bones—the soft, rhythmic thud of booted steps on packed earth outside. She was already awake, the book pressed to her chest, listening to Marisol’s steady breath from the pallet across the room. Marisol shifted, a sigh escaping her, and Pip’s hand found the edge of the book, ready.

Then the door shattered.

Not knocked down, but splintered inward with a violence that sent splinters raining like dead insects onto the straw matting. Fire followed the breach, orange and ravenous, licking through the gap as if the night itself had been waiting for kindling. Pip’s breath hitched, and she shoved the book beneath Marisol’s pallet, toward the back wall where a loose floorboard hid a small hollow she’d dug weeks ago, just in case.

“Move!” a voice barked, harsh and breathless.

Pip didn’t need to be told twice. She scrambled toward Marisol, grabbing her twin’s shoulder, shaking her gently. “Marisol, wake up. They’re here.”

Marisol’s eyes cracked open, glassy and slow-to-focus. “Pip? What—”

“Get up. Now.” Pip pulled Marisol to her feet, but the floor tilted beneath them. A beam from the ceiling, already weakened by the fire’s hunger, groaned and gave way. A chunk of burning timber crashed down where Marisol had been standing, sending up a flare of sparks and a wave of heat that knocked the breath from Pip’s lungs.

“This way!” Pip shouted, dragging Marisol toward the rear wall. The air was thick with smoke, acrid and stinging, and she could feel the heat searing the hair at her temples. Behind them, the room was already filling with flame, the furniture catching like kindling, the shadows dancing to the tune of the Order’s torches.

They reached the hidden compartment. Pip shoved Marisol inside, then followed, pulling the floorboard back into place just as a boot kicked through the wall mere inches from her head. The compartment was small, cramped, smelling of damp earth and the faint sweetness of the moon-ore Ansel had smuggled through Thornfell passes. But it was dark. It was hidden.

“Stay quiet,” Pip whispered, pressing a finger to her own lips. “Stay down.”

She didn’t wait. She backed out into the corridor, her eyes adjusting to the dancing flames, her heart hammering a rhythm that felt synchronised with the crackle of the fire. The safehouse was a labyrinth of rooms she and Ansel had carved from the city’s forgotten underbelly, but the Order knew it too. She saw gray-cloaked figures moving with purpose, their faces obscured by cowls, their hands holding torches and crossbows that clicked with lethal intent.

“Key’s in here!” one of them shouted, voice distorted by the smoke. “Burn it out!”

A torch was thrown. It arced through the air and landed squarely on the floorboards Pip had just crossed. The fire spread instantly, consuming the wood, licking up the walls. Pip screamed, a raw, animal sound, and threw herself toward the back exit, the one Ansel had carved into the cliffside, a tunnel that wound through the smuggler’s veins like a root through stone.

But the Order was already there, blocking the passage. Two of them stood at the mouth of the tunnel, crossbows drawn, their faces set in grim determination. Pip skidded to a halt, the heat at her back, the fire roaring behind her like a beast awakened.

“Don’t shoot the merchandise,” another voice called from somewhere deeper in the house. “We want the book intact.”

“Alive too, if possible,” the first replied. “The Key’s a slippery customer.”

Pip’s mind raced. She had seconds. She saw a wooden crate near the tunnel entrance, filled with discarded smuggler’s gear—ropes, lanterns, a collapsed satchel. She grabbed a lantern, its glass still intact, and hurled it toward the Order agents. The glass shattered, oil splashed, and the flame caught the dry rope and gear, creating a sudden wall of fire that forced the agents back a step, coughing, shielding their faces.

“Go!” Pip yelled, not knowing if Marisol was still inside, if the crew was anywhere nearby. She turned and ran, not toward the tunnel, but toward the only other exit she knew: the underground river passage Ansel had spoken of in hushed tones, a route used for moving moon-ore past the Thornfell patrols.

The corridor twisted, narrow and damp, the air cooler here but thick with the scent of wet stone and distant flame. Pip’s lungs burned, her sides aching with each ragged breath. She could hear the Order behind her, booted steps echoing, voices muttering about burning the place down and searching the tunnels. She had to keep moving, had to put distance between Marisol and the fire, between the book and the hands that wanted it dead.

She emerged into a small cavern where the underground river gurgled darkly, its surface reflecting the orange glow of the fire above. A boat was tied to a rusted ring—Ansel’s doing, always prepared. Pip climbed in, shoving off with a pole she found nearby, and began to pole the boat downstream, away from the safehouse, away from the Order, toward the smuggler’s network she’d helped map.

Behind her, the safehouse continued to burn, a pyre of wood and memory. She thought she heard a cry, a name called through the roar of flame. *Marisol.* The sound tore at something in Pip’s chest, a raw place she hadn’t known existed until now.

The river carried her forward, away from the fire, away from the Order’s torches, toward the smuggler’s tunnels Ansel had whispered about in the dark, when she thought no one was listening. Pip paddled with grim determination, her arms screaming, the book hidden beneath her shirt, the weight of it a promise she intended to keep.

Hours passed—or maybe it was minutes; time had lost meaning in the roar and the smoke. The river widened, the walls of the tunnel giving way to a larger cavern, one she recognized from Ansel’s maps. This was the heart of the smuggler’s network, a web of hidden passages that wound beneath Hollowfen like veins beneath skin. She guided the boat to a narrow mouth that led upward, into a tunnel dry enough to walk, and pulled herself out onto cold, gritty stone.

She was alone now, the crew scattered, the safehouse a memory of flame. She wiped soot from her face, her hands trembling not just from cold but from the aftermath of what she’d just lived through. She thought of Marisol, of the fire, of the Order’s boots echoing behind her. She thought of the book, the prophecy, the life it represented and the death it courted.

Then she heard it—a cough, weak and ragged, from the shadows of the tunnel mouth.

“Pip?”

The voice was thin, terrified, but unmistakably Marisol’s. Pip lunged forward, parting the darkness, and found her twin sitting on the stone, one arm wrapped around herself, the other stained with smoke and something darker. Marisol’s face was pale, her eyes wide and wet, but she was alive. She had a burn on her shoulder, a deep gash on her side, and she was clutching something to her chest.

“Marisol, thank the—” Pip fell to her knees beside her twin, pulling the injured arm away to assess the damage. “What happened? Why are you—”

“They came,” Marisol whispered, her voice breaking. “I tried to get you out. I tried to get the book. I—” She coughed, a wet, racking sound that sent a spray of spittle onto the stone. “I pushed you. I pushed you toward the tunnel, and then the beam fell, and I—I couldn’t move it. I couldn’t—”

“Shh.” Pip pulled Marisol into a hug, careful of the burns, and held her close. “You didn’t let me down. You saved me. You always save me.”

“No.” Marisol’s grip on Pip’s shirt tightened, knuckles white. “I failed. I let the fire take the safehouse. I let—”

“Stop.” Pip pulled back just enough to look Marisol in the eye. “You protected me. That’s not failure. That’s the only thing you’ve ever been good at.”

The words hung between them, heavy and true. Pip felt a strange mixture flood her chest—guilt that she had been the one to escape, that Marisol had borne the brunt of the Order’s wrath, and yet a terrifying warmth blooming in the space where fear had lived. Marisol had died for her, or at least given herself so that Pip could live. The thought should have crushed Pip with the weight of survivor’s guilt, but instead it unspooled something softer, something that felt almost like love, though Pip had never allowed herself to name it as such. She and Marisol had survived by keeping each other at arm's length, by being invisible together. But now, with the flame licking at their heels and the Order at their throats, the invisibility had cracked, and something real had stepped through.

“We have to go,” Pip said, helping Marisol to her feet. “The crew. They’ll—”

“Where?” Marisol’s voice was small, exhausted. “The safehouse is ash. The Order is everywhere.”

“Ansel’s tunnels.” Pip pulled Marisol’s arm over her shoulder, supporting her weight. “She knows every root and river. We’ll find the crew. We’ll—”

“Pip.” Marisol stopped them, turning to face her twin. “I’m hurt. I can’t—”

“Then I’ll carry you.” Pip’s jaw set, stubborn and fierce. “That’s what twins do. That’s what we do.”

They began to move, Pip half-carrying, half-guiding Marisol through the smuggler’s tunnels. The passageways were narrow, damp, and winding, but Pip knew them by heart—every turn, every hidden alcove, every place where the stone was thin enough to break through into another’s territory. She moved with the certainty of someone who had spent a lifetime mapping the spaces between people, and now she was using that map to keep her sister alive.

Above them, the fire continued to burn, a beacon against the night. Behind them, the Order searched the safehouse ruins, their torches swinging like angry fireflies. Pip didn't allow herself to think about what came next. She focused on the rhythm of her own breath, the weight of Marisol against her, the distant memory of Ansel’s voice, the smuggler’s promise.

They emerged from the tunnel mouth into a small, hidden cove where the river met the sea, the tide coming in slow and steady. A smuggler’s skiff was pulled up on the shore, its canvas cover half-torn, but intact. Beside it stood Odell, the crew’s gruff captain, his arms crossed, his expression a mask of annoyance that cracked the moment he saw Pip dragging Marisol toward him.

“You’re late,” Odell grunted, but his eyes took in the soot, the injury, the fear. “And you’re bringing company.”

“Order burned the safehouse,” Pip said, breathless. “Marisol took the brunt of it. We need to move. Now.”

Odell didn’t hesitate. He motioned for the rest of the crew—Pip had only glimpsed them in passing, but now Hesk’s massive form emerged from the shadows, Pip and Marisol’s twinship making sense of the easy camaraderie, and Pip felt a surge of relief that the family she’d kept at arm's length had already become the only thing keeping her alive. Pip helped Marisol into the skiff, settling her down, covering her with a blanket Odell produced from somewhere dry.

“Where’s Ansel?” Odell asked, pushing the skiff into the water.

“She’s… she’s ahead of us. She knows the routes.” Pip climbed into the boat, taking the oars. “We need to get her back here. She needs to see Marisol.”

The skiff cut through the water, the paddles dipping in unison, the night sky above a vast indifferent canvas. Pip rowed with a strength she didn’t know she possessed, driven by the twin instincts of guilt and devotion. Behind her, the safehouse burned on, a column of smoke rising like a prayer to a god she didn’t believe in, but to which she was now, unwillingly, appealing.

As they moved farther from the flames, Pip allowed herself a moment to look at Marisol’s face in the pale light of the stars. Her sister’s eyes were closed now, exhaustion claiming her, but her hand remained clenched around the prophecy book, pressed to her chest as if it were the only thing keeping her alive. Pip reached out, interlacing her fingers with Marisol’s, and felt the faint, steady pulse of life beneath the calluses and the smoke.

“You did good,” Pip whispered, more to herself than to Marisol. “You did good.”

The words hung between them, a bridge across the guilt and the warmth, across the fear that had defined so much of their lives. And for the first time in a long time, Pip allowed herself to believe that maybe—just maybe—being seen, being protected, being worth something, wasn’t the same as being lost.

The river carried them forward, toward Ansel, toward the crew, toward whatever came next. But Pip knew, with a certainty that grounded her in a way nothing else had, that the fire had changed something irrevocable. The Order would come for the book, for the Key, for the prophecy that named her the Thirteenth Key. And Marisol would bear the scars of tonight for the rest of her life, but she would also bear the knowledge that she had been seen, and loved, and protected.

The skiff rounded a bend in the river, and Pip saw the faint outline of Ansel’s hidden cove, the smuggler’s network she had built and guarded and kept at a distance, now the only thing standing between her and the darkness that chased them. She pulled the oars harder, cutting through the water, toward the warmth of the fire she had almost lost, and toward the crew who had become the only family she had ever allowed herself.
