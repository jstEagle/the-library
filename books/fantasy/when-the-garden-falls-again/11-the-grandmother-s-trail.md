## 11. The Grandmother's Trail

The road to Nettlebed did not appear on any map Ansel had ever stolen, bought, or memorized, which was precisely why she trusted it. Maps were made by people who wanted to be found. The cart track that peeled away from the river road three miles past the Hollowfen tollstones—narrow as a scar, hemmed in by hazel scrub that grew back over itself each autumn as if ashamed of the gap—was the kind of road that existed only for people who already knew to look for it.

She had known to look for it since the letter.

*Bramble's* letter. Three days on the road and she still caught herself thinking of it in his voice, still hearing the dry rasp of his amusement in the margins where he'd written things like *you always were too clever by half, girl, and half again after that.* The letter sat against her ribs now, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining of her coat where she'd stitched it herself by candlelight in the crew's borrowed loft, her hands steady and her stomach a knot of something she refused to name. Guilt was a luxury. Grief was a luxury. She had a destination, and destinations were the only currency that mattered to a woman with a death date.

*Nettlebed,* Bramble had written. *Where the hedge-witch was born, and where she went to un-be born. Ask for the keeper of the well. Don't ask for Wren. There's no one left who'll say the name.*

Ansel said the name anyway, under her breath, to the hazel scrub, because saying it felt like a small rebellion against a dead woman's silence. *Wren.* A bird's name. A small, quick, ordinary name—the kind of name you'd give a daughter you wanted to be overlooked. Ansel Amberdale, who had spent three years sanding her own name down to nothing, felt an uncomfortable kinship with a grandmother she had never met and had, until a week ago, never once thought to mourn.

The track climbed. Thornfell's shoulders rose ahead of her, blue-grey in the afternoon haze, and the air changed the way it always did near the old mountain—thinner, sweeter, with an undercurrent of something mineral and watchful, like the smell of a struck flint. She had run these passes a hundred times with moon-ore and contraband silk, had known every goat path and smuggler's stair, and the mountain had never once felt like this: like a lung holding its breath.

She made the village as the light was failing.

* * *

Nettlebed was less a village than a memory of one. A dozen cottages huddled around a green gone soft and tussocky, their thatches silvered with age, their windows glowing the particular amber of lamps trimmed low to save oil. A chapel with a moss-eaten roof. A well at the center of the green, ringed in stones so old they had slumped into the earth like tired men.

And no people. Or almost none. A figure crossed between two cottages and was gone. A dog regarded her from a doorstep with the flat, incurious stare of an animal that had learned not to bark at strangers because strangers never stayed.

Ansel stood at the edge of the green with her hood up and her hand on the knife she hadn't needed to draw since Hollowfen, and felt the particular loneliness of a place that had decided, a long time ago, to stop expecting anything.

*Ask for the keeper of the well.*

She crossed to the well. Up close she could see the stones were carved—faintly, weathered nearly to nothing—with the same motif repeated over and over: a flowering vine, thorned, wound in a tight spiral. She had seen that spiral before. She had seen it two nights ago, burned into the inside of her eyelids, on the cover of a stolen book.

She was still crouched over the carving when a voice behind her said, "You're the second one this year to look at that like they recognized it."

Ansel came up fast, blade half-drawn, and found herself facing an old woman with a walking stick and a face like a walnut left out in the weather—deep-lined, dark, and shrewd. She wore a man's coat over a dozen shawls and she did not look remotely afraid of the knife.

"Who was the first?" Ansel asked, because it seemed safer than *who are you.*

"Knight. Quiet Bloom." The old woman's mouth twisted. "Came in spring, all polite and terrible. Asked the same thing you're going to ask me. I told her nothing, on account of she was Bloom, and then she told *me* something, which was that if anyone else came asking, I was to report it to the chapterhouse at Greywater within the day." Her eyes, pale as shallow water, went over Ansel's hood, her boots, the knife. "You're not Bloom."

"No."

"You're not from here."

"No."

"But you're *hers*." The old woman said it simply, the way one says *it's going to rain.* "I can see it in the hands. She had the same scarring. Hedge-witch's hands. You get them from years of thorns and boiling bark." She turned and started toward the nearest cottage, walking stick tapping. "Well? Are you coming in, girl, or do you mean to stand on the green until the Bloom comes back for you? Because they do come back. That's the whole of what they're good at."

Ansel followed her. She told herself it was because the cottage was the only lit doorway in the village and the night was coming down cold. She told herself a great many things, those first few minutes, and believed almost none of them.

* * *

The old woman's name was Tansy Gorse, and she was, by her own accounting, the last person in Nettlebed who remembered the year the hedge-witch left—which was to say, the last person who remembered the year everyone agreed to forget her.

"Sit," Tansy said, and Ansel sat, in a chair by a fire that smelled of applewood, in a cottage so crowded with dried herbs hanging from the beams that it seemed the room itself had grown a coat of leaves. Tansy poured something dark and hot into a clay cup and pushed it across. "Nettle wine. Drink it. It tastes like punishment but it's good for the shaking you're doing and pretending you're not."

Ansel drank. It did taste like punishment. Her hands, she noticed, did not stop shaking.

"Start anywhere," Tansy said, settling into the chair opposite with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions. "But know that I'll know if you lie. I'm old, not stupid, and this village has had a hundred years of practice at knowing what not to say. I can hear the shape of a lie under a true story the way you'd hear a stone under snow."

Ansel set down the cup. She had rehearsed nothing, which was unlike her; she was a woman who rehearsed everything, who entered every room with three exits mapped. But she found she didn't want to manage this old woman. She found she wanted—God help her—to ask the questions she'd been carrying since the garden, the ones that rattled in her chest like loose coins.

"The book in the Verdant Garden," she said. "The prophecy. It was written by a hedge-witch named Wren. The Order wants it back. They want the girl it describes dead, or—Bramble's letter said the Order killed him for sheltering *her.* For sheltering someone connected to that book." She made herself keep going. "Wren was my grandmother. I never knew her. My mother never spoke of her. I didn't even know her name until four days ago. So start anywhere you like, mistress Gorse, because I don't even know what year to begin in."

Tansy Gorse looked at her for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the dog barked once, at nothing, and was silent.

"A hundred years ago," Tansy said, "give or take the years I've lost to the wine, this village had a witch. Not a grand one. Hedge-witch, like your letter says—bonesetting, birthing, the fever-cures, the little charms against blight. Wren Amberdale."

The name went through Ansel like cold water. *Amberdale.* Her name. The name the seal had taken from her at the fourth moon, the name the crew had sworn to hold for her, the name that had been hers before it was her mother's before it was—

"Amberdale," Ansel repeated. Her voice came out strange.

"Amberdale," Tansy confirmed. "And she was loved here, girl, that's the first thing you have to understand. Loved the way villages love a well—thought nothing of it, missed it terribly when it was gone. She delivered half the folk my age. She sat with the dying. When the fever came up from the fens the winter I was six, she worked three days without sleeping and I'm told she never lost one she didn't have to." Tansy's eyes had gone somewhere far away, back down a hundred years of a life that should not have been possible. "And then the war-god's prison, down under the old Garden, began to fail. And Wren Amberdale went down into it. And sealed it. And that's the second thing you have to understand."

Ansel's mouth was dry. "The book says the Key seals or shatters. It says the Key is—"

"You. Yes. I know what the book says; the Bloom knight recited enough of it at me to last a lifetime." Tansy leaned forward, firelight catching the deep lines of her face. "Here's what the book doesn't say, because Wren wrote the book and Wren was honest in it the way a map is honest—it shows you the road but not the walking. Sealing that prison costs. It doesn't cost blood. It costs *self.* Every payment, a piece of you. Wren went down with a whole life and came up with holes in it, and the holes were the size of a village. She sealed the god, and she stood on this green, and she looked at the faces of people she'd delivered and cured and loved, and she knew them the way you know a stranger in a market."

The fire cracked. Ansel realized she had stopped breathing and made herself start.

"She could have stayed," Tansy went on, quieter now. "We'd have kept her. Fed her, housed her, told her the stories of herself until some of them stuck. Some of us wanted to. My grandmother—she was one of the ones Wren pulled through the fever—she told me they tried. But Wren said—" and here Tansy's voice changed, took on a cadence worn smooth by a century of retelling, "—she said, *'A woman who cannot remember her debts will incur them again. Better I owe no one.'* And she went cottage to cottage, and she took herself out. Not her body. Her *record.* Every ledger she was written into, every christening roll, every marriage line, every memory the villagers would give up of her—and they gave them, girl, they gave them freely, because she asked, and because by then the seal was still hungry and she was still paying, and every memory of Wren that left this village went down into the hedge instead of out of the world."

Ansel's hands were flat on her knees. She watched them as though they belonged to someone else. Scarred hands. Hedge-witch's hands. The same scarring.

"She unmade herself," Ansel said. "On purpose. She paid the seal with her own place in the world."

"She paid until there was no Wren Amberdale left to pay," Tansy said. "And then she walked out of Nettlebed on a morning in autumn, and the village watched her go, and not one of us—we, the ones born after—could have told you who she was. My grandmother remembered, because she refused. Stubborn woman. She's the reason I know any of this. She kept one memory, hidden, the way you'd keep a seed against the winter, and she passed it to me in her will like it was a spoon." Tansy snorted. "A spoon would've been more use, most days. But not today."

Ansel sat very still.

The pattern of it was rising in her like water in a well, and she could not stop it, could not look away from it: *her mother, who never spoke of the grandmother, whose own history stopped clean at some border she would never explain. Ansel herself, three years of sanding herself nameless, debtless, loyal to no one, proud of it, proud of the emptiness, calling it freedom. Three years of a discipline she had thought was hers, invented by her, chosen by her—and it had never been chosen at all. It had been inherited. Handed down like a coat. Wren had erased herself to save the world; the erasure had soaked down through the blood like ink through cloth, until her granddaughter mistook the family wound for a personality.*

"I did it too," Ansel said. Her voice sounded very small in the herb-hung room. "The erasing. Three years. I thought I was being careful. I thought it was—" She stopped. She had no ending for the sentence that wasn't a lie.

Tansy Gorse watched her with eyes that had seen a hundred years and had not, apparently, enjoyed much of it. "And did it keep you safe?" she asked. "The erasing."

Ansel thought of Bramble's shop, burned. Of Bramble, dead. Of Odell's gruff shelter, Pip's guilty face, Marisol's bandaged side, Hesk's—she flinched away from the ford, away from the bridge, away from the sound of it. Safe. The word was a joke the world had told at her expense.

"No," she said. "It kept me alone. It turns out those are different things."

"Aye." Tansy reached over and refilled her cup, unasked. "That's the lesson, then. That's the inheritance, if you want the truth of it. Not the magic. The loneliness. Wren paid the seal and the seal took the village, and what was left of her taught her daughter to keep clear of people, and her daughter taught you, and none of you ever once asked whether the paying had to be done *alone.*" She sat back. "Wren saw two endings, they say. The book's got two endings in it. Seal it and lose yourself, or open it and lose the world. You know why she only saw two?"

"Because—" Ansel began, and stopped, because she didn't know, and Tansy was shaking her head.

"Because she'd already paid the seal everything that would have let her see a third," the old woman said. "You can't imagine your way out of a trap with the part of you that imagines already sold. She went down into that garden a whole woman and came up with the imagining gone out of her, and she wrote her book with what was left, and what was left could only count to two." Tansy's voice dropped. "The Bloom knight asked me where Wren went, after. Where she died. Where she's buried."

Ansel looked up. "And you told her nothing."

"I told her nothing because she was Bloom." Tansy smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "You're not Bloom. And you've got her hands, and you've got her name in your mouth even if you don't know it yet. So I'll tell you what my grandmother told me, and then you'll go, tonight, because if the Bloom comes back and finds you here, they'll burn this village for the second time, and we haven't the people left to survive it."

"Second time," Ansel said. "They burned it before?"

"They burned it the year after Wren left. Looking for the book. Found nothing, because Wren had already hidden it better than they could search, and burned it anyway, because that's what fire is *for,* to the Bloom. Ask me how I know what I know about burning." Tansy's face did something complicated and old. "She went east. Into the Thornfell roots, past the old shrine, to a cottage that isn't on any road because she built it off every road. She went there to finish the paying, or to finish the writing—I was never sure which. And my grandmother said—" the cadence again, worn smooth, handed down like a spoon, like a seed, "—she said, *'If anyone with her hands ever comes asking, tell them: the last page isn't in the book. The last page is where she stopped being a record and started being a riddle.'*"

The fire had burned low. Outside, the night had come down fully over Nettlebed, and the mountains stood against the stars like something crouched and waiting.

"The cottage," Ansel said. "East, past the old shrine. That's all?"

"That's all anyone living knows." Tansy hauled herself up with the stick and went to a chest in the corner, and came back with a bundle wrapped in waxed cloth: bread, hard cheese, a flask of the punishing wine, and—she pressed it into Ansel's hands—a small iron key, green with age, shaped like nothing so much as a thorn. "From the well-house. My grandmother left instructions for that too, curse her thorough heart. *For the girl with the hands.* I've been keeping it forty years, and I'll tell you honestly I'd begun to hope it was a fool's errand and no girl would ever come."

Ansel turned the key over. It was warm from the old woman's pocket, and it weighed nothing, and it felt like it weighed everything.

"What does it open?"

"I don't know," Tansy said. "That's the whole point of a riddle, girl." She walked her to the door, and the cold came in around them, and the stars over Thornfell were hard and white. "One thing more. Free of charge, because you've got her face when you're tired, and it's a shame to let a face like that walk into the dark unwarned." The old woman's grip on her arm was stronger than it looked. "Wren paid alone because she believed alone was the price. Don't you believe it. Whatever the book tells you the cost is—whatever the seal demands—*don't* you believe the alone is part of the bill. That's not the seal talking. That's the family."

Ansel stood on the dark green of Nettlebed with a key in her hand and her grandmother's name in her mouth and her grandmother's loneliness in her blood, and found that she could not say thank you, because thank you was too small for a village's hundred-year secret and too large for everything else it meant.

"I have people," she said instead. It came out rough, unpracticed, like a phrase in a language she was only learning to speak. "A crew. They hid me when I gave them no reason to. I keep—" She stopped. Started again. "I'm trying to stop erasing them."

Tansy Gorse smiled, and this time it reached her eyes, shallow-water-pale and bright. "Then you're already further down the road than she ever got," the old woman said. "Go east. Mind the shrine—the Bloom watch it on the quarter-moons. And girl—" as Ansel turned away, "—when you find the cottage, and you will, because it wants finding: she left something there on purpose. Wren never did a thing in her long life without leaving a door in it. She just needed someone to come who'd thought to look."

* * *

Ansel walked east through the night, past the shrine with its thorned spiral worn into the stone, past the last field wall, into the foothills where the hazel gave way to pine and the pine gave way to the old, dark, root-woven country beneath the mountain's shoulder. The key rode in her fist. The letter rode against her ribs. Somewhere behind her, Nettlebed's single lamp went out, a village folding itself back into its long practiced silence.

The moon rose—fat, a night or two off full—and she counted without wanting to. Five. Five moon-turns since the garden. Eight left.

She found the cottage at dawn, or rather she found the place where a cottage should not have been: a fold in the hillside where the brambles grew in a perfect, deliberate spiral, thorned and flowering out of season, and at the center of the spiral a door of black oak with no handle and no hinge she could see—only a keyhole, old and green and thorn-shaped, waiting a hundred years for a hand like hers.

Ansel Amberdale stood before it, and the wind moved through the brambles, and the flowers turned, all together, to face her like a hundred small watchful eyes.

The key fit as though it had been cut yesterday.

The door opened on darkness, and out of the darkness came a smell of ink and dried herbs and dust and, faintly, impossibly, of applewood smoke—as if a fire had gone out in that room only an hour ago, as if someone had only just stepped away.

On a table inside, one page lay waiting, torn from a book, its edges brown with a century of patience.
