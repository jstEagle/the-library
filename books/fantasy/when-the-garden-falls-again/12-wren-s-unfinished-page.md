## 12. Wren's Unfinished Page

The dark inside the cottage was not the dark of a place long abandoned.

That was Ansel's first thought, and it unsettled her more than anything else had in three years of keeping herself small and unremarkable. Abandoned places had a particular quality of darkness—thick, settled, indifferent, the darkness of things that had stopped expecting anyone. This darkness felt held. Tended. As though it were being kept warm for her arrival, the way Odell kept a kettle hot when he knew Pip would come stumbling in half-frozen from the night watch.

Ansel stood on the threshold with one hand still resting on the door, and she did not go in.

Behind her, Nettlebed lay quiet under its blanket of early snow, woodsmoke rising straight up from chimneys into windless air. Behind her were the villagers who had looked at her too knowingly, who had said *the hedge-witch's blood* as if it were a diagnosis rather than a name. Behind her was the road south to Hollowfen, where Marisol lay fevered and stitched together, where the crew waited without knowing where she'd gone, where Serah Ivo might or might not be hunting her still.

Ahead of her was a table with one page lying on it, waiting, patient as a trap.

"You're stalling," Ansel told herself quietly, and the words made little clouds in the cold air, and the cottage did not answer, which was somehow worse than if it had.

She went in.

* * *

Her eyes adjusted slowly. The single room was smaller than she'd imagined—she had built her grandmother a cottage of the mind over the past days, walking out of Hollowfen and up through the passes, and in that imagining Wren had lived somewhere grander, stranger, a tower hung with charms. Instead there was this: low beams blackened with age, a hearth swept clean, a bed narrow as a coffin with a quilt folded at its foot in precise squares. Shelves lined one wall, holding jars whose contents had gone the color of old tea—dried yarrow, dried something else, dried years. A mortar and pestle on the sill, a bowl stained purple inside, as though someone had been crushing berries for ink the day before yesterday, a century ago.

Everything was clean. That was the second thing that unsettled her. Dust lay on the windowsill and along the beam-ends, thick and gray and undisturbed, but the floor was swept. The hearthstones showed no ash. The quilt was folded. It was as if the cottage itself had been tidied by hands—or by something that remembered hands—for an occupant who never came home.

*She sealed Vaskar,* Ansel thought. *And then she erased herself. Every record. Every memory. She made sure no one could find her.*

Except, apparently, she had left the door unlocked. And a key growing in a bramble for a hundred years.

And a page.

Ansel crossed the room. Her boots sounded wrong on the boards—too loud, too present—and she caught herself walking on the outside edges of her feet the way she did in Order territory, silent as smoke, and then laughed at herself, a short bitter sound, because who was she hiding from? A dead woman? A folded quilt?

The page lay on the table exactly as promised. Beside it sat a stub of candle, a flint, and—her throat tightened—a small clay pot of ink, stoppered with wax, and a quill worn down to a nub. The tools of writing. The tools of *seeing*, if what the book in her satchel had taught her these last weeks was true: that her grandmother hadn't just written prophecies, she had *read* them, walked ahead through time the way Ansel walked ahead through mountain passes, memorizing turns before she ever took them.

Ansel lit the candle. Its flame stood perfectly still in the windless room.

Then she picked up the page.

It was heavier than paper should be. Older. The same stock as the prophecy book hidden against her ribs—the book she had stolen from a ruin, the book that named her the Thirteenth Key, the book that carried her death date written in a hand she now recognized as her own mother's teaching-hand, learned from *this* hand, this grandmother, this Wren. She turned it over. Blank on the back. On the front, in ink faded to the brown of dried blood:

*I only saw two endings because I had no one to imagine a third with.*

Ansel read it once. Then again. Then a third time, moving her lips, because the words refused to behave like words. They behaved like a key turned in a lock she hadn't known was in her chest.

*No one to imagine a third with.*

Not *no way*. Not *the third ending is impossible*. Just—loneliness. Stated plainly, without self-pity, the way you'd note that you'd run out of lamp oil. *I saw two endings because my imagination ran out at the edge of my isolation.* The great hedge-witch who had bound a god beneath a mountain and then cut herself out of history like a rotten thread—that woman had been defeated not by Vaskar, not by the Order, not by any magic at all.

She had been defeated by being alone.

Ansel set the page down very carefully, because her hands had begun to shake, and she recognized the shaking. It was the shaking from the night in the Verdant Garden when she'd first read the prophecy and seen her own scarred hands inked onto the page. It was vertigo. But it was vertigo of a different color this time. Because the book had shown her two endings—seal and shatter, sacrifice and apocalypse, close the door with your life or open it with your soul—and she had spent every night since carrying those two futures like stones in each hand, weighing which death she preferred.

And here, in a buried cottage under a hill, her dead grandmother had left her a note that amounted to: *I couldn't see a way out, but I never said there wasn't one.*

"Hope," Ansel said aloud, testing it. The word felt foreign in her mouth, smuggled goods, contraband. She hadn't let herself carry hope in three years; it was heavy, it clinked, it drew attention. "You absolute—" She didn't finish. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until she saw sparks.

Because it wasn't only hope. It was also a mirror. *I had no one.* Ansel had spent three years perfecting the art of having no one. No surname spoken. No debts owed. No hearth-night invitations accepted, no winter fires shared, no names learned past what was useful for survival. She had called it discipline. She had called it freedom. She had stood in the ruins of the Verdant Garden reading a prophecy about a Thirteenth Key who must choose between two deaths, and she had thought: *of course it's me, of course it ends alone, that's the shape of my life.*

And her grandmother had sat at this table a hundred years ago and drawn the exact same shape around herself, and called it fate, and died of it, and left behind one sentence scrawled like a confession: *it wasn't fate, it was loneliness, and loneliness is not a law of nature, it is just a room you haven't left yet.*

The riddle of it settled over Ansel slowly, the way snow settles. Hope arriving disguised as a riddle. Because the sentence didn't tell her what the third ending *was*. It didn't say how to defeat the price of the moon-turns, how to keep her memories, how to save Marisol, how to outrun the Order, how to stand before a god's prison and choose neither door. It only said: *find someone to imagine with.*

And she thought of them despite herself. Odell, gruff as gravel, who had hidden her without asking why. Hesk, who hummed while he cooked. Pip and Marisol, twins in mischief if not in blood, who had adopted her the way ivy adopts a wall—slowly, relentlessly, whether the wall consented or not. Even Serah Ivo, knight-captain, hunter, traitor-to-her-own-order-in-a-small-unrecorded-way, standing on a cliff road letting her go, planting doubt in the machinery that hunted her.

*A crew you don't choose,* she had thought once, bitterly, in Hollowfen. But standing here, in her grandmother's swept and waiting kitchen, she understood the sentence differently. Maybe the choosing went both directions. Maybe the crew had chosen her, and all this time the missing piece—the piece Wren had lacked, the piece that made the prophecy blind—was simply Ansel choosing back.

"That's it?" she whispered to the empty room. To the folded quilt. To the woman who had been dust for decades and left her this. "That's the whole secret? Have people?"

But even as she said it, she knew it wasn't small. She knew it was the hardest thing anyone had ever asked of her. Harder than sealing a god. You could seal a god alone, with enough blood and enough moons. You could not be loved alone. You could not imagine a future *with* someone unless you had already let them past the walls, and Ansel had spent three years making those walls load-bearing.

The candle flame finally flickered—some breath of air from a crack in the stone—and in its stutter the shadows on the wall moved, and for one absurd heartbeat Ansel saw two figures bent over this table: a young woman with ink-stained fingers and an older one with a quill, heads together, arguing about endings, laughing about beginnings, building a future between them the way you build a fire, one small piece at a time, together.

Her grandmother had never had that. Had never had a crew. Had sealed a god, erased herself, and sat down at this table to write one final honest sentence about why the world only got two endings.

Ansel picked up the page again, folded it with the care she gave to maps of dangerous passes, and slid it inside her coat, against her ribs, next to the prophecy book. Two documents now. Two futures and a confession.

"I'll imagine a third with them," she said to the cottage—to Wren, wherever Wren was, whatever was left of her. "I don't know how yet. But I won't do what you did. I won't leave them a swept floor and a folded quilt and one sad sentence."

The hearth, cold for a century, gave a soft *pop*.

Ansel spun, knife half-drawn from her boot—and then froze, staring.

In the fireplace, in the grate that had held no fire since before her mother was born, one log had kindled. Just one. A small, stubborn flame, applewood-scented, burning with no source, no spark, no explanation—burning the way the flowers in the bramble-door had turned to face her, the way the key had grown for a hundred years, the way everything in this place seemed to have been waiting for precisely her.

Wren's magic, maybe. Or Wren's attention. Or just the cottage itself, doing the last chore it remembered: warming the house for family.

Ansel stood in her grandmother's kitchen with a stolen page against her heart and watched the impossible fire burn, and for the first time since the Verdant Garden, since the book, since the date and the storm and the scarred hands, she felt something that was not fear harden into defiance or grief curdle into resolve.

She felt found. Hunted by the Order, hunted by prophecy, hunted by her own bloodline—and found anyway.

"All right," she said softly, and held her palms to a heat that shouldn't exist. "All right. I'm listening."

The fire burned on. And outside, far down the white road toward Hollowfen, she could not see—but would soon learn—that a rider had turned off the pass road, riding hard through the snow, carrying news that would make the third ending suddenly, desperately urgent.

For the Order had burned Bramble's shop. But they had missed one thing: Bramble had kept copies.
