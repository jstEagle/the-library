## 14. Enemies at the Same Fire

*Serah*

The blade at her throat was a good blade—well-oiled, well-honed, held by a hand that had learned to hold it the way smugglers learn: not for ceremony but for necessity. Serah kept her hands open and her breathing slow. She had been on the other side of that edge enough times to know what made a hand shake and what made it steady.

"I'm not here to take you back," she said again, into the dark beyond the firelight. "I'm here because they're herding you to the ravine—and we have until the next moonrise to prove them wrong."

The blade did not move. The fire did not move. Somewhere behind her, up the switchback she'd descended, the wind carried the smell of pine resin and old stone, and beneath it, faint as a lie told quietly, the smell of the Order's incense.

"Say it again," said a voice from the fire—a woman's voice, roughened by pipe smoke and years of giving orders. "Slowly. And don't move your hands."

Serah didn't move her hands. "The Order of the Quiet Bloom has known your route since Hollowfen," she said. "Not because of the boy who sold it—" she heard a sharp intake of breath from the fire, and filed that away, gently, for later "—but because I reported it. A false report, in Greywater Ford's garrison ledger, naming a different pass. They read my report. They corrected it. Which means they no longer trust me, which means they are already hunting me, which means everything I know is now worth exactly what you decide it's worth."

"And what do you know?" the captain asked.

"That they're not following you anymore." Serah let the silence sit for one heartbeat, two. "They're ahead of you."

The blade at her throat trembled—not with fear, she thought, but with the effort of not using itself.

* * *

*Ansel*

Ansel had the knight's throat bared under her knife and every reason in the world to open it.

Three years of reasons. Three years of keeping her back to walls and her name to herself and her heart in a locked box buried so deep even she'd forgotten where the key was. And here was the Order of the Quiet Bloom kneeling in her firelight with its hands open like an offering, saying all the right words in all the right order, and Ansel Amberdale—who was not supposed to be Ansel Amberdale anymore, who was nobody, who was a route-memory and a pair of scarred hands and nothing else—wanted very badly to believe her.

That was the problem. That was exactly the problem. The wanting was new, and new things were how people died.

"She's telling the truth about the ravine." Marisol's voice, low, from beside Odell. Marisol's arm was still bound and slung, and she stood anyway, the way she always stood, like pain was a tax she paid without complaint. "I scouted it this morning. There's a shelf above the narrows where fifty archers could stand and never be seen from below."

"There are not fifty archers," Serah said. "There are twelve. But twelve is enough for a ravine."

"You'd know," Pip said bitterly, from the far side of the fire. He hadn't looked at her since she'd spoken his crime aloud. His knees were drawn up and his arms wrapped around them, making himself small, and Ansel felt something twist in her chest that she refused to examine.

"I would know," Serah agreed simply. "That's rather the point of me."

Hesk shifted, and the ground seemed to shift with him. The big man had not sat down since the knight walked into their light; he stood behind Ansel's shoulder like a second wall, and when he spoke it was the low rumble he saved for things that mattered. "If this is a trap, girl, say the word and it ends before her next breath."

It would too. That was the terrible thing about Hesk—he was gentle the way avalanches were gentle right up until they weren't.

Ansel looked at the knight. Really looked. Serah Ivo knelt in the dirt of a smuggler waystation with her sword still belted at her hip—an armed woman kneeling before armed strangers, which no trained killer did unless the training had broken somewhere—and her eyes were steady and tired and honest in the specific way that eyes are honest when they have stopped caring whether anyone believes them.

"The book," Ansel said at last.

Serah blinked. "What?"

"You came down that hill knowing we'd put a knife on you. You came anyway. Nobody does that for a warning about archers." Ansel lowered the blade an inch. Kept it lowered the way you keep a door closed but not barred. "You came for the book. Wren's book. You want to see the thirteenth page."

For the first time, the Knight-Captain of the Quiet Bloom looked uncertain. It transformed her face entirely—made her young, made her human, made her someone who had once been a girl learning sword-forms in a courtyard somewhere, believing every word her teachers said.

"Yes," Serah said. "Yes, I do."

* * *

*Serah*

They searched her first, which was correct procedure and she told them so, and Pip—the small angry one, the one whose guilt was sitting on him like wet wool—did the searching with more force than thoroughness, taking her sword, her knives, the signal-whistle she'd already disabled, and her Order seal, which he held up to the firelight with an expression of pure venom.

"This is what they stamped Bramble's shop with," he said. "After. On the door. So everyone would know why it burned."

Serah looked at the seal in his hand—the silver bloom, the eight petals folded inward around an empty center—and found she could not make her mouth form a defense. There wasn't one. There had never been one. She had spent twenty years polishing that symbol and three weeks discovering what it was polished over.

"I know," she said.

"It doesn't bother you? Holding what you did?"

"Pip." The captain's voice cracked across the fire like a whip, but Serah raised a hand.

"No. Let him ask." She met the boy's eyes. "No, it doesn't bother me the way you want it to. Not yet. Guilt is a luxury I haven't had time for—I've been too busy committing treason. Ask me again in a month, if I'm alive, and I'll give you a better answer. Probably the one you're hoping for."

Pip stared at her, thrown, and thrust the seal back into her palm as if it had burned him. "Keep your flower," he muttered. "Weeds grow better in the dark anyway."

From her pack—her whole pack, surrendered—they took out the documents she'd stolen from the garrison at Greywater Ford: movement orders, supply requisitions, and the letter bearing the High Bloom's own cipher that had turned her stomach to stone the night she'd decoded it. And then, last, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom, she brought out the thing she had risked everything to carry here.

Wren's page.

She had taken it from the Order archive at Thornfell eleven days ago, in the hour between the evening bell and the watch change, with her heart hammering so hard she'd been certain the whole cloister could hear it. It was a scrap of vellum no bigger than two spread hands, brittle with age, covered in a hedge-witch's slanted hand. The Order had catalogued it as *fragment, apocryphal, suppressed by decree of the Ninth Bloom*. They had kept it for a hundred years in a locked drawer because a dead grandmother's unfinished sentence frightened them more than the war-god did.

Ansel took it the way people take newborns or live coals.

She read it once in silence. Then again, her lips moving. Then she sat down abruptly on the waystation's fallen lintel, the page shaking in her scarred hands, and Serah watched the girl's whole architecture of distance—the three years of it, the careful nobody-ness of her—crack straight down the middle.

"'I only saw two endings,'" Ansel read aloud, voice unsteady, "'because I had no one to imagine a third with.'" She looked up. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "This is hers. This is her handwriting. I'd know it anywhere, she wrote out recipes in this exact—she pressed too hard on the downstrokes, she always—"

She stopped. Pressed the page flat against her chest. Looked at Serah with an expression that contained, in equal measure, gratitude and the desire to kill her.

"Why did they suppress it?"

"Because it means the prophecy is incomplete." Serah spoke carefully now, aware that every person at this fire was deciding her fate in real time. "A sealed text can be enforced. An open question cannot. If the Key can choose a third path—if the choice isn't sealed or shatter, kill or be killed—then the Order loses the only authority it has ever had. Fear of a fixed future is the whole foundation. Aurelia built her tower on it."

"Aurelia," Odell repeated. "The Grand Bloom herself."

"She's coming to the Garden on the thirteenth moon-turn. Not to stop the opening." Serah let that land. Watched it land. "To perform it."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, a night bird called and got no answer.

* * *

*Ansel*

Later—much later, when the alliance was struck and the watches set and the knight's weapons returned to her with visible reluctance—Ansel sat apart with the page and tried to breathe.

Hesk settled down beside her. He didn't speak. He never wasted words, Hesk; he just arranged his enormous frame between her and the wind, and the wind gave up, and after a while he said, "Your gran sounds like she was smart."

"She was a hedge-witch in a village of forty people." Ansel's thumb traced the downstrokes, the too-deep press of the pen, a hundred years old and still leaving grooves. "She grew feverfew and lied about her age and taught me which mushrooms would kill me by feeding me the safe ones first. She was not supposed to matter to the world."

"Nobody's supposed to matter to the world," Hesk said. "That's what makes it matter when they do."

Across the fire, Serah Ivo sat sharpening a knife she didn't need sharpened, because her hands needed something to do and her pride needed somewhere to hide. Watching her, Ansel felt the old reflex rise—the one that said *keep the wall up, keep the box locked, everyone you let close becomes a debt and debts get collected*—and underneath it, quieter, Wren's sentence turning over like a coin:

*I had no one to imagine a third ending with.*

Well. Ansel looked around the fire. A gruff captain pretending to sleep. Twins—one guilty, one wounded, both awake. A mountain who smelled of pine sap and kindness. And a defected knight with a murderer's seal in her pocket and a traitor's truth in her mouth.

Maybe her grandmother had lacked for company. Ansel had run out of excuses.

"Knight-Captain," she called across the flames.

Serah looked up. "Just Serah, I think. The title belongs to people I no longer am."

"Serah, then." Ansel held up the page. "My grandmother wrote this alone, in a cottage, a hundred years ago, and it cost her everything she loved, and it still wasn't enough—because she was right. One person can't imagine a third ending. It takes—" she gestured at the fire, at all of it, at the improbable, impossible circle of them "—it takes whatever this is."

"And what is it?" Serah asked. There was no mockery in it. There was hunger in it, the hunger of a woman who had eaten oaths for twenty years and was starving for something else.

Ansel thought about lying. Thought about the wall, the box, the locked door.

"I don't know yet," she said honestly. "But you're standing in it."

* * *

*Serah*

The watch changed at moonrise. Odell took first, Hesk second, and Serah volunteered for third and was surprised when the captain nodded agreement instead of laughing in her face.

"You're either the best liar I've ever met," Odell said, settling onto her bedroll, "or the first honest soldier the Bloom ever produced. Either way, you'll want to be awake when the lying hours come. That's when you'll find out which one you are."

Serah took the third watch alone at the waystation's edge, facing downslope, toward the ravine she'd warned them about, toward the twelve archers who would find an empty narrows at dawn and know exactly who had emptied it.

Behind her, the fire murmured. The twins argued in whispers about whose fault the broker had been, and arrived, as siblings do, at the conclusion that it was both and neither. The girl called Ansel slept sitting up, the way fugitives sleep, with Wren's page tucked inside her coat against her heart.

And Serah Ivo, knight of nothing, watched the moon climb over Thornfell's black teeth and counted what remained. Nine days to the thirteenth moon-turn. A hedge-prison grown over a god. A Grand Bloom carrying something whispering in her chest. A crew of smugglers who had every reason to cut her throat in her sleep and had instead given her a place at their fire, and a girl with her grandmother's handwriting pressed to her ribs who had said *you're standing in it* as if it were a fact already, as if trust were a country you could simply walk into and claim citizenship in.

Her teachers would have called this weakness. Her teachers were ash and treason now.

In the dark below, far off, almost imagined: a lantern. Moving along the ravine road. Pausing where the narrows began.

Then another lantern beside it. Then a third.

Twelve archers, arriving to find their ambush already dead—and, finding it, understanding precisely who had killed it. By morning, every hunter in the Order would know Serah Ivo's name the way they knew Vaskar's: as a thing that had to be put down.

Serah did not wake the camp. They'd know soon enough, and they'd need their sleep, and besides—

Besides, she realized, watching the lanterns gather in the dark like slow cold stars, some part of her had already decided. Somewhere between the knife at her throat and the page against the girl's heart, she had stopped being a knight hunting a fugitive and become something the Order had no word for.

She settled her stolen sword across her knees and kept the watch, and waited for dawn to make her a traitor officially.
