## 15. Moon Four: The Price of Names

She woke to the sound of rain and the absence of a word.

It was not silence, exactly. Silence would have been kinder. It was the sensation of reaching into a pocket she had carried all her life—her own mouth, her own mind—and finding it empty. There had been something there. Something that belonged to her the way a scar belongs to skin, worn smooth by years of answering questions: *And what's your name, girl? Who do you belong to? Where are you from, Amberdale?*

The name was gone.

Ansel sat up in the loft of the safehouse above the tannery, heart hammering, and said it aloud to prove it was only a dream, a trick of waking. "Amber—" The syllable dissolved before it reached her teeth. She tried again. "Amber. Am. Am—" Nothing followed. There was no second half. There was a shape where the second half should have been, the way a missing tooth leaves a hollow the tongue keeps returning to, probing, disbelieving.

"Amberdale," she whispered.

Nothing. Not fog, not forgetting—the particular, surgical nothing of the book's price paid in full. She knew that nothing now. She had met it once before, on the first moon-turn, when her mother's face had gone out of her like a lamp snuffed between cupped hands. She remembered that she had loved a face. She could not summon it. And now—

Now she did not know who she was.

* * *

The moon-turn had come four nights after Greywater Ford, four nights after they had buried Hesk beneath a rowan tree on the ridge with Odell's coat for a shroud because no one could bear to put him in the ground uncovered. Four nights of grief sitting among them at the table like another guest who never left, and Ansel had known—I had known, she corrected herself, I knew—what was coming. She had read the page a dozen times until the letters swam:

*On the fourth turn, the Key shall pay in names. What she is called, she will cease to be called.*

She had thought, in the arrogant way of the untested, that she understood it. A price of names. Perhaps she would forget the names of streets. Perhaps the twins' nicknames for each other, or the pet name Hesk had called his mule. She had braced herself for small losses, catalogued them like a smuggler cataloguing cargo, told herself she could afford any of them. Names were light things. Names were handles. You could live without a handle.

She had not understood that the price would take the handle off *her*.

She dressed in the dark because she could not bear the dark to see her cry about a thing she couldn't even name, which was its own absurd cruelty—grieving a loss she couldn't describe, weeping for a word. Her hands shook as she laced her boots. In the grey pre-dawn she crept down the stairs past the room where Marisol slept propped on pillows, her ribs bound in linen, breathing in the careful shallow rhythm of a body that had learned pain as a language. Past Pip's corner, where he slept curled tight as a fist. Past Odell, awake already as always, smoking on the back step under the eaves, watching the rain come down over Hollowfen's crooked rooftops.

Odell didn't look at her. That was a mercy. Ansel slipped out into the wet street and walked.

* * *

Hollowfen at dawn in the rain was a city pretending to be asleep while every one of its thousand eyes stayed open. Smoke rose from a hundred chimneys and flattened against the low clouds. Gulls fought over fish guts on the quay. Somewhere a bell rang the hour in a cracked voice, and the sound went through Ansel like a blade finding a gap in armor, because bells marked time and time was the thing eating her alive, one moon-turn at a time.

She stopped at the mouth of Cooper's Lane and tried once more.

"My name is Ansel."

The words came. Those words came. *Ansel* still lived in her, small and stubborn, a coal refusing the ash. But it floated loose now, unmoored, a single plank from a ship whose hull was gone. Ansel *what*. Ansel of *where*. Daughter of *whom*. Granddaughter of the woman who had written the book that was eating her life away in monthly installments.

"I'm Ansel Amberdale," she said to the rain, and heard a stranger say it—a girl playing at being someone, reciting a name she'd been told, wearing it like a borrowed cloak two sizes wrong. Because it *was* borrowed now. She had written it on a scrap of paper three nights ago—she'd had the forethought, gods rot her, she'd had the terrible clever forethought to write things down since the first payment—and so she knew the word the way you know a word in an old language. Correctly. Coldly. Without it meaning anything in your chest.

She fished the scrap from her inner pocket, rain spotting the ink. Her own handwriting stared back at her: *My name is Ansel Amberdale. My grandmother was Wren. Bramble raised me after my mother died. I have a scar on my left palm from a fence wire at fourteen.*

All true. All verified. None of it hers anymore. She was becoming a stranger with excellent documentation.

A laugh climbed out of her, ugly and short, startling a gull off a piling. Three years she had spent erasing herself. No surname spoken. No debts owed. No loyalties kept. She had done it deliberately, carefully, the way you burn a field to stop a fire—scorched earth, nothing for anyone to hold over her or hang her with. She had been so proud of it. *No name worth stealing,* she used to tell herself at border crossings while other travelers sweated over their papers.

Well. She had her wish. She was nobody now in truth, and the universe, which apparently had a sense of humor like a rusted trap, had made the Order's work redundant.

"You're going to catch your death."

She hadn't heard Odell come up behind her; few people ever had. The captain stood under an oilcloth awning, arms crossed, grey beard dripping, watching her with those river-bottom eyes that gave away nothing except, occasionally, everything.

"I'm fine," Ansel said.

"No, you're not." Odell came to stand beside her, shoulder nearly touching, both of them looking out at the rain-greyed harbor. "You've got the look Marisol had when the fever took her memory of the shipwreck year. Knew facts. Couldn't feel them." A pause. "It took something last night. Don't tell me what. Just—yes or no?"

Ansel's throat worked. "Yes," she said, and hated how small it sounded.

Odell nodded slowly, the way she nodded at reports of storms or customs patrols: information received, to be dealt with. "Come inside. You're not standing in the rain turning yourself into a stranger on an empty stomach."

* * *

They ate hard bread and smoked fish in the safehouse kitchen, and Ansel learned what a family does when one of its members starts disappearing.

Pip noticed first—not the loss itself, but her flinch when he said good morning with her surname attached, a habit she'd never managed to break him of no matter how many times she'd snapped at him for it. He'd said "Morning, Amberdale," bright as brass, and she had dropped her mug.

The crack of it brought Marisol limping to the doorway, one hand pressed to her bound ribs, grey-faced but upright. One look at Ansel's face and Marisol said quietly, "It happened, didn't it. Last night."

"It took the name," Ansel said. She made herself say it plainly, because hiding it felt worse than the loss. "Amberdale. I can't—it's just a word now. Someone else's word. I keep saying it and it doesn't—" Her voice betrayed her, cracking on the last syllable, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Three years I worked at having no name and I *chose* that, don't you understand? It was mine to give up. This isn't—this is being robbed, this is—"

"Hey." Pip crouched beside her chair, awkward, gangly, his knuckles still scabbed from the ford. "Hey. Look at me." She looked. His eyes were wet and furious, and the fury wasn't at her. "You're not a stranger. You hear me? Whatever that cursed book takes, it takes paper, not people. We know you."

"You knew a girl named Amberdale," Ansel whispered. "I'm the stranger who has her handwriting."

The kitchen went very quiet. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below, the tannery vats breathed their sour smell up through the floorboards, the smell that meant safety, that meant home, and Ansel realized with a fresh, vertiginous grief that she was mourning a smell's associations she could no longer trace.

Marisol lowered herself into the chair across from Ansel, wincing, and laid her scarred hands flat on the table—deliberately, ceremonially, the way you lay cards down.

"Then we'll remember it for you," she said.

"What?"

"The name. All of it—all the things that book steals. You write your lists, fine, ink's cheap. But ink burns." Marisol tapped the table with one finger, once, twice, like a heartbeat. "So we'll carry it too. Every moon-turn, whatever it takes, you tell us what's gone, and we say it back to you until it sticks somewhere deeper than paper. That's how it works with me and Pip—we're twins, we hold each other's memories like coin in a shared purse. Half of what I remember of our childhood is really him remembering it at me." A ghost of her old grin flickered and died. "That's what family *is*, Amberdale. People who keep your name when you can't."

Ansel hadn't agreed to be family. She had spent three years making sure of it—turning down the hearth-night invitation, keeping watch alone, sleeping apart, giving nothing that could be taken. And here were these battered, stubborn people, offering to be her external heart, asking nothing, and the horrible thing—the unbearable thing—was that some starving part of her wanted it more than she had ever wanted anything, wanted it the way the drowned want air.

"That's dangerous," she said hoarsely. "For you. The Order follows attachments. Everything I love becomes a door they can kick."

"Everything we love was always a door," Odell said from the hearth, poking the coals. "We're smugglers, girl. Our whole lives are doors with bad locks. Difference is now we know which ones matter." She straightened, and her voice went rough. "Hesk held a bridge for you. Don't you dare make that cheap by going back to being a ghost. He bought a person, not a shadow."

Ansel's breath caught. She looked around the table—at Pip nodding fiercely, jaw set, guilt and love tangled in his face; at Marisol with her broken ribs and steady eyes; at old Odell silhouetted against the fire—and something in her chest that had been clenched for three years loosened half a turn, painfully, like a rusted gate.

"Say it, then," Ansel said. Her voice shook. "If you mean it. Say the whole thing."

Marisol reached across the table and took Ansel's scarred left hand, turned it palm-up, and spoke clearly, formally, like an oath being sworn—which, Ansel understood dimly, was exactly what it was.

"Your name is Ansel Amberdale. Your grandmother was Wren of Nettlebed, hedge-witch, and she wrote the book that's hunting you, and we will find her trail together. Bramble of Hollowfen raised you after your mother died, and he loved you like his own, and the Order burned his shop for it, and that debt is ours now too. You have a scar on your left palm from fence wire at fourteen. You know every pass in Thornfell better than the mountains know themselves. You take your tea bitter and your roads steep and your company at arm's length—but the arm's coming down, girl. Bit by bit. Whether you like it or not."

Pip laughed, wet-eyed. Odell grunted something that was almost a smile.

Ansel listened with her eyes closed, and it was strange and terrible and sweet: hearing herself described from outside, the shape of her held up by other voices, like a lantern passed hand to hand in the dark. When Marisol finished, Ansel opened her eyes and found she was crying without permission.

"Again," she said. "Please. Again."

* * *

They said it twice more that morning, and then made a ritual of it, because rituals are how small stubborn people fight large cruel magic. Each evening at the hearth, whatever else was happening—wound-dressing, route-planning, the endless quiet arithmetic of grief—they would speak her back to herself. They added lines as the days passed. Odell contributed dry, precise entries: *She counts stairs out of habit. She cheats at cards but only against Pip.* Marisol offered warmer ones: *She sings when she thinks no one's listening, old Thornfell ballads, badly, beautifully.* Pip, shameless: *She once punched a customs man at Saltmere for calling me a gutter-rat, broke his nose, and lectured him the whole time about proper punching form.*

Ansel memorized herself through their mouths. It was like being born backwards—receiving a self instead of building one. Some nights it comforted her. Some nights it terrified her, because each recitation reminded her of the ledger running in the background: eight more payments due, and the pages of the prophecy book turning whether she read them or not.

On the sixth evening after the payment, she spread the book open on the kitchen table by lamplight and forced herself to study the next entries, because ignorance had stopped being a luxury somewhere around the moment a god's prison entered her life.

*On the seventh turn, the Key shall pay in love.*

She read it three times. The letters did not change. Outside, rain again, soft on the thatch. Behind her, the crew murmured over charts of the Verdant Garden ruins—Serah's maps, the defector knight's precise, beautiful cartography, annotated in her narrow hand. Ansel heard Odell say something about the descent beneath the roots, about the passage Serah claimed existed below the old reflecting pool, and heard Marisol argue that they should wait for Marisol's ribs to knit, and heard Serah's low voice answer that waiting was a luxury the thirteenth moon would not grant them.

*Pay in love.* The first price had taken a face. The fourth had taken a name. Love was larger country than either. She thought of the recitations at the hearth, the ledger of small true things, the arm coming down bit by bit—and imagined the book reaching into all of it, scooping it out like pulp from a fruit, leaving her sitting among people she'd recognize and not cherish, hearing their voices and feeling nothing, empty-eyed as the Gardener waiting alone in the dark beneath the Garden.

"We leave for the Garden in three days," Odell said, catching her eye across the room. "Can you walk that long?"

Ansel closed the book gently, as if it were sleeping and might wake cranky.

"I can walk anywhere," she said. "I'm the girl who knows every pass in Thornfell better than the mountains know themselves."

Marisol grinned from her chair. "See? Sticks somewhere deeper than paper."

But that night, lying awake under the eaves with rain on the roof and the ledger open in her mind, Ansel took out her scraps of paper and, by stub-candlelight, added a new line in her own hand—one she did not read aloud at the hearth, one she kept folded against her heart like a splinter:

*Nine turns remain. It will ask for my love next. Find the third ending before it finishes asking—or teach them to mourn me properly, and go into the dark beneath the Garden knowing exactly what I stand to lose.*
