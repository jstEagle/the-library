## 17. The Gardener

The word hung in the cavern's throat like a stone dropped into a well—*Wren*—and the silence that followed was not empty. It was *waiting*.

Ansel's hand found her knife before thought could catch up. Her fingers closed around leather-wrapped hilt, the motion so practiced it had become reflex, like breathing or the way her boots found purchase on scree without her eyes needing to check. Beside her, Serah's sword whispered from its scabbard, the sound absurdly loud in the hush. Somewhere behind them, Odell cursed—low, vicious, the sound of a man who had survived thirty winters of bad decisions by knowing exactly when to be afraid.

"*Wren*," the voice came again. Not a question this time. A recognition. Deep as bedrock, resonant as a bell struck in a cathedral, and threaded through with something that made Ansel's chest ache—*longing*. "You have returned. The seasons turned. The hedge held. I kept my vigil. *Wren*."

The syllables rolled through the dark, bouncing from walls slick with mineral weep, gathering moss and memory as they traveled. Not human. Not god. Something *grown*.

"Stay close," Serah murmured, barely moving her lips. Her blade caught the faint phosphorescence of the cavern fungi—pale blue-green light painting her face in angles that made her look carved from something harder than flesh. "Whatever this is, it knows the name. It knows *her*."

*Her*. Not *me*. The distinction mattered, even if the thing in the dark didn't seem to hear it.

Ansel's throat worked. Three years she had spent unmaking herself—no surname, no history, no anchors that could be seized and used to drag her back. Three years of becoming *Ansel*, simply *Ansel*, a woman who knew every smuggler's pass in the Thornfells but couldn't tell you the color of her mother's eyes. And now, in the space of a heartbeat, something ancient had reached up from the roots of the world and called her by a name she had never owned.

*Grandmother.*

Wren Amberdale. The hedge-witch who had sealed Vaskar a century past. The woman who had written the prophecy book with her own blood and bound it in her own skin—so the stories went, though Bramble's letter had suggested something less theatrical and more terrible. The woman who had walked these same tunnels, stood before this same prison, and made a choice that had cost her everything.

Including, apparently, a piece of herself left behind to tend the cage.

"Is it—" Pip's voice cracked from somewhere to Ansel's left. She didn't turn. Couldn't afford to look away from the dark ahead. "Is it *him*? Vaskar?"

The exhalation came again. That vast, warm breath, red-gold and smelling of crushed pine needles and old blood and something sweeter underneath—honey, perhaps, or the perfume of flowers that only bloomed once a century.

"*No*," the voice said, and amusement rumbled through it like landslides in slow motion. "The Sleeper dreams still. His chains hold. The hedge *grows*. I am the Gardener. I *tend*."

A pause. Then, softer: "*Wren? Why do you not answer? The thorns require pruning. The southern root has pushed through the third seal. You showed me how. Your hands—*"

The voice broke. Or perhaps the cavern simply swallowed the rest.

Ansel let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding. *The Gardener*. A fragment of Wren's magic, Bramble's letter had hinted. A construct woven from hedge-witchery and desperation, left to maintain the living prison while its maker faded into legend and lies. A hundred years alone in the dark, tending a god's cage.

No wonder it was half-mad.

"We should go," Odell said from the rear. His voice was tight, controlled. "Back to the others. Regroup. This changes—"

"*Wren*."

The word was a whip-crack. The air pressure shifted, ears popping, and the phosphorescent fungi along the walls *pulsed*—brightening, dimming, brightening again in rhythm with something vast and unseen moving through the dark.

"*You carry the book. I feel its pages turning. You carry the *key*. Why do you hesitate? The ritual requires your blood. Your voice. *Your hands*." A sound like branches creaking under snow-load. "*Come. The hour approaches. The thirteenth moon turns in nine nights. The hedge must be ready.*"

Ansel's hand tightened on the prophecy book strapped beneath her coat. The leather was warm against her ribs, heated by her own body, and she swore she could feel a faint thrumming through the layers of wool and linen—a heartbeat not her own.

Nine nights. The thirteenth moon-turn.

The prophecy had been specific: *On the thirteenth turning, the Key shall open or close the door.* She had thought she had time. Weeks, perhaps. Moon-cycles to decipher Wren's torn page, to understand the third path her grandmother hadn't seen.

But the Gardener—*this* thing wearing her grandmother's magic like a second skin—spoke as if the ritual were already underway. As if *waiting* was the only part that remained.

"Serah," Ansel said, her voice steady despite the hammer of her pulse. "Can you—"

"I don't know what this *is*." Serah's blade lowered a fraction, but her stance didn't relax. "The Order's archives mention... constructs. Hedge-witch familiars grown to purpose. But nothing that persisted a century. Nothing that *waited*."

"Then we find out," Ansel said. She took a step forward. Then another. The knife stayed in her hand, but she loosened her grip until her knuckles showed white through the gloom.

"Ansel—" Marisol. The twin's voice was strained, edged with the pain she carried from the Ford, from the arrow she'd taken shielding Ansel. "Don't. Please. We don't—"

"I have to." The words came out quieter than she intended. "It thinks I'm her. It thinks I'm *Wren*. And if the ritual requires Wren's blood, Wren's voice, Wren's hands..." She swallowed. "Then maybe it can tell us what the ritual *is*. What the third path requires. What Wren couldn't see."

A lie. Not entirely—she did need to know. But the real reason pulsed in her chest, hot and shameful: *It called her name. It waited for her. It has been alone for a hundred years, and it knew her the moment she came close.*

Because she was Wren's blood. Wren's granddaughter. The Thirteenth Key.

*Because some part of her had always known she would end up here, in the dark, answering a call she had spent three years trying to silence.*

***

The tunnel narrowed as they descended, the walls pressing close until Ansel's shoulders brushed stone slick with condensation. The air grew warmer—unnaturally so, smelling of loam and root-rot and the copper tang of deep water. Bioluminescent moss clung to every surface in veined patterns that looked uncomfortably like circuitry, like nerves, like the lines on the prophecy book's pages when held to firelight.

*Veins*, she thought. *The mountain's veins. Or the hedge's.*

The singing had stopped. She realized that now—realized it had been singing, a low, wordless melody that had vibrated through the stone so subtly she had mistaken it for the mountain's own hum. The Gardener's lullaby to its charge, perhaps. A century of crooning to a sleeping god.

*What happens when the god wakes? Does the singing stop? Does the Gardener break?*

The thought refused to release her.

They emerged into a chamber that stole breath from all of them.

Vast. Cathedral-vast. The dome they had seen from above was only the crown of something far larger—a hollow carved from living rock by forces that predated human tools, its walls ribbed with roots thick as ship's masts, woven together in a lattice that pulsed with slow, green light. The roots *were* the walls. The ceiling. The floor beneath their boots, springy and yielding, drinking their footfalls without sound.

And in the center, rising from a pool of water so still it mirrored the root-lattice above like a second sky—

A tree.

Not a tree. *The* tree. The Hedge made manifest: a trunk three men could not encircle, bark silver-grey and scarred with centuries of growth, branches spreading into the dome's ribs like arteries into a heart. No leaves. Instead, thorns. Thousands of them, each the length of Ansel's forearm, wickedly curved and glistening with something that might have been sap and might have been poison.

The prison. The cage. Vaskar's chains made visible.

And beneath it, rooted in the black water—

*Her.*

Ansel stopped. The breath locked in her lungs.

The Gardener sat cross-legged on a raft of woven root-fibers, floating motionless on the mirror-water. *She*—it—wore a robe woven from living moss, green fading to brown at the hem where it trailed in the pool. Her hair—*its* hair—was a cascade of vine and creeper, threaded with tiny white flowers that opened and closed in time with the cavern's breathing. Her skin was bark-textured, lined with growth-rings, the color of storm-clouds over pine forests.

But her face—

*Her face was Wren's.*

Not identical. Softer at the jaw, the eyes larger, the mouth less carved by severity. But the *structure*—the high cheekbones, the nose with its slight crook from a childhood fall, the scar bisecting the left eyebrow where a thorn had raked deep—Ansel knew that face. She had seen it in the single surviving portrait Bramble had kept hidden in his almanac. She had traced it with her fingertips in the dark of a dozen safehouses, wondering if her own face held the same geometry.

The Gardener's eyes opened.

They were not human eyes. No whites, no irises. Only green—deep, fathomless, shot through with gold flecks that swam like fish in pond-water. *Chlorophyll and time*, Ansel thought wildly. *Photosynthesis made sentient.*

"*Wren*."

The word came without lips moving. The Gardener's mouth—*Wren's mouth*—remained closed. The voice simply *was*, rising from the water, the roots, the very air.

"*You have returned. The cycle completes. The thirteenth key turns in the lock.*"

Ansel's knees threatened to buckle. She locked them. *Stand. Breathe. Think.*

"I'm not—" Her voice cracked. She cleared her throat, tried again. "I'm not Wren."

The Gardener's head tilted. The vine-hair swayed, flowers chiming softly. "*You wear her face. You carry her blood. You hold her book. You walk her paths. *Of course* you are Wren.*"

A pause. The golden flecks in those green deeps swirled.

"*Unless... unless the seasons have turned further than I counted. Unless the root-count was wrong. *How many moons?*"

"Nine," Ansel said. The number fell from her before she could stop it. "Nine moons until the thirteenth turning."

The Gardener was silent for a long moment. Then a sound emerged—laughter, perhaps, or weeping, or the creak of ancient timber. "*Nine. Only nine. I thought... I thought there would be more time. The hedge grows slower now. The southern root... I could not hold it alone. I *tried*, Wren. I *tried*."*

The anguish in that not-voice cracked something in Ansel's chest.

She moved before she decided to. Boots finding purchase on the springy root-floor. One step. Two. The water's edge. The raft drifted toward her as if pulled by current, though the pool was perfectly still.

Up close, the Gardener was larger than human—seven feet tall, perhaps, when unfolded. Her hands rested on moss-robed knees, fingers long and jointed like branches, tipped with thorns instead of nails.

"*Your hands*," the Gardener said. "*They are different. Smaller. The scars... yes, the same scars. But the fingers... *slender*. *Delicate*." A vine-finger reached out, hovered inches from Ansel's face. "*You have not tended the hedge. Your hands have done... other work.*"

Ansel didn't flinch. Didn't pull away. The vine-finger brushed her cheek—cool, dry, smelling of winter pine and old earth.

"*What work, Wren? What did you do, while I waited?*"

The question hung between them, vast as the cavern.

Ansel's mind raced. *Lie. Deflect. Say you were lost, that you forgot, that the years erased you.* But the Gardener's roots went deep—deeper than lies could reach. This thing was *made* of Wren's magic. It would taste falsehood like rot in water.

"I smuggled," she said. The truth, stripped bare. "Moon-ore, mostly. Contraband through the Thornfell passes. I memorized routes. I forgot names. I made myself... small. Invisible. So no one could use me."

The Gardener was silent. The gold flecks in her eyes slowed their swimming.

"*Small*," she repeated. "*Invisible*. A seed that refuses to sprout. *Why?*"

"Because the world breaks things that matter." The words surprised her—too raw, too true. "Because every name I spoke became a debt. Every loyalty became a lever. So I stopped speaking them. I became *Ansel*. Just Ansel. No surname. No history. No—" She stopped. Swallowed. "*Grandmother*."

The word hung. The Gardener's vine-finger traced the line of Ansel's jaw, the curve of her ear, the scar on her own left hand where a smuggler's blade had slipped three winters past.

"*Ansel*," the Gardener tested. "*An-sel*. A name without roots. A cutting planted in ash. *Poor child. Poor, stubborn child.*"

"Don't call me that."

"*What would you have me call you? You are Wren's blood. Wren's choice. The Thirteenth Key. The prophecy names you.*"

"The prophecy was *wrong*." Ansel's voice rose, sharp in the chamber's hush. "Wren saw two endings. Seal or shatter. Die or doom the world. She didn't see a third. She *couldn't*." She stepped closer, until the moss-robe brushed her knees. "*Because she had no one to imagine it with.*"

The Gardener went still. Utterly, terrifyingly still. The pool's surface didn't ripple. The root-lattice overhead didn't pulse.

"*No one*," the Gardener whispered. "*Wren... had no one?*"

"She erased herself," Ansel said. "After the sealing. She walked out of history. Burned her name from every record. Left only the prophecy book and a letter for a hedge-witch she trusted." Her throat tightened. "She *chose* to be alone. Because she thought that was the price."

A sound from behind her—Serah shifting, perhaps. Odell's boot on root-fiber. The crew, watching. Witnessing.

The Gardener's vine-hair stirred. The tiny flowers all closed at once, petals sealing tight.

"*The price*," the Gardener said. "*Yes. The price. The seal requires... the seal *always* requires.*"

"What does it require?"

The green-gold eyes fixed on Ansel's face. "*Everything. The Key gives everything. Blood. Memory. Name. Love. The anchor holds the door; the Key *becomes* the door. Wren knew this. Wren *chose* this. She gave her memories of *him*—of the man she loved, the village she saved, the child she bore—to power the first sealing. She became... less. Hollow. A vessel emptied for the hedge to fill.*"

Ansel's blood ran cold. "*Memories of him*. Of—of my grandfather?"

"*The carpenter. Tomas. He built the cradle for the child. He sang to the hedge-seeds. He held Wren when the first payment took her mother's face.*" The Gardener's voice softened, became almost gentle. "*The second payment took his name from her tongue. The third took the color of his eyes. The seventh... the seventh took the *feeling* of his hand in hers. She could not recall *why* she had loved him. Only that she had. And that the seal demanded more.*"

Horror climbed Ansel's spine, rung by rung. *The payments. The moon-turns. Each one a price.*

"Moon One," she whispered. "The first payment. I already paid it. My mother's face. I gave it willingly and didn't know why I was crying."

"*The first payment is always the easiest*," the Gardener said. "*A face. A name. A single memory. The Key chooses. The hedge accepts. But the payments *escalate*. Moon Four takes the Key's own name. Moon Seven takes *love*—the feeling, not the memory. Moon Nine takes *home*—the land itself becomes foreign. Moon Eleven takes *hope*. Moon Twelve takes *lineage*—all memory of those who came before.*"

"And Moon Thirteen?"

The Gardener's vine-finger pressed against Ansel's sternum, thorn-tip dimpling skin but not breaking. "*Moon Thirteen takes the Key. Entire. The seal closes. The god sleeps. The Key becomes... the Gardener. A fragment of magic left to tend the hedge. To wait. To sing. *Alone*."*

The words fell into the pool. Ripples spread, slow and deliberate.

*That's what happened to you,* Ansel realized. *You're not a fragment of Wren's magic. You **are** Wren. Or what's left of her. The Key who became the Gardener. The anchor who held the door and lost herself in the holding.*

"*Wren*," the Gardener said, and the not-voice cracked again. "*Is that true? Did I... did *we*...?*"

"You *are* Wren," Ansel said. "Or the part of her that stayed. The part that *paid the price*."

A long silence. Then the Gardener's shoulders slumped—a motion so human it hurt to watch. The vine-hair fell forward, shielding her face. The tiny flowers remained closed.

"*I remember... I remember *waiting*," the Gardener whispered. "*A century of waiting. Counting root-rings. Pruning thorns. Singing the lullaby. And the memories... they *faded*. Not all at once. Petal by petal. Ring by ring. First the carpenter's face. Then his voice. Then the child's first cry. Then... then even *why* I waited. Only the *duty* remained. The hedge. The seal. The count.*"

She lifted her face. Tears tracked down bark-cheeks—clear, viscous, smelling of rain on hot stone.

"*I forgot *you*," she said to Ansel. "*My granddaughter. My Wren's-granddaughter. I forgot you existed. I forgot *me*. I became only... the Gardener.*"

Ansel's knife slipped from her fingers. It landed on root-fiber with a dull thud, unheeded.

She fell to her knees at the raft's edge, heedless of the damp seeping through her trousers. Her hands found the Gardener's—rough bark, cool thorns, the pulse of something that had once been human blood.

"You're not *only* the Gardener," she said fiercely. "You're *her*. You're my grandmother. The woman who wrote a prophecy in her own blood because she loved the world enough to save it. The woman who erased herself so the seal would hold. *You're still in there.*"

The Gardener's green-gold eyes widened. A vine-hand curled around Ansel's, thorns pressing warning-pricks into palm and wrist.

"*Love*," the Gardener breathed. "*You... you still have *love*. For *me*. For *them*." Her gaze flicked past Ansel's shoulder—to Serah, to Odell, to the twins and Hesk standing silent in the tunnel's mouth. "*The crew. The knight. The giant. The healer. You *kept* them. Even after Moon Seven.*"

"I haven't paid Moon Seven yet," Ansel said. "It's not due until the seventh turning. Three moons from now."

"*But you *know* it comes*," the Gardener said. "*And you *know* the cost. The feeling of love. Not the memory—the *feeling*. You will remember *that* you loved them. You will recall every moment, every word, every sacrifice. But you will not *feel* it. It will be... a story told by someone else. A book read in a language you no longer speak.*"

Ansel's breath hitched. *Moon Seven. The price of love.*

She thought of Hesk, dying on the bridge at Greywater Ford, holding the line so they could escape. Of Marisol taking an arrow meant for her. Of Pip selling his honor for medicine, then stealing it back with blood. Of Odell's gruff patience, Serah's quiet treason, Bramble's letter burning in her pocket.

*They are my memories. But the feelings—*

"Is there another way?" she asked. "Wren's torn page. The thirteenth page. It said... it said the seal can be powered by *willing sacrifice of memory instead of bloodline death*. But only if someone the Key loves *anchors* her afterward. Or she becomes empty as the Gardener."

The Gardener's throat worked—a sound like sap rising in spring.

"*The thirteenth page*," she murmured. "*Wren tore it. Hid it. *Hope arriving disguised as a riddle*, she called it. *I only saw two endings because I had no one to imagine a third with.*" The green-gold eyes locked on Ansel's. "*She meant *you*. She meant the crew. She meant... *anchors*. Plural. Not one. *Many*.*"

"Can it work? Multiple anchors?"

The Gardener was silent for a long time. The cavern breathed. The hedge-thorns creaked. Somewhere in the dark, Vaskar turned in his sleep.

"*The hedge requires a *single* anchor*," the Gardener said finally. "*One heart to hold the Key when the memories burn. One love strong enough to pull her back from the void. Wren... Wren had only Tomas. And when the seventh payment took her love for him... there was no anchor left. She *chose* the seal anyway. She *chose* to become me.*"

Her vine-hand tightened on Ansel's.

"*But you*," the Gardener whispered. "*You have *many*. The crew. The knight. The village that remembers your grandmother's name. The smuggler fleets you've guided. The roads you've walked. *All of them*... all of them are threads. If the anchor is not one heart but a *web*...*" She trailed off. The gold flecks in her eyes spun faster. "*The prophecy book. The torn page. The words Wren wrote in her final hour. *Let me read them.*"

Ansel fumbled at her coat, fingers numb. The book came free—leather warm, pages whispering against her thumb. She held it out, both hands, an offering.

The Gardener took it. Her vine-fingers caressed the cover. The thorns did not puncture the leather.

"*Wren's hand*," she murmured. "*Wren's blood. Wren's *sacrifice*." She opened the cover. The pages glowed faintly in the cavern's green light, script shifting and settling as she turned them. "*Moon One... Moon Two... Moon Three...*" She stopped. A page near the end. Blank, save for a single line of handwriting—cramped, desperate, ink bleeding into fiber.

*I only saw two endings because I had no one to imagine a third with.*

The Gardener traced the words with a thorn-tip. Sap welled from the puncture, beading like ruby.

"*She wrote this *after* the sealing*," the Gardener said. "*After she became me. After the payments took everything but the duty. She wrote it *with my hand*. *Her* hand. *Our* hand.*" She looked up at Ansel. "*She *knew*. Even hollowed out. Even empty. Some part of her *knew* there could be a third. She just... couldn't *reach* it. Alone.*"

Ansel's vision blurred. She blinked hard, forcing clarity.

"Then we do it differently," she said. "We do it *together*."

The Gardener's mouth—Wren's mouth—curved. A gesture that might have been a smile, if smiles could grow on bark.

"*Together*," she repeated. "*A word I forgot the meaning of. A century alone, and *together* becomes... a foreign language.*" She closed the book. Pressed it to her moss-robed chest. "*Nine moons, Ansel. Nine payments remaining. Four... Seven... Nine... Eleven... Twelve... Thirteen.*" She counted on vine-fingers. "*Each will take more. Each will hurt more. And at the end... at the end, *you* become *me*. Unless—*"

"Unless the anchor holds," Ansel finished. "Unless they pull me back."

"*Yes.*" The Gardener's voice dropped to a whisper. "*But the anchor must be *willing*. Must *choose* to hold you, knowing what it costs. Knowing they will watch you forget them. Forget *why* they matter. Forget *that* they matter. And still... still hold on.*"

The words settled in the chamber like stones in deep water.

Ansel thought of the crew. Of Serah, who had killed her superior to follow a stranger into treason. Of Odell, who had never asked her name but had given her a bunk and a share and a place at the hearth. Of Pip and Marisol, twins who bickered like breath and bled like kin. Of Hesk, gone now, his massive frame forever rooted at Greywater Ford.

*Would they? Could they? Knowing the cost?*

"They already have," she said. "Every time. Without being asked."

The Gardener studied her face—Wren's face, Ansel's face, the same geometry of jaw and cheek and scarred eyebrow.

"*Then perhaps*," she said slowly, "*the third ending is not impossible. Perhaps the riddle has an answer.*"

She extended the book back to Ansel. As their fingers brushed, a jolt passed through Ansel—not magic, not shock. *Recognition*. A memory that wasn't hers: a woman with Wren's face, younger, softer, standing in a garden bright with impossible flowers, pressing a book into the hands of a figure made of vine and root and saying—

***Wait for her. She will come. She will be *more* than me.***

The vision shattered. Ansel gasped, staggering back a step.

The Gardener watched her, head tilted. "*Did you see?*"

"Yes." Ansel's voice shook. "She—you—*we*—you told yourself to wait. For *me*."

"*For the Thirteenth Key*," the Gardener corrected. "*For the one who would not walk alone. The one who would bring *anchors*." She paused. "*But there is something else you must know. Something Wren wrote on the page *before* the thirteenth. The page the Order suppressed. The page Serah quoted.*"

Ansel froze. "Serah quoted a page?"

"*The knight. Yes. She spoke the words in the cavern above. The page of *Warning*.*" The Gardener's green-gold eyes darkened. "*Wren wrote: *Beware the faithful who would open the door. The god does not wake to conquer. He wakes to *consume*. And those who call him master become his first meal.* *"

A chill crawled down Ansel's spine, unrelated to the cavern's warmth.

"The Order," she said. "Aurelia. The High Bloom. She wants to *open* the prison."

"*She carries his seed*," the Gardener said. "*A whispering fragment of Vaskar's essence, planted in her heart. She believes she controls it. She believes *purification* comes through fire. She is... *wrong*." The word carried centuries of sorrow. "*The Order has been corrupted for generations. The Quiet Bloom was founded to *guard* the seal. Now it tends the *opening*.*"

Serah stepped forward from the tunnel's mouth. Her sword was sheathed, but her hand rested on the pommel. Her face was pale in the green light, eyes fixed on the Gardener.

"You knew," she said. "You knew the Order was corrupted. You knew Aurelia carried the seed. *Why didn't you warn anyone?*"

The Gardener's gaze shifted to Serah. The vine-hair stirred. "*Who would listen to a thing in the dark? A madwoman tending a hedge? The Order *is* the guard. The world *trusts* the Order. And I... I forgot *how* to speak to anyone but the roots. The thorns. The water. *Wren*." She looked back at Ansel. "*I forgot *language*. I forgot *people*. I became only *function*." *

*This is what total sacrifice costs,* Ansel realized. *Not just memory. Not just love. *Humanity*. The ability to reach across the dark and say: *here I am. I see you. Do you see me?* *

She looked at the Gardener—at her grandmother, or what remained of her—and saw the future written in bark and vine and green-gold eyes. *This is what I become. If the anchor fails. If the web breaks. If I pay the thirteenth price alone.*

*But I won't be alone.*

The thought settled in her chest, solid and certain as a keystone.

"Nine moons," Ansel said aloud. "We have nine moons. Three payments before Moon Seven. Five before Moon Thirteen." She met the Gardener's gaze. "Tell me everything. Every payment. Every ritual step. Every way the hedge can be strengthened, the seal reinforced, the *third path* walked."

The Gardener's vine-fingers closed around the prophecy book. The tiny flowers in her hair began to open, one by one, releasing scent into the warm air—jasmine, pine, something like *home*.

"*Everything*," the Gardener agreed. "*But first... first, you must *prune the southern root*. It has pushed through the third seal. I could not hold it alone. I *need* your hands. *Wren's* hands. *Ansel's* hands.*"

Ansel glanced back at the crew. Serah nodded once, sharp and decisive. Odell spat into the water. The twins exchanged a look—Marisol's face pale but set, Pip's jaw tight with something that might have been fear and might have been resolve.

Hesk wasn't there to nod. But Ansel felt his absence like a phantom limb, solid and certain.

"Then let's get to work," she said.

***

The southern root was a nightmare of thorn and sap and malicious intent.

It had punched through the third seal—a woven barrier of root and rune that had held for eighty years—and now snaked through a side cavern like a diseased artery, pulsing with corrupted energy. The thorns here were blackened at the tips, dripping something that smoked on contact with stone. The air around it shimmered with heat, and a
