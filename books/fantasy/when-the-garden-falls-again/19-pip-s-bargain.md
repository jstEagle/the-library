## 19. Pip's Bargain

The apothecary in Hollowfen's Silt Quarter wanted forty crowns for a vial of marrowbloom tincture, and he wanted it in coin, not promises, not favors, not the charming smile Pip had been leaning on since they were nine years old and first learned that the world gave discounts to pretty liars.

"Forty," the apothecary said again, as if repetition might make it cheaper. He was a narrow man with ink-stained fingers and the flat eyes of someone who had watched too many desperate people do arithmetic behind their faces.

Pip did the arithmetic anyway. Sixteen crowns hidden in their boot. Four owed to them by a fishwife who would never pay. A knife worth perhaps eight if the fence was drunk or sentimental. And Marisol upstairs in Odell's cousin's attic, burning with a fever that had climbed every night since the fire at the safehouse, her bandages weeping yellow, her breathing gone thin and whistling, like wind through a cracked shutter.

The physician who'd looked at her—looked being generous; he'd lifted the blanket with two fingers the way you lift something offal-spoiled—had said one word before leaving: *marrowbloom*. Then he'd named his price and left faster than he'd come.

Forty crowns. Pip had stolen things worth more than forty crowns. Pip had *been* things worth more than forty crowns, briefly, to people who paid well for clever hands and quicker mouths. But everything Pip had ever been good at required time, and time was the one thing the fever wasn't selling.

"I'll have it by tomorrow," Pip said. "Hold the vial."

"Hold it against what?" The apothecary shrugged, already turning away. "Someone else will pay full price by morning. Fever season."

*Fever season.* As if it were a festival. As if somewhere in the city people were stringing lanterns for it.

Pip walked out into the Silt Quarter's perpetual brown twilight—smoke from the tanneries hanging over the lanes like a ceiling that had given up—and stood in the street hating everything: the apothecary, the fever, the Order of the Quiet Bloom most of all, but underneath all of it, quietly, honestly, themselves. Because here was the truth Pip kept stepping on like a nail in the dark: Marisol had taken that wound shielding Ansel. Ansel, who had never once let any of them past arm's length until it was too late to matter. And Pip was about to consider doing something unspeakable to save her, which meant that in the ledger Pip kept behind their eyes—the one where every debt and every sin got entered in a neat hand—Ansel's name was already written on this page too.

Love made you stupid. Not romantic-stupid. *Structurally* stupid. It removed load-bearing walls.

Pip was still standing there when the broker found them.

*

They noticed the boots first. Everyone in Hollowfen noticed boots; half the city made its living noticing things. These were wrong for the Silt Quarter—too clean, too well-heeled, the leather oiled a deep black without a scuff on the toe. Nobody walked these streets in unscuffed boots unless they wanted everyone to know they didn't belong.

The wearer stood beneath the sagging awning of a dyer's shop, wrapped in a grey traveling cloak, face shadowed by a mask of pale porcelain—plain, featureless except for two eye-holes, the kind cheap at any carnival. But the posture under the cloak was very still, very straight, and the hands folded at the front were gloved in white kidskin without a stain on them.

"Pip of the crew *Wren's Wake*?" The voice came out level and sexless through the mask. "Captain Odell's crew?"

Pip's hand drifted toward their belt. "Depends who's asking and what for."

"A broker." A slight inclination of the porcelain head. "I buy information. I hear you're a person who sells it."

"I hear a lot of things I don't repeat." Pip let the grin come up, the good one, the one that had talked them out of three arrests and one marriage. "Who told you I sell?"

"The fishwife on Anchor Row. She also mentioned you owe her four crowns."

The grin stayed up but something behind it went cold and careful. This person knew things. Small, stupid, true things—the kind of knowledge that only came from paying attention for longer than an afternoon. "Everyone owes the fishwife. What kind of information?"

"Routes." The masked head tilted. "Roads. Which passes are watched and which aren't. Where certain travelers intend to be, and when."

And there it was, laid out plain as a market stall, if you had ears for it. Pip had grown up in a city that ran on secrets the way mills ran on water; they knew a buyer of *travel intelligence* when they heard one. Ordinarily Pip would have walked away on principle—selling routes was how crews died, whole families of them, asleep in wagons—but ordinarily Pip had sixteen crowns and a sister with a heartbeat like a steady drum.

"Specific traveler?" they asked, testing.

"Perhaps. Perhaps just roads." The white gloves opened slightly, palms up, a gesture of reasonableness. "I'll pay ten crowns for general conditions on the Thornfell high passes. Twenty for anything more... particular."

Twenty crowns plus the sixteen was thirty-six. Four short. Pip's mouth was already shaping the negotiation when the thought arrived, unwelcome as a rat across the floor: *thirty-six is close enough that they'll round up for the right item.*

"What particular thing are you hoping to hear about?" Pip asked lightly.

"A girl." The mask didn't move, but the voice dropped half a tone, going softer, more intimate, the way voices do when they lean in. "Scarred hands. Travels with smugglers. There's a bounty—a private commission, nothing official—and my client pays extremely well for her current route."

The world went very quiet, the way it does in the moment after you've stepped on ice and felt it speak.

Pip thought of Ansel at the waystation fire, turning Wren's torn page over in those scarred hands, reading aloud in a voice gone careful. Thought of her at Greywater Ford, screaming Hesk's name while the bridge held and then didn't. Thought of her sitting up nights outside Marisol's door because she couldn't sit inside it, because she said—only once, only to Pip—that watching Marisol breathe hurt too much to watch directly.

Thought of forty crowns. Of marrowbloom. Of the whistle in Marisol's breath getting thinner every night.

"How much?" Pip heard themselves ask.

"Thirty-five crowns for her road out of Hollowfen. Where she goes next, and by which path." The gloved hands produced a purse—leather, heavy, the sound of it obscene. "Half now. Half on delivery of the information itself. You needn't give me her name. I suspect I already have it."

*I suspect I already have it.* The sentence sat in Pip's chest like swallowed glass.

Here was the trap, and Pip saw it perfectly, saw every tooth of it, the way you see the teeth of a snare exactly one heartbeat too late to matter: this buyer knew too much to be a merchant and asked too little to be honest. No real broker paid thirty-five crowns for a single route. No real broker wore porcelain in the Silt Quarter. Every instinct Pip owned was standing at attention screaming *Order, Order, Order*, and Pip stood there in the tannery smoke and heard Marisol's cracked-shutter breathing, and began, horribly, to negotiate.

"Sixty," Pip said. "Full route, timing, and the pass she means to take. Sixty, half now, and I keep my mouth shut forever after."

The mask regarded them. Underneath the porcelain, Pip could feel the smile—not see it, feel it, the way you feel weather change.

"Fifty," the voice said, "because you bargained, which tells me you're frightened, and frightened people overprice. Half now." The purse arced through the air and Pip caught it on reflex, twenty-five crowns' weight dragging their hand down like a confession. "The rest when I have the route. Tomorrow. The dyer's awning, dusk."

"And if I don't come?"

"Then I'll find another seller. This city is full of people who love someone sick." A pause, delicate as a scalpel finding its depth. "You're simply the nearest one."

*

Pip didn't go back to the safehouse right away. They walked. Down along the silt-flats where the tide stank and the gulls argued, out onto the breakwater where the wind scrubbed some of the smoke out of the air, and there they stood with the purse in their fist like a dead bird and tried to construct a version of events in which they weren't doing what they were doing.

It went like this, the construction: the Order already knew Ansel was in Hollowfen—they'd burned the safehouse, hadn't they? They'd hunted her from Thornfell to Nettlebed to here. So telling them her next road changed nothing, truly, nothing, because they'd learn it anyway from some other bought mouth, some hungrier spy, some frightened soul worse positioned than Pip to lie about what they sold. Whereas the medicine was *now*. The fever was climbing *tonight*. Marisol had woken once in the dark calling for Hesk, calling him like he could still answer, and Pip had held her hand and lied to her with the good smile and then gone into the stairwell and put both fists through the plaster so there'd be a reason for the noise they were making.

That was the construction. It held up about as well as plaster.

Because here was the other entry in the ledger, the one in the honest hand: the Order had followed Ansel's routes before. That was how they'd found the safehouse—someone had sold a road, or a schedule, or a name, and the Bloom had come with torches, and Marisol had thrown herself over a stranger she barely knew and caught a blade meant for the book in Ansel's pack. And now Pip was going to draw the map of the next road with their own clever hands and hand it to the same white gloves, and the arithmetic of it ended in blood no matter how many times Pip moved the numbers around.

*Love makes you vulnerable,* Bramble used to say—he used to say it to Pip specifically, ruffling their hair when they were small and quick-fingered, *to exactly the wrong hands, little bird. Anyone who knows what you love owns a door into you.* Bramble, whose shop had burned for exactly that reason. Bramble, dead for sheltering Ansel before Pip ever met her.

Pip laughed at that, alone on the breakwater, and it came out wrong, and a gull screamed back at them as if offended.

They went back at dusk-dark. Climbed the creaking stair to Odell's cousin's attic with the purse still heavy and unspent, because twenty-five crowns wasn't forty and pride is a luxury the dying can't afford, and pushed open the door.

Marisol lay on the pallet with her color gone to candle-wax, hair stuck to her forehead, the bandage around her ribs spotted through with yellow-brown. But her eyes were open. Open and fixed on Pip with the old sharpness swimming up through the fever like a pike rising through murky water.

"You've been crying," Marisol said. Her voice was sand and wire. "Or punching walls. One of your two solutions."

"Walls," Pip admitted. They sat down on the stool beside the pallet, careful, careful, the way you sit near something you're afraid of breaking. "How are you feeling."

"Lovely. I'm being courted by a fever. It brings flowers. It won't leave." A ghost of her real smile flickered and sank. "Did you get it? The medicine."

"Not yet." Pip smoothed the blanket over her feet without looking up. "Tomorrow. I've got a line on the rest of the coin."

Marisol was quiet long enough that Pip risked looking at her face, and found her watching with that terrible twin-sight, the one that had read Pip's pockets and Pip's heart since before either of them could write. Fever had dimmed it but not drowned it.

"You're going to do something stupid," Marisol said.

"I do something stupid roughly twice a week. Be specific."

"For me." Her hand came out from under the blanket—slow, trembling, hot as a stove-lid—and closed around Pip's wrist. "Whatever it is. Don't."

"It's medicine, Mari. It's a vial and a spoon and you drinking it and complaining about the taste. That's all this is."

"That's not what your face says."

"My face says lots of professional things. My face is a tradesman." Pip squeezed her fingers, gently, gently, feeling how much strength the grip had lost, feeling the exact width of the gap between the sister they'd crossed half the world with and the wax figure on the pallet. "Sleep. I'll be back tomorrow with your horrible flower-courting tincture and you can insult my bargaining."

Marisol's eyes stayed on theirs a moment longer. Then the fever pulled her back down, and her hand slid off Pip's wrist, and her breath went thin and whistling, wind through a cracked shutter, counting down.

Pip sat in the dark of the attic until the moon moved, doing the arithmetic one last time, moving the numbers around one last time, arriving at the same sum.

Then they took out a scrap of paper and a stub of charcoal, and wrote down the route. Ansel's route. The Serpent's Road pass, the timing, the waystation, every detail they'd heard around the crew's own fire, in Ansel's own voice, planning the road home to the Garden. Their hand shook so badly the letters came out crooked, like a child's, like a liar's.

They folded it small enough to hide in a palm.

*

Dusk again. The dyer's awning dripped its slow brown drips. The masked figure stood precisely where it had stood the day before, unmoving, patient as furniture, and when Pip approached across the mud it inclined the porcelain head with something almost like warmth.

"You came," the voice said. "I'm pleased. Most sellers find excuses."

"Most sellers have spines." Pip held out the folded paper without letting go of one corner. "Payment first."

The white glove extended the second purse. For a moment they stood there, each holding what the other wanted, the exchange balanced on the wet air between them—and Pip, even now, even drowning, even ashamed down to the marrow, noticed things. Noticed that the glove's cuff bore a thread of green embroidery, fine as spider silk, worked in a pattern of five-petaled flowers. Noticed that beneath the cloak's hem, the boot-toes pointed with military precision. Noticed that the mask's porcelain had, at the left cheek, a faint seam—removable, fitted, made by craftsmen who supplied institutions rather than carnivals.

The Quiet Bloom. Five-petaled. Green thread. Pip had seen that stitching once before, on a dead knight's collar at Greywater Ford.

"You're them," Pip said. The paper trembled in their grip. "You're the Order."

"I am a servant of the Bloom," the voice agreed, mild as milk. "As I suspect you knew yesterday. As I suspect you decided didn't matter." The mask tilted. "Don't look so stricken. You sold a road, not a life. Roads are only roads. What happens on them is other people's business."

"Is that what you tell yourself?"

"No." The purse dropped into Pip's numb hand, fifty crowns' weight, heavier than any cargo they'd ever carried. "I don't tell myself anything. Take your money, little bird. Buy your medicine. Love whoever you love, quickly, while it's cheap."

Pip let go of the paper. Watched it cross the space between them like something molting—no longer information, no longer theirs, already becoming consequence. The gloved hand received it, unfolded it with a connoisseur's care, and read.

"Serpent's Road," the voice murmured. "Ambitious. She always was."

The mask came up sharply, and for the first time the voice beneath it lost its level calm, and Pip understood in one cold drop that this agent had known Ansel before—known her, maybe, the way hunters know the ones that get away—and that the hunt had just become personal.

But that wasn't the hook that stopped Pip's heart in the muddy street. That came a breath later, when the agent turned away, then paused, and spoke without turning back:

"Oh—and a courtesy, free of charge. Your captain should check his crew manifest. He has a leak besides you. Someone aboard the *Wren's Wake* has been writing letters to the Bloom since Nettlebed." A pause, delicate as a blade being sheathed. "Do give my regards to your sister. I do hope the tincture works."

Porcelain and grey wool moved off down the lane, unhurried, unscuffed, and vanished into the smoke, and Pip stood alone in the dripping brown light holding fifty crowns and the beginning of a new and much worse question:

*Who on their own crew had sold them all?*
