## 20. Ambush at the Ford

**Hesk**

The river spoke to Hesk before anything else did.

He had grown up on water—mudflats and eel-weirs, a father who smelled of brine and cheap pipe-smoke—and he had learned young that rivers told you things if you were patient enough to listen. The Greywater was telling him something now. It came down from the high passes swollen with snowmelt, brown-green and muscular, and where it should have run smooth over the ford's stone shelf it stuttered. Broke against something that wasn't stone.

Bodies. Men standing in the shallows upstream of the crossing, still as herons.

"Hold," Hesk said quietly, and raised one enormous hand.

Behind him the wagon creaked to a stop. He heard Odell swear under her breath—a short, bitten-off word—and the twins go silent in a way that was worse than noise. Pip and Marisol could chatter through a tax audit, through a funeral, through their own hangings; silence from them meant they'd seen it too.

And Ansel. Ansel came up beside him without being called, which she never did, which she had only started doing in the last few days, and stood at his elbow looking down the long grey slope of the riverbank with those flat, remembering eyes.

"Twelve," she said. "Maybe fourteen. Two archers in the alder stand on the far bank. They've been there since before dawn—the reeds are bent wrong."

"How can you possibly—" Marisol began.

"The birds." Ansel nodded at the far bank, where a pair of wagtails worked the mud with nervous little hops, refusing to go anywhere near the alders. "Birds remember who's been standing in one place too long."

Hesk looked at her—at this small, sharp woman he had carried across three countries without ever once asking why she kept herself folded up like a letter no one was meant to read—and felt the old warm ache in his chest, the one he'd stopped trying to name. She had let them close. Not all the way, not yet, but she had slept inside the ring of their fire two nights running instead of out in the dark beyond it, and she had laughed once, actually laughed, at one of Pip's terrible impressions of Odell, and Hesk had thought: *there she is. There she's been all along.*

"We go around?" Odell asked, coming forward. Her voice was all business, but Hesk had known her twenty years and heard the calculation underneath: the ford was the only crossing for thirty miles, and Marisol's wound had gone hot again, and around meant two days over the fells that Marisol did not have in her.

"They're not here for us to go around," Ansel said. "They're here because someone sold my route."

Nobody looked at Pip. That was the cruelest part, Hesk thought later. Nobody looked at him, and Pip sat very straight on the wagon bench with his hands white-knuckled on the rail, and said nothing, and the nothing was louder than any accusation.

"There's the old weir path," Hesk said. "Half a mile down. It floods this time of year, but—" He stopped. He listened to the river again, properly, the way his father had taught him, reading the water's grammar. "No. It's flooded already. We'd lose the wagon and Marisol with it."

"So we cross," Odell said.

"So we cross," Hesk agreed. "I'll go first."

Odell turned and looked up at him. She was half his size and had never once treated him as though he were made of anything softer than she was, and in that moment her eyes said everything her rank and her pride would not allow her voice to say.

"You'll go first," she repeated. "And we'll be right behind you."

"Aye," said Hesk, and smiled his slow smile, the one that made children in six port towns stop crying. "Right behind me."

**Serah**

Serah Ivo knelt in the alder stand and watched the smugglers' wagon come down the hill, and hated every second of what she was about to do.

Fourteen Bloom knights in the shallows and along the banks, chosen from the quiet ones, the ones who didn't ask questions. Her orders were precise: take the book, take the girl, kill anyone who stood in the way. *Persuade first,* High Bloom Aurelia had written, in that beautiful looping hand that Serah had once admired the way other people admired scripture. *But do not let sentiment delay you. The thirteenth moon will not wait.*

Sentiment. As if Serah's problem were sentiment.

She watched the big one—Hesk, the crew called him, though she'd learned that from informants' reports rather than any right of hers—walk out into the ford alone. He moved like a man wading into his own bathwater, unhurried, massive, carrying an iron-shod quarterstaff taller than most men. The reports called him gentle. The reports said he'd once carried a drowning child nine miles through a blizzard.

*Kill anyone who stands in the way.*

"Knight-Captain." Brother Callow crouched beside her, young and eager, his bow already strung. "On your word."

Serah looked at the wagon. At the small figure climbing down from it—Ansel Amberdale, or whatever name she wore now, moving with that uncanny economy, checking the wind, checking the light, checking her people. Because that was what Serah had finally understood, watching her these past weeks through scrying-glass and informant's eye: the girl checked her *people*. The famous isolation, the arm's-length living—all of it was a wall built around something, and the something was them. The gruff captain. The twins. The giant walking into the water.

Wren Amberdale had seen only two endings, the archives said. Serah was beginning to understand why. A woman alone sees two endings. A woman with people to imagine alongside—

"On your word, Knight-Captain," Callow pressed.

"Not yet." Serah's mouth was dry. "Let them commit to the crossing."

It was a soldier's reason. It was even a true one. But underneath it another truth sat like a stone in her gut: every hour she delayed, every order she softened, every report she'd filed slightly wrong since the cliff road—that was treason accreting, layer by layer, and some part of her had known for weeks that she was not going to come back from it.

The big man reached the middle of the ford. The water broke white around his thighs. And he stopped, and turned, and looked directly at the alder stand—as if the river itself had whispered him her position—and called out in a voice like a landslide given manners:

"I know you're there! Twelve of you, maybe more! These folk are just passing through!"

Callow shifted. "Shall I—"

"No."

"Then they're passing through *with her*," Hesk called on, "and with the book, and we'd take it kindly if you'd find another ford!"

Serah closed her eyes briefly. *Oh, you magnificent fool. You're giving us time to aim.*

"Take them," she said. "Archers first. *Disable*, damn you—disable—"

But the Order of the Quiet Bloom had not been trained to disable in a hundred years, and the first arrow left the alder stand before her second word was finished.

* * *

**Pip**

Pip had sold them.

He kept trying to make the thought smaller. *Not sold, exactly. Leased. One route, one date, one broker with a soft voice and a masked face, and Marisol burning up with fever and the physicker in Hollowfen wanting coin Pip didn't have.* He had done the arithmetic the way he did all arithmetic—fast, clever, and certain that cleverness would cover the difference. It hadn't. The masked broker had been the Order. Of course the masked broker had been the Order. Pip had always been able to con anyone except himself.

Now the arrows came out of the alders like a flock of black birds, and the world went loud.

"Down! DOWN!" Odell's roar, and the wagon lurching as she hauled the reins, and Marisol falling against him with a cry, her bandage blooming red at the shoulder. Pip got his body over hers, small and useless, thinking with insane clarity: *I built this. Every arrow is mine. I paid for these.*

"Hesk!" Ansel's voice, cracking across the water. "HESK!"

Pip lifted his head in time to see the giant turn—not toward the arrows, but toward them, toward the wagon, toward the family that had never once asked him to be anything but exactly what he was—and raise that impossible staff, and step between the ford and everything Pip loved.

The second volley hit him.

Three shafts, maybe four. Pip saw them strike and saw Hesk barely rock, saw him snap the nearest arrowshaft with a flex of his shoulder as if it were a straw, and begin to walk *upstream*, toward the hidden line of knights in the shallows, swinging the staff in great scything arcs that turned the Greywater to spray and sent two armored men spinning downstream like dolls.

"He's clearing the crossing," Ansel breathed beside Pip. She had appeared at the wagon's flank without seeming to move, knife out, eyes wide and wet and blazing. "He's—he's clearing it for us. GO. ODELL, GO NOW."

The wagon plunged into the ford. Water crashed over the wheels. Arrows hissed past Pip's ear close enough to part his hair, and he held Marisol's head down against the boards and screamed wordlessly, and somewhere in the screaming he found words after all:

"HESK! You great beautiful idiot, you COME WITH US—"

Hesk glanced back. Just once. Just long enough.

What was in his face, Pip would carry for the rest of his life. Not fear. Not even resolve, exactly. It was the look of a man confirming that everyone he loved was behind him and safe, and finding it so, and being satisfied. It was the look he gave the crew every hearth-night, sitting at the edge of the firelight because the benches weren't built for him, content just to watch them all be together.

"Go on, little brother," Hesk said.

Then the knights reached him—six of them, eight, closing from both banks—and the ford became a churn of white water and steel, and Pip lost sight of him, and lost sight, and lost sight.

* * *

**Ansel**

Later, Ansel would not be able to say how much of the crossing she remembered. The mind protected itself; she knew that better than anyone alive. What remained was fragments, sharp as glass:

Odell standing in the wagon box with a hand-axe in each fist, deflecting an arrow off one blade, roaring curses in three languages.

Marisol, grey-faced and fevered, pressing her good hand flat against Pip's chest and saying, "Whatever you did, we'll fix it, you absolute disaster, we'll *fix it*"—and Pip making a sound Ansel had never heard a human being make.

The far bank rising to meet them. Mud. Alder roots. Safety shaped like a mistake.

And Hesk.

She looked back from the trees because some traitor part of her needed to see, and she saw him kneeling in the shallows with four arrows in him and a knight's sword through his side, and she saw him *rise*. She saw him take the last two knights down with him—one crushed beneath his falling weight, one flung so hard into the current that the river took him like a toy. She saw the staff float free, turning slowly in the eddy, and Hesk himself go down onto his knees in the brown water with his head bowed, as if in prayer, as if in rest.

As if he had simply finished the work and set it down.

"*No.*"

Ansel was running before she knew it. Back down the bank, into the water, the cold hitting her like a wall—Odell shouting her name, arms grabbing her, and she fought them, she fought Odell with a strength she didn't know she had, because Hesk was fifty feet away and dying and she had spent three years learning how to leave people before they could matter and it had *not worked*, none of it had worked, they had gotten in anyway, all of them, and now the price of letting them in was standing knee-deep in a cold grey river and refusing to let her reach him.

"Ansel." Odell's voice, right against her ear, rough as gravel. "Ansel. Look at me. Look at me. More are coming—we have minutes. *Minutes.*"

"He's—" Her voice broke apart. "He's still—"

"I know." And Odell's own voice cracked on it, one hairline fracture in twenty years of iron, and that broke Ansel more than the river, more than the arrows. "I know, girl. I know."

Across the water, in the alder stand, a figure in Bloom-white stepped clear of the trees. Even at distance Ansel knew the bearing: Knight-Captain Serah Ivo, the woman who had let her go on the cliff road, standing motionless above the place where Hesk knelt in the current. For a long moment neither moved. Then Serah raised one hand—a sharp, cutting gesture—and the remaining knights withdrew upstream, vanishing into the trees, leaving the ford to the dead and the river.

Leaving Hesk to them.

Ansel stopped fighting. She stood in Odell's arms in the freezing water and watched the current tug gently at the biggest, gentlest thing she had ever known, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade sliding between her ribs, exactly what she had done. Not Pip's betrayal—Pip's betrayal was its own country, and they would cross it later, in pain. This was hers. She had spent three years perfecting the art of belonging to no one, and the moment she had finally, finally admitted she belonged to *them*—the Order had come to collect the interest.

Every person who loved her was a door the enemy could kick in. Bramble's shop. Marisol's shoulder. Now Hesk, floating.

*I should run,* she thought. *Run tonight, run alone, run until no one is left to bleed for me.*

And then she heard Pip, upstream on the bank, kneeling in the mud with Marisol's head in his lap, saying over and over, "Come on, big man, come on"—and Marisol whispering, feverish, "he held the bridge, he *held* it"—and Odell's arms around her, shaking, the captain who never shook.

Running was what Wren had done. Running was the pattern, the family tradition, the self-erasure passed down like a loom. Wren had seen two endings because she'd had no one to imagine a third with—but Ansel had them. She had a gruff captain holding her up in a river, a pair of twins broken open on the bank, a defected knight calling off her hunters, and a giant's staff turning slowly in the eddy like a compass needle pointing home.

They had killed Hesk to get to her. So she would finish what Hesk died protecting, and she would do it surrounded by every person the Order thought was a weakness, and she would make each one of them a weapon instead.

"Odell," she said. Her voice came out low and steady and strange to her own ears. "We're going to the Garden. All of us. Together."

Odell pulled back and looked at her—this soaked, shaking, transformed woman—and something passed between them that needed no words, the way it passes between soldiers, between sisters, between people who have just buried one of their own in cold running water.

"Aye," Odell said. "All of us."

Upstream, unnoticed by any of them, the current finally turned Hesk's body gently toward the far bank, and laid it among the reeds with a tenderness the Order had never managed—as if the river, at least, had been listening when he asked it to hold the crossing a little while longer.

And in Ansel's coat pocket, against her heart, the prophecy book grew suddenly, unmistakably warm.
