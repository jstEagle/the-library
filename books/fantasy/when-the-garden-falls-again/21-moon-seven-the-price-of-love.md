## 21. Moon Seven: The Price of Love

The seventh moon came up over Hollowfen like a coin dropped in dirty water—orange at the rim, smeared with smoke from the city's ten thousand chimneys—and Ansel watched it rise through the cracked shutters of the safehouse on Tanner's Row and felt the book grow warm against her ribs.

She had taken to carrying it strapped beneath her coat, against her skin, because she could no longer bear to be more than an arm's length from it. Not out of devotion. Out of accounting. The book was a ledger now, and every moon-turn it collected a debt, and she had learned the hard way that debts you cannot see are the ones that ruin you.

Six payments made. Six holes in her.

She kept a list of them, written in her own hand in a cheap notebook she'd bought from a stationer who asked no questions: *Mother's face — gone. The name Amberdale — gone. Thornfell, the shape of the mountains — gone.* Three more entries below those, smaller losses, stranger ones: *the smell of rain on the road to Nettlebed; the sound of my own voice at nine years old; the way Bramble said my name when I was small.* She could read about them. She could know they had existed, the way you know a country exists that you've never visited. But knowing was not remembering, and the difference between the two was a wound that never quite scabbed.

Tonight the book was warm, and warm meant due.

Ansel climbed down from the shuttered window and looked around the safehouse kitchen, where the crew had gathered for no particular reason except that it was cold and Pip had stolen a wheel of cheese from somewhere and Hesk was trying to teach Marisol a drinking song with only half its verses intact. Odell sat by the hearth mending a net that hadn't seen water in months, purely because his hands needed occupation. Serah stood apart as always, armor off but sword within reach, watching the door.

They were laughing. All of them, even Serah, whose laugh was more of a controlled exhalation, but still—it counted. They were laughing in the firelight, and Ansel felt the book pulse once against her sternum like a second heart, patient and hungry, and she understood with perfect clarity what the seventh payment would take.

Not a face this time. Not a name, not a mountain range, not a smell.

The feeling itself.

*What Memory Costs*, the book had titled its cruelest chapter, and the seventh entry was explicit in the way of all its cruelties: *On the seventh turn, the Key surrenders love, for a seal built on memory alone will crack, but a seal built on renounced love holds fast.* It didn't say which love. But Ansel had done the arithmetic six times now, and the book always took the heaviest thing she carried, and there was nothing in her heavier than these five people and the knight who had become a sixth without anyone voting on it.

"Ansel." Marisol had stopped singing. Her arm was still bound from the fire at the safehouse, slung in a scarf Pip had dyed an aggressive yellow, and she tilted her head the way she did when she'd caught someone lying. "You've got your ledger face on."

"My what?"

"That face," Pip said, pointing with a cheese knife. "The one where you're doing sums nobody else can see."

"I'm always doing sums."

"Yeah," Marisol said softly, "but usually you win them."

Ansel almost smiled. Then she went upstairs, took paper and ink from her pack, and began the most important work of her life.

* * *

She wrote until the candle burned down to a puddle and she had to light another, and then another after that.

Not a journal. Journals were reflections, and reflection required distance, and she had no distance left. What she wrote was closer to cartography—a map of her own heart drawn by someone who knew the surveyor would be robbed on the road and the map was the only thing that would survive.

*Odell,* she wrote. *Captain. Sixty if he's a day, hands like tree bark, pretends he hired me for my routes but really hired me because I reminded him of his daughter, though he has never once admitted this and will deny it under torture. He hums when he thinks no one is listening. The song is the one his daughter used to sing. When Hesk died at Greywater Ford he didn't cry at the funeral; he went back to the bridge and stood there until dawn, and when he came back he said "we move at noon" and that was how I knew he was broken, because Odell is never punctual.*

*Pip. Smallest, loudest, sold my route to the Order to buy medicine for Marisol and then crawled bleeding through their camp to steal the ritual focus back, so let the record show that betrayal and redemption can live in the same body and neither one cancels the other. Pip talks constantly because silence is where guilt lives. Pip braided my hair once on the downriver run and I pretended to hate it and I did not hate it.*

*Marisol. Took a burning beam across the shoulders shielding me from the collapse at the safehouse and has never once made me feel like a debt. She is the one who decided I belong to this crew before any of us agreed to it. She decides things. That is simply what happens around Marisol; you find yourself already decided.*

*Hesk.* Her pen stopped here for a long time. Ink pooled and spread into a small black continent. *Hesk died holding Greywater Ford so we could live. He was gentle the way bridges are gentle—they just hold you up and ask nothing. I loved him. I am writing this so that tomorrow, when I don't, I will at least know the shape of what I lost.*

*Serah Ivo. Knight-Captain of the Order of the Quiet Bloom, traitor to it now, killer of her own superior, burner of Bramble's shop—she confessed this at the ford crossing and I forgave her, or rather I chose forgiveness and am waiting for the feeling to catch up, which is maybe the same thing. She reads the thirteenth page aloud every night. She stands watch outside my door without being asked. She is learning to be a person instead of an oath, and I am—*

Ansel paused. Looked at the word she was about to write. Wrote it anyway.

*—I am learning to be loved instead of invisible.*

Then she turned to fresh pages and did the harder work. Not facts—facts were armor, and the book had shown it could cut through armor. Feelings. She wrote the feeling of the crew's laughter through the floorboards. She wrote what it did to her chest, described it the way she'd describe a smuggler's route to someone who'd never walked it: *left at the sternum, down past the old fear, opens onto something warm and terrifyingly large.* She wrote about the night at the waystation when knives were drawn and she'd shared Wren's torn page and the whole room had chosen, together, not to kill each other—that moment had felt like the first true thing in three years of careful falseness. She wrote it all, every angle, every detail, until her hand cramped and the candle stubs lay around her like spent soldiers.

Because here was the thing she had figured out, hunched over the paper while the moon climbed: the book took memories. It reached into her head and unmade what lived there. But ink on paper was not in her head. Ink on paper was in the world. The book could empty the house of her, but it could not burn down the neighbor's house too—or so she gambled. So she hoped, fiercely, furiously, with the same ingenuity she'd once used to walk contraband through a mountain pass ahead of a toll patrol.

If the price of love was love, then she would pay it—but she'd leave herself instructions for buying it back.

Near dawn she heard the stair creak and didn't turn. Only one person in the house moved that quietly and still wanted to be heard.

"You've been writing all night," Serah said.

"Yes."

"The moon turns tonight."

"I know what night it is."

Serah came into the candlelight. Without her armor she looked younger and more tired, gray threading her cropped hair, a scar along her jaw that Ansel had catalogued weeks ago and never asked about. She looked at the pages spread across the table—at the dense, cramped handwriting covering every inch—and Ansel watched her understand it, watch the comprehension move through her face like weather.

"You're writing yourself down," Serah said. "Before it takes you."

"I'm writing *you* down. All of you. If it takes how I feel, fine. Let it take it. But I'll have the record." Ansel stacked the pages, squared their edges with a violence that surprised her. "Wren saw two endings because she couldn't imagine a third. Maybe I can't either. But I can imagine a loophole."

"It might not work." Serah said it gently, which was worse. "The payments have taken things you'd hidden. Your mother's face—you'd tried to preserve that too, hadn't you? Drawn it?"

Ansel's jaw tightened. She had. Forty drawings, and by morning they'd been portraits of a stranger.

"This is different," she said. "A face is a thing you remember. This—" she pressed her palm flat against the stack of pages, "—this is a thing I *know*. There's a difference. A face lives in the blood. Knowing lives in the bones. It can take the blood. I don't think it can take the bones."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then tomorrow I'll be a woman reading letters from a stranger about people she supposedly loved." Ansel met the knight's eyes. "And you'll read them to me. Every night. Until it stops being words and starts being true again. That's the third ending, Serah. Not escaping the price. Making someone hold the receipt."

For a long moment Serah said nothing. Then she pulled the room's only other chair to the table, sat, and picked up a pen.

"Then I'll write too," she said. "In case it takes more than you planned. Two records are harder to lose than one." She drew a fresh sheet toward her, and her hand—the hand that had killed men for the Quiet Bloom, the hand that had signed false reports and true confessions—hesitated above the paper. "Where do I start?"

"Start with why you stayed," Ansel said. "When you caught me on the cliff road and let me go. Start there. I want to know what that felt like from the inside, because I only ever saw it from the outside, and tomorrow the outside won't mean anything to me."

* * *

The moon-turn came at midnight, and it did not come quietly.

Previous payments had been whispers, a warmth behind the eyes, a soft unwinding. The seventh arrived like a tide going out—all at once, everywhere, dragging.

Ansel was sitting upright in bed with the notebook open on her knees when it hit. She felt it first as coldness in her hands, then as a strange double vision: the pages in front of her, and beneath the pages, a vast receding, like standing on a shore and watching the sea pull back farther than the sea should ever go. The crew's laughter from downstairs stretched thin and changed key. The warmth she'd mapped on paper—*left at the sternum, down past the old fear*—drained out of her body along some channel she couldn't name, and she gripped the notebook so hard the cover bent, and she thought, with the last coherent part of herself: *read, read while you still want to—*

She read. Or her eyes moved over the words. *Odell hums when he thinks no one is listening.* The sentence was true. She could verify it was true the way she could verify that Hollowfen paid taxes to a duke she'd never seen. True, distant, irrelevant.

*Hesk died holding Greywater Ford.*

True. Irrelevant. A line in a report about strangers.

Something in her was screaming—not grief, grief was already gone, but the animal knowledge that something was being taken, the way a house screams in a storm even after everything inside it has been carried away. She held onto that scream. It was hers. It was the only thing left in the room that was hers.

Then the tide finished going out, and the scream stopped, and Ansel Amberdale—who was not called anything, who had no name she remembered—sat in a cold room holding a notebook full of claims she could not confirm, and felt absolutely nothing about the five people laughing downstairs.

She tested it, the way you test a numb limb. *Marisol shielded me from a burning beam.* Nothing. *Pip betrayed me and then bled for me.* Nothing. *Serah reads to me every night.* Nothing—only a faint, baffled awareness that the armored woman down the hall had been crying earlier, and that Ansel had noticed, and that noticing had cost her nothing at all.

Nothing. Nothing, nothing, nothing. The absence was enormous. It had a shape. It was shaped exactly like everyone she had ever—

No. Not everyone. She had no everyone.

Downstairs, someone laughed again. Ansel looked at the sound the way she'd look at weather in a country she was passing through.

Then she looked down at the notebook, at her own handwriting, at the sentence she'd written in the smallest hours: *I am learning to be loved instead of invisible.*

She didn't feel it. But she could read it. And reading it, she found—buried under the nothing, small as an ember under ash—one single stubborn coal of objection. Not a feeling. A fact-shaped splinter: *this handwriting is mine, and I do not write things that aren't true.*

It wasn't love. It was barely curiosity. But it was a foothold, and Ansel Winterbourne—no, that name was gone too, whoever she was—had built a life on footholds.

She went downstairs.

* * *

The kitchen went quiet when she entered, the way rooms go quiet around something fragile. Five faces turned toward her. Odell's needle paused mid-stitch. Pip stopped chewing. Marisol's eyes narrowed—not with suspicion but with the terrible tenderness of someone who has seen this before, after the fourth payment, after the fifth.

"Well?" Odell said. His voice was rougher than usual. "How bad?"

Ansel held up the notebook. "According to this document, which appears to be in my handwriting, I love all of you very much." She heard her own voice—flat, precise, a courier delivering someone else's letter. "I'm afraid I can't corroborate it. But I've read it twice, and I don't think I lie in writing. It's inefficient."

Nobody laughed. That was the worst part—she could see, from the outside, the exact place where a joke should have landed, see the crew flinch around the space where her humor used to be, and she registered their pain as data and felt nothing, and hated—no. Did not hate. Could not hate. *Would have hated*, the notebook said, *and hatred is also a form of caring*, and she clung to the grammar of it.

"Read it to us," Marisol said quietly. "What you wrote. All of it. We'll sit here and you'll read it, and if any of it's wrong, we'll correct you."

"It won't be wrong."

"Read it anyway."

So Ansel sat down at the kitchen table among people she did not love, in a house that was not home, under a moon that had taken its price, and she opened the notebook and began to read her own heart aloud in a clear, level voice—*Odell, captain, sixty if he's a day*—while Odell put down his netting needle and closed his eyes, and Pip cried silently into the cheese cloth, and Marisol listened with her chin up like a soldier receiving orders, and Serah stood by the door with her hand pressed flat against the wall as if the room might otherwise tip over.

And when Ansel reached the last page—*I am learning to be loved instead of invisible*—she paused, and looked up, and said:

"There's an addendum. In different ink. Someone else's handwriting." She frowned at it. "It says: *'Read this to her every night. Sign nothing. Leave nothing blank. Love is a route, and routes can be walked twice.'*"

"That's mine," Serah said. "I wrote that."

"It's good advice," Ansel said, and did not understand why the knight's face crumpled, and did not understand why she herself, feeling nothing whatsoever, had to grip the edge of the table until her knuckles went white—as if some part of her, deeper than the book could reach, deeper than bone, deeper than ink, was objecting to the theft with the only weapon it had left:

Presence. She was still here. Whatever they took, she kept waking up in the room.

Later, when the others had gone to bed, Serah remained at the table across from her, and Ansel—because the notebook instructed her that this woman could be trusted, and because instruction was all she had—pushed Wren's torn thirteenth page across the wood between them.

"I can't make sense of this anymore," she said. "Whatever I understood about it, the book took that too, or I never understood it. But you've been reading it aloud for weeks. You know it better than I do now." She tapped the fragile parchment, the hedge-witch's final riddle. "So tell me, Knight-Captain. Tell me what my grandmother figured out that the Order killed her for knowing. Because according to this notebook, we're running out of moons—and I'd like to know what we're walking toward before it takes the next thing."

Serah drew the page into the candlelight, and her eyes moved over Wren's faded script, and slowly, terribly, she began to smile.
