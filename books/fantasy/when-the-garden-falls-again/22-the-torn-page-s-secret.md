## 22. The Torn Page's Secret

The safehouse smelled of tallow smoke and wet wool, and the candle had burned down to a thumb's width of light when Ansel finally understood what her grandmother had been trying to say.

She had the thirteenth page flat on the table before her, weighted at the corners with four river stones Pip had collected for luck. Around it sat everything else: the prophecy book itself, its remaining pages gone dark and quiet since Moon Nine; Wren's note in its careful hedge-witch hand; three nights of Serah's cipher work; and Ansel's own marginalia, dense as moss, written in the shorthand smugglers used for routes that must never fall into another crew's hands.

The page was written in two hands. That was the thing she'd kept missing.

The first hand was Wren's—no, not Wren's. She looked again, tilting the page toward the candle so the light raked across the fibers. The older script beneath was cramped and formal, the letters of someone trained by the Order's own scriptoria, and it covered most of the page in tight columns: *the seal drinks what is freely given; blood is given but memory is truer; a life ends the working, a mind unmade sustains it.* And threaded through those columns, between the lines, above and below them like ivy through a fence, ran Wren's additions—smaller, looser, urgent, the writing of someone amending a text she hadn't been allowed to amend anywhere official.

"Say it again," Serah said from across the table. She had her boots off, which Ansel had learned meant she intended to stay until dawn. "The middle part. The part you keep reading and then going quiet about."

Ansel read it aloud. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "*The prison is a door that eats. Feed it a death and it closes for a hundred years. Feed it a remembering—a whole remembered life, surrendered willingly—and it closes for as long as the surrenderer endures. But the surrenderer must be held. One who loves her must hold her name against the dark, or she will empty past all returning, and become as the Gardener became.*"

Silence. Somewhere outside, the winter wind worried the loose shutter, and Marisol turned over in her cot with a small hurt sound, and Hesk's absence filled the room the way it always did now—not a silence where he'd been but a silence shaped exactly like him.

"That's not an ending," Serah said slowly. "That's a sentence."

"It's a sentence without a term." Ansel pressed her fingertips to the old Order script. "Wren sealed Vaskar a century ago. The book said bloodline death—that's what I assumed happened to her, why there were no records, no grave, nothing. But she didn't die, did she? She did *this*. She gave her memories instead of her life, and someone anchored her—" Ansel stopped. The thought arrived whole and terrible, the way true things do. "Or no one did. Or whoever was supposed to hold her name failed, or died, or never knew they were supposed to."

"The Gardener," Serah said.

"A fragment of her magic, animate for a hundred years, half-mad with loneliness, calling me 'Wren' because the last face it saw was hers." Ansel's throat tightened. "It isn't just foreshadowing. It's a *specimen*. That's what happens to the Key when the seal takes everything and nobody holds on to her afterward. You don't die. You just... pour out. You keep walking around with nothing in you. A hundred years of tending a hedge and waiting for a girl with your own scarred hands to walk out of the storm."

Serah was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her knight's voice had roughened into something more human. "And if someone does hold her?"

"Then she lives." Ansel traced Wren's last line, the one written large and slightly crooked, as though the pen had been moving fast: *I only saw two endings because I had no one to imagine a third with.* "She lives empty. Everything gone—every road, every name, every face, every reason. And then the people who love her spend whatever time it takes teaching her to be a person again. Teaching her why any of it mattered." Ansel looked up. "That's the third path. Not seal or shatter. Seal, and survive it—but only if I'm not alone on the other side."

* *

Serah Ivo had taken vows at fourteen, and in eleven years she had learned that the hardest discipline was not the swordwork or the fasting or the vigils in the cold. It was sitting still while hope moved through a room, because hope was the Order's first enemy and her training screamed at her to smother it wherever it kindled.

She sat still now. Across the table, Ansel Amberdale—*Amberdale*, the name the crew had begun repeating to her nightly like a litany, *your name is Ansel Amberdale, we'll keep it for you*—was looking at the torn page as if it might look back.

Hope moved through the room. Serah let it.

But her training also made her read documents differently than smugglers did, and something in the old Order script was bothering her. She reached across and turned the page a quarter-turn, angling it into the light.

"Look at the margins here. The original text—the scriptorium hand—it has erasure marks. Palimpsest work. Someone scraped away lines before Wren ever wrote over them." She pulled the little knife from her boot and, with Ansel's wary nod, eased the blade edge along the parchment at an angle to catch the low light. Ghost-letters rose in the raked glow, shallow grooves where ink had been lifted. "This wasn't a prophecy composed for the Order's archive. It was a prophecy *edited* for it. Whoever wrote the original version wrote more than we're seeing."

"Can you recover it?"

"Some." Serah worked methodically, the way she'd been taught to interrogate captured correspondence, feeling obscurely guilty about turning interrogation craft on holy text. Groove by groove, the ghost-writing surfaced: fragments, then phrases, then—near the bottom of the page, where the scraping had been harshest—two full lines.

She read them once silently and felt the floor tilt under her certainty.

"What?" Ansel said. "Serah. What does it say?"

Serah set down the knife. She found that her hands wanted to shake and overrode them; there would be time to shake later, alone, or perhaps never. "It says the anchoring isn't optional. In the original. Listen." She read aloud, translating the formal scriptorium cadence back into plain speech:

"*Let it be recorded plainly, that no Key after me should learn this too late: the seal will accept the surrender of memory only from a heart that holds love. If the Key surrenders alone—if she comes to the door having cut herself off from every hand that might anchor her—the door will refuse her sacrifice and take her blood instead. Isolation is not protection. Isolation is how the door chooses.*"

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

"That's why the Order wants me dead before the thirteenth moon," Ansel said at last, very quietly. "Not because either ending serves them equally. Because they need me to arrive *alone*. They've spent three years—longer—making sure I had no one. The Quiet Bloom, the hunters, burning Bramble—" her voice snagged on his name, went on rawer, "—every person who ever got close to me, driven off or killed, so that when I stand at that door, I'll have nothing to offer but my life. So the door will take the death. So the hundred-year clock starts again, and the Order keeps its purpose, its power, its war."

"And the High Bloom goes further," Serah said. "Aurelia doesn't want the clock restarted. She wants the door opened. But even that requires you isolated—willing, or breakable." She met Ansel's eyes across the candle. "Every path they've built for you runs through loneliness. The seal-or-shatter choice, the death, the opening—all of it assumes you come to the Garden the way your grandmother's enemies designed: with no one behind you."

"Then Wren's sentence is the counter-prophecy." Ansel touched the crooked final line. *I had no one to imagine a third ending with.* "She figured it out too late. After she'd already done it. After she'd already—" Ansel's breath caught. "After she'd already sent everyone away. Because that was the pattern. That's what the Gardener is. That's what I've been doing for three years without knowing why. No surname spoken, no debts owed, no loyalties kept. I thought it was survival. It was *preparation*. Someone, somewhere, has been grooming me to arrive at that door alone, and I did half their work for them because being invisible felt like safety."

"Yes," Serah said simply, because there was no gentler word for it.

Ansel laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of someone finding the trapdoor in their own floor. "So the third path demands everything anyway. That's the teeth in it. I don't get to keep my memories and close the door. I give up every memory I have—every road, every face, Bramble, my mother, Odell's grumbling, Pip stealing my gloves, Marisol's terrible singing, Hesk carrying me out of a flooded ford when I was nineteen and too proud to admit I couldn't swim. All of it, poured into the seal. And then I wake up knowing none of you, and the whole thing only works if you—" she faltered, and Serah watched pride and terror fight openly across her face, "—if you all decide an empty-eyed stranger is worth a year of your lives. Worth telling her stories until she believes them. Worth loving someone who can't love you back yet."

"That was always the shape of it," Serah said. "Since Moon Four, when the price took your name. We've been practicing."

"You have."

"Odell started it. He won't admit it was tenderness—he calls it 'operational redundancy.' Keeping backups of critical personnel." Serah allowed herself the smallest smile. "Pip writes everything down. Marisol sings the stories so they stick. Even the villagers in Nettlebed know your grandmother's name now; they'll remember yours too. And I—" She paused. Here was the part she had rehearsed and never delivered. "I was trained to erase people. It's the Bloom's deepest art: make someone unwitnessed, and they're easier to kill. So I am going to spend the rest of my life doing the opposite. Witnessing you. Writing down every day. Holding your name against the dark with both hands. Consider it treason against everything I used to be."

Ansel stared at her. Then, slowly, she began to laugh again, and this time it broke halfway through into something else, and Serah pretended very hard not to notice, because some mercies are best performed by looking slightly away.

* *

They told the crew at dawn, gathered around the banked fire with the page laid open among the teacups and the whetstones and Marisol's splinted arm propped on a pillow.

Odell heard it out with her jaw working, staring into the coals. Pip took notes until her hand shook and she had to stop. Marisol listened with her eyes closed, and when Ansel finished, she opened them and said, "A year. You're saying we teach you everything from the beginning. Like a child."

"Like a stranger," Ansel said. "You'd owe me nothing. Most people wouldn't—"

"We're not most people." Marisol's voice was hoarse from the smoke she'd breathed at the safehouse fire, but iron underneath it. "Hesk carried strangers' carts out of mud for free. You think we'd stop at our own?"

Nobody said Hesk's name again, but everybody thought it, and the silence held him the way the silence in this room always did now.

It was Odell who finally spoke, gruff as gravel, not looking at anyone. "Rules. One: we start memorizing now, all of us, every story we've got, so the year after isn't guesswork. Two: nobody treats her like glass between now and the thirteenth moon—she's still the best route-finder on the mountain, and we'll need her. Three—" her voice dropped, and for a moment the captain sounded very tired and very old, "—three: when it's done, when she looks at us and sees nothing, nobody gets drunk and bitter about it. Nobody makes her feel worse for surviving. We chose this. All of us. Say aye."

Four voices said aye. A fifth would have, if he'd lived to hear the plan.

* *

Serah stood watch on the ridge above the safehouse as the sun came up, red and slow over Thornfell's eastern shoulders, and let herself finally do the shaking.

Because she had not told them everything. She had shown Ansel the recovered ghost-lines, and together they'd deciphered the mechanism, and the mechanism was real—she believed it with her whole trained, traitorous heart. But there had been a third line in the scraped-away text, shorter than the others, and she had read it, and she had not spoken it aloud.

*Let it further be recorded: the anchor must be true. If the one who holds the Key's name does not truly love her—if duty stands where love should—the holding fails, quietly, and neither of them will know until the emptiness has already begun.*

Duty. Serah had eleven years of duty. Duty had burned Bramble's shop. Duty had filed false reports and killed her superior and walked away from the only structure her life had ever had. Duty was the strongest thing she owned.

And somewhere between the cliff road and the waystation fire and tonight's candlelit table, without her noticing the exact moment of it, duty had quietly stopped being the name for what she felt when she looked at Ansel Amberdale bent over her grandmother's handwriting.

Which meant it could work. Which meant it *had* to work—and that she could never, ever tell Ansel the third line's condition, because if Ansel knew the anchor had to be love rather than loyalty, she would spend the next moon-turns pushing everyone away again to spare them the burden, and the door would take her blood.

Some secrets, Serah thought, watching the sun clear the ridge and gild the ruined Garden far below, are also anchors. You carry them so the other person doesn't have to.

Behind her, inside, she heard Ansel call the crew to the table to start the first memorization session, her voice steady, already reciting the first line of the story they would someday have to tell her from nothing:

*"Tell me again how we met."*

Serah wiped her face, checked her sword out of eleven years of habit, and went inside to begin.
