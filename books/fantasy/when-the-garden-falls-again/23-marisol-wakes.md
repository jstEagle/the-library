## 23. Marisol Wakes

Pain came back to Marisol before her eyes did.

It arrived the way tide arrives—slow, then all at once, filling every channel of her body with a gray, grinding ache that centered somewhere beneath her ribs and radiated outward like a struck bell. She tried to move and discovered that moving had been cancelled, apparently by committee, and that the committee had voted against her.

"Careful." Pip's voice, close and cracked. "Careful, careful—don't—here, drink this, it's—"

A cup pressed against her lips. The taste was bitter and green and underneath it, faintly, honey, which meant Pip had argued with the medicine instead of just making her drink it. Marisol drank anyway, because her throat felt like she'd been swallowing gravel from the Greywater, and because Pip's hands were shaking so badly the cup chattered against her teeth.

She opened her eyes.

The safehouse. The new one, the one Odell had gotten through a cousin of a cousin in the Saltrow district—low-ceilinged, smelling of lamp oil and river damp, with a hearth that smoked unless you fed it just so. She knew the ceiling by heart already. She'd stared at it for what felt like years.

"Pip," she said. Her voice came out ruined, a whisper dragged over broken glass.

"I'm here." Pip was there, folding down onto the cot's edge, and Marisol saw with a distant, clinical part of herself that her twin looked *bad*. Not slept-in-days bad. Not worried bad. *Hollowed* bad. There was something behind her eyes that Marisol had never seen there before, not once in twenty-six years of looking, and it took her a moment to name it because she'd never had to.

Guilt. Pip was wearing guilt the way other people wore a wet coat—like something she couldn't take off and couldn't stop shivering in.

"How long?" Marisol asked.

"Nine days."

"Nine—" Marisol tried to sit up and the bell beneath her ribs tolled again, and Pip's hands were on her shoulders pressing her back down, gentle and frantic at once.

"Nine days. The healer said—she said you weren't supposed to wake up this soon, and if you did wake up you weren't supposed to *talk*, so obviously you're already ruining everything, so you might as well—"

"Pip."

"—might as well ruin it *thoroughly*, that's always been your—"

"*Pip.*"

Pip stopped. Her hands were still on Marisol's shoulders. They were trembling.

Marisol looked at her sister for a long moment. The hearth smoked. Somewhere in the house, floorboards creaked under someone's weight—Hesk's weight, her mind supplied automatically, Hesk's slow tread, and then corrected itself, and the correction hit her harder than the wound had.

Hesk was dead. She remembered that. She remembered all of it, actually—the ford, the water, the arrows, Hesk's enormous hand shoving her toward the far bank and then turning away from her, turning to face the Order's knights on the bridge with that same unhurried gentleness he'd used on frightened horses and crying children and her, once, when she was nineteen and stupid and bleeding in an alley in Hollowfen. She remembered the sound the bridge made when it came down. She remembered that she'd screamed his name and the river had taken the scream the way it took everything.

"Ansel," Marisol said.

Pip flinched. Actually flinched, like the name had been thrown at her.

"She's here," Pip said. "She hasn't left the house in six days. She sits with you when you sleep. She—"

"Get her."

"Mari, you need to rest, the healer said—"

"Pip." Marisol reached up and caught her sister's wrist. It took almost everything she had. "I have spent nine days dying, and I have spent those nine days dreaming about a bridge. Get her, or I will get up myself, and we both know how that ends."

Pip looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and went to the door, and Marisol lay in the gray ache and listened to her sister's footsteps cross the room, and thought about the strange arithmetic of families—that you could love someone your whole life and still not know the shape of the worst thing they'd ever done until it walked through the door wearing their face.

* * *

Ansel came in quietly, the way she did everything.

Marisol had known her for two years now, and in two years she had never once heard Ansel Amberdale enter a room. She arrived like weather changes—suddenly present, no announcement, no creak of floorboard or scrape of chair. Odell swore the girl had been born in a smuggler's tunnel and raised by the silence in it. Marisol had always thought it was something sadder than that. People who made no noise coming in were people who'd spent a long time needing to leave unnoticed.

She stood in the doorway now, half in shadow, and Marisol saw that she was holding a bowl of something—broth, maybe—and that she'd clearly been crying, recently or continuously, Marisol couldn't tell which, and that she was looking at Marisol the way people look at a fire they started.

"You're awake," Ansel said.

"I'm awake."

"I brought—there's broth. If you can eat. The healer said—"

"Ansel." Marisol's voice was gravel and river water. "Put the broth down and come here where I can see your face."

Ansel set the bowl on the shelf by the door with exaggerated care, the way people do when their hands aren't steady, and came and stood at the foot of the cot, and Marisol looked at her.

The girl looked *wrecked*. Not the pretty kind of wrecked that bards sing about—the other kind, the kind that comes from not sleeping and not eating and carrying something heavy in the chest for days without putting it down. Her scarred hands hung at her sides. Her eyes had the flat, careful look of someone who has decided to accept whatever is coming to them.

Good, Marisol thought, with a ferocity that surprised her. *Good. Stand there and take it.*

"You're angry," Ansel said. Not a question.

"I'm furious," Marisol said. "I'm so furious I can taste it. It's sitting in my mouth next to the medicine. And before you say anything—before you do that thing you do, that thing where you apologize for existing—I need you to listen to me, because I've had nine days to compose this speech and I've rewritten it eleven times and I'm only going to have the strength to say it once."

Ansel said nothing. She stood very still.

"Nine days ago I took an arrow for you," Marisol said. "Do you know why?"

"Because—"

"Because you're *ours*," Marisol said, and her ruined voice cracked on it, and she let it crack, because she'd earned the crack. "That's why. Not because of a prophecy. Not because you're the Thirteenth Key or whatever the book says. Because two years ago a silent, suspicious, arm's-length girl walked onto Odell's deck and we fed her and she stayed, and somewhere in there—without you ever once letting us—you became family. And I took an arrow because that's what family does. And Hesk—" her breath hitched, and she pushed through it, because stopping meant not finishing, "—Hesk died on that bridge because that's what family does, and you have spent nine days in this house acting like you're a *guest* at your own grief, and I cannot—Ansel, I *cannot* watch you do it anymore."

"Marisol—"

"No. Not yet." Marisol dragged in a breath that cost her. "Here's the other thing. The harder thing. Pip sold your route."

She saw it land. She saw it hit Ansel like a physical blow—the small flinch, the way her eyes went to the door where Pip had gone, the way her scarred hands curled in slightly at her sides.

"She didn't know," Marisol said. "She was desperate, Marisol was dying, the medicine cost more than we've made in a year, and a masked broker with soft hands offered her silver for a route map, and she took it, because she loves you and she loves me and love makes people stupid in exactly the wrong direction. And that broker was the Order. And their ambush at the ford killed Hesk. So." Marisol's voice was shaking now, all of it, the anger and the grief and the terrible love tangled up into something with no clean edges. "So the man who taught me to whittle is dead, and the woman who took the arrow is me, and the girl who sold the map is my sister, and the reason all of it happened is a prophecy written before any of us were born. And you are standing there looking like you're going to apologize for *all of it*."

"I was going to," Ansel said. Her voice was very quiet. "I've been practicing."

"*Don't.*" It came out as a shout and Marisol paid for it immediately, the bell under her ribs tolling so hard she saw white, but she didn't stop, because she was finishing this. "Don't you dare. You don't get to carry it all. That's the whole—that's the *whole point* of what I'm saying. You've spent three years owing nothing to nobody, and now you owe everything to everybody, and instead of letting us carry it *with* you, you're trying to pay it all yourself, alone, in a corner, quietly, like a debt collector's going to come for you if you make noise. Well, the debt collector came, Ansel. The debt collector came and it shot me and killed Hesk and burned down our house, and we're still *here*. We're still here, and you're still here, and Pip is in the other room hating herself so hard she's forgetting to eat, and Odell is on the roof because Odell cries where nobody can see, and I am lying in this bed unable to walk to the privy by myself, and none of us—*none of us*—are leaving. Do you understand me? Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Ansel's composure broke.

It didn't break the way Marisol expected—no sob, no collapse. It broke the way ice breaks, all at once and invisibly, so that one moment she was standing there whole and the next moment she was simply *falling*, catching herself on the cot's frame, her scarred hands gripping the wood, her head bowed, her whole body shaking with a silence so total it was worse than weeping.

Marisol reached out and put her hand on Ansel's head. It was all she could reach. Her arm shook with the effort and she didn't care.

"You're ours," she said again, softer now. "That's not a debt. That's not a price. That's just a fact, like the river being wet. And facts don't get apologized for."

For a long time, neither of them moved. The hearth smoked. The river-damp walls wept gently in the cold. And Ansel Amberdale, the Thirteenth Key, the girl the prophecy had named and the world had hunted, knelt on the floorboards beside a smuggler's cot with a smuggler's hand in her hair, and shook, and did not leave.

When she finally spoke, her voice was raw and small.

"I don't know how to be carried," she said. "I've never—I don't know how."

"Then you'll learn," Marisol said. "We'll teach you. We're very good teachers. Pip taught me to pick locks when I was six. Hesk taught us both to be gentle." Her own tears came now, finally, sliding hot down into her ears. "It's the last thing he taught anybody. We're not going to waste the lesson."

* * *

They gathered that evening, all of them, because Marisol demanded it and because Odell, when she heard the demand, had come down off the roof with red-rimmed eyes and said, "About time somebody said it out loud."

The room was small. It was not built for five people and a grief the size of Hesk, and they crowded into it anyway—Marisol propped on her cot with pillows stacked behind her, Odell in the one chair by the hearth, Pip on the floor with her back against Marisol's cot, and Ansel in the doorway, because some part of her still couldn't quite cross the threshold of being included, and nobody made her, and nobody let her leave either.

Odell spoke first, because she was the captain, and because captains go first into bad weather.

"I've buried crew before," she said, to the fire more than to them. "Eleven, over thirty years. You don't get to my age in this trade with all your people. And every time, I've told myself the same lie—that it gets lighter. It doesn't. You just get stronger arms." She turned the cup in her hands. "Hesk was the best of them. The gentlest. And I want it said, in this room, in front of everyone: what happened at the ford was not any single person's doing. It was the Order's doing. It was a war coming for us that started before we were born. If anyone in this room spends the rest of their life punishing themselves for it, I will consider it an insult to his memory, because Hesk never once blamed anybody for anything, and he would be *furious*—"

Her voice went. Just for a moment. She turned her face to the fire and let the pause be long, and no one filled it, because some pauses deserve to be empty.

"—furious," she finished, "that we were wasting our grief on guilt instead of on each other."

Pip raised her head from the floor.

"It was me," she said. Her voice was steady, which meant she'd been practicing too. "I want it said plainly. I sold the route. I didn't know who the broker was, but I sold it, and Hesk is dead, and Marisol nearly died, and I'll carry that. I'm not asking anyone to take it from me. I'm asking—" and here the steadiness finally failed, and Marisol reached down and found her sister's hand without looking, "—I'm asking to carry it *here*. With you. Not out there. Not alone. Please."

"You'll carry it here," Odell said. "That's an order."

"And I'll carry it with you," Marisol said. "That's a twin thing. Non-negotiable."

Ansel hadn't moved from the doorway. But when she spoke, her voice came out low and rough and certain.

"I forgive her."

Everyone turned.

"I forgive her," Ansel said again, and Marisol watched her say it, watched the girl who'd spent three years owing nothing stand in a doorway owing everything and give away the one thing she had left. "I forgave her the day I found out. I just didn't know how to say it without it sounding like I was forgiving myself too. And I wasn't ready—" her hands turned over, scarred palms up, an offering or a surrender, "—I wasn't ready to be forgiven. But Marisol's right. I've been standing at the edge of this family for two years waiting for you all to realize I was trouble and cut me loose. And you keep not doing it. And I'm—" the breath she took shuddered all the way through her. "I'm done waiting to be cut loose. I'm in. All the way in. Whatever the book says. Whatever it costs. I'm in."

The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, the river moved in the dark, taking things and giving things back, the way rivers do.

Odell lifted her cup. "To Hesk," she said.

"To Hesk," they said, four voices in a small smoky room, and drank, and the grief was still there—it would always be there, Marisol knew, a stone in the chest that never fully passed—but it was a stone they were all carrying together now, and stones shared weigh less.

* * *

Later, when the others had gone to their beds, Marisol woke to a sound.

It was Ansel, at the table by the window, in the gray hour before dawn, writing. She had the prophecy book open beside her and a stack of cheap paper, and she was copying something out in her careful, hidden hand, line after line, and her lips were moving as she wrote, and Marisol lay still and watched her and understood, suddenly, what she was seeing.

The girl was building an ark.

She was writing down everything the moon-turns would take from her—names, faces, the feel of roads, the taste of home—and sealing it in ink before the flood came. Marisol didn't know how she knew that. She just did, the way you know a thing about family, the way you know a thing in your bones before your mind catches up.

Ansel looked up. Caught her watching. For a moment, the old reflex flickered across her face—the flinch, the arm's-length, the urge to hide the work and the hurt together.

Then she stopped. And turned the paper around, so Marisol could read it across the dark room.

It said: *Marisol. Loud, brave, takes arrows for people she loves. Laughs with her whole body. Has a twin she'd burn the world for.*

"That's not how I'd have written it," Marisol whispered. "It's missing 'terrible singer.'"

Ansel's mouth twitched. "I have limited paper."

"Write it anyway. If I'm going to be a stranger to you someday, I want to be an *accurate* stranger."

Something moved behind Ansel's eyes—fear, and gratitude, and the beginning of something that might, in time, become peace. She dipped her pen. She wrote.

And outside the window, over the black line of the Thornfell mountains, the moon hung low and heavy and a little less than full, counting down toward the thirteenth night, while inside the safehouse a girl who had spent her whole life erasing herself wrote down the people she loved, one name at a time, like planting a garden in the path of a winter she could not stop—only hold things through.

Marisol watched her until sleep took her, and her last thought, slipping under, was of Hesk's big gentle hands, and of how he'd always said the same thing about broken things he mended: *it holds better where it's been mended than where it was never broken at all.*

She believed him. She was going to make everyone believe him.

Even the girl who was learning, line by line, to be carried.
