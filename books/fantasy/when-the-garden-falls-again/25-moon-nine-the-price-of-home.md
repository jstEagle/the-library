## 25. Moon Nine: The Price of Home

The ninth moon came up fat and white over the pass, and Ansel felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her eyes like a thumb pressed to the inside of her skull, patient, inevitable.

She sat apart from the fire. That was tradition now, or ritual, or simply habit worn smooth by repetition: on the nights the book demanded its price, she took the watch alone, with the prophecy open on her knees and the crew sleeping in a loose ring of blankets behind her. Pip snored. Marisol's breathing had gone deep and even since the fever broke, though a scar still pulled at her shoulder when she rolled. Odell slept like a man who trusted no one but his own exhaustion. Serah kept her armor on even in sleep, which Ansel had stopped finding strange around the fourth moon.

Ansel turned the page.

The script had changed again. It did that—shifted as the payments shifted, the grandmother's hand growing more urgent, more cramped, as though Wren Amberdale had been writing against a tide coming in. The ninth entry was short. They were all short now. There was only so much one could say about being unmade.

*The ninth payment is the land itself,* it read. *It will take the ground from under you. Forgive me, little bird. I did not know how else to warn you.*

"Little bird," Ansel whispered. She'd never known anyone who called her that. She'd never known anyone whose voice she could put to those words. The name landed somewhere soft and hollow in her chest, an ache without a story attached—the shape of grief with all the memory boiled out of it.

She looked up at the mountains.

That was the thing about Thornfell. Everyone else saw mountains. Ansel saw a language. She had spent three years learning every fold of these ranges the way other people learned scripture—the way water moved through them, where the snow lay longest, which passes lied about their width until you were already committed. She knew the Greywater gorge by the smell of its iron-stained stone. She knew that the eastern face of the Widow's Tooth held warmth until midnight and then gave it up all at once, so that climbers who trusted their first hour of comfort froze before dawn. She knew the sound the wind made funneling through the Serrated Gate—a low moan, almost musical, that old smugglers said was the mountain singing to itself.

She had told herself once, back when she still told herself things, that if everything else was taken from her—if her name went, if her mother's face went, if love went—the mountains would remain. The land didn't ask anything of you. The land just was, and knowing it was enough to be somebody.

The moon climbed higher. The pressure behind her eyes became a pulling.

It started at the edges, the way all the payments did. The far peaks first—the ones past the Serrated Gate, blue-grey silhouettes she'd used to fix her bearings on a hundred night marches. She watched them, and they watched her back, and then they were simply *mountains*, anonymous, interchangeable, the way they must have looked to everyone else. A range. Elevation. Nothing more.

"No," she said aloud, and her breath smoked in the cold. "No, take something else, take—"

But there was no bargaining with it. There never was. The book took what the book took, and the only mercy in nine months of this had been that it always took cleanly, without pain, so that she only ever grieved backward, for things she could no longer quite name.

The Serrated Gate went next. She knew there had been a sound there—she could feel the absence of it like a missing tooth, a groove in her mind where knowledge had lived—but the wind blew through the pass now and it was only wind. Then the Widow's Tooth, and the trick of its stored warmth, gone. Then the smell of the Greywater gorge, iron and wet slate, and she thought, distantly, *I am losing the smell of home*, and then she thought, *what gorge?* and then she thought nothing at all about it, because there was nothing left in her to think it with.

She gripped the book so hard the cover bit into her palms. Her scarred hands. Her hands, at least, were still hers.

Below her, the campfire burned. Behind her, the crew breathed. And all around her, in every direction, stretched a country she no longer knew.

* * *

Dawn found her sitting exactly where the moon had left her, and it was Odell who found her, because Odell always woke first, grumbling at the cold like the cold owed him money.

"You look like death's unpaid debts," he said, crouching beside her. He said things like that instead of saying *are you all right*, because Odell believed tenderness was a private matter conducted entirely through insults.

"I don't know where we are."

He blinked. "We're three days out of Nettlebed, girl. Same place we bedded down when the moon rose."

"I know that's what you'll say." Her voice came out flat and strange to her own ears. "I mean I don't *know*. Look." She turned her head slowly, deliberately, the way a person turns to face a fear. "Tell me what you see."

Odell squinted at the valley below them, the river threading silver through it, the dark spine of ridgeline beyond. "Greywater country. River runs south-southeast toward the ford. Ridge to the west is the Widow's—" He stopped. He was not a quick man, but he was not a slow one either, and nine months of watching this girl pay the book's tolls had taught him the shape of each new loss. His weathered face went carefully blank. "Ah," he said. "It took the land."

"It took the land."

"And you're telling me because?"

"Because I need you to tell me about it." The words cost her. Every word of this conversation cost her, and she paid anyway. "Every day. Everything you know about where we're walking. I need it the way Marisol needed the medicine. I need you to describe the ground to me like I'm blind, Odell, because as of last night, I am."

He was quiet for a long moment. Down in the camp, Pip stirred, swore at the cold, and burrowed deeper into his blanket. Somewhere a bird Ansel couldn't have named sang a song she couldn't have placed.

"All right," Odell said finally. "But I'm charging you. One copper per fact, payable when this is over."

"That's extortion."

"That's business." He stood, joints cracking, and offered her his hand. "Come on, then, blind girl. First lesson's free. See that notch in the ridge, due east?" He pointed. "That's the Lamb's Jaw. Narrowest crossing on the whole river, and the water runs fast enough through it to strip the boots off a drowning man. You taught *me* that, two years ago, coming down from a moon-ore run. You said—" his voice caught, just slightly, and he covered it with a cough— "you said the river there moves like it's angry about something. Said you'd never heard water sound personal before."

Ansel looked at the notch. Just a notch. Rock, shadow, the glint of moving water far below. But Odell's voice wrapped around it, and slowly, painstakingly, the notch began to mean something. *The Lamb's Jaw.* A name was a small thing to hold onto. She held it.

"Again," she said. "Tell me again."

* * *

They walked for six days, and the crew became her eyes.

It was Pip who narrated best—he had a smuggler's gift for color, for turning terrain into tale. "Left fork ahead, boss," he'd say, hopping a fallen trunk. "You told us once the right fork floods in spring melt, washes out to nothing, looks like a road the whole way until it kills you. You called it the Liar's Fork. Said you lost a whole shipment of silk to it your first year and cried about it for a week, which, no offense, is very hard to imagine."

"I believe I cried for two days," Ansel said. "A week is slander."

Pip grinned so hard he nearly tripped. "You remember!"

"I remember what you told me yesterday. That's not the same thing." She kept her voice light, because Pip needed it light; the boy carried guilt like ballast since the ford, since Hesk, since the broker with the masked face, and she would not add to it. But the truth under the joke was true: she remembered nothing except what they gave her. She was walking through her own life wearing someone else's description of it.

Marisol, slower since her wound, rode the pack mule and pointed out flowers. That was her contribution, offered shyly, as though it weren't precious: names of things that grew. "Fireweed," she'd say, nodding at a spike of magenta along the trail. "You showed me that the first month I ran with the crew. Said it grows where the ground's been burned. Said—" and here Marisol would smile, small and crooked— "said it was the honest flower. Only plant in the mountains that admits what happened to it."

Ansel looked at the fireweed. *Honest flower.* She held that too.

Serah said the least, but what she said was load-bearing. The knight had a soldier's eye for ground—sightlines, choke points, where an ambush would bloom—and she gave it all to Ansel without being asked, in a low even voice, like reports rendered to a superior officer. "High ground on both sides for the next half-mile. If I were hunting us, I'd wait until the narrows." And Ansel would nod and file it away, and understand that Serah was doing penance in the only currency she had: information, freely given, about the country that had raised them both.

On the fifth evening, camped under an overhang Ansel had once—according to Odell—used to shelter a full cargo of contraband through a three-day storm, she asked the question she'd been carrying since the moon rose.

"If it keeps going," she said, to the fire more than to any of them, "if it takes the rest—Wren, the third path, the sentence, all of it—how will I know what I'm supposed to do on the thirteenth night? How can I choose an ending I won't remember wanting?"

Nobody answered right away. Pip poked the fire. Marisol's hand found Ansel's sleeve and stayed there.

Then Serah spoke, from the edge of the light, armor creaking softly. "Because we'll remember it for you. That's what an anchor is. You wrote it down, we memorized it, we say it every night. The memories are yours, Ansel. But the *keeping*—" she paused, and something raw moved under her soldier's calm— "the keeping is ours. Let us do that much."

"The keeping is ours," Marisol repeated, fierce and quiet.

Odell grunted, which from him was a vow.

Ansel looked around the fire—at the smuggler, the twins, the knight—and felt the strangest sensation, like standing in a house she'd never seen and yet knowing exactly where every room was. She didn't know these mountains anymore. But she was beginning to suspect she knew these people, in some place deeper than the book could reach, and that maybe that had always been the real map.

* * *

On the sixth day they reached the mouth of the Serpent's Road, and everything Odell and Pip and Serah had given her was about to be tested against the deadliest pass in Thornfell.

The entrance was a crack in the cliff face, barely wide enough for two riders abreast, swallowing the trail into darkness. Cold air breathed out of it, mineral and ancient. Above the mouth, someone long dead had carved a warning into the rock in letters worn nearly smooth:

*THE MOUNTAIN DECIDES WHO PASSES.*

Ansel stared at the carving, and at the dark beyond it, and felt nothing. No dread. No recognition. No whisper of the hundred times she must have stood on this exact spot. The Serpent's Road was, to her, a hole in a wall.

"Boss?" Pip said quietly. "You don't have to lead us through. We can go around, add a week, risk the Order's—"

"We can't add a week." She heard her own voice come out steady and was grateful to whatever part of her still knew how to speak. "Aurelia's ritual is eleven days out. We go through."

"But you've never seen it," Marisol whispered. "Not—not *this* you. The you who knew it is gone."

Ansel crouched and took out the little oilskin packet she carried now everywhere: the crew's descriptions, written in her own hand over five nights, plus Wren's page, plus the sentence sewn into her coat lining. She unfolded the pages in the wind at the mountain's mouth and read her own future route like a stranger reading directions to a city she'd never visited.

*"Narrow for the first mile, then opens into the Gallery—a cavern big enough to hold a cathedral, ceiling full of pale crystals that glow faint as drowned stars. After the Gallery, three forks. Take the middle, always the middle; the left descends to gas pockets, the right climbs to a scree chute that funnels you onto open rock where the Order could see you for miles. Middle fork narrows again to the Squeeze—turn sideways, exhale, trust the stone. Past the Squeeze, listen for dripping. If the dripping stops, the air has gone bad; backtrack and wait. Two hours' walk beyond, daylight."*

She read it twice. Three times. Then she folded the pages away, stood, and looked at the dark mouth of the Serpent's Road.

"I've never seen it," she agreed. "But apparently I described it well enough that five people believed me. So." She stepped toward the crack in the world, and the cold breath of the mountain touched her face like a stranger's hand. "Let's see if the girl in those pages was worth trusting."

Behind her, boots scraped stone as the crew fell in—Odell first, because he'd follow her anywhere and pretend it was tactical; Pip and Marisol together; Serah last, watching the trail behind them, sword loose in its sheath.

And Ansel walked into the dark of the mountain she no longer knew, guided only by the ghost of her own handwriting and the voices of the people who refused to let her be lost—

—and thirty paces in, in the black, her boot found a ledge, her hand found a hold, and her body moved through the narrow going with a sureness that had nothing to do with memory at all, as if the mountains had not been taken from her mind but had simply gone to ground, waiting deeper than the book could dig.

The mountain, it seemed, had decided.
