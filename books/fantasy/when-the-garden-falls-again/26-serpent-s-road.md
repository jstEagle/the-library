## 26. Serpent's Road

The pass had a name that was less a name than a warning. Old-timers in Hollowfen called it Serpent's Road because it moved like one—a long, scaled spine of switchbacks coiled through the throat of the Thornfell range, doubling back on itself so many times that a traveler standing at the bottom could look up and count the same stretch of trail four times, stacked like the coils of a sleeping thing. It was the only way east over the mountains in deep winter that didn't require wings or a death wish, and it killed more people every year than the war-god beneath them had killed in a century.

Ansel stood at its mouth and felt nothing.

That was the horror of it, or would have been, if she still knew enough to be horrified. Three days ago—she thought it was three days; Odell counted by burning candles and she'd lost track of even that—the ninth payment had come due, and Thornfell had been taken out of her like a tooth pulled whole. She remembered mountains existed. She remembered *these* mountains existed, because the crew told her so, over and over, patient as priests: *Thornfell, Ansel. The ones we're standing in. You grew up in their shadow.* But when she looked at the peaks above her, white-capped and indifferent against a bruised dawn sky, she saw scenery. Beautiful, hostile, anonymous scenery. The kind of thing you'd find painted on the back of a cheap inn room door.

She did not see home.

"Tell me again," she said.

Marisol shifted against her crutch, arm bound tight to her chest where the Order's fire had broken something that was still knitting. Her face was pale with cold and pain, but her voice came steady, the way it always did now when they did this ritual. "The first switchback is honest," Marisol said. "Wide as a wagon road, safe as any road gets here. The second lies. Looks wider but the outer edge crumbles—there was a rockslide two winters back, you said Hesk nearly went over with a mule." She paused, just slightly, at Hesk's name, the way everyone did now, a small shared flinch none of them talked about. "You said the trick is to walk the inner wall side and let the mule think he's winning the argument."

"I said that?"

"You say it every time. Word for word."

Pip snorted from somewhere near the pack-mules. "It's uncanny, is what it is. Like listening to someone pray in their sleep."

Ansel turned the words over. *Inner wall side. Let the mule win.* They were just sounds, just a story about some girl she'd apparently been. But when she stepped onto the second switchback, her feet went to the inner wall without being told. When the loose shale hissed under her boot at the crumbling edge, she was already three paces past it, weight forward, breath measured the way you breathe when your body knows the drop is close even if your head doesn't.

Her body remembered. That was the thing no one had warned her about—that memory wasn't all kept in the same place, that some of it lived in the hands and the soles of the feet and the small muscles of the eyes, deeper down than the moon-turn's knife could reach. The seal had taken Thornfell from her mind, but it seemed it hadn't been able to take Thornfell out of her bones.

*Good,* she thought, and then wondered who she was being good for.

* * *

They climbed through the morning, and the pass narrowed, and the wind came down off the high snowfields with a sound like a whetstone drawn along the world's edge. By midday they had gained the first great coil, where the trail wrapped around the shoulder of a peak called—Odell said—Grey Widow, though Ansel looked at the grey granite face and felt the name slide off it like rain off oilcloth.

There was a shrine there. A niche cut into the rock, old, older than smugglers, holding a carved stone serpent no bigger than a forearm, worn smooth by centuries of superstitious hands. Travelers left offerings. Coins mostly, and ribbons, and once—Pip claimed with relish—an entire wheel of cheese, which had gone hard as siege rations before anyone dared eat it.

"You used to leave buttons," Pip said, falling into step beside Ansel as they passed it. "Not coins. Buttons. You said coins were for people who wanted something, and buttons were for people who were only saying hello."

Ansel stopped. Stared at the little serpent. Its eyes were two hollows, dark and patient, and something in her chest pulled taut, a string plucked in an empty room.

Without deciding to, she reached into her coat pocket—found, to her complete lack of surprise, that she was carrying buttons—and set one in the serpent's curled tail. A horn button, cracked down the middle.

"Saying hello," she said.

Pip made a noise that started as a laugh and ended as something wetter, and walked quickly ahead, pretending to check a mule's harness.

* * *

The trouble came in the afternoon, at the place Odell called the Throat.

Every pass has its pinch, the point where the mountain stops pretending to be generous. On Serpent's Road it was a cleft barely wide enough for the mules single-file, roofed by a leaning slab of granite that had hung there since before maps, groaning faintly in the wind like an old man muttering in his sleep. Ice glazed the floor of it. Past the Throat the trail dropped into a hanging valley and then climbed again toward the eastern shoulder, but between here and there lay three hundred yards of path that winter had half-erased, and behind them, far below, the Order's banners were visible as red threads crawling up the lower switchbacks.

Serah stood at the mouth of the cleft and read the ice the way she'd been trained to read battlefields. "We can't take the mules through loaded," she said. "The footing won't hold the combined weight. We double-haul the cargo, then the animals, one at a time. It'll cost us an hour we don't have."

"They're a day behind us at best," Odell growled. "Maybe half a day if their climbers are Bloom-blessed."

"They're a day behind us because Ansel put us on the ridge trail last night instead of the valley road," Marisol said quietly. "Which nobody else knew existed. Including, I'm guessing, most of the Order."

Everyone looked at Ansel.

She hated this. Not the looking—she'd stopped minding the looking weeks ago—but the question underneath it, the same question every time: *do you know what you're doing?* Because the answer was that she didn't. Not in any way that could be spoken aloud. Ask her what the Throat was like and she would give you silence. Ask her body, and—

"Unpack the mules," she heard herself say. Her voice came out flat and certain, a stranger's voice wearing her confidence like a borrowed coat. "But not all the way. Leave the grain sacks loaded—they ride low and wide, they'll act as ballast against the gusts coming through the cleft. Take everything off the lead animal. We go through in this order: me, lead mule, Marisol, the rest of the train, Serah, Odell last. And nobody touches the walls."

"Why not the walls?" Pip asked.

"Because—" and there it was again, the knowledge arriving in her mouth before it arrived in her mind, "—the walls hum. If you put your hand on them you'll feel it, and then you'll stop walking, and stopping in the Throat is how it kills people. Keep your eyes on the heels in front of you and count your steps out loud. Fifty-two steps. I'll count."

Silence. Then Odell spat, which among the crew constituted a benediction. "You heard her. Move."

They went through the Throat in fifty-two echoing steps, Ansel counting in a low voice while the leaning slab groaned overhead and the wind screamed through the cleft like something cheated of its meal. The ice held. The grain sacks swayed and steadied. At step forty-one Marisol's boot skidded and Ansel reached back without looking—without *thinking*, without anything so deliberate as thought—and caught her wrist and hauled her weight forward onto the safe stone, all in one motion, the way water finds a channel.

Afterward, on the far side, Marisol sat down hard on a boulder and laughed until it turned into tears, and Ansel stood looking at her own hand as if it belonged to a puppeteer she couldn't see.

"How did you know?" Marisol asked finally. "To catch me. How did you know?"

"I don't know," Ansel said honestly.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have. Something in me knows this road. It knows it better than I know myself, which granted isn't a high bar anymore." She flexed the hand. "Whatever I was, whoever taught me—I can't remember her face or her voice or a single conversation. But the roads stayed. Maybe the roads were the truest thing about me. Maybe everything else was decoration."

Serah had come up beside them, silent in the way knights learned to be silent even off-duty. Now she spoke, carefully. "In the Order we had a doctrine. That a person is what they remember, and to lose your memories is to lose yourself entirely. It's why Aurelia fears you. Why the Order has always feared the Keys—not the power, the erasure. They think what's erased is gone."

"And you?" Ansel asked. "Do you think that?"

Serah looked back down the pass, at the red thread of hunters climbing patiently through the coils below, and then at Ansel's scarred hands, and something in her jaw set. "I think the Order writes doctrine for the same reason smugglers write almanacs," she said. "To make a complicated world feel navigable. And I think you just led six people and five mules through the Throat on instinct alone, which means whatever they took from you, they didn't get all of you." A pause. "I think maybe they never could."

* * *

They camped that night in the hanging valley, in the lee of a fallen pillar of stone that some ancient violence had toppled across the trailhead. Fires were small and shielded. Above them the stars came out in the brutal clarity of high altitude, and the eastern shoulder of the pass rose black against them, one last day's climb between the crew and open country beyond the Order's easy reach.

Ansel couldn't sleep. This was normal now; sleep had become a country with shifting borders. So she sat up by the fire, wrapped in Odell's spare cloak, and listened to the crew's voices going soft around the flames—Pip describing, for the hundredth time, the exact dimensions of the cheese once offered to the serpent shrine; Odell correcting the number downward each telling out of pure spite; Marisol dozing upright with her bound arm propped on a saddlebag.

"Ask her," Pip whispered suddenly, nudging Odell. "Go on. Tonight's as good as any."

Odell grumbled, but the captain dug into her coat and produced a battered tin flask, and poured a measure of something amber and lethal-smelling into the cap, and held it out to Ansel.

"Custom," Odell said gruffly. "Crew crossing the Serpent drinks at the top or not at all, and we drink at the top tomorrow, so tonight you get the rehearsal. It's plum brandy. Bramble distilled it."

The name landed in Ansel's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for grief and found only the shape where grief should be, an absence with edges, and around the edges, faintly, warmth—because these people were giving her the shape itself, handing her the outline of a man she'd loved, trusting her to hold even the hollow of him.

"Bramble," she repeated, tasting the name. "Tell me about him. Tell me again."

So they did, taking turns, passing the flask, building him out of anecdote the way sailors build a lighthouse stone by stone into the sea. He smelled of ink and juniper. He cheated at cards and denied it magnificently. He called Ansel *little compass* because she could find a route anywhere and never once ask directions. He died because he refused to give up her name, and he would do it again, and they would let him, and they missed him every day, and—Pip said, voice cracking—he'd have been unbearable about how proud he was of her right now, standing here having led them through the Throat blind.

Ansel drank the brandy. It burned all the way down, and the burn was the first honest feeling of the day, and she held onto it.

"I don't remember loving you," she said at last, plainly, into the fire. "Any of you. I want you to know that I know that. I know what it costs you, telling me these stories every night, watching my face for something that isn't there yet. And I know you do it anyway." She looked up, around the circle of faces lit orange and gold. "I can't repay it. But tomorrow I'll get us over the eastern shoulder, and after that I'll keep getting us wherever we need to go, and maybe—if the roads are the truest part of me, like Serah says—then somewhere on some road, I'll meet the girl who loved you. And I'll introduce myself."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Marisol reached over with her good arm and gripped Ansel's scarred hand, hard.

"She'd like you," Marisol said. "I think she'd like you a lot."

* * *

They summited the eastern shoulder at noon the next day, under a sky scoured clean by wind, the whole world spread out below—green foothills going gold toward the riverlands, the thin silver scratch of the Greywater far downstream, freedom laid out like a map nobody had to draw.

Odell produced the flask. They drank, all of them, even Marisol wincing through her splint, even Serah accepting a swallow with a knight's grave courtesy. The brandy tasted of plums and smoke and a man none of them would bury properly, honored instead on a mountaintop with stolen rituals.

Ansel stood at the edge of the descent and looked east, and felt—faintly, tentatively, like frost forming on a windowpane—something that might have been recognition. Not of the land. Of herself. Of a girl who left buttons for stone serpents and counted steps for frightened friends and knew every secret vein of these mountains the way a lover knows a body in the dark.

*They didn't get all of me,* she thought. *They never could.*

Then Pip, scanning the trail below with the spyglass, went very still.

"Pip?" Odell said.

"There's smoke." Pip lowered the glass, and her face had gone the color of the snowfields. "Behind us. Down the pass, at the valley mouth where the road forks." She swallowed. "That's Nettlebed, Captain. Someone's burning Nettlebed."

Far below, thin and black against the gold of evening, the smoke rose—straight and deliberate, the way fires rise when they're meant to be seen.

And Ansel, staring at it, felt the string in her chest pull taut once more, humming with a note she couldn't name but knew, somehow, was hers.
