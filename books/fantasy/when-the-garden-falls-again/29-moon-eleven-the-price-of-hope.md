## 29. Moon Eleven: The Price of Hope

The moon was a thin nail-paring in the sky when Ansel felt it begin to pull.

She had learned to recognize the sensation by now—the way the air around her went taut as a bowstring, the way her own thoughts grew suddenly loud, as if something vast had leaned close to listen for which one to pluck. The first time, back at Moon One, she had mistaken it for a fever. By the fourth she had learned its true shape: a hand reaching into the house of her mind, turning over her possessions with terrible patience, deciding what to take.

Eleven payments. Eleven things gone. Her mother's face. Her family name. Thornfell's mountains, the ones she had once known better than her own heartbeat. And after the ambush at Greywater Ford—after Hesk—the price had grown crueler still, taking not just memories but the *feel* of them, the warmth that made a memory more than an inventory entry. She remembered now that she had loved Hesk because Pip had told her so, and because her own grief sat in her chest like a stone with someone else's fingerprints all over it.

But this one—this one she could feel circling already, three nights before it came due, and she knew what it wanted before it ever touched her.

It wanted Wren's page.

* * *

They had made a ritual of it without ever agreeing to. Every night since Nettlebed, since the waystation fire where knives had been drawn and then quietly put away, the crew gathered around whatever fire they dared light—smaller now, screened behind stone, because the Order's reach had proven longer than any of them liked—and someone read aloud from the torn thirteenth page.

*I only saw two endings because I had no one to imagine a third with.*

That sentence alone would have been enough, maybe. But beneath it, in Wren's cramped hedge-witch hand, ran the rest: the mechanism of the seal, the willing sacrifice of memory instead of bloodline death, and the warning that had teeth in it. *The Key who gives everything must be anchored afterward, or she will empty out as I emptied out, and the Gardener is what remains when a person has given past the point of personhood.*

Ansel had read it silently a hundred times. That did nothing. Serah had explained it once, in her flat knight's cadence, drawing diagrams in ash. That did nothing either. It was only when the words were spoken aloud—in Odell's gravel rasp, or Marisol's careful post-injury slowness, or Pip's too-fast tumble—that they seemed to take root somewhere the eleventh payment couldn't reach.

"They can't take what isn't only yours," Serah had said one night, apropos of nothing, staring into the fire. "That's the flaw in the design. The seal takes from the Key. But you've been spending your memories like coin for months, and you're still here. Do you know why?"

"Because I'm stubborn," Ansel had said.

"Because you keep giving pieces of yourself away to other people to hold." Serah's eyes had come up, sharp and gray in the firelight. "A hoard can be robbed. A shared thing has to be taken from everyone at once."

Ansel hadn't answered then. She thought about it constantly now.

* * *

On the second night before the payment fell due, she couldn't sleep. She climbed out of the hollow beneath the fallen watchtower where they'd made camp—a day's hard walk south of the Verdant Garden, close enough now that on quiet evenings she fancied she could smell the old hedge, green and patient under the rot—and found Pip sitting watch with his knees drawn up and the page in his hands, reading it by moonlight even though he knew it by heart.

"You'll wear it out," Ansel said.

"It's not the paper I'm worried about wearing out." He didn't look up. In the months since Greywater Ford, since the broker and the betrayal and the forgiveness he'd finally asked for aloud, Pip had lost the restless brightness that used to make him seem younger than he was. What remained was quieter and, Ansel thought, more durable. "Marisol says I fidget when I'm scared. She's asleep, so I'm allowed to say I'm scared."

"What are you scared of?"

He turned the page over, though there was nothing on the back but blank vellum. "Tomorrow night. When it comes for you." His voice dropped. "I've watched it take your mother's face. I watched it take the mountains. I know how it works now—I know you don't feel it happen, that's the worst part, you just wake up and something's *gone* and the rest of us have to tell you what you lost." He swallowed. "But this one's different, isn't it? This one isn't a memory. It's—" He struggled. "It's the thing that's keeping you *you*. The belief. The third path."

"Yes," Ansel said simply. "I think that's exactly what it wants."

"And if it takes it?" Pip looked up at last, and his eyes were wet and furious. "If you wake up tomorrow and Wren's page means nothing to you, if you look at us reading it and think we're mad—what then? We can't write hope down. You can't sew a feeling into a coat lining. Believe me, I've tried. I wrote down everything about Hesk after—" He stopped. Started again. "Some things don't survive being copied."

Ansel sat down beside him on the cold stone. Below them the valley lay silvered and still, and far off, a smudge of darker dark against the mountains: the Garden.

"Pip," she said. "Read it to me again."

"Now?"

"Now."

He blinked at her. Then he smoothed the page across his knee, took a breath, and read. Not just the famous line—all of it, every crossed-out word and marginal note, Wren's voice rising thin and fierce in the night air: *the seal drinks willingly given memory; bloodline death feeds it once, but surrender feeds it forever; the Key must be anchored or she empties; I had no one to imagine a third ending with.*

When he finished, Ansel felt it—that settling, like a keel finding water. "Again," she said.

"You're not going to sleep at all tonight, are you."

"No."

So he read it again. And partway through the third reading, Marisol emerged from the hollow, hair loose, moving carefully on the leg that still ached in cold weather, and sat down on Ansel's other side without a word, and when Pip's voice faltered on the fourth reading hers picked up seamlessly, slower, steadier, each word placed like a stone in a wall.

By dawn all five of them were awake, and the page had been read eleven times, and Ansel's jaw ached from clenching against tears she couldn't fully explain—but the belief sat in her chest, stubborn and warm, like a coal passed hand to hand.

* * *

The payment came at moonrise the next evening, and it came like weather.

She felt it building through the afternoon—a pressure behind her eyes, a wrongness in the quality of silence. The world began to seem slightly too far away, as if she were watching it through glass. And threaded through everything, soft as a whisper in another room, came a voice she recognized with a lurch: Vaskar, speaking through the book she still carried wrapped in oilcloth against her spine.

*You already know how this ends,* the god murmured. *Two endings. Close the door and forget, or open it and burn. Your grandmother saw truly, little key. There is no third. Give me the page. Give me the hope. You'll feel so much lighter.*

"Shut up," Ansel said aloud, and Odell, sharpening his knife by the fire, didn't even glance up. They'd all gotten used to her talking to the air.

She sat in the center of the camp with the torn page spread on her knees and the crew arranged around her—not by design, at first, but by gravity. Serah stood at the perimeter, sword loose in its scabbard, watching the treeline for Order scouts. Odell sat cross-legged at her left. Marisol at her right, with Pip beside her and Hesk's enormous carved walking-staff—recovered from the ford by villagers who had loved him, carried two hundred miles by a girl from Nettlebed—rested upright against a stone where he would have been.

"Whenever you're ready," Odell said gruffly. "Or not ready. Doesn't matter. We start anyway."

The pressure crested. Ansel felt it close around the belief itself—felt it take hold of that bright, improbable certainty the way a thief takes hold of a purse-string—and she gasped, because it hurt, it genuinely hurt, like a hook set behind her sternum.

"Read," she managed.

And they read.

Odell started, because Odell always insisted on starting, though he read like a man reciting cargo manifests—flat, deliberate, every word load-bearing. *The seal drinks willingly given memory.* Then Marisol, whose voice had a singer's control even now. Then Pip, fast and fervent. Then Serah, from the perimeter without turning around, her knight's diction making the hedge-witch's words sound like scripture, and somehow that was the strangest and most comforting version yet.

The hook pulled. Ansel felt the belief begin to slide—not vanish, nothing so clean, but *loosen*, the way a tooth loosens, the way certainty curdles into mere idea. *It's just words,* the pulling suggested, silky and reasonable. *Old words, written by a frightened woman a century dead. You've seen the Gardener. You've seen what giving everything makes of a person. Why do you imagine you'd be different? Why do you imagine there's a door marked THIRD when no one has ever walked through it?*

"Because," Ansel said through her teeth, "she wasn't alone when she wrote it. Not anymore. We found her page. We're her evidence."

*You're her repetition,* whispered Vaskar. *Hope is just grief that hasn't finished its arithmetic.*

"Read louder," Ansel said.

They read louder.

Something remarkable happened then, something she would spend the rest of her life trying to describe. As the voices layered—Odell's gravel, Marisol's music, Pip's urgency, Serah's iron, four strangers bound to her by roads and wounds and a dead man's bridge—the belief stopped behaving like a possession and started behaving like a *place*. She could stand in it. The voices built it around her, beam by beam, and the hook scrabbling at her sternum was suddenly trying to drag away not a purse but a foundation, and foundations do not come loose.

The pressure broke like a wave against a seawall.

It left her shaking, drenched in cold sweat, the fire burned down to embers, and the page still in her lap, and—she checked frantically, patting her own chest like a woman confirming her heartbeat—still *there*. The third path. The impossible arithmetic. Hers.

Around her, the crew sat breathing hard as if they'd run a mile, though none of them had moved except their mouths.

"It let go," Pip whispered. "Did it—it let go, right? You still—"

"I still believe," Ansel said. Her voice cracked on the second word, and she laughed, wet and disbelieving. "It couldn't take it. It tried and it *couldn't*."

Serah finally turned from the treeline. In the ember-light her face was unreadable, but her voice, when it came, was quieter than Ansel had ever heard it. "I told you. A shared thing has to be taken from everyone at once." She paused. "It will try that eventually. Understand that. The twelfth payment comes due soon, and after it, the thirteenth night. Whatever it does next, it won't be subtle."

"Then we keep reading," Marisol said. It wasn't bravado; it was logistics, stated the way she'd state a route or a ration count. "Every night. All of us. Until the thirteenth night comes and we don't need the page anymore because we'll be standing inside the ending she couldn't see."

Ansel looked around the fire—at the smuggler captain who had hidden her when hiding her cost everything, at the twins who had forgiven and been forgiven, at the exiled knight committing treason nightly just by sitting here—and felt the belief settle deeper, roots going down through stone.

For the first time since the almanac, since the storm, since the page with her own scarred hands drawn on it, she was not afraid of what the moon would take next.

She should have been. Because far to the north, beyond the passes, torches were kindling in their hundreds along the Order's pilgrim road—and at the head of that slow river of faithful light, Grand Bloom Aurelia lifted her face to the same thin moon and smiled, and in her heart, the seed whispered: *Soon.*

* * *

They broke camp at grey dawn, five figures and a carved staff moving south through the frost, toward the Garden, toward the thirteenth night, reading as they walked.
