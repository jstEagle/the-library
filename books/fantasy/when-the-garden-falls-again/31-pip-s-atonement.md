## 31. Pip's Atonement

The Order's camp smelled of incense and iron, and Pip hated it with her whole small body.

She crouched in the drainage ditch at the camp's southern edge, mud to her chin, watching the sentries walk their rounds. They were precise, these Quiet Bloom soldiers—each patrol spaced exactly forty heartbeats apart, each lantern trimmed to the same low gold flame. Pip had counted everything twice, because counting was what she did when she was afraid. Counting was how she kept the world from coming apart.

Forty heartbeats. She'd been counting them since sundown, and she still hadn't stopped shaking.

*You sold her route,* she told herself, pressing her face into the cold mud until it hurt. *You sold Ansel's route for a bottle of feverfew tincture and a promise.* The broker's masked face swam up behind her eyes every time she blinked—the smooth voice, the coin purse heavy as a sleeping cat, the way Pip had told herself it was nothing, just roads, just names, just weather-talk about which pass they'd take through the Serpent's Road. And then Greywater Ford had burned with arrow-fire, and Hesk had died on the bridge holding it so the rest of them could run, and Pip had learned exactly what her information bought.

It bought Hesk's body going into the river. It bought Marisol's screams. It bought the look Odell gave her afterward—not rage, which she could have survived, but that long, tired grief, like a father looking at a child who'd broken something that couldn't be mended.

So now she was lying in filth outside the enemy's camp, stealing back the only thing she could think to steal: Aurelia's ritual focus, the thing the Grand Bloom needed to open Vaskar's prison on the thirteenth night. Two nights away. One moon left in its turn.

Pip had volunteered before anyone else could. She'd seen the look on Ansel's face when they planned it—the empty-eyed girl who used to be their smuggler, staring at maps she no longer understood—and Pip had thought: *I put her in danger. I can pull something out of it.*

Or she would die trying, and maybe that counted too. She hadn't decided yet.

Forty heartbeats. The sentry passed. Pip went over the palisade wall like a rat up a drainpipe, because rats were what she knew how to be.

* * *

Inside, the camp was worse than she'd imagined. Not cruel, exactly—that was the horror of it. Tidy. Rows of white tents with green trim, knights polishing armor by firelight, novices chanting the Litany of the Unfolding in soft unison. Somewhere a cook was baking bread, and the smell made Pip's stomach cramp with longing. These people believed they were saving the world. You could smell belief like you smelled bread.

She moved tent to tent in the shadow-gaps, small and quick, wearing the stolen novice robe she'd taken off a laundry line two miles back. Her fingers found the almanac trick without thinking—Bramble's old trick, the hollowed book, except this time she was the one carrying secrets sewn into false pages. She wondered if Bramble had ever done anything like this. She wondered if he'd have been ashamed of her. Probably. Bramble had believed in clean work.

The command tent stood at the camp's center, guarded by four knights and flying the banner of the High Bloom—a white flower opening on green silk. Pip circled it twice, memorizing sightlines, and nearly gave up twice. Then the horns sounded for evening devotions, and all four guards turned toward the great fire, and the tent flap lifted in the wind like an invitation.

Pip slipped inside.

The focus sat on a table beneath a glass bell, and it was not what she expected. She'd pictured a jewel, a blade, some grand thing. Instead it was small—a seed, dark and veined like a walnut, resting in a bed of moss. It pulsed faintly, slowly, like something breathing in its sleep. Like a heart keeping time for a god.

Vaskar's whispering seed. The thing Aurelia carried in her own chest, grown out and cut free and fed for years on prayer. Pip stared at it and felt, absurdly, watched.

"Don't," she whispered to it. "Don't you dare wake up."

She swapped it for a river stone wrapped in moss, hands steady because she forced them to be, and got the seed into the hollow of her almanac, and was three steps from the flap when the lamp behind the table flared and a voice said:

"You're not a novice."

Aurelia herself sat in the tent's corner, where shadows pooled deepest, hands folded in her lap like a woman at prayer. She was older than Pip expected, silver-haired, with a face like a kind grandmother in an old story—the kind who turned out to be the witch. Her eyes were fixed on Pip with terrible gentleness.

"Novices don't walk like thieves," Aurelia said. "Novices don't breathe like liars. And novices—" she tilted her head, "—don't look at the seed with hate."

Pip ran.

She heard Aurelia rise, unhurried, heard her call out—not an alarm, just a single clear word, *"Bloom"*—and the whole camp came alive around Pip like an anthill kicked. She went under a supply wagon, through a gap between tents, over the palisade where she'd come up, and the first crossbow bolt took her in the shoulder before her feet touched the ground.

The pain was white and total. She fell the far side, rolled down the ditch bank, and ran anyway, because stopping meant dying and dying meant Hesk's death stayed a thing she'd caused instead of a debt she'd paid. Bolts hissed past her ears. Someone shouted distances and bearings in a calm liturgical voice, like reciting grace while shooting a child in the dark.

She ran through the marsh with a bolt in her shoulder and the seed against her heart, and she did not stop until the trees closed over her and the shouting faded and her legs simply quit beneath her.

Then she lay in the bracken, bleeding, and laughed until the laughing turned into something else.

* * *

It took her most of the night to get clear. She tore her sleeve for a bandage, snapped the bolt shaft so it wouldn't shift, and walked when she should have rested and rested when she should have walked, following the crew's agreed fallback line toward the old quarry road. Twice she heard riders. Both times she lay flat in dead ferns and counted heartbeats until they passed.

Dawn found her at the meeting point—a fallen beech over a dry streambed—and no one there yet. She sat with her back against the roots, took out the almanac, and checked the seed. Still pulsing. Still breathing its slow god-breath. She talked to it, because talking kept her awake.

"You're just a seed," she muttered. "My sister grows beans bigger than you." Her vision swam. "Hesk grew pumpkins. Did you know that? Big ones. He'd carry Marisol on his shoulders to see over the fence rows. He never once dropped her." Her voice cracked. "I dropped everything."

The light shifted. Boots on leaves—many boots—and Pip's hand went to her knife before she registered the shapes: Odell's barrel chest, Marisol pale but upright, Serah in half-armor with her sword drawn, and Ansel.

Ansel, who looked at Pip with those emptied grey eyes and said nothing, because she didn't know what there was to say.

"Well?" Odell's voice was gravel. "Tell me you didn't."

Pip opened the almanac with shaking hands and showed him the seed, dark and veined and pulsing in the hollowed pages.

Odell stared at it a long moment. Then he crossed to her, knelt, and gathered her up with one arm the way he used to carry her when she was nine and too stubborn to admit a sprained ankle. He didn't speak. He didn't have to. Pip buried her face in his salt-and-tar coat and shook.

"I'm sorry," she said into the wool. "For the ford. For Hesk. I sold the route, I sold it for medicine, I—I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

"I know," Odell said.

"No, you don't, you have to hear it—you have to *hear* me say it—"

"I know," he said again, gentler. "And I forgive you. But that's not mine alone to give, is it?"

Pip pulled back. The whole crew was there—Marisol with her arm still bound, Serah standing awkward at the edge of the family like a guest at someone else's table. And Ansel, watching with her stranger's face.

Pip went to her last. Her legs barely held. She knelt in the wet leaves, which hurt her shoulder and she didn't care, and held up the almanac like an offering.

"I gave them your route," she said. "People died because of what I sold. Hesk died. And I can't give him back, and I can't undo it, and I don't even know if sorry is a real thing or just a noise people make. But I stole this back. It's not enough. Nothing's enough. But it's what I have." Her voice broke completely. "Forgive me. Please. Or don't—but say it either way. Say it out loud. I can't stand not knowing anymore."

The silence went on so long Pip heard her own blood dripping off her fingertips onto the leaves.

Then Ansel crouched down, slow and careful, and took the almanac from Pip's hands. She studied the seed with her empty grey eyes, and when she spoke her voice was quiet and strange and hers.

"I don't remember you selling anything," she said. "They tell me what happened, but it's like hearing about weather in a country I've never seen. So I can't forgive what I can't remember." Pip's heart cracked down the middle. "But I remember *this*," Ansel went on, tapping her own chest, over her heart. "Serah says memory lives deeper than the head. And right now, looking at you, bleeding for us, something in here says: *this one is ours.* That's what I've got. It'll have to do."

She closed Pip's fingers around the almanac.

"It does," Pip whispered.

"It has to," Ansel said, and almost smiled. "We've got a god to bury in two nights, and I need every hand I trust. So trust me back: you're done paying. You're just ours now."

Marisol limped forward and pressed her forehead to Pip's, the way they'd done since they were children sharing one blanket. Odell put his huge hand on both their heads like a benediction. Even Serah exhaled, and Pip thought she saw the knight's shoulders drop an inch she'd been carrying for weeks.

It lasted four heartbeats. Pip counted.

Then Serah's head came up sharply, eyes on the eastern ridgeline, where dust was rising in a long white plume.

"That's not a patrol," she said. "That's the Order moving. All of them. Tonight they march on the Garden."

Ansel rose, the seed cradled in her arms like something sleeping, and looked east toward Thornfell, toward the Verdant Garden, toward the prison she alone could close.

"Then we stop being hunted," she said, "and start being the hunt. Everyone rides. Now."

And Pip, bleeding and forgiven and lighter than she'd felt since the ford, climbed onto the horse Odell held for her, and rode east with her family toward the thirteenth night.
