## 32. Moon Twelve: The Last Full Payment

The twelfth moon came in like a held breath.

Ansel felt it three days before it arrived—a pressure behind her eyes, a hollowness in her teeth, the same low ache she'd learned to read as the seal taking its measure of her. By the time the moon rose fat and white over the Verdant Garden's broken colonnade, she had stopped pretending to herself that this payment would be like the others. She could feel its shape. It was vast and old and soft at every edge, and it sat in her chest like a stone warmed by long years of sun.

It was going to take Wren. All of Wren.

She had known since Moon Nine—since Thornfell itself had dissolved out of her mind and she'd woken on the Serpent's Road not knowing which way was home until Marisol described the mountains to her, peak by peak, pass by pass, *the tall one with the crooked snowline, the one that looks like an old man sleeping*. The payments were escalating. Memory of a face. A name. A homeland. Love itself, at Moon Seven, though she'd cheated that one with ink and paper. Whatever logic governed the seal, it was stripping her down layer by layer, working from the outer walls of her inward, and now there was only one wall left standing between the seal and the bare room where Ansel Amberdale lived.

The last thing inside it was her grandmother.

* * *

They made camp in the Gardener's courtyard, in the lee of what had once been a greenhouse—the iron ribs of it still stood against the sky like the skeleton of some enormous patient fish. The hedge-prison hummed beneath them, faintly green-lit even now, even at night. Hesk's bridge was two days' march behind them and none of them spoke of it, but Pip had started carving small wooden figures in the evenings, and every one of them was broad-shouldered and gentle-faced, and he gave them away to no one. He kept them in a pouch. That was all anyone needed to know.

Ansel sat apart, as she always did, with the almanac open across her knees and a candle guttering between her boots. Around her the crew arranged themselves with the practiced casualness of people who had learned exactly how much space she needed and how close they could stand within it. Odell sharpened knives that did not need sharpening. Marisol dozed against a saddle, her wound long healed but her sleep still careful. Serah sat nearest, armor off for once, whetstone moving along her sword in slow strokes that matched, whether she knew it or not, the rhythm of the hedge's breathing below.

"You're doing the face," Pip said, from his side of the fire.

"What face?"

"The one where you've decided something terrible and you're trying to decide how to tell us so we won't make it worse."

"I don't have that face."

"You have six faces," Pip said. "I've catalogued them. That's the worst one." He set down his carving knife. "Twelfth moon's tomorrow night, isn't it."

Silence took the fire. Even Odell's knife went still.

"It comes due at moonrise," Ansel said. Her voice came out level, and she was grateful for that, at least. Three years of practice keeping her voice level, and here at the end of everything it finally paid for itself. "I can feel what it wants. It's been circling it for days."

"And?" Odell asked. Gruff, flat, captain's voice—but her eyes weren't on the knives anymore.

"Wren." The name sat strangely in her mouth. It had been sitting more strangely every day. "Everything I have left of my grandmother. Every memory. The cottage. Her hands. Her voice. The way she—" Ansel stopped, because the way she what, and found she could not finish the sentence without something in her chest tearing along a seam she hadn't known was there. She tried again. "The seal doesn't take things halfway. Not anymore. Moon Seven wanted love; I wrote it down and starved it. But this—" She touched the almanac. "I can't write down twenty years. I can't write down a whole person. Paper holds sentences. It doesn't hold the smell of bread on a cold morning, or—"

Her voice failed after all. So much for three years of practice.

Serah set down the whetstone. "Then don't give it willingly," she said. "Let it tear. Fight it the way you fought Moon Seven. We'll hold you through it. We'll say her name at you until your ears bleed."

"That's not—" Ansel pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. When she lowered them, the fire doubled and swam. "When it fights me, it takes badly. You've seen what it leaves. After Moon Seven I couldn't feel anything about any of you for four days. Four days of looking at you all and feeling *nothing*, and knowing I was supposed to feel something, and knowing the nothing was wrong. If I fight it over Wren—if I make it rip her out in pieces—it'll leave wreckage. It'll leave her half-gone, mangled, a stranger wearing her memory. And I'll spend whatever's left of me carrying that instead of her."

"So you're saying what, exactly?" Marisol had woken. Of course she had; Marisol slept like a cat with one ear always turned toward trouble. "You're saying you're just going to hand her over?"

"I'm saying," Ansel said slowly, feeling each word as she chose it, testing it like a foothold on wet stone, "that there's a difference between losing someone and being robbed of them. I've been robbed eleven times now. I'm done being robbed. If the seal wants Wren—if Wren built this seal, if Wren paid into it before me—then I'll pay the way she paid. Open-handed. On my feet. Looking at it."

Nobody spoke. The hedge breathed below them, long and slow.

"That's horseshit," Pip said at last, thick-voiced, "and also it's the bravest thing I've ever heard, and I hate you for both."

"Both are fair," Ansel said.

Odell grunted. It was, from Odell, a eulogy and an embrace in one sound. "What do you need?"

"Paper," Ansel said. "Ink. And one night."

* * *

She spent the first hours of the dark writing.

Not everything—she had learned by now that everything was impossible, that grief does not fit through the eye of a pen. Instead she wrote what a person is when you reduce them to what they planted in you. *Wren said: mend the fence before you need the fence.* *Wren said: never trust a road that's too easy.* *Wren smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and the particular green smell of crushed nettle stems, and she used to hum when she worked, tuneless, like the kettle deciding to sing.*

She wrote until her hand cramped and Marisol silently took the pen from her and made her drink tea, and then she wrote again. And between the pages of instruction and inventory—because that was how her grief organized itself, into lists, into routes, into things that could be memorized like smuggler passes—one sentence kept surfacing, over and over, the way a drowned thing surfaces.

*I only saw two endings because I had no one to imagine a third with.*

Wren's final line. Torn from her own book, hidden in a buried cottage, waiting a hundred years for a granddaughter who hadn't been born yet. Ansel had carried it sewn into her coat lining since Nettlebed, and she had read it perhaps four hundred times, and it had not dulled once. Other memories dulled. This one rang.

Near midnight she put down the pen and did the last thing, the hard thing. She closed her eyes and walked through Wren deliberately—not fleeing the memories, not clutching them, just walking through, the way you walk through a house the night before you leave it forever, touching the doorframes. The cottage kitchen with its crooked shelf. Wren teaching her to bind a cut with moss and cobweb. Wren's laugh, rare and startling, like ice cracking on a spring pond. The night Wren told her stories about a god under a mountain and a garden grown over him, and little Ansel had thought they were just stories, because children think everything is just stories until the stories come for them.

And underneath all of it, older than all of it: arms. Being very small and being held, and a heartbeat under her ear, steady as a hearth.

*That one,* she thought, standing in the doorway of her own past. *If I get to keep one. Let it be that one.*

She knew she wouldn't get to keep it. That was the shape of the payment. The seal didn't bargain.

* * *

Moonrise came up copper behind the eastern crags, and then the copper drained away and left the moon white and enormous and merciless, and Ansel stood alone in the center of the courtyard with the crew ringed around her at the distance she had asked for—close enough to catch her, far enough not to touch her while it happened—and she opened herself to the seal the way you open a door for a guest you cannot refuse.

It came up through the ground like sap rising.

She felt it enter her feet, her legs, her spine, patient and green and utterly impersonal, the way winter is impersonal. There was no malice in it. That was somehow the worst part. Eleven payments in, she understood the seal at last: it was not cruel. It was a lock, and locks do not hate the key. It simply required what it required, and it had been built by a woman who had loved the world enough to feed it everything she had.

*Take it,* Ansel thought, standing straight, chin up, hands loose at her sides. *Take it open-handed. Take it on my feet.*

The memories began to leave.

It did not hurt the way she expected. It felt like falling asleep in summer—warmth moving up through her from the ground, and images loosening, drifting, becoming lighter and less hers. The crooked shelf went first, dissolving like sugar in water. Then the smell of rosemary. Then the humming. She watched each one go with something that was not calm but had made its peace with calm, the way a soldier makes peace with a battle already joined.

*Goodbye,* she thought at each of them. Not *don't go*. Goodbye. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole point.

The cottage went. The moss-and-cobweb lesson went. Wren's laugh went, and Ansel felt it leave like a bird leaving a branch, and she did not reach for it, and her knees shook, and she stayed on her feet.

The arms went last.

She felt them go and something in her cried out—not aloud, her voice held, but somewhere deeper than voice, in the place where the small child still lived who had once fallen asleep against a heartbeat steady as a hearth. For one terrible moment the seal paused, weighing, considering, and Ansel understood with perfect clarity that she could fight now, could snatch that one back, could keep the heartbeat and lose everything else to the tearing—

She let it go.

*Goodbye. Thank you. Goodbye.*

And then it was done, and the warmth withdrew, and Ansel Winterbourne—who had no idea, anymore, why that name mattered or whose name it had been before hers, or even, quite, what a grandmother was to a girl except a word—stood in the courtyard under the twelfth moon with an empty place inside her shaped exactly like a woman she could no longer see.

* * *

She didn't remember sinking to her knees, but she was on them, and Marisol was there, and Marisol's arms were around her, and Ansel was crying—great silent racking sobs that seemed to come from nowhere, from everywhere, from the empty place itself.

"I don't know why I'm crying," she heard herself say, and heard how young she sounded. "I don't—I know I lost something, I know it was important, I know it was *her*, but I can't—there's no picture. There's nothing. It's just a hole that hurts."

"I know," Marisol said, rocking her slightly, the way you rock someone smaller than you even when they're not. "We know. That's all right. That's what we're for."

"She sounds like she was good," Ansel whispered. "From the letter. From what I wrote down. She sounds like she was so good, and I can't even—"

"She was," Odell said, from somewhere above her. Rough. Certain. "Anyone who taught you to be this stubborn had to be."

Pip crouched down into her eyeline. His face was blotchy; he'd been crying too, and wasn't hiding it, which was Pip's particular form of courage. "Okay," he said. "Ceremony time. You said you wanted ceremony. Everybody up. Yes, Odell, you too, up."

They got up. All of them—even Serah, who stood a little apart, hand over the sigil of an Order that no longer existed for her, watching with an expression Ansel was too emptied-out to read. Pip built the fire higher. Marisol brought the candle. And Ansel, kneeling in the circle of them, took out the pages she had written in the dark hours before the moon rose, and began to read her grandmother back into the world in the only form she had left.

*"Wren said: mend the fence before you need the fence."*

One by one, as she read, the others took the pages from her and read them back—each line spoken twice, once by her and once by a voice that loved her, so that the words went into the night air and into all their memories at once. *Never trust a road that's too easy. Count your knots twice in the dark. Fear is a compass if you hold it steady.* Page after page, passed around the fire, until the crew had read every scrap of Wren Amberdale that existed on paper, and the words belonged to all of them now, not just to the hole inside Ansel.

"This is how we keep her," Marisol said quietly, when the last page was read. "All of us. Five heads holding one woman. They can't take what everybody carries."

Ansel looked down at the final page—the one she had saved for last, the one she had copied out separately in a steadier hand, the one that had been sewn into her coat lining since Nettlebed and would be sewn there still when this was all over, however it ended.

*"I only saw two endings,"* she read, *"because I had no one to imagine a third with."*

She looked up. At Odell and Pip and Marisol. At Serah, standing in the firelight in her shirtsleeves like a woman who had burned her own flag. At the pouch of little carved figures in Pip's pocket, and the empty bedroll that still traveled with them because none of them would pack it away.

"Well," Ansel said, and her voice cracked, and she let it crack, because she was done with level voices tonight. "Grandmother. Look around. You've got five."

The fire popped. Somewhere below them, deep beneath the regrowing hedge, something vast shifted in its sleep—and far to the north, where no one in the courtyard could see it, lights were kindling along the ridgeline of the Thornfell passes. Too many lights. Too orderly. Marching in the formation of a faith coming to collect its god.
