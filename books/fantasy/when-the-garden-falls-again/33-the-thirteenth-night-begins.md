## 33. The Thirteenth Night Begins

The moon came up wrong.

Odell had watched the sky over Thornfell for thirty years of smuggling runs—knew every phase, every halo, every liar's fog the mountains threw up—and she had never seen anything like this. The thirteenth moon did not rise so much as *arrive*, swelling fat and red-gold above the Verdant Garden's broken terraces as though it had been waiting just below the horizon for a century, patient as a debt collector. Its light fell on the regrown hedge in long amber stripes, and where it touched, the leaves shivered.

"They're coming," Ansel said.

She stood at Odell's shoulder on the eastern wall of the inner garden, her hands flat against the old stone, and there was something in her voice that Odell had learned to fear over these three terrible weeks—not fear itself, but its opposite. Calm with edges on it. The girl had given away pieces of herself to a book until what remained was mostly blade.

"How many?" Odell asked.

"Four hundred. Maybe more." Ansel's scarred fingers tapped once against the stone, a habit she'd never explained and no one had ever needed to. "Torches at the Serpent's Road fork. They'll reach the outer gate within the hour."

"You can feel them?"

"I can feel the book feeling them." She touched the satchel at her hip, where Wren's prophecy slept under oilcloth. "It's like standing next to someone else's heartbeat."

Below them, the garden was alive with everything they'd managed to gather. Nettlebed villagers—forty-odd farmers and grandmothers with scythes and hunting bows—were stacking brush along the western hedge-line under old Tam's shouted direction. Three smuggler ships' worth of crew from Hollowfen, come downriver on Odell's marker and Marisol's furious letters, moved crates of moon-ore and blackpowder into the sunken cloister. A dozen knights in torn grey livery, Bloom sigils scratched out, stood awkwardly apart from everyone else near the ruined fountain, led by a broad woman with a burned face who would not say her name.

And at the center of it all, at the base of the living hedge that covered the door to a god's prison, Serah Ivo knelt in the dirt with her sword laid across her knees, praying to an Order she no longer belonged to.

* * *

Serah had stopped asking the Bloom for guidance eleven days ago. Tonight she prayed anyway, but the words came out sideways, aimed at no one: *let me be fast enough, let me be strong enough, let me not freeze when I see Aurelia's face.*

The hedge behind her breathed. That was the only word for it—the great green wall rose forty feet above the sunken garden, woven of thorn and hawthorn and things that had no common name, and in the still air it expanded and contracted like the flank of a sleeping animal. Beneath it, deep enough that she felt him through her knees rather than heard him, Vaskar turned over in his sleep. The whole valley hummed with it, a note below hearing that made her teeth ache.

"Serah." Ansel dropped down beside her, light-footed even now. "Tell me again why you're not up on the walls."

"Because if they break through," Serah said, "it happens here, and I intend to be standing between them and this door when it does."

"And if it's Aurelia herself?"

Serah looked at the hedge. Through the woven branches, here and there, glinted pale shapes—berries, or teeth, or both. "Then I'll do what has to be done. You should be above. If the ritual starts, you're the only one who can stop it."

"The book says the Key stops it or starts it." Ansel's mouth twisted. "The book says a lot of things. My grandmother wrote two endings and couldn't imagine a third, and I've spent twelve moon-turns paying through the nose for the privilege of choosing." Her voice caught slightly on the last word—on whatever memory had been taken that week; Serah had stopped keeping track because keeping track was unbearable. "I'm not hiding on a wall while other people bleed for my prison."

"It's not your—"

"It's exactly mine. That's the whole problem." But Ansel put her hand briefly on Serah's shoulder, and the touch said what the argument didn't: *stay alive, knight.* Then she was gone, up the stairs two at a time, and Serah returned to her prayer, which had simplified itself over the weeks into a single line, repeated:

*Let me be better than what they made me.*

* * *

Pip had never been brave. He wanted that on the record, somewhere, in ink, in case anyone survived to write accounts. Pip was a coward who kept doing brave things by accident, and tonight he crouched in the sunken cloister with a blackpowder charge under each arm and his heart going like a drummer boy's, telling himself the same lie he always told: *just one more minute, then I'll run.*

"Pip." Marisol limped down the cloister steps toward him, her healed-but-stiff leg dragging slightly, a bow across her back. "You're talking to yourself again."

"I'm strategizing aloud. It's different."

"It's the same thing you did before the ford." Her voice went quiet on the word *ford*, the way everyone's did now. Hesk's name was a stone in all their throats; they stepped around it carefully, and sometimes, like now, they stumbled on it anyway. "You talked yourself steady, and then you held the line with me, and then we lived. So talk."

Pip looked at the charges in his arms. Moon-ore and salt-petre, packed by Odell, meant to collapse the eastern colonnade onto anyone who came through it. Meant to kill people who were, once, his brothers-in-craft—he'd sold information to their kind, after all, and bled for it, and been forgiven for it, which was somehow worse.

"What if I'm not sorry enough yet?" he said. "For Hesk. What if I spend my whole life being sorry and it's still the wrong currency?"

Marisol sat down beside him on the cold step, took one of the charges out of his arms, and set it aside. Then she hugged him, hard, bow and all, her chin hooked over his shoulder.

"You're asking forgiveness to pay a debt," she said. "That's not how it works. Forgiveness isn't money, Pip. It's a fire. You don't buy your way in. Somebody just has to decide you're warm."

Above them, on the eastern wall, a horn sounded—one long note, then two short ones. Tam's signal. And beneath it, faint but rising, the sound Pip had been dreading since dusk: boots. Hundreds of boots, coming down through the orchard ruins in disciplined silence, torchlight blooming among the dead apple trees like fever spots.

"Fire later," Marisol said, standing, nocking an arrow. "Warmth now. Go light your lines."

* * *

Aurelia, Grand Bloom of the Order of the Quiet Bloom, walked at the head of her faithful and felt nothing but love.

This was what her knights would never understand, she thought, surveying the ruined garden as they advanced—the broken terraces silvered under the huge red moon, the enemy scurrying along the walls like ants defending a nest. They thought her cruel, or mad, or hungry. But cruelty required hatred, and madness required confusion, and she was neither hateful nor confused. She loved the world. That was the entire truth of her. She loved the world the way a mother loves a fevered child, and the world was fevered, and Vaskar's fire was the only medicine left.

In her chest, behind her sternum, the seed whispered.

*They are few,* it murmured, in a voice like wind through a burned forest. *They are afraid. Open me, little bloom, and I will make the winter end forever.*

"Soon," she said aloud, and some of the nearest knights glanced at her, then quickly away. Let them look. In an hour none of it would matter—in an hour the god would rise and burn the rot out of creation, and the faithful would stand in His light and be remade.

She raised her hand, and four hundred voices fell silent, and the whole orchard went still enough to hear the hedge breathing beyond the walls.

"People of the Quiet Bloom," Aurelia called, and her voice carried without effort, pitched to carry further than any human throat should. "Behind those walls sleeps the god who made the spring. For a hundred years, liars have told you He is a monster. Tonight we wake Him, and tonight you learn what monsters actually are: the small gray lives you've been told to settle for. Follow me, and be remade."

She lowered her hand.

The Order came on like a tide, silent except for their boots, torches streaming backward in the wind, and above the walls the defenders loosed their first arrows into that advancing light, and the thirteenth night began.

* * *

It did not go the way battles go in songs. There was no clean line, no charge and countercharge. What happened instead was controlled chaos, the kind Odell knew from boarding actions—narrow spaces, bad light, everything happening everywhere at once, victory decided in inches and seconds.

The Order hit the eastern colonnade first, exactly as Ansel had predicted, and Pip's blackpowder brought half of it down on their vanguard in a roar that shook apples loose from trees dead fifty years. Screaming. Dust lit red by the moon. Through the gap came the second wave anyway, shields locked, and Marisol's archers on the wall picked at them like gulls after a haul, and the villagers' scythes met the first shields at the breach in a grinding, ugly scrum that had no glory in it whatsoever.

Odell fought at the breach herself, axe in both fists, roaring orders between blows—"Left side! Stack the barrels! *Tam, your people fall back to the second wall!*"—and beside her fought the burned-faced knight with no name, who swung a maul like she was threshing wheat and said nothing at all, which Odell decided she respected.

On the western hedge-line, the rogue knights held. Barely. Serah could see the moment the line began to bend—a shield went down, then another—and she was moving before she'd decided to move, wading into the crush with her sword flat-edged, breaking weapons, breaking grips, shouting the old formation calls of the Bloom because Bloom-trained bodies answered them before their minds could object. It was grotesque and effective: her own training turned against her own Order, knights who had prayed beside her staggering back from a voice they'd obeyed for years.

"Serah Ivo!" someone cried from the enemy press, horrified. "Traitor! *Traitor!*"

"Yes," she said, and cut the spearpoint off his haft. "Now kneel down and think about it."

Ansel was everywhere and nowhere. Odell caught glimpses of her along the walls—a flicker on the roofline of the gardener's cottage, a shape dropping into the cloister, always moving toward the center, always toward the hedge, and always with one hand pressed to the satchel at her hip as though holding shut a door. Once, passing close in the chaos, she grabbed Odell's arm and shouted, over the din: "They're not all attacking! A column's peeling off—they're going *under*, through the root-cellars!"

"Aurelia?"

"She's not with the main force. She's already inside the hill." Ansel's eyes were wild and clear and terribly certain. "The book is screaming, Odell. The door is opening from the other side."

Then she was gone again, down into the earth, and Odell turned back to the breach with her heart gone cold, and fought harder.

* * *

Under the hill, the world went quiet.

Ansel descended alone through root-cellars older than the village, past walls furred with white threads of root, following a sound only she could hear—the book, clutched now in both hands, humming against her ribs like a hive. Behind her, far above, the battle rolled on as muffled thunder. Ahead of her, the roots thickened, braided, became architecture: the living hedge grown inward, downward, around the sleeping god for a hundred years, tended by hands no one living had seen.

The Gardener waited for her at the threshold of the inner sanctum, and it was weeping.

It had Wren's face—or a version of it, worn smooth by a century, features blurred like a carving left out in rain—and it stood before a gap in the hedge-wall where green branches hung torn and smoking, and sap ran down its fingers like blood.

"You promised," it said to her, in a voice full of rust and love. "You said you'd come back before the thirteenth turn. You said we'd tend it together, Wren. You said—"

"I'm not Wren." Ansel's voice cracked. She reached out slowly, the way you'd approach a wounded animal, and took the Gardener's sap-wet hand. "Wren was my grandmother. She wrote the book. She sealed this door. And she left you here alone for a hundred years, and I am so sorry—I'm so sorry—but right now I need you to help me keep this closed."

The Gardener stared at her with rain-blurred eyes. Somewhere beyond the torn hedge, in the dark beneath the world, something vast exhaled, and the roots around them shivered, and dust sifted down like snow.

"He's waking," the Gardener whispered. "She opened the door from the outside. The one who smells of flowers and burning. She's speaking the old words, and he's listening, and I'm only made of her leftovers—I'm not strong enough, I was never strong enough—"

"You're not alone anymore." Ansel slung the satchel off her shoulder and pressed the prophecy book into the Gardener's sap-sticky hands. "Hold this. Whatever happens, hold this, and listen for my voice. Can you do that? Can you listen for me?"

The Gardener clutched the book to its chest like a child. And Ansel drew her knife, turned to the ragged gap in the hedge, and stepped through it into the dark where a god was waking—where, somewhere ahead, flower-scented and burning, the Grand Bloom of the Order of the Quiet Bloom stood with her arms raised and her heart full of love, calling the dawn of the end of the world.

The last thing the Gardener saw was the girl's silhouette against a light that was not moonlight, and the last thing it heard was her voice, steady as a root:

*Aurelia. Step away from my door.*
