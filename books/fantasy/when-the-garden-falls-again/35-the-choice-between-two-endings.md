## 35. The Choice Between Two Endings

The heart of the Verdant Garden was not a chamber. It was a wound.

Ansel stood at the edge of it, boots planted on roots as thick as temple pillars, and looked down into the place where a god had been buried alive for a hundred years. The hedge-prison rose around her in walls of living green gone strange with age—blackthorn braided through hawthorn, nightshade flowering in the seams, every branch grown over and over itself until the whole structure breathed like something sleeping. Which, she supposed, it was.

Below her, at the bottom of a hollow ringed by those impossible roots, the seal lay open.

It had been beautiful once. She could see that even now, even with war shaking loose dust from the canopy a hundred feet above and the distant percussion of Aurelia's ritual still rolling through the stone like a second heartbeat. The seal had been a garden gate—wrought from white iron and woven vine, tall enough for a giant to walk through, and Wren had grown it shut with a century of patience and a lifetime of magic. Now it hung torn down its center, the two halves peeled apart like petals forced open by an impatient hand, and through the gap poured a light that was not light at all. It was *attention*. The focused, hungry attention of something vast turning over in its sleep and noticing, for the first time in a hundred years, that the world above had come to collect it.

Vaskar's first breath had cracked the mountains. His second was coming.

"Ansel." Serah's voice, raw and close. The knight stood three steps behind her, sword drawn, blood on her cheek that might have been hers or someone else's—the duel with Aurelia had ended badly and then suddenly, with Aurelia walking away untouched into the sanctum while Serah lay gasping among the broken pews of what had once been a chapel of the Quiet Bloom. "We have to move. The ritual is nearly—"

"I know."

"You don't understand, the High Bloom has already begun the *unbinding*, if we don't stop it at the source—"

"*I know.*"

Serah went quiet. Ansel heard her breathing, heard the small wet sound of a rib that was broken and knew it, and felt the old familiar ache of caring about someone so much it hurt to stand near them.

She climbed down.

The roots made a stair of themselves, or perhaps they had always been one—Wren's magic remembering its purpose even now, offering footholds to the granddaughter it had been waiting a hundred years to receive. Ansel descended into the hollow with the prophecy book clutched against her chest, and the closer she came to the open seal, the louder the whispering became.

*Little key,* said the dark beyond the gate. *Little thirteenth key. I know what they took from you.*

She did not answer. She had learned that much in eleven moon-turns: never answer the god.

The book was warm against her sternum. It had been warm since Moon Nine, when Thornfell itself had been scraped out of her memory like marrow from a bone, and she had woken on the Serpent's Road knowing the mountains only because Pip had spent an entire night describing them to her in loving, profane detail—*the third peak looks like a grumpy old man, Anse, honest to all the dead gods, you used to laugh at it*—and she had navigated by his words alone, by love transcribed into directions, and it had worked. It had worked because they had built her a map out of themselves.

Now she stood before the gate, and the map was useless, and there were only two endings left.

She opened the book.

The pages turned themselves—she had stopped being surprised by this around Moon Four—and stopped at the final written page. Wren's handwriting filled it top to bottom, tight and urgent, the script of a woman who had run out of time:

*Two futures, and only two, because I saw them alone. In the first, the Key closes the gate and pays the whole price—all of it, every memory, every love, everything that makes her herself—and Vaskar sleeps another thousand years, and the Key walks out of the Garden empty as winter, a stranger to everyone who ever held her name.*

*In the second, the Key refuses, and the gate stays open, and the world burns.*

*I searched for a third. I swear it on my own grave, I searched. But I was alone when I wrote this, and a woman alone can only see the roads she has walked. If you are reading this, child, forgive me. I gave you a choice between dying and forgetting, and I never thought to ask whether anyone would stand beside you and remember for you.*

Ansel read it twice. Three times. Her hands were shaking.

Behind her, above her, the battle for the Garden roared on—she could hear Odell's bellowed commands, the crackle of smuggler fire-oil, the singing shriek of rogue Bloom knights turning their blades against their own Order. Nettlebed villagers had come with sickles and lanterns. Half the Hollowfen fleets were holding the eastern terraces. All of them, every one of them, had come for *her*—for the invisible girl who had spent three years making sure no one had any reason to.

And below her feet, the seal breathed its last breaths, and the whispering swelled.

*I can give it back.* The voice was honey over gravel, patient as erosion. *Every memory. Your mother's face—you cry sometimes and cannot say why; I can give you the reason. Your name. Amberdale. Say it, little key, say it and feel it fit again. Your grandmother—I will show you Wren laughing. Your mountains. Your home. Everything they took, returned, and all it costs is that you let me rise. You were always going to lose yourself tonight. The only question is whether you lose yourself for nothing.*

That was the trap of it. That was the exquisite, unbearable craft of the thing. Because he wasn't lying. She checked—she had learned to check, Moon Seven, when the seventh payment had tried to eat her love for the crew and she had caught the lie mid-swallow—he wasn't lying. He could do it. A god could pour a life back into a person like wine into a cup, and she would walk out of this hollow *whole*, and the world would burn behind her, and Hesk would still be dead under Greywater Ford, and Bramble would still be ash in Hollowfen, but she—she would remember why she cried.

Her eyes stung. Her hands shook.

*Or,* the god whispered, gentler now, almost kind, *close the gate. Save your burning little world. And forget them forever. Forget the captain who fed you. Forget the twins who argued over which of them you liked better. Forget the knight who committed treason for you. Forget the sentence in your coat. You will stand in the snow this winter and strangers will feed you soup and you will not know why they look at you like that. Is that saving? Is that a victory? I am offering you mercy, Ansel Amberdale. They are offering you nothing.*

"They," Ansel said, and her voice cracked, and she let it, "are standing on the terraces above us right now bleeding for a girl they met hauling contraband ore. That's not nothing. That's—" Her throat closed. She pressed her palm flat against the book. "That's the whole point, you great sleeping idiot. That's the *third ending*."

Silence. Then, softly, amused: *There is no third ending. Your grandmother looked. Your grandmother, who grew these very roots, who sealed me with her own hands—she looked, and found two.*

"She looked alone." Ansel knelt at the threshold of the gate, the torn halves of white iron on either side of her like the doors of a tomb. Below the seal, deeper than the light, she could feel him—vast, coiled, furious, *afraid*. Gods were afraid too. That was the secret no one told you. "You keep saying it like it doesn't matter. She looked alone. She wrote it herself: *a woman alone can only see the roads she has walked.* Well, I didn't walk alone. I just spent a year pretending I did, and then a year learning I was wrong."

She untied the cord at her coat and drew out the folded papers—Moon Seven's insurance, the external memory, the fierce clumsy method that had saved her love from the payment that came for it. Pages and pages of her own handwriting. *Odell takes his tea bitter enough to strip rust. Marisol hums when she sews and denies it. Pip talks to fill silence and I would kill for that silence-breaking voice. Hesk carried me four miles through the ford country and apologized for sweating on me. Serah sleeps sitting up, sword across her knees, like a woman waiting to be forgiven.* And at the bottom of the last page, copied out in careful letters she had traced a dozen times until the shape lived somewhere deeper than memory:

*I had no one to imagine a third ending with.*

"Well," Ansel said, to the god, to the gate, to the grandmother whose unfinished page had started all of this, "I'm not alone either."

She tore the pages free.

Not to burn them. Never to burn them. She pressed them flat against the open seam of the seal, against the white iron and the wounded vine, and spoke the words Wren had left half-written on the thirteenth page—the ones Serah had helped her decipher by lamplight, the ones that required not a death and not a forgetting but a *giving*: memory offered willingly, freely, anchored. Not erased. Anchored. Held on the other side of the seal by everyone she loved, kept safe the way you keep a fire alive—not by hiding it from the wind but by feeding it together.

The seal drank the pages like rain on drought.

And the crew—she felt it happen, felt it as surely as a hand closing around hers—felt it too. Somewhere above, on the burning terraces, Odell dropped to one knee without knowing why. Pip grabbed Marisol's arm and said, "*She's doing it, she's doing it right now, hold on to me*," and Marisol held on. Serah, broken-ribbed and bleeding at the top of the root-stairs, pressed her palm to the blackthorn wall and whispered the line she had memorized from Wren's page, the anchor-line, the one the Order had suppressed for a century because it was the one thing that could undo a thousand years of doctrine: *what the Key gives in love, the loved ones hold.*

The gate began to close.

Vaskar screamed. It was not a sound; it was a season changing wrong, it was every avalanche at once, it was grief the size of a mountain range—and through the narrowing gap his voice came one last time, no longer honey, no longer kind, just enormous and betrayed: *YOU WILL NOT KNOW THEM. YOU WILL STAND IN THE SNOW AND NOT KNOW THEM—*

"Yes," Ansel said.

She was crying. She found she didn't mind. Whatever was about to be taken, she wanted it witnessed—wanted to feel every ounce of its weight as it left, the way you feel the full weight of a burden only at the moment you set it down.

"But here's what you don't understand," she said, and put both hands against the closing gate, and pushed, and the roots answered her, a hundred years of Wren's patience rising up through her arms like a tide. "You're taking what I *remember*. You're not taking what's *true*. They'll still have carried me. They'll still tell me the stories. And next winter, when some empty-eyed stranger comes to their fire, they'll feed her soup anyway. That's not forgetting. That's—" The gap was a handspan wide now. Light poured through it, and attention, and fury, and beneath all of it, faintly, incredibly, fear. "That's love with nowhere to live yet. It'll find me. They'll make sure of it. My grandmother couldn't see this ending because she had no one to imagine it with."

She smiled through the tears, at the god, at the dark, at the last sliver of his attention fixed on her face.

"I had a whole crew of idiots."

The gate closed.

The seal took everything—her mother's face, her name, the grumpy-old-man peak, the taste of Odell's terrible tea, the sound of Pip's voice filling silences, the weight of Hesk's shoulder, the sight of Serah sleeping upright with her sword, the smell of Bramble's shop, the sentence in her coat, the word *home*, the word *Amberdale*, the word *herself*—it took it all, willingly given, freely paid, and Ansel Amberdale stood at the heart of the Verdant Garden holding an empty book in empty hands, feeling the strangest sensation of having set down something enormously heavy and forgotten what it was.

Above her, muffled by a hundred feet of healing hedge, the battle was turning. The mountains had stopped cracking. Somewhere a man with a gruff voice was shouting her—no. Shouting *someone's* name.

Footsteps on the root-stairs. Hurried, uneven, desperate. A woman in broken armor came down into the hollow with blood on her face and terror in her eyes, and stopped when she saw Ansel standing there, whole and calm and looking at her like a stranger.

"Ansel?" the knight said. Her voice broke on the name. "Ansel, it's me. It's Serah. Can you hear me?"

Ansel tilted her head. The stranger seemed kind. The stranger seemed to be crying, which was confusing, and reaching out toward her, which was more confusing still.

"I'm sorry," Ansel said politely. "Do I know you?"

* * *

They carried her out through the regrowing Garden, past the hedge knitting itself closed over the god's tomb, past the Gardener weeping green tears among the roots it had tended for a hundred years. They carried her because her legs worked fine but she kept stopping to stare at things—lanterns, faces, the sky—with the wide wondering eyes of someone seeing the world for the first time, which, in every way that mattered, she was.

Pip walked beside her the whole way, talking. Talking the way Pip talked, filling every silence, telling her about a grumpy old man shaped like a peak, about tea bitter enough to strip rust, about a girl with scarred hands who had once guided a shipment of moon-ore through a storm and never once smiled at anyone.

Ansel listened. She didn't understand any of it.

But when the stranger called Pip paused for breath, she found—to her own surprise—that she minded the quiet.

"Keep talking," she said.

And Pip, weeping and laughing at once, said, "Oh, Anse. Oh, you have no idea how long I've waited to hear someone say that."

Somewhere far below them, behind a hundred years of new-grown hedge, a god slept and dreamed of a girl who had beaten him with a crew of idiots. And somewhere ahead, in a safehouse buried deep in mountains she no longer recognized, a fire was waiting, and people who loved her were already planning how to teach a stranger to be theirs again.

The Garden fell. The Garden bloomed. And Ansel—whoever she had been, whoever she would become—walked out of it into the snow, held upright on either side by two people whose names she did not know, and did not let go.
