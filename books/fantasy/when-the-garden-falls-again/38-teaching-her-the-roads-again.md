## 38. Teaching Her the Roads Again

The thaw came late to Thornfell that year, and when it came, it came all at once—snowmelt roaring down the passes in silver ropes, the whole mountain range groaning and dripping like some enormous creature waking from a long sleep. Marisol stood at the mouth of the Kestrel Cut with her arms crossed against the last of the morning chill and watched Ansel look at the pass as if she were seeing it for the first time.

Which, of course, she was.

"You walked this," Pip said, hopping up onto a boulder beside them, their boots swinging. "Three years ago, give or take. Carried forty pounds of moon-ore through here in a whiteout with a broken rib and didn't say a word about it until we were over the ridge."

Ansel turned to look at them. Her face did the thing it always did now—that careful, polite blankness, like a shut door in a house where every window had been painted over. "I don't remember."

"We know." Marisol said it gently, but not too gently. They'd learned that much in the winter: pity made her flinch worse than cruelty ever had. "That's rather the point, love. We're going to tell you about you until you believe us."

"Or until you get bored of us," Pip added. "Whichever comes first. Historically, boredom wins."

Something flickered at the corner of Ansel's mouth. Not quite a smile. But the ghost of one, maybe—the place where a smile used to live before someone else moved into the house. "I doubt I'll get bored of the mountains," she said. "They're..." She paused, searching. Marisol watched her do it, watched her reach into that hollowed-out place and come back with nothing, and something in Marisol's chest twisted and held. "They're familiar," Ansel finished, sounding almost surprised.

"Familiar," Pip repeated, delighted. "She says *familiar*. Mari, write it down. Day forty-one of spring, the stranger knows the mountains are familiar."

"I'm standing right here," Ansel said.

"That's why I'm narrating."

Marisol laughed despite herself and started up the trail. Behind her, she heard Ansel's boots find the path—hesitant at first, then steadier—and heard Pip fall into step beside her, chattering the way Pip chattered when they were nervous, which was always, which was how everyone knew Pip loved you.

* * *

The plan had been Odell's, though he'd pretended it wasn't. He'd spread his old route-maps across the hearth-table one night in late winter and grunted at them for an hour while the rest of them pretended to read, and then he'd said, "Come thaw, we walk 'em. All of 'em. The ones she ran for three years." And when Hesk's empty chair had gone quiet in the corner of everyone's mind, he'd added, roughly, "It's what he'd have wanted. Showing her the roads. He carried half of 'em on his back."

So they walked them.

The first week was the Kestrel Cut and the Widow's Stair, and Marisol told stories the whole way—not grand stories, because they didn't have those, but small ones, the kind that only matter to the people who were there. The time Ansel had talked a toll-keeper out of double payment by reciting his own ledger back to him from memory. The time she'd found a shortcut through a rockfall that saved them two days and never once said *I told you so*, though she'd had every right. The time she'd sat up all night with a fevered stranger on the road because leaving him would have been faster, and cheaper, and easier.

"She wasn't kind," Pip said, perched on a ledge above Marisol, legs dangling. "Don't get the wrong idea. She was the least kind person I ever met. She just *did* kind things anyway, like it annoyed her."

"It did annoy her," Marisol confirmed. "You could see it. Like a cat bringing you a dead mouse and being furious about it."

Ansel, walking below them with her hood up, said nothing. But that evening at the fire, when Marisol handed her the tea without being asked—strong, no honey, the way Ansel had apparently taken it for years—she drank it without comment, and her shoulders came down from around her ears by a full inch.

Small victories. Marisol was learning to count in small victories.

* * *

The trouble came at Hollowfen.

They'd debated skipping it. The city still smelled of Bramble's burned shop; the name *Amberdale* was still whispered in certain corners like a curse or a prayer, depending on who was whispering. But the routes went through Hollowfen—all of them did, eventually, the way all rivers go to the sea—and Odell said you couldn't teach a woman her own roads by lying to her about where they led.

So they went in quiet, hoods up, and Marisol took her down Rope-Walk Lane herself, past the blackened shell of the alchemist's shop, its windows like dead eyes, its doorway still wearing the scorch-mark of a hand that had been reaching out.

"This is where Bramble kept shop," Marisol said. "He was your mentor. Before any of us. He taught you letters and ledgers and which lords' seals were forged most often, and he called you *little wren* because—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Because of your grandmother. You didn't know that either, back then. Neither did he, not really. Not until the end."

Ansel stood very still in front of the ruin. The wind off the harbor moved her hair, and Marisol saw her hands—the scarred hands, the hands from the prophecy book—curl slowly at her sides.

"Tell me about him," Ansel said quietly. "Not what happened. Him."

And oh, that was the trick of it, Marisol thought later. That was the whole trick. Not the grief—they couldn't give her the grief, it belonged to a woman who no longer existed—but the *person*. You could rebuild a person from the outside in, story by story, kindness by remembered kindness, until the shape of them stood solid enough that whatever lived inside could grow to fill it.

So she told her. About Bramble's terrible singing and his worse tea. About how he'd pretended to be stingy and secretly paid the crew's debts twice. About how he'd looked at Ansel, that first day, a feral half-starved girl with no name she'd admit to, and said, *ah, there you are,* like he'd been expecting her for years.

"He said that?" Ansel asked. Her voice was strange.

"Word for word. Pip was there. Ask them."

"Pip exaggerates."

"Pip does not exaggerate about Bramble," Pip said, appearing at Marisol's elbow with the suddenness Pip did everything. Their voice was lighter than their eyes. "He said *ah, there you are*, and then he fed you until you were sick, and then he made you wash dishes, and you stayed eleven years. That's not exaggeration. That's just arithmetic."

Ansel looked at the burned shop a long moment. Then she reached out and laid her palm flat against the scorched doorframe, the way you'd touch a grave-marker, or a wall you hoped might still be warm.

"I'm sorry," she said—to the ruin, or to Bramble, or to the woman she'd been, Marisol couldn't tell. "I wish I remembered you."

And then, so quietly Marisol almost missed it: "*Ah, there you are.*"

She said it like she was testing whether the words fit in her mouth. They fit. Marisol saw it land somewhere behind her eyes—a spark, brief as a struck flint—and then Ansel blinked and stepped back and was the polite stranger again, thanking them for the story, asking if they should rejoin the others.

But Marisol had seen it. And she caught Pip's eye over Ansel's shoulder, and Pip's grin was wet and fierce, and neither of them said anything at all.

* * *

The Serpent's Road nearly killed them in April.

It always nearly killed people; that was why it paid so well. A knife-edge of trail stitched along a cliff face above a gorge that swallowed sound and light alike, narrow enough in places that you pressed your back to stone and shuffled sideways, praying the mountain didn't shrug. Marisol hated it. Had always hated it. Had once watched Ansel cross it in fog, leading them by touch and memory alone, humming under her breath like a woman walking to market.

Now Ansel stood at its mouth and went white.

"I can't," she said. Simply, flatly, without drama. "I know I'm supposed to know this. Everyone keeps telling me I know this. But I look at it and there's nothing. There's nothing *there*."

"There doesn't have to be anything there," Marisol said. "That's the point of us. We're your memory now. We outsourced it. Very modern arrangement."

"Listen," Pip said, crouching to Ansel's eye level on the scree. "You don't need to remember the road. You just need to trust the people telling you about it. That's the whole game. That's what we're doing out here—it's not the roads, love. It was never the roads."

Ansel looked at the gorge. Looked at them. And then she did the thing that made Marisol's heart ache in a way she hadn't known hearts could ache: she nodded, and held out her hand, and let Pip take it, and crossed the Serpent's Road blind, step by step, on nothing but their voices.

"*Left foot forward, there's a hold at knee height—*" Pip, steady as a metronome.

"*The rock sounds hollow here, don't lean—*" Marisol, remembering the way Ansel herself had once warned them.

"*Forty more steps. You counted them once, you know. Out loud. Drove Odell mad.*"

At the far side, on the safe shelf of stone where the trail widened, Ansel sat down abruptly and put her face in her hands. Marisol thought she was crying, and moved toward her, and then realized she was laughing—laughing the way people laugh when something breaks open, helpless and startled.

"Forty steps," Ansel said through her fingers. "I counted them. Why would I have counted them?"

"Because you were you," Pip said simply.

* * *

They were three weeks out when the déjà vu started properly.

Not the big things—the big things stayed dark, the sealed rooms, the locked doors. It was the small things that leaked through. Ansel reached for a loose stone in a cairn and found the cache hidden beneath it before Marisol could tell her it was there, and stood holding the oilskin packet with an expression like a woman hearing her own voice on a recording. She took a fork in the trail and chose left, correctly, and when Marisol asked her why, she frowned and said, "The right fork floods. Doesn't it? I don't know how I know that." She hummed a tune on the second night of the high passes—a smuggler's counting-song, older than any of them—and stopped mid-note, stricken, staring at her own hands.

"I know that song," she whispered.

"You sang it every crossing," Pip said softly. "Said your mother sang it. Said she got the words wrong on purpose, to make you laugh."

Ansel's breath caught. For a long moment she stood in the wind with her eyes closed, chasing something down a corridor that had been swept clean, and Marisol watched her fail, and watched her grieve it, and watched her—this was the part that undid Marisol, this was the part she'd carry—watch her *let it go*. Open her hand around it. Keep walking.

"Tell me the words," Ansel said at last. "The wrong ones. The ones she got wrong on purpose."

And Pip sang them, voice cracking on the third verse, and Ansel learned them again, wrong on purpose, and by the time they made camp she was humming along like she'd never stopped.

* * *

On the last night of the routes, camped beneath the shoulder of Thornfell itself with the prison-mountain looming black against the stars, Marisol woke to find Ansel sitting up by the banked fire, turning something over in her scarred hands.

It was the little tin whistle. Hesk's. The one they'd sewn into her coat pocket months ago, back when she flinched from everything, back when they'd hoped objects might be doors. She'd never touched it before. Now she held it the way you hold a bird—careful, astonished, afraid it would startle.

"Hesk played this," Marisol said quietly, settling beside her. "Badly. All the time. On watch, at dinner, at funerals. Especially at funerals. He said music was how you carried people."

Ansel turned the whistle over. "Was he carrying me?"

Marisol's throat closed. She managed, after a moment: "Yes. Right up until the bridge. And after, too, if you ask me. Some people keep carrying you after they're gone. That's not magic. That's just love with nowhere to sit down."

Ansel raised the whistle to her lips. She didn't play—didn't seem to know how, or didn't dare—but she held it there a long moment, breathing through it, and the firelight moved on her face, and Marisol saw her mouth shape a word she didn't say aloud.

Then Ansel lowered the whistle and looked at Marisol with those clear, emptied eyes, and said:

"The woman you knew. The one who walked these roads. Was she worth all this? The winter. The walking. Teaching a stranger her own life?"

Marisol considered lying. Considered comfort. Gave her the truth instead, because Ansel had always, apparently, preferred it that way.

"No," she said. "She wasn't worth it. Nobody's worth what you gave. That's not how it works—you weren't a price, love, you were a person. But she was *ours*. And you're ours. And we've got room enough for both of you, however long it takes."

Ansel looked at her for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the high pines, and far below, faint as a heartbeat, the mountain breathed its slow geothermal warmth against the night.

"I dreamed about the garden last night," Ansel said suddenly. "The Verdant Garden. There was... something tending it. Something that waited a long time. It looked at me and it said—" Her brow furrowed. "I can't remember what it said. But when I woke up, I wasn't afraid. I was—" She searched again, that careful searching, and found something this time. "I was *expected*."

Marisol felt the hairs rise on her arms.

"Tell me the rest in the morning," she said, keeping her voice level, keeping her hand steady on Ansel's shoulder. "Tell me everything, bit by bit. That's how we do it now. Bit by bit."

But when Ansel slept, Marisol sat watching the coals and did not sleep at all, because she had seen the Gardener once, in the deep roots of the world, and she knew what it meant to wait a century for someone expected.

And in the morning, when they broke camp and turned toward home, Ansel paused at the edge of the trail and looked back at the Verdant Garden's distant green haze on the horizon—looked at it the way you look at a letter you haven't opened yet—and said, unprompted, in a voice that was not quite her own and not quite anyone else's:

"It's blooming early this year. We should go see."

Pip and Marisol exchanged a glance over her head—wonder and dread sharing the same face—and shouldered their packs, and followed her down toward the valley, toward the Garden, toward whatever was waiting there that already knew her name.
