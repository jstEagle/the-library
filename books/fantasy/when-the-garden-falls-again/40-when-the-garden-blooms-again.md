## 40. When the Garden Blooms Again

The morning came up gold through the hedge, the way it always did now, and Ansel was there to meet it with dirt under her fingernails and a watering can balanced against her hip.

She had learned the garden's rhythms the way she had once learned smuggler routes—by walking them until her feet knew better than her head. Except this knowledge had not been taken from her. This knowledge she had built herself, stone by stone, season by season, from nothing but willingness. That made it hers in a way nothing else had ever been.

"Careful with the southern bed," said the Gardener, shuffling along beside her on legs like woven willow, its bark-face tilted toward the light. A century of waiting had aged it strangely; where once it had been half-mad with loneliness, a year of company had softened it into something almost grandfatherly—still strange, still given to long silences, but no longer mistaking her for a dead woman every time she turned around.

Most of the time, anyway.

"You've watered the southern bed twice already," Ansel said.

"I have not."

"You have. I watched you do it while I was eating breakfast. You forgot again."

The Gardener's leafy shoulders drooped. "Memory is a fickle thing," it said, "in things that were never meant to last this long."

Ansel crouched and pressed her palm flat against the warm soil. Beneath it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, she felt the seal—the great slow pulse of the prison holding Vaskar beneath Thornfell. It had been steady for a full turn of seasons now. Stronger than Wren had ever managed, if the Gardener's accounts could be believed. Stronger because it had not been paid for in bloodline death, nor even only in memory, but in something the old prophecy had never thought to name: love, given freely, and then rebuilt by hand.

She did not remember giving it. She would never remember giving it. But she had been told the story so many times—in Odell's gruff voice, in Marisol's fierce one, in Pip's halting, ashamed one—that sometimes she dreamed it, and in the dreams she felt what they described: the moment she had stood before the open seal with the book in her hand and chosen neither ending the pages offered. The moment she had poured everything she was into the roots of the world and let the hedge close over the god's fury like a mother drawing a blanket over a sleeping child.

In the dreams, she was crying. In the dreams, everyone she loved was calling her name, and she was smiling as she forgot it.

She woke from those dreams with tears on her face and no idea why, and one of the crew was always there—because there was always someone there now—and they would sit with her until the feeling passed, saying nothing, asking nothing, just being present the way you keep a lamp lit in a window for someone who may or may not come home.

That was the shape of her life now. Not the absence of grief but its transformation: grief tended like a garden, pruned and watered, growing into something that fed instead of choked.

* * *

The Verdant Garden had been ruins when she first saw it—a century of neglect, the hedges gone wild and gray, the fountains cracked, the statuary faceless with moss. Now it was coming back. Not all at once; gardens never do anything all at once. But the roses Wren had planted a hundred years ago bloomed again in colors that didn't exist anywhere else in the world, deep violet-reds that seemed to hold the light a moment longer than they should. The fountain ran clear. Bees had found their way up through the passes, impossibly, and hummed among the beds like small contented gods.

People came, too. That was the strangest part. Nettlebed villagers climbed the mountain path every market week with seeds and cuttings and gossip. Rogue Bloom knights—there were more of them than Serah had ever guessed, knights who had walked away when Aurelia fell—came to stand quiet among the hedges, hats in hands, as though the garden were a church, which perhaps it was. Even smugglers came, though they pretended they'd merely gotten lost, and left coins in the fountain that nobody spent.

Ansel had stopped being invisible somewhere in that first winter, without noticing exactly when. It happened the way dawn happens: gradually, then completely. She was simply theirs—the crew's, the village's, the garden's—and the name *Amberdale*, which she could not remember ever having owned, belonged to all of them now, kept safe in their mouths for her.

Odell still said it sometimes, deliberately, watching her face. "Morning, Amberdale." Testing whether it landed as a stranger's word or a bell rung inside her.

This morning it had landed somewhere in between. Progress, Marisol called it.

* * *

Midmorning brought footsteps on the gravel path—two sets, uneven, familiar—and Ansel straightened, brushing soil from her knees.

Serah came around the hedge first, gray threading her temples now, her knight's bearing worn soft at the edges like an old coin. Behind her limped Pip, whose leg had never quite forgiven Greywater Ford, leaning on a carved walking stick Hesk's brother had sent down from the northern holds. Pip carried a basket. Pip always carried a basket. It was how Pip apologized for things that had been forgiven a hundred times over.

"Brought bread," Pip announced. "And eggs. And also I broke a jar of honey on the way up, so mostly bread and eggs and a story about honey."

"The story better be good," Ansel said.

"It's got a bear in it."

"Then it'll do."

They ate at the little stone table near the fountain, the four of them, and the Gardener hovered at the edge of the conversation like a moth unsure whether it was invited, until Serah patted the bench beside her and said, "Sit down, you ancient shrub, you're letting a draft in," and the Gardener sat, and the morning went on.

Pip told the bear story, which involved a bear, three jars of honey, a customs official named Dossit, and—at least according to Pip—a daring escape across a rooftop in Hollowfen. Marisol, arriving halfway through with a crate of seedlings, took over the telling and corrected it into something that actually resembled the truth, which was somehow less heroic and funnier. Ansel laughed until her ribs hurt, and noticed—as she noticed every time—that she was laughing at people she had, by any honest accounting, known for barely a year.

It didn't matter. That was the secret nobody tells you: it doesn't matter how long you've known someone. Love isn't measured in years. It's measured in mornings like this one, in bread broken and stories corrected and the particular way Marisol tucked a strand of hair behind her ear when she was concentrating, and the way Serah always saved the heel of the loaf for the Gardener because she'd figured out months ago that it liked the crust best.

Ansel had lost a lifetime of knowing these people. And they had spent a year giving it back to her, piece by piece, story by story, until the second version of her love for them was so thickly layered it hardly showed the seams.

Sometimes, at night, she wondered about the first version. The girl who had kept everyone at arm's length, who had turned down the invitation to hearth-night, who had learned every road in Thornfell so she'd never need to learn a single heart. She felt tenderness toward that girl, the way you feel toward a photograph of yourself as a child. *You were so afraid,* she thought. *You had no idea what was coming to save you.*

* * *

Afternoon, when the others had gone back down the mountain, Ansel went into the cottage—Wren's cottage, rebuilt on its old foundations, every stone laid by villagers who claimed to remember the hedge-witch fondly despite the fact that Wren had erased herself from every record. Memory was like that in Nettlebed. It persisted in the bones of things.

Inside, on the shelf above the hearth, sat the prophecy book.

It had been blank for a year. Whatever power had filled its pages—Wren's seeing, or the god's dreaming, or something older than both—had drained away on the thirteenth night, when Ansel chose the third path and the book chose to stop pretending it knew how stories ended. Page after page of empty vellum. The Order's scholars had begged to study it. Serah had told them, with a perfectly straight face, that the book had crumbled to ash, and Ansel had backed her lie without hesitation, because some things should belong to the person they were written about and no one else.

But it wasn't entirely blank anymore.

One line, in ink that hadn't been there yesterday, in handwriting she recognized because it was her own:

*When the garden blooms again, the Key will be no key at all, but a door left open onto morning.*

Ansel read it standing very still, the way you stand still when a wild bird lands on you.

She had not written it. She was nearly sure she had not written it. And yet it was her hand, her slanted letters, the little hook she made on her g's. Perhaps it had come from her the way dreams come—from some part of her deeper than memory, the part that had navigated the Serpent's Road blind, the part that had known, even emptied, how to find its way home.

Or perhaps the book was learning to see again. Perhaps it had finally understood what Wren's torn page had tried to tell it: that futures are not counted, they are imagined, and imagination needs company.

She touched the line once, lightly, then closed the cover.

Outside, the Gardener was singing. It sang old songs, songs Wren must have taught it a century ago, in a language that might have been the first language of gardens. The words meant nothing Ansel could translate, but the tune was gentle, and the bees droned harmony, and beneath it all she felt the seal pulse its slow, patient, sleeping rhythm—strong, and getting stronger, and tended.

* * *

Evening came down the mountainside in ribbons of rose and copper. Ansel banked the cottage fire, watered the last of the beds, and stood a while at the garden gate looking out over Thornfell—the peaks going purple, the passes filling with blue shadow, the whole vast country she had once known better than her own heartbeat and now knew by other means: by the crew's descriptions, by her own new footprints, by the simple daily act of choosing to stay.

Down in the valley, lights were kindling in Nettlebed. Somewhere beyond, in Hollowfen, the crew's safehouse windows would be glowing too, and Odell would be complaining about the state of the river trade, and Hesk's chair—nobody had ever moved it—would stand by the fire with a cushion on it, because the crew did not believe in letting the dead go cold, only in letting them rest.

Tomorrow Pip and Marisol would come up with more bread and probably another bear story. Next week the villagers would bring autumn cuttings. In winter they would all crowd into this cottage, too many bodies for one hearth, exactly the way hearth-nights were supposed to be, and Ansel would sit in the middle of the noise and warmth and feel no vertigo at all.

Three years she had spent erasing herself. One year they had spent writing her back.

She turned the sign on the gate—it was a small wooden thing, carved by the Nettlebed children, that read THE GARDEN IS OPEN—and went inside to light the lamp.

Behind her, in the darkening beds, the roses held the last of the light a moment longer than they should.

And on the shelf above the hearth, the blank book waited, patient as soil, ready for whatever she would write next.
