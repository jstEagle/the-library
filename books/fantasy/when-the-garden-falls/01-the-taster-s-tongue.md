## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The candles at King Aldric's winter banquet burned the color of old honey, and their light fell across the long table like a blessing that no one in the hall had earned.

Sebastian Kavanagh stood where he always stood — one pace behind the king's right shoulder, close enough to smell the rosemary roasted into the boar, far enough back that his shadow did not trouble anyone's conversation. Ten years of standing in that exact place had worn a groove in him the way water wears stone: invisible, patient, permanent. He was the crown's food-taster. He was furniture that swallowed poison and lived.

He had learned, over those ten years, to make his living body a ledger. Every dish that passed the king's lips passed his first — a spoonful here, a swallow there, measured with the precision of a man defusing something with his own throat. The court believed he simply had a strong stomach. They were wrong, but they were wrong in the way courts preferred to be wrong: comfortably, and without looking too closely.

What Sebastian had never told anyone — not the king who paid him, not the physicians who marveled at his survival, not the lovers who left him baffled by their ordinariness — was what the poisons taught him on the way down.

Arsenic tasted like a churchyard in rain. Hemlock sang faintly of hempen rope. Belladonna was sweet, treacherously so, like a lie told by someone beautiful. But beneath the flavors of the toxins themselves lay other flavors, subtler ones, ones no physician had ever written down. When Sebastian swallowed a poisoned dish, he tasted the hand that had poisoned it. Not metaphorically. He tasted *intent* the way other men tasted salt. He tasted fear, or greed, or — once, memorably, in a dish meant for the old Lord Chancellor's predecessor — grief so thick and specific it had made him weep at the table, alone among two hundred guests, for a woman whose name he never learned.

He had spent a decade pretending that gift did not exist. Tonight it would refuse to be ignored.

* * *

The banquet was Aldric's own idea, which surprised no one; the king loved spectacle the way thin ice loves weight. It was the feast of Saint Verene, patroness of hearthfires, and the great hall blazed with them — three hundred candles, braziers of applewood, the enormous hearth roaring hard enough to make the tapestries shiver. Outside, the first true snow of the season was falling early, earlier than any year in living memory, though only the servants murmuring at the service doors seemed to notice. Winter had been coming wrong for years now. It came like something summoned.

Sebastian moved through the courses with the mechanical calm of long practice. Oyster stew — clean, brine and cream, nothing hidden in it. Boar glazed in quince — rich, unctuous, safe. The wine steward poured from a bottle of Thornfell red, dark as garnets, and Sebastian took his measure of it and found nothing but cold earth and black cherry and the faint mineral bite of the mountain vineyards he knew better than any lawman ever would.

Then came the chancellor's wine.

It should have been the same vintage. It was not. Sebastian lifted the silver cup set before Lord Chancellor Edmun Hale — a soft-spoken man, gray at the temples, beloved enough that even the kitchen staff called him *the good lord* — and drank the taster's measure, a single swallow held on the tongue longer than courtesy required.

The arsenic announced itself first. White arsenic, refined, dissolved — it bloomed in the back of his throat like frost forming on glass, a cold sweetness that was not sweetness at all but the taste of small crystals settling into living flesh. He knew it instantly. He had dosed himself with it for six years, in a locked room above the taster's quarters, swallowing grains of it between bread and honey until his body learned its shape and stopped dying. He could taste a lethal dose the way a mother could pick her child's cry out of a crowd.

But it was the second flavor that stopped his heart mid-beat.

Grief. Underneath the arsenic, woven through it like a thread through cloth, was grief — dense, aged, patient grief, the kind that has stopped being a wound and become a way of seeing. And under the grief, unmistakable, certain as a signature: *certainty*. The calm, settled conviction of someone who has decided, after long and terrible thought, that a death is deserved. That justice requires it. That God, if God watched banquets at all, would look away.

Sebastian set the cup down with hands that did not shake, because ten years of practice had taught them not to, and because shaking hands at a royal table were a confession.

"Chancellor," he said quietly, leaning toward Hale without breaking the rhythm of the service. "I'd ask you not to drink the wine."

Hale looked up, startled, his mild face folding into confusion. "Is something wrong?"

"The vintage is off." Sebastian kept his voice level, bored, the voice of a man complaining about a cork. "Bitter finish. I'd send for the Thornfell red instead." It was a lie built inside a truth, the kind he'd gotten good at. The wine was not bitter. The wine was murder, dressed in velvet.

A page was dispatched. A fresh bottle arrived. Hale drank deeply of the honest vintage and thanked Sebastian with a smile that had no idea what it was thanking him for, and the banquet rolled onward like a cart over a body in the road.

And Sebastian stood behind the king's shoulder, tasting his own heartbeat, and understood that somewhere in this candlelit hall walked a grieving soul who had just watched their careful work fail — and did not yet know why.

* * *

He found the poisoner's trail later, in the way he found everything: through his mouth.

The failed cup was carried out with the other clearing-plates, destined for the scullery, and Sebastian intercepted it with the casual authority of a man whose job gave him dominion over every vessel that touched the king's table. In the dim passage behind the dais, he swirled the dregs and breathed them in, then touched a fingertip of the residue to his tongue.

More grief. Closer now, fresher, less diluted by distance. And with it, a new note: the taste of a place. His gift had edges he was still discovering, but he had learned that strong intent carried geography with it — the poisoner's sorrow tasted faintly of lilies and cold stone, of incense burned down to nothing, of a room where someone had wept for years. A memorial chapel. Someone who mourned at a tomb.

He knew whose tomb smelled of lilies and cold stone. Everyone did. Queen Isolde maintained the memorial of her sister Elira with a devotion the ballads called touching — twenty years of weekly visits, candles never allowed to gutter, fresh lilies in every season. Elira, dead young and mysteriously, in the same year the winters began arriving early and staying late.

Sebastian rinsed his mouth with watered wine and told himself the thought was absurd. Told himself grief was common as dirt, that half the court mourned someone, that a taste was not a testimony. Told himself this the way he told himself everything, standing in his groove behind the king's shoulder, invisible and complicit and safe.

He almost believed it. Then he felt eyes on him.

* * *

She stood near the eastern pillars, where the candlelight thinned, and she had been watching him for some time. He was certain of it now, though he could not say when he'd become certain — the gift worked on sight too, sometimes, faintly, like heat rising off stone. Maren Voss. The queen's new inquisitor, appointed barely two months past, a woman with ash-brown hair bound severely at her nape and a manner that made bishops check their consciences. She had come to court from somewhere north, from some posting no one spoke of twice, and within weeks of her arrival three people had already been taken below for questioning and not returned.

She was watching him the way a falcon watches a hedgerow. Not hungrily. Patiently. As if she already knew there was something in it worth waiting for.

Sebastian turned back to the table and performed his duties — the cheese course, the spiced pears, the king's final cup of mulled wine, each tasted, each pronounced clean with a nod. Behind his own steady face he ran calculations he hated running. *Did she see me warn the chancellor? No — she was across the hall. Did she see my face when I tasted the cup? Perhaps. Perhaps.* A man who had spent ten years making his body unreadable knew exactly how much of it still showed. His pupils, maybe. The half-second pause before he set the cup down. Small things. But Voss collected small things; everyone said so. She had once identified a smuggler by the way he flinched at the word *north*.

When the king rose and the hall rose with him, Sebastian melted into the dispersing crowd with the ease of long habit, and Voss detached herself from the pillars and crossed the floor directly toward him.

"Taster Kavanagh." Her voice was quiet, pleasant, pitched to carry no further than his ear. "You saved the chancellor tonight."

"I sent him a better bottle. The other was corked."

"Mm." She smiled. It was a well-made smile, symmetrical, warm at the edges, and entirely empty. "Corked wine doesn't usually concern you. You taste for the king. The chancellor's cup is another man's office." She let that sit between them like a stone dropped in still water. "Yet you concerned yourself. Quickly. Decisively. Before he raised the glass."

"Dead chancellors are bad for the kitchen's reputation."

"Yes," she said softly. "They are. Though I wonder—" and here she tilted her head, birdlike, curious in a way that had teeth behind it, "—what a man must have *tasted*, to move so fast."

The words went through him like cold water through cloth. She could not know. Surely she could not know. But the phrasing — *tasted*, chosen precisely, her eyes holding his with the flat patience of an examiner — sat against his skin like a hand checking for fever.

"A bad year for Thornfell vintages," Sebastian said. "Inquisitor."

"Of course." She stepped back, gracious, unhurried. "Enjoy the feast, Kavanagh. I imagine you'll be busy this season. There's so much poisoning about lately." Another smile, thinner. "So much of everything, lately."

She turned and walked away into the candlelight, and Sebastian stood among the draining cups and guttering wicks and felt, with terrible clarity, the exact sensation he had trained himself never to feel again: the taste of being seen.

* * *

His rooms above the taster's quarters were cold, and he did not light a fire. He sat in the dark with the day moving through him like a swallowed stone.

Somewhere in this palace, someone had tried to kill the good lord chancellor with white arsenic and a heart full of grief, and that someone grieved at a tomb that smelled of lilies, and the queen visited that tomb, and the winters came early, and none of these facts belonged in the same sentence and all of them refused to separate. Somewhere in this palace, a woman with empty smiles had asked him what he tasted, and meant more than wine.

He had survived arsenic, hemlock, belladonna, aconite, and things the physicians had no names for. He had built himself a body that could not easily be killed and a life that could not easily be read. He had done it all so that he could stand in one groove behind one shoulder and remain, forever, invisible.

Tonight, for the first time in ten years, invisibility had failed him. He could still taste Voss's attention lingering in the air of the banquet hall, faint and cold, like frost forming on glass.

By morning, he knew, the criers would be in the squares. By morning, everything would change.

He did not sleep. He listened to the snow falling wrong outside his window, earlier and heavier than any winter in memory, and he waited for dawn the way a man waits for a verdict.
