## 2. A Hunt Is Proclaimed

The criers came at first light, as he had known they would.

Sebastian heard them from his window before he saw them — three voices raised in the grey hush of the snowbound square, pitched to carry over the rooftops like the calls of carrion birds. He had spent ten years learning to read the city by its sounds: the baker's knock at the fourth bell, the water-carts groaning through the slush lanes, the particular rhythm of boots that meant a patrol and not a deliveryman. This morning the city sounded wrong in a new way. It sounded *listened-to*.

He dressed in the dark and went down into the square with his collar up and his hands in his sleeves, one face among forty drawn faces gathered beneath the criers' platform. The snow fell straight down, thick as ash. Someone had lit a brazier at the square's edge, and the crowd orbited it in a slow, silent rotation, warming their fingers and saying nothing to one another.

"Hear ye, hear ye." The central crier was a broad man with a herald's sash and a voice trained to joy, which made what he read sound worse, somehow — a wedding announcement for an execution. "By decree of Her Majesty Queen Isolde, Protector of the Realm, let it be known that a corruption walks among us. There are those born with gifts unnatural — tongues that taste lies, ears that hear the hearts of beasts, eyes that see what is hidden. These powers are not blessings. They are doors, and behind every door stands something hungry."

A murmur moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. Sebastian kept his head down, studied the cobbles, counted the cracks between stones the way he'd once counted heartbeats after swallowing a taster's dose.

"Let any citizen who knows such a person come forward," the crier continued. "Denunciation shall be rewarded with fifty crowns of silver. Conviction confirmed, one hundred more. The Crown asks only that you open your eyes. The Inquisition will do the rest."

Fifty crowns. Sebastian did the arithmetic without wanting to. Fifty crowns was a year of wages for a dyer's apprentice. It was a winter's coal, a debt forgiven, a daughter's dowry. It was, precisely measured, the price of a neighbor.

He looked up despite himself, and saw the crowd looking at each other.

That was the thing no proclamation could capture, he thought — the way fear turned a market square into a room full of mirrors, every face suddenly a question. The cooper's wife was looking at the chandler. The chandler was looking at the widow Perren, who took in lodgers. Old Tam Fletcher, who had sold Sebastian eels off a cart for nine years and once sworn brotherhood over a shared flask, was looking directly at him — and then away, too quickly, the way a man looks away when he has seen something he must now decide whether he knew.

*They haven't named me,* Sebastian told himself. *No name has been spoken.*

But names were never spoken first. First came the looking. Then came the knocking.

* * *

He spent the morning doing what a guilty man does: nothing remarkable. He bought bread. He argued mildly about the price of lamp oil. He walked past the palace kitchens out of habit and stood a long moment at the gate where he had shown his taster's seal every day for a decade, until the guard on duty — a boy who had called him *master Kavanagh* with genuine warmth not two weeks past — glanced at him twice, and then a third time, and shifted his weight in a way that meant he had been told to watch for someone.

So they had described him already. Not named him. Described him. There was a difference, but it was a difference measured in days.

He went home by three streets he did not usually take and entered through the shared stable yard rather than the front, because the front door had been oiled recently — he could smell the tallow-and-lye of it from the alley, fresh hinges meant to swing silently. He had not oiled them. His landlady was seventy and complained about the cost of candle-tallow; she would sooner have sold her teeth.

Someone wanted to hear him come home.

Sebastian stood in the cold of the stable yard among the sleeping goats and felt the fear arrive properly at last — not the cold sweat of the banquet hall, not the prickling awareness of Voss's attention, but the true animal thing, rising from the gut, older than thought. Ten years he had survived arsenic and nightshade, cantharides and distilled yew, things that killed kings in agony while he stood beside the throne tasting honeyed wine and waiting to see if his blood would hold. None of it had ever frightened him like a set of quiet hinges.

Poison was honest. It wanted you dead and said so. Fear made men creative.

He climbed through the hayloft window into his own rooms like a thief, which was what he had become in the eyes of the law sometime between midnight and dawn.

* * *

His records lived under the third floorboard from the hearth, beneath a loose plank he'd pried up the week he'd taken the position, back when the gift was still new enough to frighten him and he'd believed that writing things down would make them manageable. Ten years of a madman's ledger, in his own cramped hand:

*Chancellor Aldery's wine — arsenic, yes, but beneath it grief so thick I nearly wept into the cup. He believes the king deserves death. He believes it the way other men believe in spring.*

*The duchess of Marle — her tears taste of hemlock she has not yet decided to use.*

*The king himself, when he broke bread with me at the autumn feast: nothing. No flavor at all but salt and bread. Either the man is exactly what he appears, or there is nothing left inside him to appear as.*

Page after page. Every secret the court had ever steeped into its food and drink, catalogued by a man who had told himself he was a servant of the crown, a shield against poisoners, and had slowly become the most dangerous archive in the kingdom.

He carried the ledger to the hearth and knelt there a moment without lighting it. Outside, somewhere below, a door opened and closed. Boots crossed the yard. Stopped.

Sebastian held his breath and listened with the whole of his strange tongue pressed flat against the roof of his mouth, as if taste could reach through floorboards. But the gift worked on what entered the body, not the air, and all he had was his ears: the boots moved on, unhurried, toward the lane. A neighbor. Or a man paid to be a neighbor.

He struck the flint.

The pages caught reluctantly — old paper, dry as tinder but dense with ink — and he fed them to the fire one at a time, watching each entry curl and blacken. *Grief.* *Hemlock undecided.* *Nothing left inside him to appear as.* The flames ate the words faster than he could reread them, and still he found himself mourning, absurdly, the loss of the only proof that his life had meant anything beyond survival. Ten years of swallowing death for other men, and the record of it gone in the time it takes to boil water.

When the last page was ash he raked the fire apart and scattered the char into the cold grate, then swept the hearth clean. Then he sat back on his heels in the empty room and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that burning the ledger changed nothing. Voss did not need his records. She needed only him — or rather, whatever it was inside him that tasted truth in wine. The rest of the gifted, whoever they were, whoever they had been all these years hiding in plain sight among bakers and coopers and eel-sellers — they needed only to be found.

And the crown had just offered the whole city silver to do the finding.

* * *

He packed the way smugglers pack, which is to say he packed almost nothing. A knife. A change of wool. The small clay vial of bitterleaf tonic he kept against the days when some lingering dose made his hands shake. Silver, sewn into his belt where a search would find it last. And at the very bottom of the bag, wrapped in oilcloth, the one thing he could not explain even to himself: the plain pewter cup from which he had drunk at every tasting for ten years, worn smooth by his mouth, holding no memory he could taste but his own.

The light was failing when he went down through the stable yard for the last time. In the lane, the lamps were being lit early against the heavy snow, and he saw the notice nailed to the post beside the well, the ink still sharp: *DENUNCIATION REWARDED.* Beneath it, someone had already scratched a word in the wet wood with a knife-point — a name, half-legible, belonging to the herb-woman on Tanner's Row who had pulled Sebastian's tooth once and charged him nothing. By morning there would be more names. By morning the wood would be crowded with them, a fence built of neighbors.

He turned his collar up and did not look at it again.

* * *

The smuggler's gate lay beneath the old tannery on the river side of the city, at the end of a passage that smelled of lime and rot and thirty years of secrets. Sebastian had found it at nineteen, running contraband brandy for a man who was later hanged with it, and he had told no one since — not Odile, though she claimed to know every hole in Thornfell; not the kitchen masters who trusted him with their lives daily. Some knowledge you keep the way you keep a knife under the floorboard: not because you plan to use it, but because the day you need it is never a day you can predict.

The gate itself was iron-banded oak, swollen shut with disuse, and it took him twenty minutes and both shoulders and a strip of skin off his palm to force it. Beyond lay the drainage culvert, low and black, running out beneath the wall to where the river slid past the city's foundations, carrying ice south toward the lowlands. The air that breathed out of it was cold and mineral and utterly without the smell of people, and Sebastian stood in it a long moment, letting it fill his lungs.

Behind him: the city, the square, the brazier, the mirrors. Tam Fletcher's glance sliding away. The oiled hinges. The herb-woman's name already carved into wet wood. Fifty crowns, the price of everyone he had ever broken bread with.

Ahead: darkness, and the mountains he knew better than any lawman alive, and whatever waited there.

He thought of Voss's attention settling on him across the banquet hall, faint and cold as frost forming on glass, and he understood at last that it had not been suspicion. Suspicion was a question. What he had felt from her had been recognition — one hunter knowing another creature that did not belong in the tame world. She had marked him the way he might mark a rare poison in a common cup: with interest. With appetite.

Well. Let her come hunting. He had spent ten years learning what it was to be swallowed.

Sebastian ducked into the culvert and pulled the gate shut behind him, and the sound of the city — the criers, the bells, the beginning whisper of denunciation — cut off as cleanly as a throat.

Three days' walk north, if the passes held, lay the village of Cold Sarrow, where Odile Ferre owed him a debt and a warm fire and, if the gods were kind, a road that did not yet have the crown's riders on it.

He walked into the mountain's shadow and did not look back.
