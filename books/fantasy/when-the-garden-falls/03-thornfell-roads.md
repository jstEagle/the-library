## 3. Thornfell Roads

The mountains took him back the way an old dog takes back a boy who left too long — with suspicion first, then a slow, grudging warmth.

Sebastian climbed for three days without seeing another living soul, and he counted that as mercy. The Thornfells did not forgive carelessness. The paths he followed were not paths at all to any crown surveyor's eye: a scree chute that looked like certain death and wasn't, a streambed that vanished into limestone and rose again half a mile on, a shelf of granite above the treeline where his boots had worn their own private road over ten years of carrying contraband in the king's name and out of it. He knew which lichen meant water beneath. He knew which cairns were cairns and which were markers left by men who'd hanged for less than what they carried.

His body still remembered everything. That was the trouble with a gift like his — it lived in the flesh, not the mind. His tongue remembered arsenic's bitter almond ghost, and under it, faint as a watermark, the chancellor's grief from that banquet night: *the king deserves death.* He had swallowed that truth along with the poison and had never managed to spit it out.

And Voss had seen him taste it. Not the poison — anyone could see a taster work. She had seen him taste the *why* of it.

He climbed faster after dark, when the cold came down off the peaks like something poured.

On the fourth morning he crested the ridge above Hollowmere and stopped so abruptly that loose stone clattered away beneath his heel and fell a long time before it found bottom.

Hollowmere had been a smuggler's village, which is to say a village that understood the difference between law and survival. Thirty roofs when Sebastian had last stood here, two years ago, buying passage for a chest of unmarked silver. Smoke from every chimney. Odile's crooked house at the bend of the stream with her drying racks strung with mountain thyme, and her voice carrying up the valley like she was arguing with God and winning.

Now there were perhaps fifteen roofs standing, and only four of them smoked. The rest were burned down to black ribs against the snow, collapsed inward, the way houses die when fire comes from outside rather than within. The drying racks were bare. The stream ran brown with ash.

Sebastian stood in the wind a long moment, tasting nothing, because there was nothing left to taste but distance.

He went down anyway. Caution said turn around; guilt said go, and guilt had always been louder in him.

The first door he tried belonged to a woman he didn't know, young, hollow-eyed, a child behind her skirts. She opened it a hand's width and no more.

"I'm looking for Odile," he said. "Of the bend-house."

The woman's eyes went past him to the ridgelines, both directions, quick as a bird checking for hawks. "Gone," she said. "Taken or fled, depending who you ask." Her voice dropped. "The riders came at midwinter. Grey cloaks. A woman led them — tall, quiet, never raised her voice. They had lists." She said the word *lists* the way other people said *plague*. "Names of the gifted. Names of the ones who sheltered them. Names of the ones who *might*." 

"Odile's name was on one?"

"Her name was the first one." Something moved through the woman's face — fear, and under it a bitter kind of shame. "They took her grandson first. To make her talk, I expect. She talked all right. Told them where to put their questions." The woman almost smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see. "Then she walked out of here ahead of their horses with her hands bound and her chin up, and nobody's seen her since. Some say the riders took her south. Some say—" She stopped. The child behind her made a small sound and she shut the door on it gently, and on him.

*Some say,* the closed door finished for her.

Sebastian stood in the lane with the ash-smell in his throat. Ten years he had run these roads. Ten years of moving poison and pardon-money and people through these passes, and he had told himself it was neutral work, honest work, the work of a man whose tongue was already damned so his hands might as well be useful. The roads had been safe because everyone needed them safe — crown and smuggler alike.

Until the crown decided it wanted souls instead of silence.

He tasted copper then, and realized he'd bitten his cheek.

* * *

He found the second rider's camp by its smell before he saw it — horse and woodsmoke and, underneath, the sweetish reek of the grey cloaks' torch-oil, the same that had eaten through Hollowmere's roofs. Six tents in the fold of the next valley, a picket line, a signal post with fresh-cut pine stacked beside it for burning messages skyward.

Sebastian lay on the ridge above them through the afternoon, doing what he did best, which was paying attention.

There were eight men visible. None wore the grey of Voss's own company — these were hired blades, county men, the sort who took silver for denunciations now instead of for wolf pelts. One of them dragged a girl out of a tent near dusk.

She couldn't have been more than sixteen. Small, wiry, wrists bound with cord that had bitten through skin. Mud to the knees. And she was *laughing* — a thin, cracked sound, but laughing — while the man shook her and shouted a question Sebastian was too far to hear.

The girl spat at him. Then she cocked her head, sharply, the way you'd listen to something far off, and whatever she heard made her stop laughing and start talking fast, urgent, pointing east with her bound hands.

The men looked where she pointed. Two of them saddled and rode out at once, hard, into the last light.

Sebastian watched the camp until full dark, mapping watch rotations out of habit, and might have simply gone around — his plan had been Odile's southern route, and grief makes a narrow road — except that near midnight, the girl was moved to the picket line's far end, tied to a post alone, and the nearest sentry settled into the warm drowse of a man convinced no one is fool enough to come here.

Fool enough. Sebastian had been called worse.

He went down through the rocks the way water goes down, without argument, taking the route his feet knew better than his head approved. The sentry got a forearm across the throat and a knee behind the knee and folded without a sound. Sebastian took the man's knife and his waterskin and cut the girl free, and when she woke enough to understand, she didn't scream — she went absolutely still, listening, head tilted, eyes enormous in the dark.

"You're not one of them," she whispered. It wasn't a question.

"No."

"They're coming back early. The two riders. The birds told me they'd turned at the ford." She flexed her cut wrists and winced. "We should be gone before—"

"Birds told you."

She looked at him then, really looked, and something passed between them that needed no explaining: the weary recognition of one strange animal finding another. "I hear truths in birdsong," she said. "It sounds mad when I say it aloud. Everyone I've ever said it to either sold me or tried to save me, and both kinds are gone now." She held out her raw wrists like evidence. "Wren. My name's Wren."

"Sebastian." He hesitated. "What did the birds say about me?"

"That you taste things you shouldn't. That your mouth is full of other people's secrets and it's been making you sick for years." She said it plainly, without wonder, the way you'd report weather. "A thrush doesn't lie. Neither do I."

The mountains had never felt so large around him. "Can they tell you where Odile is?"

Wren's face did something complicated. Pity, mostly. "The birds don't carry names that far. But they've been saying for days that the taken ones went south. Down. Toward the old archive road." She paused. "And they're saying something else. Something that's spooked even the crows, and crows spook at nothing." She wrapped her bleeding wrists in strips of the sentry's cloak, matter-of-fact about her own blood. "They're saying the hunt isn't a hunt. Hunts end. This one keeps going, and everything it catches goes *down* the mountain, not out of it. Nothing comes back up."

Behind them, faintly, hoofbeats on the frozen ford — the returning riders, earlier than any bird had promised, or later, depending how you counted.

Wren grabbed his sleeve. "East," she said. "Trust me east. There's a place the birds know, a hidden community, people like us. There's an archivist there who's been waiting for someone with your particular tongue." Her eyes caught the starlight. "She says you'll want to hear what the queen sold."

Sebastian looked south, toward Odile, toward whatever the riders had done with her, and every instinct he owned pulled that way like a hooked fish.

But the birds had said *down*, and *nothing comes back up*, and somewhere beneath those words he could already taste the shape of a truth larger than his grief.

He went east.
