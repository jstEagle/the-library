## 4. What the Queen Sold

Wren heard the valley before she saw it.

Not with her ears — her ears were ordinary, dull as anyone's — but in the birds. The finches that had ridden her shoulder since Thornfell's first pass went quiet as they descended, then began to sing again in a language that was not song at all. *Safe*, they said. *Safe, safe, below the stone.* Birds never lied. That was the whole of her gift and the whole of her curse: every sparrow's chatter carried its true meaning beneath it, plain as a name called across water, and she had never once been able to stop hearing it.

"Down here," she said, and did not wait to see if the taster followed.

The path dropped through a seam in the mountain that no cart could find and no rider could ride, a crack in the world filled with fern and dripping rock. Sebastian Kavanagh came behind her without complaint, though she could hear his breathing change when the walls narrowed, when the dark pressed close enough to taste. A man who had spent ten years swallowing poison for kings did not frighten easily, but he had the look of someone whose fear had simply grown more patient.

"You knew this place," he said at last. His voice echoed strangely off the wet stone. "Before the hunt."

"I was born three valleys east." She kept climbing down, hand over hand on a root older than the kingdom. "My mother heard truths in loom-song. My grandmother in the creak of ice on the lake. We didn't call it a gift. We called it Tuesday." She felt him almost smile behind her in the dark. "The birds led me out when Voss's riders burned our village. They've been leading me back ever since. Birds remember where the safe places are. Even when the safe places don't want remembering."

The seam opened like a held breath released.

Below them, cupped in a hollow of the mountain where no road ran and no map admitted anything existed, lay a village that was not a village. It had no smoke rising straight from chimneys — the fires were banked and vented sideways through cracks in the cliff, so that from above the hollow would look like bare stone. The houses were built into the rock itself, doors of woven hazel flush against the mountainside, windows glowing faint amber. Terraces of winter greens climbed the walls in stone shelves. And everywhere, everywhere, there were birds: rooks on the ridgelines, wrens in the eaves, a kestrel turning slow circles over the hollow like a sentry that had taken the watch a hundred years ago and never been relieved.

"They see everything," Wren said. "And they tell me everything. No rider has come down this path unannounced in forty years."

Sebastian stood at the lip of the hollow and looked down at it for a long moment. In the grey light his face was hard to read, but Wren had learned to read people the way she read birds — not their words, which lied, but the small involuntary music underneath. What she heard in him now was grief wearing the mask of calculation.

"My roads brought them here," he said quietly. "The smugglers' paths. I showed Odile half these passes myself, years ago. I thought—" He stopped.

"You thought keeping secrets was the same as keeping people safe." Wren shrugged. "Everyone thinks that. Come on. There's someone you need to meet before you finish apologizing."

* * *

The archivist lived in the deepest house, the one carved farthest into the mountain, and Wren had never once seen her in daylight.

Her name was Sable Fen, though no one used it; she was simply the archivist, an old woman bent like a question mark over tables of paper, her eyes gone white as frost but her hands still quick. She had been Keeper of the Winter Rolls in the royal capital once, Wren had heard — keeper of the records the crown preferred not to exist — until the night she copied something she was not meant to copy and fled north with the truth sewn into the lining of her coat.

She was waiting for them. Of course she was. The kestrel had announced them an hour before they arrived.

"A food-taster," the old woman said, before any greeting. Her blind face turned toward Sebastian with unsettling precision. "I wondered if one would ever come. Sit, boy. You've been eating lies so long I can smell them on you."

"I eat what I'm given," Sebastian said carefully. "That's rather the profession."

"No." Sable Fen's voice was dry as old vellum. "You eat what you're given and you taste what's hidden inside it. Arsenic in wine. Grief in soup. Intent in bread." She smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "Do you imagine we don't know what you are? The birds have been talking about you for two days. A man walking the high passes who leaves a wake of silence behind him, because every creature that tastes his scent goes quiet, listening to what he carries." She gestured to the bench across from her table. "Sit. Both of you. And tell me, taster — what do you know about winter?"

Sebastian sat. Wren perched on the edge of the table itself, among the papers, as she always did. Below them, faintly, the village sounds rose — children laughing somewhere, a hammer on stone, someone singing a harvest song out of season.

"Winters come early now," Sebastian said. "They've come early for thirty years. Everyone says it's the mountain's temper. Bad omens. Old sins."

"Everyone says that," the archivist agreed, "because everyone is wrong in the same direction. Now. What do you know about Queen Isolde?"

"That she has reigned thirty-one years. That she took the throne young, after her sister died. That the court loved her for her mercy." His voice flattened slightly on the last word, and Wren heard the thing beneath it — the taster tasting something bitter in his own mouth.

"Mercy." Sable Fen laughed, a sound like pages tearing. "Yes. Let me tell you about her mercy."

She drew a roll of paper from beneath the table — not parchment but something older, thin as moth wing, covered in a script that seemed to shift when Wren looked at it directly. The birds on the windowsill went utterly still.

"Thirty-one years ago, there were two sisters. Isolde, the elder, clever and hungry. Elira, the younger, beloved — beloved by the court, beloved by the lords, beloved, some whispered, by the man Isolde intended to marry. Isolde would inherit. But inheritance is a thin thing, and love is thinner, and Isolde lay awake at night doing arithmetic: how long before her sister's popularity became her sister's claim? How long before the crown slipped from her head to Elira's?"

"She wouldn't," Sebastian said. But his voice had gone strange, and Wren realized he was tasting the truth of the old woman's words the way he tasted wine — and finding nothing in it but truth.

"The court beneath the mountain was old then, older than the crown above it. Pale lords in pale halls, feeding on things we have names for only in dead languages. Memories. Years. Souls. And they were starving — their food had run thin, the way a fishery runs thin, the way a forest runs thin when too many axes swing. They made offers, sometimes. Quiet ones. To the desperate and the ambitious." Sable Fen laid her hand flat on the ancient page. "On the night of the Long Feast — the winter solstice, thirty-one years past — Queen-to-be Isolde walked down alone into the dark, and she sold them her sister's soul. Not killed her. Sold her. Elira lives still, taster, in whatever sense the pale court permits living. Caged. Fed upon. Kept sweet, the way you keep a hive."

The silence in the room had weight. Wren found she was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to hurt her fingers.

"And the winters?" Sebastian asked. His voice was very quiet.

"There is the arithmetic no one above does." The archivist's white eyes seemed to fix on him. "A soul is not a coin, boy, spent once and done. A soul is a hearth-fire. Take it from a land, and the land grows cold around the absence. Every year the court beneath feeds on Elira, and every year the cold spreads a little farther from the wound of her. First frost came a week early, then a month. Then the rivers froze in autumn. Then the harvests failed. The wise men blamed the stars. The priests blamed sin. And the queen—" Sable Fen smiled again, terribly. "The queen knew exactly what the cold was. Because she had bought her throne with it."

"But the hunt," Wren said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "Voss. The purge. Why hunt us? We didn't—"

"Why do farmers cull livestock before winter, child?" The old woman turned her blind face toward Wren, and her voice gentled into something worse than cruelty — pity. "You've been reading it backwards, all of you. The gifted aren't persecuted. We're *harvested*. Every soul Voss's riders take is carried down beneath the mountain — did you think the disappeared were executed? There are no bodies, are there? No trials, no heads on spikes. Just wagons going south, and wagons coming back empty." She tapped the ancient page. "Elira was the first course of a feast that has lasted thirty years. But one soul, however fine, is not enough forever. The court grows hungrier as the land grows colder — the cold is their leavings, child, the ash of what they burn. So the queen opened the larder doors. Anyone who can taste truth, hear it, see it, carry it — souls like ours are *rich*. Full-flavored. The pale lords pay crowns for them. And the queen pays silver to the neighbors who point us out."

Wren thought of the riders. Of her mother's loom still standing in the ashes. Of the neighbors who had waved goodbye to the families on the wagons and then bought new boots that spring.

Sebastian had gone the color of the paper on the table. When he spoke, each word came slowly, as if he were chewing something poisonous and refusing to spit it out.

"I tasted it," he said. "At the banquet. The poisoner — the chancellor's cupbearer. Under the arsenic there was grief, and under the grief there was certainty. I thought he simply hated the king. But it wasn't the king he was certain about. It was the *court*. He'd lost someone to the wagons." He looked up, and his eyes had a terrible clarity. "He wasn't trying to kill a man. He was trying to start a war he thought was already being fought against us."

"Now you begin to taste it properly," said Sable Fen. "The hunt isn't the crown defending itself from monsters, taster. The hunt is the crown *feeding*. And it will not stop — not for mercy, not for law, not while the queen's throne stays warm and the mountain's appetite stays cold. There is exactly one thing in this world that ends it."

"Kill the queen," Sebastian said.

"Worse than that." The archivist leaned forward, and the shifting script on the ancient page seemed to lean with her. "Free what she sold. Bring Elira's soul up out of that court alive, and the bargain breaks — broken goods, a debt unpaid, and the pale lords collect their debts from the debtor. The cold follows the soul home. The winters end. And Queen Isolde stands in front of her entire court knowing that the truth is walking toward her with her sister's face." She sat back. "It has never been done. The court beneath does not give back what it eats. Its hospitality is legendary, and its hospitality is poison — every dish, every drink, every courtesy designed to unravel whoever accepts it. No one who has gone down has come up."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above, the kestrel cried once and fell silent.

Sebastian Kavanagh looked at his own hands for a long moment — the hands that had swallowed ten years of death and refused to die.

"Well," he said finally, and there was something in his voice Wren had never heard from an adult before, something almost like relief. "Poisoned hospitality happens to be the one dinner party I've trained my whole life to survive."

Sable Fen's torn-paper smile returned.

"Then sit down, taster," she said, "and let me tell you what it will cost you. Because the mountain always names its price — and it always lets you think you're the one setting it."
