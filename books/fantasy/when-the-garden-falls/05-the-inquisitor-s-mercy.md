## 5. The Inquisitor's Mercy

Maren Voss had learned long ago that mercy was a tool like any other — a knife with a softer grip, better balanced for the hand that meant to use it often.

She rode into Hollowmere at dusk, when the smoke of supper fires hung low and blue over the rooftops, and she let her riders spread through the village the way frost spreads across a window: slowly, silently, from every edge at once. No shouting. No doors kicked in. That was the old way of hunting, the way of butchers, and it taught people to run. She preferred the stillness first. Stillness told you everything. A village with nothing to hide went on eating its supper; a village with a secret held its breath, and Maren Voss had spent twenty years learning to hear a held breath across a courtyard.

Hollowmere held its breath.

She sat her grey mare in the square beside the well and waited, gloved hands folded on the pommel, while her riders moved door to door with their lists. The mountains rose black behind the village, the Thornfells wearing their early snow like a shroud pulled up to the chin. Another winter coming early. Another winter the crown would not explain. Maren did not ask questions anymore. Asking questions, she had learned, was how the gifted were made — or how they were caught.

The third house on the miller's row belonged to Odile Ferrant.

Maren knew the name before she knew the face. The smuggler's ledgers, seized from a burned waystation two valleys east, had named her in a careful clerk's hand: *F., O. — routes, tolls, discretion guaranteed.* And one line more, underlined twice by some frightened informant: *knows Kavanagh.*

So when the woman was brought out — tall, grey-streaked, wrists bound with cord rather than iron because Maren had ordered it, cords made prisoners talk where irons made them silent — Maren looked at her for a long moment before speaking, and let the silence do what silence does.

"You keep a clean house," Maren said at last. "Cleaner than most. You burned your papers."

"Didn't have any papers," Odile said. Her voice was low and even, a river voice, unhurried over stones. "I'm a widow. I sell wool."

"You sell roads." Maren dismounted and stood close enough to smell woodsmoke and dried herbs on the woman's clothes — yarrow, feverfew, things gathered against wounds. "You sell them to men who need not to be seen. One of those men is named Sebastian Kavanagh. He came through these mountains within the month. Tell me which road he took, and you'll eat supper in your own kitchen tonight."

Odile's face did nothing. That was the first thing Maren noted, and the first thing that troubled her: nothing was very difficult to arrange. Fear could be masked. But there are small betrayals the body commits without permission — a flicker toward the north ridge, a swallow, a breath taken a half-second too carefully — and Maren watched for them the way a fisherman watches the float.

She saw none. And that itself was wrong, because everyone betrayed something.

* * *

They put her in the tithe barn, because Maren did not use cellars. Cellars were for men who needed darkness to break. Maren had found that light worked faster — good light, honest light, lamplight and the smell of hay, so that the prisoner never quite believed she was a prisoner until the moment it mattered.

She sat with Odile through the evening. She brought bread and hard cheese and a cup of small beer, and ate half of it herself to show it wasn't drugged, and talked about nothing — the price of wool, the early snow, the miller's crooked chimney. Odile answered in monosyllables and ate nothing.

Near midnight, Maren set down the cup and said, quietly, as if continuing an old conversation: "My mother was gifted."

The barn went very still. Outside, a rider's boot crunched on frozen mud, passed, faded.

"She could sweeten water that had gone bad. Just by holding it. The village called it a blessing until the year the tithe failed, and then they called it witchcraft, and my father —" Maren turned the cup in her fingers. "He did what fathers do. He gave me to the Church of the Veil, and the Church gave me to the crown, and the crown taught me that the gift is a sickness and I am the cure. I have signed forty-one writs of taking. I remember each name. Do you know why I remember them?"

Odile's eyes lifted for the first time in an hour. They were dark and dry and very tired.

"Because forgetting is the first mercy," Maren said, "and I am not permitted mercy. Not even for myself. So I will offer it to you instead, while I still can. Sebastian Kavanagh. Which road?"

For a long moment Odile said nothing, and Maren felt something she had not felt in twenty years of this work: hope. Hope that the woman would refuse, would spit, would make herself a martyr — because martyrs were simple. Martyrs could be logged and buried and grieved cleanly. It was the ones who broke, the ones whose eyes went wet and grateful as they sold their friends, that Maren carried home at night behind her eyes.

"I don't know any Kavanagh," Odile said finally. Softly. Almost kindly.

"No," Maren agreed. "But you know the high shepherd's path past the Grey Stair, and you know which passes the crown's maps get wrong, and a man doesn't survive ten years in the palace kitchens without learning to disappear, and disappearing is a trade, and trades have tolls, and tolls have keepers." She leaned forward. "I am not asking you to betray him. I am asking you to tell me where the roads go. What he does after that is between him and the winter."

Something moved behind Odile's face then — not fear. Maren had expected fear. It was pity, and it was worse.

"You're going to take him either way," Odile said.

"Yes."

"And the others. The girl with the birds. The archivist. All of them."

"Yes."

Odile nodded slowly, as if confirming a sum. Then she said, "There's a pass above the Grey Stair. Old drover's road. He'd take the western fork — there's a shrine at the fork, a stone with a carved hand. He always marks his stops with chalk on the left side of the shrine stones. Left side, do you understand? You'll find him inside three days if you ride tonight."

It was perfect. Too perfect. Every detail offered freely, precisely, in the accent of a woman relieved to finally put down a weight. Maren had heard confessions like this from a hundred mouths, and ninety of them had been true, and ten had been knives wrapped in silk, and she had never once learned to tell the difference until afterward.

*Afterward,* she thought, and hated herself for the thought, because afterward was the word cowards used.

* * *

They rode that night, twelve riders with torches hooded, up the drover's road through falling snow. Maren led them herself. The pass narrowed. The shrine stood where Odile said it would stand, a squat stone furred with lichen, a carved hand raised in blessing or warning — the old carvers had never been clear which — and Maren dismounted and ran her own glove along the left side of the stone.

Chalk. Faint, fresh, a single stroke.

She stood looking at it for a long time while the snow thickened around her and her riders waited in a patient half-circle, breath steaming, horses stamping.

Left side. As promised.

And that was when the crack came — not loud, not dramatic, nothing like the breaking she had witnessed in others. It was small and cold and precise, like the first fissure in a frozen lake, running out beneath her feet further than she could see. Because she understood, suddenly and completely, that Odile Ferrant had known exactly what she was doing. Had weighed her own life against Sebastian Kavanagh's road, and had chosen, calmly, with that terrible river patience, to spend herself buying him three days. Three days. Perhaps less. Perhaps it would mean nothing at all.

And Maren Voss, who had signed forty-one writs and remembered every name, was about to add a forty-second — no. Forty-third. There had been the miller's boy in spring, the one the records listed as *escaped, presumed dead.* She remembered. She always remembered.

"Inquisitor?" her lieutenant asked. "The trail?"

"The trail is false," Maren said.

The words came out of her before she had decided to say them, and once they were out she could not call them back, and she found she did not want to. The snow kept falling. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a long exhalation, like the mountain itself breathing out after holding its breath.

"False, my lady?"

"She sent us to empty rock. Turn the column south. We take Hollowmere again at dawn, and we search it properly this time — every loft, every root cellar, every grave that looks recently turned." She remounted, and her voice hardened back into the instrument the crown had shaped it into. "And bring me the woman. If she wants to spend her life on lies, the crown will collect the full price."

* * *

They hanged her at dawn in the village square, because Maren ordered it done quickly and the law required it done publicly, and the two requirements met in the middle with a horrible efficiency. Odile Ferrant went to the rope without trembling. She scanned the faces of her neighbors — heads bowed, eyes averted, the whole village complicit in its own terror — and then, in the last moment, she looked up and found Maren Voss among the riders, and she did not curse her.

She smiled. Small, brief, almost gentle. The smile of a woman passing a message over a wall: *you'll understand soon, and then you'll be the one on the rope, and I forgive you now so you'll know it can be done.*

Maren gave the signal herself. She always did. Delegating the drop was a cowardice she had never permitted, and she stood motionless through it, watching, memorizing, adding the name to the ledger she kept behind her eyes where no clerk could burn it.

*Odile Ferrant. Widow. Wool merchant. Keeper of roads.*

The body swung. The crows came down out of the early snow. And Maren Voss mounted her grey mare and rode out of Hollowmere with her face composed into the mask her office required, and behind the mask, in the place where her loyalty to the crown had lived for twenty years like a lamp in a window, something guttered.

Not extinguished. Guttered. The way a flame gutters when a door opens somewhere in the house — when cold air moves through rooms that have been warm too long.

She thought of her mother's hands sweetening bad water. She thought of forty-three names. She thought of the queen's seal on the hunt's commission, and of the strange arithmetic beneath it all — winters deepening with every soul taken, harvests failing, the court beneath the mountain fed and fed and never satisfied — and for the first time she allowed herself to ask the question that asking-questions was forbidden:

*What are we feeding?*

Three days later, a bird came to her camp — a small brown wren, absurdly bold, landing on her map case as she bent over it by firelight. It sang four notes. Simple, clear, unmistakable to anyone who had grown up in a village where such things were whispered:

*She knows. She knows. She knows.*

Maren sat very still, her hand nowhere near her blade, and listened to a bird tell her the truth her whole life had been built to avoid.

Then she rolled up the map, and did not mark the valley where the wren had come from.
