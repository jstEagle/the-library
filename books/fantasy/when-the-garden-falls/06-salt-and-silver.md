## 6. Salt and Silver

The news came up the mountain on the back of a crow.

Not a bird — Wren would have known its true name, heard whatever truth it carried in the grain of its call — but a woman named Halda, who kept the community's ledger of the lost and who had once, in another life, been a cook in the queen's own kitchens. She came limping into the hollow at dusk with her boots wrapped in sacking to hide her tracks, and she did not sit down so much as collapse into the chair nearest the fire, as though her body had finally been given permission to stop carrying what it carried.

"Odile," she said. "Odile is dead."

Sebastian had known it before she spoke. He had tasted it three days past, in a cup of well water drawn from the spring below the ridge — a faint bitterness like scorched iron, like grief steeped too long, the taste of someone dying badly somewhere downstream of his sorrow. His gift had never learned to distinguish between knowing and merely suspecting; it poured everything into him at the same temperature, and he had spent ten years learning which truths to trust and which were only the mountain's old pains seeping through stone. But this one he had trusted and said nothing, because to say it aloud was to make it real, and Odile had taught him, twenty years ago, that a thing spoken could not be unsaid.

Now it was spoken by another mouth, and there was no taking it back.

He did not weep. That was the poisoner's discipline in him, the taster's discipline — feeling everything and showing nothing, bottling the storm until the bottle either held or burst. But his hands shook as he took the cup of broth Halda offered, and when he drank it he tasted nothing at all, which was worse than any flavor. For the first time since the gift had woken in him, his tongue went mute. Grief, it seemed, could silence even truth.

* * *

They buried her name, not her body. There was no body. Voss's riders had taken her from the waystation at Cold Ford and burned what remained, and all that came back down the smugglers' roads was her knife, passed hand to hand through three villages of the frightened until it reached the hollow wrapped in oilcloth like something holy. Sebastian held it across his palms and remembered the first night he'd met her, seventeen years old and starving, when she'd fed him mutton and black bread and told him the roads were older than any crown and belonged to no one who claimed them.

"The roads are safe," she'd said. "The roads stay safe. It's the people walking them who get careless."

His roads. His fault. The thought sat in him like a swallowed stone. Voss had needed a trail, and Odile had refused to give her a true one, and now the waystations stood empty and the beacon-fires on the high passes burned unattended and every soul who had ever moved quietly along those paths moved in fear instead. He had brought the hunt to the mountains simply by running into them. You cannot flee into a place without making it part of your flight.

Wren found him that night sitting alone at the edge of the hollow, where the trees thinned and you could see the lowland fires — the hunt's camps, ringed like a noose around the foothills. She sat beside him without speaking, because she understood silence better than anyone he'd ever known; a girl who listened to birds all day learned when not to add her voice to the world.

"You're tasting it," she said eventually. "Aren't you. The whole valley."

"I'm trying not to."

"Don't." She turned to look at him, and in the starlight her eyes had that uncanny stillness they always carried, as if she were listening to something just behind him. "My mother used to say that swallowing grief without tasting it is how it kills you slow. Taste it. All of it. Then decide what it's for."

And beneath the taste of Odile's death — the iron, the smoke, the particular sourness of a lie told under torture and then abandoned for the truth — Sebastian tasted something else. Something moving through the valley like weather. Fear. Not his own. The hunt's riders, camped in their hundreds below, each of them carrying a private terror they fed every night with drink and cruelty: fear of the dark, fear of their commander, fear most of all of being discovered to be exactly what they hunted. Half of Voss's men, if the water and wine that reached them could be believed, carried some small gift themselves. A knack for finding lost things. A way with animals. A grandmother's second sight, never spoken of, never used, buried alive inside them the way the court buried everything it couldn't burn.

They were hunting themselves. They simply didn't know it yet.

* * *

The archivist — an old man called Emmerich, exiled from the royal library for cataloguing truths the crown preferred uncatalogued — spread the plan out on his table like a map, except the map was made of bottles.

Small ones, clay-stoppered, each holding a measure of amber liquid that smelled of juniper and bitter bark and something underneath that made Sebastian's tongue prickle from a foot away, the way a struck bell makes the air around it ache. He knew every poison in the crown's arsenal by heart; he had drunk them all, deliberately, over ten years, building his immunity one near-death at a time. This was not a poison. It was the opposite of a poison. It was a key.

"Fear-tincture," Emmerich said. "An old recipe. Older than the kingdom. The gifted communities kept it through the last purge, two hundred years ago, and the one before that. You put it in the hunt's supplies — the wine, mostly, riders will drink anything after a week in the cold — and for a few hours every man who drinks it tastes what others feel. Their own hidden gifts wake up. And they taste the fear of everyone around them. Including the fear of the man standing next to them, who is also secretly gifted, and who is terrified of exactly them."

Sebastian lifted one bottle and held it to the light. "And then?"

"And then," Emmerich said, "a hundred men discover in a single night that the hunt is a mirror. That they've been riding for months alongside people like themselves, denouncing people like themselves, burning villages full of people like themselves. Fear doesn't make men gentle. But shame does things fear can't. Some of them will desert. Some will turn informer against their own officers. A few — the dangerous few — will turn their blades on the hunt itself."

It was elegant. It was bloodless, or nearly. It was exactly the kind of solution Sebastian might have designed himself, back when his work was saving chancellors with a swallow and a steady face: use the enemy's own sustenance as the weapon, let the poison do the confessing, keep your own hands clean of violence. He had spent a decade believing that the cleanest justice was the kind administered invisibly, in a cup, by someone no one ever thanked.

He agreed. That was the mistake, or perhaps it wasn't — he would spend the rest of the chapter, and possibly the rest of his life, unable to decide.

* * *

The wagons came up the valley road on the fourth morning, six of them, escorted by forty riders, hauling salt and hardtack and casks of cheap red wine from the lowland vintners who had learned that selling to the hunt was cheaper than being suspected by it. Getting to the wine was the easy part. Sebastian had spent half his life among smugglers, and the community's runners — lean, silent children who could cross a frozen river without cracking the ice — slipped into the supply camp at night the way water slips under a door. Three casks tapped, three stoppers of tincture dissolved into the bungs, and by dawn the wagons rolled on toward the high camps carrying a cargo no manifest would ever list.

The effect took two days to show, and when it showed, it showed everywhere at once.

The first sign was small: a rider found deserted at a crossroads, his horse gone, his sword driven point-down into the snow like a grave marker for himself. Then two more. Then, on the third night, a sergeant in the Third Company woke his entire tent screaming — he had dreamed, vividly, of the village his troop had burned in the autumn, and the dream had arrived with flavors attached, and he had tasted the char and the terror and the sweet rot of his own complicity, and he could not make his mouth forget it. By week's end, eleven men had deserted, four had denounced their own captain as secretly gifted — which, given the tincture, may even have been true — and one lieutenant had hanged himself from the beam of a supply barn, leaving a note that named Maren Voss in letters so ragged they looked written by a drowning man.

In the hollow, they celebrated. Someone opened a jar of preserved cherries saved from summer. Wren sang — actually sang, aloud, for the first time since Sebastian had met her, a snatch of some mountain hymn about the fox outwitting the hounds — and the birds around the clearing sang back, and for one evening the community allowed itself to believe that the hunt could be broken the way a fever breaks: suddenly, from inside, without a single new wound.

Sebastian drank the cherry brandy they pressed on him and tasted, in it, hope. Real hope, thick and red and warm. And underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the taste he had come to dread more than arsenic: the taste of consequences still traveling. Somewhere south, something had been set in motion that had not finished arriving.

* * *

It arrived on the ninth day, as smoke.

Halda's cousin sent word from the foothill settlements, and the word was ash. Voss had stopped taking villages prisoner. She had stopped, in fact, taking anything at all. Where before her riders had seized the gifted and marched them toward the mountain in chains — harvest, the archivist had called it, and the phrase had haunted Sebastian ever since — now they burned first and asked questions of the ruins. Three settlements in five days. Greystone Hollow, where a family of hearth-listeners had lived undetected for four generations. Millbrook, which had sheltered two of the runners who'd tapped the wine casks. And Fenwick Cross, which had done nothing at all except stand where Voss's column happened to pass, and which now stood nowhere.

She had left a proclamation nailed to the church door at Fenwick Cross, and a copy of it reached the hollow folded inside a survivor's coat, and Sebastian read it twice and felt his blood go cold both times:

*"For every act of sabotage against the hunt, two villages will answer. The crown does not negotiate with poisoners. The crown burns."*

The arithmetic of it was simple and monstrous. Their strike had cost the hunt eleven deserters and one dead officer. Voss's answer had cost, by Halda's best count, upward of two hundred souls, most of them not gifted at all, most of them guilty of nothing except living downhill from people who were. The tincture had worked precisely as designed. It had accomplished nothing except to teach the inquisitor that her enemy fought dirty, and to give her a reason to stop pretending the hunt had rules.

Sebastian stood at the clearing's edge again that night, in the same spot where Wren had told him to taste his grief, and he tasted this instead: smoke, and rendered fat, and the sweet-rot smell of burned grain stores, and beneath it all a thread of something he recognized from the banquet hall a lifetime ago — the chancellor's poisoner's certainty, the flat metallic conviction that atrocity is simply policy continued by other means. Voss's signature. He could taste her now in the wind off the fires, the way you can taste a specific cook in a familiar dish. She had seasoned the whole valley with herself.

"We made it worse," he said aloud, to the fire, to Wren, to whoever was listening. "We gave her permission. We handed her a justification with our own hands and she accepted it gratefully and multiplied it by two hundred."

Wren did not argue. She only said, quietly, "Or we showed her that we can reach her. Both things are true. They're always both true."

Emmerich argued, though. The old archivist, who had spent two centuries of accumulated exile convinced that knowledge was a weapon that never missed, grew defensive in the way scholars do, retreating into precedent: the last purge, the one before, the tincture had always eventually cracked the hunts. Give it time. Give it more casks. Escalate. Meet escalation with escalation, and eventually the hunt would drown in its own reflected fear.

But Sebastian had spent ten years drinking poison in small doses, and he knew something about escalation that armchair strategists did not: the body adapts, and so does the conscience. Voss had already survived her own crack — the moment at Cold Ford, when Odile died and something in the inquisitor had bent and then, horribly, straightened itself harder than before. She was not going to drown in shame. She was going to metabolize it into fuel. Every village they poisoned her men into burning was a log on her fire, and she had an endless supply of matches, and they had an endless supply of faith in cleverness, and only one of those things was renewable.

That was the night the anger curdled. He felt it happen physically, the way milk turns — warmth becoming thickness becoming something you can't drink anymore. He had wanted justice. Justice, it turned out, tasted like other people's ashes when you administered it through a wine cask. Sabotage saved no one; it only chose whose deaths you were willing to spend. And the spending was easier than it should have been, and that ease frightened him more than Voss did, because it meant the hunt had gotten inside him too. It was teaching him its arithmetic.

He went to Emmerich's table and set down the remaining bottles, all twelve of them, in a row, like evidence at a trial where he was both accused and judge.

"No more," he said. "Not this way."

"They'll say you're soft—"

"They can say what they like. I've tasted what this costs, and I'm the only one here equipped to do the tasting properly, and I'm telling you the price has already exceeded the purchase." He looked at the old man until the defensiveness drained out of him and left only exhaustion. "There's a road that ends this. You know there is. You've known since the night you told me about the queen and her sister. Everything else is just killing time while the winter kills us."

Emmerich was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind moved through the hollow, and somewhere in it Wren heard — she swore afterward — a bird singing a song no bird should have known, notes from a lullaby of the southern court, a song about a sister who walked into a mountain and did not walk out.

"There is a road," Emmerich admitted at last. "Down. Through the deep ways, the ones the smugglers sealed a century ago and swore never to reopen. It ends beneath the Thornfells, in the dark, at the court that eats. And the door only opens for someone carrying something it wants."

"What does it want?"

"That," said the archivist, "is the bargain. And bargains beneath the mountain are never explained in advance. Only paid."

Sebastian picked up one of the twelve bottles — the last one, the one he'd kept apart from the beginning without quite admitting why — and turned it in his hands. Amber liquid. Juniper and bitter bark. A key, Emmerich had called it. Keys open doors.

He wondered, with a cold clarity that felt like the first honest thing he'd felt in weeks, whether it might open the wrong one.
