## 7. The Descent Bargain

The fire had burned down to its red bones by the time anyone spoke again.

Sebastian still held the twelfth bottle. Around him the hidden hall of the gifted had gone quiet in that particular way quiet falls on people who have just learned the shape of their own doom — not with screaming, but with the careful stillness of hands that do not want to tremble where others can see.

Emmerich, the exiled archivist, sat wrapped in a cloak too thin for the mountain cold, his ink-stained fingers laced over one knee. He was an old man built like a folded letter, all creases and hidden text, and when he finally spoke it was in the voice he must have used for reading wills aloud: flat, precise, merciless in its clarity.

"You should understand what you're asking before you ask it," he said. "So I'll say it plainly. A soul kept beneath the mountain is not stolen goods sitting in a vault. It is *bound*. Woven into the court's oldest covenant, sealed in wine and salt and oath. You cannot simply take Elira's soul out, the way a thief lifts a ring from a tray." His eyes, pale as skimmed milk, found Sebastian's across the fire. "It has to be *carried*. And something has to carry it willingly."

"Willingly," Sebastian repeated. The word tasted like copper.

"Flesh consents, or the binding holds. That is the first law under the mountain, older than the queen's grandmother's grandmother. They cannot seize a vessel. They can only be *given* one." Emmerich unfolded himself and rose, joints cracking like green wood in a hot hearth, and crossed to the shelf where he kept his heresies — ledgers, fragments, a jar of mountain ash berries pickled in something that smelled of lightning. "Every soul they've harvested in three hundred years was taken by trickery, by contract, by desperate people signing away what they didn't understand. But a soul given freely—" he tapped the ledger, "—a soul given freely can also *leave* freely. If it binds itself to flesh that walks out under its own power."

"And no one has ever done it," said Odile's sister Marta from the shadows by the wall. Her voice was raw; she had not stopped weeping since word came down from the pass that Odile was dead, though she wept the way stone weeps, silently, wearing channels into herself.

"No one has ever been able to," Emmerich corrected gently. "Because to reach the chalice, you must survive the court's hospitality. And their hospitality is poison, friends. Not metaphor. Every cup poured beneath the mountain carries a draft of forgetting, a distillation of surrender. One sip and you sit down at the table forever. Two sips and you forget why you ever wanted to leave." He turned back to Sebastian, and there was something almost apologetic in the old man's face. "Except."

"Except me," Sebastian said.

"Except you." Emmerich spread his hands. "Ten years of dosing yourself with every poison the crown could buy. Your body has learned to refuse them. Your gift has learned to *taste* them — to taste truth, memory, intent. You are the only living soul who could drink from their cups and remain himself. Which means you are the only living soul who could bind Elira to your flesh and walk her out through a door they believe is sealed against exactly that."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a long exhale.

Sebastian looked down at the bottle in his hands. Juniper and bitter bark. He thought about how many times he had swallowed death for a king who never learned his name, tasting treason in the chancellor's wine while the orchestra played a gavotte. He had spent ten years being the kingdom's mouth — the one tongue trusted to know what was safe to swallow. It seemed fitting, in a grim and circular way, that the same tongue would now be asked to swallow freedom itself.

"The cost," he said. "Name it. All of it. I'm done learning prices after the fact."

Emmerich did not flinch. That, at least, Sebastian respected. Most men, when asked to name a terrible price, softened it with padding — *perhaps*, *possibly*, *it may be*. The archivist spoke as if reading from a bill of sale.

"First: the binding requires consent, truly given, which means you must drink the chalice *knowing* what you drink. The soul will enter your blood, your marrow, your memories. For the rest of your life — however long that is — you will not always be certain which longings are yours and which are hers. Grief is contagious, Sebastian Kavanagh. So is hope."

"I've lived with other people's grief in my mouth for ten years. Go on."

"Second: the court will feel it the moment the binding takes. There will be no quiet exit. You will have perhaps an hour — less, if they are quick — between the drinking and the mountain waking. Whatever road you mean to run, you must choose it before you raise the cup."

"Third?"

Emmerich hesitated for the first time. It was small — a flicker in the milk-pale eyes — but Sebastian had spent a decade reading faces across banquet tables, hunting the micro-tremor that preceded a lie or a confession. He caught it.

"Third," Emmerich said, "is that souls remember how they were bound. Elira's was taken by her own sister's signature. She has spent thirty years beneath the mountain marinating in that betrayal. When she wakes inside you, the first thing she will taste is *your* relationship to Isolde's crown. If any part of you still serves the throne—" he let the sentence die, and its corpse said enough.

"If I waver," Sebastian translated, "she'll know. And a betrayed soul in a traitor's body might do anything."

"Might," Emmerich agreed. "Or might simply grieve. With souls, grief and fury are neighbors who share a wall."

* * *

They argued until the candles drowned in their own wax.

Marta wanted to go in his place, which was noble and impossible — her body would fold at the first cup, and everyone at the fire knew it, including her, which made the offer more grief than strategy. The tanner twins, who could taste lies in smoke, offered to scout the tunnels. The old hedge-witch Sefia pressed a pouch of dried rowan into Sebastian's hand and told him to chew it if the forgetting came, though her eyes admitted it was a prayer more than a medicine.

And Wren stood apart from all of it, arms crossed, birds perched along the beam above her head like a jury that had already decided.

"No," Sebastian told her, before she even opened her mouth.

"You don't get to say no to me. I'm not yours."

"You're sixteen."

"I'm gifted, and I'm the only person alive who can hear what the mountain's birds are saying, and you'll be blind down there without me." She stepped forward into the firelight, chin up, and for a moment she looked so much like a young falcon deciding whether the glove was worth the jesses that Sebastian almost laughed. "You think I haven't noticed? Everyone plans around you because you're the key. But keys get lost, keys get broken, keys get *kept*. Someone has to come find you when they lock you in a drawer."

"Wren—"

"Birds sing underground," she said. "Did you know that? Starlings nest in the deep caves below Thornfell. My gift doesn't stop at the tree line. Down there, where the pale lords have eaten every memory but their own, where nothing sings — I'll be the only song in the dark." Her voice cracked, just once, and she hated it, he could see her hating it. "Odile died buying you a road. I'm not letting you walk it alone."

The name landed like a stone in still water. Odile, who had fed him stew and lies in equal measure and loved him through both. Odile, whose false trail had bought him these weeks, this fire, this terrible clarity. He carried her death in his chest the way he carried arsenic once — a dose he'd survived, but never metabolized.

"If you come," he said at last, "you follow my orders. Even when they're stupid. Especially then."

"They're going to be stupid," Wren said.

"Frequently."

"Then we understand each other." She stuck out her hand, and they shook on it like smugglers closing a deal, which, Sebastian supposed, was precisely what they were.

* * *

He did not sleep. Instead he climbed to the cave mouth and sat with the night, and the mountain, and the last of the juniper liquor, which he did not drink.

Thornfell spread below him, black and silver under a moon two days from full. Somewhere south, beyond the passes, the hunt's fires dotted the valleys like a pox — Voss's riders burning what the harvest missed. Somewhere farther south, a queen sat behind walls built on her sister's silence, keeping warm through a winter she had purchased. And somewhere directly beneath his boots, past miles of smugglers' tunnels that Odile had mapped in her head and never written down, a dead woman's soul waited in frozen wine for someone willing to become her door.

*Willing flesh,* Emmerich had said. Sebastian flexed his hands, scarred from a decade of holding cups he wasn't allowed to refuse. His whole life, his flesh had belonged to other people's purposes. The king's safety. The crown's secrets. Now a stranger's ghost, and a kingdom's justice, and a girl with starlings on her shoulders.

It occurred to him, with a humorlessness that felt almost holy, that he had finally found the one thing he'd never been asked to taste.

His own choice.

Behind him, footsteps — Wren, wrapped in a blanket, a starling asleep in her hood like a fist of feathers. She didn't speak. She just sat down beside him in the cold and put the map between them: Odile's route, drawn from memory onto birch bark, the tunnels marked in charcoal, the deepest descent circled twice.

At the bottom of the page, in Odile's blunt hand, were three words:

*Don't trust doors.*

Sebastian looked at them a long time. Then he took the twelfth bottle from his coat, set it upright on the stone between them, and watched the moonlight move through the amber like something alive, turning over in its sleep.

"Tomorrow," he said.

Below them, faint as a heartbeat, the mountain answered.
