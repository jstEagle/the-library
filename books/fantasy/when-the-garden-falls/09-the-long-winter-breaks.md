## 9. The Long Winter Breaks

*Sebastian*

The chalice sat at the center of the feast like a wound the court had learned to call beautiful.

It stood on a dais of black glass, taller than a man, its bowl wide as a rain barrel, and within it wine had frozen mid-pour — a cascade of garnet ice, suspended for what the archivist's maps suggested was thirty years. The pale nobles circled it in their slow procession, lifting their own small cups in homage, and Sebastian understood now what they were toasting. Not the wine. The prisoner.

He could taste it from across the hall. Even through the cold, even through the reek of candle-tallow and old memory that hung over the court beneath the mountain like fog over a drowned field, Elira's soul reached for him. It tasted of juniper and snowmelt, of a garden before frost, of someone laughing in a kitchen — and underneath all of it, thin and constant as a wire under tension, grief. Thirty years of grief, poured into a cup no one would drink from, because drinking it would mean setting her free.

"Brother Taster." A steward glided at his elbow, gray as ash, smiling with too many teeth. "The Regent of the Deep Table bids you approach. She wishes to know if the vintage pleases."

Sebastian bowed. His hands did not shake. Ten years of practice had bought him that much — a body that lied beautifully even when everything inside it was screaming *run*. Wren was somewhere behind him among the serving galleries, wrapped in borrowed livery, three songbirds hidden in the lining of her coat, and every heartbeat he spent on this dais was a heartbeat she spent alone among things that ate memories for dessert.

"The honor is mine," he said, and took the silver ladle they offered him.

The frozen wine came away in curls, like shaving wax. He raised them to his lips with the ceremony the court expected — the deep inhale, the reverence — and let the soul of Queen Isolde's sister dissolve against his tongue.

The world broke open.

A garden. Sunlight through pear leaves. A woman's hands, younger than his mother's, pressing seeds into dark soil while another woman — Isolde, unlined, unafraid, wearing a crown of woven flowers instead of gold — laughed beside her. Then winter. Not weather-winter but the other kind, the kind with teeth: a door of stone, a voice like silk sliding over a knife, *the throne is not safe while you live, sister, but your soul — your soul will keep me safe forever.* And then the tearing, the unbearable tearing, a self pulled out of a body like bread torn from dough, and darkness, and the chalice, and thirty years of watching through frozen glass as the seasons above forgot how to turn—

Sebastian knelt. He could not help it. The court murmured approval; they mistook his collapse for awe.

"It pleases," he said, and his voice came out ruined, and the steward smiled wider.

Behind the regent's shoulder, deep in the vaulted dark where the feast-light did not reach, something vast shifted its weight. The mountain itself, or what lived in the mountain. Hungry. Patient. Fed on a queen's treachery and a kingdom's slow freeze.

*Drink it all,* Sebastian told himself. *Every drop. And then run.*

He lifted the ladle again.

* * *

*Wren*

The birds were singing lies, and Wren had never loved them more.

She moved along the serving gallery with a tray of candied roots she had no intention of delivering, and around her the court beneath the mountain wore its illusions like gauze. To the eye, the hall was magnificent — pillars of veined marble, nobles in cloth-of-silver, a ceiling painted with constellations. But her wrens sat hidden against her ribs, and through their ears she heard the truth humming under the beauty: rot. Stone gone soft as bread. Voices that sounded like voices but were really wind moving through hollow bones. Half the nobles glittering at the feast had been dead for centuries, and their splendor was a memory the mountain wore the way a beetle wears the shell of the thing it killed.

*Steady,* she told the smallest bird, the one pressed closest to her heart. *Not yet. Wait for the taster.*

She had watched Sebastian kneel at the great chalice and her stomach had dropped through the floor, because kneeling in that place was how people stopped being people. But then he rose, and there was something in his face she'd only seen once before — on Odile's daughter, the morning Odile's rider-warning arrived and everyone in the hidden valley understood what Voss's mercy cost. Grief with a spine in it. Grief that had decided what it was going to do.

The tray trembled. Wren set it down.

Above them — miles above, past the smugglers' tunnels, past the Thornfell passes buried in early snow — the hunt was still riding. Villages still burning. And every winter that fell was falling because the thing in this mountain drank and drank and was never full.

Her grandmother had taught her the songs. *Birds don't lie,* the old woman said. *People lie to birds all day long, thinking feathers make them deaf. But a thrush remembers every word spoken under its tree.* Wren had spent her whole life hearing truths other people didn't know they were telling.

Now she drew a breath, put two fingers to her lips, and gave the signal — a low whistle, three notes rising, the call her grandmother used to bring the flock home before a storm.

In the lining of her coat, three small throats opened.

* * *

*Sebastian*

The second ladleful went down easier than the first, which frightened him more, because it meant his body was accepting the soul, weaving it in, making room. That was the bargain the archivist had named: *willing flesh, freely given. The soul must have somewhere to live when it leaves the cup.* He had volunteered knowing what it meant. Knowing that part of him might not come back up the mountain. Knowing that whatever walked out of these tunnels wearing his face might carry a stranger's laughter, a stranger's grief for a garden thirty years dead.

He was on the fourth ladle when the singing started.

It began small — a thread of birdsong threading through the feast-hall's murmur, so out of place that half the court turned toward it with expressions of dull, ancient confusion. Then it grew. Three birds, four, a dozen voices braided together, and each note landed on the court like acid on paint.

The illusions began to peel.

Sebastian saw it happen in waves. Marble pillars went gray, then translucent, then vanished altogether, leaving supports of wet black root. The constellation ceiling guttered out like a snuffed lamp. Nobles flickered — silver gowns becoming rags, beautiful faces becoming skulls wearing smiles, and beneath the skulls, worse: shapes of hunger, long and folded, things that had been eating memories so long they had forgotten they once had bodies. They shrieked. The sound was not sound so much as absence, a hole in the air where music should be.

The regent of the deep table rose from her seat, and her face was still beautiful, which meant she was the most dangerous thing in the room.

"The taster," she said, and her voice came from everywhere. "The taster is drinking what belongs to the court."

Sebastian swallowed the fifth ladleful and felt Elira settle inside him like a bird coming home to roost — a warmth behind his sternum, a pressure of presence, *someone else awake in the house of his body.* She knew where she was. He felt her recognition, her horror, and then, astonishingly, her fury, clean and bright as a struck flint.

*Give me your hands,* she seemed to say, without words. *I remember this place. I remember how it locks.*

His hands were already moving. Around the chalice's base ran a ring of runes, and where his fingers touched, the frozen wine groaned — thirty years of ice remembering, all at once, that it had once been water, that water moves, that nothing caged stays caged forever.

The chalice shattered.

Wine and ice and light exploded outward, and the whole court screamed as one throat, and the mountain — the mountain *convulsed*, the way a body convulses when the thing parasitizing it is suddenly torn loose. Dust rained from the vanished ceiling. Somewhere far below, doors that had been closed since before Sebastian's grandfather was born slammed open on their own.

"RUN," Elira said, out loud now, in his own voice, in a voice that was also hers, and Sebastian ran.

* * *

*Wren*

The tunnels were screaming.

Wren fled through corridors that had been stable for a thousand years and were now shedding stone like a molting bird, and behind her the court's hunger poured after the fleeing soul like floodwater after a breached dam. She could hear them — the memory-eaters, stripped of their pretty shapes, moving fast on limbs that made no sense. Her birds flew ahead of her through the dark, singing the way home, and she followed their song because it was the only true thing left in the world.

She burst into the antechamber where the smugglers' stair climbed toward the surface and found Sebastian already there, hauling himself up the first steps, and clinging to his back — she saw it and her heart nearly stopped — a figure of light shaped like a woman, translucent, laughing and weeping in the same breath.

"You found her," Wren gasped.

"She found *me*," Sebastian said. "Climb."

They climbed. The stair shook. Stones the size of coffins dropped past them into the dark below, and the hungry shapes came pouring up behind, faster than any living thing should move, and Wren's birds wheeled back down the shaft to harry them, singing truths until the truths burned the creatures like sunlight.

Then the passage leveled, widened — and Maren Voss stood in it.

Lantern-light caught the inquisitor's blade, already drawn. Her riders were nowhere; she had come down alone, following the convulsion like a shark following blood, and now she blocked the only road to the sky with snowmelt dripping off her cloak and murder in her stance.

"Kavanagh." Her voice was steady. Only her eyes weren't. "Give me the soul. The crown will forgive—"

"The crown sold its own sister," Sebastian said, Elira's light blazing off him like a torch. "You've hunted children for *that*. For *this*." He gestured at the shaking dark, at the hunger climbing toward them. "You've fed it. You're still feeding it."

Voss's blade did not waver. But Wren, who heard truths in everything, heard the crack running through the inquisitor's silence — heard, beneath the orders and the doctrine, a memory Voss had never spoken aloud: a village, a fire, a child's voice asking *why*, and no answer the crown had ever given that held weight.

The first of the memory-eaters rounded the corner behind them.

Wren saw Voss see it. Saw the inquisitor understand, in one lurching instant, what she had served. What she had *been*, all these years — not the crown's sword but the court below its fork, the hand that shoveled souls into the dark and called it justice.

Voss turned her blade around.

"For Odile," she said — and Wren's breath caught, because the name was a confession, the name was a wound, the name was the first honest thing Maren Voss had ever said in Wren's hearing — "and for every village I burned while telling myself it was mercy."

She met the hunger head-on, lantern raised, blade singing, and her light filled the tunnel like a sunrise that refused to end.

"Climb!" she screamed at them. "*Climb!*"

They climbed. Behind them the light flared, and flared, and then the mountain shrugged its whole shoulder, and the passage behind collapsed in thunder, and whether Voss's dawn or the mountain's dark won, the rock sealed the answer away where no one living would ever taste it.

Snow. Cold, real, honest snow, falling through the cave mouth onto Sebastian's upturned face. Wren collapsed beside him, birds scattering upward into a sky gone gray-gold with dawn, and Elira's light stepped free of his shadow and stood blinking in the open air like a woman waking from a thirty-year sleep.

Above them, far to the south, the towers of the capital glittered against the breaking winter.

Elira looked at them a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was quiet, and it carried anyway.

"Now," she said, "we go tell my sister what I remember."
