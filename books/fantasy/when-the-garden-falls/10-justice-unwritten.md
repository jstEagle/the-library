## 10. Justice, Unwritten

The mountain had not stopped shaking since they came up out of it.

Sebastian felt it still in his teeth, in the soles of his boots, a low grinding tremor like a millstone turning somewhere far beneath the world. The court below was dying or dead — he did not know which, and could not bring himself to care. What he knew was the weight against his back, the cold that seeped through wool and leather and skin, the presence that rode him like a second shadow and would not be put down.

Elira. Queen Isolde's sister. A soul thirty years caged in frozen wine, now poured into willing flesh that had survived every poison the crown ever fed it.

She weighed nothing. She weighed everything.

Wren walked beside him on the switchback road, her hood back despite the wind, her face turned upward toward the grey morning. Somewhere above them, unseen, a bird sang — one of hers, one of the wild things that had followed her out of the dark. Its song carried no words Sebastian could taste, but Wren smiled at it, and that was enough.

"She's quiet," Wren said.

"She's listening."

"To what?"

Sebastian considered. The snowfields slid past below the road, blue-white and silent. "To being," he said finally. "Thirty years of being drunk from a chalice by pale things in the dark. I imagine being is very loud right now."

* * *

They came down out of the Thornfells in four days, moving fast along roads only smugglers and the dead remembered, and on the fifth day they stood on the ridge above the capital and saw the smoke.

Not burning. Cooking-fires, hearth-smoke, the ordinary breath of a city — but thinner than it should have been, as if half the chimneys had gone cold. The hunt had emptied houses. The hunt had emptied whole streets. From this distance the city looked like a table set for a feast where most of the guests had fled before the first course.

"They'll have heard," Wren said. "About the mountain."

"Everyone will have heard something. None of it will be true." Sebastian shifted Elira's weight — she walked when he asked her to walk, ate when he fed her, but mostly she drifted behind his eyes like a passenger watching weather. "That's why we're going to the palace instead of away from it. Lies fill empty rooms fastest. We need to be there when they do."

"You're going to tell them everything."

"I'm going to let *her* tell them everything." He glanced sideways at Wren. "I'm just the cup she's served in."

* * *

The palace gates were held by soldiers who did not know whether they were still soldiers.

Sebastian saw it in their faces, tasted it in the air of the gatehouse — fear without a shape yet, discipline dissolving like sugar in rain. They had orders, presumably. But the inquisitor Maren Voss had ridden out with two hundred riders and come back with eleven, and the mountain had screamed in the night hard enough to crack windows in the lower town, and no one in the whole sprawling body of the crown knew anymore what they were guarding or for whom.

A young lieutenant with chapped hands looked at Sebastian's travel-worn clothes, at the girl beside him, at the way Sebastian moved — like a man carrying something precious through a crowd of thieves — and made the oldest decision in any kingdom: he decided to be on the winning side of whatever happened next.

"The lords are assembled," the lieutenant said. "In the great hall. They've been assembled for three days. Nobody will tell us why."

"Because nobody knows," Sebastian said. "We do. Take us to them."

* * *

The great hall smelled of tallow and fear and old stone.

The lords of the realm sat in their rows like crows on a fence — barons and marshals, chancellors and churchmen, the men who had signed the hunt's proclamations and the men who had merely failed to stop them. At the far end, on the dais beneath the winter banner, Queen Isolde sat her throne.

Ten years since Sebastian had seen her close. She had been beautiful once; she was still beautiful the way a frozen river is beautiful, all surface and pressure underneath. Her hands were folded in her lap. Her eyes were fixed on the middle distance, on some point thirty years away.

And beside the throne, standing where no guard stood, in a grey dress that fit her like a memory, was a woman none of them recognized.

Sebastian stopped at the hall's center, where the light from the high windows fell, and the doors boomed shut behind him, and three hundred lords turned to look.

He had planned a speech. In the tunnels, in the snow, in the long grey dawns of the descent, he had composed it sentence by sentence — the taster's testimony, the harvest, the souls, the winters. He opened his mouth to begin it.

And the woman beside the throne stepped forward, off the dais, down the steps, into the hall, and spoke first.

"My name," she said, and her voice filled the stone the way water fills a dry well, "is Elira. Thirty-one years ago my sister sold me to the court beneath the mountain to buy herself a throne. You have been eating the price of that bargain every winter since. Your children have been taken for it. Your neighbors burned for it. And your queen has known, every year, exactly what the cold was for."

The silence that followed had texture. Sebastian tasted it — copper and salt, the flavor of three hundred minds refusing, all at once, to believe what they had already understood.

Queen Isolde rose from her throne.

Sebastian watched her hands. He had spent ten years reading hands — poisoners' hands, liars' hands, grieving hands — and he knew every language fingers speak. Hers said: *I am deciding whether to reach for a knife.* Her eyes found Sebastian's across the hall, and in them he tasted something he had never expected from a queen.

Relief.

"You came back," she said to the woman in grey. Only that. As if three hundred witnesses were not present. As if the hall were a sickroom and Elira a sister returned from a long voyage.

"I was brought back," Elira said. "By a man whose tongue you poisoned into a weapon, and a child whose birds you taught to listen. Sister. I am not here for vengeance. Vengeance is what you bought with me. I am here for the truth, because the truth is the only thing you never paid for."

She turned to the assembled lords.

"Here is the truth. The hunt was never about the gifted. It was a harvest — your souls, gathered to feed the court below, payment on a debt your queen has been servicing for thirty-one years. Every denunciation you rewarded, every neighbor you sold, every fire you lit — it all went down the mountain's throat. And the winters came earlier each year because the court grew fatter and hungrier, and because a soul sold is not a soul paid. Debts of that kind are never paid. They are only renewed."

She lifted her hand, and in it — Sebastian had not seen her take it, had not seen anyone give it to her — was the chalice. Frozen wine, black glass, cold enough to frost the air around it even here, even now.

"This is what remains of the contract. The court beneath the mountain is broken. The debt dies with it. There is nothing left to renew." She set the chalice on the flagstones between herself and the throne, and stepped back from it, and left it lying there like a shed skin. "So the question before you is not what my sister owes me. That account is closed. The question is what you owe each other — and whether you will build your justice on law, which can be bought, or on truth, which can only be told."

* * *

They did not kill her.

Sebastian had expected it — had braced for it, had stood ready to taste the hall's fear turn to bloodlust, to shout over it, to spend whatever remained of himself stopping it. Three hundred lords, one deposed queen, thirty-one years of stolen winters: the arithmetic of vengeance was simple, and crowds love simple arithmetic.

But Elira had spoken of debts and accounts, and the metaphor had done its work. The lords argued for six days behind sealed doors while the city held its breath, and on the seventh day the heralds read the settlement from the palace steps:

Queen Isolde, stripped of the crown, remanded to house arrest in a northern abbey for the term of her natural life — alive, because Elira asked it, and Elira's word outweighed three decades of grievance. The hunt dissolved by proclamation. All writs of denunciation burned in the palace yard, publicly, by the same lieutenants who had once served them. And a new accord, drafted in haste and hope, granting pardon and protection to the gifted of the realm —

— provided each of them came forward, registered, and signed.

* * *

They brought the accord to Sebastian on the eighth day, because he had become, without seeking it, the most famous man in the kingdom. The taster who went under the mountain. The lords wanted his signature. The lords needed his signature; a pardon endorsed by the hunted meant more than a pardon decreed by hunters.

They brought it to a small room off the great hall, and laid it before him on a table, and set an inkwell beside it, and left him alone with it except for Wren, who sat on the windowsill with a sparrow on her shoulder.

He read every word. He was, after all, a professional.

It was a good document. Genuinely good — he tasted no poison in it, no hidden clause, no bitter aftertaste of exception or escape-hatch. The drafters had been careful. The pardons were real. The protections were real.

And that was precisely why he would not sign it.

"Ink is just a promise wearing a uniform," he said to Wren, though he was really saying it to the room, to the lords waiting beyond the door, to every law that had ever been written to protect people and later rewritten to hunt them. "I've tasted the crown's ink for ten years. It always starts sweet. Proclamations, oaths, accords — they all taste like honey in the first year and arsenic in the tenth. Not because the writers lie. Because the paper outlives the intent. Some future chancellor, some frightened king, some hungry winter — and this becomes the list. A register of the gifted is a hunting ledger waiting for a hunter."

Wren watched him. "They'll be hurt if you refuse."

"Some of them will be angry. Anger heals." He dipped the quill anyway — and drew, beside the accord, not his name but a single line of text, small and precise, in the margin: *Pardons granted by truth need no signature. Pardons granted by law will need defending. Defend them.*

Then he set the quill down and pushed the document away.

"Tell them the taster declined," he said to the clerk who came to fetch it. "Tell them I said the accord is worth more unsigned — a promise kept outside the law stays honest, because it has to be re-earned every day. Tell them—" He paused, tasting his own words before serving them, an old habit. "Tell them I'll be in the mountains, if the truth ever needs a witness again."

* * *

They left the city on a bright cold morning, the kind of morning that used to mean early winter and now, perhaps, meant only morning.

Odile's grave was on the way north. They had brought her home in the autumn, after; Sebastian had carried the spade himself, because Odile had given him roads when he had nothing else, and you bury your debts with your own hands. The marker was river stone, uncarved. Odile would have mocked anything fancier.

He stood over it a while. He did not apologize — she'd have hated that. He simply ate a piece of the bread he'd baked that dawn, standing there, and let the taste carry what words couldn't: flour and salt and grief and gratitude, all of it true, all of it offered to the ground.

"She'd say we're wasting daylight," Wren said.

"She'd say we're wasting *her* daylight, and charge us for it." Sebastian smiled, and it hurt, and he let it hurt. Then he turned north, toward the Thornfells, where the passes were opening and the villages were filling again and somewhere Odile's cousins were rebuilding a smugglers' road into an honest one, or as honest as anything got.

They walked. The mountains took them back the way mountains do — without ceremony, without forgiveness, without asking what they'd learned. Snowmelt ran loud in the gullies. Wren's birds followed overhead in a loose dark ribbon, singing truths to each other, and Wren laughed at something in the song that Sebastian couldn't hear yet.

But Elira heard it. He felt her stir behind his eyes, felt her attention sharpen like a scent on the wind, and when she spoke — for the first time since the great hall, for the first time since she had chosen mercy and set down the chalice — her voice was quiet and strange and aimed at the peaks ahead.

"Taster," she said. "The court below is broken, but the mountain is older than the court. And something else is waking up in the deep places. Something that was starving before my sister ever sold me. Something that has just lost its only food."

Sebastian stopped on the trail. Below them the valley smoked gently with spring, green and safe and utterly unaware.

"How long?" he asked.

"Longer than a winter," Elira said. "But not longer than a life. Walk well, Sebastian Kavanagh. And keep that tongue of yours sharp. You're going to need to taste something far older than poison."

He looked at Wren. Wren looked at him. Above them, the birds went suddenly, completely silent.

Then, far to the north, past the last white shoulder of the Thornfells, something answered the silence — a sound like a vast throat swallowing, deep under the roots of the world.

Sebastian shouldered his pack, and began to walk faster.
