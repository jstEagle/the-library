## 1. The Door That Should Not Knock

The valley had no weather of its own. That was the first thing every child of the Verdant Guild learned, though it was never said aloud in quite those words. Rain came to the rim of the hills and stopped there, politely, like a guest who had forgotten an invitation. Wind combed the ridgeline and left the orchards untouched. Even the light seemed filtered, poured through cloth, so that nothing inside the ward ever burned or froze or raged. The elders called this *peace*. Lior had spent nineteen years suspecting it was something closer to a held breath.

On the eve of the centennial rite, the whole guild smelled of beeswax and crushed rosemary. Every surface in the great hall had been scrubbed; every banner of green-and-gold had been shaken out and rehung so that the stitched leaves caught the lamplight like a forest remembering fire. Cooks moved through the kitchens with arms full of late apples. Somewhere a choir rehearsed the Hymn of the Sealed Root, the one song permitted in the valley that was not a work-song, and its melody drifted through the open windows and settled over everything like pollen.

Lior carried water. It was what they were good at — carrying things, fetching things, standing at the edge of rooms while others worked the true craft. In the guild's reckoning, Lior Okonkwo had been tested four times for the gift and found wanting four times. Seeds did not quicken under their palm. Cuttings did not lean toward their voice. When they sang the germination verses, the verses simply ended, unanswered, like letters posted into a well. *Talentless*, Elder Sefu had said once, not cruelly, which somehow made it worse. *Useful*, he had added, as one might describe a wheelbarrow.

So: water. Two yokes of buckets from the cistern to the hall, where the ceremonial basins waited to be filled with water that had never known a river's opinion. Lior made the trip without thinking, boots scuffing the packed earth of the lane, shoulders braced against the familiar ache. The evening was going violet at the edges. Moths had begun their slow arithmetic around the lanterns. And then, between the third trip and the fourth, someone knocked on the valley gate.

No one knocked on the valley gate.

The gate stood at the narrow mouth of the valley, iron-banded oak older than any living elder, and it had not opened for outsiders in living memory. Pilgrims came sometimes to the outer wall and were turned away with bread and a blessing. Merchants knew better than to try. The ward hummed along the ridgeline like a struck bowl, and every creature with sense kept its distance from that sound.

But the knock came again — small, arrhythmic, more a drumming of fists than a knuckle's courtesy — and Lior stood in the lane with two full buckets and felt the sound go through them like a crack through ice.

They looked around first. Habit. Shame was also a habit here; Lior had learned to check who was watching before feeling anything too large. But the lane was empty, the cooks were singing, the choir was drowning in its own hymn, and so Lior set down the buckets — carefully, always carefully, even now — and walked toward the gate alone.

* * *

The child was mud to the eyebrows.

That was the first impression, absurdly domestic: a small figure caked in dried river-silt, hair matted into dark horns, bare feet black to the ankle, standing on the threshold stone as if she had grown there overnight like a mushroom after rain. She could not have been more than six or seven. Her clothes had once been fine — Lior could see the ghost of embroidery at the hem, gold thread gone grey with grime — but they hung off her in ribbons, and her ribs showed when she breathed.

She was speaking.

Not crying. Not begging, though her eyes did plenty of that, wide and wet and burning. Speaking — a stream of syllables that rose and fell with the cadence of meaning, urgent, repeated, her small hands opening and closing like birds trying to remember flight. The sounds were unlike anything Lior had ever heard. They were not the trade-tongue of the lowlands, not the clipped liturgy of the guild, not the shepherd's whistles of the hill folk. The consonants came from somewhere behind the teeth and before the throat, soft and ancient, and the vowels opened like doors.

*Hallowen. Hallowen ira. Sennu hallowen—*

"I don't understand," Lior said, uselessly, in the only language they had.

The child stared at them. Then she said something else — slower, deliberate, pressing each word out like a hand pressed to glass — and Lior felt it land somewhere beneath language, in the body's oldest rooms. Not comprehension. Resonance. Like hearing one's own name spoken in a dream.

And then the garden answered.

It began at the gate itself. The old hedge roses that flanked the arch — roses that bloomed, by guild law and guild nature, precisely at midsummer and not one day sooner — shivered. Lior watched, buckets forgotten, as buds that should have slept until the rite swelled and split and opened, all along the hedge, in a wave, like applause traveling down a row. White petals unfurled in the violet dusk. Then the crocuses in the border broke soil they had no business breaking. Then the wisteria over the gatehouse, bare since autumn, budded and burst into hanging purple chandeliers, and the scent hit the air all at once — honey and rain and something greener underneath, something wild — and the moths went mad, and somewhere a nightingale began to sing out of season, badly, joyfully, as if drunk.

Lior had seen magic fail. They had watched it fail their entire life, patiently, personally. They had never seen it *answer*.

"Stop," they whispered, to the garden, to the child, to their own hammering heart. "You can't — the ward doesn't allow—"

The child tilted her head. Mud cracked at her jaw. And Lior saw it then, the thing they would carry for the rest of their life: the flowers were turning toward her. Not drifting, not swaying in some errant breeze the valley never had. Turning. The hedge roses leaned their white faces inward, toward the small mud-caked figure on the threshold, the way sunflowers follow the sun, the way people in a dark room turn toward a voice saying their name. Crocus and wisteria and the pale bells of something Lior had no name for — all of them angled toward her like faces at a window.

She was still talking. *Sennu hallowen. Ira sennu.* Her voice was hoarse, worn down to thread, as if she had been speaking these words for days, for miles, to no one who could hear.

Lior looked back over their shoulder. The lane was still empty. Lamplight spilled warm from the hall windows; the choir had reached the swelling verse about the root that keeps the world asleep. No one had heard the knock. No one had seen the impossible bloom. The valley went on being sealed and peaceful and held-breath quiet, and Lior stood alone at the gate with a feral child and a garden losing its mind, and felt — for the first time in nineteen years — absolutely necessary.

"All right," they said softly, kneeling, bringing their face level with hers. Mud and petals between them. "I don't know your words. But I'm listening. I'm — I'm actually listening."

The child went still. Something in her posture changed, the way a bowstring changes when the arrow is finally loosed. She reached out one filthy hand and touched Lior's cheek — gently, deliberately, the way one tests whether a thing is real — and said a single word.

*Iri.*

Her name, Lior would learn later. But in that moment it sounded like an answer to a question Lior had been asking their whole life without knowing how to phrase it.

* * *

They should have raised the alarm. That was procedure; Lior knew procedure the way they knew water-carrying, thoroughly and thanklessly. An unknown presence at the gate on the eve of the centennial rite was a matter for Elder Sefu, for the wardens, for the sealed council chamber and its maps of the ward-lines. A child who made dead gardens bloom was either a miracle or a breach, and both belonged to the guild, not to a talentless water-carrier.

Instead Lior filled a cup with clean water from their own bucket and held it out, and Iri drank like something that had crossed a desert, and Lior looked at the gate — massive, barred, humming faintly with the ward's contentment — and understood that the knock had not come through the gate at all. There was no gap in the iron. No broken seal. The child was simply *here*, inside the ward, as if the valley had opened a door that did not exist and then closed it behind her, embarrassed.

The flowers were still turning toward her. Lior watched them do it, minute by minute, while the dusk deepened and the choir finished and the lamps were lit along the lanes. Wherever Iri moved — and she moved little, huddled against the gatepost, exhausted, watchful as a wild thing deciding whether a hand meant food or harm — the blooms followed. A crocus near the path rotated its whole stem, slowly, creaking, to keep its face toward her. Lior had tested seeds under their palms four times and been found empty four times, and here was a child made of ordinary mud and hunger whom the garden loved on sight.

What did that make the garden? What did that make the ward? What did that make *her*?

"Lior!" A voice up the lane — one of the hall stewards, harried, holding a list. "There you are. The basins, we're waiting on the basins, Elder Sefu wants the water blessed before moonrise—"

"Coming," Lior called back.

They looked at Iri. Iri looked at them. Around her the hedge roses glowed faintly white in the dark, out of season, out of obedience, faces turned toward her like a congregation toward its saint.

Lior picked up the buckets. Walked three steps. Stopped.

"Elder Sefu," they said, turning back, and heard their own voice come out steadier than they felt. "Someone needs to tell him there's a child at the gate."

The steward frowned. "At the *gate*? Don't be foolish, the ward—"

"I know," Lior said. "I know exactly where the ward is." And they handed over the buckets, and turned, and led the way back down the lane toward the hall — toward the elders, the hymn, the hundred years of sealed roots — with a mud-caked stranger walking close behind them, and every flower in the valley turning to watch her pass.

Behind them, unremarked by anyone but Lior, the last crocus finished opening. At its center, where the pollen should have been gold, it was silver — the exact pale silver of the ward-light on the ridgeline, as if the barrier itself had sent down a piece of itself to see what had knocked.
