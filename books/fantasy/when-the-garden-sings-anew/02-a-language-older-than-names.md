## 2. A Language Older Than Names

The elders did not know what to do with the child, so they did what the guild always did with things it could not name: they sang over her.

Lior stood at the back of the hall that evening, among the buckets and the brooms where a person of no talent belonged, and watched the rite of assessment unfold. The three elders sat beneath the great carved lintel of the Verdant Guild — oak, ancient, its grain worn into shapes like sleeping faces — and Elder Sefu held a tuning bowl of river clay while the other two chanted the Hymn of Rooting. The sound filled the hall the way water fills a cup: patiently, completely, leaving no room for anything else.

It was supposed to work on everything. On seeds, to wake them. On soil, to settle it. On any living thing touched by magic, to draw the touch out into the open where it could be seen, weighed, and if necessary bound again.

On the child it did nothing at all.

She sat on the stone floor in a circle of chalked leaves, small and filthy and utterly unbothered, watching the elders' mouths move with the frank curiosity of someone observing birds at a feeder. When the hymn ended and Sefu struck the tuning bowl — a note like a bell heard through deep water — the silence afterward was so total that Lior could hear their own heart.

Then the child opened her mouth, and spoke back.

Not words. Not the valley tongue, not the trade-speech the caravans brought up from the lowlands twice a year. It was something else: syllables that moved like roots move, slow and branching, each one seeming to arrive from underneath the one before it. The sound went through Lior's chest and settled somewhere behind the sternum, warm as sun-struck soil, familiar in a way that made no sense at all — the way a smell can be familiar from a dream you've never had.

And the hall answered.

Every potted cutting along the walls leaned toward her. The ivy in the high window, which had not grown a finger's length in thirty years, unfurled two new leaves with an audible creak. The cut flowers in the offering bowls — dead for days, brown-stemmed — lifted their heads.

Elder Sefu set down the bowl very carefully, the way one sets down a thing that might go off.

"Take her to the east room," he said. "Post a warden. No one speaks to her."

* * *

Lior did not sleep that night. They lay in their narrow cell above the toolshed — the smallest room in the guild, given without ceremony to the member with nothing to give — and listened to the valley breathe.

That was the thing about the ward that no one talked about: the silence of it. Magic had a sound, or so the oldest texts claimed, a hum beneath all things, and the valley had been built deliberately deaf to it. Lior had lived inside that deafness for twenty-two years, and had always assumed the faint ache at the edge of hearing was just their own blood, just the ordinary noise of being alive. Tonight, though, remembering the child's voice — those syllables arriving from underneath — the ache sounded different. It sounded like a word repeated so long it had worn smooth, like a stone in a stream.

*Something is trying to say something,* Lior thought, *and we built our whole home on top of its mouth.*

They got up before dawn, because talentless members were also the ones who fetched and carried, and the east room needed its morning fire laid. This was true, and also it was a lie Lior told themselves while pulling on their boots in the dark.

* * *

The warden posted outside the child's door was Brammel, old and half-deaf and fond of Lior, who had polished his boots every festival week for a decade. He let them past with a grunt and a warning about not getting bitten.

The child was awake. Of course she was awake; children who had walked out of whatever wilderness lay beyond the ridgeline did not sleep like valley children. She was crouched in the corner by the window, watching the frost on the glass with an expression of intense grievance, and when Lior came in with the kindling she turned and looked at them — really looked, the way the flowers had looked at her, turning like faces — and said something in that under-and-branching speech.

"I don't understand you," Lior said quietly.

Her face fell. And then — this was the part Lior would remember for the rest of their life, though they didn't know it yet — her expression changed to something deliberate. She pointed at herself. Spoke again. Pointed at the frost. Spoke again, slower, breaking the sounds apart like a child showing a stranger how a knot comes undone.

Teaching him.

Lior knelt down on the cold flagstones, forgetting the kindling entirely, and pointed at themself. "Lior."

She tried it. Got it wrong in a way that made the firewood in the basket rustle, which neither of them noticed. Tried again. "Li-or." Then she pointed at herself and produced a short, round sound, a word like a seed between two fingers: "*Iri.*"

"Iri," Lior repeated.

The candle on the table, unlit, guttered anyway — a single curl of smoke rising from a wick that had never known flame — and neither of them saw it, because they were both laughing, softly, at the strangeness of a name passing between two mouths.

* * *

It became a ritual over the following days, hidden inside another one. The east room needed its fire, its water, its swept floor; the guild asked no questions about why the least talented member volunteered for chores no one else wanted. Lior came every morning with bread and a slate, and Iri came to the window-seat, and between them they built a bridge out of garden-words.

Because that was what Lior knew. That was the whole of the guild's teaching, poured year after year into a vessel that would not hold it: the names of plants, the grammar of growth, the rites of sowing and pruning and grafting. Lior had failed every practical examination the guild offered — nothing they sang ever *took*, the elders said, nothing bent toward them — but they had memorized everything. Every word. Twenty-two years of words with nowhere to go.

Iri drank them like rain after drought.

She had words of her own, of course — hundreds of them, thousands, a whole architecture of sound that Lior slowly began to map — but many of them had no referent she could show. She would say a word and point at the sky, at the ground, at her own chest, and grow frustrated when Lior couldn't follow. But the garden-words, the guild-words, those she seized on hungrily. When Lior taught her *blossom* she said it back and every bud on the windowsill's neglected geranium cracked open at once. When Lior taught her *root* she pressed her palm flat to the floorboards and the frost on the underside of the door melted.

"You're doing magic," Lior whispered, half-terrified, the fourth morning.

Iri frowned at the word *magic*, which translated badly, and then said something long and explanatory in her own tongue, gesturing: *no, this is only speaking, speaking properly.*

Lior wrote that down on the slate, and stared at it, and felt the first cold thread of understanding begin to pull through them like a root through clay.

* * *

It was Iri herself who led them to the archives, in a way. On the sixth day she drew on the slate — she drew constantly, spirals and branchings and figures with crowns — and beside a tall figure she wrote, in careful copied letters she must have taken off the guild's signage, a string of sounds rendered in the valley alphabet as best it could hold them: *krrth-nn-vael*. And then she pointed at the crown she'd drawn, and wrapped both arms around herself, and shivered, and said the sentence that Lior never afterward managed to forget:

"*Aldric vel korrith. Korrith vel Aldric.*" — and mimed, with terrible clarity, a hand closing around her own throat.

The king wears something that wears him.

The guild archives occupied the cellar beneath the hall, and were tended by nobody, because nobody in a century had needed them; the guild's knowledge lived in the elders' memories and the hymns, and the books were regarded the way one regards a grandparent's furniture — honored, dusted, unread. Lior had keys to every room in the guild. Talentless members made excellent key-holders. No one watched them.

By lantern-light, in the cold and the smell of vellum and mouse, Lior searched. They searched for the First Tongue, because Iri's language was called that in the margins of the oldest herbals — *the speech before speech, that which living things obey* — and they found what they found in a ledger dated ninety-nine years back, in the founding elder's own hand, in a section marked with the seal of closed matters.

*The binding is complete,* the founder had written. *The Tongue no longer lives in any mouth; it sleeps in the soil, and the ward keeps it dreaming, and keeps the world's magic from waking it. We have made a garden over a grave and called it sanctuary. May the earth forgive us the lie. May it never need forgiving.*

Below that, in different ink, added later, smaller:

*Note: the ward draws its strength from the bound material itself. Should the Tongue stir — should it gather itself toward speech again — the ward will weaken from within. Watch for flowering out of season. Watch for silver pollen. Watch for voices the hymns cannot quiet.*

Lior sat down on the archive floor amid a hundred years of dust, holding a lantern with a steady hand and everything else shaking.

The child upstairs spoke the First Tongue. The ward was failing because of it — or perhaps, some colder thought suggested, the ward had been failing for years, and the child had come *because* of that, drawn along the crack like water finding a seam. Either way: Elder Sefu had ordered her confined. The founders had bound a living language into the dirt. And Lior Okonkwo, born in the valley, useless in every rite, immune to every hymn —

— immune to every hymn.

They thought of Iri saying *root* and pressing her palm to the floor. They thought of the ache at the edge of hearing, wearing smooth, wearing smooth, all these years.

"Watch for voices the hymns cannot quiet," Lior read aloud, and their own voice sounded strange in the dark, like a word in a language they were only beginning to speak.

* * *

Sefu summoned them the next afternoon. Lior expected a rebuke about the firewood, the hours spent in the east room. Instead the old man stood at the hall window looking down the lane toward the gate, his hands folded behind his back, and did not turn around.

"The flowers," he said. "You've seen them turn toward her."

"Yes, Elder."

"And the out-of-season bloom. The crocus with the silver heart." Now he turned, and his eyes were tired in a way Lior had never seen, tired the way a wall is tired that has held back a river longer than it was built to. "I have read the closed ledger, Lior. I have read it more times than you have been alive. So understand me when I tell you what happens next, and do not mistake it for cruelty."

He told them. The east room would be sealed. Warded — truly warded, with rites Lior had never been permitted to learn. The child would be kept fed, kept warm, kept *quiet*, until the centennial rite, when the guild would renew the binding and the ward with it, and whatever fragment of the First Tongue had walked out of the wilds to knock at their gate would be folded back into the soil with all the rest.

"She came here to be safe," Lior said.

"She came here because the door was cracking," Sefu said. "There is a difference, child, between a refuge and a grave, and I am too old to pretend I know which one we built." He looked at Lior a moment too long — a look Lior would turn over and over in the nights to come, searching it for meaning — and then dismissed them with a flick of two fingers. "Say nothing. Tend your fires. Forget the words she taught you, if you can."

Lior bowed. Left. Walked down the corridor with their heart going like a trapped bird.

And that night, long after the wardens changed watch, they climbed through the toolshed window with bread in one pocket, the slate in the other, and a stolen archive book against their ribs — the oldest herbal, the one with *First Tongue* written in its margins — and let themselves into the sealed east room through the coal chute, as the frost crept up the glass outside like a gathering audience.

Iri was waiting by the window, wide awake. She had drawn something new on the slate since evening: a valley, a ridgeline, a wall of light. And beyond the wall, coming closer, a line of small figures — and at their head, unmistakable even in her crude strokes, an old man with a staff, walking with his face turned away from the valley, toward the dark.

She tapped the drawing twice. Said one word in the First Tongue, soft and urgent.

Lior didn't know it yet. But they were going to learn it very soon, and it meant *run*.
