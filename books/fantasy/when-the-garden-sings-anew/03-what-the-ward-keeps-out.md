## 3. What the Ward Keeps Out

The silence has a taste.

Iri knows this the way she knows her own hands — not by being told, but by carrying it. It tastes of copper gone cold, of a spoon left too long in milk, of the space where a tooth used to be. The whole valley tastes like that. Every morning she wakes inside the taste and lies very still on her pallet in the guildhouse attic, listening to the nothing with her whole skin.

Because the nothing is not empty. That is the first thing they do not understand here, the tall ones in green who feed her and measure her words and write them down in books that smell of dust and fear. They think the valley is quiet. But quiet is a sleeping thing, and this is not sleep. This is a held breath. A mouth pressed shut with both hands.

She can feel the seam of it.

It runs along the rim of the world like a scar along the inside of a wrist. When she walks to the well, the seam is there, past the orchard rows, past the low wall, humming the way a struck bell hums after the sound is gone — except here no one ever struck the bell, and still it hums, and still the sound cannot come out. She puts her palm against the air sometimes, when the tall ones are not watching, and the air pushes back. Not cold. Not warm. *Refusing.*

*You are not welcome,* says the air. *Nothing is welcome. Nothing arrives. Nothing leaves.*

But things arrive. She arrived. Mud-legged and name-hungry, she came through the gate on the night of the hundred-year rite, and the garden bloomed for her out of season, and the tall ones looked at the flowers and then at her and then at each other, and their faces did a thing she has learned to read: the thing that means *afraid*.

Good, she thinks, sucking the sweetness from a stolen plum stone. Be afraid. Fear keeps hands honest.

* * *

The boy — Lior, the one whose eyes are always apologizing — brings her words.

Not many. The tall ones took most of them away, locked them in the archive-house with the dust-smell, but Lior smuggles them out in pockets, in cupped palms, the way other children smuggle plums. Lior crouches beside her in the herb yard and says *sun* and points up, and Iri says the true word back — the word that is not a name *for* the sun but the sun's own listening — and when she says it, every leaf in the yard turns its face toward her like small green worshippers, and Lior's breath goes strange and quick.

"Again," Lior whispers. "Please."

Iri likes Lior. Lior is a cracked pot, and cracked pots let the light leak out sideways. All the other tall ones are sealed jars. Their magic-deadness sits on them like wet wool; she can stand next to Elder Sefu and feel nothing at all, nothing but the great flat nothing of the valley itself, doubled. But under Lior's wool there is a thread. One thread, thin as spider silk, gold as honey held up to fire. It hums against her own threads when they sit close. It feels like finding a sibling in a crowd by the shape of their shadow.

Lior does not know about the thread. Iri has decided not to say. Some truths are doors, and you should not open a door until the person behind it has finished becoming whoever is going to walk through.

* * *

At night the tide comes.

It does not come into the valley — nothing comes into the valley, that is the whole point of the valley, the whole wound of it — but it comes to the *edge*, and presses, and pulls back, and presses again. Iri lies on her pallet and feels it against the far side of the seam the way a fish in a jar feels the sea: not near, never near, but *of* it, kin to it, answering it.

Pull. Press. Pull. Press.

Beyond the rim of the dead place, the world is singing. She can hear it even here, muffled, like music through a wall — the long green drone of forests thinking their slow thoughts, the silver chatter of rivers counting their stones, the deep brown patience of mountains that have been saying one word for ten thousand years and are not finished saying it. And threaded through all of it, louder now than it was a moon ago, louder every night, a sound like a voice calling from underwater:

*Come home. Come home. Come home.*

The magic wants her. She understands this without understanding how she knows it. She is made of the old words the way bread is made of grain; somewhere beyond the hills, the oven is open and the smell is reaching her across the whole dark land, and her body leans toward it in her sleep the way the garden leaned toward her on the night she came.

And something else leans toward her too.

This is the part she tells no one, not even Lior. Because when the tide pulls at her, there is a hook in the tide. A hunger shaped like a question. It is vast and patient and it wears a crown, and it has noticed the last unbound piece of the First Tongue moving through the world like a candle flame moving through a house of paper.

*Little tongue,* it says, in the space between press and pull. *Little loose word. Come and be worn.*

She pulls her blanket over her head. Blankets are thin armor, but armor is armor.

* * *

In dreams, she drowns with him.

She is always in water that is not water — thick, golden, slow, like honey or like light poured too densely to move through. It fills her mouth and it tastes of oaths. Old ones. Layered and layered, oath upon oath upon oath, each one laid down by kneeling mouths in marble halls, each one sweet at the moment of speaking and heavy forever after, and all of it pooled here, in this drowned hall, around a man on a throne.

The man is kind. She knows this immediately, the way she knew the garden was alive, the way she knows rain before clouds. His face, above the waterline, is tired and honest and full of a goodness that has been eaten away at the edges like a leaf chewed by invisible mouths. He is trying to lift his hand. He has been trying to lift his hand for years. Perhaps decades. The hand rises an inch and falls.

*Iri,* he tries to say. He does not know her name yet, but he will; names travel strangely in the drowned places. What actually comes out of his mouth is the tide-voice, the hooked one, the vast patient hunger: *Come home. Come and be worn.* And his eyes while the voice speaks are the worst thing she has ever seen, because they are awake. He is in there. He is watching his own mouth say someone else's words, and he cannot stop it, and he is sorry.

On his head sits the crown.

It is not gold. Gold would be kinder. It is made of the oaths themselves, compressed and gleaming, a ring of every promise ever sworn to the kings of that far country, and it has grown roots. She can see them in the dream, thin and pale as the threads in Lior, sinking down through the drowned king's skull, down through his throat, wrapping his heart the way ivy wraps a statue — gently, gently, the way everything terrible is gentle, because it means to keep him, not to hurt him. Keeping is the hurting.

*Wear me,* the crown says, with the king's mouth. *Wear me. Wear me.*

And beneath the water, beneath the oaths, so deep she almost misses it, the king's true voice — small, cracked, human, a thread of spider-silk gold exactly like the thread in Lior — says the only word he has left:

*Help.*

* * *

She wakes gasping, tasting copper and honey, and the attic is dark and the seam of the world is humming and the tide is pressing, pressing, pressing, harder tonight than any night before. Hard enough that the floorboards shiver. Hard enough that somewhere below her, in the archive-house, a shelf of locked books rattles softly on its shelf, though there is no wind.

She creeps to the window. Below, the ancient garden stands silver in the starlight, every bloom turned outward — not toward her, for once, but toward the rim of the valley, toward the seam, faces lifted like flowers facing a sun that is coming from the wrong direction. Listening. Waiting.

And past the wall, past the orchard, at the very edge of the dead place, the air is *thinning*. She can see it the way she sees wind in wheat: a shimmer, a wavering, a place where the refusal is growing tired. The held breath of the valley is almost spent. Whatever was done here a hundred years ago — whatever crime was pressed into the soil to make this silence — is coming undone, and the tide knows it, and the hooked hungry crowned thing beyond the hills knows it, and it is pulling now not gently but *urgently*, reeling in its little loose word, its last uncorrupted thread, its key.

*Come home,* says the tide. *Come and be worn.*

"No," Iri whispers to the window glass, and the frost on the pane blooms instantly into a flower, out of season, out of place, beautiful and damning.

Behind her, on the stairs, a floorboard creaks.

She turns. Lior stands in the doorway in a nightshirt, holding a candle, and Lior's face is white, and Lior's eyes are fixed not on her but on the frost-flower — and then, slowly, terribly, on the shimmering seam beyond the wall, visible now even to magic-dead eyes, flickering like a lamp about to go out.

"Iri," Lior whispers. "What's happening to the ward?"

And Iri, who trusts almost no one, looks at the cracked pot with the leaking light, and decides — a moon early, a lifetime early — to open the door.

"The crown is hungry," she says. "And it has found us."
