## 4. The Guild's Quiet Lie

The night after Elder Sefu ordered Iri confined to the east cell, Lior could not sleep.

They lay in their narrow cot beneath the eaves of the archivists' wing, listening to the valley's famous silence. It was the silence the guild taught its children to love — the hush of a place where magic could not follow, where no spell could twist a root or sour a well or whisper through a sleeping mind. Safe, the elders called it. Blessed. The one quiet room in a shouting world.

But tonight the silence felt less like a blanket and more like a hand pressed over a mouth.

Lior rose, dressed in the dark, and let themselves out into the corridor. The stone underfoot was cool and familiar, worn smooth by a hundred years of guild feet. They knew every turn of it the way they knew the backs of their own hands — and that, they thought bitterly, was the whole of their inheritance. A hundred years of the Verdant Guild, and Lior Okonkwo, nineteen years old and talentless, knew only where the floors were worn.

The archive was not locked. It never needed to be. Who would steal from a library of dead languages and older ledgers? Who would even want to? Lior had spent more hours among its shelves than any living member, cataloguing, copying, dusting — the work given to those who could do nothing else. They had read, over the years, nearly everything the guild possessed.

Nearly.

There was one cabinet in the deep stacks that Lior had never been permitted to touch. Elder Sefu kept the key on a cord around his neck, and when Lior had asked about it once, years ago, he had smiled his patient smile and said, *Ledgers, child. Taxes and seed counts. Nothing that would interest you.*

Tonight, Lior intended to find out whether that was true.

* * *

The cabinet's lock was old iron, older than anything else in the guild's keeping, and it yielded not to force but to patience. Lior had spent their childhood watching the gardeners coax stubborn things open — a bud, a seed casing, a knot in a root — and they had learned that nothing truly living resists forever. The lock was not living. But it had been made by hands, and hands leave habits. Lior tried the shapes of every key they had ever seen Sefu carry, and on the eleventh attempt — a long, plain key with a collar of green bronze, the kind used on the granary doors — the mechanism sighed and gave.

Inside were not ledgers.

Inside were nine books, bound in bark that had never died, still faintly warm to the touch, and a scroll wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath them all, a flat stone tablet no bigger than a hearth-shelf, its surface carved with letters that seemed to move when Lior was not looking directly at them.

Lior knew those letters.

They had seen them only once before — three nights ago, tumbling out of a mud-caked child's mouth in shapes no one in the valley could read, while the garden bloomed out of season in answer. The First Tongue. The language that had once commanded living things, the language the guild's oldest texts dismissed in a single dry sentence: *a dead tongue, mercifully, which no living mouth recalls.*

A living mouth had recalled it at the valley gate. And the letters on this tablet were not a record of that tongue.

They were a contract.

Lior carried the tablet to the reading desk and lit a lamp with hands that would not quite steady. The carving was deep, deliberate, the work of someone who had wanted it to last. Around the edges ran the First Tongue's script, dense and interlocking like root-work. But the center had been translated, carved in the guild's own hand, in the guild's own tongue, and Lior read it standing up because they did not trust their legs.

*Let it be set down, in the hundredth year of the wandering, that the Verdant Guild, having taken the Tongue of the First Garden, did bind it into the soil of this valley, that it might speak no more. What was free we made root. What was voice we made stone. And the land, thus silenced, shall keep our secret as it keeps our seed: beneath the earth, and growing nothing.*

Lior read it again. And again. The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the walls, a beam settled with a sound like a held breath released.

*What was voice we made stone.*

The ward — the great, blessed, beloved ward that kept magic out of the valley, that the guild maintained with rites and vigils and a hundred years of solemn gratitude — was not a shield. It had never been a shield. It was a knot. A gag. The guild had not built a wall against magic; they had taken the living voice of the world, the First Tongue itself, and driven it down into the valley's soil like a stake through a heart, and everything the guild called protection was only the sound of that burial holding.

Lior set the tablet down very gently, the way one sets down something sleeping.

The scroll beneath it told the rest. It was a founding record, in a firmer, more official hand, and it did not flinch from what it described. The guild had not fled a magical world to find sanctuary, as the children were taught. The guild had *taken* — from a dying king's court, from a war the scroll named only in euphemism — the last unbound fragment of the First Tongue, carried in a vessel of living root, and had brought it here, to the one place its power could be pinned. They had sung it into the ground in a rite that took nine days and cost the lives of four founders. And the valley had gone quiet, and the world outside had gone on shouting, and the guild had called the quiet peace.

The last page of the scroll was different. Newer. The ink was younger than Lior's grandmother.

*The binding holds, but the bound is not dead. It dreams in the soil. And a dreaming thing, given a hundred years, will dream itself a body. Watch for a child born of the valley who does not wither. The thread may have woven itself a hand.*

Lior's own hands were on the desk. They looked at them for a long time.

They had been born in the valley, in the guild's own infirmary, to a mother who had died of a fever no one could name and a father no record mentioned. They had been tested at seven, at nine, at twelve, and had failed every trial — no green-sense, no root-calling, no whisper of the old craft. *Talentless*, the elders said, with pity. *The valley keeps what it keeps*, Sefu said, with a hand on their shoulder that was meant to be kind.

But the guild's rites were all songs. Lior had never been able to make anything grow — and yet they had always, always been able to *sing*. They knew the ritual grammar of the guild the way other children knew their own names. They had thought it was compensation. A talentless child, memorizing the music of a magic they could never touch.

Or — a thread of the bound thing, dreaming in the soil for a hundred years, learning the shape of the songs that held it down. Learning them by heart. Learning to wear them.

Lior thought of the flowers turning toward Iri like faces. Thought of the way the garden had bloomed out of season, and the way the ward had been failing — the elders had said so themselves, in the closed council Lior had not been meant to overhear. *The ward thins. The ward frays. Something pulls at it from beyond the valley, something with a crown.*

*Something with a crown.*

The scroll had named a dying king's court. The child at the gate had babbled of a king *wearing something that wears him*. The ward was failing because what it held was not a dead tongue at all, but a living one, and living things strain toward their kin — and somewhere beyond the valley, a crown made of bound oaths was pulling at the buried voice of the world like tide pulling at a shore.

And the guild's answer was to lock the child in the east cell.

Lior stood in the archive until the lamp burned low, and the betrayal did not arrive all at once. It came the way rot comes to a tree — from the inside, quietly, along lines that had always been there. It was in Sefu's patient smile. In the *ledgers, child*. In every rite of gratitude the guild had sung over the silent soil. In the pity they had swallowed their whole life, which had never been pity for a talentless child at all — it had been the guild's own guilt, looking for somewhere safe to stand.

They had been raised by the people who had done this. Fed by them. Taught by them. Loved by them, perhaps — Lior could not yet decide whether love and this could live in the same house. Sefu had carried the key against his chest for years, over his heart, and had told Lior it was taxes and seed counts. Perhaps he had told himself the same. Perhaps that was the most terrible possibility of all: that the guild had buried the truth so deep in its own soil that it grew into something that looked, from the inside, exactly like goodness.

Lior wrapped the tablet, the scroll, and two of the bark-bound books in the oilcloth and carried them back to the cabinet. They hesitated over the lock, then left it open. A closed secret was a buried one. Lior was done burying things.

In the corridor outside, the valley's silence pressed in around them, and for the first time Lior heard it rightly: not peace, but a held note. A hundred years of a single sustained sound, sung by the soil, by the bound and dreaming thing beneath it — and Lior's own bones hummed faintly along with it, the way a string hums when its note is played in the next room.

They were not talentless. They were *made of the note*.

Lior walked the dark paths to the east cell, moving between the moon-shadows the way they had done a thousand times on a thousand small disobediences, and stood a while outside Iri's door, listening. The child was singing inside. Softly, sleepily, in the First Tongue — and beneath the floor, beneath the foundations, beneath a hundred years of stone and silence, Lior felt something answer. Felt the valley itself lean toward that small voice like a plant toward light.

The ward was failing. The guild would blame the child, and then they would blame the king's distant crown, and they would never — not once, not in a hundred years of solemn rites — blame themselves.

Lior pressed their palm flat against the cell door, and the wood was warm, and through it they made a promise in the guild's oldest ritual grammar, the grammar written into their bones: *I will not help you keep her buried. I will not help you keep any of it buried.*

Tomorrow, the elders would meet to decide what was to be done with the child who spoke the dead tongue. Lior had heard Sefu give the order for her confinement, and had seen the look that came with it — not cruelty, but fear wearing duty's face — and fear, Lior now understood, was the one crop the guild had always grown best.

Tomorrow they would decide. Which meant tomorrow was the last day in which deciding could still be done quietly.

Lior turned from the door and went to pack what little they owned, and the valley's held note sang on beneath their feet, and for the first time in their life, they hummed back.
