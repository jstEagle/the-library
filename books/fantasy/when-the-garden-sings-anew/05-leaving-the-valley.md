## 5. Leaving the Valley

The moon did not rise that night, and Lior took it as an omen in their favor.

They had packed by feel: a knife with a chipped handle, a coil of guild-cord braided from river reed, three days of bread gone hard as roof tile, and the one thing they had never been permitted to carry beyond the garden wall — a small clay vial of valley soil, taken years ago in secret, when they were nine and already learning what it meant to want something the elders would not name. The soil was warm even now, warm as a sleeping animal. Lior had always told themselves it was imagination.

It was not imagination. Nothing about this night was going to be imagination ever again.

Iri waited where Lior had left her at dusk, curled in the hollow beneath the old willow at the ward line's inner edge — the tree every novice learned first, because the willow stood closest to the boundary and yet had never once grown toward it. She had wrapped herself in Lior's spare cloak and looked, in the dark, like nothing so much as a bundle of sticks that had decided to be a child. But her eyes were open. Her eyes were always open now. And she was listening.

She had been listening all day, Lior realized. Listening to something on the far side of the gate.

*Iri.* Lior crouched, keeping their voice below the wind's register, though there was hardly any wind. *Can you hear me? We're going. Now. Before the elders—*

The child's hand shot out and pressed flat against Lior's mouth. Her palm smelled of crushed stems and cold ash. Then she pointed — not at the gate, but past it, up the slope of the eastern ridge, where the valley's rim rose black against the starless sky.

Lior listened.

At first there was nothing, which in the valley was normal; the whole place held its breath the way a held note holds itself, and you stopped hearing it by your second year. But tonight the silence had a texture. Tonight the silence had *edges*. Somewhere above them, moving along the ridge path with the discipline of people who had done this for decades, were boots. Many boots. No lanterns. No voices.

Sefu had not waited for tomorrow either.

*Hunters,* Iri said — not in words Lior knew, but in a pressure against the ear, a shape made of sound that meant *those who follow*. She had been teaching Lior her language in fragments, two words a night, stolen between chores and confinement checks, and Lior understood suddenly why the elders feared her speech: because once you had heard a word shaped like *hunters*, you could not unhear it. It lodged under the ribs like a splinter.

*How many?*

She held up fingers. Six. Then, after a moment, folded one down. Five. One of them, she indicated by touching her own chest, carried grief in his step.

Lior filed that away — five hunters, one grieving — and found, to their surprise, that their hands were steady. All their life they had waited for a task worthy of them, and here it finally was, arriving dressed in terror, and it turned out that terror and purpose weighed almost exactly the same in the palms.

*The stream way,* Lior signed, using the guild's old hunting signs, which Iri had learned in a single afternoon while the novices needed a week. *The ward is thinnest where the water crosses it. It always has been. Nobody remembers why.*

Nobody remembered why, because nobody had been allowed to ask.

They went down through the terraced gardens, past the sleep-houses dark and breathing, past the drying sheds where bundles of feverfew hung like hanged men. Lior moved the way the archives said the founders had moved — low, weight on the outer edge of the foot, breath drawn only when moving — and marveled at how well their body knew things their teachers had never bothered to teach. Perhaps the guild had trained them after all. Perhaps everything Lior had ever been dismissed for was training, mislabeled.

At the third terrace, Iri stopped dead and grabbed Lior's sleeve.

Ahead, across the seed-beds, a lantern bobbed. An elder — no, a novice, young Perrin, doing rounds he was far too earnest to fake his way out of. He would raise the alarm. He would—

Perrin saw them. Froze. Looked at Iri. Looked at Lior's pack. Looked back at the path behind him, where the hunters would come down within minutes.

Then he stepped aside, into the shadow of the shed, and very deliberately turned his lantern down until it was barely an ember.

He didn't say a word. Neither did Lior. Some disobediences are safer unwitnessed even by their accomplices, and some kindnesses can only exist in silence or not at all. Lior bowed — a full novice's bow, the kind given to masters — and Perrin flushed crimson in the dark, and that was all.

* * *

The stream ran shallow and quick over its stones, loud enough to cover footsteps, and Lior led Iri along it upstream toward the notch in the eastern wall where the water left the valley. The closer they came to the boundary, the stranger the night grew. The valley's held note — that constant subterranean hum every member felt in their teeth and stopped noticing — began, for the first time in Lior's life, to *change pitch*. It deepened. It slowed. It sounded, absurdly, reluctant.

Or perhaps Lior was projecting. Or perhaps the valley itself had moods, and everyone who lived inside it had spent a hundred years pretending otherwise.

Iri had gone rigid. She walked with her head tilted back, mouth slightly open, drinking air the way other children drank water.

*What is it?* Lior signed.

Her answer came not in sign but in shiver. She pointed at the stream, then at herself, then pressed both hands over her heart — and Lior, translating through two days of fragments, got it: *it calls me. The outside. It pulls like tide.*

*And you want to go?*

Iri looked at them then with an expression no child should have had cause to wear — the expression of someone being asked whether they wanted to stop drowning. She nodded once.

They reached the notch as the true darkness before dawn settled in. Here the valley walls pinched together, and the stream slipped through a gap barely wide enough for a cart, and across the gap — invisible, but present to every sense Lior possessed — lay the ward line.

Every guildchild learned the ward's location before they learned letters: a seam running the circuit of the valley, older than the guild's oldest record, humming faintly if you pressed an ear to certain stones. Beyond it, magic could not follow. That was the lesson. That was the whole of it, recited for a century: *the ward keeps the wildness out, and keeps us safe within.*

Standing before it now, Lior felt the lie in their body before their mind caught up. The ward did not hum outward. It hummed *inward*, a great slow inhale, drawing everything loose and living toward the center of the valley — drawing it down into the soil, into the roots, into the deep archive vaults where the elders went alone with lamps and returned without explanations.

The ward was not a wall. It was a *mouth*.

And Lior had been born inside it.

There was no time to be sick about it. Boots on the ridge, now audible even over the stream — descending, spreading out, fanning the way Sefu taught his hunters to fan. Five points of disciplined silence converging on the notch, because of course Sefu had guessed the stream too; Sefu had taught Lior everything Lior knew about leaving places.

*Iri.* Lior knelt at the waterline. *When we cross, everything changes. The sounds out there have weight. Real weight. Words do things out there. So we speak carefully. We speak little. Do you understand?*

She understood better than they did, clearly; she was already pressing her hands flat against the wet stone of the threshold, reading the boundary the way a blind woman reads a face. What she read made her go pale.

But she took Lior's hand.

Together they stepped through the gap in the rock, through the stream's cold knee-deep rush, through the line where the valley ended and the world resumed.

* * *

Crossing the ward was like surfacing from a lifetime underwater.

Sound arrived first — real sound, weighted sound, sound with *consequences*. Every drip of the stream off every stone rang out distinct and deliberate, each droplet a small bell announcing itself. Lior's own heartbeat became audible, became insistent, became almost rude. And beneath it all, threading through everything, ran a vast low music that had been inaudible from inside the valley: the sound of the land itself, singing to itself, the way a sleeper murmurs.

Iri burst into tears.

Not fear-tears. Recognition-tears. She stood in the shallows with her arms flung wide and her face tipped to the sky and wept as if greeting a mother thought dead, and the trees on the far bank — Lior saw it plainly, refused to doubt it — leaned toward her. A few degrees, no more. Trees do not move quickly. But they moved.

And then Lior felt what else the crossing had done.

It began as warmth in the sternum. Spread outward along the arms, down through the hands, pooling in the fingertips like light gathering at the bottom of a well. When Lior lifted their hands, they *glowed* — faintly, fitfully, a green-gold shimmer rising off the skin like heat-haze, pulsing in time with that vast buried music. Where the light touched the stream, moss bloomed in a streak. Where it dripped onto a stone, lichen unfurled in seconds, soft as green fire.

This was what the ward had hidden. Not kept out — *hidden*. This was what the elders had bound into the soil a hundred years ago, and what had somehow, impossibly, ended up woven into the least gifted novice the Verdant Guild had ever produced.

*Iri,* Lior whispered, and their voice came out carrying leaves. Actual leaves, small and new, spiraling from their lips and dissolving midair. *Iri, look at me. Is this— am I—*

The child looked at them with an expression of perfect, uncomplicated wonder, and signed something slow and careful, a phrase she had never used before:

*You are the last thread.*

Behind them, torchlight broke over the ridge. Five flames, five silhouettes, and one tall figure standing apart from the rest with his hands clasped behind his back — Sefu, who had come himself, which meant the elders believed this was not a punishment to be delegated.

Sefu's voice crossed the water, level and terribly gentle.

*Lior Okonkwo. Come back inside the line, child. Whatever you think you've found out there, it will eat you alive.*

Lior looked down at their glowing hands. At the moss spreading green and glad around their feet. At Iri, who had stopped crying and was watching the hunters with the stillness of a deer deciding.

*No,* Lior said — and their single word set every willow catkin on the far bank trembling, and made Sefu take one involuntary step backward, as though the valley itself had spoken through a mouth it had finally finished making.

*No,* Lior said again, louder, and the word went rolling up the slope like weather.

Then they took Iri's hand, turned north, and ran — out of the valley's long held note, into a world where every syllable had edges, with the torches behind them and the whole singing dark ahead.

Behind them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the ward line *cracking* — a single high note, like ice giving way on a spring pond.

Sefu's shout followed them into the trees: *It's failing! The ward is failing!*

Lior ran faster.
