## 7. The Court of the Hollow King

The capital announced itself long before we saw it — not with walls or spires, but with a sound. A low, ceaseless thrumming in the air, like a hive the size of the sky, and beneath it something thinner and more terrible: voices. Thousands of them, layered, braided, all speaking at once in a rhythm too regular to be natural.

Iri stopped dead on the road and pressed her small hands over her ears. "It hurts," she said in the First Tongue, and then, because she had been learning mine, "it *hurts*."

"I know," I said. I could feel it too, now that we were close enough. Every word spoken in that city seemed to sink into the ground instead of fading, and the ground was full — stuffed like a granary past bursting. Oaths, I realized. Vows sworn on knees in coronation halls and marriage beds and courtroom floors, promises made by farmers to their fields and soldiers to their kings, all of it pooling, century upon century, into one vast reservoir beneath the flagstones. The guild's archives had taught me what bound magic looked like from the outside. I had never imagined it could look like *this* — a city drinking its own words.

"They're all still talking," Iri whispered. "Nobody stops. Nobody lets go."

* * *

We came in with the carts, under sacking, among turnips and barrels of salt fish, because the gates were not merely guarded but *listened to*. I felt it as we passed beneath the arch: a pressure against my skin, a great ear pressed to the world, searching for something. Searching for her.

Iri held perfectly still against my chest. She had learned that trick from the garden back home — how to be green and quiet and unremarkable, how to be *soil*. It nearly broke my heart, watching a child who spoke the oldest language in the world pretend to be dirt so that no one would hear her breathe.

The pressure slid over us like water over a stone. Then we were through, and the cart jolted on, and I let myself breathe again.

The courtier found us before we found him. He peeled back the sacking at dusk, when the cart halted in a courtyard smelling of hay and horse and old stone, and he looked at us with the weary, precise eyes of a man who has been counting the days until something arrived.

"Lior Okonkwo," he said. "And the little queen." He said it without ceremony, the way one states a fact about weather. "I am Master Corvane. Second Secretary to the Office of Petitions. Which is to say, I read the letters no one else will touch."

"How did you know—"

"A bridge sang to you," he said. "A river took your bargain. A village lit candles against an omen." He almost smiled. "Word travels, when the land starts remembering. Come. Quickly. The palace listens hardest after dark."

* * *

Corvane moved through his own palace like a man walking through a house where someone has died — familiar with every room, flinching at every echo. He wore the grey livery of a minor office, deliberately forgettable, but I noticed how the servants bowed to him a half-beat too deep, how guards looked away as we passed, as though each of them had decided, privately and silently, that they had seen nothing. There were more of them than I would have guessed. More people who had chosen not to see.

"The king holds audience at moonrise," he murmured, guiding us down a stair that smelled of candle smoke and dust. "You should understand what you'll see. Everyone speaks of the crown as if it were jewelry. It is not jewelry. Do you know what an oath is, Guildsman?"

"Words given weight," I said. "Given somewhere to live."

"Then understand this: every vow ever sworn to the crown of Aldric lives in that crown. Every kneeling. Every 'so help me.' Eleven generations of them, packed into gold and iron until there was no room left for anything else. And things that are packed too tight—" he paused at a door, hand on the iron ring, "—begin to want out. Or want *more*."

"And the king?"

Corvane's face did something complicated. Grief wearing the mask of efficiency.

"The king is lucid," he said. "That is the cruelty of it. Some mornings he wakes entirely himself, and he writes letters — true ones, honest ones — that never leave his desk, because by afternoon his mouth belongs to something else. He knows. That's the worst part, Secretary's honor. He *knows*, and he cannot stop smiling when it wears him, because the thing inside the crown has learned that a smiling king commands more love than a weeping one."

He opened the door onto darkness and cold air and the smell of roses gone slightly wrong — sweet, but underneath, something like copper.

"You'll have perhaps ten minutes of him," Corvane said. "The lucid minutes come at moonrise, when the oaths are quietest. Even a parasite must sleep." His voice dropped. "There are others who will help you. Remember their faces. They chose the *man* over the crown. It is a dangerous thing to choose, here."

* * *

The royal bedchamber had once been beautiful. I could see its bones — carved rafters, a window shaped like a blooming flower, tapestries faded to ghosts of gardens. But everything in it had been arranged around the bed the way a body arranges itself around a wound.

King Aldric lay very still, hands folded on his chest, and at first glance he seemed already dead. Then his eyes opened, and they were — I understood in one lurching moment why Iri had described her dream the way she had — *drowning*. Clear brown eyes far below the surface of a face, looking up through fathoms of something dark and patient.

"Guildsman," he said. His voice was hoarse, rusted from disuse. Not the voice that had been addressing courts all day; that voice had been smooth as poured honey. This one was just a tired man's. "Sefu sent you? No — no, Sefu would sooner send hunters. You came anyway. Good. Good boy. Or girl. Forgive me. It's hard to see anything clearly except what's *inside* me."

Iri wriggled free of my arms and stood at the bedside, unafraid, and said something soft in the First Tongue.

The king wept. Simply, terribly, like a man hearing his mother's voice across forty years.

"Yes," he whispered. "Yes. That's my name. I haven't heard it in — say it again, child."

She said it again. And then, because children do not perform mercy, she climbed onto the bed and put her small hand flat against his chest, right over the place where his heart worked, and listened the way she listened to the garden.

"It's loud in there," she told me gravely. "Like the city. Like everyone who ever promised him anything is still in there, shouting."

"That's exactly what it is," the king said. His eyes found mine, and there was a desperate clarity in them, a man clinging to a ledge. "Listen to me quickly, while the moon is high. It grows stronger every year. It eats my days whole now — weeks pass and I surface like a drowning man, and the world has changed shape without me. It rules through my mouth. It signs my decrees. Last winter—" his breath caught, "—last winter it ordered the burning of the Thornwood, and I watched my own hands give the command. The trees screamed for three days. Could you hear them, child?"

Iri nodded solemnly. "They're still screaming. Far away. Getting quieter."

The king made a sound that was not quite a laugh. "Then hurry. Listen — the crown cannot be cut off, melted, stolen. We tried all three, years ago, before it grew wise. It is made of oaths, and oaths can only be answered. Unbound. Spoken *back* to their givers. But no one alive speaks the language they were first sworn in. No one except—"

He stopped. Looked at Iri. Then at me, and his drowned eyes went wide.

"Except whatever you two are," he finished softly. "Oh. Oh, the land sent you. The land *remembered*."

* * *

The moon was sinking when the change came. I saw it happen — saw the exact moment the king left his own face. It began at the mouth, a smile assembling itself piece by piece, muscle by muscle, like something being built rather than felt. Then the drowning eyes surfaced into something bright and shallow and utterly without depth, and the tired hands unfolded and became regal, and the voice that came out was warm as hearthsmoke and wrong as a mask.

"Well," said the crown, through the king's mouth, pleasantly. "Visitors. How *wonderful*."

Every oath in the palace woke at once. I felt it through the floor — the vast buried chorus beneath the city turning over in its sleep, listening. Iri pressed against me, trembling, and I gathered her up, and Corvane materialized from the shadows with the smoothness of long practice.

"Your Majesty's guests were leaving," he said blandly. "Petitioners. Nothing."

"Of course, of course." The smile widened. The king's face around it was slick with sweat, and I knew — I *knew* — some part of him behind it was screaming. "But do bring the little songbird back soon. She has such a lovely voice. And yours—" the hollow eyes fixed on me, and something ancient and hungry looked out through eleven generations of stolen authority, "—yours I recognize, gardener. You carry a thread of the old binding in your bones. How careless of your elders, to let it walk out of the valley."

"We'll be going," I said, and my voice only shook a little.

"Oh, you'll be staying," said the crown, cheerful as a bell. "Everything that comes to the capital stays, eventually. Everything kneels, eventually. Ask anyone here — they'll tell you it's an *honor*."

Corvane got us out. Through servants' passages, past guards who suddenly and conveniently needed to be elsewhere, down into cellars that smelled of the earth itself, real earth, unbound. Only when we were three streets away and breathing did he speak, and his voice was ash.

"Now you've seen it. Loyalty, worn as a skin. Every person in that court who bows is feeding it — not because they're wicked, but because they *love* their king, and the thing wears their love like armor." He looked at me, and beneath the secretary's composure I saw a man who had spent years carrying this alone. "So. Gardener. You've met the parasite and you've met the man. Tell me you have a plan that ends with the man still breathing."

I thought of the valley ward, straining. Of the soil that held the First Tongue like a held breath. Of Lior Okonkwo, born of a binding, taught to be nothing, standing in a cellar that smelled of freedom.

"I need every loyal soul in this palace in one room," I said. "Tomorrow night. And Corvane — I need them ready to take back what they swore."

His face went pale. "That's treason."

"No," I said, thinking of a drowning king, of a screaming wood, of a child pretending to be dirt. "Treason is what the crown's been doing for eleven generations. What we're about to do is *gardening*."

Behind us, far above, through miles of stone and oath-soaked air, I heard the palace bells begin to ring — and beneath them, faint but unmistakable, the sound of the crown laughing with the king's mouth.
