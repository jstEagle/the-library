## 8. Sefu at the Gate

The city smells of iron pretending to be bread.

Iri knows this because she has learned to know things now — Lior's doing, Lior's patient hands shaping words out of the air for her like a woman making bread from fog. *Wall*, Lior says, and touches stone. *Bell*, and the bronze above them hums its one round note. Iri takes each word and swallows it whole, and inside her chest the swallowed words glow like coals wrapped in cloth.

But iron is iron in every tongue, and the city is full of it, and it sings wrong. It sings of oaths said too often by people who did not mean them, oaths stacked like stones until they became walls, walls until they became a crown. She can feel the crown from here. It sits on the far side of the city like a second sun that gives no light, only weight. At night it presses against her sleep and she dreams again of the king drowning, his mouth full of gold water, his hands open and empty, reaching up through the shine toward someone who has not come yet.

*Me*, she thinks sometimes. He is reaching for me. The thought does not frighten her as much as it should. Nothing frightens her as much as it should; Lior says this is because she was made from something older than fear. Iri thinks it is because fear needs a home, and she has never had one.

She stands at the high window of the courtier's house — Maravel, the woman with kind eyes and a sword under her kindness — watching the road that climbs toward the capital through country gone strange with returning magic. The hedges have begun to move when no wind moves them. A well two streets down has started singing in a voice like an old woman remembering.

And on that road, three days' ride out but closing, come seven riders whose shapes she knows better than her own name.

She feels them before she sees them: the guild-hunters, wrapped tight in silence-wards, their horses shod with dead iron so the ground cannot gossip about their passing. But Iri is not ground. Iri is the thing the valley could not hold, the last uncorrupted piece of a king, and their silence slides off her like rain off feathers.

*Sefu.*

The elder's name arrives in her mind not as a word but as a sensation — dry paper, cold tea, a hand held just above flame. Sefu, who never struck her. Sefu, who locked her in a room with no garden in it. Sefu, who looked at her across the ward-line the night they fled and did not shout, which was somehow worse than shouting, because the not-shouting had meant *I will find you, child. I have all the patience of stone.*

Iri goes to find Lior.

* * *

Lior is in the lower room learning court manners from Maravel, which mostly consists of Lior bowing wrong on purpose and Maravel laughing in a way that makes the candles lean toward her. They stop laughing when Iri comes in. They always know. Lior says Iri walks like weather changing.

"Riders," Iri says. Her new words are still small coins, spent carefully. "Seven. Coming fast. The old one first." She puts her hand flat against her own breastbone, where Sefu's presence sits like a swallowed pebble. "Here."

Lior's face does the thing faces do — a quick collapse and rebuild, like a wall deciding whether to be a wall. "Sefu." They look at Maravel. "How long?"

"A day. Less, if they push." Maravel is already moving, pulling a map from beneath a false bottom in her writing desk. "The gate guards will admit guild riders without question — the Verdant Guild still holds charter rights from before Aldric's grandfather. That's how they'll come in. Not as hunters. As *guests*."

"Guests," Lior repeats, and there is a bitter taste in how they say it. "We were guests too."

Iri touches Lior's sleeve. When she speaks she uses the First Tongue, because some things are too large for small coins. *They will ask you to give me back. And you will want to give me back, because you love your guild even though it lied to you, even though it made you from a crime. Love is not wise. I have watched the trees love the sun right down into winter.*

"I won't," Lior says — aloud, in the small language, as if answering her thought. Perhaps she spoke it anyway. Sometimes her words leak out of her without permission, like sap through bark. "Whatever they offer. Whatever they promise. Iri—" They crouch to her height, which they do not do often anymore, because Maravel told them a companion of the crown's guest must stand straight, and Lior obeys rules the way rivers obey valleys, reluctantly and only while it suits them. "Listen to me. What Sefu offers will be true. That's what makes it dangerous. Safety can be true and still be a cage. You taught me that. Or — the valley taught us both."

Iri studies them. Under the candle-smell and the iron-smell, Lior smells of the valley soil still, faintly, the way a bird carries its nest in the oil of its feathers. But underneath that, growing stronger every day since the ward line, is another smell: green lightning. Wild thread. The thing Lior is made of, waking up.

*I am not afraid of cages,* she signs-and-says together, hands and mouth working at once. *I am afraid of being put back where the world cannot hear me. In the valley I was a bell with no clapper. Here—* She spreads her fingers against her own throat. *Here I ring.*

* * *

They come at dusk, when the city gates are crowded and the light makes everyone honest-looking.

Iri watches from the courtyard wall as the seven riders pass beneath the portcullis, and the sound their hooves make on the cobbles is the sound of the valley floor — that dead-packed silence wearing horseshoes. The six hunters are grey and identical as fence posts. The seventh dismounts slowly, and it is Sefu, and Sefu is smaller than Iri remembered, or the world has grown larger around her.

Age has gotten into the elder's face like frost into a windowpane. But the eyes are the same: patient as sediment, and just as impossible to argue with, because sediment does not argue. Sediment waits.

Maravel meets them at the door with charters and courtesies. Lior stands beside her, spine straight, hands very still. Iri stays on the wall, half-hidden behind a gargoyle whose face has eroded into something almost gentle, and she listens with everything she has — ears, skin, the coal-glowing words in her chest.

"Elder Sefu of the Verdant Guild," Maravel says, "the court welcomes you, though I confess we didn't expect—"

"You expected exactly this." Sefu's voice carries the way dry things carry fire. "You have the child. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise. I can feel her from here. All of the old blood can." A pause, and then, softer, in the tone elders use when they want to seem like grandmothers instead of jailers: "Lior. Child. Look how you've grown."

Liot does not flinch. Iri is proud of them for it. "Elder. You crossed half a kingdom. Say why."

"To bring her home." Sefu lifts one hand toward the wall — toward Iri — palm open, the gesture of offering feed to birds, and every part of Iri that remembers the locked room wants to fly to it, and every part of her that remembers the flowers turning toward her like faces wants to bite it. "The ward is failing. You've seen what's happening out here — the wells singing, the hedgerows walking. That is a wound opening, Lior, and the child is the needle that can close it. Return her to the valley, restore her to the center of the binding, and the ward re-tightens. The kingdom keeps its magic outside its borders where it belongs. No more crowns that eat kings. No more land that remembers too much." The hand stays open. Patient. "She will be safe. Fed. Taught. Honored, even — we understand now what she is. We were wrong to lock her away, and I will say so before the whole guild if it eases her. But she must come home."

"And if she doesn't?" Lior's voice is steady, but Iri hears the crack under it, hairline-thin. "If the ward fails anyway?"

"Then everything the old magic touches, it remakes." For one moment the grandmother falls away and something older looks out through Sefu's face — something that helped bind a living language into dirt and called it protection. "You've felt it, Lior. Out past the line. The songs that take root in your teeth. Do you think the crown devouring Aldric was cruelty? It was *courtship*. The wild power wants vessels. It found a king. If the ward fails, it will keep looking." The eyes lift to the wall. To Iri. "It already found her once. Would you put that choice back on a child? Give her the safety she was born owed."

The courtyard is very quiet. Somewhere beyond the walls the singing well has started up again, and Iri realizes everyone can hear it, and that everyone is trying not to listen, and failing.

*Come down, little one,* Sefu says — and this time it is not spoken aloud. It arrives in the old channel, the deep one, the way the valley used to speak to her before the valley learned to be silent. *You are tired. I know you are tired. Being the last of anything is heavy. Come home and set it down. We will carry it for you.*

And oh, it is a beautiful lie, or perhaps not even a lie — perhaps a truth offered like a trap is offered, with the door wide and warm inside. Iri imagines it: the valley, the soft dead quiet, a room with a garden in it this time, hands that feed her, years folding over her like snow. Safe. Carried. Asleep.

Then Lior turns and finds her on the wall, and their face does the collapse-and-rebuild thing again, and this time it does not rebuild all the way. This time it stays open, cracked, honest — the dangerous truth standing next to Sefu's safe one, wearing mud and borrowed clothes and a smell of green lightning.

*Iri,* Lior says, in the First Tongue, badly pronounced, beautifully meant. *I won't choose for you. But whatever you choose — I'm going to be standing wherever you're standing.*

That is the difference, she understands suddenly. That is the whole difference, small as a seed.

Sefu offers her a place where nothing will ever hurt her again. Lior offers her company while everything might.

The king in her dream reaches upward through gold water toward someone who has not come yet.

Iri stands up on the wall, small and visible against the darkening sky, and every eye in the courtyard turns to her, and the singing well falls silent as if listening.

"I go with Lior," she says, in the small language, so that all of them will understand, so that none of them can pretend otherwise. Then, because fairness matters even in war, she adds in the old tongue: *But hear me, stone-patient one. The ward is not failing because I left. It is failing because it was built on a stolen word. You cannot tighten a bandage around a crime. Only the king can loose what the guild bound — and he is drowning. So you may ride home, Elder. Or you may ride with us tomorrow, to the palace, and watch what happens when a stolen word is given back.*

Sefu's open hand closes, slowly, like a flower deciding against blooming.

Above the city, unseen in the dusk, the crown's weight shifts — and somewhere in the palace a king opens his eyes, lucid for one clear moment, and whispers a name that is not his own.

It is hers.
