## 9. The Unbinding

The throne room of Aldric's court smelled of cold iron and old incense, and beneath both — so faint that only Lior could catch it, the way one catches the scent of rain before it falls — there was rot. Sweet and green and wrong. The smell of a garden growing in the dark.

They had been smuggled in through the servants' stair by Courtier Vessal, a narrow man with ink-stained fingers and eyes that flinched toward every shadowed archway. Now he pressed them against the cold stone behind a pillar hung with faded banners, his whisper barely more than breath.

"The court assembles at the ninth bell for the king's *address*." He said the word like it tasted of ash. "You'll have perhaps a hundred heartbeats once he speaks. No more. The crown does not tolerate interruption."

Lior looked out across the hall. It was vast, vaulted, its pillars carved into the shapes of trees that no longer grew anywhere — trees whose branches had been stone for so long that even the memory of leaves had worn away from them. Banners of the royal house hung dead along the walls. And at the far end, on a dais beneath a canopy of blackened gold, sat the king.

Aldric was not what Lior had expected. They had imagined a monster, or a husk, or something in between. Instead they saw a man still tall, still broad through the shoulders, his beard trimmed, his robes immaculate — a king assembled piece by piece by hands that loved order more than they loved him. His face was the worst part. Half of it was lucid, human, exhausted, a man drowning quietly behind his own eyes. The other half — the half nearest the crown — was smooth. Not scarred. *Smooth*, like river stone, like something the water had been wearing down for years.

And the crown.

It did not look like gold. It looked like a *sentence*. A circlet of intertwined filigree that seemed to move when Lior wasn't looking directly at it, its patterns shifting like roots seeking soil, like veins seeking a heart. Where it touched his temples, the skin had gone pale and faintly luminous, and Lior understood — with a sick lurch, with the certainty of their own bones — what Iri's babble had meant all those months ago in the archives.

*A king wearing something that wears him.*

The bound oaths of a kingdom, generations deep, every vow of fealty and service and love twisted into a single instrument of holding. The guild had bound the First Tongue into valley soil; the kingdom had bound it into a crown. Two prisons built by people who feared what freedom might cost them.

Beside Lior, Iri shifted. She had been silent since they entered the hall — unnaturally silent, for her — her small hand gripping Lior's sleeve with the desperate strength of a child who has learned that the world can take things away. But now she leaned forward, staring at the dais, and made a sound low in her throat. Not a word. A note. A single held tone that made the nearest banner tremble on its rod.

"Iri," Lior breathed. "Not yet."

But she wasn't answering Lior. She was answering *him*. Somewhere inside the drowning man, something had heard her — the last uncorrupted piece of Aldric, the fragment the crown had never managed to digest — and that fragment had turned toward her voice the way the valley garden had turned its flowers toward her on the first night. Like faces. Like sunflowers finding the sun.

The ninth bell rang.

Doors opened along the length of the hall, and the court poured in: lords in winter furs, ladies in silks stiff with embroidery, soldiers whose armor sang softly as they walked. They arranged themselves in the ancient choreography of attendance, and Lior watched them do it and felt their oaths — felt them, the way you feel heat from a fire — hundreds of invisible threads running from every breast in the hall up to the dais, up to the crown, feeding it, holding it, *being held by it*.

Vessal's hand closed on Lior's shoulder. "When it begins," he whispered, "the guards will move within moments. Whatever you mean to do—" He swallowed. "Do it fast."

"It won't be fast," Lior said. "It has to be *whole*."

The herald's voice rang out, naming the king, and the court knelt, and the threads pulled taut, and Aldric rose from his throne.

He moved like a puppet learning again how to be a man. His lucid eye found the crowd and moved across it — searching, always searching, Lior realized, for something the crown would not let him reach. Then his gaze snagged. Stopped. Found the pillar, the shadows, the small mud-brown child who stood half-hidden behind Lior's legs.

The king's mouth opened. What came out was not the king's voice.

It was a voice like a landslide, like a verdict, like ten thousand oaths spoken at once in perfect unison, and it spoke the courtly tongue with flawless, terrible precision:

"MY PEOPLE. THE WARD IN THE WEST VALLEY FAILS. THE OLD POWER SEEKS A NEW VESSEL. REJOICE—"

"*No,*" said Iri.

It was not loud. It was barely a whisper. But the First Tongue does not travel by air alone, and the word went through the hall like a blade through water. Every thread in the room shuddered. The crown-thing stopped mid-syllable, its filigree writhing, and for one impossible moment the entire court hung suspended between two languages — the language of binding and the language of beginning.

Then the guards moved, exactly as Vessal had promised, and there was no more time for anything but wholeness.

Lior stepped out from behind the pillar into the open floor of the hall, into the sight of a hundred nobles and forty crossbows, and began to sing.

* * *

They had told Lior all their life that they had no gift. That the words sat in their mouth like stones. That the rites came out of them flat, tuneless, dead. And every elder had been right, and every elder had been lying, because the rites were never meant to be performed in a valley where magic lay bound and buried under the very soil of your birth. The songs were not songs. They were *keys*. And keys make no music until they meet their lock.

Lior's lock was standing thirty feet away, wearing a king.

The first note left them and the world changed.

It was not beautiful, not at first. It came out rough and cracked and enormous, a sound with dirt in it, with root and rain and the long patient pressure of things growing through stone — because that is what Lior was, what they had always been, the last wild thread of the First Tongue woven into human shape and hidden in a dead valley for twenty years. The guild had not raised a talentless child. The guild had raised a seed and called the waiting worthless.

The sound hit the court and the court *broke open*. Banners unfroze and billowed though no wind blew. The stone trees carved into the pillars shivered, and somewhere deep in the rock, sap remembered itself. The oath-threads that fed the crown snapped taut and began — one by one, lord by lord, soldier by soldier — to *sing back*. An old countess clutched her chest and wept without knowing why. A young guard dropped his crossbow and said, wonderingly, in a language he had never learned, "*I remember grass.*"

The crown screamed.

There was no other word for it. The parasite of bound oaths, feeling its food turn against it, seized the king's body whole — Aldric's spine arched, his hands clawed the arms of the throne, and the smooth half of his face split into patterns of light like cracks in ice. Through the king's mouth, in a voice layered with centuries, it spoke:

"LITTLE THREAD. YOU ARE ONE STITCH OF A GARMENT TEN THOUSAND YEARS WOVEN. PULL YOURSELF FREE AND THE WHOLE CLOTH UNRAVELS. THE VALLEY DIES WITH ME. THE VALLEY DIES WITH *YOU*."

And here was the moment Lior would carry for the rest of their life, the moment they would return to in dreams and in silence and in the teaching of children not yet born: the moment they understood the price and paid it anyway.

Because it was true. They could feel it true, feel it in the ward-thread that ran through their own marrow like a second spine. The valley's prison and the crown's prison were woven from the same cloth, bound by the same fear, and to unmake one was to unmake both. To free the king was to open the gates of home forever. To sing the truth was to lose the only belonging they had ever known.

Lior thought of the garden blooming out of season. Of Sefu's face in the lamplight of the archive, old and frightened and loyal to a lie. Of Iri's hand in theirs on the road, small and certain. Of every flower turning toward her like a face.

Belonging inherited is just another kind of cage.

They sang louder.

And Iri walked out into the open floor beside them — walked, calm as morning, through a hall full of kneeling, weeping, remembering people, past guards who had forgotten which orders they were sworn to obey — and climbed the steps of the dais, and stood before the drowning king, and took his ruined hands in her small ones.

She said his name.

Not *Aldric*, the courtly name, the name stamped on coins and carved on treaties. She said the older name underneath it, the name his mother must have used, the name that had never been written down because names like that live only in mouths — and she said it in the First Tongue, and the saying of it was a key of its own, and the two keys turned together.

The crown shattered.

Not broke — *shattered*, the way a lie shatters when it is finally spoken against, into a thousand fragments of light that were never gold at all but oaths, freed oaths, each one flying apart like a spark, each one finding its way home to the chest of the person who had first spoken it. The hall filled with returning vows like a snowfall rising upward. The threads dissolved. The parasite, unbound from its vessel, thinned into the air with a last sound that was almost — almost — gratitude.

And Aldric fell forward into Iri's arms, weeping, whole, *himself*, and the backlash went out from the dais like a ring of dawn.

Lior felt it pass through them and knew, in the same heartbeat, what it carried.

Far to the west, in a valley where magic had lain bound for a hundred years, the ward came apart like frost in sunlight. Every hidden thread, every buried vow, every stolen syllable of the First Tongue rising out of the soil at last — free, scattering, *going home to the world*. Lior felt their own foundation go with it, felt the thing that had rooted them to that particular earth dissolve, and understood that they could never go back. Not to the valley as it was. Perhaps not to any place that would simply, effortlessly, have them.

The cost of truth, fully paid. They had known the price. They sang the last verse anyway.

When the light faded, the court was on its knees — not in ceremony this time, but in awe. Aldric sat slumped on the steps of his own dais, a tired man holding a feral child while she patted his shoulder with the grave solemnity of someone comforting a large, foolish animal. Vessal was laughing and crying at once. And every eye in the hall turned, slowly, to the figure still standing in the center of the floor, throat raw, hands trembling, singing the final fading note of a language the world had thought dead.

The king looked up at them.

"You," Aldric said hoarsely, in his own voice at last, cracked and human and small. "What do they call you?"

Lior opened their mouth to give the name the guild had given them — and found, for the first time, that they wanted to choose a different answer. Because the valley was opening. Because somewhere west, an old man was about to kneel in the mud before an unlocked gate. Because the world beyond the ward was full of roads that remembered, rivers that bargained, bridges that asked for true names — and Lior Okonkwo, seed no longer waiting, was finally going to walk them.

"They call me many things," Lior said. "Ask me again tomorrow. Tomorrow I'd like to start choosing."
