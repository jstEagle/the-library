## 10. When the Garden Sings Anew

**Lior**

The valley did not open its gates so much as the gates simply stopped being closed.

Lior stood at the threshold on the morning after the unbinding and watched the ward-line — that faint silver shimmer they had lived beneath all their life without ever truly seeing — dissolve into the air like breath from a cup. Where it had been there was nothing: no barrier, no hum, no absence shaped like a wall. Just morning. Just wind coming up the valley from a world it had never been permitted to touch.

The garden felt it first. Of course it did. The ancient beds that had bloomed out of season for Iri bloomed again now, out of *every* season at once, roses and snowdrops crowding the same branch, wisteria and holly sharing a trellis like old friends making up for lost centuries. The whole valley was drunk on its own returned voice.

"It's loud," Iri said beside them, and Lior understood she didn't mean sound.

It was loud. Magic moved through the soil like water through a dry sponge, and everything drank. The pines at the ridge line leaned eastward as if listening to something over the mountains. The stream ran a half-tone higher than it had yesterday. And everywhere, in every leaf and stone and handful of dirt, Lior could feel the First Tongue stirring — the language the guild had bound into this ground three generations ago, waking now in ten thousand small throats.

They had done that. Lior and a mud-caked child with a king's name hidden in her mouth. They had sung a man free of his crown and unmade a lie older than anyone living, and the cost was written plainly across Lior's own skin: they no longer belonged here. The valley's last wild thread had been spent. Whatever Lior was woven from had been given away in the singing, note by note, until what remained standing before the open gate was only a person. Not an instrument. Not a binding. A person, which was somehow more frightening than either.

"You're sad," Iri observed, with the blunt precision she applied to everything.

"I'm grieving," Lior said. It was the first time they had said it aloud. "The guild raised me. Even the parts that were lies were... home."

Iri considered this with the gravity of a much older creature wearing a very small body. Then she took Lior's hand and pressed it flat against the nearest rosebush, thorns and all.

"Listen," she said, and spoke one word of the First Tongue — the word for *stay*, or perhaps for *root*, the two being nearly the same in her mouth.

The bush answered. Not in words. In warmth, rising through Lior's palm like blood returning to a numb limb. The garden knew them still. The garden had always known them. The guild had merely taught Lior not to hear it.

"Home isn't a place you're kept," Iri said. "Elder Sefu told me that when he locked my door. He thought it was a kindness." Her face did something complicated — grief and forgiveness tangled together, too large for her years. "He wasn't entirely wrong. He was just holding the wrong end of it."

* * *

Sefu came down to the gate at noon, and the whole valley seemed to hold its breath to watch him.

He walked slowly, leaning on a staff of rowan that had been cut, Lior now understood, from the oldest tree in the ward-grove — the tree whose roots held the binding. The staff had gone grey and quiet since the unbinding. Dead wood at last, honest wood. Sefu carried it anyway, the way one carries a crutch after the leg has healed: out of habit, out of shame, out of not knowing yet how to stand without it.

He stopped before Lior. The hunters who had chased them across half the kingdom stood behind him, uncertain, their oath-knots unraveled, their purpose dissolved along with the ward. One of them — Maren, who had once taught Lior to read star-charts — looked away first.

"Lior Okonkwo," Sefu said. His voice was the same voice that had pronounced them talentless a hundred times, but it moved differently now, like a man walking on ground that might give way. "I have spent my life guarding a prison and calling it a sanctuary. I bound children to silence because I was bound. I told you that you were empty because your fullness frightened me — because if the least of us carried the last wild thread, then everything we had built was built on theft."

He knelt.

The elders of the Verdant Guild did not kneel. In a hundred years of rites and records, no elder had bent knee to anything but the soil itself. Sefu went down onto the cold flagstones of the gate with the care of an old man and the deliberateness of a judge passing sentence on himself, and he pressed his forehead nearly to the earth where Lior stood.

"I ask no forgiveness," he said. "Forgiveness is a door, and doors must be opened by the one inside. I ask only for work. Let me be a steward instead of a jailer. Let me spend what years remain pulling the binding out of this soil root by root, the way one pulls bracken — patiently, knowing it will outlive me, planting something better behind it."

Lior looked down at him for a long moment. They thought of the archives, the dust, the ledger with their own birth recorded in a hand that trembled. They thought of Iri's locked door. They thought of the word *stay*, spoken by a child to a rosebush.

Then they knelt too — not in submission but in meeting, the way two people kneel to plant something together — and put their hand on the old man's shoulder.

"The garden needs turning," Lior said. "Come. You can start with the beds you can't reach anymore."

Something broke in Sefu's face, and it was not despair. It was the look of a man setting down a weight he had forgotten he was carrying, and discovering his arms still worked.

* * *

**Iri**

The seed was small and brown and ordinary, and Iri loved it immediately.

Lior had found it in the deepest archive vault, in a clay jar sealed with wax and a knot of bound words — a seed taken from the wild lands before the binding, kept all these generations the way misers keep a single coin. The knot of words had crumbled at Lior's touch, which meant, Iri decided, that the seed had been waiting. Seeds waited. That was their whole nature and their whole courage: to sit in the dark doing nothing but remaining possible.

They planted it at the center of the old ward-grove, where the rowan stump stood pale as bone. Lior dug; Iri knelt at the edge of the hole with her hands in the loose dark earth, feeling the soil's new voice move against her fingers like a cat's purr. Around them, half the valley had gathered — guild members in their green sashes, farmers from the road villages, a delegation from the capital with Aldric himself at its head, thin and white-haired and *present* in a way he had not been in twenty years, looking at the sky like a man seeing weather for the first time.

The king did not speak. He had asked Iri, on the journey back, whether he should abdicate, and she had told him the truth: that a crown was only a chain if everyone agreed to hold the links. Whether to be a king or a man was his own word to say. He was still deciding. She liked watching him decide. It made his face interesting.

"Now," Lior said, and covered the seed with earth.

And Iri began to teach.

She had been afraid of this part — more afraid than the crown, more afraid than the hunters. Words that command living things are dangerous the way rivers are dangerous, and she had been the last speaker of the First Tongue alone in the world, and alone is how dangerous things stay safe. But Lior had shown her another way. Lior, born of the binding, carrying the language in their bones without knowing it, had sung the king free using grammar learned from *gardening* — from guild rites about pruning and grafting and tending, ritual forms that had been chains in the elders' hands and were suddenly, in Lior's mouth, just instructions. Care, formalized. Love, with good technique.

So Iri stood in the turned earth and gave the gathered people their first word.

"*Selu*," she said. *Grow.* Not a command — she was careful about this, careful as crossing ice — an invitation. "Say it to the seed. Not *at* it. To it. Like you'd say it to someone small."

A hundred voices tried it, badly. *Selu.* *Sell-oo.* *Seh-lu.* The word stumbled and blurred, and that was right too — a living tongue is supposed to blur, supposed to wander, supposed to pick up the accents of everyone who loves it. A dead language stays pure. This one would get gloriously messy.

The seed heard.

Iri felt it the way she felt everything now — through her skin, through the soles of her feet, through the scar of the crown that still ached sometimes behind her eyes. The seed unfolded its first intention downward, a root thin as a hair, tasting the unbound soil. Above, in the space between heartbeats, something green gathered itself and pushed.

By evening there was a shoot as tall as Iri's hand, and by moonrise it was a sapling, and the people of the valley stood around it in the dark not speaking at all, which Iri thought was also a kind of First Tongue.

* * *

**Lior**

On the last night of the centennial rite — the rite that had once been a locking and was now, by unanimous and slightly bewildered vote of the guild, an opening — Lior walked the valley walls alone.

Not the gates. The walls themselves: the high hedges and stone terraces that had defined the boundary between the safe and the damned for a hundred years. Wild vines had already claimed them, morning glory and briar weaving through the old mortar, and where the ward-stones stood exposed the lichen grew thick and bright, eating the binding-marks like sugar.

At the northern corner, where the view opened toward the capital and the road and the wide remembering land beyond, Lior found Iri sitting cross-legged on the wall-top, swinging her feet over the drop, watching fireflies assemble in the dusk below.

"They're asking me to stay," Lior said, sitting beside her. "The guild. As teacher. As..." They laughed a little. "As an elder, eventually. Can you imagine."

"Yes," Iri said, with devastating simplicity.

"And they're asking you. To stay. To be safe. To be — " Lior searched for the word the elders used, and found it had rotted like the rowan staff. "To be kept."

Iri was quiet for a while. Below them the garden sang — actually sang now, audibly, a low green chord that the whole valley had stopped pretending not to hear.

"When I lived in the king's shadow," she said at last, "everyone wanted to keep me. The crown wanted to keep me most of all. It wanted me the way a fire wants its last unburned piece of wood." She picked a morning glory blossom and turned it in her fingers. "But keeping isn't the opposite of leaving. Staying can be chosen. That's the word Lior. *Selu* means grow, but there's another word — the one you said to Sefu without saying it. The one that means: I am here because I choose, and I could choose otherwise, and I still choose this."

"What's the word?" Lior asked.

Iri smiled, and for a moment the feral child was gone and something ancient and kind looked out through her eyes — the last uncorrupted piece of a king, or perhaps something older than kings, the part of the world that keeps deciding to begin again.

"You'll learn it," she said. "Everyone will. That's rather the point."

Far off to the north, past the valley's open mouth, a light was moving along the dark road — a lantern, or several, traveling toward them through the remembered land. Word of the unbinding had spread, as truth does, faster than any rider. People were coming. People with questions, with wounds, with seeds of their own. The guild's messengers had already reported villages where the old oaths were loosening, bridges that sang, rivers that bargained.

The world was waking up, and it was going to need teachers who understood both sides of the wall.

Lior looked at the approaching lights, and at the sapling growing silver in the moonlight, and at the small fierce child beside them on the wall, and felt the grief of the lost valley settle at last into something that could grow.

"Well," Lior said, standing, brushing the earth from their knees — the gesture of a gardener, of a steward, of a person belonging exactly where they chose to stand. "We'd better put the kettle on."

Below them, softly, the garden sang anew.
