## 1. The Charm Seller's Hours

Quentin Iversen woke before the bell, because he always woke before the bell, and lay in the dark listening for the sound that had not come in four hundred and eleven days.

The bell was the census crier's, two streets over at the Fountain of the Nine Martyrs, and it rang at six to mark the lawful beginning of commerce in the city of Vell. The other sound—the one he listened for underneath it—was a hum. A low, warm, patient hum that rose through the foundations of his shop like a cat purring under floorboards, except that no cat had ever purred with the weight of a world behind it.

Four hundred and eleven days of silence.

He got up, dressed in the dark by touch—wool shirt on the left peg, coat on the right, boots by the door where he could see them first thing—and went down the narrow stair into the shop without lighting a lamp. He didn't need one. Fifteen years will teach a man the geography of his own cage.

The shop was called, in faded paint on the window, *IVERSEN'S NOTIONS — KNOTS, TINCTURES, SMALL COMFORTS*, and that was true as far as it went. Shelves of luck-knots in red cord. Jars of fever-charms that were, mostly, honey and ginger and willow bark, which is to say they worked, but not the way people hoped. Bundles of dried lavender. Candle stubs. A rack of copper bells that rang in keys too sweet for their size, though Quentin had long ago filed down whatever made them do that, because sweetness invited questions.

Magic had been outlawed in Vell for sixty years. This was the official story, stamped on every Ministry notice: magic had been outlawed, magic had ended, and anyone who said otherwise was either a fool or a criminal, categories the Ministry considered roughly equivalent. What the notices did not say—what only a handful of living people knew—was that magic hadn't ended. It had been *sealed*. Packed away behind a door in the deep places of the world, and that door stood directly beneath the worn floorboards of a notions shop on Coppersmith Lane, and its warden sold luck-knots for two coppers apiece and hated himself with the quiet efficiency of a man who had gotten very good at it.

Quentin opened the shutters. Winter light came in grey and thin, the color of dishwater, and he watched the street wake up: the baker's boy hauling his cart, the lamplighter going home with his pole over his shoulder like a fisherman with nothing to show for the day, old Widow Sarelle across the lane shaking out her rug with the violence of a woman who had outlived everyone she'd ever been angry at.

"Morning, Quentin," she called, not looking up.

"Morning, Sarelle."

"Census tomorrow."

"I know."

"You'll be counted?"

"I'm always counted." He said it lightly, but the words sat wrong in his mouth. Tomorrow was the winter census, when Ministry inspectors walked every street with their white gloves and their brass registers, and every soul in Vell would be asked their name, their trade, their household, and—the new question this year, the question that had made Widow Sarelle's voice drop when she mentioned it—*any unusual resonances in the home?*

Resonance. That was what they called it now. Not magic; magic was a dead word, a museum word. Resonance. As if the world were an instrument and the Ministry merely tuning it.

Quentin turned from the window and began his morning rounds, and if his hands shook slightly as he took down the first jar of charms, he chose not to notice.

* * *

The first customer came at half past seven, as customers always did: a young mother with a baby on her hip and a look of exhausted desperation that Quentin recognized the way sailors recognize weather.

"Fever-charm," she said. "It's her chest. She coughs all night."

"Third shelf, blue jar." He fetched it down himself. "Two spoons in warm milk, morning and night. And—" he glanced at the door, at the empty street, then reached beneath the counter and pressed something small and warm into her palm along with the jar, closing her fingers over it, "—a knot. For luck. No charge."

She opened her hand. It was a red-corded knot, tied in a pattern no Vellish cord-maker used—a pattern with a logic older than kingdoms, loops that folded inward like water going down a drain, or a door opening onto somewhere else.

"Mr. Iversen," she whispered. "Is it real?"

"It's cord," he said. "Cord and hope. The milk is the medicine." But he held her eyes a moment longer than a charm-seller needed to, and something passed between them that neither would ever name aloud, and she paid him three coppers for the jar and left holding the knot like a coal.

This was the whole architecture of his life, and he knew it, and some mornings it fit him like his own coat and some mornings it strangled. Sell the harmless thing openly. Give the true thing secretly. Keep the seal. Count the days.

He sold a luck-knot to a gambler—he told gamblers honestly that luck-knots didn't work on cards, and they bought them anyway, which told Quentin everything about hope. He sold lavender to a funeral procession. He talked an inspector's clerk out of a "random audit" by producing ledgers so meticulously boring that the clerk left yawning. At noon he ate bread and hard cheese standing up, the way he ate every meal, sitting being somehow a luxury he'd never permitted himself.

At noon, also, the Ministry cart came down Coppersmith Lane.

It came every week—two inspectors in dove-grey coats, white gloves folded neatly in their pockets, posting notices, measuring nothing, seeing everything. Quentin had learned their rhythms the way prey animals learn the hunting calls of specific predators. This pair was routine. They posted a notice about resonance reporting. They glanced in his window. One of them, the younger one, lingered a moment over the copper bells.

"Bells are loud," the young inspector said through the glass.

"They're tuned low," Quentin said. "For sleepers."

The inspector looked at him. Quentin looked back with the flat, mild, unremarkable face of a man selling knots, and after a moment the inspector moved on, and Quentin discovered he had been holding his breath since the word *bells*.

*That's new,* he thought, wiping the counter that didn't need wiping. *They never used to stop.*

Four hundred and eleven days of silence, and now they stopped at his window. Coincidence, probably. The Ministry stopped everywhere these days. But dread is a patient animal, and it curled up behind his ribs and stayed.

* * *

Evening closed the shop early, the way winter demanded. Quentin banked the fire, locked the door, drew the curtains, and performed the ritual he had performed every night for fifteen years, the ritual that was the actual reason the shop existed and the luck-knots were merely its costume.

He knelt on the floor beside the hearth, pressed his palm flat against the third floorboard from the wall—the loose one, the one that looked warped and was in fact a key—and spoke the word.

Not in Vellish. There was no Vellish word for it. He spoke it in the First Tongue, the language older than any kingdom, the language the Ministry had burned out of books and memory both, and even now, saying it alone in the dark, his throat tightened around it like a hand around a bird.

The floorboard sank a finger's width. Below it, a stair descended into the earth, and below the stairs—

Silence. Four hundred and eleven days of silence.

Once, the gate had hummed. That was the only word for it. A vibration in the bones of the house, in the fillings of his teeth, a sound you felt rather than heard, like standing near a cathedral organ mid-note. It meant Everwood was there, on the other side—the fading realm, the garden-world, alive behind the door. It meant the covenant held. It meant his fifteen years of exile and loneliness and small dishonesties had purchased exactly what they were supposed to purchase: the safety of two worlds, sealed apart, each forgetting the other gently, the way a wound forgets it was ever a wound.

The hum had begun to falter last spring. Little gaps, like a singer losing breath. Then, four hundred and twelve days ago—it had been autumn, he remembered frost on the window, he remembered thinking *I should count this*—it had simply stopped.

He lit a candle and went down.

The stairs were stone, older than Vell, older than anything Vell had built, and at the bottom stood the gate.

People imagined gates as doors. This was not a door. It was a *threshold*—an archway of living wood grown from the bedrock itself, silver-barked, leafless now, its grain swirling in patterns that hurt to follow with your eyes. Through the arch should have been Everwood: orchards and rivers and a sky like late afternoon forever. Instead the arch showed only darkness—not the darkness of a cellar, but the darkness of a held breath. A stillness. A waiting.

And around the base of the arch, where once the covenant-script had glowed faint gold in continuous slow rotation like the rings of a slow planet, the letters were dim. Some had gone dark entirely. He counted them every night, because counting was the only prayer he trusted.

"Forty-one dark," he murmured. Last month it had been thirty-six. Before that, thirty. The script was dying letter by letter, a sentence being erased from the end backward, and Quentin had a terrible intuition about what happened when the sentence ran out of words.

He put his hand against the silver bark. Cold. Once it had been blood-warm, faintly, like the flank of a sleeping animal.

"Hold on," he told it, feeling immediately foolish, because he had been telling it to hold on for over a year and it had responded the way stone responds to encouragement. "I don't—I can't fix this myself. I was never the one who could fix things. I was only ever the one who kept watch."

The darkness in the arch did not answer.

He climbed back up, sealed the floorboard, and sat a while by the dead fire with his ledger open, pretending to do accounts. On the inside cover, in pencil, in a code that was barely a code—just numbers with no explanation, the kind of thing a bored shopkeeper might scribble—was a column of tally marks. He added tonight's mark. Then he looked at the total for a long time.

Four hundred and twelve.

There was a superstition among wardens—there had been other wardens once; he tried not to think about the others, about *her* especially, and failed most nights—that a silent gate was not a broken gate but a *listening* one. That the realm beyond went quiet when it needed something. When it was calling for someone.

He had never believed the superstition. Believing it would mean the silence was addressed to him, and he could not bear the idea that after fifteen years of faithful, joyless, meticulous service, the world he'd saved was calling his name and he had nothing left to give it.

But tonight, blowing out the candle, he paused.

Because for half a second—less than half, a flicker, the length of a moth's wingbeat—the floorboard under his hand had warmed. Just slightly. Just the way silver bark warms when something on the far side of a threshold presses close.

Quentin sat in the dark with his heart going hard, listening.

Then, faintly—from above, from the street, from the direction of his own front door—came a knock.

Three knocks, spaced strangely, in a rhythm that made the hair on his arms rise, because it wasn't a stranger's knock or a neighbor's knock or any knock known to the city of Vell. It was a knock in an old pattern. An old, old pattern. The kind of knock you'd use, if you were very young and very desperate and had walked a very long way, to announce yourself at the door of a keeper.

Quentin Iversen did not move for five full seconds. Then he rose, crossed the dark shop, and put his eye to the curtain gap.

Standing barefoot in the snow on Coppersmith Lane was a child.
