## 2. A Knock in the Old Tongue

The census was three days away, and Quentin Iversen had already begun lying.

Not out loud. Out loud he said nothing at all, which was the safest kind of lie. But every year, on the eve of the winter census, he performed the same ritual: he walked the shop from floor to rafters, touching each charm on its peg, checking that nothing hummed, nothing glimmered, nothing answered when a Ministry resonance-sweep passed through the walls like a hand pressed flat against the skin of the world. Luck-knots braided from ordinary hemp. Fever-charms of willow bark and beeswax, inert as buttons. A shelf of "warding stones" that were, in truth, river pebbles he'd bought in bulk from a mason's yard and sold for twice their weight in copper to people who wanted to believe the old world still had teeth.

It was good business. It was also, he knew, a kind of prayer — the way he arranged the pebbles, the way he angled the knots so they'd catch lamplight and seem almost alive. Fifteen years of selling harmless things and calling it survival. Fifteen years of pretending magic had died with the last dragon-fire, so thoroughly that some mornings he almost believed it himself.

Almost. Except for the nights he lifted the floorboards.

* * *

The shop sat at the crooked end of Candlemakers' Row, wedged between a chandler who hated him for reasons neither of them remembered and a boarding house whose windows never lit. Quentin closed at dusk, as he always did. He banked the stove, turned the sign, counted the till twice — forty-one coppers and a bad silver piece someone had bitten, which he kept because it reminded him that even metal could lie about what it was.

Then he knelt, moved the rug, and lifted the third board from the hearth.

Cold air rose from below, smelling of wet stone and something older — leaf-rot and lightning, the smell of a place that hadn't been rained on properly in fifteen years. He lowered the shuttered lantern into the dark until it found the gate.

The gate was not grand. That was the joke no one would ever get to laugh at: the hinge between two worlds, the last standing door of the covenant, and it looked like a cellar archway some drunk mason had abandoned halfway through. Grey stone, uncarved. The threshold worn smooth by feet that had stopped coming long ago. No light behind it now. No sound. For the first ten years there had been a hum — low, patient, like a hive dreaming — and Quentin had checked it nightly the way other men check on sleeping children.

The hum had stopped four hundred and twelve days ago.

He knew the number the way he knew his own age. He didn't keep a tally anywhere; writing it down would have been evidence, and evidence got you a visit from men in white gloves. He kept it in his head instead, where all his worst possessions lived. Four hundred and twelve nights of silence. And tonight, when he held the lantern close and watched his own breath fog in air that should have been warm, he thought: *four hundred and thirteen*, and felt the number like a stone dropped into still water, the ripples spreading outward toward a shore he didn't want to name.

Because gates didn't go quiet and then come back. Gates went quiet, and then they went *gone*.

He set the board back down. He put the rug over it. He poured himself two fingers of plum brandy he couldn't afford and drank them looking at the door of his shop — the real one, the street one — because some part of him, some animal part that had been counting days without being asked, expected something to knock.

Nothing knocked.

He went up the narrow stair to his room above the shop, hung his coat on the hook shaped like a heron that Wren had carved for him a lifetime ago — then took the coat off again and turned the heron to face the wall, because tomorrow was census-eve and even hooks could be witnesses. He lay down in his boots. He did this every night, had done it for fifteen years: slept dressed, one eye's worth of attention always open. A man who keeps a door doesn't get to sleep deeply.

He dreamed, briefly, of a voice saying words he refused to remember, and woke before dawn with the taste of ash in his mouth and the certain, stupid conviction that today the number would change.

* * *

Census-eve came down grey and hard, the first true snow of winter arriving sideways on a wind that smelled of iron. Vell huddled under it. By law, every soul in the city was to be home by nightfall, doors marked with the white chalk cross of the tally-wagons, names entered, resonances sampled. The Ministry called it counting. Everyone called it what it was: they were weighing the city, listening for anything that rang wrong.

Quentin spent the day doing brisk, boring trade. He sold a fever-charm to the chandler's wife, a luck-knot to a carter leaving for the coast road, and three warding stones to a young widow who clutched them like eggs and thanked him with eyes too desperate for pebbles to ever help her. He gave her the fourth one free and hated himself for the kindness, because kindness drew attention, and attention, in Vell, drew white gloves.

At dusk the tally-wagons rolled past, lanterns swinging, clerks shouting house numbers through cupped hands. Quentin stood in his doorway with his mug of barley tea and let a clerk chalk the cross on his lintel, giving his name — *Iversen, Quentin, sole occupant, trade: charmseller (licensed, non-resonant)* — in the flat voice of a man reciting his own epitaph.

"Quiet street this year," the clerk said, stamping his ledger.

"Best kind," Quentin said.

The wagons rattled on. The snow thickened. And Quentin did what he did every census-night: he waited until full dark, then climbed to the attic and checked the house itself.

The wards were his one extravagance. Not Ministry magic — nothing that rang, nothing that sang — but the old kind, the quiet kind, taught to him by a woman whose name he only allowed himself to think once a year. Threads of intent knotted into the doorframe. A listening-silence laid over the windows like frost. A word sunk into the threshold stone that meant, roughly translated from a language older than any kingdom, *nothing here is yours*. They weren't strong. They were barely legal. But they were his, and they had never once spoken aloud, because wards that spoke aloud were wards announcing themselves to anyone with ears, and Quentin had spent fifteen years teaching them to be deaf and dumb and safe.

He was halfway back down the attic ladder when every ward in the house began to sing.

* * *

It started as a shiver in the floorboards — not the gate-side boards, the *street*-side ones, which made no sense at all — and then the knotted threads in the doorframe flared visible, gold-white, burning through their own concealment like wires of lightning, and the silence-frost on the windows melted into running script, and the word in the threshold stone rose up through the soles of his boots and into his teeth and *spoke*.

Not the word he'd buried there. Something older. Something underneath it.

The whole house rang like a struck bell, and Quentin stood on his own staircase with his heart trying to climb out through his ribs, thinking with perfect clarity: *they've found me, fifteen years, and it ends with me standing on the stairs holding a candle.*

Then, through the ringing, through the snow-hush, he heard it.

A knock. Small. Bare knuckles on wood, twice, politely.

And a voice — high, cracked with cold, unmistakably a child's — speaking the First Tongue.

Quentin's body reacted before his mind could stop it. His mouth opened. His throat, which had not formed those sounds in fifteen years, which had sworn an oath of forgetting sealed with more than pride, shaped the reply. One word. Two syllables. *Enter, little spark.* It came out of him rough and rusted and absolutely fluent, in the accent of a country that no longer existed, and the moment it left his lips the wards went silent — all of them, everywhere, as if the house had been holding its breath for years and had finally been given permission to stop.

He stood there shaking. Then he went down the stairs, unlatched three locks, and opened the door onto the snow.

* * *

The child was small — eight years old, maybe nine, though something in the way they stood made the number feel like a guess. Barefoot in the snow, feet blue-grey, toes curled against the cold with a stubbornness that looked less like endurance and more like habit. A coat too big, patched at both elbows, soaked through. Hair the colour of woodsmoke, cut short and uneven, as if by knife or fire. And around their neck, on a cord of woven grass gone brown with age, a pendant that caught the lamplight and threw it back wrong — flat black, faintly irised, like oil on water.

A scale. Dragon-scale, or the fossil ghost of one. Quentin knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat, and knowing it made the cold in the room drop ten degrees, because scales burned cold, everyone who had ever stood near a living fire knew that, and this one was burning cold right now, in his doorway, on census-night, on his *threshold*.

"*Serra-veth,*" the child said. The First Tongue, spoken with the easy roundness of a native speaker, the way Quentin hadn't heard it spoken aloud since a night he kept locked behind his teeth. "*An sel i vethan? An keeper?*" Are you the keeper? Are you the keeper?

Every ward in the house answered aloud, one after another, like bells rung in sequence down a corridor: *yes. yes. yes. yes.*

Quentin did the only thing he could think to do. He grabbed the child by the collar, hauled them inside, slammed the door, threw all three locks, and stood with his back against it breathing like a man who has just surfaced from deep water.

The child looked around the shop with wide, unimpressed eyes. At the luck-knots. At the pebbles. At Quentin, barefoot-checking his own bare feet, wincing.

"You're the keeper," the child said — in trade-tongue now, accented but clear, the words learned somewhere far from any schoolroom. "They said there was a keeper at the crooked end of the row. They said follow the smell of beeswax." A pause. "You don't look like much."

"I'm not," Quentin said. It came out automatic, reflexive, fifteen years deep. "There's no keeper. There's no anything. This is a charm shop. I sell pebbles."

The child looked at the ceiling, where the last traces of gold-white wardlight were still fading from the beams like dying embers. Then back at him. Then, pointedly, at the pendant on their own chest, which pulsed once — a slow, cold pulse, like a heartbeat made of winter — and made the lamp flames lean toward it.

"The stones in your house know your name," the child said. "I heard them say it when I knocked. *Quen-tin.*"

"They're broken," Quentin said. "Old houses talk."

"My mother talked too," the child said. Simply. Without drama, which was somehow worse. "Before they came. She spoke the same words you spoke just now, when you answered the door. Nobody speaks those words anymore except—" The child stopped. Looked at their feet, which were leaving small melt-puddles on his clean floor, and frowned at them as if the puddles were a personal failing. "Your floor is very cold. There is something under your floor that is colder."

Quentin crossed the room in three strides and knelt and pulled the child's feet into his lap before he could think better of it, rubbing warmth into them with both hands, rough, urgent, the way you warm something you're terrified of losing. The child tolerated this with the dignity of a cat.

"What's your name?" Quentin asked.

"Ash."

"Ash *what*?"

"Just Ash. Mother said names are doors." The child looked down at him, and in the lamplight Quentin saw their eyes properly for the first time — dark, flecked with a gold that might have been lamplight and might not have been, and old, old in a way no child's eyes should be. "She said if the door closes, find the keeper. The keeper knows the way through."

Quentin's hands went still on those freezing feet.

Four hundred and thirteen days of silence under his floorboards. A child at his door speaking a language dead longer than the child had been alive, wearing a scale that burned cold, and every ward he'd spent fifteen years silencing singing out his name like a welcome-home.

The gate wasn't just quiet anymore. The gate was calling for help.

And it had sent him the one thing he could not refuse, could not turn away, could not pretend was a pebble or a knot or a harmless lie: a barefoot child, standing in his snow-melted footprints, waiting for him to answer a question he'd spent half his life dreading.

"How long," Quentin heard himself ask, "have you been walking?"

Ash considered. "Since the leaves fell. Maybe before. I stopped keeping count."

Quentin closed his eyes. He was suddenly, horribly certain of his own number, and of the fact that tomorrow — census-day, sweep-day, the worst possible day in the calendar of Vell — he was going to have to lift the floorboards again, and this time he wouldn't be alone.

"Stay here," he said, and went to find dry socks, and did not let himself think about the fact that somewhere behind him, faint and cold, the scale was pulsing like a second heartbeat in the dark — faster now, as if it, too, had finally reached the end of its count.
