## 3. What the Ministry Burns

* * *

The village had no name after.

That was the first thing Ash understood, though they were small then, though they did not have words for it yet: the village had a name the night before, and in the morning it did not, and the people stood in their doorways moving their mouths around the shape of nothing, like fish lifted out of water, like bells with their tongues pulled.

Ash's mother still said the old words under her breath while she worked. *Elu veth, elu sara.* The fire remembers. The fire waits. She said them when she kneaded bread and when she washed Ash's hair and once — only once, and Ash never forgot — when she looked up at the sky over the orchards and saw that the color had gone wrong, gone grey as dishwater, gone ash.

"Where is the name?" Ash asked her. "What happened to it?"

Her hands went still in the flour. She looked at the window for a long time, at the inspectors walking down the lane with their white gloves and their ledgers, writing down names one by one, and each name they wrote seemed to leave the person who owned it, the way heat leaves a stone at dusk.

"They're not taking names," she said finally. "They're taking what names are *for.*"

Then she kissed Ash's forehead and spoke the old words again, faster this time, like someone sewing a wound shut before the blood comes through.

* * *

Here is what the Ministry does. Here is what Ash learned to watch for, the way other children learned to watch for weather:

A cart comes. Not often — maybe twice a year, maybe less, because unmaking is expensive work and requires paperwork. Men step down in clean boots. They wear gloves of white leather so soft it looks like skin, and they do not carry weapons, because weapons are for things you want dead, and the Ministry does not want anything dead. Dead things keep their names. Dead things are honest about being gone.

The men knock on doors and smile. They ask questions in pleasant voices. *How long have you lived here? And your family before you? And would you say this house holds meaning for you? Meaning is important to us. Meaning is our whole work.*

And the people answer, because the men are polite, because there is nothing to be afraid of from polite men, and every answer goes into the ledger, and every name in the ledger becomes a line, and every line becomes a thread, and the threads get gathered up into the white-gloved hands like harvest.

By evening the village is still there. By morning it is less.

Not burned. Never burned — burning leaves a scar, and scars can be read. Unmade. The houses stand but nobody lives in them, or people live in them who cannot say why they came. The well still gives water but the water tastes of forgetting. Dogs go first, Ash noticed. Dogs know. They howl all night before the carts come, and in the morning they are quiet, quiet, quiet, sniffing at their masters' hands as if checking whether these are the same hands.

Ash's mother held them close that last night and spoke the old words into their hair until Ash slept, and when Ash woke the house was full of strangers' furniture and a woman was calling a stranger's child to breakfast and Ash's own name was a smooth blank place inside them where something used to live.

But not everything came out.

Some things are written too deep for white gloves. Some things live below the ledger-line, in the dark sediment of a person, where even the Ministry does not like to dig.

Ash still had the pendant. Ash still had the scale.

And Ash still had the words — not many, a handful, a child's handful, gathered from their mother's mouth like berries: *elu veth*, the fire remembers; *sara iel*, wait for me; and one whole sentence, the sentence their mother said most often, pressing it into them like a coin into wax:

*Ash-ven tora elum. The gate knows its own.*

* * *

After, there were years of after.

Ash learned to be small and quick and forgettable — which was hard, because forgettable was exactly what the Ministry made people, and Ash refused on principle. They learned which towns still had hearth-stones carved with the old script. They learned that if you hummed certain notes near a standing stone, sometimes it hummed back, faintly, like an animal too long asleep to remember why it growled.

And they heard the rumor.

It came from a tinker woman in the market town of Lowmarsh, a woman with a burn-scarred jaw who traded in luck-knots that actually worked — not Ministry charms, real ones, knotted in the old way. She sold Ash a fever-charm for their cough and then looked at the dragon-scale pendant, and her face did a strange thing, folding in on itself like paper crumpling.

"Where did you get that?"

"My mother."

"Your mother." She glanced over both shoulders. Then she leaned close, and her voice dropped so low Ash had to read her lips more than hear. "There's a man in Vell. Big city, south, past the river. Sells charms out of a shop in the Tanner's Row quarter. His charms don't work — that's how I know he's real. Anyone selling magic that works gets collected inside a season. But him—" she tapped the counter twice, "—him, they've left alone fifteen years. Fifteen years, child. Either he's nothing at all, or he's the best-hidden thing in Vell. And there's a story," she said, "an old story, older than me, that when the gate between worlds was sealed, a keeper stayed behind to watch it. A warden. Living on the human side, keeping the door."

"The gate," Ash said. Their heart had begun doing something painful and enormous in their chest. "The gate to Everwood."

"Tanner's Row," the tinker said. "Look for a shop with no sign. A man who counts things."

She wouldn't take payment for the fever-charm. She pushed it back across the counter and touched Ash's pendant once, lightly, with one finger, and drew breath through her teeth as if she'd grabbed a hot pan.

"Cold," she whispered. "That scale's cold. That means the fire's dying." Her eyes came up to Ash's, and they were wet and frightened and fierce all at once. "Run fast, little one."

So Ash ran.

* * *

A year is a long time when you are small and alone and following a rumor south.

Ash crossed the river at Sorrowside on a barge whose captain asked no questions because Ash paid in the only coin they had left — three songs in the First Tongue, sung low in the dark of the hold, and the captain wept without knowing why and gave them bread besides. Ash walked through towns where the hearth-stones had been chiseled blank. Ash slept in hay-barns and bell-towers and once, memorably, in the hollow of a tree that was warm all through the winter night, warm as a sleeping animal, and in the morning there was a ring of tiny mushrooms around it, and Ash took that for a yes.

The city of Vell rose out of the plain like a wall against the sky, all chimneys and slate and smoke, a city that had decided magic was a disease it had cured. There were posters on the gates. UNREGISTERED RESONANCE IS A REPORTABLE CONDITION, they said, above a picture of a smiling family pointing at a neighbor's door. Ash kept their head down and their hand closed tight around the pendant, which had grown colder every mile south, cold as river-bottom, cold as a held breath.

Tanner's Row smelled of lye and old fat. The shop had no sign. Of course it had no sign.

Ash watched it for two days.

This is what Ash saw: a narrow door, a dusty window displaying knots and ribbons and little braided charms that meant nothing. A man came and went at precise hours — tall, grey at the temples, careful in his movements the way a person is careful when they have spent years practicing being unremarkable. He bought bread at the same stall. He nodded to the same neighbors. He counted his coins twice before paying.

He counted everything. The tinker woman had been right. He counted his steps past the corner; Ash could see his lips move. He counted the seconds between the church bells. Once, when a Ministry inspector strolled down the row — white gloves, ledger under his arm, smiling his pleasant smile — Ash saw the charm-seller go absolutely still, the way prey goes still, and then resume dusting his window with hands that did not shake, because he had trained them not to, and Ash recognized that training. Recognized it the way you recognize a limp you share.

*He's afraid,* Ash thought, with a kind of wonder. *He's been afraid for fifteen years and he's still here. Still keeping the door.*

On the second evening, when the lamps were lit and the row emptied out and the cold came down off the roofs like a poured thing, Ash stopped waiting. Their mother had not raised them to wait outside doors. Their mother had pressed a sentence into them like a coin into wax, and coins are for spending.

Ash knocked.

Three knocks — no. Not knocks. Words. Ash rapped the rhythm of the oldest greeting they knew, *el-u-ven, el-u-ven*, fire-and-welcome, welcome-and-fire, the pattern their mother tapped on crib-boards and table-tops, and their knuckles had barely fallen the third time when—

Every ward on the house answered aloud.

Ash felt it before they heard it: a shudder running up through the cobbles, through the doorstep, into the frame, and then the house itself spoke — a low chorus of voices layered under the wood like roots under soil, speaking syllables in the First Tongue, *vennath, vennath, elu sara*, and the windows glowed faintly gold for half a heartbeat, half a breath, and somewhere inside Ash heard a man stop breathing.

Footsteps. Fast ones, then stopping. Then slow ones, controlled ones, coming to the door.

It opened a crack. Chain on. One grey eye in the gap, and behind the eye a face going through terror and calculation and disbelief so quickly it was almost funny, almost, except Ash was too cold and too hungry and too full of a year of roads to laugh.

"What do you want?" the man said. His voice was flat, practiced, bored. It fooled no one. It certainly didn't fool the wards, which were still murmuring overhead like bees.

Ash reached into their collar and drew out the pendant. The dragon-scale caught the lamplight and threw it back wrong, cold, blue-white, a sliver of a fire that had almost gone out. They held it up so he could see.

And because their voice was small and hoarse from a year of disuse, and because the words were the largest thing they carried, larger than hunger, larger than fear, Ash spent the coin their mother had minted. They spoke the sentence in the First Tongue, the whole sentence, carefully, the way you set down something breakable:

"Ash-ven tora elum. *The gate knows its own.*"

The chain didn't come off. The door simply opened — the man's hand must have moved without his permission — and light spilled out, lamplight and hearth-light and something underneath both, something old and gold and shaking.

Up close he smelled of dust and dried herbs and fear sweat, and his eyes went from the scale to Ash's face and back, and Ash watched him understand, watched it land on him like a falling roof. This stranger, this keeper, this man who had hidden so long and so well that even the Ministry walked past his door — he flinched. Actually flinched, a small full-body recoil, as if Ash's face were a debt come due.

Ash knew that flinch. Ash had seen it on faces all down a year of roads, on innkeepers and ferry-men and priests, everyone who suspected what the child at their threshold might cost them.

Hope is heavy. Ash had carried it a year, and their arms were tired, and here at last was the door the rumor promised, and the man in it was afraid of them.

"I'm cold," Ash said, in ordinary trade-tongue now, because the First Tongue was for true things and this was merely a fact. "I walked a long way. Everyone says you're the keeper."

The man's throat moved. Behind him the wards had gone silent, listening.

"I sell charms," he said. "Luck-knots. Nothing works. Nothing here works, that's the point, that's—"

"*Elu veth,*" Ash said quietly. *The fire remembers.*

And the man closed his eyes as if the words had struck him between the shoulders, and when he opened them again he was already unlatching the chain, already glancing up and down the empty row with those counting eyes, already pulling Ash inside by the sleeve with a grip that trembled and did not let go.

The door shut. The bolt slid home.

Above them, faint as a memory of warmth, the wards began — very softly — to sing.

* * *

That night, in a room above a shop with no sign, Ash slept for the first time in a year without holding the pendant. They hung it on the nail by the bed instead, because the room itself was warm in a way rooms aren't, warm from underneath, warm the way the hollow tree had been, and somewhere far below the floorboards Ash could feel it — faint, guttering, but alive.

A heartbeat under the house. A door. A gate.

The keeper sat across the room pretending to read, and every few minutes his eyes came up and found Ash and flicked away again, guilty as a thief. He thought he was hiding it. He counted badly when he was frightened.

Ash watched him back through their lashes and thought about the tinker woman's finger on the scale, and the captain weeping in the dark of the barge, and their mother's hands still in the flour, and the white gloves gathering names like harvest, and a mountain on the horizon of every old song, a mountain with a fire in it that was going out.

Tomorrow, they decided. Tomorrow they would make him open the floorboards.

Tonight, the wards sang, and the fire under the world remembered just enough to keep them both alive until morning.
