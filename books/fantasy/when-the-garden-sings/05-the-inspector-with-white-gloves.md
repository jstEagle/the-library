## 5. The Inspector with White Gloves

The knock came at half past nine in the morning, which was wrong on two counts.

First, nobody knocked on Quentin Iversen's door before noon. His shop didn't open until the bells rang terce, and his regulars knew it—the feverish mothers, the gamblers wanting luck-knots, the old men who bought love charms they were too embarrassed to admit never worked. Second, and far worse: the knock came in threes. Three sharp raps, evenly spaced, like a metronome.

Ministry men knocked like that. They couldn't help it. It was trained into them the way obedience is trained into dogs.

Quentin stood very still behind his counter, a luck-knot half-tied between his fingers, and listened to the house.

The wards said nothing. That was the strange part. For fifteen years the wards had hummed their low note whenever anyone with ill intent touched the door—a sound only he could hear, a wasp trapped in the walls. Now there was silence, and somehow the silence was louder than any warning could have been. As if the house itself had gone still the way a rabbit goes still.

He set down the knot. He untied it first—never leave a charm half-made; Wren had taught him that, back when teaching was something she still did—and he smoothed his apron, and he opened the door.

"Master Iversen." The man on his step was tall and narrow as a folded letter. Grey coat, grey hat, grey eyes, and gloves of white kid leather so clean they seemed to hold their own light against the morning grime of Candlemaker's Row. He smiled, and the smile was warm the way a banked fire is warm: technically true, practically useless. "Inspector Corvane. Ministry of Unmaking, Office of Resonance. Might I come in? It's cold enough to crack stone."

Behind Corvane stood two assistants in matching grey, hands folded, faces smooth as eggs. They did not smile. Their job was not to smile.

"Inspector," Quentin said, and stepped aside, because refusing would have been its own confession. "I don't open till terce."

"I know." Corvane crossed the threshold with the delicacy of a man entering a chapel. His white-gloved fingers brushed the doorframe—not idly, Quentin noted. Testing. "Your hours are one of the things I admire about you, Master Iversen. Regularity. A man who keeps regular hours is a man who has nothing to hide."

"Then you'll find me very boring."

"One hopes." Corvane removed his hat and looked around the shop with an appraiser's eye: the bundles of dried herbs that were only herbs, the knots and braids hanging from the ceiling beams that were only rope, the jars of river-water and grave-dirt labeled in Quentin's careful merchant hand as *bath salts* and *fertilizer*. Fifteen years of camouflage. Corvane took it all in the way a customs officer takes in luggage—thoroughly, politely, missing nothing. "Charming establishment. Very... quiet."

"It's a shop. Shops are quiet in the morning."

"Mm." Corvane drifted toward the counter, trailing one gloved finger along its edge without quite touching it. "May I be direct with you?"

"You may try."

That earned a small laugh, genuine around its edges. "Directness, then. Three nights ago, my office recorded an unregistered resonance in this district. A pulse. Old-pattern, deep-frequency—the kind of signature we haven't logged in Vell since before the Closing." He turned, and those grey eyes settled on Quentin with the weight of a hand on the back of the neck. "You'll understand why your street drew our attention. This block sits over some of the oldest foundations in the city. Pre-Imperial, some of it. And you, Master Iversen, are the longest-tenured resident. Fifteen years in one shop. Men who stay rooted in old ground tend to know what grows there."

"The pulse," Quentin said. His voice came out level. He was almost proud of it. "You mean the tremor. Half the Row felt it. Cracked my good crockery."

"A tremor." Corvane tasted the word. "Yes. We've been calling it a tremor too. In our reports. Though between us—" he leaned in, confiding, terrible, "—tremors don't carry language."

The room tilted, very slightly, and righted itself. Quentin kept his face doing nothing at all. Behind him, up the stairs, behind two closed doors, Ash was awake—he'd heard the child moving at dawn, restless, murmuring in that tongue older than kingdoms—and if Ash made one sound, spoke one syllable—

"There's no language in a cracked crockery jar," Quentin said.

"No. There isn't." Corvane straightened. "But there was language in the resonance. My analysts spent two nights on it. They recovered four words. Would you like to hear them?"

"I'd like you to buy something and leave, honestly. I have stock to move."

"*The gate remembers*," Corvane said, as if Quentin hadn't spoken. "*The gate remembers.* Two words, twice repeated. Old Tongue. Pre-Covenant dialect, if you can believe our luck—we haven't heard a clean sample in a generation." He smiled again, and this time the warmth was entirely gone from it, and what remained was worse: patience. "Language requires a speaker, Master Iversen. Speakers require mouths. And mouths, in this city, require registration. So we're conducting interviews. Door to door. Yours is the forty-first."

"And thirty-nine people told you nothing."

"Thirty-eight told me nothing. One lied beautifully." Corvane's gloved hand rose, and he adjusted his cuff with a tiny, precise tug. "I do respect a beautiful lie. It takes craft. But a lie, however lovely, has a frequency of its own. Did you know that? Fear resonates. Guilt resonates. Even competence resonates—you're lying *well*, Master Iversen, and well is a measurable quantity."

The stove ticked. Somewhere out on the Row, a cart rattled past, ordinary as bread. Quentin became aware that he was holding his breath and made himself release it slowly, through the nose, bored.

"Inspector," he said, "I sell knots to gamblers and bath salts to widows. If that registers on your instruments, your instruments need recalibrating."

"They might." Corvane nodded agreeably. "They might indeed. Which is why I won't detain you today. Or search the premises." He let that hang in the air like smoke. "Today I'm simply visiting. Making acquaintance. Building the map." From inside his coat he produced a small card—grey, of course—and laid it on the counter between them. "If anything occurs to you. Any sound, any dream, any customer who asks unusual questions. You'll send for me."

"Of course."

"And Master Iversen?" Corvane put his hat back on, and paused at the door, and looked back with an expression of such mild, reasonable kindness that Quentin's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the cellar, through the hidden gate beneath the cellar, all the way down into whatever dark held the world together. "Winter census is in three days. Every soul in Vell counted, every household entered in the rolls. Do make sure your count is accurate. An inaccurate census is, historically, how everything begins to unravel."

The door closed behind him. The three knocks, reversed, going away.

Quentin stood in his quiet shop and did not move for a long time.

* * *

He gave them ten minutes. Long enough for the grey coats to turn the corner, short enough that whoever watched the Row—and someone always watched the Row now, he understood that with total clarity—wouldn't see anything worth reporting about a shopkeeper checking his stock.

Then he took the stairs two at a time.

Ash sat on the floor of the back room, knees drawn up, the dragon-scale pendant clutched in one small fist. The scale burned cold—that was the wrongness of it, cold where fire should be—and even now, across the room, Quentin could feel it the way you feel ice near bare skin. The child looked up at him with those eyes that were too old and too young at once.

"He's gone," Quentin said. "For now. Did you hear—"

"The words." Ash's voice was hoarse. They'd been speaking Common for his benefit all week, clumsy with it, but now the First Tongue poured out of them like water finding its level, and Quentin understood every syllable, God help him, though he'd sworn for fifteen years he'd forgotten. "*The gate remembers.* I said it in my sleep. I say it every night. It's the seal-song, keeper. It's what the gate sings when it's dying."

"I know what it is."

"Do you?" Ash tilted their head, and there was no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse. "Because you flinched when he said it. Like it bit you."

Quentin crouched down, bringing himself to the child's level, and made himself look at the pendant instead of the eyes. "Listen to me. In three days there's a census. Men will come to this door with a list of names, and they will count everyone under this roof, and when they count you, you will exist officially, permanently, in Ministry ink. That cannot happen. So we leave before then. Before dawn tomorrow, we go down through the floor, and we don't come back."

"Down." Ash's grip tightened on the scale. "Through the gate."

"Through the gate."

"To Everwood." The child said the name the way other children said the names of dead grandparents—reverent, secondhand. "My mother talked about it. She said the sky there was green once."

"It was." Quentin's throat worked. "It may still be. I haven't—I don't open it. I check it. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

Fifteen years of difference. Fifteen years of lifting the floorboard every night and pressing his palm to the cold iron ring set in the stone beneath, feeling for the hum, counting the days since it last sang. Four hundred and six days, as of last night. He'd stopped writing the number down months ago because seeing it in his own handwriting frightened him more than the silence did.

"The difference," he said, "is that opening it is a choice, and choices have prices. Checking is just cowardice with a schedule."

Ash considered this with the terrifying seriousness of children. Then: "You're not a coward."

"You've known me eight days."

"I've known you long enough." The child unfolded, stood, and looked at him squarely. "You hid me from the man with the white gloves. Cowards don't hide people. Cowards hand them over."

Quentin had no answer for that, so he busied himself pulling the travel-bag from under the bed—the bag he'd packed fifteen years ago and never unpacked, because a man who keeps a packed bag under his bed is a man who knows, somewhere in his marrow, that every safe life is temporary. Bread, dried apples, the good knife, the bundle of letters tied with string that he did not look at and Ash, mercifully, did not ask about.

"Sleep if you can," he said. "We move at the black hour, before the lamplighters douse. And Ash—" he caught the child's shoulder, "—not one word of the old tongue until we're through. Not a whisper. Your dreams sing loud enough."

* * *

He waited until Ash slept—truly slept, curled around the pendant like it was a hearthstone—before he went down to the cellar.

The shop above was quiet. The Row outside was quieter. Winter pressed against the shutters like a creditor.

Quentin knelt on the cold flagstones and lifted the loose board, and beneath it the iron ring lay in its bed of stone, and beneath the ring, the gate.

He put his palm flat against the metal and felt what he had felt for four hundred and seven days now: almost nothing. A vibration so faint it might have been his own blood, might have been a cart passing on the Row, might have been wishful thinking. Once—it felt like another life—it had sung under his hand like a struck bell, warm and vast, the heartbeat of a whole world conducted up through the stone into his bones. Wren used to press her ear to the floor and laugh, said she could hear the orchards growing.

Now there was barely a whisper. And whispers, Inspector Corvane had reminded him this morning, are measurable.

He should have seen it coming. That was the sick clarity settling over him now, cold and complete, like water filling boots. Fifteen years of small lies—bath salts, fertilizer, a shop that opened at terce, a warden pretending to be a merchant—and he'd told them so long and so smoothly that he'd believed the magic was safely buried. But the gate was failing, and failing things cry out, and crying things get found. The Ministry hadn't come for him. They'd come for the sound. He was incidental. He had always been incidental; that was the entire architecture of his survival.

And yet Corvane had known his name. Had known his tenure. Had counted his years the way Quentin counted the silent days.

They'd be watching the shop now. Listening-sigils, probably—one left behind, tucked somewhere, drinking in every word. Quentin swept his eyes across the cellar, the stairwell, the shop above, looking for it: a chalk mark, a knot of grey thread, a sliver of bone. Nothing he could see. That was the point of listening-sigils. Wren had taught him those too, back when she taught him everything, back before he—

He shut that door in his mind. It groaned on its hinges but it shut.

Before dawn, then. Down through the floor. Into a realm he'd sealed himself into exile from, carrying a child the covenant had apparently grown to replace him, hunted by a man whose kindness was the most frightening thing Quentin had encountered in fifteen years of frights.

He pressed his palm to the iron ring one last time.

*Open when I knock,* he thought at it, the old formula rising unbidden in the old tongue. *Open when I knock, and remember me kindly, because I have not deserved it.*

Far below, faint as a dying lamp, the gate flickered—and answered.

Quentin Iversen climbed the stairs to pack the rest of his life into one bag, and began counting the hours until dawn.
