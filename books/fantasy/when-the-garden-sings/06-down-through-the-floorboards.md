## 6. Down Through the Floorboards

**Quentin**

The listening-sigil Corvane left behind sat on my windowsill like a spider made of frost, and I could feel it drinking the room. Every hour it fed, it learned a little more of us—the creak of the third stair, the way Ash hummed in their sleep in a language no census would ever record.

So we had until dawn. Maybe less.

I packed the way I'd rehearsed packing for fifteen years without ever admitting it to myself: one bag, three changes of clothes, the ledger of charms I'd sold and shouldn't have, and a tin of fever-tea because even at the end of the world, children get colds. Ash stood in the middle of the shop floor watching me with those eyes that were too old for their face, holding the dragon-scale pendant against their chest like something that might bolt.

"You're scared," Ash said. Not a question. In the First Tongue it came out as a statement about weather.

"Yes," I said, in the same language, because lying in the old words felt like blasphemy now that I'd remembered I knew them.

"Good," Ash said. "Mother said fear is just the body remembering it's real."

I didn't ask what else their mother had said. I wasn't sure I could survive the answer before breakfast.

The floorboards came up in the corner behind the counter, where I'd pried them loose ten thousand times and nailed them back down nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine. Underneath: darkness, and a smell like rain on hot stone, and—when I knelt and pressed my palm flat against the hidden hatch—the faintest vibration. A hum. The gate's old song, down to its last dry note.

It hadn't hummed in forty-one days. I knew because I counted. I'd been counting since the night the number first frightened me, which was the night I understood that counting was all I had left of being a warden instead of a shopkeeper who told himself stories.

"Ash." I looked at the child. "Once we go through, there's no coming back this way. Not through here. You understand?"

Ash nodded. Then, with the terrible directness of children: "There was never anything back your way anyway."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth to argue. And then I looked around my shop—at the luck-knots and the fever-charms and the fifteen years of small harmless lies stacked on shelves—and closed it again.

The hatch opened onto stairs that hadn't existed when I built them, spiraling down into stone that breathed.

We went down through the floorboards, out of the world.

* * *

**Ash**

The stairs went down longer than any cellar should. Quentin carried a lantern that burned green instead of yellow, and the light did strange things to his face—made him look younger, then older, then like someone else entirely, someone I thought maybe he used to be.

I counted my steps the way Mother taught me, one through nine and back again, because nine was the number of the First Tongue and everything true came in nines. At step sixty-three—nine nines—the walls changed. They stopped being brick and started being root. Living root, warm under my fingers, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart.

"This is the vein," Quentin said quietly. He said it like an apology. "The gate runs on it. Ran on it."

"It's barely beating," I said. I could hear it. Everyone from Everwood could hear it the way you hear your own pulse—in the silence between other sounds. It had been slowing my whole life. Mother said the fire was guttering. She said it the way people say a thing they've stopped hoping to be wrong about.

Then the stairs ended, and there was the door.

I had imagined the gate my whole life. Mother described it: two doors of black wood carved with the covenant in the First Tongue, older than kingdoms, older than names. But she never said it would be *small*. Human-sized. Humble. A door you'd pass in any alley and never look twice at, except that every ward on it woke as we approached—sigils flaring along the frame like embers stirred by wind—and spoke aloud in voices like struck bells:

*Who comes? Who comes? Who comes?*

Quentin stepped forward. His voice shook only a little. "Quentin Iversen. Warden of the gate. Fifteen years' keeping."

The bells considered him. I felt them weighing him, the way water weighs a dropped stone.

"And who comes with you?" the door asked.

I stepped up beside him. My pendant burned cold against my chest, colder than it had ever burned, and I lifted it so the scale caught the green light. "Ash," I said. "Born across the threshold. Seal of the covenant."

The silence that followed was the longest of my life.

Then the locks turned—not with the sound of iron but with the sound of a throat clearing after years of disuse—and the doors swung inward, and light poured out that was not light at all but *memory*, thick and golden and smelling of orchards in late summer, and I heard Quentin make a sound like a man being forgiven.

We stepped through together.

* * *

**Quentin**

Fifteen years I had guarded that door and never once opened it wide enough to see what waited on the other side. I checked it each night with a hand on the wood, feeling for the hum, and told myself the hum was enough. Told myself Everwood was fine behind its wall of fading magic, fine the way a patient is fine while the fever climbs.

I was not prepared for grey.

The orchards stretched to the horizon—or where the horizon should have been—and every tree stood bare and silvered, leaves gone, fruit long fallen, bark pale as bone left in sun. They weren't dead. That was the horror of it. I pressed my hand to the nearest trunk and felt it thinking, slow and stubborn, a tree deciding each morning whether today was worth waking for. Grey orchards, but alive. Dying the way old men die in winter rooms: gradually, and with their eyes open.

The sky above was the color of ash, and I mean that literally—it had the texture of ash, a fine drifting graininess, as if the whole vault of heaven were the residue of something burned. No sun. Just a diffuse pallor that cast shadows the wrong way, soft-edged, apologetic.

"Oh," Ash said beside me. Small sound. Terrible sound.

"I'm sorry," I said, because it was the only sentence I owned that fit.

"Don't be sorry," Ash said. "Be angry later. Walk now."

So we walked. The path wound between the trees, and the ground beneath our feet was soft with fifteen years of uncollected leaf-fall, and somewhere ahead a river made a sound rivers don't make—a rushing that ran the wrong direction in my ears, upstream, uphill, backward.

We crested a low rise and saw it: the river, running toward its own source. Water climbing. Fish hanging motionless in the current like thoughts refused. On its banks, willows leaned the wrong way, branches trailing upstream as if combed by a hand moving against time.

And beyond the river, far off, the mountain.

Even dying, it took the breath out of me. It rose from the plain like a fist from a table, black-shouldered, crowned with a glow that should have been constant and was not. The dragon-fire flickered. Once. Twice. A rhythm like a lamp running out of oil, or a heart deciding. Between flickers the mountain went dark, and in the dark I could see the snowline creeping lower than any map I'd memorized said it should.

"That's Ember-that-Waits," Ash whispered. Their hand found mine without either of us arranging it. "That's where the fire lives."

"Lived," I said, and hated myself for the correction, and meant it anyway, because lying had gotten me fifteen safe years and I was done spending them.

Ash squeezed my hand hard enough to hurt. "Lived," they agreed. "And will again."

I looked at the child saying it, and at the mountain refusing to say it back, and I understood—for the first time, truly—that I had spent fifteen years guarding a door out of guilt, and guilt makes a poor watchman. Guilt checks the lock. Love walks through.

"Come on," I said. "Before the light goes."

* * *

**Ash**

Everything smelled like Mother.

That was the worst part, walking through the grey orchard. Not the bare trees, not the backward river, not the sky like burnt paper. The smell. Late apples and woodsmoke and rain-on-root, the exact smell of her kitchen in the village that didn't exist anymore, rising out of the dead ground as if the land itself were dreaming of her.

I walked with my hand in Quentin's and pretended I wasn't crying, and he pretended not to notice, and we were both very good at pretending, which I think is something he learned in Vell and I learned from her.

The village had been three days' walk from the gate. We reached what was left of it by the time the ash-sky dimmed toward whatever Everwood used for evening.

There wasn't much left. There wasn't anything left. That was the point of Unmaking—you didn't get ruins. Ruins remember. You got a clearing where streets had been, foundations like scars, grass growing in the shapes of doorways. The Ministry had come through four years ago, Mother said. White gloves. Lists of names. By morning the village was a rumor and by winter it was nothing at all.

But the orchard trees around the clearing still stood, and they were memory-trees—I could feel it, the way you feel heat from a stove—and I let go of Quentin's hand and crossed to the nearest one before he could stop me.

"Ash—"

"They keep things," I said. "Everyone knows that. Even in Vell you must know that."

"I know," he said. "I know what they keep."

Something in his voice made me look at him. He had gone pale under the ash-light, and he was looking at the trees the way you look at a person you owe money to.

I touched the bough anyway. Children always touch.

The memory that poured into me wasn't mine. It was cold and green and full of shouting, and there was a woman's voice—no, a man's voice, young, terrified—and hands, and a door closing, and a choice being made the wrong way, and I pulled my hand back like I'd touched a stove after all.

Quentin was watching me. Watching my face. And I watched his face change as he realized what I might have just seen, and I watched him decide not to ask, and I decided not to tell him what I'd felt.

Not yet. Mother said: *fear is just the body remembering it's real.* She never said what to do when the thing you're following turns out to have done something terrible. I put my hand back in his anyway. His palm was sweating. Good. Honest.

"We camp here tonight," he said, too brightly. "The trees break the wind. Tomorrow we cross at Sorrow Ford. There's a keeper there who can help us."

"A keeper," I said. "One of the three."

"One of the three," he said. And then, quieter, almost to himself: "She won't want to see me."

"Why not?"

He looked at the backward river, and the flickering mountain, and the sky the color of everything the Ministry had ever burned.

"Because I'm the reason she can't speak," he said.

Above us, the dragon-fire flickered once—twice—and went dark, and the dark lasted three heartbeats too long before it kindled again, weaker, like a promise being renegotiated.

In the morning, we would walk toward it.
