## 7. The Orchard That Remembers

The trees were wrong.

I knew trees. I had climbed them in the village before the white gloves came, had slept in their forks with my cheek against bark that smelled of rain and beetle-burrow and the green dark under leaves. Trees grew things. Apples, plums, the bitter red berries Mother boiled into tea when my throat went raw from speaking words that made the inspectors write in their books.

These trees grew nothing I could eat.

They stood in long rows, grey-limbed, patient as gravestones, and where fruit should have hung there hung instead small pale shapes like eggs, like closed eyes, like lanterns waiting for someone to light them. When the wind moved through—and the wind here moved strangely, sliding sideways along the ground instead of falling from the sky—the shapes swayed on their stems and made a sound no wind should make.

A sound like a room where many people are sleeping.

"Ash." Quentin was already walking down the row, his coat pulled tight, his eyes doing the thing they did when he wanted to look at something but also wanted very much not to. "Stay close. Don't touch anything."

That was the first thing he'd said all morning that wasn't about rivers or fords or how far we still had to go. So of course it was the thing I thought about most.

"What are they?" I asked.

"Memories." He said it the way you'd say *mud* or *weather*—a thing too ordinary to be frightening. "This is a memory orchard. The old covenant-keepers planted it. Every tree holds what someone gave up, or what someone couldn't carry anymore. They store it so it isn't lost."

"Lost where?"

He didn't answer. He had stopped at the end of the row and was looking back the way we'd come, toward the gate behind us and Vell beyond it, and his face had the shut look, the door-closed look, the one I had learned to read in a year of asking strangers about keepers. Everyone in Everwood had that look somewhere on them. It was the look of people who had survived something by putting it in a box, and who checked the lock every hour without knowing they did it.

I touched nothing. But I looked.

* * *

We walked through the orchard all morning because Quentin said the road to Sorrow Ford ran through it and around it would take two days we didn't have. The rows went on longer than rows should. The light came from everywhere and nowhere, grey-gold, the color of the inside of an eyelid. My bare feet found the ground soft, softer than any forest floor, and warm—warm like something living just beneath the soil, breathing slow.

The trees hummed.

Not out loud. Not exactly. It was more that if I stood near one too long, I began to hear things. A woman laughing in a kitchen. Rain on a roof that belonged to someone. The smell of bread, arriving from nowhere, gone again before I could turn my head. Each tree was full of somebody's yesterdays, and the yesterdays leaked, the way warmth leaks from a house with bad walls.

Quentin walked fast between them. Faster than he needed to. He kept his hands in his pockets and his shoulders up around his ears, and whenever a humming tree leaned its branches over the path—as some of them did, gently, the way a dog leans against a leg—he stepped away from the touch.

"You've been here before," I said.

"I've passed through."

"When?"

"A long time ago."

"With Wren?"

His step hitched. Just half a beat, a stone in his shoe that wasn't there. "Eat your bread," he said, and handed me the heel of the loaf he'd bought in Vell, the last food from the world I'd been born in and would probably never see again, and I ate it because I was hungry and because I was learning that Quentin answered questions with bread.

But I watched him. Watching him was becoming a habit, the way counting was. In the village I used to count things when I was scared—fence posts, stars, the seconds between lightning and thunder. Now I counted Quentin: the number of times he checked the sky for the flickering mountain (eleven since dawn), the number of times he said *stay close* (four), the number of times his hand drifted to the floorboard-shaped scar of absence at his hip where the gate-key used to hang (I stopped counting).

A man who checks a lock that often is a man hiding something behind it.

* * *

It happened in the middle of the orchard, where the rows widened into a clearing and the oldest tree stood alone.

I knew it was the oldest the way you know a grandmother in a line of mothers—not from anything visible, but from the weight of it, the way the air bent toward it. Its trunk was wide as a door. Its branches spread over the whole clearing like a roof, and from every branch hung hundreds of the pale memory-shapes, more than any other tree carried, dense as grapes, and every single one of them was humming the same note.

The same name, almost. A sound like *Wren*.

Quentin stopped at the edge of the clearing and would come no farther.

"We'll rest here," he said, and his voice had gone thin. "Then the ford by dusk."

"Why won't you come under it?"

"It's not mine," he said. Which was a lie. I didn't know yet that it was a lie, but I felt it land wrong in my ear, the way a false coin feels wrong in your palm, and I filed it away with the other wrong-feeling things: *she can't speak because of me*, *a long time ago*, *eat your bread*.

I sat against the roots instead. The roots were warm. And the tree—well.

Nobody told me the trees could reach.

* * *

The bough came down the way sleep comes down. One moment I was chewing the last of the bread; the next there was a branch beside my shoulder, low and slow, its pale fruits swinging like censers, and the nearest one brushed my hair.

And I fell into Quentin.

I didn't know whose memory it was at first. That's the strange mercy of it—you arrive inside a yesterday and it fits you like a coat whether it was cut for you or not. I was standing in a place I had never seen, on a night I had never lived, wearing a body that was not mine and was unmistakably his.

Rain. A hill above a gate. Torches below, dozens of them, and men in white gloves moving through the torchlight with the terrible calm of people doing paperwork. The Ministry had come to close the gate for good, and the closing required a price, and the price was written in the First Tongue on a stone tablet that I could feel under my hands because Quentin's hands were on it, and his heart—I had his heart now, beating in his chest—was going like a rabbit's.

*I can't,* he was thinking. The thinking was loud, louder than the rain, the way thoughts get when a body is terrified. *I can't lose it. Fifteen years. Fifteen years of mornings, of the shop, of learning her laugh, of—*

*Ash.*

No. Not Ash. The other name. The one the whole orchard hummed.

Wren came up the hill through the rain, and I saw her face, and I understood in one blow why the tree sang her name. She was young then, younger than Quentin, with mud to her knees and rain flattening her hair and eyes that held the particular brightness of someone who has already decided to do a brave thing and is only looking for the moment to do it in.

"The covenant takes what the keeper loves most," she said. Not a question. She'd read the tablet too.

"There has to be another way—" His voice. My mouth. I felt the lie forming in his throat before it became words, felt it the way you feel a sneeze coming.

"There isn't. You know there isn't." She smiled, and the smile was kind, and the kindness of it was the worst thing in the whole memory. "You built the shop, Quentin. You built the life. You have fifteen years of reasons to stay. I have a river and a boat and no one waiting anywhere." She put her hand flat on the tablet, over his hand. "Let me be the keeper. Let me pay."

And here is the thing I will carry longer than any scar:

He let her.

There was a moment—a real one, a door-sized one—where he could have said no. Where he could have gripped the tablet and said *the price is mine, I am the warden, take my memories, take my shop, take everything.* I felt that moment open in his chest like a doorway, and I felt him look through it, and I felt him count what was on the other side—her face, her laugh, fifteen years of her—and then I felt him step back from the door and let it swing shut.

"I'm sorry," he said. And he meant it. That was the horror of it. He meant it with his whole coward's heart, and he stepped aside anyway, and Wren walked past him down the hill into the torchlight with her chin up, and the gate drank her voice, and the gate drank her name, and the gate sealed with a sound like a book closing, and Quentin stood in the rain and felt—

Relief.

Underneath the guilt, underneath the grief, down at the bottom where nobody looks: relief, warm and shameful as a stolen coat. *It wasn't me. I still have my mornings. I still remember her.*

The memory ended there. The tree let me go.

* * *

I came back to myself sitting against the roots with my hands shaking and bread crumbs on my knee and Quentin crouched in front of me saying my name.

"Ash. Ash. What happened? Did you touch—"

I looked at his face.

It was the same face. Mud-freckled, tired, thirty-some years of careful living written around the eyes. The face of the man who had opened his door to a barefoot stranger at midnight because the wards sang and he couldn't pretend not to hear. The face that had fed me soup, and lied to an inspector with white gloves, and walked backward through a dying world because a child asked him to.

And it was the face from the hill. The face that had stepped back from the door.

"Ash?"

"The tree showed me something," I said.

Everything in him went still. Not calm-still. Hunted-still. "What did it show you?"

"Someone," I said. "A woman. On a hill. In the rain."

I watched him understand. I watched it move through him like cold water through a pipe—first the eyes, then the jaw, then the hands, which curled slowly closed on his knees. He knew exactly which night the tree kept. Of course he did. You don't forget the worst thing you ever did. You water it. You plant it in the middle of an orchard and hang a hundred lanterns around it and you walk past it fast with your hands in your pockets for fifteen years.

"That tree is old," he said quietly. "Sometimes it shows strangers things that aren't theirs. Things it shouldn't."

"Was it true?"

The question hung between us. Above us the pale fruits swung and hummed their one note, *Wren, Wren*, patient as rain.

"Does it matter?" he said. "What matters is the ford, and Maren, and the fire going out, and—"

"Was it true?"

He looked at me for a long time. And I want to say, in fairness, whatever else I say about him after this: he did not lie. I saw the lie assemble itself behind his teeth—I saw it, whole and ready, *no, child, the tree lies, trees are tricksy*—and I watched him choose not to say it. Maybe because lying to me was one door too many even for him. Maybe because he was tired of carrying it alone. Maybe both.

"Yes," he said. "It was true."

The word went through me like the scale-pendant goes through cold: burning the wrong direction.

"She gave everything," I said. "And you let her. Because you were afraid."

"Yes."

"And she can't speak. And she doesn't remember her own name. Because of you."

"Yes." His voice cracked on it, quietly, like ice under a boot. "Yes. All of it. Every word."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to; my legs decided, the way they'd decided to run from the white gloves, the way a body decides things when the mind is still arguing. I put six feet between us. Seven. The width of the path.

Quentin stayed crouched where he was, and he didn't follow, and somehow that was worse—if he'd reached for me I could have hated him cleanly. Instead he just knelt there among the roots of his own worst night, an older man in a wet coat, and waited to find out what I would do, the way you wait outside a door you've locked yourself out of.

"I followed rumors of you for a year," I said. "The keeper. The one who kept the last door. People said you were brave."

"People say a lot of things about charm sellers."

"They're wrong, then."

He flinched. Small, but I saw it. I had spent a year learning to read adults the way Mother taught me to read weather, and I had never once seen Quentin flinch until now.

"I'm not going to leave you," he said. "Whatever you think of me now. I want that understood. The fire is dying, and you're the seal, and I am—whatever I am—but I am not leaving you in this orchard or any other place. You can hate me the whole way to the mountain. Hate travels well."

"I don't hate you," I said, and it was true, and that frightened me more than hatred would have. "That's what scares me. Yesterday I trusted you with everything. Today I know what you did. And I still—" My hands made fists. "How does that work? How can both be true?"

He rose slowly, joints cracking, and looked past me at the great tree and its hundred humming lanterns.

"Fifteen years," he said, "I've asked myself a version of that every single day. I don't have an answer. I have a road, and a ford, and a woman downstream who has every right to drown me, and a fire on a mountain that's going out." He picked up his pack. "Come or don't come. But come."

* * *

We walked the rest of the orchard in silence.

The trees hummed their stolen yesterdays around us—kitchens, laughter, rain on roofs—and I kept my arms tight against my sides and let none of them touch me. Once, near the orchard's edge, a low branch dipped toward Quentin himself, one pale fruit swinging level with his face, and I watched him stop, and look at it, and I swear the fruit glowed a little warmer, the way a dog perks when its person passes.

He didn't touch it. He walked on.

At the treeline the ground began to slope down toward the sound of water running the wrong way, and the air changed, and I smelled the ford before I saw it: river-smell and iron and something older underneath, something like grief that had been sitting out in the sun a long time.

"There," Quentin said. "Sorrow Ford."

And standing in the shallows, waist-deep in the backward water, was a broad grey woman with a ferry-pole and no voice at all, and when she turned and saw who was coming down the bank, the river around her went suddenly, perfectly still—

—and then it showed us, plain as daylight on its surface, a hill, a rainstorm, and a young woman walking down into torchlight while a man stepped back from a door.

Maren looked at Quentin.

Quentin looked at the water, and did not look away, and said, "Hello, Maren. I expect you already know why I've come."
