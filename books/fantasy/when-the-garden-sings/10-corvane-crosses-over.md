## 10. Corvane Crosses Over

The sigil woke at midnight.

Inspector Corvane felt it the way a man feels a tooth begin to ache—faintly, distantly, and with the absolute certainty that it would get worse. He was in his office on the fourth floor of the Ministry of Unmaking, working late as always, cataloguing the week's confiscations: eleven luck-knots, three fever-charms, one music box that played a tune no one had written. He set down his pen.

On his desk, in a lacquered box lined with white silk, the listening-sigil pulsed. A soft grey light, like breath on a window. It had been silent for six days, since he'd left it beneath the windowsill of Quentin Iversen's shop, tucked behind a loose brick where no honest man would ever look.

Six days of nothing. And now this.

Corvane opened the box. The sigil lay there like a coin of pressed ash, and as he watched, it spoke—not in sound, but in resonance, the way a tuning fork speaks to another fork across a room. He closed his eyes and let it pour into him.

*Old words. A child's voice. The First Tongue.*

And beneath it, fainter, the groan of hinges that had not moved in fifteen years.

Corvane sat very still for a long moment. Then he rang the small brass bell on his desk, and when his junior clerk appeared—sleep-rumpled, apologetic—he said only, "Prepare the crossing kit. And wake Director Hale. Tell her the gate under Vell is open."

"Open, sir? I thought—"

"So did I," Corvane said. "That was our first mistake."

* * *

He dressed carefully, as he did for everything. Grey coat, white gloves, boots polished to a dull shine that reflected nothing. In the Ministry's early days—the days his grandmother had spoken of in whispers—the inspectors had worn red, and had enjoyed it. Corvane considered enjoyment a professional failing. Red invited passion, and passion made men sloppy. Sloppy men burned villages by accident. Corvane had never once burned anything by accident.

The crossing kit weighed less than people expected. Most imagined the Ministry's tools as instruments of terror: black irons, unmaking-hooks, the long silver shears they used on particularly stubborn enchantments. But the kit contained mostly paper. Contracts, mostly. Blank ones. Corvane had learned long ago that the most powerful magic left in the world was the kind a person signed away willingly.

Director Hale met him in the underground corridor beneath the Ministry, where the old sealed doors stood in their alcoves like teeth pulled from a jaw. She was seventy, sharp as a paper cut, and she looked at him the way she always did—as though measuring him for a coffin she'd already paid for.

"You're certain it's Iversen's gate?"

"The sigil doesn't lie. It's tuned to resonance, not speech. Someone crossed."

"Then burn him," Hale said flatly. "Burn the shop, burn the child if there's a child, salt the floorboards, and seal the corridor behind you. We've managed fifteen years without an incident. I see no reason to begin having them now."

Corvane folded his gloves together, finger to finger. "With respect, Director—that was our second mistake."

Hale raised one eyebrow. It was an expression she'd practiced for forty years and it still worked.

"Fifteen years ago we didn't close the covenant," Corvane said. "We *paused* it. There's a difference, and the difference is standing in a dying world right now, getting hungrier. The fire under Everwood hasn't gone out. It's been starving. And everything that starves eventually either dies or comes looking for food." He paused. "A child came looking for food, Director. Speaking the First Tongue. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means Iversen has been negligent."

"It means the seal is *alive*," Corvane said, and heard his own voice go quiet, the way it did only when he was entirely certain of something. "It means the covenant grew itself a keeper. And it means that everything we planned—the Harvest, the Long Account, all of it—is suddenly on a schedule."

Hale was silent for a moment. Somewhere down the corridor, water dripped from the vaulted ceiling, patient as a clock.

"And if I refuse you?"

"Then I'll go alone, and I'll be right, and you'll have wasted the advantage of surprise." He said it without heat, which was, he'd found, the only way to say difficult things. "Director, I have served this Ministry for twenty-two years. I have never asked you for anything but precision. I'm asking now. Let me cross."

Hale studied him for a long time. Then she took the blank contract from her own coat—the one every director carried, the one signed in advance by the Chancellor—and held it out.

"Bring back the seal," she said. "Alive, if convenient. Dead, if necessary. But bring it back, Corvane. Everything depends on it."

"I know," Corvane said, and took the contract. "That's what worries me about you."

* * *

The gate beneath Iversen's shop was smaller than legend claimed. Corvane had read the old reports—there were always old reports; the Ministry kept records the way dragons kept gold—and the reports described a doorway tall enough for a giant, blazing with ward-light, humming with the accumulated song of two worlds.

What he found was a trapdoor under rotten floorboards, a ladder of iron rungs, and a stone archway barely taller than himself, its surface crawling with faded script. The air around it tasted of copper and rain. And through the arch, instead of brick, there was a country.

Corvane stood at the threshold for a full minute before crossing. This too was discipline. Men who rushed through doors died behind them.

The world beyond was grey.

Not dark—grey. An orchard stretched to the horizon, trees heavy with fruit that had no color, leaves that hung motionless in air that should have been moving. A river ran past the archway, and Corvane watched it for some time before he understood what was wrong with it: it was flowing uphill. Away from the sea. Toward the mountain on the horizon, where a smudge of light flickered like a lamp running out of oil.

"Well," he said softly, to no one. "There you are."

He had spent his whole life being told that Everwood was a threat. That beyond the gate lay a realm of wild magic that would devour Vell given half a chance—that the Ministry's founders had sealed it away to save humanity from being drowned in wonder and eaten by it. Every schoolchild in Vell could recite it. *The Unmaking keeps us safe.*

Standing here, Corvane understood finally that everyone had misunderstood. Not lied—misunderstood. The founders hadn't sealed a wound. They'd clamped a hand over a mouth that was singing.

He took out his instruments and began to work.

The listening-sigil had done its job; now he planted its siblings—a ring of five small stones around the archway, each attuned to a different frequency of resonance. When the last one settled into the soil, they began to hum, very quietly, in harmony. Corvane listened to the harmony for a while, eyes closed, white gloves hanging loose at his sides.

Then he laughed. Once. Quietly. It startled even him.

Because the harmony was *dying*, yes—but dying the way a song dies when the singer runs out of breath, not the way a machine dies when the fuel runs out. It could be fed. It wanted to be fed. And the Ministry had spent a century building the machinery to do exactly that, on exactly this, if anyone had ever thought to ask the question.

The Harvest wasn't destruction. It had never been destruction. The Ministry's founders had built the Unmaking-engines to *draw* power, not erase it—to siphon the covenant's resonance out of Everwood and store it, refine it, feed it into Vell's foundations until the human city became what Everwood had once been: permanent. Ageless. Immortal.

One realm would live forever. The other would finish dying, gently, completely, and be forgotten.

Corvane opened his eyes and found that he was smiling, which was so unusual that he checked, with one gloved finger, to make sure it was really his face doing it.

*Mercy,* he thought. *This is mercy. We simply never had the courage to name it.*

* * *

He followed the trail for two days.

They were not subtle, the warden and the child. Iversen had lived in Vell so long he'd forgotten how to move through a living world—his footprints stayed too long in soft ground, his camps showed the ash of careful, lawful fires. And the child—Corvane tracked the child by the flowers.

That was the thing he wrote in his report that night, sitting against a grey tree with his coat buttoned to the chin: wherever the child walked, things *leaned toward them*. Not grew—leaned. Grass bent. Branches reached. On the second morning Corvane found a dead orchard row where every tree within ten paces of their campsite had put out a single bud, pale and futile, like old men turning toward a window.

The living seal. The covenant grown into flesh and sent across the threshold to find help.

Corvane catalogued the observation without sentiment, because sentiment was a professional failing. But he found, filing it away, that his hands were not quite steady, and he examined that too, and concluded it was the cold.

On the third day he caught up with them at a river crossing—an actual river, running the correct direction, wide and slow and brown with silt. They were on the far bank. Iversen was arguing with a ferry-woman who never spoke, gesturing at something in the water, and the child stood beside him holding a pendant up to the light.

Corvane crouched in the reeds and watched.

He watched the water go still. He watched the ferry-woman's face change. He watched Iversen kneel in the mud of the riverbank like a man at a graveside, and he watched the water rise around his knees—not violently, not threateningly, but the way a dog rises to greet someone it has missed—and he watched the water *show him something*. Corvane couldn't see what. But he saw Iversen's face while he looked at it, and Iversen's face was the face of a man being forgiven, which was worse than the face of a man being punished, because forgiveness meant the water loved him, and love was resonance, and resonance was—

Resonance was exactly what the Ministry needed. Exactly what the Harvest required. A covenant this alive, this *willing*, this desperate to be saved—it would refine beautifully. It would give and give and give. The people of Hollowmere had traded their memories for safety and called it peace; the whole of Everwood would trade its soul for survival and call it mercy, because Corvane would be gentle about it. He would explain. He was very good at explaining.

He withdrew from the reeds without a sound and made his notes by the light of a shielded lamp.

*Subject demonstrates capacity for voluntary resonance transfer. Covenant-keepers responsive to emotional stimulus rather than coercive force. Recommend revised approach: negotiation before Unmaking. The asset may cooperate fully if properly motivated.*

He underlined *properly motivated* twice, then crossed it out and wrote instead: *if shown kindness.*

Because that was the truth of it, the truth he'd carried for twenty-two years like a stone in his boot: the Ministry's error had never been cruelty. Cruelty was inefficient. The error was fear. The founders had been afraid of Everwood, and fear had made them crude—they'd built engines of erasure when they should have built engines of *invitation*. You didn't need to unmake a dying world. You needed to help it die well, and take what it offered, and give the survivors—Vell, humanity, the future—everything.

Everwood was a garden at the end of autumn. The Ministry had spent a century trying to burn it down. Corvane intended to harvest it.

He thought of his mother, who had died at fifty-three of a wasting sickness any charm-seller in the outlawed quarter could have eased, and who had refused, to her last breath, because the law was the law and she was an honest woman. He had knelt at her bedside and promised her that no one else would ever have to make that choice. That the choice itself would be *unmade*.

He had been twenty-four. He had joined the Ministry within the year.

People assumed men like Corvane hated magic. He didn't. He pitied it—the way one pities a beautiful animal born into an age with no room for it. Magic was real, and real things deserved dignity, and the greatest indignity the world had ever committed was forcing wonder to survive in scraps: luck-knots sold in back rooms, fever-charms hidden under floorboards, a warden counting days alone in a shop full of small mercies he couldn't admit to.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years Quentin Iversen had lived a half-life so that two worlds could live a quarter-life each. Corvane had read the file. He knew about Wren. He knew about the betrayal at the closing. He knew precisely what the man had lost, and he felt—genuinely, sincerely—something adjacent to respect.

Which was why, when he finally stepped out of the treeline onto the riverbank, he did it slowly, with his hands visible, and his voice pitched to carry without carrying menace.

"Master Iversen," he said. "Don't run. Please. I've come a very long way to talk."

* * *

Iversen turned, and Corvane watched the recognition land—the way the man's shoulders dropped before his spine stiffened, the way his body betrayed him in stages, hope first, then horror. Behind him, the ferry-woman went utterly still, and the child turned, and the pendant in the child's fist flared cold enough that frost bloomed on the reeds between them.

"Corvane." Iversen's voice was flat. "How?"

"A brick behind your windowsill was loose. You should have it seen to." Corvane stopped a courteous distance away—ten paces, no closer, close enough to speak, far enough to honor. "I'm not here to arrest you. I want you to know that first. Whatever you decide in the next hour, no one is going to burn your shop."

"That's not yours to promise."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "But I keep my promises, and Director Hale keeps hers, and between us we control most of what burns in Vell." He let his gaze move, briefly, deliberately, to the child. Frost still feathering outward from the scale. Eyes too old for the face. "So. This is the seal."

"This is a child," Iversen said, stepping sideways, just slightly, just enough. "Ash. Their name is Ash."

"Ash," Corvane repeated, and inclined his head, formally, the way one greets a foreign dignitary—because that was what the child was, wasn't it? An ambassador from a dying nation. "Ash. I've read about you. Not you specifically—you, the *seal*. Born across the threshold. Half of one world and half of another. Do you know what you are, in the Ministry's ledgers? You're listed as an anomaly. A rounding error. A thing that shouldn't exist." He smiled, and made it a kind one; he'd practiced kindness in mirrors the way other men practiced threats. "I've always thought that was cruel. Nothing that exists should be called a mistake."

Behind Iversen, the river had begun to move again—slowly, watchfully, like an audience leaning forward.

"What do you want, Corvane?" Iversen asked.

"The same thing you want." Corvane spread his hands, white gloves open, empty. "Look around you, Master Iversen. Truly look. Your sky is the color of dishwater. Your rivers run backward. Your mountain is guttering like a candle in a doorway. This world is dying, and it has been dying for fifteen years, and everyone who might have helped it has been hiding in a shop in Vell selling luck-knots." He let that sit. "I'm not here to gloat. I'm here to offer the only honest solution anyone has proposed in a century."

"There is no honest solution that ends with you harvesting a living world."

"*Harvesting*," Corvane said, and shook his head, genuinely saddened. "You make it sound like theft. Master Iversen—when a garden is dying at the end of the season, is the gardener a thief for gathering what remains? When a patient is terminal, is the doctor a murderer for easing the passage?" He took one step forward—nine paces now—and the frost on the reeds crackled louder, and the river's surface went mirror-still. "Everwood cannot be saved. You must know that. Even your keepers know that—the stone, the chain, whatever relics you're collecting. Relics don't restart hearts. But the *power* in this place, the resonance, the covenant—it can be saved. Moved. Given to a world that will use it. Vell lives forever, and Everwood gets to stop suffering. That isn't conquest. That's euthanasia with an inheritance."

The child spoke then. Not in the common tongue—in the First Tongue, the old words rolling out low and clear, and Corvane felt them hit him like weather. He didn't speak the language. Almost no one in the Ministry did; it had been unmade from the curriculum decades ago. But the sigils in his pockets resonated anyway, all five of them, singing faintly in his coat, and he understood—dimly, the way one understands thunder—that the child had just told him *no*.

"I know," Corvane said gently, to Ash, in Vellish. "I know it hurts. Children always understand death better than adults pretend they do. But I'm offering your world a good death, Ash. A *dignified* one. And I'm offering your friend—" a nod at Iversen— "an end to fifteen years of lying in the dark. No more census-eves. No more floorboards. No more counting days since the gate last hummed. Doesn't that sound like rest?"

Iversen's face did something complicated. Corvane catalogued it: guilt, fury, and underneath both, horribly, a flicker of temptation. Good. Honest men were easiest to negotiate with, because they already knew the price of everything.

"You're wrong," Iversen said quietly. "About all of it. The fire doesn't need to be harvested, Corvane. It needs to be *fed*. There's a difference, and the difference is going to bury you."

"The fire is a metaphor, Master Iversen. Fire is what men call it when they don't understand thermodynamics."

"The fire is a *dragon*," Iversen said, "and it is waiting, and it remembers your Ministry, and when it wakes it is not going to distinguish between the men who starved it and the men who apologized."

For the first time, Corvane hesitated.

Only for a moment—a half-breath, a hairline crack in twenty-two years of certainty. Because he had read the old reports too, the deep ones, the sealed ones, and somewhere in the oldest of them was a phrase he'd always dismissed as founder's poetry: *the fire that waits is the fire that judges.*

Then he straightened his coat, and the crack sealed, because Corvane was not a man who permitted himself doubt on riverbanks in dying worlds.

"Dragons are extinct," he said. "The reports confirm—"

"The reports were written by your founders," Iversen said, "who never came this far in. Corvane—go home. Take your stones out of my riverbank and go home. I'm asking you. As one tired man to another."

Corvane looked at him for a long moment. Then at the child, who stared back with those old, old eyes, unafraid, which unsettled him more than any threat had. Then at the ferry-woman, whose mouth never moved but whose silence somehow filled the entire valley like a held note.

"No," he said at last, and it was almost tender. "I don't think I will. You see, Master Iversen—I made a promise at a deathbed once. And I have never, in twenty-two years, broken a promise." He began to withdraw, backward, unhurried, hands still visible, a courteous man leaving a dinner party. "You have three days' start. Use them well. And when this is over—when Vell is immortal and this poor grey garden is finally allowed to rest—remember that I offered you kindness first. History will call me many things. I intend to deserve at least one of them."

He was nearly to the treeline when the child's voice stopped him—one word, in the First Tongue, hurled across the water like a stone.

He didn't know what it meant.

But every sigil in his coat sang at once, high and sharp, and the river rose a hand's width against its banks, and Corvane walked into the grey trees knowing—with the calm, terrible certainty of a man who believes he is the kindest person in any room—that he had just been *warned*.

He filed the word away. He would find someone to translate it.

And in the morning, he sent the signal flare up through the gate: *crossing confirmed, seal located, send the Harvest engines.*

Three days, he had promised them.

He gave them two.
