## 11. The Smith Who Hates Dragons

The Cinderholds did not look like a place where anyone had ever been happy.

Ash had expected fire. Everyone said the dwarves kept forges burning day and night, that the mountain glowed red at its seams like a banked heart. But the passes into the holds were cold, and grey, and the only light came from the sky itself—a bruised amber smear above the peaks where the dragon-fire mountain flickered on the horizon, guttering like a lamp running out of oil.

Quentin walked ahead of them, hunched in a borrowed coat Maren had fished from her ferry-house, and he hadn't spoken in hours. Wren walked beside Ash without speaking too, which was different—Wren's silence had weight to it, like a held breath. Since the river, since the stone, she'd been watching Quentin the way you watch a door you're not sure is locked.

Ash watched everything. That was what Ash did. Watched, and listened, and kept the words of the First Tongue folded up inside like a coat too warm for the weather.

*You are the seal,* the river had shown them. *You are the hinge.* Ash still didn't entirely know what that meant, except that when they spoke the old words, the world leaned in to listen.

"Tell me again," Quentin said finally, his breath smoking, "what we're walking into."

"A smith," Ash said. "Who forged the chain."

"You know him?"

"No." Ash kicked a loose stone off the path and watched it clatter down into the dark. "But my mother knew the songs. The Chain-Singer, they called him. Brannoc Under-Mountain. He made the first link with his own hands, before I was born, when the covenant was new."

"And now?"

"Now he hates dragons."

The word landed in the silence between them and stayed there.

"Why?" Wren asked. It was one of the few questions she'd asked since Hollowmere. Her voice had come back strange—not her own yet, borrowed pieces of other people's voices stitched together, but it worked.

"Nobody says why," Ash said. "That's how I know it's bad. When something's small, people tell you all about it. When it's big, they go quiet."

Quentin made a sound that might have been a laugh if it hadn't hurt so much coming out. "You sound like your mother."

Ash didn't answer. The path bent around a shoulder of black rock, and there it was: the Cinderholds.

Not fire after all. Not anymore. The great forge-gates stood open, and behind them the halls were dim, lit only by scattered coals raked into patterns on the floors—the way you keep embers alive when you've decided, for now, to stop making anything new. Dwarves moved through the gloom without looking at the strangers. Their beards were grey. Their hands hung empty at their sides.

A hold full of smiths who had stopped smithing.

"It's the fire," Ash realized aloud. "It's going out everywhere. Even here. They can feel it."

"They can feel it dying," Quentin corrected quietly. "There's a difference."

At the far end of the entry hall, one forge still burned.

It was a huge thing, big as a house, and the dwarf working it didn't work like a craftsman—he worked like a man fighting. His hammer came down and down and down on a bar of metal that had clearly already taken a hundred strikes too many, and sparks flew off it in angry bursts, and his beard was singed at the edges and his eyes, when he looked up, were the red-rimmed eyes of someone who has not slept in days and does not intend to start.

He saw them. He put the hammer down. The hall got very quiet.

"You," he said, in a voice like a rockslide deciding which way to fall. "Human. And—" his gaze found the pendant at Ash's throat, and everything in his face closed like a gate, "—that."

"We've come about the chain," Quentin began. "The covenant-chain. We need—"

"I know what you need." Brannoc Under-Mountain came around the forge, and up close he was enormous, scarred from wrist to shoulder, and he smelled of smoke and iron and grief. "Everyone who comes here needs something. The river-keeper sent word ahead—yes, she did, don't look surprised, the water talks faster than any bird. She says the seal is walking. She says the fire is failing. She says the chain must be reforged." He spat into the coals. "She forgot to say why I should care."

"The realms will die," Quentin said. "Both of them."

"Good."

The word dropped like an anvil. Ash heard Quentin stop breathing for half a second.

"Excuse me?" Quentin managed.

"You heard me, warden." Brannoc's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part. It just kept falling, slow and heavy, like something being lowered into a grave. "I spent two hundred years keeping a pact between worlds because the dragons asked it of me. Two hundred years of forge-work and oath-work, and do you know what I got? I got a daughter with fire in her blood and a mountain to guard, and one morning the Ministry men came over the pass with their white gloves and their harvesting engines, and my daughter stood between them and the fire-mountain because that is what the covenant *asked* of her." His jaw worked. "And the dragon watched her die."

Silence.

"The dragon watched," he repeated, softer now, which was worse. "Ember-that-Waits, oldest of the old, keeper of the last flame. It could have burned those men to shadows. One breath. One. Instead it stayed coiled on its hoard and obeyed the old law—*the fire does not fight, the fire endures*—and my Sefa died on the mountainside holding a hammer she forged herself, and the great wise ancient dragon *watched*." He turned away. "So no, human. Let the realms die. Let the fire go out. Let the whole rotten bargain burn. I'm done being kind to dragons."

Ash stepped forward.

Quentin's hand came out to stop them—and missed. Wren caught Quentin's arm instead, or maybe she was catching Ash's; it was hard to tell, the way they'd all become one rope of people holding each other back.

Ash walked up to the smith until they had to tilt their head all the way back to see his face. They were very small and he was very large and the space between them smelled like a burned house.

"My mother died too," Ash said.

Brannoc looked down. For a moment his expression didn't change at all, and then something flickered behind it, quick as a spark off bad iron.

"Don't," he said.

"She spoke the First Tongue. The Ministry came to our village in the night and unmade it, every house, every name, everyone who remembered us. I ran for a year following stories about a keeper. My mother told me about the Chain-Singer. She said if everything else failed, find the man who made the first link, because a maker always knows how to mend what he made."

"I said *don't*—"

"You hate dragons," Ash went on, stubborn as water finding a crack, "because one watched your daughter die instead of saving her. I understand that. I've thought about hating things every single day since my village stopped existing. It would be so easy. Hate is warm. Hate keeps you busy." They reached up—their hand so small against his forearm, scarred and hot from the forge—and touched the dragon-scale pendant at their own throat, then pressed it, gently, into the smith's enormous palm.

Brannoc flinched like he'd been struck.

The scale was warm. Dragon-warm. And it sang—that low, subterranean hum that Quentin had heard once under his floorboards and never forgotten—and in the hollow of the smith's calloused hand it did what it had done for no one else in all the long years of its carrying.

It showed him something.

Later, Ash couldn't say whether it was the scale or the child or the mountain itself reaching through—but Brannoc stood absolutely still, staring at nothing, and his breath went ragged, and the hammer slipped from his other hand and rang against the floor like a bell tolling.

"What did you see?" Ash whispered.

The smith's lips moved. Nothing came out. Then:

"Sefa." His voice cracked clean through. "On the mountain. She wasn't—she was facing *away* from the dragon. Facing down the pass. And there were Ministry engines, more than we knew, and she—" His fist closed around the scale, knuckles white. "She wasn't guarding the dragon's hoard. She was guarding the *fire*. From *them*. And Ember-that-Waits—" the great shoulders shuddered, "—it couldn't leave the flame. The law binds it to the fire. If it had moved to save her, the mountain would have gone dark right then and there, and everyone, everything, both worlds—"

He sat down. A dwarf that size, that old, that furious, simply folded onto the floor of his own forge hall with a dead daughter's story finally true in his hands, and wept the way stone weeps—slowly, terribly, without any sound at first, and then all at once.

Wren crouched beside him. She didn't touch him. She just stayed, the way you stay with someone whose grief is bigger than the room.

Quentin stood apart, arms wrapped around himself, wearing the face of a man doing arithmetic he hates: *another debt, another cost, another thing the covenant took.*

Ash knelt in front of the smith and waited.

It took a long time. The coals shifted. Somewhere deep in the holds, water dripped in the dark like a clock counting nothing.

Finally Brannoc raised his head. His eyes were ruined, red and wet, but underneath the ruin something had turned over—something that had been lying face-down for years, and now, painfully, faced the light.

"If I reforge the chain," he rasped, "it'll want a price. It always wants a price. What does it want from me, seal-child? What's left to take?"

"I don't know," Ash said honestly. "But I know it doesn't take things to hurt you. It takes things so the fire has something to burn." They paused. "My mother taught me that. She learned it from the songs. Your songs."

The smith stared at the child for a long moment. Then he laughed—one broken, astonished note of it, like a bell with a crack in it that still rings.

"Your mother," he said, "sounds like she'd have argued with a mountain and won."

"She usually did."

Brannoc pushed himself up off the floor. He was older than he'd seemed standing—older than mountains, maybe, or just tired the way only makers get tired—and he looked at the cold forges lining the dark hall, one by one, like a general reviewing soldiers he thought he'd lost.

Then he raised his voice, and it filled the whole hold, and it cracked and broke and did not stop:

"Hearths up! Rake the coals! Every forge in the Cinderholds—I want fire in this mountain tonight if I have to breathe it there myself!" And to the three of them, quieter: "The third keeper. Where?"

Ash and Quentin exchanged a glance. Quentin's face did the thing it did when a lie was loading.

"Hollowmere," Ash said, before he could.

The smith went still. "Hollowmere." He said it the way you say the name of a disease. "The city that traded its memory to the white-gloved devils. Nobody comes out of Hollowmere, seal-child. Whatever keeper you think is in there, whatever face they wear—they aren't them anymore. You'd be walking into an empty shell and asking it to save the world."

"She's in there," Ash said. "Wren."

The name hit the room like the hammer had hit the floor.

Brannoc looked at Quentin. Then at Wren. Back at Quentin. Something old and sharp moved across his face—recognition, and pity, and the particular grim knowledge of a man who has read enough grief to know how this chapter goes.

"Wren," he repeated slowly. "*That* Wren. The warden's mentor. The one the covenant took." He shook his head, and it was almost gentle. "Boy, whoever you're hoping to find in that city, hope smaller."

"I know," Quentin said, and his voice was thin and steady and terrible. "Believe me. I know exactly what's waiting for me in Hollowmere."

Brannoc studied him a moment longer. Then he turned to his dead forge, seized the poker, and drove it into the coals—and the coals, impossibly, reluctantly, caught.

"Two days to temper the chain-links," he said. "Then we walk to Hollowmere together. All of us." He glanced back, firelight climbing his ruined face. "Because if the Ministry's harvest engines are already moving—and they are, I felt them crossing the pass this morning—then none of us are walking anywhere alone ever again."

Far above, through the mountain's high vents, the amber smear of the dragon-fire mountain flickered—

and, for one heartbeat, went out.
