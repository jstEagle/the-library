## 12. Scale and Hammer

The Cinderholds did not forgive visitors. That was the point of them.

Brannoc Ironhail had spent nine years making sure of it—nine years since the fire took Torva, nine years of hammering grief into hinges and horseshoes and grave-iron for other people's dead. The mountain road up to his forge was scree and switchback and old warning-stones he'd carved himself: TURN BACK in three languages, and beneath them, smaller, because he was honest even in his cruelty: THERE IS NOTHING HERE YOU WANT.

So when the knock came at the anvil-hour past midnight—a knock, on his door, in the Cinderholds—he didn't reach for a weapon. He reached for the tongs.

"Go away," he said through the iron. "Nothing here you want."

"We want the chain," said a man's voice. Tired. City-tired. The kind of voice that had been apologizing to doors for years. "The covenant-chain. You forged it. We need it reforged."

Brannoc laughed. It came out like slag hitting water.

"The chain's been dead longer than my daughter," he said. "Everything it held is dead with it. Go home."

A pause. Then a child's voice, small and steady and wrong in a way that made the hair on his arms stand up—not wrong like sickness, wrong like a bell ringing a note that hadn't existed until now.

"It isn't dead," the child said. "It's starving. There's a difference."

Brannoc stood very still in his own forge, surrounded by nine years of iron, and hated how much the voice sounded like someone who had never been told no by anything that mattered.

He opened the door.

* * *

They were an odd pair. A lean man in a shopkeeper's coat gone grey at the elbows, standing like a man at his own funeral trying to look casual about it. And behind him, half in the wind and snow—

Brannoc had seen a lot of things come up that mountain road. Pilgrims. Fools. Ministry men in their white gloves, once, whom he'd turned back with a hammer and no apology. He had a catalog of faces he expected, and this wasn't any of them:

A child, barefoot in the snow, and not cold. Snowflakes stopping an inch from the skin, melting before they landed, as if the air around the child remembered summer.

And around the child's neck, on a cord, a dragon-scale the size of a spread hand.

The forge went quiet. Not the coals—they roared on—but something under the coals, some listening thing in the deep stone of the Cinderholds, went quiet, the way a room goes quiet when a name is spoken.

"Where," Brannoc said, and his voice came out lower than he meant it, "did you get that scale?"

The man—Quentin, he'd said his name was, Quentin Iversen, which meant nothing to Brannoc and everything to the way he flinched—stepped half in front of the child. Old habit. Guardian-shaped. Brannoc marked it and didn't trust it.

"Ash was born across the threshold," Quentin said. "The scale chose them. It's not—it wasn't taken off anything. I want you to know that first."

"First," Brannoc said. "Before what? What comes second?"

Quentin's jaw worked. The child, Ash, just looked at Brannoc with eyes that were too calm, the way children are calm when they've already survived the thing adults are still dreading.

"Second," Ash said, "we ask you to test it against your fire. If you dare."

* * *

His forge was built into the mountain's shoulder, and its heart was older than his family, older than the holds, maybe older than the covenant itself. The first smiths of the Cinderholds had found a seam of coal that burned without smoke and called it the Deepfire and built everything else around it—the guilds, the oaths, the graves. Brannoc's father had told him: the Deepfire doesn't melt metal, boy. It melts what's true in it. Anything forged there keeps its truth forever. Anything tested there can't hide its lies.

He had tested wedding-bands on it. Oath-blades. Once, a coin a merchant swore was gold.

He had never tested a dragon-scale. No one had. Dragon-scales were relics, museum-pieces, things that sat in glass cases in Vell while men in white gloves argued about whether magic existed. The last dragons were ash and story. Everyone knew that.

Everyone in his family had known that, the night the dragonfire came over the ridge and burned the eastern pasture, and Torva—who had run toward the light like she always ran toward light—hadn't come back.

Nine years, and he could still see the pasture. Still see what the fire left. Still feel his own hands, useless, holding a bucket of water against a sky.

"Stand back from the hearth," he said now, gruff, because if his voice cracked he would throw both of them off the mountain. "If the stone doesn't like what the scale remembers, it'll say so. Loudly."

Ash stepped forward instead of back. Of course.

"I'm not afraid of loud," the child said.

"No," Brannoc said. "I can see that." He took the cord, careful, the way you handle another person's grief, and held the scale out over the Deepfire seam.

The coals turned white.

Then they sang.

Not crackled, not roared—sang, a low chord that came up through the floor and into Brannoc's teeth and bones, and in the heart of the flame images rose like sparks: he saw the fire-mountain, Everwood's dying peak, the one they called Ember-that-Waits in the old songs; he saw figures climbing its flank in the dark, white-gloved hands driving iron stakes into the rock, siphon-chains drinking the glow out of the ground; and he saw—

He saw Torva.

Older than he remembered her, harder, her beard braided back for travel, wearing the journey-cloak he'd made her and sworn she'd never wear because she'd never leave. She stood between the white gloves and the mountain's wound with a smith's hammer in both hands, and she was shouting words he couldn't hear over the singing of the fire, and the agents raised a device—a resonator, a stolen thing, covenant-iron bent to Ministry purpose—and they turned it on her, and it screamed, and the scream came out of the device looking like fire, burning like dragonfire, killing like dragonfire—

But it wasn't dragonfire.

It had never been dragonfire.

Brannoc heard himself make a sound. It didn't sound like him. In the flame, the image held: Torva falling, the scale—her scale, given freely, pressed into the hand of a fleeing child whose mother spoke the First Tongue, *keep it, keep it safe, tell the keepers*—and the vision folded down into embers, and the Deepfire went dark and quiet as a closed eye.

The silence after was the worst part. Nine years he had built a cathedral to a lie: that dragons killed his daughter, that all of dragonkind owed him a debt of ash. He had hammered that lie into every hinge in the Cinderholds. He had refused two kingdoms' worth of people because the lie needed feeding.

The Ministry had killed her. The same gloved hands that knocked politely on doors in Vell. The same machine that now, if Quentin's ragged telling was true, was coming for what was left of the fire itself.

"They told us it was a dragon," he said. His hands were shaking. Master-smith hands. Forty years at the anvil and they shook like a boy's. "The survivors said dragonfire. The Ministry report said dragonfire. They wrote the word themselves and let us hate with it."

"That's what they do," Quentin said quietly. "They don't just burn things. They aim the burning."

"And you knew?" Brannoc rounded on him. "You knew what they were, all these years in that little shop, selling luck-knots—"

"I knew nothing," Quentin said, and there was something raw under it, something that had been flayed recently and often. "I spent fifteen years pretending the fire was already out so I wouldn't have to look at what put it out. That's not knowing. That's the coward's version of it. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to be better at grief than I was."

The words hung there. Brannoc should have hit him for them. Instead they landed where they landed, in the soft ruined place where Torva used to live, and stayed.

Ash touched his arm. Small fingers, warm as a banked coal.

"She gave the scale away," the child said. "She gave it to me so I could carry it here. To you. She said the smith would know what to do with it."

"She said—" His voice broke clean through. He knelt, because his knees decided for him, down to the child's level, and looked at the scale on its cord, at his daughter's last free choice hanging around a stranger-child's neck. "She always did decide things without consulting me."

"So will you?" Ash asked. "Reforge the chain?"

* * *

He worked for three days without sleeping more than two hours at a stretch, and he let them watch, which was as close to trust as the Cinderholds got.

The original covenant-chain hung in his deepest vault, or what was left of it: five links of star-iron, each one keyed to a keeper, each one dead as river stones. Four links. One had been lost—or taken; the Ministry's fingerprints were on everything, he understood that now—and the remaining four were cold, pitted, singing nothing when he struck them.

"You can't reforge what was never broken," he explained, turning a dead link in the firelight while Quentin and Ash watched from the shadows of the vault. "You have to unmake it honestly. Melt it back to truth. And then you have to add what's missing, and what's missing is—" he tapped the fourth socket, empty, "—whoever held this link. The third keeper. The voice-link, if the old records are right. The chain speaks with the covenant's voice, and right now it's mute."

"Wren," Quentin said. Just the name, and the way he said it told Brannoc a whole history he didn't ask for. "She's alive. Hollowmere. But her name was traded. She may not—she might not agree to anything."

"Then you'd better be persuasive," Brannoc said. "Because I'm not striking this chain hot unless every link has a keeper willing to hold it. That's not stubbornness. That's metallurgy. A chain is only a chain if every link agrees to bear load. You pull with one link unwilling, the whole thing fails at the worst possible moment. Ask me how I know."

"How do you know?" Ash asked, solemn as a magistrate.

"My first master chain snapped at the winch and took two fingers." He held up the hand. "Point stands."

He set the scale on his workbench then, in a cradle of sand, where he could see it from the anvil. He didn't wear it—that belonged to Ash, that belonged to the road ahead—but he worked in its company, and somewhere in the second night he caught himself talking to it, low, in the dwarven way, the way you talk to iron you're working: *hold on, girl, I'm coming to the hard part, you'd laugh at me.*

On the third day he quenched the first remade link, and it didn't go silent in the oil—it rang, faint, a single note like a far-off bell, and the Deepfire seam in the next room glowed briefly warmer, and Ash, asleep on a bench by the door, smiled without waking.

Brannoc stood there with steam rising off the iron and understood that he had crossed some line and could not find his way back to the far side of it, and found, to his enormous surprise, that he didn't want to.

Rage had kept him alive nine years. Rage was a fuel, but it burned dirty, and it left slag in everything it touched. This was different. This was ore. Heavy, unrefined, full of impurities—but ore, at last, instead of ash. Something you could work.

"All right," he said, when Quentin appeared in the vault doorway on the third morning, wary as a cat near water. "The chain will take a week to finish, and I'll finish it whether you're here or not. But I won't strike the binding-pass while the third link sits empty. So here's the bargain, shopkeeper. You go to Hollowmere. You bring me Wren—willing, half-willing, dragged willing, I don't care which, though the first works best. You bring her here, and we finish this together."

Quentin looked at him for a long moment. "You're trusting me with that?"

"Hells, no," Brannoc said. "I'm trusting her with it. You're just the one who knows the road." He picked up his hammer and turned back to the anvil, and paused, and without turning around added, gruff, almost too quiet to catch:

"She'd have liked the child. My Torva. Stubborn as weather, both of them."

Behind him he heard Quentin's breath catch, and the small sound of Ash waking, and the far-off note of a remade link still ringing in the oil like something that had waited nine years to sing.

"Hollowmere," Quentin said at last, heavily, like a man reading his own sentence. "Where they trade memories for safety."

"Yes," Brannoc said. "And shopkeeper—go quickly. The Deepfire showed me more than Torva, these last nights. It showed me white gloves on the road east. Whatever's left of the Ministry isn't done harvesting."

He struck the anvil once, hard, and the whole forge answered, and outside the snow began again on the Cinderholds, falling straight down, like the world holding its breath.
