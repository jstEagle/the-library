## 13. The City That Forgot Its Name

The road to Hollowmere was the first road in Everwood that still deserved the name.

Everything else we'd walked since crossing under the floorboards had been a suggestion of a road—a deer track gone feral, a riverbed with ambitions, once an actual highway that simply stopped mid-span and hung in the air like a sentence someone had abandoned. But this stretch was cobbled, graded, lined with mile-stones whose inscriptions had been sanded smooth by hands that wanted them forgotten. It led downhill, through grey orchards that no longer surprised me, toward a smudge on the horizon that resolved, by late afternoon, into walls.

Walls meant people. People meant danger. I'd learned that much in fifteen years of selling charms to frightened customers: nothing draws the Ministry like a crowd.

"Quentin." Ash said my name the way they always did now—half question, half command. They'd been quiet since Sorrow Ford, quieter even than Maren's silence, which at least had a shape to it. Ash's silence was a held breath. "Are you scared?"

I looked down at them. Barefoot still—I'd bought boots in the Cinderholds, two pairs, and Ash had lost one pair inside a day and declared the other "too loud," which I eventually understood to mean they could hear the stitching whisper against stone, some frequency of attention I didn't have and never would.

"Yes," I said. Because lying to Ash had become impossible somewhere between the river and the forge, and because the honest answer was worse than yes. "I'm scared of what we're going to find."

"What do you think we'll find?"

I thought about it honestly, which is a discipline. "A city where everyone smiles," I said. "And nobody remembers why."

Ash considered that for a long moment, head tilted, dragon-scale pendant swinging cold against their collarbone. Then they said, "That's what my mother was afraid of," and walked on, and I carried the sentence the rest of the way down the hill like a stone in my boot.

* * *

Hollowmere announced itself before we reached the gate—not with sound but with its absence. Every town I'd ever entered had a noise to it: dogs, argument, cart-wheels, someone always singing badly. Hollowmere had none. We stood outside the walls for a full minute listening to a city the size of Vell produce less sound than a single kitchen, and what little there was came in rhythms too regular to be human. A bell every quarter hour. Footsteps in unison, hundreds of them, like rain.

The gate itself was open. No guards, no checkpoint, no questions asked—which should have been our first warning, because in my experience the places that ask no questions are the places that have already decided all the answers.

Inside, the streets were immaculate. Swept. Painted. Flower-boxes on every sill, bright geraniums spilling over rails in colors so vivid they looked freshly remembered, which, I realized as we walked, is exactly what they were. The Ministry had repainted this city from a ledger. Every shade matched a record. Every face matched a file.

Because the faces were the thing. I want to be precise about this, because everything else about Hollowmere you could mistake for peace if your heart were tired enough—and mine was tired enough, God knows, after three weeks of dying orchards and backward rivers and a smith who'd taught me that grief can be load-bearing. But the faces.

They were blank the way a page is blank before the ink. Not sad. Not empty-eyed in the way of the starving or the broken. Smooth. Composed. Each one wearing the same faint pleasant smile, calibrated to the millimeter, identical across four thousand citizens. I watched a woman drop a clay pot on the cobbles and shatter it, and she looked at the pieces with mild interest, swept them up without distress, and smiled the same smile afterward that she'd worn before. Nothing in her registered loss. Nothing in her registered anything.

"They traded it," Brannoc said beside me. He'd come the whole way from the Cinderholds without complaint, which for Brannoc was its own kind of miracle; he complained about weather, about boots, about the price of salt, about the moral character of rivers. He hadn't complained once since the ford. "Their memories. All of them. Pooled and sold."

"To the Ministry," I said.

"To the Ministry." He shook his head slowly, a dwarf working through arithmetic he didn't like. "Fifty years ago, maybe more, there was a plague here. Or a war. My grandfather's generation told stories of it—Hollowmere burned, Hollowmere starved, Hollowmere buried its dead in the square because the ground outside was unsafe. And then the Ministry came with white gloves and an offer. Safety, forever. Clean streets, full bellows, no fear. In exchange—" He paused, and his voice dropped into the register he used when he was about to say something that cost him. "In exchange for memory. The whole city's memory, signed over, notarized, witnessed. Every name. Every grudge. Every grandmother's recipe. Every grave."

"And they took it."

"And they took it." Brannoc spat, delicately, sideways, a dwarven courtesy. "And look at them. Fed. Housed. Smiling. Safe as stones in a wall. My daughter would have called it a tomb with the heating left on."

Ash stopped walking.

I felt it happen—the small sudden stillness of them, the way the pendant went from cold to colder, and I turned back and found them standing in front of a fountain in the main square, staring at the water. Or not at the water. At something in the water, or near it, or reflected in it, something I couldn't see.

"Ash?"

"There are names under the city," they said. Their voice had gone thin, stretched, the way it did when the First Tongue rose in them unbidden. "Under the fountain. Under the streets. The old names, the real ones. They're not gone. They're *kept*." They pressed a palm flat against the fountain's rim, and for a moment I saw the water dimple outward around their hand, as if the stone were breathing. "Someone has to remember to give them back."

I didn't understand it yet. I want to be honest: I understood almost nothing that happened in Hollowmere while it was happening. Understanding arrived later, in fragments, the way understanding does when you've spent fifteen years training yourself not to look directly at things. But I knew what I felt, walking those streets, and what I felt was a horror so clean it didn't even frighten—it *instructed*. This was Corvane's product. This was the finished good. Somewhere in a Ministry archive in Vell, in a vault with white-gloved custodians, sat the entire inner life of a city, filed and indexed and accruing interest. And Corvane had stood in my shop, drunk my tea, complimented my luck-knots, and promised me—with perfect sincerity, with that terrible gentle voice—that Vell could have this too. Safety. Order. An end to fear.

He'd shown me a sample and I hadn't known it was a sample. I'd thought it was a threat.

It was neither. It was an advertisement.

* * *

We found lodging the only way you find lodging in Hollowmere: by being assigned it. A woman at a hospitality desk—smiling, helpful, entirely without curiosity—gave us a room above a bakery that produced bread no one tasted. She asked us nothing except how many nights, and when Brannoc said "until our business concludes," she wrote down *business* in a ledger with a smile, and I understood that the word had been entered into a record somewhere, and that somewhere a copy of this conversation already existed in triplicate.

That night I couldn't sleep. I sat at the window watching the city perform its nightly ritual, which was the same as its daily ritual, which was the same as its hourly ritual: order, repetition, the bell, the footsteps, the lights going out street by street in a sequence so exact you could set a watch by it. Below, two citizens passed each other and exchanged the smile. Not a greeting. Just the smile, deployed at the correct range, like a handshake made of muscle.

And I thought about Wren.

I'd been thinking about Wren since the ford, since the river showed me her face mid-confession and Maren's water went still with recognition I couldn't read. I'd been thinking about her harder since the orchard, though I didn't know Ash had seen her there too—didn't know until later, didn't know until the guilt of it arrived in me like weather. Fifteen years I'd assumed her dead. The covenant had demanded a life or a love, and I had arranged, carefully, quietly, with the cowardice I've confessed to a river and will confess again wherever confession is required, for it to take hers instead of mine. I'd watched the gate close on her side of it. I'd told myself for fifteen years that what closed behind her was death, because death was simpler than the alternative, and because a man can survive having killed someone but cannot survive not knowing.

But Brannoc's daughter hadn't died the way the stories said either. And Maren's river had shown truths, plural, unsorted. And now we were in a city where the Ministry kept things instead of destroying them, where nothing was ever truly unmade, only *filed*—

The thought arrived whole, the way the worst ones do, at three in the morning, fully formed, refusing to be unknoted:

*What if the covenant didn't kill her? What if it took her name?*

What if Wren was alive? What if she was here—or somewhere like here, or anywhere the Ministry kept its inventory—walking around with a blank face and a pleasant smile, holding someone else's memories in fragments, sustained and hollowed in the same gesture? What if my betrayal hadn't even had the decency to be murder?

I got up. I woke Ash, gently, the way you wake a child who sleeps armed—because Ash slept armed, always, the scale within reach, and I'd stopped finding this strange sometime around the Cinderholds.

"I need to tell you something," I said, "and I need you to listen before you decide what to feel."

Ash sat up. Waited. Children wait better than adults; nobody's taught them yet to fill silence with noise.

"Fifteen years ago," I said, "I did something unforgivable, and I've been doing penance in installments ever since, and none of the installments have helped. You know some of this. The river knows all of it. But here's the part I haven't said out loud: I don't know that Wren is dead. I've *needed* her to be dead, because the alternative means she's been alive this whole time, without a name, without—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I let it. "Without anyone looking for her. Including me. Especially me."

Ash looked at me for a long time. There was something in their face I'd learned to read by then, a quality of attention that wasn't quite forgiveness and wasn't quite judgment—more like an accountant reviewing figures that had finally, at long last, balanced.

"The third keeper," Ash said. "The one the chain needs. The one whose voice completes it."

"Yes."

"You think it's her."

"I think," I said, "that the Ministry doesn't waste assets. And I think a keeper's voice, even a surrendered one, even a filed-away one, is exactly the kind of asset Corvane's people would keep rather than burn. And I think—" and here I stopped, because the next part was true and terrible and I'd been carrying it since the fountain, since Ash touched the stone and said the names were kept. "I think we're already standing in the place where they keep things."

* * *

We found her on the second day, and I want to describe it accurately, because accuracy is the only penance I have left that works.

There was a market in the eastern quarter—of course there was; the Ministry permitted markets, orderly ones, stalls in ranked rows, prices fixed by schedule. The citizens moved through it purchasing things they couldn't remember wanting, guided by lists they received each morning, and the lists were correct, and the bread was fresh, and no one starved, and no one grieved, and I have never in my life seen anything so obscene as that market functioning perfectly.

She was at a stall selling thread.

Not dyeing it, not weaving it—selling it. Spools of it, arranged by color, and her hands moved with a competence that survived the erasure the way a river survives drought in its bed. Hands remember. That's the cruelty of it, and maybe the mercy. Her fingers knew thread the way they'd once known sigils, known the gate's deep hinges, known the weight of a boy's head against her shoulder when he cried about the price of things.

I saw her profile first. Fifteen years had done what fifteen years do, but the line of it was hers, unmistakably hers, the jaw and the tilt and the way she stood slightly forward on her feet as if perpetually about to step into a current. I knew that stance. I'd watched her stand like that on the last night, on the far side of the closing gate, waiting for it to take her.

I stopped walking. Brannoc, who missed nothing, stopped a half-step later and then looked at me with an expression I can only call *foreknowledge*—the look of a man who has already guessed what I'm about to discover and is bracing on my behalf.

"Quentin," Ash said softly. "Your face."

I don't know what my face was doing. Something involuntary. Something that would have alarmed a stranger.

I approached the stall the way you approach any dangerous animal: slowly, without eye contact, with a purchase in mind as cover. Thread. I needed thread. I had no earthly use for thread, but I took a spool of blue off the rack and held it out to her, and she took it, and her hands—her hands, Wren's hands, scarred at the knuckle from forge-adjacent work I'd watched her do a hundred times—wrapped the spool in paper and tied it with a knot I recognized.

A luck-knot. The old pattern. The one from before the closing, from the years when the gate hummed and the covenant held and she'd taught it to me in a workroom that smelled of cedar and hot copper. Her hands had forgotten her name, her history, her grief, her love. They had not forgotten the knot.

"That's a fine knot," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Fifteen years of lying to inspectors buys you a certain steadiness at the worst moments. "Where did you learn it?"

She smiled the city's smile. Then, underneath it, something flickered—something brief and uncalibrated, a muscle firing from a depth the Ministry's ledgers hadn't reached. Her brow creased. Her head tilted, just slightly, the way Ash tilted theirs.

"I don't know," she said. And then, quieter, with genuine puzzlement, with the tone of a woman reporting a leak in a roof: "I don't know, but my hands do. Isn't that strange? Everything here is strange. I notice that sometimes. I'm not supposed to notice, I don't think, but I notice."

My heart was doing something medically inadvisable.

"Do you have a name?" I asked. Carefully. Gently. The way you ask a question you already know will wound.

"Sixteen," she said pleasantly. "The city assigns them by district and rotation. This month I'm Elset. Last month I was Corrin. Before that—" She waved a hand, the gesture dissolving the past as smoothly as the Ministry had dissolved it. "Does it matter? Names are just handles. Everyone here agrees on that. It's very peaceful, agreeing."

"It matters," I said.

Something happened to her face then. Not the flicker—something longer, slower, a tide going out. She looked at me, really looked, and behind the smooth pleasant surface I watched something move the way a fish moves under ice: present, unreachable, undeniable. Her hands went still on the counter. The spool of blue thread sat between us in its paper like an offering.

"Why?" she said. "Why does it matter to *you*?"

And I opened my mouth to answer—to lie, probably, or to half-lie, or to construct some careful scaffold of a story that would let me test her without exposing myself—and before I could speak a single word, Ash stepped up beside me at the stall, barefoot, small, impossible, and spoke instead.

In the First Tongue.

Three words. I knew them—I knew them the way I'd sworn I'd forgotten them, the way I'd known them in my doorway on the night the wards answered aloud. Three words of the old covenant-language, the greeting-between-keepers, the words Wren herself had taught me across a workbench twenty years ago: *the fire sees you.*

The woman behind the stall went white.

Not pale—white, drained, the color leaving her face like water from a cracked basin, and her hand rose halfway to her chest and stopped, hovering, pressing at nothing, at the place below the collarbone where the Ministry's extraction-sigils were cut. Her mouth opened. Her eyes widened. And then, from somewhere in her—somewhere filed, somewhere indexed, somewhere locked in a vault under a city I'd sold charms in for fifteen years without knowing what slept beneath my own floorboards—something answered.

One word. One word in the First Tongue, ragged, torn out of her like a stitch pulling free of old cloth, and it was not a word of greeting.

It was my name.

Not Quentin. Not the name I'd worn in Vell, the safe name, the invented name, the shopkeeper's alias I'd hidden behind for a decade and a half. My *true* name—the one given at the gate, the one written in the covenant-chain, the one only two living beings had ever known.

Her. And me.

She stared at Ash. She stared at me. The market flowed around us, four thousand smiling citizens buying bread they couldn't taste, and none of them noticed that at one thread-stall in the eastern quarter, a woman with no name had just spoken a word that predates every kingdom, and that the word was me.

"I know you," she whispered. Her hands were shaking now, both of them, the competence gone to pieces. "I don't—I don't know *how*, I don't know your face, I don't know anything, but I know you. I know your name the way my hands know the knot. Who *are* you? Why does hearing you hurt? Why does it hurt like—" Her voice fractured, and beneath the fracture, rising, I heard it: the ghost of a cadence I'd have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea. "—like something owed?"

Brannoc put a hand on my shoulder. Heavy, dwarven, steadying, the grip of a man who'd lost a daughter and found a purpose and knew exactly what it costs to hear an answer you've spent years dreading.

"She's alive," he said, flatly, so I wouldn't have to say it. "Quentin. She's alive."

And the woman who had been Elset and Corrin and sixteen other borrowed names looked at the three of us—at the child with the scale, at the smith with the hammer-calluses, at the man whose name she'd spoken without knowing why—and the city's smile fell off her face entirely, and what was underneath was older than the Ministry, older than the trade, older than my betrayal.

It was Wren. It was the beginning of Wren. A woman standing in the wreckage of her own erasure, holding one word of herself, and the word was the name of the man who had let them take her.

She looked at me across that counter, across fifteen years and a folded paper of blue thread, and her eyes filled, and she didn't know what she was crying about, and I did.

"Come back tomorrow," she said. "Customers aren't supposed to linger. I'm not supposed to linger. But—" and here her voice dropped to the register I remembered, the one she'd used for secrets, for sigils, for the last night at the gate—"come back anyway. Whatever you are. Whoever I am. Come back, and bring whatever it is you're carrying, because I think—" Her hand pressed flat against her sternum, over the scar, over the absence. "I think you're carrying *me*."

The bell rang the quarter hour. The market exhaled and resumed. She turned to her next customer and smiled, the calibrated smile, the city's smile, and I stood there holding a spool of blue thread I didn't need, shaking, undone, found and condemned in the same instant.

Behind me, Ash said quietly, in English, in the plain tongue, the translation arriving like a verdict:

*"The fire sees you. And it remembers what you did."*
