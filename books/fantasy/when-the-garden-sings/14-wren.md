## 14. Wren

The woman who walked through Hollowmere had no name, and she had stopped minding.

That was the first thing to understand about her. Not that she had lost a name—losing implies you remember what was taken. She had simply woken one morning, years ago now, or perhaps yesterday, in a city where everyone's face was smooth as river stone, and the absence inside her fit so neatly against all the other absences around her that grief had nowhere to stand. You cannot mourn a self you cannot picture. You can only be hungry.

She was always hungry.

The Ministry called them the Quiet Citizens. They had traded their memories for safety, every one of them, whole streets at a time, and in exchange the white-gloved inspectors had promised that nothing would ever hurt them again. Nothing did. Nothing could. Hurt requires a before and an after, a wound measured against a wholeness, and the Quiet Citizens had no wholeness to measure against. They rose when the bells rang. They worked at whatever work was nearest. They slept without dreaming, because dreams are just memories wearing costumes, and they owned no costumes.

But the Ministry had made one mistake, small and technical and enormous: it had not figured out how to unmake hunger. So it fed them scraps. Fragments. Borrowed recollections poured into them like soup into cracked bowls, enough to keep a person walking, talking, answering when addressed. The fragments came from everywhere—from donors, from the dead, from confiscated archives of lives the Ministry had catalogued and shelved. The woman had lived inside other people's Tuesdays for so long she no longer knew which hands had touched hers first.

This morning's fragment was a kitchen. That was how they arrived—not as scenes but as temperatures. A kitchen, warm, someone humming while water boiled. She stood in the middle of Hollowmere's grey market square holding the warmth against her chest like a stolen loaf, trying to make it last, knowing it would thin by noon and be gone by dark, and tomorrow she'd be given someone else's afternoon and told, in the gentle administrative language they used, that this was living.

A child ran past her chasing a paper windmill. The child had a face. That was unusual here—most faces in Hollowmere were pleasant and blank, faces designed not to snag on anything—but this child had smudges on its cheeks and a scowl like weather. The woman watched it go and felt something she could not name, because naming required a vocabulary built out of remembered things, and hers had been confiscated too. She felt it anyway. Feelings, it turned out, were harder to harvest than facts. The Ministry could take what you knew. What you *were* leaked through the cracks.

* * *

He arrived on a Tuesday, though she wouldn't have known it was a Tuesday if a fragment hadn't told her.

She saw him first across the square, and the seeing was wrong. Everything in Hollowmere slid past the eye without catching—faces, buildings, the slow grey drift of Quiet Citizens going about their borrowed days—but this man caught. Her attention snagged on him like cloth on a nail. He was ordinary-looking, tall, tired around the eyes, wearing the dust of a long road, and he moved through the blank crowd the way a splinter moves through skin: noticeable only because the body wants it out.

Except her body didn't want him out. Her body wanted him *close*, and that frightened her more than anything had frightened her in years, because wanting was a memory-shaped hole and he looked exactly like the shape of it.

He was not alone. There was a child with him—the scowling child from the market, she realized, the one with the smudges—and a broad silent man with a smith's shoulders, and somewhere behind them, carried in a sling of woven reeds, a stone that seemed to hum when the wind crossed the square. A strange company. A pilgrimage, almost. Hollowmere got few pilgrims these days; there was nothing left here to be devout about.

The man stopped ten paces from her and went very still.

She knew stillness like that. It was in one of her fragments—a soldier's fragment, donated or stolen, she never learned whose—stillness in the moment before a confession. The body bracing to be struck. She watched him look at her face, and she watched his face do something complicated and terrible, something that had layers, and she thought: *he knows me.* People who know you look at you differently. They look *through* the surface at a person who isn't standing there anymore.

"Hello," the child said, in a voice that rang strangely, old-sounding, like a bell made of wood. "We're looking for someone."

Hollowmere's citizens answered questions the way mirrors answer—accurately, emptily. But the woman found herself answering differently. She heard herself say words she hadn't chosen, sentences rising out of her like bubbles from a drowned place:

"You've come a long way. You should eat. The bread is bad here, but it's warm."

The man flinched. Actually flinched, a small full-body movement, as if the words had been spoken directly into a wound. The child noticed. Children notice everything; it's adults who learn not to.

"What's your name?" the child asked her.

And here was the strange part, the part she would turn over for nights afterward: she opened her mouth to give the non-answer all Quiet Citizens gave—*I'm whoever you need, I'm no one, we don't keep names here*—and instead something older spoke. Something said, in a voice that was hers and yet came from beneath her, beneath the fragments, beneath the hunger:

"Wren."

She had not said that word in years. She had not known that word. It arrived in her mouth like a bird returning to a house that had burned down, circling once, landing anyway. Wren. A small brown bird. A name that meant *small and quick and easy to overlook*, and she felt it settle over her like a coat that fit perfectly and smelled of someone else's smoke.

The man made a sound then. Not a word. A sound, low and broken, the kind of sound a door makes when it hasn't been opened in fifteen years and the hinges have rusted through.

The child looked between them with the terrible clarity children have. "You know her," the child said to the man. It wasn't a question.

"No," the man said. And then, immediately, worse: "Yes."

* * *

They did not leave that day. That was the second strange thing, and stranger than the first, because everything in her understood that strangers pass through Hollowmere the way weather does—they arrive, they ask their questions, they go. But this one stayed. He took a room above the empty tavern. The child stayed with him. The smith set up in an abandoned forge and hammered at something late into the night, metal singing against metal, and the singing kept her awake in a way she resented and needed both.

She began, without deciding to, to arrange her days around him.

This was absurd. She knew it was absurd. She was a bowl full of strangers' soup; he was a road-dusty man with guilt written into the architecture of his posture. There was no *between* them. There couldn't be. And yet when she walked to the well each morning, she took the route that passed the tavern. When she drew water, she drew enough for two, and left a bucket on his step, and could not have explained it under torture except to say that her hands remembered being useful to him. Hands, she was learning, held onto things the mind released. The Ministry had catalogued her knowledge down to her mother's face and the color of her childhood door. It had not catalogued the way her fingers wanted to hold a certain weight, a certain shape, a hand she could not picture attached to anyone.

He started leaving things on her step too. Small things. A sprig of some grey-green herb that didn't grow anywhere in Hollowmere. A button. Once, a strip of dried fruit from a world she'd never seen, sweet in a way that made her cry without knowing why, tears arriving like rain on a day with no clouds, her body mourning a sweetness her mind had never met.

They didn't speak much. When they did, it was sideways, oblique, two people circling a fire they couldn't see.

"I'm going away soon," he said once, at the well. "All of us. There's a mountain."

"How far?" she asked, and heard herself ask it in a tone that wasn't curiosity. Curiosity is light. Her tone was heavy. Her tone was the tone of someone asking *how long do I have to learn to live without you again.*

"A long way," he said. Then, quieter, almost to himself: "I've been there before. A long time ago." A pause like a held breath. "I don't remember it kindly."

Something in her chest turned over. She said, before she could stop it, in that voice from beneath the fragments: "You will. Remember it kindly. Eventually. That's what you're like."

He stared at her. She stared at the ground. Neither of them acknowledged what had just happened—that she had made a claim about his character, about his *future*, based on nothing, based on a hollow, based on the ghost-shape of a person she had been and could not find.

That night she sat awake in her narrow room above the dye-works, holding the word *Wren* in her mouth like a hot stone. The fragments cycled through her, other people's kitchens, other people's funerals, a fisherman's patience, a bride's nerves. None of them were hers. Beneath them all, one constant: the man at the tavern. Not his face—she could look at his face and feel nothing specific, no history, no shared joke surfacing, none of the furniture of acquaintance. Just his *voice*. When he spoke near her, something in her ribs rearranged itself, quietly, painfully, like a bone setting wrong.

She hated him for it. She wantted to be clear about that, even in the privacy of her own headless thoughts: she hated him. He had a gravity she couldn't account for and couldn't resist, and hating a gravity is like hating falling. You do it the whole way down.

And underneath the hate, in a chamber so deep the Ministry's instruments had never reached it, something else. Something that felt the way the dried fruit had tasted. Something patient. Something that had been waiting, apparently, for years, in the dark, without a name to wait *with*, for the sound of one particular voice coming up a road.

* * *

On the fourth day, the child came to her.

Not the man—the child. Ash, the others called the child, though the name sounded borrowed too, provisional, like everything in this story. Ash found her at the well in the blue hour before dawn, when even the Quiet Citizens were still asleep in their dreamless beds, and stood there barefoot in the frost with that dragon-scale pendant glinting cold at the child's throat.

"I need to show you something," Ash said. "It's about you. Sort of. It's about who you were."

The child spoke carefully, in that old-ringing voice, and the woman listened the way the starving listen to descriptions of food—with her whole body, desperately, unable to help herself.

"There's an orchard," Ash said. "Far from here. The trees remember things. If you touch a branch, you taste someone's life. We found it on the way here. And there's—" the child hesitated, choosing words like stepping stones, "—there's a tree that grew after we passed through. A new tree. Young. And its fruit tastes of a woman named Wren. Of her life. Before."

Before. The word landed in her like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known she owned.

"Come with us," Ash said. "When we go to the mountain. Come and touch the tree. Maybe—" the child's voice did something young and brave and scared, "—maybe you can remember yourself. Even a little. Even one season."

The woman stood very still. Frost formed on her lashes. Somewhere behind her ribs, the patient thing leaned forward.

"And him?" she heard herself ask. "Is he going too?"

Ash looked at her with an expression no child should have had to learn: the expression of someone carrying a secret that belongs to two other people. "Yes," the child said. "He has to. He's paying for it. For you. With—" the child stopped, visibly struggling, visibly protecting something, "—with a lot. With everything he's got."

She didn't understand. She wrote the sentence down anyway, scratched into the plaster of her room with a hairpin, where the Ministry's collectors wouldn't think to look: *He is paying for me. With everything.*

She read it each morning. She did not know why those words mattered. She only knew that reading them was the closest thing to eating she had felt in years.

* * *

The last evening before they left, he came to her door.

He knocked softly, twice, the way you knock when you're afraid of what answers. She opened it because her body decided before she did—her body kept doing that, kept voting against the Ministry's arithmetic, kept insisting on motions her mind couldn't justify.

He stood in the doorway holding nothing. No gift this time. Just himself, and the tiredness, and something underneath the tiredness that radiated off him like heat off a forge.

"I wanted to say goodbye," he said. "Properly. Before we go."

"You'll come back," she said. Not a question. The voice-from-beneath again, asserting, certain, insufferably certain, and she hated it, hated how sure it was of a future she had no data for.

"I don't know what I'll be when I come back," he said. His voice caught on something. "That's the truth. I'm going to pay a price, and the price is—I won't be able to carry what I'm carrying now. Some of it. Maybe most of it." He looked at her then, straight on, and his eyes were wet and he didn't apologize for it, and somehow that was the most honest thing anyone had done in Hollowmere in a decade. "So if I come back and I don't know you—if I look at you like a stranger—you should know that it isn't because you weren't—" he faltered, searched, failed, tried again, "—it isn't because of anything you did. Or didn't do. It will be because I spent it. All of it. On you."

Her chest did the bone-setting thing again. Worse this time. She stood in her doorway and let the words fall into her hollows, and they didn't fill anything—they were too true to fill, truth slides through cracks—but they *warmed* the cracks, the way embers warm a room without lighting it.

"I don't know you," she told him. It was important to say it out loud, to keep the ledger honest. "I want you to hear me say it. Whatever you think I am to you, I don't have it. There's nothing in me that remembers you. Do you understand? You're speaking to a bowl of borrowed soup."

"I understand," he said. And then, gently, terribly: "But you said my name once. In the square. You said *Wren*, and then later you told me what I'm like. You told me I'd remember kindly eventually." He smiled, and it was a ruined smile, worn down to the wire. "Bowls of borrowed soup don't do that."

Silence. Frost. The distant hammer-song of the smith, still working, still singing metal against metal in the dark.

She looked at him and felt the two truths grind against each other like millstones: she did not know him, and she could not lose him. Both at once. Both fully. Grief, she had been told once, in a fragment, by a dead woman's voice—grief at least gives you something to hold. A coffin. A grave. A photograph. Grief is love with an address. What she had was worse. What she had was love with the address torn off, love arriving at her door with no return label, knocking anyway, refusing to explain itself.

"I'll come," she said finally. "To the mountain. To the tree."

He nodded, and started to turn away, and her hand came up before she authorized it and caught his sleeve. Just caught it. Held it for three seconds. Four. Long enough for the frost to form a thin white seam along the fabric where her breath touched it.

Then she let go, and he went, and she stood in the doorway watching four shapes walk out of Hollowmere's grey gate into the white morning—a tired man, a scowling child, a smith, and a stone that hummed—and she followed them, because of course she followed them. Because the patient thing beneath the fragments had waited years already and was done waiting politely.

She did not know where they were going. She did not know who she had been. She knew only that the man's voice pulled at her like tide pulls at a shore, wearing her away, shaping her, and that somewhere ahead of them stood a tree with fruit that tasted of her name, and somewhere beyond that a mountain that was running out of fire, and somewhere inside the tired man a price already counted out, every coin of it marked *for her*.

She walked. The frost crunched. Behind her, Hollowmere closed its blank face like a book nobody would read.

Ahead, she could hear the child singing, in the old tongue, a song about doors opening.

She found she knew the next line.
