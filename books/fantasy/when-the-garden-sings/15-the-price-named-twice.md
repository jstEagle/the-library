## 15. The Price Named Twice

The covenant-keepers' road out of Hollowmere climbed through hills that remembered being green. Quentin could see the ghosts of it in the hedgerows—bramble canes gone silver with frost that still held the shape of summer leaves, as if the land had died mid-breath and hadn't bothered to fall over.

Wren walked three paces behind him. She had walked three paces behind him since Hollowmere, and he had not once been able to close the distance without her drifting back to exactly three paces, like a boat keeping station off a harbor wall.

"She's getting worse," Ash said quietly, at his elbow.

"I know."

"No, you don't." The child's voice had an old flat certainty to it that Quentin was still learning to dread. "She stopped saying my name yesterday. She calls me *small one*. This morning she called Brannoc *the fire man* twice before she got it right. The fragments are burning up. Whatever's holding her together is running out."

Quentin glanced back. Wren was watching the horizon—or watching through it—with the patient blankness of someone waiting in a very long queue for something they'd forgotten wanting. Her eyes found his, and held, and something flickered there. Not recognition. Recognition would have been a mercy. It was more like the look you give a word on the tip of your tongue.

"Then we're almost out of time," he said.

"We were always almost out of time," Ash said. "That's what a guttering fire is."

They camped that night in the shell of a shepherd's hut whose roof had grown back as living wood—Everwood logic, everything half-alive and half-remembered—and Quentin sat by the fire turning over in his hands the thing Maren had given him along with her river-stone: a small clay token, unglazed, shaped like a seed. A price-token. He had known what it was the moment the water pressed it into his palm, because fifteen years ago he had held its twin and refused it.

Brannoc sat sharpening the reforged chain-links across the fire, each stroke of the whetstone ringing a note that made the flames lean toward him. Wren slept sitting upright, which she had done every night, as if even unconscious she wasn't sure she'd be given the body back.

"You've decided," Brannoc said. It wasn't a question. Dwarves didn't ask questions when they could read answers off a face like slag off a crucible.

Quentin closed his hand around the token. "You know what the covenant asks."

"I forged the chain, didn't I." The whetstone paused. "I remember when the price was named the first time. Everyone stood in the circle and pretended it would be somebody else." His eyes came up, ember-lit. "And then it was Wren. Because you let it be."

The words landed where they always landed, in the same bruise they'd been pressing for two weeks. Quentin didn't flinch anymore. That was the worst part—he'd worn a groove in himself where the guilt fit, comfortable as an old boot.

"Yes," he said. "Because I let it be."

Brannoc grunted and went back to sharpening. From Brannoc, Quentin had learned, that was practically absolution.

* * *

He couldn't sleep, so he did what he had done every night for fifteen years: he counted.

Not days this time. Moments. He lay on the cold floor of the hut and took inventory of the fifteen years the way a miser counts coin before paying out a debt he can't dodge—each one weighed, each one named.

The first year: learning to live in Vell above a sealed gate, buying flour, learning which neighbors reported things and which merely suspected them. The taste of fear that eventually became just flavor.

Year four: the winter he nearly opened the gate and went home, standing on the floorboards at midnight with his hand flat against the hidden seam, feeling the faint warmth of Everwood underneath like a cat sleeping under the floor. He hadn't opened it. He still wasn't sure why. Cowardice or faithfulness; after fifteen years the two had fused into something he couldn't separate without breaking both.

Year seven: the fever summer, when he'd charmed half the street's children well and told himself it was nothing, told himself magic was dead while his hands worked the knots their mother's mothers had taught them. The first time he laughed in Vell. He remembered the exact date.

Year nine: the census clerk, Tamsin, who brought him tea every market day for two years and never learned why he could never quite say yes to anything more. He remembered the smell of her shop. He remembered deciding, deliberately, carefully, to let her stop coming, because loving someone in a city full of white gloves was a debt you paid in other people's blood.

Years eleven through fourteen: small. That was the terrible truth of them. Small years, small mercies, a thousand mornings of tea and ledgers and checking the gate and finding it silent. Fifteen years of small lies, and every single one of them had kept him alive, and every single one of them had been worth it, and none of them—not one—had been worth what Wren had paid.

He had built a life on top of her sacrifice the way Vell was built on top of Everwood: comfortably, and without looking down.

Ash stirred in the corner. "You're doing it wrong," the child murmured, half-asleep.

"Doing what?"

"Counting. You count like they're already gone." Ash's eyes opened, dark and ancient and eight years old. "Count like you're keeping them."

Quentin looked at the ceiling beams, which were growing tiny silver leaves in the moonlight. "Is that how you keep things?"

"That's how the orchard keeps things. That's how the fire will keep yours, if it comes to that." Ash rolled over. "The fire doesn't eat memories, Quentin. It burns them into light. Nothing you give it disappears. It just stops being only yours."

Quentin lay awake a long time after that, holding his fifteen years like a bird with a broken wing, wondering if letting go might be the same thing as setting free.

* * *

They reached the covenant-circle on the third morning.

It sat in a bowl of hills where four old roads met, and it was the first place in Everwood Quentin had seen that wasn't grey. The stones of the circle were warm-colored, honeyed, humming faintly under his palms when he touched them—the same hum he'd spent fifteen years missing under his floorboards. At the circle's center stood a low altar of black glass, and set into the altar was a socket shaped exactly like Maren's river-stone.

Beyond the hills, far to the north, the fire-mountain flickered. Even from here he could see it guttering, light stuttering like a candle in a draft, and each stutter made the sky above Everwood go briefly darker, as if the whole realm were blinking and forgetting to open its eyes again.

"It has to be here," Brannoc said. "The chain, the stone, the seal, the keeper. All five points of the circle." He hefted the reforged chain, coiled now across his shoulders like a sleeping serpent, singing faintly whenever it moved. "But the price comes first. The covenant doesn't run on metal. It runs on payment."

"What payment?" Wren asked.

Everyone turned. She had spoken—really spoken, a question, a whole question—and for a moment her face had a person behind it. Then the moment passed and she frowned slightly, as if surprised to find her own mouth open.

"The covenant asks a price," Quentin said gently. "From whoever renews it. You remember that much?"

"I remember standing somewhere cold," she said slowly. "I remember being asked what I loved most." Her brow creased. "I don't remember answering."

"You answered," Quentin said. His voice came out strange. "You answered *yes*."

* * *

He told them all of it, because Maren's water had taught him that half-truths curdled in a covenant-circle, and because if he was going to do this he was going to do it with the whole weight of it in his mouth.

"Fifteen years ago the fire began to fail the first time. The Ministry was closing the gates—all of them, everywhere—and the covenant needed renewing to survive the severing. The price was named. Each keeper had to give up what they loved most. Maren gave her voice. Brannoc—" he glanced at the smith, who had gone very still, "—gave his forge-song. And the fifth keeper, the warden of the Vell gate, was asked to give up his memories. Everything he was. Everything he'd lived."

"And instead," Brannoc said quietly, "you gave her."

"Wren was my mentor. My teacher. She'd spent sixty years learning the First Tongue, and I'd spent six. When the naming came, I panicked. I told the covenant—I argued—that the gate needed its warden's memory intact, that a warden who forgot the gate couldn't guard it, that logic demanded—" He stopped. Started again, because the river had taught him that stopping was just another kind of lie. "There was no logic. There was terror. I loved my life. I loved my mind. I stood in this circle and I watched her understand what I was doing, and she stepped forward anyway, because that's who she was, and I let her. I let her pay my price. And then I sealed the gate myself, with her name in my mouth, and I walked into Vell and I bought flour."

Silence. The mountain flickered. Somewhere in the circle's stones, the old hum shifted key, minor, attentive.

Ash took Wren's hand. Wren looked down at the child's fingers around hers as though they belonged to a stranger—which they did, which everyone here did—and then looked up at Quentin.

"You let me," she said. Not angry. Worse than angry: *checking*. Testing the sentence for weight, for truth, the way she'd once tested his warding-sigils for flaws. "I paid your price. And you came to find me now because—"

"Because the fire is dying and the covenant needs a price again," Quentin said. "Yes. That's why I came."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is mine to carry. It doesn't need a reason."

Something moved behind her eyes—fragments grinding against fragments, sixty stolen years trying to assemble themselves around a hole shaped exactly like this moment. "I hate you," she said at last, wonderingly, like a woman reading her own handwriting on a letter she didn't remember writing. "I don't know why. But I hate you, and I also—" her hand pressed flat against her sternum, "—there's something here that isn't hate, and I don't have a word for it, and it's *worse*."

"Love," Ash said simply. "The word is love. It survived the taking. They never figure out how to take that part."

* * *

Quentin knelt at the altar alone.

The others withdrew to the circle's edge—Brannoc with his arms folded, Ash with the dragon-scale pendant cupped in both hands, and Wren standing closest, three paces away, keeping her station.

He set Maren's stone in its socket. It sank home with a sound like a door unlocking. Light ran around the circle's rim, slow, expectant, hungry. The covenant waking up, asking its question the way it had asked it once before:

*What will you give? What do you love most?*

His fifteen years rose in him all at once, the whole miser's hoard of them—the flour and the fear, the tea and the tally-marks, the floorboards and the fever-charms, Tamsin's shop, year seven's laughter, the cat-shaped warmth under the floor. Every moment that had made survival bearable. He'd thought, walking here, that giving them up would feel like dying.

It felt like the opposite. It felt, horribly, like coming clean.

"I offer them," he said aloud, in Vellish first, because some truths needed his mother tongue, and then again in the First Tongue, because covenants did. "*All of it. Fifteen years. Take the whole account.*"

The circle brightened. The stone drank the light. And then—

"No."

Wren's voice. Not a fragment, not a question—a refusal, whole and hard as forge-iron. She crossed the three paces between them, and for the first time since Hollowmere the distance broke, and she stood over him at the altar with her fists at her sides and sixty years of borrowed memories churning behind her eyes.

"Not like this."

"Wren—"

"You don't get to do it again." Her voice shook. "Do you hear me? Fifteen years ago you chose yourself, and it cost me everything, and now you want to choose *nobly*, and it will cost you everything, and it's still the same act. It's still Quentin deciding what gets sacrificed, alone, in a circle, without asking anyone." She knelt, dragged him half upright by the collar, and her eyes were wet and furious and achingly, recognizably hers. "You want to give me back my name by making me watch you lose yours? Then I'll be restored into a world where the man I—" the word snagged, tore loose anyway, "—where the man I love doesn't know my face. That's not a gift. That's just a second betrayal with better lighting."

The circle dimmed, waiting. It would wait. Covenants were patient; that was the cruelest thing about them.

"So what do we do?" Quentin whispered. "The fire is guttering, Wren. Someone pays, or both worlds go out. Those are the terms. They were always the terms."

"I know what the terms are." She released his collar. Sat back on her heels. For a long moment she stared at the socket, at the stone, at the hungry patient light, and Quentin watched sixty lost years war behind her face.

"There were two prices named," she said finally. "Fifteen years ago. Did you ever wonder why they called this chapter of it *twice*?"

Quentin went cold. "What?"

"The covenant's price was never one payment. It was a price *named twice*—once by the giver, once by the receiver. The giver offers. But the receiver must accept. That's the balance. That's the whole architecture of it." Her eyes lifted to his. "I accepted your price fifteen years ago. Willingly. God help me, I *chose* it, because I loved you enough to pay for your cowardice. So the covenant holds a claim on you, Quentin Iversen. It has held it for fifteen years, gathering interest." She put her hand flat on the altar, over the stone. "You don't get to offer. You never got to offer. The question was always whether I'd collect."

The circle flared. The mountain, far north, flared with it—one long, steady breath of light instead of a stutter—and Quentin understood, with the sick clarity of a man whose careful life had ended twice now, that the covenant had been listening the entire way here. That the price had been counted out in coins marked *for him* long before he'd reached this hill.

"You can't," he said. "If you take them—if you use my years to restore yourself—the fire gets nothing. The covenant—"

"The covenant gets *truth*." Ash's voice, from the circle's edge, ringing clear in the First Tongue. The child walked forward, dragon-scale raised, and the scale burned cold-white. "That's what the fire eats. That's what Ember-that-Waits has been starving for. Not memories—*paid debts*. An honest price, freely transferred, fully accepted." The child's eyes were enormous. "A story worth burning for. And oh, Quentin. Oh, *keeper*. The story of a man who owed everything, and the woman who came to collect—"

The ground trembled. Far to the north, past the grey orchards, past the backward rivers, a horn sounded—no, several horns—thin and disciplined and terribly human, carrying down the wind off the high passes.

Brannoc's head snapped up. "Ministry horns. On *this* side of the gate." He was already uncoiling the chain from his shoulders. "Corvane didn't send inspectors. Corvane sent an army."

Wren's hand was still flat on the stone. Her eyes never left Quentin's.

"Choose fast, warden," she said. "Do you fight me for your fifteen years? Or do you finally, *finally*, pay what you owe?"

The horns sounded again, closer. The circle waited, lit like a held breath.
