## 17. Storming the Fire-Mountain

### Maren

The river had been waiting nine years to be asked.

Maren stood at the head of Sorrow Ford with her hands in the water and her stone held against her breastbone, and she did not speak — she had nothing left to speak with — but she *asked*, the way you ask a thing you have loved your whole life: by opening yourself and letting it see everything.

The river saw fifteen years of Quentin Iversen's memories burning in the fire-mountain. It saw a child in a cage of white canvas. It saw the Ministry's siege lines, ranked along the mountain's western shoulder, four hundred inspectors with resonators and unmaking-rods, digging their little arithmetic into the world's oldest bone.

And the river said yes.

It came down out of the high country all at once. Not a flood — Maren had seen floods, and floods were stupid, indiscriminate things. This was a *decision*. The whole watershed of Sorrow Ford stood up from its bed and went where it was pointed, carrying boulders like thoughts, carrying the winter's ice, carrying every truth the water had ever shown anyone. It hit the siege lines at the hour before dawn, when the watch fires were low and the inspectors were dreaming of immortality.

Maren rode the crest.

She was eighty-one years old and she rode a river like a girl rides her father's shoulders, and the water bore her gently over the barricades while it tore the barricades apart. Tents went first. Then the resonator wagons, which screamed when they drowned, a sound like chalk on slate played backward. The inspectors scattered, and the water did not chase them; the river had no interest in men. Only in what men had built. It took the instruments, the cages, the listening-sigils, the neat white catalogues of names — took them all and ground them smooth against the mountain's flank until they were indistinguishable from mud.

Below her, through six feet of churning dark, Maren saw a tent that would not drown.

Corvane had raised his command pavilion on a plinth of poured stone, and the river broke around it in two white halves, roaring, and could not touch it. Inside, she knew without knowing how she knew, a man was calmly reorganizing his numbers around a new variable.

*Let him count,* Maren thought, at the river, or perhaps the river thought it through her. *Count what's left.*

What was left was not much, and getting less, and none of it dry.

She came ashore on the mountain's knee, soaked to the ribs, laughing silently, and Brannoc caught her arm before she fell.

"Show-off," he rumbled.

She grinned at him with the whole of her ruined throat, and pointed up.

### Brannoc

The chain sang.

Brannoc had forged it across forty days and forty fires with ore he'd sworn never to touch again — dragon-iron, the deep vein, the stuff that remembered being alive — and he had told himself the whole time that it was just metal, that metal was honest work and grief was dishonest rest, and then Ash had carried it up the mountainside wrapped in a blanket like a sleeping child, and the moment the air touched it, the moment it came within sight of the fire-mountain's bleeding crown —

— it *sang*.

Not a ringing. A song. A low three-note phrase that repeated and repeated, patient as breathing, and every dwarf in Brannoc's line felt it in their teeth, in the bones behind their eyes, in the place where their dead daughters lived.

"It's calling the others," Brannoc said. His voice came out wrecked. He cleared his throat and failed to fix it. "River-stone answers to water. Chain answers to fire. And voice—" He stopped. "Voice is already up there."

They climbed.

The Ministry's second line had formed on the switchbacks above the flood line, tighter now, desperate, and Brannoc's dwarves hit them like a door hitting a wall. No killing — Brannoc had drawn that line at dusk and meant it; these were clerks with weapons, children of a city that had been lied to for a century — but there were ways to end a fight that weren't death. Hammers took resonators apart in midair. Shields took rods. The chain sang louder with every step, and where its song reached, the unmaking-rods simply *stopped working*, because you cannot unmake a thing while a made thing is singing in your ears.

An inspector — young, wet, shaking — swung a rod at Brannoc's head and the rod bent like wax.

"You're doing this wrong," Brannoc told him kindly, and put him to sleep with the flat of a gauntlet, and stepped over him, and climbed.

Halfway up, the sky cracked open.

### Corvane

Inspector-General Aldous Corvane had lost four hundred men to a river, and he noted this without emotion, because emotion was noise and noise corrupted data. What concerned him was the variable.

The variable was standing in front of him.

Quentin Iversen had walked into the camp at dawn — out of the smoke, down the switchback, past two collapsed picket lines — with his hands open and empty, an old man in a scorched coat, and he had stopped ten feet from Corvane's pavilion and said, "I've come to trade."

"Trade," Corvane repeated.

"Myself for the child."

Corvane studied him. This was the man the files called the Warden of Vell, the gatekeeper who had hidden in plain sight for fifteen years, selling luck-knots and lying with his whole life. Corvane had expected cunning. He had expected, at minimum, a weapon.

Instead he got tiredness.

"She's twelve," Quentin said. "She spent a year walking here on rumors. She has burns on her feet from your sigils. Whatever you think you're building, Aldous—" he used the first name deliberately, and Corvane felt it land somewhere inconvenient, "—she doesn't have to be part of it. I do. Take me instead. You'll get better numbers anyway. I'm the one who closed the gate. My memory of it is already in the fire. I'm worth more than she is, by any measure you use."

"That isn't true," Corvane said, "and we both know it. She's the seal. She's the only living thing that belongs to both worlds." He folded his gloved hands. "But I appreciate that you offered. It tells me exactly what you are."

"And what's that?"

"A man who has finally found something he won't calculate." Corvane almost smiled. "Do you know, I spent years thinking you were a coward? The file says cowardice. The file says you let Wren take the price so you wouldn't have to pay it. But cowards don't walk into camps. Cowards don't give rivers their names back." He rose. "You're worse than a coward, Quentin. You're a convert. And converts can't be reasoned with. They can only be removed."

He signaled, and four inspectors came out of the pavilion's shadow with bindings woven from null-silk, and Quentin did not resist, because resisting had never been the plan.

The plan was the ten minutes.

Behind the pavilion, small and quick as a mouse, a woman with no name slipped under the canvas wall.

### Wren

Wren did not remember being a keeper.

She remembered fragments: the taste of river-water that showed truths. A forge-song in three notes. The weight of a child asleep against her shoulder — though whose child, she could not say, and the not-saying hurt worse than the forgetting ever had.

What she remembered, newly, furiously, since the fruit in the orchard and the fire on the mountain and the sound of one particular man saying her name like an apology — what she remembered was *him*. Not his face. His choice. Some root-deep part of her, older than the traded name, had been standing in that orchard night watching Quentin Iversen walk away from the price, and it had never once stopped watching, and now it had teeth.

She moved through the camp like a rumor. The Ministry had never catalogued her; a woman with no name does not appear in ledgers. She passed between the tents, following the thing she could feel in her sternum — a cold-burning thread that pulled toward the center of the camp, toward the big transport cage with the null-silk lining, toward—

Ash.

The child sat cross-legged in the cage, wrists bound, and looked up at her with eyes the color of both skies, and did not look surprised.

"I knew someone would come," Ash whispered. "I didn't know it would be you."

"Do you know me?" Wren asked. Her voice cracked on it. Everyone knew her lately. It was unbearable and it was bread.

"You're the third keeper." Ash lifted bound hands, showing a scrap of scale tied at the wrist — Ember-that-Waits' own, shed and given. "You're the voice. And the voice is the last piece, and the fire's almost out, and there isn't much time, so—" the child swallowed, and for one moment looked every day of twelve years old and terrified, "—so I need you to be angry a little longer. Can you do that?"

Wren found, examining herself, that anger was the one supply she had in abundance.

"Yes," she said.

Guards came around the corner of the cage then — two of them, rods drawn — and Wren turned to face them, and opened her mouth, and spoke.

Not words. The First Tongue doesn't have words the way other languages do; it has *names*, and she named the rods. She named them honestly, completely, the way you'd name a frightened animal, and the rods remembered what they were before the Ministry machined them — ore in a hillside, warm in the dark, belonging to no one — and they went home to that memory so fast they fell out of the guards' hands and lay in the mud humming like contented bees.

The guards stared. Wren stepped past them and tore the null-silk off the cage bars with her bare hands, and the silk, having no name anyone respected anymore, gave up.

Ash came out of the cage into her arms, and the child was lighter than the fragment-memory had promised, and Wren held on for exactly one second — she counted it, one full second, the only luxury she allowed — and then they ran.

### Ash

Above them, the mountain was dying in colors.

Ash felt it through the scale at their wrist and the seal in their blood: the great slow heartbeat stumbling, forty-two, thirty-eight, thirty-one, each beat further apart, the light at the crown going from gold to orange to a bruised and guttering red. Somewhere inside the stone, something ancient was trying to stay awake long enough to hear the covenant spoken, and it was running out of *listening*.

They ran up the switchbacks through the battle's wreckage — flooded lines below, singing chain above, the air full of river-smell and forge-smoke and the sweet green stink of magic coming back after a century of drought. Brannoc met them at the third turn, hammer shouldered, blood on his beard that wasn't his.

"The chain's at the crown," he said. "Maren's holding the spring. Where's Iversen?"

"Traded," Wren said, and her voice shook. "He gave himself for the child."

Brannoc spat. "Of course he did. Fifteen years too late and exactly on time. Come on."

They climbed the last stretch together — smith, ferry-woman, nameless keeper, living seal — and the chain was waiting at the crown in its circle of dwarves, singing its three notes to a sky going grey at the edges, and beside it the spring pool Maren had called up from the mountain's own heart steamed and waited, and the scale on Ash's wrist burned cold, and far below, small as an ant, a man in white gloves was beginning to climb.

"He'll bring the primary resonator," Ash said. Everyone turned. "I can feel it. It's — it's tuned to me. To the seal. If he fires it at the mountain while the fire's this weak, it won't just kill Everwood. It'll take Vell's memory with it. Everyone he's trying to save. He doesn't know. His numbers are wrong and he doesn't *know*—"

"Then we finish this before he finishes climbing," Brannoc said.

Maren took the stone from her breastbone and set it in the spring. The water lit from beneath, silver-green, remembering.

Brannoc brought the chain, and it quieted in his hands the way a hound quiets at the den mouth, and laid itself ring by ring around the pool's rim.

Wren knelt, and breathed, and waited for her voice to be needed.

And Ash stood at the center of it, above the drowning camp, above the climbing inspector, above the whole unraveling seam of the world, and put a hand on the scale and a hand in the water and listened for the last heartbeat of the last dragon.

Thirty-one seconds apart.

Twenty-nine.

"Ember-that-Waits," Ash said, in the First Tongue, the language their mother had sung over their cradle in a village that no longer existed. "*We brought you a reason.*"

Deep in the mountain, something ancient opened one eye.

Far below, Corvane reached the second switchback, raised the resonator, and began, carefully, to aim.

* * *

The fire saw them coming and had one heartbeat left to decide what it believed in.
