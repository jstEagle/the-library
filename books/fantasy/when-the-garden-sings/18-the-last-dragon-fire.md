## 18. The Last Dragon-Fire

The mountain breathes.

Ash feels it before they see anything—a slow pull and push in the air, like the whole peak is a chest rising and falling, except each breath takes longer than the last. In. A hundred heartbeats of in. Then out, warm and thin, smelling of cinders and something older underneath, something like rain on hot stone.

They climb through the broken gates of the Ministry's siege camp—abandoned now, tents shredded, siege-engines toppled into the ravine where Maren's river took them—and up a stair carved into the living rock. The stair is wide enough for ten men abreast. It was built for pilgrims, Ash thinks. It was built for people who came here singing.

Nobody has climbed it singing for a long time.

Quentin walks ahead of them with his lantern held low, and every few steps he glances back—not at Wren, not at Brannoc, but at Ash. Checking. He's been doing that since the ford, since the confession, since all the things Ash saw in the orchard's memory-fruit and never said aloud. Ash used to think the looking meant Quentin didn't trust them. Now they understand it's the opposite. It's the way you watch something you're afraid to lose.

Wren walks beside Ash without touching. She still moves like someone wearing her own body as a borrowed coat, but her voice came back at Hollowmere, whole and furious and hers, and when she speaks now the mountain seems to lean in and listen. Even the stone remembers her.

"Almost there," Brannoc rumbles behind them. His hammer rides his shoulder and his reforged chain coils across his chest, links as long as Ash's forearm, and every so often one link shivers and rings softly against its neighbor, though nothing touches it. "I can feel her from here. Feel how *thin* she is."

*Her.* Ash has been thinking of the fire as an it. A thing. A furnace at the world's center that either burns or doesn't. But Brannoc says *her*, and Wren says *she*, and even Quentin—who spent fifteen years pretending none of this existed—says it quietly, like a name he's ashamed to have forgotten.

Ember-that-Waits.

Ash puts a hand against the rock as they climb. The stone is warm. Not fire-warm. Body-warm.

*Oh,* Ash thinks. *Oh no.*

* * *

The heart of the mountain is a cavern so vast the lantern-light dies before it finds any wall. They stand on a ledge above a floor of black glass, and in the center of that glass floor lies the dragon.

She is smaller than Ash expected. That's the first cruelty of it. Songs describe dragons as storms given scales, as mountains that learned to move—but Ember-that-Waits is gaunt, folded in on herself like a bird in winter, her hide gone grey as old ash except along the spine, where a single line of ember-light still runs from skull to tail-tip. Faint. Flickering. Like a lamp in a window of a house where everyone has fallen asleep.

Her eye opens when they step onto the glass.

It does not focus. It looks at them the way you look at a sound in another room—aware, too tired to be afraid, too tired to be glad. The eye is the size of a cartwheel and the color of cooling coals, and Ash walks toward it before anyone can stop them, bare feet silent on the warm glass, because there is a word rising in their chest in the First Tongue and it will not wait for permission.

"*Sethra-vael.*"

*I see you.*

The great eye shudders. Focuses. Finds them.

And the mountain exhales, and the breath of it nearly knocks Ash down, and somewhere far above, snow that hasn't moved in years slides off a peak and falls glittering through the grey sky.

"Ash." Quentin's voice, tight. "Ash, come back here."

But the dragon is speaking now, and her voice doesn't come through ears. It comes up through the glass, through Ash's feet, through the bones of them, in words that are half language and half memory:

*Little seal. Little door-child. You came.*

"I came," Ash says aloud, and hears their own voice crack, and keeps walking until they are standing between the dragon's foreclaws, which are curled around—Ash's breath catches—a hollow in the glass floor, a basin worn smooth by centuries, and inside the basin lies a bed of coals that are not quite dead. Not quite alive. The last of the last of the fire.

Behind them, footsteps. Quentin arrives first, then Wren, then Brannoc filling the space like a wall. None of them speak. Even Brannoc, who hates dragons, has taken off his hat.

"You're dying," Ash says. It isn't a question.

*Everything made of story dies when the story ends,* the dragon says. *I have waited a long time for mine to end well.*

"The covenant—" Quentin starts, and stops, and Ash watches him struggle the way he struggled over soup in the shop, piecing things together, hating every piece. "The covenant needs fuel. It always needed fuel. Memories, voices, names—that's what the keepers paid. That's what I should have—"

*No.* The word comes up through the floor hard enough to make the glass ring. *You still don't understand, warden. None of you do. Fifteen years you starved me with your small payments, your pieces, your prices—and the fire grew smaller and smaller, because you thought I was a hearth. A thing you feed.*

The ember-line along her spine gutters. Ash's chest goes cold.

*A fire is not fed,* she says, gentler now, the way you'd speak to a child at bedtime. *A fire is *given a reason*. Why do you think your mother's little charms worked, little seal? Fever-charms, luck-knots, the knot that kept your door shut against the inspectors? Not because magic leaked through a gate. Because somewhere, a fire remembered being loved, and love is stubborn, and it burned a little longer than it should have.*

Ash looks down at the pendant at their throat—the dragon-scale, burning cold against their skin since the night it appeared, since the night their village unmade itself around them. Their mother's voice saying the old words. Their mother's hands tying the knot.

"What do you need?" Ash asks. And then, because the answer is already rising in them like water finding its level: "What do you actually need?"

*Not memories,* the dragon says. *Memories are only the vessel. I need what's carried in them. A story worth burning for. Held whole, held willingly, spoken by a mouth that belongs to both worlds—because the covenant was never a contract between realms, child. It was a promise that neither world would be allowed to forget the other. And a promise only lives while someone speaks it.*

The cavern is very quiet. Somewhere high above, the breach in the mountain where Corvane's forces will come echoes faintly with the sounds of regrouping. They don't have long. Ash knows it the way they know weather coming.

"The First Tongue," Wren says softly. She has knelt at the edge of the basin, and her restored voice trembles but holds. "Spoken by someone born across the threshold. That was always the sealing-word. That was why the original pact named a keeper from each side and a seal from both."

Everyone is looking at Ash.

Ash looks at the coals. At the dying light. At the enormous tired eye.

They are eleven years old. They walked a year through a country that wanted them erased, following rumors of a keeper, sleeping in ditches, learning which inspectors wear white gloves and which ones just wear boots. They watched their village become a rumor, their mother become a name on a list, their own name become something they had to whisper to remember.

And now a mountain-sized dying creature is looking at them with the patience of centuries, waiting for them to say the words that will decide whether two worlds live or end, and there is no adult in this cavern—no warden, no bridge-keeper, no smith, no returned ghost of a mentor—who can do this for them. Quentin's face is doing something terrible and tender. Wren's hand has found Ash's shoulder. Brannoc is gripping his hammer like prayer.

*They would take it from you if they could,* the dragon says, only to Ash, only through the bones. *That is what makes them worth saving.*

"I'm scared," Ash says. Out loud. In Vellish, because some things should be said in the plain tongue.

*Good,* says Ember-that-Waits. *Fear is the body telling the truth. Burn it too.*

Ash kneels at the basin's edge. The coals below are barely glowing, and this close Ash can feel the cold coming off them, the terrible cold of something that was once the warmth of the world. The scale at their throat has stopped burning cold. It is warming. It knows.

They close their eyes.

And they begin, in the First Tongue, the language older than kingdoms, the language their mother sang over fevers and stitched into luck-knots, the language that made every ward in a hidden shop answer aloud on a winter night—to tell the fire a story.

Their story.

* * *

They tell it in fragments, the way memory comes: the smell of their mother's bread. The knot she tied on their wrist the morning the inspectors came, saying *this will hold what matters*. The white gloves lifting names off doors like flies off honey. The year of ditches and rumors. The door in Vell with the crooked charm-sign, and the man who opened it and flinched—and then, crucially, did not close it.

They tell the orchard, and the fruit full of a stranger's guilt. The ferry-woman whose river showed truths. The smith who hated dragons until grief turned around and showed him its other face. The city that forgot its name, and the woman inside it who hated a man she couldn't remember, which was its own kind of remembering.

They tell the river flooding the siege lines. The chain singing. The warden walking unarmed into the enemy camp, holding out his own self like a coin.

And they tell the part they have never told anyone, the part that has been sitting in their chest like a swallowed stone since the orchard: *I was afraid of him. I saw what he did and I was afraid of him, and I stayed anyway, because he looked at me like I was the first true thing he'd touched in fifteen years, and I decided that a person who looks at you like that might still become someone worth the walk.*

The coals are listening.

Ash can feel it—the heat beginning to turn over beneath the ash, slow as a sleeper rolling toward a window at dawn. Each memory laid into the basin lands like a spark on tinder. The dragon's ember-line brightens, vertebra by vertebra, a dawn running backward down her spine.

Quentin makes a sound. Ash doesn't look at him. If they look at him they will stop, and they cannot stop, because the story is almost at its end and the end is the price and the price is the point.

Because here is what Ash understood, kneeling in the dark with a dying god's heartbeat under their palms: the fire doesn't need fuel. Fuel runs out. The fire needs a reason, and reasons are heavy, and no adult in this cavern could carry this particular weight because it isn't theirs. It was always going to be theirs. Born across the threshold. Belonging to both worlds. The living seal.

A seal isn't a person, Ash thinks, speaking the old words faster now, giving the story away piece by piece, watching it leave and feeling lighter and heavier with every breath. A seal is a promise that chose a body.

The last of the story is the simplest. Ash leans over the basin, over the coals that are now glowing a deep, waking red, and says—in both tongues at once, the First Tongue and the plain one braided together, because that is what they are, both worlds in one small stubborn body:

"My name is Ash. My mother spoke the old words and the new ones. I crossed the gate and I did not die and I did not turn back. These worlds are both my home, and I am the promise that neither one gets to forget the other. Burn for us. We are worth it. I will hold the door."

The coals flare.

* * *

Light goes up through the mountain like a shout through a house.

Ash is thrown back—caught, vaguely, by arms; Brannoc's maybe, or Wren's, or both—and for a moment everything is white and roaring and the taste of copper and cinnamon, and through it all the dragon's voice fills the world, no longer coming up through the floor but everywhere, vast and waking and *laughing*:

*YES. Oh, little seal. OH, YES.*

The ember-line blazes skull to tail. Grey hide flushes with color—deep bronze, old gold, veins of living crimson spreading like sunrise through the scales. The great eye, when it opens again, is molten. Ember-that-Waits rises, unfolding joint by ancient joint, wings spreading wider than the cavern should allow, and the black glass floor sings beneath her, and every crack in the mountain seals with a sound like a struck bell.

Above them, muffled by miles of stone, Ash hears the other sound: the breach splintering. Boots. Shouting. Corvane's forces, coming down the pilgrim stair with their white gloves and their machines for unmaking, arriving precisely in time to find the fire they came to harvest fully awake.

"They're in the stairwell," Quentin says. His voice is steady, but his hand on Ash's shoulder is shaking, and he doesn't take it away. "We have minutes. Maybe less."

"Then we finish it," Wren says. She stands in the dragonfire light with her restored voice and her borrowed-coat grace, and she looks more solid than Ash has ever seen her. "Maren's stone. Brannoc's chain. My voice. Ash's tongue. And Quentin—" she pauses, and something passes over her face, grief and fury and tenderness all tangled, "—and whatever Quentin is willing to pay."

Brannoc is already unwinding the chain, links ringing like a choir. Far off in the flooded lowlands, Ash somehow knows, a river-stone is growing warm in a ferry-woman's keeping, and she is already walking toward the mountain, drawn by the pull of the reforging.

The dragon lowers her immense head until one molten eye is level with Ash, near enough that Ash can see their own small reflection swimming in it.

*You held the door,* she says. *Now we hold it together. Bring me the keepers, little seal. And tell the warden—*

The mountain shudders. Dust sifts from the ceiling. From the stairwell, closer now, comes the measured, courteous voice of Inspector Corvane, echoing down through the breach, calling out words that land in Ash's chest like ice:

"—no need for further suffering, Master Iversen! I've merely come to collect the child!"

Ash looks at Quentin. Quentin looks at Ash.

The fire roars at their backs, and the enemy is on the stairs, and the covenant is waiting for its final price.
