## 22. When the Garden Sings

The spring came to both worlds at once, which had not happened in living memory, because living memory was now mostly Quentin's, and his did not go back far.

In Vell the thaw came first as water — gutters running loud down Copperlane, icicles letting go of the Ministry eaves all at one hour of one morning, as if someone had counted to three. In Everwood it came as color: the ash-grey sky cracking along a seam of blue, the backward rivers hesitating in their channels, then turning — slowly, with the creak of old hinges — toward the sea again.

Maren stood at Sorrow Ford and felt the current change against her legs before she saw it. She hadn't spoken since her voice was taken, but she laughed anyway that morning, a sound like water over stones, and the river laughed back.

* * *

Brannoc heard it from the Cinderholds: a note out of the reforged chain where it hung above his forge, a single clear tone like a struck bell that would not stop. He put down his hammer and listened until his eyes stung. The chain sang only once before, he told his apprentices — the day it was forged, three hundred years ago, when the covenant was young and everyone involved still believed it would hold.

"It's singing again," he said. "Get back to work."

He said it gruffly, but he let them stop for an hour anyway, all of them standing in the forge-light listening to a piece of metal be glad.

* * *

Ash split the season between two doors.

That was the arrangement now, loose and unlegislated: mornings in Vell, afternoons through the gate beneath the floorboards of a shop that no longer sold harmless charms, evenings wherever the letters needed carrying. Ash ran messages between realms the way other children ran errands for their mothers — dragonfruit wrapped in cloth, Wren's neat lists, Maren's answers written in water on paper that dried into poems. The Ministry had tried, in the first weeks, to reassert itself. Then Corvane's reports had come back changed — shorter, quieter, ending in questions instead of orders — and by midwinter the white gloves had stopped appearing at all. Nobody quite knew what to file under "the inspector knelt down in a mountain and wept." The archives left a blank page. It was the most honest thing the Ministry had done in a century.

On a bright morning in the new spring, Ash came up through the floorboards into the smell of bread and ink, and found the shop full of people.

Not customers. Well — customers too. But also Wren, arguing cheerfully with Brannoc about load-bearing charms; Maren seated by the window with her hands folded, watching the street like it was a river; and Quentin behind the counter, selling a luck-knot to a woman who had clearly been crying and was clearly laughing now, and doing it all with the careful, courteous attention of a man meeting everyone for the first time, every time.

Because he was.

"Ah," Quentin said when he saw Ash, and something in his face opened — recognition of a shape without a name. "There you are. Your friend says you've been across the water." He nodded at Wren. "She says I know you. She says we're—" He paused, and Ash watched him reach for the word and find the shelf empty, the way they always did, and watched him smile anyway, because he had learned to smile at the empty shelf. "Family, she says. I'm afraid I'll have to take her word for it."

"That's all right," Ash said. "I can tell you how we met. Again."

"Again," Quentin agreed gravely. "You always say that part like it's funny."

"It is a little funny."

"Good. Sit down, then. We've got soup."

It was, Ash thought, taking off their coat, a strange kind of home: a man who didn't remember them, tending a door he couldn't explain why he kept, feeding a table of people who remembered everything he'd lost. But it was theirs. And it was warm.

* * *

Wren had learned to live beside a ghost of herself.

That was what Quentin was, if she was honest — not the man she had known, but his echo, kind where the man had been sharp, brave where the man had flinched, generous with a freedom she'd never seen in him before because the fear that had governed him had burned away with the memories it lived in. Some days she grieved the man who was gone. Most days she liked this one better, and hated herself a little for it, and got on with things.

She had taken a room above the shop. She had taken, more slowly, the work of teaching him who he had been — carefully, in pieces, the way you feed a fire that must not flare. She told him about Vell's outlawed years and never about the night the covenant broke. That story she kept for herself, or thought she did.

Then the tree came up.

They found it in the orchard on the first true day of spring, all of them together, which was how the orchard seemed to arrange things now. It grew in the middle of the rows of memory-trees, in the exact place where Ash had once touched a bough and seen a betrayal — a young tree, silver-barked, already heavy with fruit that glowed faintly from inside, each one holding a season of light.

Fifteen trees' worth of seasons, gathered onto one small crown.

Quentin stood before it a long time. The others gave him room.

"Those are mine," he said at last. Not a question. He had learned to recognize himself by feel now, the way you recognize your own handwriting. "Aren't they. The fifteen years."

"Yes," Wren said.

"But they aren't going back into me."

"No." She took his hand. His grip was steady; hers was not. "Memory isn't a possession here anymore, love. The garden taught us that. You paid them into the fire, and the fire is the orchard, and what the orchard grows, it shares. Anyone may taste a season. Anyone who needs it."

"And me?"

"You may taste them too," Wren said. "Whenever you want. As a stranger." Her voice caught. "I'm sorry. I know it's a strange inheritance. It's the only kind this family has ever given."

Quentin looked at the tree a long moment more. Then he reached up — he was tall enough, barely — and picked a single fruit, and turned it in his fingers like a charm he wasn't sure worked.

"Not yet," he said. "But soon. I think I want to meet him properly. The man who kept the door." A pause. "Was he any good?"

Ash answered before anyone else could, the way children do. "He was terrible," Ash said. "And then he was wonderful. Sometimes in the same day."

Quentin laughed — a real laugh, unburdened by anything — and put the fruit in his pocket like a letter he'd read later.

* * *

Corvane came to the orchard in April.

He arrived alone, on foot, in his grey coat, though the white gloves were gone — nobody knew where; some said buried, some said burned, one rumor held that he'd given them to Maren's river and the river had refused them. He walked the rows of memory-trees without touching anything, and the keepers watched him from the gate, and he pretended not to notice that they watched, which was its own kind of courtesy.

At the new tree he stopped.

"The fifteen years," he said quietly. "Of the warden's watch."

"You knew about them?" Wren asked.

"I knew there was a cost. I spent a decade trying to learn its size." He looked at the glowing fruit with an expression that had once passed for curiosity, back before certainty had eaten the rest of his face. "We believed memory was a resource. Something to be harvested, allocated, optimized. A city that forgets its grief is a city at peace." His jaw tightened. "I stood in Hollowmere. I saw what peace costs. I have been trying to write the report on my own error for five months and the page keeps coming back blank, and I finally understand that the blankness is the finding."

"What will you do?" Ash asked.

"I don't know," Corvane said, and it was the most astonishing sentence he had ever spoken aloud. He reached toward the tree, hesitated — asked, with a glance, permission — and when Wren nodded, he picked a fruit.

He ate it standing among the rows, and the keepers watched a man taste a stranger's loneliness, and vigilance, and small daily courage, and the quiet dread of counting days. When it was done he stood very still. Then he bowed — not to them, exactly; to the tree, or to the man whose seasons it bore — and walked back out of Everwood without another word.

Three days later the Ministry of Unmaking published its final act: it dissolved itself, and turned its archive of collected names over to the orchard for planting.

* * *

They tended the garden together, because that was the whole secret, and it had been the whole secret all along.

The old covenant had failed, Quentin's returned memories made plain to anyone who tasted them, not because the magic was weak but because the pact had been maintained by sacrifice alone — one keeper paying, year after year, while the others forgot to ask. A garden tended by one gardener dies of solitude no matter how faithfully it's watered. So now the watering was shared. Maren came upriver twice a week and spoke to the roots through the irrigation channel; the trees near her rows grew fruit that tasted like forgiveness. Brannoc forged trellises and gates and, when pressed, small bells, and denied enjoying any of it. Wren kept the records — not ledgers, stories, one per tree, so that no memory in the orchard would ever again be ownerless. Ash carried those stories between worlds and read them aloud to anyone who sat still long enough, which in Vell, these days, was nearly everyone.

And the garden sang.

Nobody could explain the singing. On warm evenings, when the light went gold through both realms' open sky, the orchard produced a sound like many small voices humming in tune — not words, just harmony — and travelers came from both sides of the gate to stand in it, and some wept, and some danced badly, and one elderly couple from Hollowmere, blank-faced citizens for forty years, heard it and remembered their wedding, and held each other in the rows until dark.

Wren heard it best on the evening she picked her first fruit from Quentin's tree.

She chose a summer one — she could see the season in its glow, the fifteenth summer, the last one before everything broke. She ate it sitting on the ground between the rows with the singing all around her, and it poured into her: a younger Quentin locking the shop at dusk, checking the floorboards, counting days; Quentin buying two cups of cider and drinking both, because there was no one to share with; Quentin practicing, alone at midnight, the words he swore he'd forgotten — *her name*, he was practicing her name, saying it to an empty room like a prayer he was ashamed of —

Wren laughed.

It startled her out loud, a bright cracked sound, the first true laugh anyone in the orchard had gotten from her since the covenant broke, and Maren looked up from the far row and grinned like a woman who'd been waiting twenty years to hear it, and Ash dropped an armful of letters running over to see.

"What?" Wren said, wiping her eyes. "What's funny?"

"You are," said Ash. "Everything's funny. Didn't you know?"

"I didn't," Wren said, and laughed again, helplessly, at the sheer absurdity of a man practicing her name to an empty room for fifteen years rather than walk ten steps through a door and knock. "Oh, that fool. That brave, cowardly, ridiculous fool."

She looked up at the tree, at all the remaining seasons glowing in its crown like lanterns waiting to be lit, and understood at last what the payment had bought — not his forgetting, but their keeping. Every year he surrendered was a year saved somewhere it could be found again. He hadn't lost his life. He'd planted it.

"He gets to taste these too," she said firmly. "Whenever he wants. All of them. I don't care what the rules are."

"He knows," Ash said.

"How?"

Ash shrugged, the shrug of a child who has stopped being surprised by such things. "He says the fruit looks like something he wrote. He says he recognizes the handwriting."

* * *

On the last evening of spring, the shop stayed open late.

Quentin sold charms until the light failed — real ones now, luck-knots that actually bent luck a little, fever-charms that hummed when you held them to a hot forehead — and then he locked the door out of habit, then unlocked it again out of newer habit, and left it open to the street. Through the floorboards, faintly, the gate hummed. It hummed all the time now, like a cat.

His friends were still at the table: Wren arguing with Brannoc, Maren writing something luminous in spilled tea, Ash half-asleep against Maren's shoulder with a dragon-scale pendant rising and falling on their chest. The fire in the hearth had come, somehow, to burn with a color none of them could name, warm and slightly golden-green, and Quentin suspected — though he could not have said why he suspected it — that it was the same fire as the mountain's, and the orchard's, and the one under his own ribs.

He stood a moment looking at all of it. The strange family. The open door. The child who called him family and could not, tonight, be bothered to explain why.

"Ash," he said gently. "Wake up a moment. Tell me something."

Ash surfaced, blinking. "Mm?"

"How did we meet? You keep saying you'll tell me. Everyone keeps telling me I should ask." He smiled, rueful, patient, a man content to be handed his own life back one piece at a time. "So I'm asking. How did we meet?"

Ash smiled sleepily, the way you smile at a question you have been saving up the answer to for months.

"You counted the days," Ash said. "Since the gate last hummed. And the number frightened you. And then one night, on the eve of the winter census—"

Outside, on the corner of Copperlane, a lamp flickered — and steadied — and the whole street smelled suddenly, impossibly, of blossoms.

"—there was a knock," Ash finished softly, "in the Old Tongue. And every ward on your house answered aloud."

Quentin went very still.

Somewhere far below the floorboards, deep beneath the shop, deeper than any gate, something ancient and warm turned over in its sleep and began — faintly, unmistakably, in a voice older than any kingdom — to sing.
