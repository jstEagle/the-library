## 1. The Shop That Sells Nothing

The rain came down on Vell like a debt being collected, and Ansel Nakamura counted the drops the way they counted everything—patiently, without hope of the sum ever changing.

They had opened the shop at dawn, as they did every morning, and performed the small liturgy of a life built to be overlooked: sweep the step, unlock the door, light the lamp in the window with a match like any honest tradesperson. The lamp was not lit with a match. It never had been. But the flame that answered Ansel's breath had been trained, over ten years, to flicker and gutter exactly as a wick should, to lean when the door opened, to cast shadows that fell the way shadows were supposed to fall. Magic, Ansel had learned, was not outlawed in Vell. Only magic that looked like magic.

So the shop sold luck.

Luck was legal. Luck was a trinket, a superstition, a copper spent on hope. The Ministry of Order had ruled, in the third year after the outlawing, that charms of *fortune*—blessed coins, knotted red thread, dried clover pressed in glass—were folk custom, harmless, beneath the dignity of regulation. Ansel had read that ruling eleven times before daring to open their doors, hunting for the trap in it, the clause that would close around their throat. There wasn't one. There never was. That was the cruelest thing about the law: it did not need traps. It only needed you to be afraid enough to make your own.

The shop itself was narrow and deep, wedged between a chandler and a shop that sold, inexplicably, nothing but buttons. Shelves lined the walls from floor to ceiling, crowded with Ansel's inventory: river-stones polished smooth and murmured over until they held a whisper of calm for the anxious; needles that never missed a seam; a jar of buttons—bought from the neighbor at cost, a small joke Ansel permitted themselves—that would always, always match whatever garment you were mending. Small magics. Legal magics. Magics that could be explained away by a skeptical inspector as coincidence, craftsmanship, or the customer's own wishful thinking.

And beneath the floorboards, under the worn oak of the counter where Ansel stood each day like a sentry who had forgotten they were a sentry, the gate slept.

Ansel did not look at it. That was part of the discipline. You did not look at the thing you were guarding, because looking was a kind of touching, and touching was a kind of opening. But they felt it, the way a person feels a healed bone before rain. A pressure. A hum below hearing. The gate had been sealed with seven bindings of Ansel's own making, layered over the older seal, the keeper's seal, the one they had inherited and never asked for. Ten years, and the bindings held. Ten years, and every morning Ansel woke with their first thought already formed: *it held. It held. It held.*

The bell above the door rang at half past nine, and Ansel's hands went still over the river-stones they were sorting.

Not a customer. Customers hesitated in the doorway, dripping, apologetic, wiping their boots. This visitor stepped through as if the door had been opened for them, and the rain seemed to bend around their grey coat rather than soak it.

"Ministry of Order," the visitor said, though the badge on their lapel had already said it. "Routine inspection. Proprietor?"

"Proprietor," Ansel agreed, and smiled the smile they had practiced until it sat on their face like a well-fitted mask. "Welcome to Nakamura's Charms. Mind the step, it swells in wet weather."

The inspector was young. That was worse. Young inspectors had something to prove and nothing to remember; they checked things off their lists with the zeal of the newly converted. This one—a woman with a narrow face and eyes the color of dishwater—produced a slate and chalk and began, without preamble, to walk the aisles.

"License of trade?"

Ansel drew it from beneath the counter, from the drawer that also held, three inches down and wrapped in oilcloth, a ring of keys that had not opened anything in a decade. The license was genuine. Ansel had made it genuine. There were ways, and they had spent the first years of their hiding learning all of them.

"Charm-class goods only?" The inspector ran a finger along a shelf, came away with dust, frowned at it as though dust might be evidence.

"Fortune, comfort, and domestic convenience," Ansel recited. "As defined under the Ordinance of Permissible Practices, section four."

"You know the ordinance."

"I've had ten years of inspections to memorize it." Ansel kept the smile. Inside, the old exhaustion stirred and turned over, a dog too tired to bark. *Here we go again. The same dance, the same steps. Show them nothing. Show them less than nothing. Be a shopkeeper. Be dull. Be the most boring person this woman has ever met, and she will carry your boringness out the door like a shield.*

The inspector paused at the jar of buttons. Picked one up. Turned it in the grey light.

"This one's blue," she said.

"They're all blue," Ansel said. "Blue buttons are lucky. Didn't you know?"

The inspector's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. She set the button down and moved on, and Ansel breathed through the interval of her footsteps the way a diver breathes between strokes.

It was the floor that nearly undid them.

The inspector reached the back of the shop, where the boards changed—older oak, darker, laid in a pattern that a careless eye would read as random and a careful eye would read as a spiral. Ansel had spent a small fortune, in the early years, aging that wood, scuffing it, spilling tallow on it, teaching it to look like nothing. But the rain had come in under the door this morning, as rain did, and where it touched the old oak the boards drank it differently. Darker. The spiral showed, faint as a watermark, for perhaps four seconds.

The inspector's boot stopped an inch from the edge of it.

Ansel's heart did not race. That was the strange thing, the thing they had discovered about themselves in ten years of vigilance: in the moment of true danger, everything in them went quiet and cold and *precise*, some old keeper's discipline rising up from wherever it had been buried, and Ansel hated it, hated that the court beneath the mountain had left this in them like a splinter, this competence at the edge of ruin.

"Mind the wet," Ansel said, and crossed the shop in three steps, and knelt, and pressed their palm flat to the darkened boards as if testing for damp. Under the skin, so lightly it was less a working than a suggestion, they asked the wood to forget. *You are only oak. You have only ever been oak. You were felled, and cut, and nailed, and you have held nothing but the weight of customers and the weight of my standing here.* The boards drank the suggestion the way they drank the rain. By the time Ansel rose, the spiral was a stain, and the stain was drying, and the oak was only oak.

"Damp gets in every winter," Ansel said. "I've been meaning to have it seen to."

"Mm." The inspector wrote something on her slate. Ansel did not ask what. Asking was a confession. "You were registered before the outlawing," she said suddenly. "Nakamura. There were Nakamuras on the rolls. Keepers' guild, minor tier."

The shop went very quiet. The lamp in the window, trained to flicker like an honest flame, held its flicker perfectly, and Ansel loved it a little for that.

"There were a great many people on the rolls," Ansel said. "The Ministry burned most of them, I understand. Very thorough."

"Thorough," the inspector agreed. She looked at Ansel then, really looked, and for a moment the dishwater eyes were not dull at all. "My mother bought a charm once. Before. A stone that made her hands stop shaking. She swore it was the stone. My father swore it was her deciding not to shake." She turned the slate over, as if the chalk marks on it embarrassed her. "I've never decided which of them was right."

"Perhaps," Ansel said carefully, "she was simply lucky that day."

The inspector held the look a moment longer. Then she snapped the slate shut, produced a stamp, and pressed it to the license: *INSPECTED — COMPLIANT — 4th of Rains, Year Ten.*

"Compliant," she said. "For what it's worth, proprietor, your dust is very convincing."

She let herself out into the rain. The bell rang behind her, bright and stupid, and the door swung shut, and Ansel stood in the middle of their shop with their hands trembling now that it was over, because that was the order of things: cold in the fire, shaking in the ash.

They locked the door. They turned the sign. They knelt on the old oak boards, all pretense abandoned, and pressed both palms flat, and let themselves feel it—the hum, the pressure, the vast patient weight of the gate below, sealed and sleeping and *there*, always there, the way a stone at the bottom of a well is there.

"Still here," Ansel whispered to it. "Still me. Still nothing to see."

The gate, as it had for ten years, said nothing.

* * *

The afternoon passed in the small commerce of luck. A fishwife bought a knotted thread for her son's nets and left with shoulders straighter than she'd come. A clerk, grey-faced, bought a river-stone and held it in his fist the whole time he counted out his coppers, and Ansel murmured the calming into the stone a second time when he wasn't looking, because some magics were worth the risk. An old woman came in out of the rain to warm herself and bought nothing and told a story about her late husband, and Ansel listened, because listening was also a kind of luck, and the cheapest to give.

Toward dusk, the light in the shop went blue and strange, the way it did before storms, and Ansel trimmed the lamp and thought about supper and did not think about the gate, because not-thinking was the discipline, the whole discipline, the ten years of it: do not look, do not touch, do not remember the last soul you watched them take through it while you stood with your keeper's oath in your mouth and your courage nowhere to be found.

Do not remember the sound the floorboards made.

The rain thickened. The streets emptied. And then, through the blue dark and the drumming water, Ansel heard footsteps on the step—small ones, uneven, bare feet slapping wet stone—and the bell above the door rang, and the door swung open, and the storm leaned in.

A child stood on the threshold. Barefoot, soaked to the skin, no older than eight, with rain running off her chin and her fists clenched at her sides like someone who had been walking a very long way and had run out of the strength to be afraid.

And when Ansel rose and said, "You'd best come in out of that," the child looked up with eyes that caught the lamplight like deep water, and answered—

—not in the trade-tongue of Vell, not in any dialect of the lowlands, but in the First Tongue, the old speech of the court and the keepers, the language that had been outlawed so thoroughly that hearing it was itself a crime, the language Ansel had not spoken aloud in ten years and had sworn never to speak again.

The child said: *I know where the door is. I can feel it under your floor.*

And in Ansel's chest, beneath ten years of careful silence, the gate hummed—and for the first time, it answered her.
