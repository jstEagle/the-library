## 2. A Child at the Threshold

The words hung in the doorway between them like smoke that would not disperse.

*I know where the door is. I can feel it under your floor.*

Ansel did not move. Ten years of discipline held their body still—the same discipline that had kept their hands from shaking during inspections, their face from flinching when Ministry clerks recited the names of the executed, their voice level as they sold luck-charms to farmers' wives and told themselves, every night, *this is enough, this smallness is enough.*

But beneath the floorboards, under forty years of dust and three layers of replaced oak, something old had opened one eye.

"Come inside," Ansel said again, in the trade-tongue, deliberately flat, deliberately dull. "You'll catch your death."

The child stepped over the threshold without being asked twice. Rain ran off her in sheets—she wore nothing but a thin shift gone gray with water, and her feet were bare, blue-white, cut in two places. She stood on Ansel's clean floor and dripped.

Ansel closed the door. Turned the lock. Then, out of a habit so deep it had become instinct, pressed two fingers against the wood and murmured the quieting charm that made the door sound, to any listening spell, like an empty room. The shop swallowed its own silence around them.

"You shouldn't speak that language," Ansel said, still in trade-tongue, low. "Not here. Not anywhere in Vell. Do you understand me?"

The child tilted her head. Water pooled at her feet, creeping toward the floorboards over the gate, and Ansel watched it spread with the particular horror of a person watching ink spill across a signed confession.

"I understand," the child said—in trade-tongue now, though her accent was strange, vowels rounded like river stones. "But you understood me before. I felt you understand." Her eyes went to the floor. To the exact spot. Not near it, not in the neighborhood of it—to the *spot*, within a hand's width, where the seal lay beneath the boards. "It's singing. It hasn't sung in a long time, has it?"

* * *

Ansel put her by the stove.

This was, they told themselves, practical. The child was hypothermic; dead children in doorways drew inquiries; inquiries were intolerable. So: blanket, from the chest by the counter. Soup, from the pot Ansel had made for one and stretched for two without deciding to. Dry socks, rolled and left by the hearth, which the child put on with the careful deliberateness of someone who had been barefoot for a very long time.

She looked eight years old. Maybe nine. Small-boned, dark-haired, with the rain-washed pallor of someone who'd been walking for days. There was mud to her knees, burrs in her hair, and a raw place on one heel where a blister had broken and kept breaking.

And she was watching Ansel with those eyes that caught lamplight like deep water—not the wary watchfulness of a frightened child, but the patient attention of someone taking inventory.

"I'm Iri," she said.

"Ansel."

"I know." She wrapped both hands around the soup bowl. "Your name is stitched into the door. Under the floor. It's the oldest thread down there." She sipped. Made a face at the salt. Kept drinking. "Everything else is sleeping. You're the only part of it that stayed awake."

Ansel sat down slowly across from her, on the stool they used for mending charms, and studied the child the way they'd once studied unfamiliar sigils—looking for the trap, the trick, the tell.

"The Ministry patrols this street at the ninth bell," Ansel said. "If they find you here—if they hear one word of that language out of your mouth—they will take you to the Hall of Order, and what happens after that is worse than anything you're imagining. So we have until the ninth bell to decide what you are and where you came from. Start with your feet. Why are you barefoot?"

"They took my shoes." Iri said it simply, without self-pity, the way children report weather. "When they took my brother, they took everything he was carrying too. Mine just happened to be on my feet. They didn't think I mattered."

Something cold walked up Ansel's spine. "Who took him?"

Iri set the bowl down. From the fold of her soaked shift—she must have been holding it clenched the whole way through the storm, through whatever miles of road and rain lay behind her—she drew out a disc the size of a coin but heavier-looking, dense, wrong. It was made of a material Ansel knew intimately and had hoped never to see again: bone-pale, faintly warm even in this cold room, veined with hairline channels of gold that were not decoration but circuitry.

A soul-token.

Ansel had made eleven of them. They could still feel each one leaving their hands—the specific resistance of the metal, the way the binding script had seemed to drink light rather than reflect it, the voice of Magistrate Corvane at their shoulder saying *you may stop whenever you like, keeper; you need only say the word and we will find another way to motivate you,* and knowing exactly what *another way* meant, because Ansel had seen the other ways, had helped build some of them.

Eleven tokens. Eleven souls filed into little pale coins and carried down the stair that wasn't there, into the court beneath the mountain, and Ansel had told themselves the lie that keepers tell: *if I refuse, they'll simply do it crueler. My hands make it gentle.* The lie had fit well enough for ten years.

Now one of those lies was sitting on Ansel's hearthstone, wearing borrowed socks.

"Where did you get that?" Their voice came out rougher than intended.

"It's Tam's." Iri turned it in her fingers, and the gold veins pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. "He's my brother. He's fourteen. A man came to our village with papers—real ones, with seals—and said Tam had been chosen for service. Service to the court, he said, and everyone laughed because everyone knows the court is just a story mothers use." Her jaw tightened. "Tam got on the wagon because the man showed a writ with a real seal, and in our village you don't argue with a seal. And then—" she stopped. Started again, quieter. "Then that night, this appeared on our doorstep. On *my* side of the doorstep, like whoever left it wanted me specifically. And it was warm, and it beat like his heart beats, slower, and I could hear him inside it. Not words. Just—him. Being afraid."

The fire popped. Outside, the rain redoubled, hammering the shutters like fists.

"That's a binding vessel," Ansel said. "Whoever gave it to you wanted you to follow it. That's not a gift, child. That's bait."

"I know what it is." No fear in it. Only a flat, exhausted certainty that was somehow worse than tears. "I followed it anyway. Because it points." She held the token out flat on her palm, and Ansel watched—helpless, sick—as the token turned slowly on the girl's hand, like a compass needle, until it pointed down.

Through the floorboards. Through the stone. Through the sealed gate that Ansel had locked with their own blood and their own oath ten years ago and had not touched since.

Beneath their feet, the gate hummed.

Not the low dormant drone Ansel had lived above for a decade, the sound they'd learned to sleep through the way you sleep through surf. This was different. This was *response*. The old harmonics rising through the joists, finding the frequency of the token in Iri's palm, answering it note for note—two instruments tuned to each other waking up across ten years of silence.

The lamps guttered. The soup in the pot shivered into rings. In the window glass, Ansel saw their own reflection flicker and—for half a heartbeat, no more—saw it doubled: the tired charm-seller of Vell, and behind that face, older, graver, robed in keeper's gray, the face they had buried along with the name.

"Make it stop," Ansel whispered.

"I can't." Iri's hand was trembling now, the token buzzing against her palm. "It knows I'm here. It's been pulling me all the way from Harrow Fen. Every night it got louder." She looked up, and there it was at last—the crack under the certainty, the eight-year-old showing through. "It wants me to open it. But I don't know how. I only know the words. I don't know the *way*."

* * *

The ninth bell came and went, and the patrol passed, and Ansel spent it standing at the shuttered window counting boots in the wet street while the child slept—actually slept, curled in the blanket by the stove with the soul-token still clenched in one fist, exhausted past the reach of fear.

Ansel did not sleep. Ansel knelt on the floor over the gate with a lamp turned low and pressed one palm flat to the boards, and listened.

The hum had settled, but it had not stopped. It moved through the wood like blood through a wrist. And threaded through it, unmistakable now that Ansel let themselves hear, was a third voice: thin, high, frayed with terror. A boy's voice. Not words—just presence, the way Iri had described, just *him, being afraid*—radiating off the token in pulses that matched, Ansel realized with a lurch, the pulse of the gate itself. As if the coin were a lung and the mountain below were breathing through it.

*Fourteen years old,* Ansel thought. *They took a fourteen-year-old off a wagon and filed him into a coin, and they sent the coin back to his sister like an invitation.*

No. Not like an invitation. Like a summons. The magistrates never did anything without design. If Corvane—or whatever hand wore Corvane's seal these days—had put that token in a child's path, then a child arriving at the gate was not an accident. It was an errand. Someone below wanted the gate opened, and had decided that a desperate girl with the First Tongue in her mouth was the key that couldn't refuse.

Which meant the moment Ansel touched that seal, they wouldn't be choosing whether to act.

They'd be playing a part someone else had written.

Ansel rose, knees cracking, and looked at the sleeping child—at the burrs in her hair, the bandaged heel, the fist tight around her brother's caged heart—and felt the old familiar architecture of the last decade groan and shift. The invisibility. The smallness. The lie of *enough*. All of it built on one foundation stone: *as long as the gate stays shut, no one else gets taken.*

But the taking hadn't stopped. It had only moved underground, where Ansel couldn't see it. Ten years of selling luck to farmers' wives while the court beneath the mountain bought children by the coin.

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though the shutters were closed.

Below, patient as tide, the gate hummed—and beneath its hum, faint but growing, Ansel heard footsteps on the stair that wasn't there.

Someone was coming up.
