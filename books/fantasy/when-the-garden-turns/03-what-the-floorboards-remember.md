## 3. What the Floorboards Remember

Iri had never slept well in strange houses, and this one was the strangest she'd ever been inside. It wasn't the smell—though the shop smelled of dried lavender and hot iron and something older underneath, like rain on stone that had never seen sun. It was the humming.

She lay on a pallet in the back room, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke, listening to it. The charm-seller—their name was Ansel, though Iri hadn't asked yet; they'd only said *sleep now* in flat city-tongue before blowing out the lamp—had gone quiet an hour ago. The floorboards above her had creaked twice as they crossed to their own bed, then stopped.

But the house didn't stop. The house hummed.

It came from below, through the soles of the pallet, through the packed earth under the shop's foundations. A low note, patient and enormous, like a song sung by something too big to notice its own voice. Iri knew songs like that. Her grandmother used to say the mountain sang to itself in winter, and everyone laughed because Grandmother was half-mad and spoke the old words to her vegetables. But Iri had heard it once, lying against the mountain's flank with Tam, and Tam had heard nothing at all.

*Tam.*

Her hand went to her chest where the token hung on its cord. In the dark it gave off a faint warmth, and beneath the warmth, fainter still, the name etched into it: **Tam**. She pressed her thumb over the letters until they bit.

The humming answered her. Not louder—closer. As if the thing below had leaned in.

Iri sat up.

The blanket slid from her shoulders. The back room was black except for one thin blade of light under the door, and beyond the door the shop waited with all its little hanging charms, hundreds of them, each one a small dead thing pretending to be lucky. She could feel them the way you feel teeth in your mouth: present, dull, harmless. But under the floor—under everything—the hum rolled on, and it was not harmless, and it was not pretending.

She stood. The floorboards were cold. She took two steps toward the door and stopped, because the third board hummed back at her footstep—a different note, higher—and for a moment she felt something pour up through the wood into her bare sole. Not heat exactly. *Memory.* It went through her like cold water through cloth, and for half a heartbeat she saw—

A door. A great round door of green-black metal set in stone, and hands—long-fingered, careful, scarred across the knuckles—pressing a seal of wax and salt along its rim while someone older said, in the First Tongue, *You are the keeper now. The gate sleeps when you sleep. Wake it never.*

Iri snatched her foot back.

The memory didn't leave. It clung to her skin like the damp. She looked down at the third floorboard, ordinary-looking, grey-brown, split at one end—and understood, in the way children understand things adults have trained themselves not to, that this house was not a house. It was a lid. Every board was a scale on the back of something sleeping, and the whole shop was built to keep the humming quiet.

The charm-seller was the keeper.

Iri should have gone back to bed. She thought about going back to bed. Then she thought about Tam's face the day the magistrates' men came to their village, how he'd stepped in front of her without hesitating, how his last look hadn't been afraid—it had been *instructions*, a whole silent letter written in his eyes: *run, don't stop, find the old speaker, don't let them take your name too.*

She walked to the third board and knelt on it.

The memory rose up through her knees this time, generous now, spilling.

* * *

She saw a young person—she couldn't tell if man or woman or neither, the memory kept sliding away from labels—standing in this same room when it was not a shop but a watchpost. Candles burned low. Maps of the mountain's roots covered the walls. The young keeper practiced seals in salt and wax, again and again, tongue between their teeth, and an old woman watched from the doorway and said, *Slower. The gate doesn't reward speed. It rewards certainty.*

She saw years pass in flickers: the keeper grown steadier, walking the perimeter of the buried door each night with a lamp, whispering the First Tongue to it like a lullaby, and the gate answering with that same deep hum Iri felt now—contented, drowsy, *held*. The keeper smiling at it. Actually smiling. Loving it, maybe, the way you love a thing that trusts you completely.

She saw the night everything changed.

Lamplight. Shouting upstairs in the world above—boots, the crash of a door, men in grey-and-gold livery moving through the streets with lists. The Ministry had come to Vell. The keeper stood in the watchpost holding the lamp with white knuckles while hammering shook the front of the building, and behind the green-black gate, faintly, something stirred and pressed, and the keeper whispered *no, no, sleep, please sleep,* and the gate slept, because it trusted them.

Then the knocking stopped being knocking and became breaking, and the door above burst open, and there were lanterns and grey coats and a tall man whose voice Iri would know anywhere, even in a memory, even ten years worn: smooth, patient, kind the way a trap is kind.

Magistrate Corvane. Younger then, but the same voice.

He didn't arrest the keeper. That was the terrible part. He sat down at the table, folded his hands, and explained, gently, that the law had changed, that magic belonged to the Ministry now, that the gate beneath this floor would be *nationalized*, its seal broken by proper officials, its keeper given a choice: cooperate, or be made an example of, and the gate opened anyway by men who did not sing to it, who did not lullaby it, who would wake it roughly and use what woke.

*I can protect it better than they can,* the keeper said. *If you leave it to me. Sealed. Forever.*

*Forever is a long word,* Corvane said, smiling. *Let's begin smaller. You will craft binding charms for the court. Small ones. Legal ones. And in exchange, this floor stays yours, and the gate stays asleep, and no one is ever taken from this house.*

And the keeper—who sang to the gate every night, who loved it, who was its keeper in the oldest sense—looked at their own hands and said yes.

That was the bargain Iri had already guessed at. But the memory wasn't finished. It pushed deeper, into a later night, a worse one.

* * *

Rain again, but warmer rain, spring rain. The keeper stood in the alley behind the shop, and a woman was there—young, mud on her skirts, a boy of maybe six clutching her leg and a girl-baby wrapped against her chest. The woman spoke the First Tongue in a desperate whisper. *They're coming. They have our names. Please—you're the keeper—you can open ways, hide us below, anyone can see you have power—*

And the keeper looked at the woman, and Iri felt the exact shape of the thought passing through the adult mind, because the memory was generous and cruel that way:

*If I help them, Corvane learns the bargain is broken. If Corvane learns, the gate opens, and what's beneath wakes angry after ten years of my lullabies, and it will not be gentle, and it will not be me who pays.*

Boots at the end of the alley. Lantern-light swinging closer. The woman's breath going thin.

*Please,* she said again.

The keeper stepped back into the shadow of the doorway. Said nothing. Did nothing. Let the darkness of the unlit alcove swallow them, the way they had learned to make themselves invisible, invisible, invisible—ten years of practice folding into one single held breath.

The lanterns passed. The woman was taken, screaming once, muffled fast. The baby girl dropped in the mud of the alley, crying, and lay there in the rain for eleven heartbeats—Iri counted, because the keeper counted, because the keeper couldn't move—before the woman's scream cut short somewhere far away and a neighbor's door finally opened and someone else's hands gathered the child up.

Eleven heartbeats. The keeper stood in the dark and counted every one and did not move.

Then the memory showed Iri what the keeper did next, which was worse than doing nothing: they went inside, descended to the gate, and sang to it. Sang the lullaby with shaking hands. And the gate hummed back, trusting, contented, *held*—and the keeper broke, quietly, alone, in the dark, and sealed that brokenness into the floorboards the way everything got sealed into this house, and told no one for ten years.

The baby in the mud had been Iri.

She knelt on the cold board in the black back room, and her own small body remembered the rain, distantly, the way you remember a fever. The woman taken had been her mother. The boy of six had been Tam. Someone else's hands had raised her afterward, hedge-hands, village hands, hands that taught her the First Tongue because it was all they had left to give.

And the keeper upstairs, sleeping the sleep of the vigilant, had counted her heartbeats in the mud and chosen the gate over her, and had spent ten years telling themselves it was for the gate's sake, for the world's sake, for any sake except the true one, which was fear.

Iri put her palm flat on the floorboard. The house hummed beneath her, patient, enormous, innocent. Even the gate didn't know what its keeper had done. Even the gate still trusted them.

*You let them take my mama,* she said silently, to the ceiling, to the sleeper above. *You watched. You counted.*

And yet—and this was the part that frightened her more than anger did—the memory had shown her something else too, something the keeper probably didn't know was visible from the outside. In every flicker of those ten years, in every nightly descent, every whispered lullaby, every charm crafted under Corvane's smiling orders, the keeper had slipped tiny rebellions into the work. A knot tied wrong on purpose. A syllable inverted in a binding word, so the charm held less than it claimed. Ten years of sabotage so small that no magistrate ever caught it, threaded through every cage their hands had built.

Not innocence. Never that. But not surrender either. Something alive, buried under all that invisibility, still beating.

Iri sat back on her heels. Above her, the floor creaked—the keeper turning in uneasy sleep, dreaming, maybe, of alleys and lanterns.

Down below, the gate hummed on. And tonight, for the first time in ten years, its note had changed. Iri felt it clearly, the way you feel a change in wind. The gate was no longer merely sleeping.

It was listening.

Because a child speaking the First Tongue had walked across its lid, and a token bearing a stolen name had warmed against its scales, and the old magic in the boards was waking one scale at a time, asking the only question gates ever ask their keepers:

*How much longer?*

Iri pressed the soul-token to the floorboard, and the hum rose through it like water finding a crack, and somewhere beneath the mountain, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to dream of doors.

Upstairs, Ansel cried out once in their sleep—a short, sharp sound, like a man hearing his own name called from a long way off.

Then silence. Then footsteps, hurrying, crossing the floor toward the back room.

The doorknob began to turn.
