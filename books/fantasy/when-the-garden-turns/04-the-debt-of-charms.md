## 4. The Debt of Charms

The token sat on Ansel's workbench like a confession they hadn't written yet.

They had turned it over a dozen times since dawn, holding it to the lamplight, tilting it so the etched name—*Tam*—caught the glow. The metal was wrong. Not wrong the way counterfeit coin was wrong, with its too-bright shine and hollow ring, but wrong the way a familiar face is wrong when you see it across ten years and an ocean of forgetting. The alloy had that faint greenish undertone, that whisper of mountain-cold in it even at room temperature. Ansel knew that undertone. They had melted that alloy themselves, once, in a workshop that no longer existed, for a client whose name they had spent a decade trying not to remember.

Corvane.

The soul-token was his work—or rather, it was made from Ansel's work, shaped by Corvane's hands into something Ansel would never have sanctioned. That was the trick of it, the elegant cruelty of it. Ansel had forged the blanks. A hundred of them. Two hundred. They had never counted past the number that let them stop counting. Corvane had taken those blanks and cut names into them, and every name was a person, and every person was now filed somewhere beneath the mountain like a debt owed against a fire.

Ansel set the token down and pressed both palms flat against the workbench, as if they could hold still the shaking that started somewhere behind their sternum.

"You knew," they said aloud, to the empty shop, to the charms hanging in their silent rows—the luck-knots and rain-charms and small warm blessings that were all they permitted themselves anymore. "You always knew what they were for."

The lie of that comforted no one, least of all Ansel. They hadn't known, not at first. That was the story they told themselves, the story they had polished smooth as river stone through ten years of handling: *I didn't know.* He came to the shop before the outlawing was even finished being written, when magic still wore half its old face, and he ordered binding charms—just bindings, just containers, just craft—and Ansel, young and flattered that a magistrate of the court beneath the mountain wanted *their* work, had said yes.

By the time Ansel understood what the bindings held, the order was complete, the payment spent, and Corvane's parting words were already a scar: *"You will make more when I ask. You will tell no one. And you will be grateful that I asked you, because the alternative was someone who would have enjoyed it."*

Ansel had told themselves that was mercy. Ten years later, in the grey light of a morning that smelled of rain and old wood, they finally heard it for what it was: a leash, fitted gently, so the dog would learn to call the collar kindness.

Iri slept upstairs, or pretended to. The child had curled into the narrow bed under the eaves without argument, clutching the second token—her own, unetched, blank as an egg—as if letting go of it might let go of her brother entirely. Ansel had checked on her twice in the night, standing in the doorway like a thief afraid of waking the house. Both times, the child's breathing had been slow and even, and both times Ansel had stood there longer than checking required, watching, feeling the gate beneath the floorboards hum its low note through the soles of their feet like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing.

The token hummed too. That was how Ansel had known, before they ever saw the maker's mark hidden in the rim—that almost invisible spiral Corvane used instead of a signature, because signatures could be traced and spirals could be denied. The token and the gate sang the same note. Whatever was written into Tam's name was anchored on the other side of that seal, filed in whatever cold archive the magistrates kept for the currency of souls.

Which meant the child downstairs had not stumbled to Ansel's door by accident. Which meant someone had sent her—or lost her, or discarded her, which was worse. Which meant the court beneath the mountain knew its ledger had a leak, and a leak this size would bring them down the stair that wasn't there with writs and wardens and that particular bureaucratic patience that was more terrifying than any rage.

Ansel had perhaps a day. Perhaps less.

There was one person in Vell who would know how much less.

* * *

Maren Oduya kept a tea house two streets over, behind a curtain dyed the color of dried blood, and she had been selling information out of its back room since before the outlawing, back when such things were merely impolite rather than capital. She was seventy if she was a day, sharp as a fishhook, and she owed Ansel nothing—which was precisely why she was useful. Debts made people lie. Indifference made them honest.

She took one look at Ansel's face when they slipped through the curtain and said, "Sit down before you fall down, keeper."

"I'm not the keeper anymore."

"You'll be the keeper until they bury you or burn you. Sit." She poured without asking—smoked leaf, bitter, the tea she saved for conversations with teeth in them. "Now. What did Corvane send you?"

Ansel's cup stopped halfway to their mouth.

"How—"

"Because he always sends someone eventually." Maren lowered herself into her chair with the economy of a woman who had stopped wasting motion decades ago. "Ten years ago it was a courier with a sealed writ. Eight years ago it was a hedge-witch named Pell who came in here shaking and left for the mountain and never came back. Now it's you, carrying that look. So. What did he send?"

Ansel set the soul-token on the table between them.

He watched Maren's face do something complicated. The old woman looked at the token the way other people looked at snakes—not with fear exactly, but with the coiled readiness of someone calculating distance. Then she reached out and turned it over with one finger, reading the spiral in the rim, and exhaled slowly through her nose.

"Tam," she read. "Whose child is this?"

"A girl named Iri. She came to my shop last night. Barefoot. Speaking the First Tongue." Ansel heard their own voice flatten, the way it did when emotion got too large to carry at full volume. "Her brother's name is in that metal. And the token resonates with the gate under my floor, Maren. It's anchored down there. Which means the court is still buying."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Outside, Vell went about its lawful morning—a cart rattling past, a vendor crying the price of onions, the ordinary music of a city that had agreed, collectively, to forget what lived underneath it.

"The court was always buying," she said finally. "You just weren't allowed to know what the price was."

"I made the bindings." The words came out of Ansel like splinters coming out of flesh, each one torn. "Two hundred blanks, maybe more. I told myself they were containers. Storage vessels. I told myself—" They stopped. Pressed the heel of their hand against their eye. "What are they *for*, Maren? What does the court want with two hundred souls?"

Maren turned her cup once, twice, a full slow circle on the saucer, and Ansel understood she was deciding how much truth an old friend could carry.

"There's a fire under the mountain," she said. "The last one. Dragon-fire, the real thing, from before the courts, from before the law, from when the First Tongue was just called *tongue*. Everything the magistrates are—their offices, their immortality, their beautiful bone-white cathedral of a court—is fed off that flame. It doesn't burn wood. It never burned wood." Her eyes lifted to Ansel's, and there was no mercy in them, only arithmetic. "It burns souls. Slowly. One at a time, filed and fed, the way a miser spends a hoard. Your bindings weren't containers, keeper. They were *coffers*. Each one holds a purchased soul fresh and whole until the ledger calls for it."

The room tilted. Ansel gripped the edge of the table and the table held, solid, indifferent, while everything they had built their decade upon quietly collapsed.

"I fed the fire," they whispered.

"You fed the cage." Maren's voice softened by a single degree, which from her was a flood of tenderness. "The fire was lit long before you. But yes. Your hands made the coffers. I won't lie to you about that, because you'll need the truth where you're going."

"Where am I going?"

"Where do you think?" She nodded at the token. "That child's brother is in your floor. Or under your floor, through your floor, down the stair that isn't there. And Corvane knows the girl exists, which means the writ is already being drawn, which means you have until tomorrow's inspection, maybe, before the Ministry walks into your shop and finds exactly what they've been looking for these ten years." She leaned forward. "So the real question isn't where you're going. It's whether you go down that stair as the man who built the cages—or as the man who opens them."

"I'm not a man."

"No," Maren agreed, and something flickered at the corner of her mouth that was almost, almost a smile. "You're something rarer. You're the keeper. And keepers, in the end, only get asked one question."

Ansel stared at the token lying on the scarred wood between them, at the little spiral in the rim that had once seemed like craft and now looked like a brand.

"What question?"

Maren stood, gathered the cups, and paused at the curtain with her back turned.

"When the garden turns," she said, "which side of the gate will you be standing on?"

Then she was gone into the front room, and Ansel sat alone with the bitter tea going cold, and the token, and the hum beneath the city that suddenly sounded very much like knocking.

Behind them, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the shop—from the floorboards, from the sealed gate that had waited ten patient years—an answer rose through the stone:

Someone was knocking from the other side.
