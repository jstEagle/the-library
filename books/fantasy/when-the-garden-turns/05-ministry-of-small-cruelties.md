## 5. Ministry of Small Cruelties

The knocking came again at dawn, and this time it wore boots.

Ansel knew the difference the way a sailor knows weather. The gate's knock was deep, patient, a heartbeat in the stone. This one rattled the shop's front door on its hinges—three sharp raps, then silence, then three more, the rhythm of men who expected obedience and had never once been disappointed by it.

"Ministry of Order," said a voice through the wood. "Inspection. Open."

Iri was already awake. Ansel found her standing in the middle of the back room, barefoot on cold boards, her fist closed around Tam's soul-token so tightly that Ansel could see the edges biting her palm. Her eyes were wide, but she wasn't crying. That frightened Ansel more than the door.

"Back room," Ansel whispered, switching to Vellan as though even their mouth needed a disguise now. "Under the counter there's a loose board. Don't touch anything else. Whatever you hear—" they caught her chin, made her look up, "—whatever you hear, you stay quiet as ash."

She nodded once. Then, in the First Tongue, soft as breath: *"They come for me."*

"They come for everyone eventually," Ansel said. "That's not the same as them finding you."

* * *

There were four of them. Ansel counted through the curtain before pulling it aside—two in grey coats with the Ministry's brass scale pinned at the collar, one clerk with a ledger chained to his belt, and one who stood slightly behind the others with his hands folded, watching the shop the way a butcher watches a market. Inspector Halloway. Ansel remembered him from years ago; he'd aged into his suspicion like wine into vinegar.

"Proprietor Nakamura." He didn't wait for greeting. He stepped past Ansel into the shop, boots ringing on the boards that hid everything that mattered. "Routine audit of unlicensed goods."

"My goods are licensed," Ansel said. "Every charm on those shelves carries its seal. You've inspected twice this year."

"And yet." Halloway lifted a luck-knot from the display, turned it over in his gloved fingers. "A child was seen on Wren Street last night. Barefoot. Speaking gibberish. A witness says she came toward your shop like a pigeon to grain." He set the charm down precisely where it had been, aligned to the shelf edge. "You reported no visitors."

"She didn't come here."

"Mm." He walked the aisles slowly, trailing one finger along the shelves, and the clerk followed, writing nothing. It was theater; they both knew it was theater. The real inspection was happening somewhere else—in Halloway's patience, in the way he kept glancing down at the floor.

"The floorboards," Halloway said suddenly.

Ansel's heart did not stop, because they had spent ten years teaching it not to. "What about them?"

"You replaced two sections after the flood. Ministry records note unauthorized timber." He crouched, pressed his palm flat against the boards, and Ansel watched him listen—with his hand, with his whole body, the way dogs listen to earthquakes. Beneath him, the sealed gate held its silence, and Ansel sent every thread of will they possessed into the wards woven under the shop: *be stone, be dust, be nothing worth noticing.*

Halloway frowned. Straightened. Whatever he'd felt, it had slipped through his fingers like water through a closed fist—the contingency charms doing their work, spending decades of hoarded subtlety in a single morning.

"No child, then," Halloway said. "And nothing beneath the floor." But he lingered at the door, and his eyes moved across Ansel's face with terrible gentleness. "You've been quiet a long time, Nakamura. Quiet people are either empty or full. I've always wondered which you are."

He left a chalk mark on the doorframe—a small circle bisected by a line. Return within the week.

Ansel waited until the boots faded down Wren Street. Waited until the kettle's ghost-heat died and the street woke fully and the first carts rolled past carrying ordinary noise. Then they went to the back room, lifted the loose board, and found it empty.

The space under the counter was exactly where Iri should have been, small and curled and breathing—and it was nothing but shadow and dust, and a scrap of paper folded into a triangle, the kind children fold when they are trying very hard to be brave.

Inside, in careful Vellan script learned from someone's stolen lessons:

*I heard them say my name. I went where the house told me. I'm sorry. I'm hiding.*

* * *

The house told her true. Ansel felt it the moment they opened their senses past fear—the contingency charms, laid down a decade ago in the paranoid years, were singing. Not the alarm threads. The *passage* threads. Someone had triggered the old escape lattice, and the lattice only answered to two things: Ansel's blood, or the First Tongue.

Iri had spoken to the house. And the house—built on the bones of a gate older than any law—had listened.

Ansel pulled up the floorboards anyway, checked the gate's seals (intact, humming, patient), pocketed the soul-token's twin resonance-stone, and followed the lattice out through the cellar wall, where a brick slid aside onto a passage that smelled of rain and old coal and the city's buried centuries.

The charms led them down through Vell's hidden ways—an aqueduct leg no map admitted to, a seam between two tenements where the mortar had been charmed soft decades ago, a stair behind a shrine to a saint nobody prayed to anymore. Ansel moved fast, heart hammering, reading the little markers the old spells left for them: a chalk arrow refreshed in fresh hands, a knotted cord hung at eye height, once a sprig of rowan tucked into a crack—rowan, which hadn't grown inside Vell's walls since the outlawing.

Someone was tending the path. Many someones, by the look of it. The contingencies were supposed to have decayed. They hadn't. They'd been *maintained.*

At the mouth of an old ice-house beneath Tanner's Row, the passage ended at a door painted with a sigil Ansel hadn't seen in eleven years: a garden gate, drawn in one unbroken line.

They knocked the way the line was drawn—once around, once through.

It opened on lamplight and herbs drying from rafters and six faces turning toward them, and in the middle of the room, wrapped in a blanket too big for her, Iri sat eating bread with the concentration of the starving.

"You're late," said Maren Ash, hedge-witch, herb-thief, and once—for one incandescent year before the outlawing—Ansel's partner in the smuggling of small magics. She looked older. Everyone did. "Eleven years, Nakamura. We said we'd drink together at midwinter."

"You said that. I said maybe."

"You always said maybe." She crossed the room and took Ansel's face in both hands, and her thumbs found the exhaustion there and read it like tea leaves. "You look like a man who slept in a locked box."

"It was a very comfortable box."

Her smile faded. She glanced at Iri, then back. "The child talks in your sleep-language. Half my people thought it was fever-talk until old Sefa heard her and went white as milk." Maren's voice dropped. "She's got a token, Ansel. We've seen tokens before—we've pulled three of them off dying beggars these two years, all stamped with the same maker's mark. Corvane's mark. Souls going *down*, under the mountain, and none coming back up." She let go of Ansel's face. "We thought you'd know. You built half those bindings."

"I built all of them," Ansel said. The words came out flat and heavy, coins dropped on a table. "Every binding charm the court uses. Corvane coerced the designs out of me before the outlawing finished settling. I told myself they'd sit in a drawer forever. I told myself a lot of things."

Around the room, the other hedge-witches had gone still. An old man by the fire—Sefa, gray-braided, eyes like river stones—spoke without turning. "We knew."

Ansel blinked. "You knew?"

"We've known for two years that souls were being bought." Sefa turned then, and his face held no accusation, only a tired, terrible kindness. "Where do you think the path that led you here comes from? Who do you think kept the rowan growing in the walls? You weren't the only one who went invisible, keeper. Some of us just did it in company."

Maren snorted. "He means we organized. He's embarrassed to say organized."

"There were six of us the first winter," said a round woman near the stove, whom Ansel almost recognized as Tally Ostrow, who used to sell weather-charms to fishermen. "Now there are forty. We pass messages, we hide the marked ones, we pull tokens off corpses and keep them safe so the names aren't lost." She patted a wooden box beside her, iron-banded, covered in protective script. "Forty-one tokens in there. Every one a person somebody loved."

The room was quiet except for the fire and Iri's small chewing. Ansel stood among them feeling something crack open in their chest—not painfully, or rather painfully in the way a healed-wrong bone hurts when it finally sets right. Ten years they had believed invisibility meant solitude, that going dark meant going alone, that survival was a private arithmetic of one. All this time, under the same law, under the same fear, other hands had been keeping the flame. Not hidden from each other. Hidden *for* each other.

"You could have come to me," Ansel said quietly.

"We tried," Maren said. "Three times. Your shop's wards turned us away polite as a customs officer." She shrugged. "So we stopped knocking and started leaving things. The chalk arrows. The rowan. You never asked what they were."

"I thought they were drift. Old magic decaying."

Sefa laughed, low and dry. "Nothing decays if enough people love it."

By the fire, Iri had stopped eating. She was looking at Ansel with that unnerving clarity children carry, and when she spoke it was in Vellan, carefully, each word placed like a stone in a stream-crossing.

"The house told me to run because the men would find the gate. Not me. The gate." She held up Tam's token. "This one is getting warmer. My brother is close. Under the mountain. And the fire is hungry." She said the last word simply, the way she might say *Tuesday*. "I can hear it being hungry."

The hedge-witches exchanged looks. Sefa rose, joints cracking, and fetched from a shelf a clay jar sealed with wax, and set it on the table between them all.

"Then hear the rest of it, keeper," he said, "because you'll want to walk down that mountain alone, and we won't let you, and you should know why before you argue." He broke the seal. Inside lay a single dried ember that had not, impossibly, gone out—glowing faintly, breathing slowly, like something asleep. "Dragon-fire. A crumb of it. It came up the mountain thirty years ago in a pilgrim's lamp, and it's been passed hand to hand ever since, and it has never once been fed on anything but love." He looked around the room—at Maren, at Tally, at the forty faces the network represented. "It doesn't need souls, Nakamura. It never did. The court just prefers souls, because souls don't ask anything back."

The ember pulsed, once, like a small heart agreeing.

"So," Maren said, folding her arms, wearing the grin she'd worn at nineteen while stealing moonlight out of a magistrate's greenhouse. "Now that you're done being invisible—what's the plan?"

Ansel looked at the ember, at the child, at the box of forty-one saved names—and felt, for the first time in a decade, not the weight of the gate above their floorboards, but the weight of every hand that had ever helped hold it.

"The plan," Ansel said slowly, "starts with the fact that I know how the court counts its debts. And tonight—" they touched the warm token in Iri's palm, and felt, far below, the answering hum of a gate that had waited ten years to be useful, "—tonight, the counting changes."

Above them, muffled through earth and stone, the city bell began to ring the hour—but underneath it, deeper than bells, something vast shifted in its long sleep beneath the mountain, and every ward in the room flared silver at once.

Sefa's face went gray. "It knows," he said. "The fire knows we're coming."
