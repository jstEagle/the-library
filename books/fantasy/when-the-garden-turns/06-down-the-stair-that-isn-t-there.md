## 6. Down the Stair That Isn't There

The gate had not changed, but Ansel had.

Ten years of standing over it—sweeping the floorboards above it, oiling them, walking across it with a customer's parcel in hand and their heartbeat steady as a metronome—had worn a groove into them as deep as any stair. They had thought, all these years, that they were guarding it. Standing watch. Now, kneeling on the cold stone of the cellar with Iri beside them and a lantern turned low, Ansel understood they had only been sitting on a door and calling the sitting a duty.

"Show me your hands," they said.

Iri held them out. Small hands, cracked with cold, still smudged with the ash of the hedge-witches' hearth where they'd hidden for three days. On the left palm, the soul-token sat like a coin of dark glass, Tam's name written around its rim in letters that moved when you weren't looking. It had hummed against the floorboards every night since she arrived. It was humming now.

"And yours?" Iri asked.

Ansel opened their own palms. The old scars were there, the ones from the binding-work—the fine white lines where Corvane's contract-charms had bitten into the skin of a younger, more obedient craftsperson. They had told themselves for a decade that those scars made them a prisoner too.

Tonight, holding them up to the lantern light beside a child's empty hands, Ansel finally saw what they really were. A signature. Proof of authorship.

"The magistrates will know these hands," Ansel said. "That's the whole plan. That's the only plan."

"You're scared," Iri said. Not cruelly. Children speaking the First Tongue said things plainly; the language had no word for *polite*.

"Yes."

"Good. The fire likes scared people. It can tell the difference between scared and lying."

Ansel looked at her. "Where did you learn that?"

"I didn't learn it." She closed her fist around the token. "The Tongue knows things. That's why they outlawed it."

Ansel pressed both palms flat to the floorboards, found the seam they had sworn never to find again, and spoke the word they had sworn never to speak.

The shop did not shake. That was the terrible part. Ten years of dread, and the gate opened the way a drawer opens—smoothly, quietly, without any sense that it had ever been shut at all. The boards parted along a spiral no carpenter could have cut, and below them there was not darkness but a stair, descending in long pale sweeps into a light like moonlight through milk.

Cold air rose up, smelling of paper and iron and something older underneath, something like the inside of a banked fire.

Iri went first. Of course she did.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain was not what the old stories promised, and it was exactly what Ansel's nightmares had always shown.

It was a cathedral built by accountants. Nave after nave of bone-white stone, pillars carved into the shapes of columns of figures—literally figures, small humanoid forms stacked ten thousand high, each one a soul entered into a ledger. The ceiling vanished upward into a glow with no source. Clerks moved through the aisles in grey robes, carrying ledgers instead of censers, and their footsteps made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than if the whole place had roared.

At the far end, past the pillars of souls, an amber light pulsed slow as a sleeping heart. The dragon-fire. Even guttering, even starved, Ansel felt it the way you feel a storm three valleys away—in the teeth, in the scar tissue of the palms.

"Don't look at it," Ansel murmured.

"I'm not looking at it," Iri whispered. "It's looking at me."

They walked. Ansel had dressed for this: the old contractor's sash, indigo and frayed, that Corvane himself had tied on them eleven years ago with the words *the court remembers its artisans*. They had kept it at the bottom of a chest like a snake in the rafters. Now it hung on them and the clerks' blank grey faces slid past, and one of them—one with spectacles and ink-stained fingers—actually inclined his head.

"Master Nakamura." His voice was dry as a turned page. "We had you listed as retired."

"The court's memory is long," Ansel said, "but its ledger has gaps. I've come to close mine."

Something flickered behind the spectacles—not suspicion. Relief. A clerk who did not have to open a file about a discrepancy was a clerk having a good day. "Gaps are frowned upon."

"Gaps are why I'm here."

He gestured them down a side aisle, toward a desk of white stone with an abacus whose beads were tiny vertebrae. "Renewals are processed in the western scriptorium. You'll want to declare your tools. Binding-grade work requires—" He stopped. His eyes had found Iri, standing half a step behind Ansel's shadow, barefoot on the bone-white floor.

Every sound in the cathedral seemed to lean in.

"My daughter," Ansel heard themselves say, smooth as poured wax. Ten years of playing harmless, of being nobody, of selling luck to people who wanted to believe in it—all of it funneled into that one lie. "Apprenticed young. She doesn't speak."

Iri, magnificently, said nothing, and stared at the clerk with the wide blank solemnity of a child who has been drilled within an inch of her life.

The clerk studied her a moment longer. Then he wrote something in his ledger—a line, a number—and Ansel's stomach dropped, because for one insane instant they imagined the man had recorded a soul.

"Age of majority?" he asked.

"Seven years yet."

"Then she isn't filed. Children aren't filed until they're useful." He said it the way another man might say *it looks like rain*, and waved them on toward the western doors. "Mind the archivists. They bite."

* * *

The western scriptorium was quieter, and quieter meant closer to the fire. Here the walls were shelved floor to unseen ceiling with ledgers, each spine stamped with a name and a date of purchase, and the amber pulse came through the high windows like light through honey.

Ansel counted doors. Six, along the far wall, each sealed with a lock of black iron shaped like a closed mouth. No keyholes. No seams. Doors that were not locked so much as *silenced*.

"That one," Iri breathed.

"How do you know?"

"It's humming my brother's name."

Ansel knelt, pretending to inspect the contractor's seal on a renewal slate, and watched out of the corner of their eye as Iri drifted toward the sixth door the way children drift toward water. When her fingers touched the iron, her lips moved—no sound, just the shape of First Tongue words, old words, words that predated locks because they predated the idea that things should be shut.

The black mouth of the lock *opened*.

Not unlocked. Opened, like a jaw, like a flower, like something that had been waiting ten years for someone to speak to it in its mother tongue. Beyond lay a narrow corridor lined with more shelves, and a cold draft that smelled of rain and green growing things, impossibly, down here under the mountain.

"Sixth shelf, third row," Iri whispered, pointing inside. "There's a book that's warm. Books shouldn't be warm, Ansel."

"No," Ansel agreed, throat tight. "They shouldn't."

They slipped inside together. And there it was: a ledger bound in grey leather, faintly warm as a living animal, its spine stamped not with a name but with a word in the First Tongue that Ansel did not know and understood anyway: *what was taken*.

Iri opened it with both hands, careful as handling a bird. Page after page of names, hundreds, each with a price and a date and a magistrate's seal. She turned them with increasing speed, breath going ragged, until—

She stopped. Her finger came down on a line and stayed there.

"Tam," she said. Just the name. Nothing else in her voice at all, which was how Ansel knew it had broken.

And beneath the name, in fresh ink, ink that gleamed wet even now: *balance remaining: sufficient*.

"Sufficient for what?" Iri asked.

Before Ansel could answer, the warm ledger's pages began turning on their own—backward, fast, toward the front, toward the first entry, toward whatever beginning this book was ashamed of. The amber light beyond the windows stuttered. Somewhere deep in the cathedral, a bell that had perhaps never rung before rang once.

Footsteps in the corridor. Unhurried. Familiar.

Ansel knew the tread before the figure rounded the shelf—knew it from eleven years of nightmares and one terrible afternoon of coercion. Magistrate Corvane stepped into the aisle, tall and grey-robed, his face exactly as Ansel remembered it except older in a way that had nothing to do with time. His eyes went to the open ledger. To Iri. To the indigo sash.

"Master Nakamura," he said softly. "The court remembers its artisans. It appears the artisans remember the court." His gaze lifted past them, through the high windows, toward the pulsing heart of the fire, and for one unguarded moment Ansel saw what lived behind the magistrate's face.

Fear.

"Come with me," Corvane said. "Both of you. Quickly. Before the others learn the fire has started *counting*."
