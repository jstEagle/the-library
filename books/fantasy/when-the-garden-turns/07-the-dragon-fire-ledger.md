## 7. The Dragon-Fire Ledger

The archive did not want to be found.

Iri felt it the way she felt a held breath—the whole corridor pressing inward, shelves of bone-pale vellum leaning close like witnesses told to keep quiet. Ansel had gone left at the last junction, toward the contractor's offices where forged credentials and a straight spine might buy them an hour. Iri had gone right, because the First Tongue had tugged her right, the way water tugs a leaf.

*Follow what remembers,* Tam had always said, back when he was only her brother and not a name on a coin-shaped thing that hummed against her ribs. *Old things remember better than new things know.*

So she followed. Past shelves of contracts bound in gray leather, past drawers of teeth—actual teeth, filed and labeled—and down a stair that narrowed until the walls touched her shoulders. The air grew warm. Not comfortable-warm. Hearth-warm, fever-warm, the warmth of something burning very far below and very tiredly.

And then the humming started.

It came up through the soles of her bare feet—Iri had refused shoes in the court beneath the mountain, some stubborn animal instinct insisting that skin should touch stone, that stone should know who walked it—and it was not the gate's hum, not exactly. It was the same song sung by a different throat. The gate sang of keeping-out. This sang of keeping-in.

She stopped before a door with no handle and no keyhole, only a circle of script so old the characters had worn to shallow scars. But Iri knew them. She had known them before she could read anything else, in the way you know your own heartbeat: the First Tongue, the tongue the fire spoke before anyone thought to make it obey.

*Ember-ledger,* said the door, or the thing behind the door. *Who asks?*

"Iri," she whispered, and then, because names had weight here, "Iri, sister of Tam."

A long silence. Then, softly, like a sigh through a keyhole:

*Tam is filed under F.*

"Filed under—" Her voice cracked. "Filed under what?"

*Fuel.*

The door opened without swinging, the way a mouth opens, and Iri stepped into the dark because the alternative was standing alone in a corridor forever.

* * *

Inside, the room breathed.

Not wind—breath. Slow, vast, labored, the respiration of something enormous asleep or pretending to be. The walls were shelved floor to unseen ceiling, and every shelf held ledgers, thousands of them, their spines glowing faintly as if each page remembered being wood, being tree, being alive. In the center of the floor, a brazier burned with a flame so small it would have been pitiful anywhere else. Here, in this deep place, it looked like a heart.

Iri approached it the way you approach a wounded animal.

"You're the dragon-fire," she said. "Or what's left of it."

The flame guttered. Leaned toward her like a flower toward sun.

*They call it that,* said a voice—not the fire's voice, but one from behind her, dry and precise as a closing ledger. *I have asked it, over the centuries, what it calls itself. It has never answered. Perhaps it no longer knows.*

Iri turned slowly.

Magistrate Corvane stood between her and the door. Up close, away from the ember throne and its assembled courts, he looked less like a judge and more like a man who had been standing too long in one position—stiff, gray at the temples, with the particular pallor of someone lit from below by a fire they cannot leave. His eyes went to her mouth first, then her hands, cataloguing threat.

"The child who speaks the dead language," he said. "You've cost me three inspectors and a night's sleep."

"You're supposed to be catching me," Iri said. Her heart hammered, but Tam's token pulsed against her ribs, steady as a second pulse, and she made herself stand in its rhythm. "Why aren't you?"

Corvane didn't answer immediately. He crossed to the central shelf instead—a gesture so practiced it was almost tender—and drew out a ledger thick as a paving stone. He set it on the reading table between them.

"Because I wanted to see which of you would find this room first," he said. "The keeper who buried themselves alive, or the child who still speaks to fire. Ansel would have taken a week. You took an hour." Something moved across his face that was not quite admiration. "That tells me things about the fire that I have been afraid to learn for two hundred years."

He opened the ledger.

Iri had imagined many shapes for her brother's captivity. A cage. A jar. A dark room with his name scratched into the wall. She had not imagined a column of figures.

Page after page, in ink that shimmered faintly gold: names, dates, prices paid, and beside each, a small notation in the First Tongue that translated itself in her mind unbidden—*drawn down, drawn down, drawn down.* Hundreds of entries. Then thousands. The earliest pages were written in a confident hand; the later ones deteriorated, letters slanting, margins crowded, as though the writer were running out of room or time or both.

She found him on the third-to-last page.

**TAM OF VELL — age 11 — acquired via soul-token (maker: NAKAMURA) — price: 40 marks — status: DRAWN DOWN 60%**

Sixty percent. The number sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. Sixty percent of her brother was in that brazier. Forty percent was wherever forty percent goes when the rest of you is fuel.

"He's still alive," she said. It wasn't a question. The token's hum told her it wasn't.

"For now." Corvane turned pages with a clerk's efficiency, but Iri saw his fingers slow near the recent entries. "The fire does not kill what it burns, child. That is its cruelty and its mercy. It keeps. Everything it takes, it keeps, the way amber keeps an insect. Your brother is in there. So are four hundred others. So am I, in a sense—we all are, we magistrates, we poured ourselves in first, long ago, when the fire was young and generous and seemed inexhaustible."

"Inexhaustible," Iri repeated. She looked at the brazier. The flame had shrunk since she entered. She was almost sure of it. "It doesn't look inexhaustible."

"No." Corvane closed the ledger. For a moment his hand rested on its cover as if it were the shoulder of an old horse he'd ridden into the ground. "No, it does not."

* * *

They stood together in the breathing dark, magistrate and child, and Iri understood she was seeing something no one in the court above was permitted to see: Corvane without his office, without his judges' gravity, just a man doing arithmetic he hated.

"When I was young," he said, "the fire filled this room. It climbed the walls. We drew from it sparingly—one soul a decade, perhaps, and only the willing, and even they were mourned. Immortality seemed a small price to ask of eternity." He laughed, and there was nothing humorous in it. "But eternity is longer than it looks from the front. The fire wanes. It has been waning for three hundred years. And the court—my colleagues—they responded the way frightened people always respond. They spent faster. If ten souls bought a century, surely a hundred would buy more than ten centuries. Surely more fuel means more fire."

"But it doesn't."

"It doesn't." He looked at her, and Iri saw that his eyes were wet, and that he was ashamed of it. "Fire is not a granary. You cannot stockpile it. Every soul we feed it buys us less than the last. The ledgers show it plainly—look at the yields, entry by entry, diminishing, diminishing. We have overdrawn the account. And now the fire is guttering, and the magistrates who fed on it are beginning to feel the draft." His voice dropped. "Two of my colleagues have aged eleven years this winter. Eleven years, child. In one winter. Do you understand what that does to men who arranged their souls around never ending?"

Iri looked at the little flame. It had leaned toward her again, straining, and now she saw what she hadn't let herself see before: it wasn't reaching for her warmth. It was reaching for the token. For Tam. For all of them, the way a starving thing reaches, without malice, without choice.

"You're afraid," she said.

"I am the most afraid person in this mountain," Corvane said quietly. "Because I read the ledgers. Because I know what happens when the fire dies. My colleagues think they will simply become mortal—grow old, die, suffer the ordinary indignity. They are wrong. We did not borrow our lives from the fire. We *gave* them to it, all those centuries ago, and it kept them, the way it keeps everything. When the flame goes out—" his hand opened over the brazier, helplessly, "—everything it holds goes out with it. Every soul. Every year we ever lived. There will be no aging gracefully. There will be nothing. We will not fall. We will be *unwritten.*"

The word fell into the dark and lay there.

"And Tam?" Iri's voice was small. "If the fire dies—"

"Is unwritten too." Corvane met her eyes. "Yes. Which is why you must understand, child, why I cannot simply let you open that brazier and pour everyone out, however much some traitorous part of me might want to. If the fire dies tonight, your brother dies tonight. All of them do. The court's cruelty has built a machine where mercy and murder are the same lever." He stepped closer, and up close she could see the fine cracks in him, hairline fractures running through his skin like glaze on old porcelain, as if he were already a fired thing. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless the fire can be taught to live without eating." He said it fast, as if saying it slowly would break it. "There are stories. Older than the ledgers. Older than the court. Stories of a fire that was tended, not fed—that gave warmth freely because it was loved freely, before anyone thought to own it. Before the First Tongue was outlawed and the speaking of it became treason." His gaze dropped to her mouth again, and this time Iri understood it wasn't suspicion. It was hunger. "You speak it. I heard you at the door. The fire answered you."

Behind him, the flame had risen. Barely. A finger's width taller, trembling, leaning toward Iri with everything it had left, and in its flicker she caught—she would swear ever after she caught—something like a face. Not Tam's. Older. Vaster. A face like weather.

*Who asks?* the fire had said at the door. Not *what.* Who.

"They'll kill you for this," Iri said. "Telling me. Helping me. Won't they?"

"Oh, certainly." Corvane straightened, and the magistrate reassembled himself around the cracks like armor around a wound. "Which is why you have perhaps a quarter hour before I am forced to report finding you here, weeping over the ledgers, and escort you—under guard, naturally—to await judgment at the ember throne." He pressed the great ledger into her arms, heavy as a sleeping child. "Carry that. Every soul's name is in it, and names spoken in the First Tongue have power even I don't fully believe in anymore. And child—" he paused at the door, and for one moment the gray clerk fell away entirely, and what remained was two hundred years of fear looking out through a man's face—

"—when they bring you before the throne, and they will, look closely at my colleagues. Look at how they lean toward the fire. And then decide for yourself whether we are jailers, or merely the longest-serving prisoners, guarding the thing that eats us because we cannot imagine any other way to be kept alive."

The door opened. Beyond it, footsteps—many, booted, converging. Ansel's contingency charms, somewhere far above, going off like small thunder.

Corvane's eyes flicked to the sound, then back to her, and he did the last thing she expected: he smiled, thin and terrible and hopeful.

"Quarter hour," he said. "Make them count."

Then he raised his voice into a magistrate's bellow—*"Intruder! The archive! Seal the stair!"*—and the corridor filled with light and shouting, and Iri ran, the ledger clutched to her chest, toward the throne she was being dragged to and the fire that had called her *who*.
