## 8. Bargain at the Ember Throne

They did not drag Ansel to the throne. That was the first cruelty of it—the courtesy. Two magistrates in bone-white robes walked ahead, their footsteps making no sound on the stair of black glass, and Ansel followed because Iri's small hand was in theirs and there was nowhere else to be.

The hall opened around them like a throat.

Ansel had imagined it a thousand times in ten years of not imagining it: the court beneath the mountain, the place they had sworn never to see again. The reality was worse in its tidiness. No gore, no horror—only order. Columns of pale stone rose into darkness that swallowed their tops. Clerks moved along the walls with ledgers chained to their belts, scratching entries in ink that glowed faintly orange, as if each line were written in ember-light and would burn when read aloud. The air smelled of hot metal and old paper. Somewhere far above, muffled by miles of stone, rain was falling on Vell, and no one down here would ever feel it.

And at the end of the hall, the throne.

It was not built. It had *cooled*—that was the sense Ansel got, walking toward it—a river of fire frozen mid-pour, black glass folded over itself in ropes and whorls, and seated in its heart a single coal the size of a heart, pulsing slow and dim. The last dragon-fire. Ansel had heard it described as a blaze. No one had said it would look so *tired*.

Corvane stood beside it.

He had changed since the archive—Iri's account of that encounter had been fragmentary, breathless—but he had not changed enough. The same long face, the same ink-stained fingers, the same way of holding his ledger like a shield held backwards. His eyes found Ansel's and something flickered in them: recognition, yes, but underneath it, shame worn smooth by years of handling.

"Keeper Nakamura." He said it formally, for the benefit of the others. "You return to the court after ten years of absence."

"I didn't return," Ansel said. "I was delivered."

The magistrates flanking the throne did not move. There were seven of them; Ansel counted without meaning to, an old keeper's habit, counting exits and threats alike. Their faces were wrong in a way Ansel had forgotten—too smooth, too still, like portraits that had learned to breathe. Immortality, Ansel thought. This is what it looks like from the outside. Wax pretending to be skin.

"You were brought here," Corvane said, "because you are the last living keeper of the gate, and the court has need of your craft. You are accused of nothing. You are offered everything."

A clerk stepped forward bearing a tray, and on the tray lay a contract so clean it seemed to glow against the tarnished silver. Ansel knew that handwriting. Had *written* that handwriting, once, under lamplight, with a magistrate's hand heavy on their shoulder and the words *you will do this or the child across the street will be filed* ringing in their ears.

"Renew the bindings," Corvane said. "Your charms have kept the souls contained for a decade. Craft them anew, freely given this time, and the court releases you. Full absolution. Your name struck from every ledger. You walk out of this mountain a free citizen of Vell, and no Ministry inspector will ever darken your door again."

*Full absolution.* The words landed in Ansel's chest like coins dropped down a well.

That was the trap of it—not the threat, which Ansel had expected, but the kindness. Ten years of flinching at boots on stairs. Ten years of selling luck-charms to people who needed miracles and calling it a life. And here it was, all of it refundable, all of it forgivable, for the price of a few weeks' work. Ink and willow-ash and the binding script Ansel could write in their sleep, had written in their sleep, dreaming of it some nights like a debt collector's knock.

Beside them, Iri shifted. The child said nothing—had barely spoken since the archive—but their grip tightened on Ansel's hand, small fingers pressing hard enough to hurt. A reminder. *I felt my brother's name in that book. Don't you dare sign anything.*

Ansel looked at the coal in the throne. Dim. Guttering. Fed on hundreds of souls and still starving, because theft is a poor diet for anything alive.

"No," Ansel said.

The word fell flat in the vast hall, smaller than it should have been. One of the magistrates tilted her head, birdlike, curious.

"No?" Corvane repeated.

"No." Ansel stepped forward, out of Iri's grip, until they stood alone on the black glass before the throne. Their voice came steadier than they felt, which was either courage or exhaustion wearing its costume. "I'll make you a different bargain. The oldest one. Older than your contracts, older than your court."

"Speak it, then."

"The keeper's oath." Ansel raised their hands, palms out, the way they'd been taught at nineteen by a woman named Odile who had tended the gate before them and whose name appeared, they now knew, in a ledger somewhere in this building. "I bind myself to the fire. Not the souls to the fire—*me*. My years, my strength, my life, poured into keeping it lit. A keeper holds the flame with their own warmth. That was the covenant before your kind turned it into accounting. It can hold. It *will* hold—for as long as I last, and I last a long time, ask anyone who's tried to kill me. In exchange, you release every soul in those ledgers. All of them. Tam. Odile. Every name your clerks have priced and shelved. You let them go home."

Silence. Even the clerks along the walls had stopped writing.

One of the magistrates laughed. It was a horrible sound, not because it was cruel but because it was rusty—the laugh of someone who hadn't needed to laugh in a century.

"With your life?" she said. "One mortal life? Against three hundred souls? The arithmetic is insulting."

"It isn't arithmetic," Ansel said. "That's the whole point. That's why your fire is dying."

The laughter stopped.

Ansel took another step, close enough now to feel the heat—or the memory of heat—rising off the coal. Up close it looked less like a flame than a wound. Something that had once been enormous, winged, singing, reduced to an ember in a chair, kept alive the way you keep a cut flower alive: in water, in the dark, cut off from everything that made it what it was.

"I've seen what happens to things fed on stolen life," Ansel said quietly. "I've sold charms to widows who kept their husbands' ghosts bottled on the mantel. They dim, magistrate. Every year they dim. You cannot pour a soul into a fire and get light. You only get hunger. Your fire isn't guttering because you've run short of souls. It's guttering because it remembers what it was, and it's grieving."

"Do not—" Corvane began.

"The oath stands," Ansel said. "My life for all of them. Take it or refuse it. But if you refuse it, know that the offer dies with me, because I will not renew your bindings, and when the fire goes out—and it *will*, soon, I've seen guttering flames my whole career—you will have traded three hundred souls and a living fire for nothing but a little more time to count your ledgers."

The magistrates conferred without speaking. That was the worst part, watching them tilt toward one another like reeds, faces blank, deciding the fate of everyone Ansel had ever loved in total silence. Ansel thought of Iri standing behind them, barefoot still, rain-damp, waiting. Thought of Tam's name etched in a token that hummed like a plucked string. Thought of the hedge-witches in the hidden ways below the city, keeping small flames of their own.

*I am done being invisible,* Ansel thought. *Even here. Especially here.*

"We refuse," said the laughing magistrate.

Of course they did. Ansel had known they would. Redemption that costs the redeemers nothing is the only kind institutions ever offer, and institutions never pay.

"Then we're finished here," Ansel said, and turned to go—to take Iri's hand and run, though run where, Ansel didn't know—and that was when the fire flared.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next. The coal leapt from its bed of glass like a startled bird, and it was *huge*, suddenly, filling the throne, the hall, the inside of Ansel's skull, and Ansel understood in a white-hot instant that this was not the magistrates' doing. The magistrates recoiled. Corvane shouted something. The fire had heard.

It had heard *keeper*.

Ten years of buried magic woke in Ansel's blood all at once—the gate beneath the floorboards, the seal they'd maintained with whispered maintenance spells disguised as sweeping, the oath Odile had passed to them in a cellar that smelled of rain—and the fire reached for it like a drowning thing reaching for a rope. It poured into Ansel through the open hands they'd raised in offering, and it was agony, it was *light*, it was a voice older than the mountain saying a word in the First Tongue that Ansel did not know and understood completely—

*Hold.*

Ansel held. Feet planted on black glass, hands burning, teeth locked against a scream. The magistrates were shouting orders now, clerks scattering, ledgers tumbling open, names spilling loose across the floor in glowing ink. Through the roar Ansel heard Iri screaming their name, heard running footsteps, felt small hands seize their burning wrists—

And the fire pulled harder. Greedy. Starving. It did not care about bargains. It had been fed on theft for a hundred years and it had forgotten any other way to eat, and it meant to eat Ansel entire, oath or no oath.

*Let go,* Ansel told themselves, and could not. *Drop your hands.* They wouldn't drop. The oldest magic had one rule Odile had never mentioned, or maybe had mentioned and Ansel had forgotten, or maybe had lied about to spare them:

*A keeper cannot release the fire. Only the fire can release the keeper.*

Through the blaze, through the pain, Ansel saw Iri's face—furious, terrified, lit orange—and saw the child draw a breath, square their shoulders, and turn to face the burning throne.

Iri opened their mouth and spoke.
