## 9. When the Garden Turns

*Iri*

The fire was not what Iri had imagined.

She had thought it would be vast, a dragon coiled in the dark, all teeth and hunger. Instead it hung above the ember throne like a lamp about to go out — a tongue of gold no bigger than her two hands cupped together, trembling, thin at the edges where it frayed into smoke. Around it, the magistrates stood in their bone-white robes, and their faces were lit from below with a light that made them look like things drowned and dredged up.

They had refused Ansel's bargain. She had watched them refuse it, watched the oldest of them laugh — a dry sound, like paper tearing — and then the fire had flared, reaching for Ansel with fingers of light, and Iri had done the only thing left.

She stepped between them.

The heat touched her face and did not burn. It felt, instead, like standing too close to someone crying — that awful warmth of grief given off by a living body. And she understood, suddenly, in the wordless way she understood rain coming before it fell, that this was exactly what it was. Grief. The fire was grieving.

She opened her mouth, and the First Tongue came out of her the way water comes out of a spring: not chosen, just true.

*"Little one,"* she said. *"Ember-of-the-long-night. Mother-of-warm-hearths. I see you."*

The fire stopped reaching for Ansel.

Every magistrate in the chamber went still, the way a room goes still when a child says something no child should know. The fire bent toward Iri, and its light changed — the gold deepening, warming, taking on colors it had not shown in centuries: the green-gold of new leaves, the red-gold of fox fur, the blue at the heart of a candle's first breath.

*"Who taught you those words?"* The eldest magistrate's voice cracked on the last syllable.

"No one," Iri said, in the common tongue, because she wanted them to understand. Then, in the First Tongue again, to the fire alone: *"They told you that you were fuel. They wrote you down in ledgers. But ledgers are only paper, and paper was alive once too."*

The fire shivered.

And Iri felt it remember.

She couldn't have said how she knew — only that knowing poured into her like warmth into cold hands. The fire remembered being a dragon. Not the last dragon, not even the greatest — a young one, a daughter, who had flown so high the stars scorched her wings, who had loved the sun so fiercely that when the sun began to die she had offered herself to keep it burning. That was the old bargain, the true one, made freely: *take my fire, warm the world.* And the world had taken, and taken, and somewhere along ten thousand years the taking had become theft, and the theft had become law, and the law had become this room full of pale men filing souls like debts.

*"You are not fuel,"* Iri told her. *"You never were. You are the one who chose to burn. And choosing is still yours."*

The fire rose.

Not the guttering tongue — something else, something unfolding, wings of light spreading through the cathedral of bone-white stone, and every soul-token in every drawer of every archive began to sing. Iri heard Tam's name in the chorus, clear as a bell over water, and she ran for the archive doors as the drawers burst open around her, hundreds of small brass tokens lifting into the air like a flock of returning birds.

* * *

*Ansel*

Ansel had spent ten years learning how to be small, and in one breath the fire taught them how large a small thing could be.

They stood at the edge of the ember dais, hair streaming in the updraft, watching the light pour upward through cracks in the vaulted ceiling — and beyond those cracks, impossibly, they saw green. The garden of ash above the court, the dead gray slope that crowned the mountain, was blooming. Ansel could feel it happening through the floor, through the soles of their boots: roots unclenching after a decade of drought, seeds that had waited under ash splitting open like fists deciding, at last, to open.

The old magic turning against its jailers.

Because that was the truth of it, the truth the magistrates had built their immortality on and never understood: the fire had never been theirs. It was borrowed. It was *given*, once, freely — and a gift revoked is older law than any writ or seal. The First Tongue was not a language of command. It was a language of recognition, and the fire had just been recognized.

The magistrates were aging.

It happened slowly enough to watch — the eldest first, his paper-dry dignity collapsing inward as decades arrived all at once, silver flooding his hair, his hands spotting and shaking, his knees folding until he knelt on the white stone among his own fallen seals of office. Around the chamber, the others followed, one by one, centuries settling onto shoulders that had refused them for lifetimes. Some wept. Some raged, shrieking about order, about debt, about the sanctity of contract, their voices going thin and human as they thinned into ordinary mortal men.

Ansel felt nothing like triumph. They felt the ache of a splinter coming out — relief and pain in the same instant, indistinguishable.

"Ansel." Corvane's voice, behind them. He had not aged as fast as the rest; whatever portion of the fire he'd drawn, he'd drawn sparingly, and now he stood in the ruin of his robes looking less like a magistrate than like a tired clerk at the end of a very long shift. "The gate. If the fire leaves fully, the gate seals itself — forever, this time, with everything inside it. You know the rite. You've always known it."

"I know it," Ansel said.

"You were appointed keeper."

"I was," Ansel said. "I'm not anymore."

They walked to the gate.

It stood at the far end of the court, the door beneath the mountain, the threshold Ansel had sealed with their own hands ten years ago and tended ever since like a wound they were afraid to look at. The binding charms they had crafted under coercion still held it — their own work, their own guilt, etched into the frame in lines of silver. Ansel pressed both palms flat against the cold iron and spoke the unsealing, but did not stop there. Unsealing was not enough. Unsealing could be resealed, by them or by whoever came after, and the court would simply grow back like rot in a damp wall.

So they broke it.

They reached into the charm-work with the part of themselves they had buried when magic was outlawed — the part that had once been proud, that had woven wards like songs, that had believed craft was a form of love — and they tore their own spells out by the root. The silver lines flared and screamed and went dark. The iron groaned. Somewhere in the frame, something fundamental gave way with a sound like a glacier calving, and the gate split down its center, and then split again crosswise, and then was not a gate at all but four pieces of expensive metal on a floor that was already growing moss.

From inside. From below. Keeper no longer — breaker.

The wind that came through carried the smell of rain and turned earth and, faintly, absurdly, roses.

* * *

*Iri*

Tam found her in the falling light.

She knew him before she saw him — the tokens were rising past her in their hundreds, each one a small brass coin holding a name, and the names were becoming people as the fire released them, souls stepping out of metal and memory into forms made of golden air. She was searching the bright crowd, calling his name in both tongues, when a hand closed around hers — a boy's hand, chapped at the knuckles, missing the same finger she was missing, the one they'd lost together to a trap in the woods two winters ago.

He was half-light and half-boy, flickering between, but his grip was solid and his voice, when he said *"Iri,"* cracked exactly the way it always had.

"You're late," she told him, and ruined it completely by crying.

Around them the court was emptying — not fleeing, *leaving*, the way guests leave a house whose owner has finally come home. Souls drifted up through the broken ceiling toward the blooming garden, and where they passed, the bone-white stone softened to limestone, to sandstone, to plain brown earth. The archive shelves flowered. Iri watched a filing cabinet put out a branch.

The fire herself came last. She had gathered herself small again — hearth-sized, the size she might have been if anyone had ever asked what she wanted — and she hovered beside Iri and looked at the child with eyes like banked coals.

*"You will be cold sometimes,"* the fire said in the First Tongue. *"That is the price of freedom. I paid it long ago and I would pay it again. Remember me kindly, little spring. Speak to your own fires now and then. They miss being spoken to."*

Then she rose, trailing warmth, and was gone into the green.

* * *

*Ansel*

Corvane opened the final door.

It was an ordinary door, oak and iron, set into the wall of the emptied court — the way out, the way up, the way back to Vell and the shop and the life Ansel had dismantled brick by brick tonight. Corvane stood beside it, mortal now, gray at the temples, holding it open with the simple courtesy of a man seeing off a guest.

"They'll try you," he said. "What's left of the Ministry. Breaking the gate, releasing the souls — there are laws."

"There are," Ansel agreed.

"You could run. With the hedge-witches' network, you'd vanish. You're good at vanishing."

Ansel looked at him for a long moment. Ten years ago this man had coerced them, had stood in this very shop with a writ and a smile and made their hands build cages. Tonight he had hesitated at the ledger, had feared the fire's dying more than the fire's freedom, and in the end had stood aside while a child unmade his whole world. Ansel found they could not hate him. That was its own kind of loss.

"No," Ansel said. "I'm done disappearing. Exile suits me fine — I've been exiled from myself for a decade. At least this way it's honest."

Iri was waiting at the threshold, Tam's hand in hers, his light already fading to the ordinary glow of a living boy. Above them, through the broken ceiling, the night sky showed green-tinged at the edges where the garden bloomed, and the first soul-lights were settling into it like fireflies finding their summer.

"Come on, then," Iri said, impatient, tugging. "Tam wants to see the sky. And you promised—" she paused, frowning at some memory that wasn't hers, pulled from the floorboards of a shop she'd slept in for three nights, "—you promised someone you'd stop hiding."

Ansel took one last look back at the quiet court — the moss creeping over the throne, the four pieces of a gate that would never seal anything again — and stepped through the door Corvane held open, into the dark stair, into the morning waiting above.

Behind them, soft as a banked coal, something in the earth went on glowing.
