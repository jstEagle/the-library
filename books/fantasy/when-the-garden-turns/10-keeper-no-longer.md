## 10. Keeper No Longer

The court beneath the mountain had gone quiet the way a house goes quiet after a funeral—full of echoes that no longer had owners. Ansel walked its bone-white corridors one last time, not because they needed to, but because a keeper's habit dies harder than a keeper's oath.

The filing halls stood open now, their drawers pulled out like tongues. Ledgers lay where they had fallen, their ink still wet with names that no longer belonged to anyone. Ansel stepped over a scattered page—*Vessa of the Tanneries, purchased 14th of Frostmoon*—and felt nothing but a tired ache where guilt used to live. The souls were gone. All of them. Risen through the broken gate like smoke through a lifted lid, and Vell above had woken to a dawn that smelled, for the first time in ten years, of green things instead of ash.

The ember throne room was last. Ansel had expected it to be dark, dead, a cold hearth in a cold cathedral. Instead, when they pushed open the doors, a small light waited.

The dragon-fire sat on the throne itself, no larger than a candle flame, burning a color Ansel had no name for—somewhere between gold and the green of new leaves. It did not flicker. It did not demand. It simply was, the way a hearth is when someone has chosen to sit beside it.

"You're smaller," Ansel said, and their voice sounded strange in the empty hall, unafraid for the first time in a decade.

The flame leaned toward them, the way a cat leans toward a warm hand. Ansel felt it then—not the old hunger, not the pull of souls being fed into it, but something gentler. Curiosity. Gratitude, maybe, though a fire had no reason to be grateful to the person who had built its cage.

"I'm not your keeper anymore," Ansel told it. "I don't know what I am."

The flame pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat deciding to continue. And Ansel understood—or almost understood—that this was answer enough. It would burn or it wouldn't. It would be tended or it wouldn't. But it would never again be fed.

Ansel knelt, stiff knees cracking, and cupped their hands around the little light the way one cups a sparrow. It was warm. Only warm. All that terror, all those centuries of purchased souls, and the thing itself had only ever wanted to be warm.

"I'll come back," they said. "Not to keep you. Just to sit. If that's allowed."

The fire did not answer, because fire does not speak. But when Ansel rose to leave, it bent toward the door after them, like a face turning to watch someone walk away down a long road.

* * *

Iri was waiting at the top of the stair that wasn't there, sitting cross-legged on the floorboards of the shop with Tam's head in her lap. The boy—restored, whole, his soul returned to a body that had been waiting empty in some Ministry orphanage until the magic found him again—was asleep, his breathing slow and even, his fingers curled around a soul-token that was now just a token, just etched metal with a name on it and nothing living inside.

"He wouldn't stop talking," Iri said, looking up. Her bare feet were still bare; she had refused shoes for three days, as if she didn't trust anything between her and the ground. "He asked if the fire hurt. He asked if I was really his sister. He asked if you were a witch."

"What did you tell him?"

"That you were worse." She almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile Ansel had seen from her, and it went through them like a splinter going in—painful, and proof of something alive.

Ansel crouched beside the children. Up close, Tam looked like Iri—the same dark eyes, the same stubborn set to the mouth. But there was a hollowness behind his eyes that would take years to fill, if it ever filled. Ansel knew that hollowness. They had been wearing their own version of it for a decade.

"Tam," they said gently. "When you're older, if you ever want to know what was done to you—what was done *for* you—you can come find me. I'll tell you all of it. The parts that shame me too."

Tam opened his eyes. He looked at Ansel with the flat, ancient gaze of a child who had been somewhere old souls go, and he said, in the First Tongue, a word Ansel hadn't heard since before the outlawing.

*Thank you.*

Then he closed his eyes and went back to sleep in his sister's lap, and Ansel had to stand and walk to the window and look at the rain for a while, because their throat had closed entirely.

* * *

The Ministry of Order came for Ansel on the fourth day.

Ansel heard them on the stairs—boots, too many boots, the particular rhythm of officials who believe they are in the right—and had time, this time, to choose. They could trigger the contingency charms one more time. The hidden ways, the hedge-witches' network, the long slide back into invisibility. The shop had a back door that wasn't a door; Ansel's whole life had been a back door that wasn't a door.

Instead, they put the kettle on.

When the inspectors broke in—Inspector Halloway at the front, the same gray-faced man from the routine inspection a lifetime ago, it seemed—they found Ansel sitting at their own counter with two cups of tea poured and a third saucer set out for the child, because Ansel had manners even at the end of the world.

"Ansel Nakamura," Halloway read from a scroll, his voice shaking only a little. "By order of the surviving magistracy of Vell, you are charged with the unlawful opening of a sealed gate, the destruction of court property, the emancipation of—of—"

"Of souls," Ansel supplied. "You can say it. It's a legal term now, whether you like it or not."

Halloway's jaw worked. Behind him, the younger inspectors shifted, and Ansel saw it in their faces—the thing that had been moving through Vell like weather since the gate broke. Not fear. Not quite. *Wonder*, worn like an illness. These were young people who had grown up on ash and law, and they had seen a garden bloom overnight in the ruins above the mountain, and no amount of scroll-reading could un-see it.

"The sentence is exile," Halloway said. "Banishment from the city of Vell and all territories under its law. Effective immediately." He paused, and his gray face did something complicated. "The magistracy wishes it noted that they are being merciful."

"The magistracy wishes a great many things," Ansel said. They stood, drained their tea, and looked around the shop one last time. The shelves of harmless charms. The counter worn smooth by ten years of their own elbows. The floorboards, one of which was splintered now, the stair that wasn't there showing through like a wound that had finally been allowed to breathe. "I'd ask you to look after the children, but I don't need to. Iri has a way of looking after herself. And everyone else."

"Where will you go?" It was one of the young inspectors who asked—young woman, ash still under her fingernails from the blooming garden, eyes too wide for her uniform.

"Anywhere," Ansel said, and found that the word tasted like bread after a long fast. "Anywhere at all. That's the whole point."

* * *

They left through the front door.

It was such a small thing, and it undid Ansel more than the dragon-fire, more than the broken gate, more than Corvane's face in the last moments—that hollow, astonished look of a man watching his own immortality drain out of him and discovering, underneath, that he was still *there*. Ansel had spent ten years leaving through the back of everything. To walk out the front of their own shop, in daylight, past the inspectors, into a street where people stopped and stared and some of them—some of them *bowed*, or pressed a hand to their heart, or simply said, quietly, "thank you"—was a kind of magic no court had ever catalogued.

The hedge-witches were waiting at the city gate. All of them. Old Maren with her herb-basket, the twins with their matching gray braids, a dozen faces Ansel had known only as names whispered through hidden doors, and now here they stood in the open, unlicensed and unhidden and unafraid, because the law that had outlawed them was busy learning how to be a law without immortality propping it up.

"You could stay," Maren said. "The city's changing. Someone has to teach the young ones the old words, and Iri can't do it alone, and—"

"I know." Ansel touched the old woman's shoulder. "But I've been keeping things my whole life. A gate, a secret, a low profile, my own name out of my own mouth. I'd like, just once, to go somewhere and not keep anything. Not even myself."

Maren studied them for a long moment. Then she pressed something into Ansel's hand: a small clay pot, unglazed, holding dark soil and a single green shoot.

"From the garden above the court," she said. "It came back wrong, you know. All those years of ash, and when the magic turned, everything grew back *eager*. This one's a plum tree. Plant it somewhere it can be greedy."

* * *

Ansel walked for a season.

East, mostly, following roads that grew narrower and older the farther they went from Vell, through villages where the news of the blooming had arrived distorted and wonderful—some said a saint had broken the mountain open, some said a child had spoken the world back awake, and in one village they insisted it had been a dragon, which Ansel supposed was true in a way that would take scholars a hundred years to untangle.

They carried the plum shoot in its clay pot. They planted nothing yet. It wasn't time.

What they found, on those roads, was that the world was fuller than they had known. Hedge-magic everywhere, half-buried, half-remembered—old women who still crossed their thresholds with salt, shepherds who sang to their flocks in fragments of the First Tongue without knowing what they sang, a boy in a river town who could find lost things and had been beaten for it until the news from Vell reached his village and the beatings stopped, and then the apologies started, which were harder. Ansel taught where they could. They unlearned where they had to. They slept under stars that felt, for the first time in their life, like a roof rather than a witness.

And always, in the evenings, they took out the little pot and held it to the last light, and the shoot grew, and Ansel let it.

* * *

The valley was not on any map Ansel had ever seen, which was how they knew it was right.

A fold of hills, a river that ran clear over white stones, a ruin—no, not a ruin, just an old house with a good roof and a garden that had gone wild a hundred years ago and never been scolded for it. The land was cheap because it was far from everything. The land was far from everything because it was *enough*.

Ansel spent the first month repairing the roof and the second month doing nothing at all, which was harder, and the third month turning the wild garden's soil, and it was in the turning that they found it: beneath the apple trees, beneath a century of leaf-rot, a flat stone with a seam in it, and under the seam, when they knelt and pressed their palm flat, a hum.

Faint. Old. A gate—not the gate, not the great stair beneath the mountain, but one of the small doors, the minor crossings, the capillaries of the old magic's body. Sealed, probably, since before Ansel's grandmother. Sleeping.

Ansel knelt there a long time, hand on the stone, feeling it hum against their palm like a bird's heartbeat.

Every instinct they had left said *seal it. Bury it deeper. Walk away, find another valley, be no one again.* Ten years of vigilance does not unlearn itself in a season.

But ten years of vigilance had also been ten years of grief, and Ansel was tired in a way that sleep could not reach.

They planted the plum tree over the stone.

Not to hide it—to *mark* it. To say to whatever came, in whatever tongue: here is a living thing, growing by choice, over an old door. Here is a keeper's answer to a keeper's burden. The roots would find the seams in time; roots always do. And when the tree was grown, and the gate beneath it woke or didn't, whoever came to that door would find shade first, and fruit, and only then the stone.

Ansel sat back on their heels in the evening light, dirt to the elbows, fifty years old and exiled and freer than they had ever been, and watched the plum shoot stand in its turned earth like a small green flag planted in the territory of a life not yet lived.

The gate hummed beneath the roots. Ansel did not seal it, and did not open it, and did not keep it.

They simply stayed.

And far to the east, on a road Ansel would never see, a barefoot girl was teaching a classroom of children the First Tongue, and when she reached the old word for *gate*, she taught them the newer one too—the one that was already spreading through Vell like the green through the ash:

*The word that means both door and beginning.*

The plum tree would bloom in three years. Ansel intended to be there, with tea, in the front of the house, door open, waiting to see who came.
