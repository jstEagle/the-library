## 1. The Child at the Gate

The frost came early that year, silvering the bean rows before the beans had finished, and Edmund Galloway stood in his garden with dirt to his wrists and thought: *there it is again. Another winter I did not ask for.*

He had learned not to mind winters. Thirty of them had passed through the valley since he'd come down from the pass with nothing but a sword he no longer touched and a name he was trying to wear smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket. The valley took him in the way valleys take in everything — without comment, without memory. Here magic could not follow. That was the whole point. He had chosen this place the way a man chooses a room with no windows: deliberately, and because he could not bear what he would see otherwise.

So when the frost came, he only pulled the last of the tomatoes and covered the basil with sacking, and told himself the ache in his hands was the cold.

It was the cold. It was always the cold.

He heard the gate before he saw anyone — the small complaint of hinges he had oiled every spring for three decades, hinges that knew his hand better than most people ever had. He turned, expecting the miller's boy or old Marta come to trade eggs for late squash.

A child stood at the gate.

She was small, seven years old perhaps, maybe eight, though hunger has a way of making children hard to measure. Mud streaked her face in long drying lines, as if she had wept clay. Her hair was dark and full of burrs, her boots were wrong for walking — fine boots once, cracked now, the kind made in cities Edmund refused to think about — and she stood the way no child stands, which is to say perfectly still, watching him with eyes that were too old by several centuries.

"Go home," Edmund said. His voice came out rusty. "Whatever village you've walked from, it's behind you. Follow the stream down."

The child tilted her head. And then she spoke.

"*Serrath vael, keth Aldermere.*"

The words went through him like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

Thirty years. Thirty years of planting and pruning and forgetting, of teaching his tongue to move only in the valley's flat market-speech, of letting the beautiful, terrible language of his homeland rot in him like fruit left on the branch — and here it was, standing at his gate in a mud-streaked child, saying *I am lost, of Aldermere.*

There was no Aldermere. There had been no Aldermere for thirty years. Every map redrawn, every record burned or rewritten, every survivor scattered so thin that even they had begun to doubt their own memories. He knew, because he had checked. In the early years he had checked obsessively, walking to the valley's edge and asking travelers about a country whose name made them frown like he'd asked after a dream. Eventually he stopped asking. Eventually he stopped saying the name at all, even alone, even in dreams — though the dreams never obeyed.

"You can't be," he said, in that same dead tongue, and hated himself for how easily it rose in him, whole and warm, like a fire banked rather than extinguished.

"*Serrath vael,*" the child said again, patient, as if repetition were all the argument she needed. Then something else — a phrase that dropped through him like a key into a lock he'd welded shut:

"*Kethan vor, sel ammarith.*"

*Sworn and bound, until the waking.*

The oath-marks. Not just the language — the *formulation*, the precise cadenced grammar of the Swordspeakers' binding, taught to children too young to understand what they were promising. Edmund had spoken those words at nine years old with his hand on a sword hilt taller than he was, in a courtyard full of white blossoms, while his mentor watched with pride and something else underneath the pride that Edmund had been too young to name and would spend thirty years naming.

No one alive spoke that formulation. No one should have been able to speak it. It wasn't a language you learned; it was a language you were *bound into*, and the binding died with the kingdom.

The child swayed. He caught the gate before she caught herself.

Against every instinct — against thirty years of cultivated stillness, of the careful small life he had built like a wall around the ruin of himself, against the voice in his head that sounded like his own but spoke with his mentor's logic — Edmund unlatched the gate and lifted the child into his arms.

She weighed almost nothing. She smelled of woodsmoke and long roads and, beneath it, faintly, impossibly, of white blossoms.

* * *

He fed her soup and she ate the way starving children eat, methodically, without pleasure, as if eating were a duty. He set her by the fire and she sat the way she had stood at the gate — perfectly still, watching everything, trusting nothing. He asked her questions in the valley speech and got silence. He asked in Aldermere's tongue and got answers of four words or fewer, each one landing in his chest like a struck bell.

Her name was Wren. She had walked a long way. She did not know from where, exactly; the road had been given to her, she said, "*sel ammarith*" — by the binding. When he pressed her, her brow furrowed and she said the strangest thing he would hear that night, and he would hear many strange things:

"The country is calling its people home. Before it goes to sleep."

He told himself it was fever-talk. Children who walk out of winters carry fevers the way other children carry dolls. But her skin was cool, and her eyes were clear, and when she looked at the frost on his window she said, softly, in the cadence of the oath-marks, "*Veth serrath kalen*" — *the long winter comes* — and something in Edmund's chest, some muscle he had spent three decades unlearning the use of, clenched around the words and would not let go.

He gave her his bed and sat in the chair by the fire with his hands empty in his lap, which was how he had sat most evenings for thirty years, except that tonight his hands wanted a sword in them, and that wanting frightened him more than anything the child had said.

Because he understood now. He understood what she was. She was not a refugee, because there was nothing left to flee. She was not an orphan, because there was no country left to orphan her. She was a *remainder* — one of the last living threads of a tapestry the world had cut away, and the world had noticed the loose thread, and the world was pulling.

And he was another thread. The last one besides her. Bound by an oath spoken in a language that should not exist, kept safe in a valley where the binding could not reach him — safe the way a man sealed in a tomb is safe.

Edmund looked at his hands in the firelight. A gardener's hands. He had made them so, season by season, callus by callus, until the swordspeaker's grip had softened into something gentler. It had taken thirty years. It had been the hardest work of his life, harder than any war, and he had believed — he had *needed* to believe — that it was finished.

Through the window, the first frost glittered on the garden he had tended back to life after letting everything else die. Beyond the garden, past the fence line, the dark hills rose toward the valley's edge, where the boundary stones stood in their ancient ring, and beyond the stones lay the world, with all its erased kingdoms and its gathering winters.

From the bedroom, small and clear, the child began to speak in her sleep. Not dreaming-talk. Recitation. The oath-marks, word-perfect, in the voice of someone much older than eight, reciting lines Edmund had not heard since a courtyard full of white blossoms fell silent forever.

He listened until the recitation ended. Then he sat very still in the firelight, an old man in a young man's body, and admitted what he had ridden down from the pass thirty years ago already knowing:

Stillness was not peace. Stillness was just a slower way of drowning.

Tomorrow, he told himself, he would take her to the boundary stones and turn her over to someone who could help her. Tomorrow he would go back to his beans and his frosts and his careful, windowless life.

But even as he thought it, his eyes went to the chest in the corner — the one he had not opened in thirty years, the one under the folded blankets, where a sword lay wrapped in oilcloth, humming, he suddenly understood, all this time, like a held breath.

Like a door, waiting.
