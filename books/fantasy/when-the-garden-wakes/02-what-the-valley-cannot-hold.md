## 2. What the Valley Cannot Hold

Edmund woke before dawn to the sound of the child talking in her sleep.

He had put her in the loft above the kitchen, wrapped in a quilt his mother had stitched in a country that no longer existed, and for three nights she had slept like something drowned. Now her voice came down through the floorboards, thin and clear as water over stones, and Edmund lay in the dark with his hands folded on his chest and listened to a dead language fill his house.

*Veth-ala. Veth-ala morren.* Wake the garden. Wake the garden's sleeping lord.

He knew the words. He had spoken them himself, once, kneeling in a circle of white-robed swordspeakers while the high bells of Aldermere rang the hour of his oath. Thirty years, and the tongue still lived in him like a splinter too deep to draw.

He climbed the ladder quietly. Wren had kicked the quilt half off; frost breathed silver on the little window, and in its light he could see the marks on her arms — the oath-marks, inked in the old way, spirals and broken lines that no needle alive could make anymore. They were fading. Not washing out. *Fading*, the way a sound fades after the bell has stopped, still present but less there every moment.

Last night they had been dark as river-stone. This morning they were gray.

Edmund sat on the edge of the loft until the sky went from black to iron to the color of ash roses, and the child talked on in her sleep, calling a garden awake in a kingdom that had been unwritten from the world.

* * *

Wren dreamed of snow that fell upward.

She was walking through it — through Aldermere, though she had never seen Aldermere, only felt it the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue — and the snow rose past her into a sky the color of an unlit lamp. Towers stood along the road, white and fine, their windows full of nothing. She knew the towers' names without being told. *Hallenvarr. The Ninefold Court. The House of Doors.* Someone had taught them to her once, or she had always known them; in dreams it made no difference.

A voice walked beside her. It did not have a body. It sounded like her own voice played backward, or like wind through a keyhole, and it said: *You are late, little speaker. The winter has been waiting so long.*

*I'm not going,* she told it. *I live here now. With the garden man.*

*The garden man is a door that nailed itself shut,* the voice said, almost kindly. *But doors forget. Doors always forget. And you, Wren — you are forgetting too.*

She looked down at her hands. In the dream they were pale as paper, and where the marks should have been there was only smooth skin, blank as fresh snow, and the terror of it woke her.

Below, the garden man was already at the stove. He moved the way he did everything — slow, exact, like a man counting his steps so he'd never take one more than necessary. But when he set the bowl of porridge in front of her, his eyes went straight to her arms, to the place where her sleeves had ridden up, and she saw something cross his face that she had never seen on it before. It looked like grief wearing fear's clothes.

"Show me," he said.

She pushed back both sleeves. The marks were lighter than yesterday. She hadn't wanted to say so. Saying things made them true.

Edmund took her wrist gently, turned her arm to the window light. His thumb hovered over the spiral at her elbow without touching it, the way you hover over a burn.

"When did it start?"

"The night I came here." Her voice came out smaller than she meant. "It was worse outside. On the road, I couldn't feel my fingers sometimes. But it didn't stop when I crossed your stones. It just got slower."

Something changed in his face then — a door opening onto a dark room. "Slower," he repeated. "Not stopped."

"No." She pulled her sleeve down. "Your valley keeps the magic out. That's what you said. But it isn't keeping *me* out. I'm already in. So it can't keep me safe, can it? Because what's taking me isn't coming from outside."

Edmund did not answer. He stood, took his coat from its peg, and stood a moment with his hand flat against the door as if listening to it.

"Eat your breakfast," he said. "I have to go look at a wall."

* * *

The boundary stones stood at the head of the western pass, seven of them, older than any road, sunk to their shoulders in the earth. Edmund had not come here in eleven years. There had been no reason. The valley held; the world stayed out; that was the whole of his arrangement with existence.

Frost furred the grass in white rime all the way up the pass, and his breath smoked, and Wren had begged to come and he had refused and she had followed anyway, three paces behind, stubborn as a burr. He had not made her go back. Some part of him — the part that remembered being nine years old and sworn and terrified — had been glad of the company.

At the seventh stone he stopped.

The border was visible this morning. That alone should have emptied his lungs. Magic could not cross into the valley; that was the oath's great mercy, the wall between him and everything he had buried. But walls are drawn lines, and lines can be *seen*, and today the line hung in the air like heat-shimmer standing on end — a faint vertical trembling running stone to stone, beyond which the world looked subtly wrong. Colors too deep. Shadows leaning the wrong way.

Beyond the line, driven point-down into the frozen ground, stood a sword.

He knew it the way you know your own heartbeat. Gray blade, plain hilt, a single spiral worked into the guard — the mark of the Swordspeakers of Aldermere, the order that spoke doors open and winters closed. *Oathbrand*, they had called it, because it was not steel so much as a promise given weight. He had driven it into the earth himself, thirty years ago, on the far side of the boundary, because a swordspeaker's blade cannot be abandoned, only planted, and planted things wait.

"It's humming," Wren whispered.

He heard it too. A low tone, below hearing, felt in the teeth and the breastbone, rising and falling like breath. Like a man asleep who is about to wake.

"That sword has been silent for thirty years," Edmund said. His voice came out rough. "It should be rust. It should be nothing."

"Why is it singing now?"

Because the kingdom is dying, he thought. Because a dying thing calls its own name. Because somewhere past that shimmering line, the long winter that had eaten Aldermere whole was reaching out with its last strength, gathering up every scrap of the erased country still loose in the world — every word, every mark, every child — and pulling it home to freeze.

And the valley, his perfect valley, his thirty years of safety — the valley could not stop it. It could only keep magic out. Wren was already inside. The pulling was already happening, slow as frost creeping up a window, and no wall would hold it, because you cannot wall out a thing that is already in the room.

"Wren." He crouched to her height. Her breath fogged between them. "When you sleep. When you dream of the snow. What does it tell you?"

Her chin trembled, but she answered like a soldier. "That I'm late. That the winter's been waiting." A pause. "That I'm forgetting."

"Show me your arm."

She pushed back the sleeve. In the space of a morning the marks had gone from gray to ghost — faint as breath on glass. By midwinter there would be nothing left of her but a girl who had never heard of Aldermere, hollowed out and empty, and then the winter would simply take the husk home.

Safety, Edmund thought, looking at the humming blade beyond the line. Thirty years of it. And all it had ever been was death arriving slowly enough to be mistaken for peace.

He straightened. Beyond the boundary stones, Oathbrand sang its low note into the frozen morning, patient as a planted seed, and far off in the west — so far it might have been imagination — he saw the light over the mountains going the wrong color. Going white. Going the white of a door about to open from the other side.

"We'll need provisions," he heard himself say. "Two weeks. Maybe three." He was already turning back down the pass, already walking, because if he stopped moving he would have to admit what he was doing. "There's a ruin east of the Salt Fen. If any door out of Aldermere still stands anywhere in the world—"

"It's there," Wren said, catching up, her hand finding his. "I know the way. It's been teaching me the way in my sleep."

Edmund looked down at her — at the fading marks, the frost in her hair, the dead kingdom's last living word — and understood that the valley had never been holding the world out at all.

It had been holding him in, while the world came apart without him.

"I'll need my boots mended first," he said.
