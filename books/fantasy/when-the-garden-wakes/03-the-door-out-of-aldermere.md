## 3. The Door Out of Aldermere

The dead tongue did not leave Wren alone. It lived under her skin now, a second pulse, and every mile north it spoke louder.

She heard it first in the crows. They sat along a fence rail in a field of grey grass, and when they lifted, they called in the rising cadence of Aldermere — *come home, come home* — and Edmund's hand closed on her shoulder hard enough to bruise.

"Don't answer it," he said.

"It wasn't asking." She hadn't meant to say it aloud. The words came out in Aldermere's old shapes, rounder than Common, older than winter. "It was telling."

Edmund looked at her the way he looked at frost on seedlings — as if measuring how much life was left. Since they'd crossed out of the valley three days past, she had been fading. She knew it even if he pretended not to see. Her shadow had gone thin at the edges, like cloth worn through. When she slept, snow gathered on her eyelashes that no fire could melt. The erased country wanted its last speaker back, and it was not particular about whether she arrived breathing.

"We're close," Edmund said, and said nothing else for a long time.

They walked the old road — or what the old road had become: a seam of pale stones running through country that remembered being something else. Trees grew wrong here, their rings spiraling inward. Rivers ran slow and silver and made no sound. Twice Wren saw towers standing in the distance that were gone when she blinked. The world had not just forgotten Aldermere; it had healed over the forgetting, the way flesh heals over a splinter, and here at the edge of the wound everything was scar tissue and ache.

On the third evening they came to the ruins.

Wren had imagined a city. What remained was a suggestion of one — arches without walls, a plaza of cracked white stone where a fountain still wept water into nothing. And at its center, rising from the rubble like a tooth from a jaw, stood the door.

It was only a door. That was the horror of it. A plain wooden door with an iron ring, standing upright and unattached in the middle of the ruined plaza, and around its frame the air shimmered faintly, like heat off summer stones, though the cold here could crack bone.

"The last door," Edmund breathed. He sounded like a man hearing his mother's voice after thirty years. "It's smaller than I remembered."

"Everything is," said a voice from the fountain.

* * *

They came out of the shadows between arches the way mist comes off a lake — without hurry, without sound. There were nine of them, tall figures in armor the color of old ice, and where their faces should have been there was only smooth steel, featureless, reflecting the ruin back at itself. Wren felt Edmund go rigid beside her.

"The Hollow Court," he said quietly.

"You remember us." The speaker stepped forward. His armor was different — darker, worked with spirals that hurt to look at, and his voice came from somewhere deeper than his chest. When he raised his hands to lift away his helm, the helm came away, but the face beneath was one Wren recognized from Edmund's rare, drunk-soft stories by the fire: weathered, kind-eyed, grey-bearded. Master Corvin, who had taught Edmund the swordspeaker's forms. Who had died at the fall, Edmund had always believed.

"Corvin." Edmund's voice cracked down the middle. "You're alive."

"Am I?" Corvin smiled, and the smile fit his face badly, like a mask worn too long. "Aldermere didn't die, Edmund. It was pulled *inward*. All its magic, all its power, gathering toward this door like water toward a drain. We who served it were caught in the current. The Court drank what was left of us and kept what was useful." He spread his arms. "I am the Court's voice now. I speak for winter."

Wren's fading shadow fluttered. The nine figures turned their blank faces toward her in perfect unison, and she understood, suddenly and completely, what she was to them: not a child. A key. The last tongue that could command the door open so the Court could pour out into a warm and unmagicked world.

"She goes nowhere," Edmund said. His hand found the sword at his hip — the old sword, humming since the valley's edge, singing now like a struck bell.

"She goes *through*," Corvin said gently. "That is all we ask. Open the door, child, and be gathered home. No more fading. No more cold. You will sleep in spring forever." His kind eyes moved to Edmund, and this was the cruelest part, Wren thought later — how sincerely he seemed to mean it. "And you, Edmund. Set down your sword and step aside, and I will give you back what you buried. Your oath restored. Your name in the rolls of Aldermere. Magic in your hands again, as it was before you ran. Thirty years of hollow peace, undone in a moment. All you must do is let the child walk through alone."

The plaza went silent. Even the fountain stopped weeping.

Wren looked up at Edmund. She watched the offer land in him — saw it in his shoulders, in the tremor of his jaw, in the way his eyes went somewhere far away and green, somewhere with a garden in it. She thought: *he'll do it. Why wouldn't he?* She was a stranger. A duty. A mud-streaked obligation he'd carried out of pity.

Edmund drew his sword.

"I swore an oath once," he said. "I thought it was the thing that hollowed me. Took me thirty years to learn the truth." He set himself between her and the Court, and the sword's song rose until the shattered windows rang with it. "The oath never emptied me. I emptied me. This child filled me back up in a single autumn, and there is no power in winter or out of it that I would trade her for."

Corvin's face sagged with something almost like grief. "Then you break it here."

"I break it *here*."

* * *

They fought in the ruin of a kingdom, and Wren, huddled behind the fallen fountain with her thin shadow shivering around her, understood that she was watching two languages collide.

Corvin did not move like a man. He moved like winter given joints — each gesture pulling cold out of the ground, frost racing ahead of his blade in white ferns across the stone. And Edmund answered him in Aldermere's oldest tongue, the sword-tongue, the speaking-of-blades: every stroke a word, every parry a grammar. Where his sword passed, the frost melted and something *green* followed, impossibly, stubbornly, small shoots forcing up between flagstones in the dead city.

Eight armored figures began to converge.

"WREN!" Edmund's voice over the ringing steel. "THE DOOR! SPEAK IT OPEN!"

She ran. Her legs were lead and her breath came out in falling snow, but she ran, and she put her hands on the iron ring, and the words rose in her throat — the words the crows had sung, the words her fading blood remembered. *"Aldermere, hear me. Last of your tongue calls. Open."*

The door shivered. The shimmer in its frame deepened, brightened—

—and the Hollow Court reached her.

Steel fingers closed on her arm, cold enough to burn, and she screamed, and across the plaza Edmund roared — not with his mouth alone. With everything. With thirty years of silence and one autumn of love, he spoke the word that no swordspeaker speaks, the word that is the whole of the art and the end of it, the word that gives the blade everything and takes from the speaker all he has left to give:

He broke his oath in a single syllable, and the sound of it cracked the sky.

Light poured out of him like blood. The eight Court figures flew apart into drifting ice. Corvin staggered back, his borrowed face sliding off like wet clay, and beneath it there was nothing at all, only an old man's sorrow dissolving into snow. And Edmund Galloway fell to his knees in the ruin, spent, empty, smiling.

Wren wrenched the door open.

Beyond the frame there was no darkness. There was *spring* — a hillside blazing with blossom, air like honey, a country remade and waiting, warm wind spilling through into the frozen plaza like mercy.

"Go," Edmund rasped. He couldn't stand. He was holding his own empty hands like a man holding something broken. "Through, Wren. Go home."

She looked at the door. She looked at him. Her shadow, whole and dark and *hers*, stretched long across the stones toward the light.

"Come with me," she said.

He shook his head slowly, sadly, and behind them, at the far edge of the plaza, the frost was already gathering itself back together, piece by piece, into nine shapes that should have been impossible.

"Wren," Edmund said quietly, "run."
