## 4. When the Garden Wakes

The door did not open so much as remember itself.

One moment it was a slab of black stone veined with frost, and the next the veins ran gold, and the gold ran light, and the light spilled across the threshold like water finding its old channel after a long drought. Warm air came through — real warmth, thick with the green smell of growing things — and Edmund stood at the edge of it, sword-scarred hands hanging empty at his sides, and understood that he was looking at spring.

Not the valley's thin, obedient autumn on the other side of the mountain. Spring. Aldermere's spring, or what spring had become in the thirty years since the country was unwritten: meadows rolling away gold-green under a low kind sun, orchards in blossom, a river running silver-bright through land that no map had carried and no memory but his had kept.

Wren stood in the doorway with her face lifted to it, mud still dried in the creases of her elbows from a journey of a hundred miles, and she was crying without any sound at all.

"It's home," she said in the dead tongue, which was not dead anymore. "Edmund. It's home."

"I know," he answered in the same tongue, and the syllables came out of him whole, unbroken, as if the thirty years of silence had been only a held breath.

She turned then and looked up at him, and her eyes were the gray of the valley's winter stones, and they were asking the question before her mouth could find it.

"Go on," Edmund said.

"You could come." Her voice cracked on it. "The door is open. You opened it. You're the one who—"

"I broke my oath, little bird." He crouched, the way he had crouched a hundred times in the garden to be level with her, weeding beside her, showing her how deep to set a seed. The habit of tenderness had outlived everything else in him. "An oath broken doesn't make a man free. It makes him door-shaped. I can stand in the way of things. I can't walk through them."

"That's not fair," she said, and it was the plainest, truest sentence anyone had spoken to him in three decades.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

Behind Wren, the meadow wind moved, and in the wind there were voices — faint, layered, the sound of a country remembering its own name. Figures were coming across the grass. Not the Hollow Court; that shadow had gone out of the world with the breaking of his sword-oath, dissolving like smoke when the fire is finally lit. These were others. People. Some wore the old colors of Aldermere's houses, faded almost to white. Some looked as though they had been standing in the long winter so long they had forgotten their own faces and were only now, blinking in the sun, recalling them. They moved toward the door because Wren was in it, and she was a speaker, and speakers were how a country found its way back.

Wren looked at them, and then at Edmund, and then she did the thing that undid him more cleanly than any blade ever had: she threw her arms around his neck and held on, small and fierce and smelling of woodsmoke and road dust and the garden she had helped him keep.

"Thirty years is too long to be alone," she whispered into his ear, in Aldermere's tongue, the old blessing children gave to travelers. "Don't be alone."

Then she let go, squared her shoulders exactly the way he had taught her to square them before hard work, and stepped through.

The people of the remade country gathered her in like a wave gathering a single bright fish. She turned once at the edge of the crowd — one hand raised, mouth shaping his name — and then the door's light swelled, and the black stone went quiet, and the frost-veins cooled to ordinary rock, and Edmund Galloway was alone in the threshold country with the wind and the silence and the whole grey span of forever ahead of him.

* * *

He did not weep. He had spent thirty years teaching himself not to weep, and the lesson held, though it cost him something to enforce it. Instead he walked.

The threshold country was neither one thing nor another — a wide moor of pale grass under a sky the color of pewter, going on in every direction, the closed door behind him and no door anywhere ahead. It was the place between oaths, he thought. The space where promises waited to be kept or broken. There would be others here, maybe. Other broken men and kept women, scattered across the pale miles, each standing at their own private distance from their own private doors. He hoped not. He hoped it was only him and the wind.

By the time the light failed — if a sky like that could be said to fail rather than simply settle — he had found a hollow in the moor where the grass grew thicker and the ground was soft underfoot, dark and loamy, impossibly fertile for a country made of nothing. He decided, with the flat pragmatism of a man who has buried too many things to fear a night outdoors, that he would sleep there.

It was when he emptied his pockets that his hands began to shake.

Seeds. A handful of paper twists and folded leaves, each one labeled in his own careful hand, gathered in the last hour before he left the valley — the way a man gathers things without admitting to himself why. Sweet pea. Marrowfat. The brown beans from the row by the gate that had come, season over season, from stock so old he no longer knew its name. A pinch of foxglove for the bees. And at the bottom of his pocket, wrapped in the scrap of blue linen Wren had once worn as a headscarf, the seeds of the heartsease — the little violet flower that had grown wild along the garden wall every year he'd tended it, dying back each winter and returning each spring without permission, without invitation, without any oath at all. Just returning.

He knelt in the dark loam of the threshold country and broke the ground with his fingers, because he had no spade and had never, in thirty years, needed one that his hands could not replace.

He planted the sweet pea first, then the beans, then the foxglove, working in the pewter half-light like a man saying prayers he didn't believe in, and he spoke over each furrow the only blessing the valley had ever taught him — the plain gardener's words, *grow, be fed, be well* — and it was nothing, it was less than nothing, it was an old man muttering over dirt at the end of the world.

The ground answered.

Not dramatically. That was the thing he could not have prepared for, though nothing could have prepared him for it. No surge of light, no thunder in the pewter sky. Just warmth — a gentle, rising warmth out of the soil into his palms, like the earth had been holding its breath for thirty years and now, at last, let it go. The grass around the hollow shivered though there was no wind. Somewhere behind him, color bled slowly into the horizon like ink into water — not dawn, exactly, but the idea of dawn, arriving early out of respect.

And in his chest, in the hollow where the oath had lived and the breaking of the oath had left a door-shaped absence, something green put down a root.

Edmund sat back on his heels. He looked at his hands, these scarred, soil-blackened, ordinary hands, and turned them over, and the warmth stayed in them, tame and patient, asking nothing.

"Oh," he said aloud, to no one, to the seeds, to the waking dark. "Oh, you've been waiting too."

He laughed then — a rusty, disused sound, the laugh of a man remembering the mechanism — and wiped his face with his wrist, and whether it was rain or the other thing he did not trouble himself to determine.

* * *

Morning came properly at last, and with it the first green hooks of the beans breaking the soil, far too fast, fast as magic and slow as grace. Edmund planted the rest of the pocketful as the light strengthened, and then he stood and looked out over the pale moor of the threshold country — endless, empty, his — and felt something settle in him that he had not felt since before the fall of kingdoms.

Not safety. Safety had been the valley's gift, and safety had been a slow death, and he was done with it.

This was purpose. Small, stubborn, seed-sized purpose.

He was already planning it the way he planned all his gardens — where the beds would run, where the water would be found, how many seasons before the first real harvest — when he saw, far off across the moor where the pewter grass thinned into silver, a thin line of smoke rising against the brightening sky.

A fire. A tended fire. Someone else was here, in the between-country, keeping warm against the waiting.

Edmund Galloway took up his empty seed packets, folded them away in the scrap of blue linen, and started walking.
