## 2. A Proclamation in Ash

The criers came at dawn, as criers always did, when the streets were still soft with sleep and the light too low for anyone to read a face.

Tamsin heard the first of them from her window above the counting room, where she had been awake since the fourth bell reconciling a discrepancy of three coppers in the guild's tallow account. Three coppers. She had been chasing them for an hour with the particular pleasure she took in small wrongnesses—the way a single mislaid coin could unravel an entire column if you pulled at it gently enough. It was, she often thought, the closest thing to honesty left in Vellamare: arithmetic did not flatter you. Arithmetic did not pretend.

Then the bell of the Sable Court began to ring, and the criers' voices rose up through the fog off the river, and Tamsin stood very still at her window with her hands flat on the sill and listened to the end of the world arriving in the shape of routine.

*"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Third, of that name and line—"* the voice droned, tinny and bored, the voice of a man who had said these words eleven times already this morning and would say them forty more before his throat gave out— *"let it be known throughout the city and its liberties that the practice, concealment, or harboring of god-gifts is hereby declared treason against the crown and blasphemy against the Silent God, who died that mortal law might stand alone—"*

She knew the words already. That was the terrible thing. She had known them since the moment the royal seal-wax had arrived smeared across a client's payment two weeks past, red as a wound pressed into paper, and she had felt something in the wax that no honest seal carried—a residue, a hum, faint as a struck tuning fork heard through a wall. The crown had been preparing this proclamation for months. Someone in the Chancellery had been paying for ink by the barrel.

*"—all persons so gifted shall present themselves at the Sable Court within thirty days for assessment and lawful disposition. All persons harboring, concealing, or failing to report such persons shall be judged accessory to treason and subject to forfeiture of goods, revocation of guild charter, and death."*

Death. Said in the same breath as *forfeiture of goods*, as though they were items on the same invoice. Tamsin supposed that to a chancery clerk they were. She had audited enough crown accounts to know how the kingdom priced things. A life was a line item. A gift was a liability to be written down.

Below her window, the street was filling—not with panic, not yet, but with that peculiar morning crowd that gathered wherever a crier planted his staff, the same crowd that would gather for a bear-baiting or a public hanging, hungry for spectacle and grateful it wasn't theirs. A fishwife stood with her basket on her hip, mouth open. Two apprentices from the dyers' row nudged each other. An old man in a nightshirt spat into the gutter and said something Tamsin couldn't hear, and the people near him laughed, but not comfortably.

Tamsin counted them without meaning to. It was what she did. Eleven adults, three children, one dog. Nine of the eleven would go home and say nothing. One would repeat the proclamation to his wife with relish. And one—the fishwife, maybe, or the old man—would lie awake tonight wondering about someone they knew. A cousin whose burns healed overnight. A neighbor's boy who never lost at dice. A woman who could tell when a storm was coming three days out and called it *a feeling in her teeth*.

That was how the hunt would actually work, Tamsin thought. Not with witch-finders and pyres, not at first. With neighbors. With the slow acid of ordinary people deciding that the strange thing they'd always half-loved about someone was suddenly evidence.

Her own hands were steady on the sill. She noticed that, and noted it, the way she noted everything, because noting was safe. Her hands were steady because her gift had never once shown on her face. Ten years of practice. Ten years of keeping the ledger of herself balanced to the last copper, every impossible thing entered under a plausible heading, every heading filed where no auditor would think to look.

She was good at hiding. She had built a life inside the hiding, a warm little rooms-within-rooms existence: the guild, the ledgers, the precise and blameless arithmetic of her days. She had told herself for ten years that invisibility was a kind of safety, and she had almost believed it.

Then the second crier turned onto Cordwainer Street, and Master Ellery Voss came out of his shop.

* * *

Ellery kept the lock-smithy two doors down from the guild's back entrance, and Tamsin had known him for six years in the way one knows a neighbor: through transactions and weather-talk and the small courtesies of shared walls. He had re-keyed the guild's strongroom twice, charging fairly both times, and once, when the river flooded, he had waded through waist-deep water to help Oren Vael move the archive chests to the upper floor without being asked and without mentioning it after. He had a wife who had died of lungfever four winters ago and a daughter, Maren, who was nine and missing two front teeth and who sold roasted chestnuts outside the smithy in winter and always gave Tamsin the ones that had split open, because split chestnuts were harder to sell and Maren had decided, with a child's unsentimental charity, that a ledger-woman probably didn't eat enough.

Maren's gift was small. That was what everyone would have said, if anyone had known. She could make locks forget themselves. A padlock in her hand would hang open like a slack jaw; a bolt would slide home without being touched. Ellery had discovered it when Maren was seven, and instead of terror there had been a long, careful conversation behind the smithy door, and after that the girl simply never touched a lock again except her own front door, which she opened for her father every evening when he came home heavy with iron and tiredness, and he would laugh and say, *Well, I suppose I'll never lose my keys,* and that was all. A small gift. A domesticated one. A gift that had been folded into love so neatly you could hardly see the seam.

Tamsin knew because her gift told her. That was the whole of what her mother's inheritance could do, or all Tamsin had ever dared let it do: she touched a thing—an object, a document, a coin—and she felt its provenance. Its true history, in impressions rather than words. Whose hand had made it, what it had been used for, whether it lied. Merchants paid the Guild of Impossible Things outrageous sums to verify trades no honest appraiser could certify, and Tamsin sat in a back room with a bolt of silk or a deed of sale against her palm and felt the truth of it come up through her skin like warmth from a hearthstone. She had verified a bottled storm. She had certified a borrowed year. She had never once been wrong, and no one in the guild knew why, and she intended to keep it that way until she died of old age surrounded by immaculate ledgers.

She had touched Maren's chestnut-seller's apron once, taking it absently to help the girl fold it, and felt the lock-forgetting in it the way you feel damp in cloth. She had never mentioned it. There had been nothing to mention. A child who could open her own front door was not a crime; she was a daughter.

But the proclamation did not distinguish, and neither, Tamsin saw now, would the men coming up Cordwainer Street.

There were six of them, in the grey-and-crimson of the Sable Court, moving with the unhurried confidence of men carrying paper that outruns any blade. Not soldiers. Assessors. They stopped before the smithy, and the one in front—a narrow man with a clerk's stoop and a voice like dry paper—unrolled a scroll and read out a name.

"Ellery Voss. Locksmith. You are named in a deposition taken under seal. You will accompany us to the Sable Court for assessment."

Ellery came out wiping his hands on his leather apron, and even from her window Tamsin could see him doing the arithmetic every hunted person does: *how much do they actually know, what can I deny, is there a version of this where I walk home tonight.* She watched him arrive at the answer. There wasn't one. Depositions under seal meant a witness, and witnesses meant neighbors, and neighbors were exactly what she'd predicted at her window an hour ago.

"I don't know what you've been told," Ellery said, "but I'm a locksmith. I've lived on this street nineteen years."

"And your daughter?"

The pause was very small. Tamsin, who had spent ten years measuring pauses the way other people measured rope, saw it plainly: the half-breath before a man decides whether to spend his family to save himself or himself to save his family.

"Maren's at market," Ellery said.

One of the assessors smiled. It was the worst thing Tamsin had seen in years, that smile—patient, unthreatening, the smile of a man who has all morning and all the king's law behind him. "We'll wait," he said, and they arranged themselves around the smithy door with the ease of men settling in for a long commission, and one of them began idly reading the assay-marks stamped on the doorframe, and the street pretended very hard to be full of other business.

Tamsin should have moved. She knew that even then, some cold ledger-line of her mind entering the debt before it came due. The guild had a back gate that opened into the tannery lane; the tannery lane ran to the river stairs; the river stairs had a boatman who owed Oren Vael more favors than he could repay in a lifetime. She had walked that route in her head a hundred times for cargo worth far less than a child. She could be down her stairs in twenty heartbeats. She could knock on the smithy door and say, *Ellery, the guild needs a lock-man urgently, bring the girl, bring your tools, now, don't ask.* Oren would grumble and then he'd forge the papers, because Oren grumbled at everything and forged whatever needed forging. There was a version of this morning where Maren Voss ate supper somewhere safe and laughed with her mouth full of split chestnuts.

Twenty heartbeats. She counted them. She was still standing at the window when she finished.

Because—and here was the entry she would carry for the rest of her life, entered in her own private ledger under no heading at all—because going down those stairs meant touching the smithy door in front of six assessors, meant putting her name in their mouths, meant the deposition tomorrow naming *the Thornbury woman, guild ledger-keeper*, meant Oren Vael dragged in for questioning and the guild's charter revoked and every impossible cargo in their warehouses seized and every client who'd ever trusted them ruined by association. Meant, eventually, her own assessment at the Sable Court, where they would take her hands and press them to things and watch her face, and Tamsin Thornbury was very good at hiding but she did not know if she was good enough to hide from men whose entire profession was watching.

She stood at the window and did the arithmetic that kept her alive, and the arithmetic was correct, and she hated it with a hatred that felt like swallowing hot ash.

Down in the street, Maren came around the corner with her market basket.

* * *

Later—for years afterward, in the nights when sleep wouldn't take—Tamsin would replay those ninety seconds frame by frame, looking for the moment she could have changed, and she would never find one, and that was the worst part. Not cowardice, which at least could be corrected. Just sequence. Just the plain fact that she was three floors up and the assessors were already at the door and a nine-year-old girl came skipping around the corner with bread and early plums, humming, and saw the grey-and-crimson coats, and understood everything at once, the way children understand catastrophes instantly and completely while adults are still negotiating with denial.

Maren stopped. The basket handle creaked in her grip. And then—Tamsin saw it happen, saw it as clearly as she had ever seen anything—the girl looked at her father, and her father shook his head, one tiny motion, *no, don't, run*, and Maren Voss dropped the basket and ran.

She ran well, for a child in market clogs. Down Cordwainer Street toward the tannery lane, toward the river stairs, toward—though she couldn't have known it—the exact escape route Tamsin had mapped in her head. For three strides Tamsin let herself believe. Then the nearest assessor stepped sideways with the unhurried economy of a man who had done this eleven times before breakfast, put out one arm, and caught her by the collar of her dress.

It shouldn't have worked. That was the thing Tamsin would return to, turning it over like a coin she suspected was false. A lock that forgot itself should have forgotten his grip. Every latch and buckle on that child should have sprung open at a thought. But Maren was nine and terrified, and fear does strange bookkeeping in a small body, and either the gift failed her or—Tamsin's stomach turned over slowly as she considered it—either the girl had spent six years training herself *not* to use it, six years of her father's careful warnings folding the gift down flat, and in the one moment it mattered, obedience was faster than instinct.

The assessor lifted her off her feet like a kitten. She didn't scream. That was somehow worse. She went rigid and silent, the way animals go silent, and her eyes found her father's, and Ellery Voss made a sound that Tamsin had heard dying men make and never a living father, and then two assessors took his arms and the narrow clerk said, "You'll all have your chance to speak at the Court," in the tone of a man announcing luncheon, and they walked the locksmith and his daughter up Cordwainer Street toward the Sable Court bell.

The street watched. That was the other entry in the ledger Tamsin would keep forever. The fishwife watched. The apprentices watched. The old man in the nightshirt watched, and did not spit this time, and did not laugh. Nobody moved. Nobody shouted. The crowd performed the oldest civic ritual in Vellamare, older than the kings, older than the Silent God: it watched, and memorized faces, and told itself that looking away would have been worse, and went home.

Tamsin watched too. From the window. Where it was safe. Where she had built her life.

She watched until the party turned the corner by the chandlery, and Maren's clogs—one buckled, one loose—flashed once in the early light and were gone, and the Sable Court bell tolled the hour as though nothing had happened, because nothing had happened, officially. No arrest had occurred. An assessment had commenced. The words were doing their work already, sanding the horror smooth.

* * *

She did not cry. She noted that too, distantly, clinically: no tears, pulse elevated, hands steady, appetite absent. She went down to the counting room because her body needed a task the way a fire needs a draft, and she sat at her desk and pulled the tallow account toward her and looked at the three missing coppers and found that she could not remember why they had ever mattered.

The morning light came through the counting-room window in a long dusty bar and lay across the ledgers like something laid out for burial. Tamsin sat with her pen uncapped and wrote nothing. In her head, unbidden, the audit ran anyway: *one child, assessed; one father, accessory; one witness at window, complicit; one escape route unused; twenty heartbeats, expended on self-preservation; balance outstanding.*

Somewhere below the city, beneath the foundations and the drowned aqueducts and the oldest stones, something she did not yet know existed lay silent in the dark, and the silence of it pressed up through the floor of the world, and Tamsin Thornbury sat in a room full of perfectly balanced books and understood, for the first time in ten years, that a balanced book is not the same thing as a clean conscience. The numbers could all be right. The sum could still be blood.

Near midday, footsteps on the stair—Oren Vael's tread, heavy and deliberate, the tread of a man who knocked on doors only when the news was bad. Tamsin capped her pen and smoothed her face into the mask she wore for partners and clients, the mild, competent, slightly boring face of a ledger-keeper, and she was reaching for the mask when Oren opened the door without knocking, which he never did, and she saw his expression, and the mask fell away before she could catch it.

"Tamsin," Oren said. "Close the books. All of them. We're calling the partners."

He paused on the threshold, and behind the grimness of his face something moved that she had never seen there before in eleven years of service—something that looked almost like apology.

"There's cargo coming in tonight," he said. "Cargo that breathes."
