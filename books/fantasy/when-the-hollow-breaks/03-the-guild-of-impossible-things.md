## 3. The Guild of Impossible Things

The partners' chamber sat above the counting room, up a staircase worn into shallow curves by four generations of boots, and it smelled the way the whole guild smelled—tallow smoke, old paper, and the faint mineral tang of the bottled storm that had lived in the cellar since before Tamsin was born. Eleven years she had climbed these stairs to report. She had never once seen every chair filled.

Tonight, all seven were.

The candles had been burned down to stubs by the time Oren Vael closed the door behind her and turned the key. That was the first wrongness. The second was the silence: no one arguing, no one laughing, none of Selke Marrow's dry commentary or young Fenwick's nervous throat-clearing. Seven faces lit from below like masks hung on a wall, and every one of them turned toward Tamsin as she entered, which was itself a third wrongness, because in this room she was furniture. Useful furniture. Furniture with a very good memory for numbers. But furniture.

"Sit," Oren said, though there was nowhere left but the eighth chair at the foot of the long table, and she took it feeling like a defendant.

She counted them out of habit, because counting was how she steadied herself. Oren at the head, silver-bearded, his left hand wrapped where a contract-guarantor's seal had bitten him years ago and never quite healed. Selke Marrow, elegant even at midnight, her gray eyes moving over Tamsin with an expression she couldn't parse. Fenwick Ostre, thirty and sweating. Old Maren Hult, who held the guild's shipping interests and had not left her house in the Harborside for anything short of a funeral in six years. The twins, Corio and Corisande Ash, who between them managed the guild's stranger acquisitions and finished each other's silences. And at the far end, half in shadow, Brother Aldous Quill—the guild's archivist, its memory, its conscience, if it had one—his hood pushed back off a skull-like face.

Seven chairs filled. Eight places set. Hers the only empty cup.

"The criers went out at dusk," Oren said, without preamble, because preamble was for men with time. "You'll have heard the proclamation by now, or you heard it three days ago in Ashmarket when they posted the first bills." He laid a folded paper on the table, and Tamsin recognized it before he'd finished unfolding it—the same bill she had stood in front of that morning, reading the list of marks while a woman two streets over was dragged weeping from her own doorway. "By order of the Crown, all persons bearing the god-gift are to surrender themselves for examination. Examination, in the language these people actually speak, means the pyre or the collar. Those who shelter the gifted share their sentence. Rewards are offered for denunciation."

He let that sit. The candle flames leaned in a draft from somewhere, righted themselves.

"I have received," Oren continued, "three messages today. One from the Crown's assessor reminding me that the Guild of Impossible Things operates under royal charter and would be wise to remember whose seal keeps us lawful. One from our counterpart house in Coldharbour, informing me that their gifted factor—a man who has moved impossible cargo for them for twenty years—was taken yesterday morning. And one"—his voice did not change, but something in the quality of it did, some grain of sand shifting—"from a client I will not name, asking whether we could move a particular item out of the city before the week is out. An item that breathes."

Maren Hult spoke first, her voice like dry reeds. "How old?"

"A child," Oren said. "Eight years old. Gifted. Her family is dead."

The silence that followed had weight, mass, temperature. Tamsin felt it press against her skin the way the bottled storm pressed against its glass down in the cellar—something contained that knew it was contained.

"You're going to say yes," Selke said. It wasn't a question, and her tone made it not an approval either.

"I'm going to put it to the partners," Oren said. "Because what I'm proposing isn't one cargo. It's a trade route. There will be more of these children, these families, these frightened people with strange talents, and every one of them we take in is a rope around all our necks. The Crown has made sheltering them high treason. I am proposing that this guild commits treason. Systematically. In writing." He smiled without humor. "In our line of work, gentlemen and ladies, we have always specialized in the impossible. I suggest we do so now."

Corio Ash leaned forward. "What does the Crown get if it catches us? Everything. Charter, assets, names. Every partner swings, and every clerk and porter in this building hangs beside us. For strangers. For *gifted* strangers."

"For clients," Corisande said quietly, and her brother looked at her.

"They pay?"

"Some pay. Some can't."

"Then we're not talking trade. We're talking charity, and charity is how merchant houses die."

"Charity is how merchant houses are remembered," Brother Aldous said, from his shadow, and the twins subsided as they always did when the old man spoke, because Aldous Quill had read more of the guild's history than any living person and knew precisely how houses died and were remembered both.

Fenwick had been working his napkin into ruins throughout. Now he blurted: "And if one of them burns a house down? You've all heard what the gifted can do. Fire from empty air. Sickness in the blood. A man in Dolefield last month froze his own street solid in his death-throes and killed four bailiffs. We take one child who can't control herself, one frightened woman, and we're not traitors, we're kindling."

"We are," Oren agreed, "and I've thought about that longer than any of you have been awake tonight. Which brings me to why Tamsin is here." He turned to her, and she fought the urge to look behind her to see if someone else had come in. "Tamsin keeps our ledgers. In eleven years, her ledgers have never once lied, never once been wrong, never once failed an audit—not the Crown's assessors, not the Coldharbour house, not the Salt Guild's inspectors, and God knows they've all tried. There are assessors in this city who trust her columns more than they trust their own wives. Am I overstating?"

"No," Tamsin said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that.

"When we begin moving people instead of goods," Oren said, "we will need papers. Provenance. Bills of lading that say this child is a consignment of Aluran wool, this woman is a case of wine, this man is a machine part bound for a mill in the provinces. Those papers will be examined—at the tollgates, at the docks, by men whose careers depend on catching exactly this kind of lie." He paused. "Forged papers fail because forgers think about ink and seals and forget about arithmetic. A manifest that doesn't reconcile gets looked at twice. A manifest that reconciles perfectly gets waved through, because clerks are lazy and numbers that agree feel like truth. So the papers we forge must be built the way she builds ledgers. Every figure true. Every total honest. The cargo fictitious; the books immaculate."

He let his eyes travel the table. "Her ledgers never lie. That is precisely why she's the one to write our lies."

Selke laughed once, softly, and Tamsin couldn't tell at what.

"And her risk?" Selke asked. "You've all noticed, I assume, that we've just described a hanging offense and assigned it to one person. If Corvasser's people ever trace a document back to this table, it will be hers. Her hand. Her habits. The assessors know her habits better than they know ours."

"That is her decision to make," Oren said, "which is why she's here and not upstairs filing tomorrow's correspondence." And then he looked at Tamsin directly, and everyone else did too, seven faces and forty years of guild history arranged against her, and she understood that the vote was already decided and this was the moment they gave her to pretend otherwise.

She looked down at her hands on the table. Long fingers, ink-stained at the nails no matter how she scrubbed, her mother's hands though she'd spent ten years refusing to see the resemblance.

*Just another entry in the books*, she told herself, and the words tasted like the lie they were, but lies were also cargo and she knew how to carry those.

*Say no.* The thought arrived with perfect clarity, the way her gift always arrived, unbidden, unwanted, showing her things she had not asked to see: Say no, walk back down the stairs, close the books, keep your ten years of hiding intact. They cannot force you. Oren is fair; he would sooner burn the guild than compel a clerk.

But underneath that clear thought ran the other current, the one she could not shut off, the one that had started that morning with seal-wax on a payment and become a woman dragged from her doorstep by men in crown colors. She saw the ledger of it whether she wanted to or not: *entry, the twenty-third day, watched and did nothing; entry, the following day, watched again.* A column of nothing adding up, page after page, year after year, until the sum of her life was a very accurate record of everything she'd declined to see.

Her mother had hidden too. Her mother had hidden for thirty years, kept her head down, kept the token buried at the bottom of a strongbox, and it had bought her exactly nothing in the end except a quieter grave.

"I'll need paper," Tamsin said. "Good paper. Three grades of it—cheap for the small cargoes, mid-grade for legitimate-looking freight, and heavy stock with proper watermarking for anything that passes a magistrate's desk. Seals I can source through the chandler on Wexler's Row, the one who owes us for the storm-bottle business; he already sells wax to four government offices and won't ask questions if I don't supply any. I'll want access to the archive, the complete manifests of the last ten years, so my forgeries match real traffic patterns. Nobody questions a ship that sails on a schedule it has sailed before."

She heard her own voice going flat and precise, the voice she used for audits, and understood distantly that she was doing what she always did with unbearable things: turning them into entries. Cargo that breathes became a line item. Treason became reconciliation. Somewhere in the arithmetic a child eight years old had become *one unit, miscellaneous, fragile.*

It was cowardice, probably. It was also the only way she knew how to hold steady.

Maren Hult made a sound that might have been approval. "The girl thinks like a smuggler."

"The girl," Oren said, "thinks like an accountant, which is worse." He rapped the table once with his scarred fist, and it was done—she watched it be done, watched seven heads incline around the table with varying degrees of reluctance, Selke's last and slowest, and felt the guild change shape around her like a ship altering course, everything below decks the same and the whole horizon different.

"Then we adjourn," Oren said. "Aldous, the old vault. Fenwick, quiet word to our harbor contacts—we'll need berths that don't ask names. The rest of you, your houses, your factors, your cousins: anyone who can be trusted with silence, sound them out gently. Anyone who can't, keep far away from them." His eyes crossed Selke's for half a heartbeat too long. "We are, all of us, about to become much less popular."

Chairs scraped. The twins left together as they did everything together. Maren departed on her cane with a nod to Tamsin that contained, improbably, respect. And Tamsin stood to gather her notes, her few lines of pencil, her figures, folding them into her coat—and found Oren waiting by the door, and Brother Aldous lingering in the shadow of the archive stairs, watching her with his hooded, patient eyes.

"A word," Oren said.

* * *

They walked him partway home, the three of them, through corridors of the sleeping guild—past the counting room where her ledgers lay closed and square as bricks, past the stairwell window where the city glowed dull orange against the sky, watch-fires burning brighter than usual tonight, the whole capital lit like a forge deciding what to hammer.

Oren stopped at the landing below the archive.

"I want you to understand what you just agreed to," he said. "Not the danger. You understand the danger; you did the sums faster than Fenwick would have. I mean the thing itself." He rubbed his bad hand absently against his coat. "I've known you eleven years, Tamsin. I've watched you balance books that would make a bishop weep and stare down Crown assessors twice your age, and in eleven years I have never once asked you a personal question. I'm not asking one now. But I'll tell you why I chose you, and it isn't only the arithmetic."

Tamsin waited.

"Because you're careful," Oren said. "Careful people make the best conspirators and the worst informants. Careful people don't panic, don't boast, don't leave loose threads. And because—" he hesitated, and she realized with a small shock that the old man was choosing his words, actually weighing them, for her—"because whoever we shelter in this building, whatever comes through those doors breathing, they will be terrified. Terrified people can smell fear in the people helping them, and it makes them stupid and desperate and dangerous. What they'll need, more than bread, more than papers, is someone whose hands don't shake. Someone who writes the false name in the register with the same steady hand they'd write a true one. That's you, Tamsin. That's always been you."

"And if my hands do shake?" she asked. "When it's not a bale of wool standing in the courtyard. When it's a child who cries at night in the room above my office."

Oren was quiet a moment.

"Then your hands shake," he said, "and you keep writing anyway. That's all courage has ever been, girl. The rest is ballads." He turned to go up, then paused. "First cargo arrives within the week. Papers by Thursday. And Tamsin—" the apology from earlier surfaced again, briefly, in the lines of his face, "—I'm sorry it had to be you. It shouldn't cost one person this much. But it always costs one person this much. That's how the big things get carried."

He climbed out of sight. His door closed above.

Brother Aldous had not moved from the archive threshold. Up close, in the light of Tamsin's lamp, the old archivist looked less like a skull than usual—there was something almost gentle in the seamed ruin of his face, something that might have been pity, though from Aldous it was hard to distinguish from indigestion.

"You'll want the old ledgers," he said. It wasn't a question either; apparently that was contagious in this building.

"The last ten years," Tamsin confirmed. "Traffic patterns, routes, consignees. To build convincing fictions I need to know what real ones look like."

"Mm." Aldous turned, unhurried, and led her into the archive, down aisles of shelving that receded into darkness like the ribs of some enormous beast, ten thousand volumes of the guild's recorded impossibilities. He walked deeper than the recent stacks. Deeper than the fire-code shelves, past the locked cases of charter documents, past the century-marked ledgers bound in green, down to the oldest aisle, where the air went cold and still and smelled of stone.

He stopped at the last shelf and drew out a volume so old its spine had gone soft as cloth.

"If we are to forge convincingly," Aldous said, "you will need to know what this house has truly carried. All of it. Not the sanitized histories we show assessors—the real accounts. This house is older than its charter, Tamsin. Older than the current crown. It has moved cargo that kings forbade to exist." He held the ledger out to her across both palms, the way one hands over a relic, or a verdict. "If you're going to write lies in this house, you should first learn what truths it keeps. Start here. The earliest volumes. And when you find entries you don't understand—"

He paused. Something flickered behind his eyes, and for the first time in her eleven years, Brother Aldous Quill looked uncertain.

"—when you find entries you don't understand, in a hand you don't recognize, come to me before you decipher them. Promise me that much."

Tamsin took the ledger. It was heavier than it should have been, cold at the center like something pulled from deep water, and as her fingers closed on the binding she felt it—that faint, familiar hum at the base of her skull, the gift stirring without her consent, tasting the object in her hands the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

And beneath the hum, for the first time in her careful, hidden life, she heard something underneath it.

Almost like a voice. Almost like her mother's handwriting.

Almost like a scream, very far away, patient as stone.
