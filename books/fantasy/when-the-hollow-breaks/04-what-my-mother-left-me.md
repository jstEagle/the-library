## 4. What My Mother Left Me

The strongbox had been sitting in the corner of Tamsin's room for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once managed to look at it without feeling as though it were looking back.

It was a modest thing, really—blackened iron, no larger than a bread loaf, its corners worn round by handling long before her mother ever owned it. No lock that any thief of the Guild of Impossible Things would have given a second glance. The lock was not the point. The point was the seal: a disc of grey wax the size of a coin, pressed with a thumbprint and nothing else, set into a groove around the lid so that opening the box meant breaking the seal first. Her mother had made her promise—*promise me, Tam, promise properly*—that she would not open it until there was nothing left of her to ask questions of.

Tamsin had been nineteen when Merrin Thornbury died, coughing river-damp out of her lungs in a rented room above a chandler's shop in Lowbridge. There had been nothing left of her to ask questions of for eleven years now. And still the seal held.

She told herself, in the way one tells oneself small respectable lies every morning before breakfast, that she simply hadn't found the time. That the guild kept her busy. That grief deserved its privacy. But the truth was simpler and uglier: as long as the box stayed shut, her mother remained a woman with secrets rather than a woman with answers, and Tamsin was not at all certain she could survive the difference.

Tonight, though, the criers' proclamations were still ringing in her ears like a bell that wouldn't stop tolling. *By order of His Majesty's Grace, all bearers of the god-gift shall present themselves for assessment.* A neighbor taken in broad daylight. The wax seals on royal writs arriving smeared and wrong, like a fever breaking through polite skin. And beneath all of it, quiet as a debt accruing interest, the knowledge she could no longer un-know: that whatever lived inside her—the thing she checked ledgers with, the thing that tasted true from false on the back of her tongue—was exactly what the crown had begun hunting.

It seemed suddenly obscene, keeping a sealed box from a dead woman who had sealed it for precisely this kind of night.

Tamsin lit two candles, because one felt insufficient and three felt theatrical, and drew the strongbox onto the table beside her bed. She sat with it a while, hands flat on either side of it, listening to the rain come off the harbor in long grey sheets against the shuttered window.

"All right," she said aloud, to the room, to her mother, to no one. "Properly."

* * *

Her mother had been a toll-keeper's widow who was never actually a toll-keeper's widow, which was the first thing Tamsin had ever learned about how stories are built to carry weight. The man whose name appeared on Merrin's marriage record—a Brem Thornbury, deceased, of the Harrowgate tollhouse—had never existed outside of ink. Tamsin knew this because at fourteen she had gone looking for him, in the stubborn way of children, and had traced the record back through three parishes to a clerk who remembered being paid two crowns to write a name he'd invented on the spot.

"He was a kind fiction," Merrin had said, when Tamsin confronted her with the discovery, more hurt than angry. "The best ones always are."

What her mother had been instead was a verifier. Not guild-sworn—she'd refused the guild twice, Tamsin later learned, in letters that survived in the partners' archive, letters written in a hand that made Oren Vael go quiet for a full minute whenever he handled them—but hired, again and again, by people who needed to know whether something impossible was also something real. A reliquary finger that wept. A deed to land that hadn't existed last Tuesday. A bride's dowry chest that sang when opened under a new moon. Merrin Thornbury would hold the object, close her eyes, and say yes or no, and her yes or no was worth more than any assay office in the kingdom.

Tamsin had grown up watching this and understanding none of it. She'd thought her mother was simply very good at guessing. It wasn't until her own gift arrived at seventeen—not dramatically, not with light or voices, just a sudden unbearable *clarity*, the sense that every object she touched was quietly telling her what it was—that Merrin had sat her down at this same table, in their rooms over the chandler's shop, and explained the family business in four sentences.

"It comes down the mother's line," she said. "Not every daughter. Some lines skip five generations and then wake up furious. You'll want to hide it. Do you hear me? Not because it's shameful. Because it's valuable, and everything valuable gets mined." She had said it the way she priced goods: flatly, finally. "You'll be asked, your whole life, to be useful. Learn to say no with your whole face."

Tamsin had not learned. She had joined the Guild of Impossible Things at twenty-two and made herself indispensable by doing, very quietly, exactly what her mother had done—except she'd wrapped it in bookkeeping, which nobody hunts. A ledger that verifies itself is a miracle disguised as arithmetic.

Now she picked up the letter knife.

The grey wax resisted, then parted along the thumbprint with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Something in Tamsin's chest cracked along with it, an old seam she'd plastered over years ago. She breathed through it. Grief, she had learned, was mostly a matter of breathing through things.

Inside: cloth. A folded garment, wool gone soft with age and smelling faintly of tallow and some herb she couldn't name—southern, her mother's herb, from wherever Merrin had come from before she'd become a toll-keeper's widow. Under the garment, a bundle of letters tied with string. Under the letters—

Tamsin went very still.

A token. Small enough to sit in her palm, carved from a material she did not recognize: dark, dense, faintly warm, veined with threads of pale gold that caught the candlelight and seemed, when she tilted it, to move. It was shaped like a closed eye. Or a seed. Or a mouth about to speak. Her fingers knew it before her mind did; they settled into its contours the way fingers settle into a tool used for years.

And it *hummed*.

Not a sound, exactly. Nothing her ears could catch. It was lower than sound, deeper, somewhere in the bones of her wrist and the hinge of her jaw—the sensation of standing near a cathedral bell that has been struck but has not yet begun to swing. The hum rose as her grip tightened, and with it rose something else, something she recognized with a lurch of vertigo because it lived in her own chest too: that tasting-of-truth, that clarity, but *amplified*, a whisper turned to choir.

"Oh," she said. Very quietly. To the token, or the room, or the dead. "Oh, you had it too."

She should have known. Of course she should have known—her mother had told her it came down the mother's line, had said it plainly, and Tamsin had heard the words without hearing them, the way one hears weather reports about distant countries. But knowing a thing in the head and holding it in the hand were different economies entirely, and the exchange rate was ruinous.

Because if the gift came down the line—if it was inheritance and not accident—then it was not a quirk, a curiosity, a private talent to be folded into ledgers. It was a *lineage*. Someone had passed it to her mother deliberately or desperately, someone above Merrin in the long dark chain of women, and each of them had carried it through years that included witch-hunts before, purges before, kings who had decided before that the god-gifted were either weapons to be conscripted or fires to be stamped out.

Her mother hadn't hidden the gift because it was dangerous to others. She'd hidden it because she was the latest keeper of something old enough to have survived every previous attempt to end it.

*Everything valuable gets mined.*

Tamsin set the token down. Picked up the letters. Her mother's hand—sharp, slanted, economical—covered both sides of six pages, and Tamsin read them by candlelight while the rain kept its patient time against the shutters.

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead and you've finally disobeyed me, or the world has gotten loud enough that you had no choice. Either way: listen.*

*The token is called a hearing-piece, where I come from. It does not give you anything you don't already have. It turns the volume up. That is its mercy and its danger both, and I will tell you why in plain terms so you cannot pretend you weren't told: there are doors in this world that only open to our gift, and once you know a door exists, it will call to you. My mother heard the door. Her mother heard the door. I chose not to listen, and it nearly broke me, and I will not apologize for wanting better for you.*

*But if you're reading this, perhaps better wasn't possible. So: the piece hums near true things. Old true things especially. If it ever sings—truly sings, a note you'll feel in your teeth—do not follow it alone. Find the guild. Vael owes me, and Vael pays his debts.*

*One more thing, and then I'll let you grieve in peace. You'll find among my papers entries written in cipher. I know you, Tam. You'll crack it eventually; you cracked my spice codes when you were nine. When you do, understand that I didn't write those entries because I wanted to. I wrote them because I was paid to keep records, and records are the only prayers that outlive the praying.*

*I loved you past the edge of sense. Forgive me the sealing-wax.*

There was no signature. There never had been, on anything; Merrin signed documents with a thumbprint, always had, and Tamsin had thought it a quirk of a woman who disliked ceremony. Now she looked at the broken seal on the strongbox lid—at the thumbprint pressed into grey wax—and understood that even her name had been a kind of cipher.

She sat back. The candles had burned down a hand's width. Somewhere below, in the street, a drunk was singing a song about a fisherman's wife, badly, with great conviction.

The token lay on the table between the letters, dark and patient as a held breath.

Tamsin knew what she ought to do. The sensible entry in the ledger of her life was clear: wrap the token in the wool, return it to the box, reseal it with fresh wax, and bury the whole business under floorboards until the current panic passed. That was the path her mother had walked, and her mother had died old, in bed, of ordinary lungs. The path worked. The path had a proven record.

But the token hummed, and the hum was not a sound but a *question*, and Tamsin had spent her entire adult life unable to leave a question unanswered. It was the flaw under all her precision, the hairline crack in the glaze: she verified impossible things for a living because she could not bear for anything to remain unverified, including, apparently, herself.

She reached out and touched it—one fingertip, the way one touches a door in a dream, knowing it will open and fearing it.

The choir swelled. And beneath the hum, for half a heartbeat, she heard—or almost heard, or dreamed she heard—a voice, vast and frayed and impossibly far away, saying something she could not make out. One word. Perhaps one word. It might have been a name. It might have been *help*. It might have been nothing but her own blood in her ears, dressed up in meaning because grief makes liars of us all.

Then it was gone, and the room was a room, and the rain was rain, and Tamsin Thornbury sat alone at her table with her mother's letters and the growing, cold certainty that the silence everyone blamed on a dead god was not death at all.

Silence, she thought, her hand still resting on the warm dark curve of the token. Silence like that isn't absence. Silence like that is a held breath. Somebody, somewhere, is holding it—

Three floors below, the guild-house door boomed. Once, twice—the heavy iron knocker Oren had installed after the smuggling riots, the knock reserved for people with official business and official manners. Tamsin was moving before the second blow finished echoing, token into her bodice against her skin where it hummed against her heart like a second pulse, letters into the strongbox, strongbox under the loose floorboard, all of it in the practiced economy of a woman who had spent ten years hiding one life inside another.

Through the window slit, down in the lamplit street, she saw the escort: six soldiers in crown livery, and at their front a tall figure in grey, unhurried, gloved hands clasped behind his back, looking up at the guild-house windows with the courteous patience of a man reading a ledger he already knows balances.

Even across the rain-dark distance, Tamsin knew his walk.

She had sworn oaths beside that walk, years ago, around campfires, back when she'd still believed oaths were simple things.

"Dain," she whispered, and the prayer-token against her breastbone went very, very quiet—as if it, too, were holding its breath.
