## 5. The Inquisitor's Courtesy

Tamsin heard the bells before she saw the riders.

Not the guild's own bell—that dull bronze thing Oren rang for deliveries and disasters alike—but the cathedral carillon across the river, striking the quarter-hour in its usual indifferent way. What was unusual was the silence that followed it. The market square outside the Guild of Impossible Things went quiet the way a room goes quiet when a creditor walks in: not all at once, but in a spreading hush, stall by stall, voice by voice.

She set down her pen mid-entry. The ledger before her recorded the sale of half a bottled storm to a glassmaker in Lowbridge—three gusts, one thunderclap, remainder held in escrow—and she had been enjoying the small pleasure of balancing it. She finished the line anyway. Whatever came through that door, the books would still need to be true tomorrow. That was the whole point of her. That was the only thing about her that had never been a lie.

Through the leaded window she watched them dismount in the square: six riders in crown grey, and at their head a man who sat his horse like he'd been poured into the saddle rather than trained into it, loose and precise at once. He wore no armor. Inquisitors didn't need it; the crown dressed them in black-trimmed grey and gave them a badge shaped like an open eye, and let everyone else do the fearing for them.

Dain Corvasser swung down from his horse, and ten years fell on Tamsin like a dropped tapestry.

He was broader through the shoulders than the boy who'd shared her watch-fires, harder at the jaw, but he still landed with that same rolling step, weight on the balls of his feet, sword-hand resting easy because it never needed to rest anywhere else. He looked up at the guild's sign—the impossible thing painted there, a door opening onto nothing—and Tamsin saw him smile. It was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a man reading a map of somewhere he intended to go.

*Don't know me,* she thought, pressing her palm flat against the cool sill. *You never knew me. You knew Tam of the Thornbury company, and she died in a ditch outside Marrow Ford along with everything else.*

The door below opened. She heard Oren Vael's voice, pitched to its public register, warm as mulled wine and exactly as thin when you tried to drink it.

"Inquisitor. This is an unexpected honor."

"Master Vael." Dain's voice carried up the stairwell unchanged, too. That was the cruelest part. Ten years, a crown commission, a title that made grown merchants sweat, and his voice was still the one that had sung off-key around fires, still the one that had said *sworn is sworn* the night they'd cut their palms together under a waning moon. "I'm told your guild handles cargo other houses won't touch."

"We handle *accounting* for such cargo," Oren said smoothly. "There's a difference. May I offer you wine?"

"You may offer me your ledgers."

Tamsin closed the storm-sale book, squared it on the shelf with its hundred brothers, and went down to be precisely, boringly helpful.

* * *

Dain Corvasser had learned, in nine years of inquisition work, that guilt had a smell. Fear smelled of sweat and quickened breath; lies smelled of over-preparation, of answers arriving too fast or too smooth. But guilt—the settled, lived-with kind—had a texture he could only describe as *care*. Guilty people cared too much about the wrong details. They straightened crooked cups. They stood where they had always stood, as if habit were camouflage.

So when Tamsin Thornbury came down the stairs of the Guild of Impossible Things with a ledger tucked under her arm like a shield she'd remembered at the last moment, Dain felt the old recognition open in his chest like a wound that had never quite healed, and he did what he had trained himself to do with wounds.

He ignored it.

"Master Vael's keeper of accounts, I presume." He inclined his head. "Mistress Thornbury."

"Inquisitor." Her voice was level. Her eyes were the same grey-green as the river after rain, and they touched his face for exactly as long as courtesy required—not a heartbeat more. *Careful,* he thought. *Too careful.* A stranger would have stared. Strangers always stared at the eye-badge, or at the scar splitting his left brow, or simply at the fact of what he was, the way people stared at a headsman. Tamsin looked at him the way you look at furniture you've decided not to trip over.

"The Crown thanks the guild for its cooperation," he said. "This is a routine inspection. New edicts require verification of provenance for all goods of... unusual nature."

"Of course." She set the ledger on the table between them and opened it to a ribboned page. "Our holdings are itemized here. Provenance documentation follows each entry. We are, if I may say so, the most audited house in the city."

"I don't doubt it." He turned pages without seeing them, letting the silence do its work. Silence was the cheapest tool in his kit and the best. Around him, his six men moved through the counting-room with practiced idleness—one by the door, one drifting toward the stair, two examining the great iron-bound strongboxes along the wall as though locks might confess to them. Oren Vael watched them with the expression of a man watching weather.

"You've changed," Tamsin said quietly, while he pretended to read.

"Have I?" He did look up then. He let himself. It was a mistake he'd known he would make from the moment he'd seen her name on the guild's charter rolls three weeks ago—*Thornbury, T., keeper of accounts*—and had sat with the paper in his hands for the better part of an hour, feeling twenty years old again and filthy with it. "You haven't. Same ink-stained fingers. Same—" He stopped himself from saying *same look*, which meant *I can see straight through you*, which was the one thing he could not afford to say, because he could not see through her, and that frightened him more than any gift ever had.

"Same what?"

"Same handwriting," he finished, tapping the page. "You used to keep the company's shares. Old Ser Aldous trusted you with the money because you were the only one who never rounded in his own favor."

"He trusted me because I was cheap," Tamsin said. "And because rounding is theft, even when it's small. Especially then."

"There it is." The words escaped him softer than he intended. *There she is,* was what he meant. The girl who'd once made a mercenary captain apologize to a cook over two miscounted coppers. The girl who'd sworn brotherhood with him over a fire that smoked because neither of them could build one properly, laughing until they cried, cutting palms, saying the words: *your debt is my debt, your truth is my truth.*

Your truth is my truth. He wondered, sometimes at night, whether the oath had been the first chain of many, or whether chains were all oaths had ever been.

"Will you want to verify the vaults personally?" Tamsin asked. Businesslike. Blade-flat.

"No need." He closed the ledger. "Your reputation precedes you, Mistress Thornbury. They say your books never lie."

"They say correctly."

It was almost funny. It would have been funny, once, and he saw the ghost of the joke pass behind her eyes and get strangled there, and he understood that she was doing exactly what he was doing: performing a stranger at someone she knew. Two actors sharing a stage, each convinced the other was the audience.

He hoped to every silent god that she was only a clerk. He had stopped believing in silent gods years ago—he'd seen what the gifted could do, had written the reports—but he found, standing in the counting-room of the Impossible Things with the ghost of a campfire between them, that he desperately wanted one miracle: that Tamsin Thornbury was exactly what she appeared to be, and the ache under his ribs was just nostalgia, and he could write *nothing irregular* in his report and ride out and never come back.

His men found nothing, of course. He'd known they wouldn't. People like Tamsin didn't leave things where six bored soldiers could find them.

At the door, he paused. Courtesy demanded a farewell; something older demanded more.

"Tam," he said, low, using the name he had no right to anymore, and watched her flinch—a hairline crack in all that careful masonry. "If anyone ever troubles this house. If anything... impossible should happen to it. Send for me. Not the local magistrates. Me."

"Why?" she asked. "Inquisitors don't do favors."

"No," he agreed. "But brothers do."

He felt her go still, all the way down, the way water goes still before it freezes. Then she smiled, and it was the worst thing he'd seen all day, because it was warm and it was false and it was perfect.

"Good day, Inquisitor Corvasser. The guild is always open to the Crown."

*That,* he thought, riding out through the gate with his men falling in behind him, *is exactly what I'm afraid of.*

* * *

Tamsin stood in the doorway until the hoofbeats faded, smiling her shopkeeper's smile at the empty square, and only let it fall when the last grey rider turned the corner.

Then she went inside, climbed the stairs on legs that felt borrowed, locked herself in the ledger room, and put both hands flat on the desk to stop them shaking.

*Brothers do.*

He'd said it lightly. He'd said it like a pleasantry, a coin tossed to a beggar. But Dain Corvasser had never in his life spent words carelessly—not since the night at Marrow Ford when careless words had cost the company everything, when he'd learned that language was a blade you sheathed or bled on. He had said *brothers* deliberately. He had said it to see what her face would do.

And her face had done it. She'd felt the crack run through her, plain as a fault-line in porcelain, and she'd known he'd seen it, and now they were both pretending otherwise, which was how they'd always handled the unbearable: side by side, eyes forward, whistling past the dead.

She pulled out the day's ledger and forced herself to write the inspection entry, because the ritual steadied her the way prayer steadied other people:

*Crown inspection, six riders, led by Inquisitor D. Corvasser. Nothing irregular observed. Nothing offered that was not asked for.*

Every word true. Every word a lie.

Because he hadn't asked for anything, that was the thing. Nine years of inquisition and he'd inspected her ledgers for the length of a cup of tea going cold and left without pressing. Either he'd found what he wanted—and wanted her to know he'd found it—or he was building something slower and worse. Dain had always been patient. He'd been the one who taught her to wait out a mark, back when they were both blades for hire and patience was a weapon they sharpened together.

*He knows,* whispered a voice in her that sounded unpleasantly like her mother. *He looked at you the way you look at a ledger with a column that won't balance. He doesn't know where the error is yet. Give him time.*

She took out the prayer-token.

She shouldn't have. Not here, not with the taste of the inspection still in the air. But her hand went to the hidden seam of her sleeve the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth, and she drew it out and laid it on the desk: a disc of dark metal, worn smooth, stamped with a spiral that wasn't quite a knot and wasn't quite an eye. Her mother's. The only thing her mother had left besides the gift and the grave.

It hummed faintly against the wood, as it always did against her skin—as it had hummed the first night she'd opened the strongbox, singing some note below hearing that lived in the bones of her wrist instead of her ears. The priests said the god was dead, gone silent these thousand years, and that whatever remained of him was silence, holy and final. But the token hummed. And Tamsin had spent ten years telling herself it was resonance, metallurgy, a trick of the forge.

Today, with Dain's voice still hanging in the room—*brothers do*—the hum felt less like a trick and more like a pulse.

A knock at the door. She palmed the token so fast it was almost a magic trick in itself.

"Enter."

Oren Vael came in, and for once the guildmaster's face held no performance at all. He looked old. He looked like a man who had just watched a wolf walk politely through his sheep-pen and tip its hat.

"Well?" he said.

"He took nothing. Copied nothing. Asked for the public ledgers only."

"That's worse."

"Yes," Tamsin said. "I know."

"He knew you." Oren lowered himself into the chair across from her, uninvited, which he never was. "I've been in this trade thirty years, Thornbury. I know what it looks like when two people share a history they're not declaring. He called you *Tam*."

"He calls everyone something familiar. It's technique."

"And what do you call it?"

She looked down at the ledger, at the entry she'd written, at the neat true-lie of it. *Nothing irregular observed.*

"I call it," she said slowly, "a problem I can't file."

Oren was quiet a long moment. Outside, the city bells struck the hour, indifferent as ever, and somewhere below, Wren—the child they'd moved through the tollgates two nights ago, currently disguised as a scullery maid with a genius for burning bread—dropped something iron in the kitchen, and the whole guildhouse held its breath and then exhaled.

"There's a second thing," Oren said at last, and his voice had changed, gone quieter, the voice he used for cargo that breathed. "Corvasser's men weren't only searching. One of them—I marked him, the tall one with the bad ear—was copying our delivery schedule off the board in the yard. Routes. Dates. Seals we use."

Tamsin's stomach went cold and slow. "We rotate those seals monthly."

"Monthly," Oren agreed, "starting next week. Which means whatever they copied will be worthless in seven days—unless they move before then. Unless they already know which entries matter." He met her eyes. "Someone fed them a page, Thornbury. Someone in this house."

The token hummed against her ribs where it lay hidden, patient as a heartbeat, and Tamsin thought of Dain's parting smile, and of the word *brothers*, and of how very much a man could learn from watching what a face does.

"Then," she said, "we'd better find out who, before the Inquisitor comes back asking better questions."

She wrote the date at the top of a fresh page, and beneath it, in her flawless, never-lying hand: *Inventory of everything we cannot afford to lose.*
