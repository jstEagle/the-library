## 6. Cargo That Breathes

The manifest said salt.

Tamsin had written it herself the night before, in her own careful hand, in the guild's own iron-gall ink: *One crate, pickled fish in brine, consigned from Greyharbor to the Vellum Street counting-house of Oren Vael & Partners. Provenance verified. Duty assessed at four coppers.* Four coppers. She had argued with herself for an hour over the duty. Too little and the toll-clerk would grow suspicious — the Crown's men were suspicious of cheapness these days, of anything that moved without proper weight behind it. Too much and he would want to look inside. In the end she had written four coppers and a small notation about damaged barrels, because clerks trusted other clerks to be lazy about damage, and laziness was the most reliable currency in Corvane.

Now she stood at the Marshgate toll with the crate six feet from her boots and her heart behaving as though it had never read a ledger in its life.

"Cold morning for fish," said the toll-clerk.

"Every morning is cold for fish," Tamsin said. "That's rather the point of them."

He laughed — a wet, obliging sound — and dipped his pen. She watched him write *four* and felt something in her chest unclench by a single degree. The forged provenance was good work. It was very good work. She had aged the paper with tea and smoke, she had copied the harbor-master's chop from a genuine waybill Oren had bought fair three years ago, and she had done the one thing no counterfeiter ever thought to do: she had made the arithmetic slightly imperfect. A smudged total. A correction in a different hand. Perfection rang like a struck bell; imperfection was just Tuesday.

"Sign here," the clerk said, "and here. And your mark there."

She signed. Tamsin Thornbury, factor for the Guild of Impossible Things, whose name appeared on more honest documents than any forger in the city could dream of, which was precisely why her forgeries passed. Reputation was a lockpick that opened locks from the inside.

The clerk stamped the waybill, waved two porters forward, and the crate began to move toward the gate arch on a handcart, and that was when the knocking started.

Three soft raps from inside the wood. Pause. Three more.

Tamsin's blood went cold and stayed cold. They had agreed — she had explained it twice, slowly, kneeling so their eyes were level — *no sounds, Wren, not one, cargo doesn't knock.* But the girl was seven years old and had spent four days in a smuggler's false floor beneath a tanner's yard, and seven-year-olds did not keep covenants. They kept fear, and fear kept them.

"Hear that?" the clerk said, glancing up.

"Barrels settling," Tamsin said. "Brine shifts. You'll get knocking all the way to Vellum Street if the cart hits a rut. Sounds uncanny, I know. First year I worked the harbor I nearly called a priest over a barrel of herring."

Another laugh. Another wave of the hand. The cart rolled under the arch, into the shadow of the gatehouse, where two of the Crown's gray-cloaked wardens stood with pikes and a brazier and the particular stillness of men who had been told to stop people rather than things.

Tamsin followed her own crate through the tollgate, carrying nothing but a ledger and a face arranged into mild bureaucratic boredom, and prayed — quickly, wordlessly, to a god everyone said was dead — that the cargo would hold its breath.

* * *

Four days earlier, the girl had arrived at the guild in a laundry basket.

Not metaphorically. An actual basket, wheeled in by Maret the laundress among forty others, and it was only because Tamsin happened to be auditing the linen accounts that morning — counting sheets, of all the blessed mundane things — that she noticed one basket weighed wrong. Not heavier. Lighter. A basket full of damp linen has a specific heft; she knew it the way she knew the weight of every coin denomination by sound alone. This one was wrong by exactly the weight of a small child curled around a small bundle of belongings.

She had lifted the lid, and a pair of enormous brown eyes had looked up at her out of a face gone gray with terror, and the girl had said, in a whisper like a held breath: "Are you the impossible things? Maret said you do impossible things."

And Tamsin, who had spent ten years making sure she was nobody's miracle, had heard herself say: "We do invoiced miracles. Get down before someone sees."

Oren had taken one look and made the decision the way he made all decisions, which was to say he had already made it and was merely informing the room. "Wren," the girl said — just Wren, no family name, or none she'd give. Her village was Ashford Fen, three days east, and the king's criers had come through it a week ago reading the Proclamation in Ash: *all bearers of the god-gift to present themselves for assessment, by order of the Crown, for their protection and the realm's.*

Protection. The neighbor Tamsin had watched taken in broad daylight had also been promised protection. He had been promised it while they put the hood on him.

"What is her gift?" Oren asked, and Wren looked at the floor, and Maret said quietly, "You'll want to stand back," and then the girl's shadow moved.

Not shifted. Moved. Detached itself from her heels and stood up against the wall like a second child made of dusk, taller than Wren, older than Wren, with hands that spread against the plaster as though feeling for a door. Then it folded back down into her heels and was only a shadow again, and Wren flinched as if she'd been struck, and said, "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. It does that when I'm scared. Mama said don't let anyone see, Mama said—"

"It's all right." Tamsin heard her own voice go low and even, the voice she used on panicking clients. "It's all right. Nobody here will hurt you for it."

But inside, some cold precise part of her that never stopped tallying wrote the entry anyway: *shadow that moves independent of source. Manifests under distress. Uncontrollable.* Uncontrollable gifts were the ones the inquisitors burned fastest. Controllable gifts could be taxed. Uncontrollable ones were evidence that the god's blood ran too strong, too close to the surface, too much like the old stories — and the old stories were exactly what the Crown had spent three hundred years drowning.

"We move her out of the city," Oren said. "North road, to the fen-kin. There's a cell of quiet folk past Marlebridge who owe the guild more than they'd like admitted."

"And the gates?"

"The gates are the problem." Oren rubbed his jaw. "Since the Proclamation, every warden checks faces against the assessment lists. Children especially. Half the gifted are children — the gift shows early or not at all, and the Crown knows it." He looked at Tamsin, and she saw the assignment arrive in his eyes before he spoke it, the way she always saw entries arrive in a ledger before the pen touched paper. "But papers aren't checked the same way. Cargo moves. Cargo has provenance. Cargo, Tamsin, has *you*."

"I'm a ledger-keeper," she said. "I'm not a smuggler."

"You're both. You've simply been honest about one of them." He slid a blank waybill across the table. "Forge me a shipment that breathes."

* * *

So now: Marshgate, morning fog off the canal, the crate rolling ahead of her into the shadow of the gatehouse where the gray cloaks stood.

The senior warden stepped away from the brazier. He was young, which was worse than old — old wardens had learned what questions cost, young ones were still collecting the answers. He put one hand flat on the crate's lid.

"Open it."

"She's sealed fish, warden, and she's been sealed since Greyharbor. If you open her you'll be smelling that seal for a week, and so will everyone within thirty feet of this gate, and I'll have to file a claim for spoiled goods against the Marshal's office, and neither of us wants the paperwork." Tamsin said it pleasantly, wearily, the voice of a woman who had filed a hundred such claims and hated every one. She produced the waybill. "Provenance verified at Greyharbor, duty paid here, consignee is my own house. You can walk the crate to my door yourself if you like. I'll even carry the ledger."

The warden didn't take the waybill. He kept his hand on the lid, and Tamsin watched his fingers — watched them the way she watched everything now, since the day she'd understood that watching was the only gift she could safely use — and saw them press down, once, testing the weight of the answer beneath.

From inside the crate came silence. Blessed, total, obedient silence.

Then the warden said, "There's a new order. All containers over two feet square get a spike-test at the corner seams. Nothing personal. Fish included."

Spike-test. An iron rod driven into the wood at the joints, to feel for hollow spaces, false floors, breathing cargo. Tamsin's mind ran the geometry at terrible speed: the false bottom sat four inches above the true floor, padded with wool, and a spike driven hard at the front-left seam would pass through packing straw and stop a finger's width above—

Above a seven-year-old girl who had already been told once today to be silent, and might not manage it twice.

"Of course," Tamsin heard herself say. "Test away. Front-left seam's the loose one, mind, she took water in the crossing. You'll want the back corners."

Why did I tell him that. Why did I help him aim.

Because refusing to help aimed him at the truth instead. Because a ledger-keeper who flinched was worth ten of a ledger-keeper who cooperated. Because terror, she was learning, transmuted — that was the alchemy nobody warned you about — terror went into the crucible as panic and came out as purpose, and purpose could hold a face steady while an iron spike walked toward a child's heart.

The warden fetched the rod from the gatehouse wall. Long, black, tipped with a blunt point. He set it against the back-right seam.

And Wren's gift woke.

Tamsin felt it before she saw it — a pressure behind her eyes, a taste like a struck coin on her tongue, the same wrongness she'd felt in the laundry room amplified tenfold. Inside the crate, the girl's fear had crested whatever wall held it, and her shadow had come loose.

It came out through the seams.

Smoke-thin ribbons of dark poured from the gaps between the boards, pooled on the flagstones, and stood up. All around the crate, on every side, shadows rose — not one shadow but dozens, a ring of tall thin figures made of dusk, featureless, faceless, their heads all turned toward the warden and his iron rod. The brazier flame bent sideways as though bowing. The fog pulled back from them like a thing scalded.

The warden froze. The junior warden froze. Somewhere behind Tamsin, a mule screamed and bolted, cart and all.

"That," Tamsin said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, "is why the fish is sealed."

The shadows took one step forward, all together, silent as spilled ink.

The warden dropped the rod. It clattered on the stones, and the sound broke the spell of his paralysis enough for him to stumble backward, hand going to his sword, and Tamsin moved — fast, before thought could catch up — planting herself between the shadows and the man, palms out.

"Warden. Warden. Look at me. That is a manifested gift, class-three, and it is *defensive*, and if you draw steel on it you will learn in the worst possible way what defensive means." She was inventing the classification on the spot. She would invent the paperwork later; paperwork could retroactively justify anything. "This is a bonded courier-gift. Guild-certified. The shadows are a warding construct — they guard the cargo. That is what you're seeing. That is all you're seeing."

"They're *moving*—"

"They're doing their job. As am I. As, I suggest, are you — because the alternative is a report that says you attacked certified guild cargo at a public tollgate and got shadowed for your trouble, and I promise you, warden, the clerk who writes that report will laugh, and the officer who reads it will ask why you drew on property with verified provenance, and neither of those conversations ends with you promoted."

The shadows stood in their ring, patient as gravestones. The fog crept back toward them and stopped, unwilling.

Slowly — a degree at a time, like a door deciding — the warden's hand left his sword hilt.

"Guild-certified," he repeated.

"Guild-certified," Tamsin agreed, and reached into her coat, and produced the document she had forged at midnight with steady hands and a hammering heart: a certificate of bonded gift-courier status, bearing the guild's seal, the Marshal's countersign-chop, and a registration number she had invented from whole cloth but formatted correctly, because correctness was nine-tenths of truth. "You'll want to note the number in your gate-log. Section twelve, cargo provisions. Your sergeant can verify the format against the manual — page forty-one, if he's thorough."

He wasn't thorough. He was twenty-two years old and wanted very badly for this morning to be over. He copied the number into his log with shaking handwriting, waved the porters through with a jerk of his chin, and did not look at the crate again, and did not look at the shadows either, which were folding themselves back down into the wood like water running the wrong way uphill, drawn home to the frightened child inside.

Tamsin walked beside her crate out of Marshgate and did not let her stride change until the gate was a gray smudge in the fog behind them.

* * *

They opened the crate in a tanner's shed a half-mile on, where Maret waited with a covered cart, and the smell of hides covered everything else.

Wren came out of the false bottom white as whey, shaking, her eyes huge and wet. "I knocked," she said. "I'm sorry, I knocked, I couldn't—"

"I know." Tamsin knelt. Up close the girl was all elbows and freckles and unwashed hair, and her shadow lay behind her on the shed floor, quivering faintly, like a dog that had bitten someone and knew it. "You knocked, and then you were silent when it mattered, and then—" she hesitated, and chose honesty, because the girl deserved at least that much truth after the morning she'd had — "and then you saved your own life. Those shadows were yours?"

Wren nodded, miserable. "I didn't mean to. They just come out when I'm scared. Mama said they make people angry."

"Mama," Tamsin said gently, "was trying to keep you alive long enough to reach people who aren't angry. She was right for the village. She'd be proud of you for the gate." She took the girl's small cold hands. "Listen to me, Wren. What you did in there — being silent when you were told, and then standing up when standing was needed — that's not a curse leaking out of you. That's courage wearing strange clothes. Do you understand? Strange clothes. We'll find you better ones."

Wren stared at her. Then, with the terrifying directness of seven-year-olds: "Are you gifted too?"

The question went through Tamsin like a blade finding the gap in armor. Ten years of ledgers, of precision, of being the woman who counted other people's miracles without performing any — and a child saw through it in one look, the way children and gods and dying men always saw through everything.

"Why would you ask that?"

"'Cause you knew," Wren said simply. "At the gate. You knew what to say about the shadows like you'd said it before. People who aren't gifted get scared of us. You weren't scared. You were *fast*."

Tamsin held the girl's gaze and felt the prayer-token warm against her sternum where it hung beneath her shirt — her mother's token, humming its faint dumb note, the secret she had carried since she was barely older than this child. Every instinct said deny it. Deny, deflect, stay small, stay safe, stay a ledger-keeper.

Instead she said: "Ask me again someday. When you're older, and safer, and I'm braver. Today, you need to get in Maret's cart, and you need to go north to the fen-kin, and you need to live. Can you do that for me?"

Wren considered this with grave seriousness. "Will I see you again?"

"If the ledgers balance," Tamsin said, "yes."

The girl scrambled into the cart, burrowed into the sacking Maret held open, and was gone — a small shape, a small wave, a small voice saying *thank you* from inside the canvas as the wheels creaked away into the fog. Tamsin stood in the tanner's shed until the sound faded, and found that her hands, which had been stone-steady through the entire gauntlet at Marshgate, were trembling now that it was over. Terror, it seemed, collected its payment on delay.

She walked back toward Vellum Street through the waking city, past criers posting fresh copies of the Proclamation, past a gray-cloaked patrol checking faces at a well, past a mother pulling her child close as a stranger glanced their way. For ten years the hunt had been an abstraction to her — entries in other people's columns, losses she tallied but never touched. No longer. Now the hunt had a face: brown-eyed, freckled, seven years old, brave in strange clothes. Now every line of the assessment lists meant a Wren somewhere, in a basket, in a crate, in a cellar, waiting for someone with steady hands and forged paper.

Purpose, she thought, walking faster. Terror goes in, purpose comes out. Fine. Let it come.

She reached the guild as the bells rang eight, climbed to the ledger room, and unlocked the cabinet where the oldest books lived — because there was a thread she needed to pull, a thread that had been nagging at her since the night she'd forged the courier certificate and, reaching for authentic seals to copy, had opened her mother's strongbox instead.

Her mother had kept guild records too. Her mother's hand was in ledgers a century older than anyone guessed. And last night, copying chops by candlelight, Tamsin had noticed — finally, horribly noticed — that certain payments in those ancient pages were marked with a cipher she had seen exactly once before:

In the margin of her mother's prayer-token box.

She pulled the oldest ledger from the shelf, set it under the lamp, and began to read the dead god's account book.
