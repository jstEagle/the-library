## 7. Entries in a Dead God's Hand

The archive room of the Guild of Impossible Things occupied the oldest bones of the building—a cellar older than the guild itself, older than the street above it, older, some said, than the city's charter. Its walls were river-stone, fitted without mortar by hands that had not signed their work. The air tasted of cold and tallow and the particular dust that falls from paper when paper is very old: a sweetness like dried violets, faintly wrong.

Tamsin Thornbury had spent eleven years in this room and had never once been alone in it at night.

She was alone in it now. The lamps were trimmed to three flames each, per regulation; she had lit a fourth against all regulation, because what she intended to do required light the way surgery requires it—close, steady, unforgiving. On the table before her, arranged in a fan like a hand of cards she had not yet chosen to play, lay seven ledgers. Not guild ledgers. Older. Predecessor ledgers, salvage ledgers, the books of concerns the Guild of Impossible Things had absorbed or bought out or buried over its two centuries of existence. She had pulled them from the deep shelves over the course of four evenings, always during daylight hours, always with an excuse ready on her tongue—an audit, a provenance check, a simple matter of tidying—and she had carried them down here one at a time, wrapped in oilcloth, as though they were cargo that might object to being moved.

Which, she was beginning to suspect, they might.

Her mother's cipher sat open beside them on a single sheet of paper, copied out in Tamsin's own hand from the notes sewn into the lining of a coat that no longer existed. Her mother had called it *the merchant's alphabet*. Twenty-six ordinary letters, but written with a slant, a weight, a doubling that meant something other than what it said. A ledger kept in the merchant's alphabet looked, to any casual eye, like the cramped penmanship of a busy woman. To anyone who knew the key, it said whatever its keeper wished it to say, in plain sight, for as long as the key survived.

Her mother had used it for prices. For names. For the small treasons of trade—the true cost of a thing versus the recorded one, the buyer who must never learn he'd been undercut. Tamsin had assumed, all these years, that the cipher died with her mother's business, that it was a tool of a modest and mercenary kind, fit only for hiding silver from tax collectors.

She had thought that right up until the night she found the word *Hollow* in a ledger dated one hundred and forty years ago.

* * *

It had happened the way most discoveries happen: sideways, while looking for something else.

Three nights past, she had been chasing a discrepancy in the estate of the Vellum Consortium, a bookbinder's guild the Impossible Things had absorbed sixty years back. A shipment of calf-skin entered twice. A payment to a "M. Corvane" that appeared in the margins of three separate years without ever appearing in any index. Small ghosts, the kind every old ledger breeds, and usually harmless—dead men's arithmetic, errors that had outlived their makers.

But the M. Corvane entries were not errors. They were too consistent. Same amount, same season, same hand, year after year after year, across the span of four different account-books belonging to four different firms. A standing payment. And standing payments, in Tamsin's long experience, were the skeleton keys of history: someone, somewhere, had wanted something done regularly enough to pay for it regularly, and had wanted the paying quiet.

She had turned to the oldest of the absorbed ledgers then—the black one, its spine cracked like river ice, its first page bearing a date so early the calendar used to calculate it had since been reformed. And there, in the winter entries of its forty-first year, she had found a line written not in ink but in the merchant's alphabet, disguised among the routine records of grain and tallow:

*For the keeping of the Hollow shut — 300 crowns, paid to the under-custodian, as agreed.*

She had read it eleven times. She had put the lamp close enough to scorch the page's edge. She had held the ledger up and tilted it against the light, hunting for signs of forgery, later insertion, fraud—the thousand tricks she knew because she had committed half of them herself in service of clean books. Nothing. The entry sat in the flow of the year like a stone in a stream, worn smooth, unquestioned, part of the current.

And beneath it, in the same disguised hand, smaller, almost apologetic:

*He does not sleep. Write that nowhere.*

* * *

Now, on the fourth night, she worked.

The cellar was cold enough that her breath showed faintly, and she had brought a blanket for her knees and a pot of tea gone bitter from sitting too long over the coals. None of it mattered. She had forgotten the tea within the hour. There was a state she entered sometimes—the state the gift lived in, though the gift had nothing to do with tonight's work, tonight was only eyes and patience—and in that state the world narrowed to a corridor of paper. Time went strange. Her back would ache later, ferociously, and she would be surprised by it, as though her body had continued existing somewhere behind her without her permission.

She built the pattern the way one builds any proof: brick by brick, cross-reference by cross-reference.

The black ledger gave her the first payment series. The second ledger—a shipping concern called Harrow & Daughters, absorbed ninety years ago—gave her a second series, in a different disguised hand but the same cipher, which meant the key had been passed between keepers, taught, sustained. That alone made her hands go still on the page. Ciphers die with their users unless someone carries them deliberately. Someone had trained her mother's predecessors. Someone had maintained a chain of secret-keepers across a century and a half, inside the guilds, inside the trade, invisible.

The third ledger gave her amounts. This was where the scholarly thrill took hold of her properly, the bright hot pleasure of a pattern resolving, and she let it take her, because she knew—from long discipline, from ten years of rationing her own curiosity—that the feeling was honest even when what fed it might not be. The payments grew. Three hundred crowns in the earliest years. Four hundred by the second generation. Six hundred, nine hundred, twelve hundred. A curve, rising with the smoothness of compound interest or inflation or fear. Whatever was being paid for was becoming more expensive to keep—or more expensive to hide—or both.

In the margin of the fifth ledger, beside an entry from a year whose name had changed twice since, she found the first annotation that was not about money:

*The under-custodian asks again whether the term may be shortened. Answer as before: the term ends when the kings' line ends. It will not end.*

Kings' line. Plural. The first kings—the Nine, the founders, the ones whose statues lined the Processional with their palms turned outward in a gesture every schoolchild learned to read as blessing. Tamsin had walked beneath those statues ten thousand times. She had once, at nineteen, drunk cheap wine in the shadow of King Aldric's bronze boot and laughed at how his outstretched hand looked, from below, like a beggar's.

His palm was not turned outward in blessing. She understood that now, with a certainty that arrived in her chest like a dropped anchor. His palm was turned outward the way you turn your palm outward to show it empty. To show you hold nothing. To swear.

Every statue on the Processional was mid-oath.

* * *

By the third lamp, the tea cold and the blanket slid to the floor, she had assembled the whole shape of it on loose sheets laid across two pushed-together tables: dates, amounts, hands, annotations, cross-references, a timeline running unbroken from the founding era to within living memory. The last disguised entry she could find was dated thirty-one years ago. Two years before her mother had come to the capital. Two years before Tamsin was born.

The final entry read:

*Payment transferred to new custody. The name changes; the debt does not. Keep the Hollow shut. Keep the ledger shut. When they ask what lies beneath the city, say: nothing. Say it often enough and it becomes true, which is the only mercy we are permitted.*

Below it, in a different ink, faded to the brown of old blood:

*M. — the key is yours now. I am sorry. It should have been a lighter inheritance.*

M. Her mother had come to the capital in the very year the chain of custody passed. Had taken up a position with a trading house that, within five years, had folded itself quietly into the Guild of Impossible Things. Had married, borne a child, buried a husband, kept immaculate books, and died at forty-four with a sealed strongbox and a prayer-token humming against Tamsin's palm like a trapped moth.

Tamsin stood up from the table because her legs insisted, and walked the length of the cellar, and stood with her hand flat against the river-stone wall, which was colder than the air and seemed, in the way of very old things, to have opinions.

The god was not dead.

That was the sentence. That was the whole of it, the thing the ledgers said in a hundred disguised voices across a hundred and fifty years, and she had known it for three days and had not yet managed to make it real. Everyone knew the story; it was the first story, the ground-floor story, the one carved over every courthouse door: *the gods spoke, and their speaking broke the world, and the first kings silenced them, and the silence has held.* Silence. Death by another name. A finished thing, a settled account, a line drawn under a column and summed.

But the ledgers did not record a death. They recorded maintenance. Upkeep. A standing cost, rising yearly, paid by custodians who inherited the debt like a mortgage on land they had never chosen to own. You do not pay three hundred crowns a year to keep a corpse shut in a hole. Corpses are patient. Corpses do not ask, through intermediaries, whether the term may be shortened.

*He does not sleep. Write that nowhere.*

Someone had written it anyway. In a cipher, in a cellar, in a hand that had learned the alphabet from another hand, going back and back, a relay of frightened clerks passing a flame between them in the dark for longer than any kingdom they served had existed in its present form. And the last of them had been her mother, who had never once said a word, who had instead left her daughter a token and a cipher and a lifetime of small evasions, trusting—if trust was the word—that Tamsin would either never find the thread or never pull it.

Tamsin pressed her forehead against the cold stone.

"Mother," she said aloud, and the cellar took the word and gave back nothing, which was, she supposed, the family condition.

* * *

She worked until the candles burned to their collars, and then she did the thing she was best at in all the world, better than swordwork, better than the gift itself: she closed the books.

Not metaphorically. She restored the archive to perfect order. Each ledger back to its shelf, its exact shelf, its exact angle of lean, wrapped again in oilcloth, the dust disturbed along its spines smoothed with a soft brush she kept for precisely this purpose. The loose sheets—her transcriptions, her timeline, her proof—she did not shelve. Those she bound against her body, flat beneath her shirt, where they rode against her skin like a second, thinner set of ribs. The originals stayed here. The originals were safe here, or as safe as anything was, and moving them would create exactly the kind of absence a careful enemy notices.

Because that was the thought that had finally landed, in the last hour, with the full weight of a vault door: *someone else might know.*

A chain of custody spanning a century and a half does not survive on two keepers per generation. It survives on many, and most of them die forgetting, but not all. Somewhere in the city—in a counting-house, in a monastery scriptorium, in a crown office with tall windows and worse secrets—there might be another hand that knew the merchant's alphabet. Another descendant of the relay. Or worse: the relay had never been only clerks. The payments had flowed *from* somewhere. Royal disbursements, disguised, but royal. Which meant the crown's own archives might hold the plain versions of everything she had spent four nights decoding—which meant the witch-hunt currently tearing through the city's gifted quarter was not, perhaps, only a hunt for dangerous magic.

Or it was exactly that, and the danger was the point. Burn the witnesses, burn the tokens, burn the last people who might hear a god that was supposed to be silent—and then burn the paper trail, and then the silence would be total at last, permanent, certified, archived.

Tamsin climbed the stairs and locked the cellar door behind her and stood a moment in the dark of the ground floor, listening to the building settle. Above her, through the floorboards, came the muffled sounds of the guild at rest: Oren's cough, the partners' dog turning in its bed, rain beginning against the shutters.

She thought of Wren, asleep two floors up in the room they'd given her, the child with the flaring gift and the nightmares she wouldn't describe. She thought of Dain Corvasser's courtesy call, still fresh, the way he had looked at the guild's shelves a beat too long. She thought of the proclamation nailed to the tollgate, its wax seal fat and red as a wound.

Then she thought of the numbers. Twelve hundred crowns a year, in the last entries, adjusted upward in a hand growing shakier. Paid to keep a god shut in the dark beneath the streets where children slept. Paid, ultimately, by everyone, folded into tariffs and tithes and the price of bread, so smoothly that no one had ever thought to ask what the silence cost, only that it held.

The scholarly thrill was gone. What remained was dread, and dread's twin, which is responsibility.

She climbed to her room, barred the door, and by the light of one guarded candle began to copy her transcriptions a second time—because a keeper of impossible things knows that any truth existing in only one place is already halfway destroyed. Halfway through the copying, her hand stopped.

At the bottom of the final sheet, beneath the last entry, there was a line she had transcribed mechanically, without reading it as language, the way one copies a flourish. Now she read it.

*If you are reading this, then the key has reached you, and I am sorry, and you must decide quickly. The Hollow is not only beneath the city. It is beneath the ledger. Every entry we ever hid was a link in his chains. Free the pages, free him. But understand what you spend, and count the cost before the kings count it for you.*

The candle guttered. Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere across the sleeping city—she could not have said how she knew, only that the prayer-token against her sternum had begun, very softly, to hum—something far underground shifted its weight against chains made of words.

Tamsin blew out the candle and sat in the dark, holding the pages that were also, somehow, the bars.

Tomorrow, she would have to tell the partners.
