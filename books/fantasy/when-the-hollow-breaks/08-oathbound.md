## 8. Oathbound

Dain Corvasser had learned, in eleven years of inquisitorial service, that a city told you everything if you asked it the right questions. The capital spoke in smoke and shuttered windows, in the way bakers opened late and beggars chose certain doorways over others. This morning the city was telling him about the Guild of Impossible Things.

He walked to their countinghouse on foot, without his escort, because escorts announced intent and he wanted the morning to feel like coincidence. Rain came down in fine gray threads, beading on his oilcloth coat, running off the brim of his hat in a curtain that blurred the guild's painted sign: a door standing open onto nothing. He had always thought it an arrogant emblem. Now he wondered if it was honest. A door open onto nothing was precisely what he intended to offer them.

The night porter knew him by reputation and let him into the entry hall with the particular stiffness of a man who would later need to explain himself to his masters. Dain gave him a coin anyway. Fear made poor hospitality; silver improved it.

He had not come for the ledgers. Well—he had come for the ledgers, but not the ones Oren Vael would assume. Let the old fox bring out the trade books, the bonded cargo, the customs receipts. Dain would admire them all with the correct degree of boredom, and while he admired them he would watch the woman at the high desk with the ink-stained fingers, the one whose pen never paused and whose eyes never quite met his.

Tamsin Thornbury.

He had known her when her name was still Tam, when she was nine years old and could out-argue any man in the company, when she kept the mercenary band's accounts in a hand so small and exact that his father—the captain—had joked she wrote like a person settling scores. She used to sit cross-legged on supply crates doing sums while the rest of them sharpened steel. He used to bring her the ends of his bread because she forgot to eat when the numbers were interesting.

That child had been marked at birth. He knew it now the way he knew the weight of his own sword: completely, and with no comfort in it.

The file had crossed his desk eight months ago, part of the crown's great census of the border parishes, and he had read the parish record with a sensation like stepping off a stair that wasn't there. *Thornbury, Tamsin, b. —, noted: caul of unusual color, presented to priest for burning; priest's notation: "the flame bent away from it."* The flame had bent away from it. He had read that line four times, then closed the file, then sat in his office until the candles burned down, listening to the rain the way a man listens for footsteps behind him.

Because there were only two explanations. Either the record referred to some other Thornbury girl—and the dates, the parish, the mother's name said otherwise—or the woman keeping the most meticulous books in the city was god-gifted, and had been hiding inside the one profession where nobody ever looked closely at anything.

And either way, the file was not his alone anymore. Files moved. Clerks talked. The Office of Inquiry had begun its cull, and the cull did not care that Tam Thornbury had once split her portion of bread with a boy who cried after his first kill.

So he had requested the inspection himself. There were procedures for this—a courtesy audit, offered before the hard men arrived. It bought time. Time was the only currency he had left to spend on her, and he meant to spend every coin of it.

"Master Corvasser." Guildmaster Vael met him in the corridor, silver-bearded, unhurried, extending a hand weathered like old rope. "An honor. We don't often receive the crown's attention."

"The crown attends everywhere now," Dain said, shaking it. "Consider me the gentle form of the attention."

Vael's smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "We'll try to make it painless."

They walked the counting floor together. Dain performed his role well—he had always been good at roles; his father had taught him that a soldier's face was armor you wore inward. He examined the vault doors, the fire precautions, the provenance chains on three impossible cargoes including, he noted with genuine admiration, a bottled storm stored in a lead-lined cellar under a signed affidavit from a storm-mage who had been dead six years. He asked sharp questions and accepted smooth answers, and through all of it, the corner of his awareness stayed fixed on the high desk.

She had seen him. Of course she had. He caught it in the half-second her pen lifted off the page—an absence of motion more eloquent than any start. Then the pen resumed, and she became again exactly what she appeared to be: a clerk, precise and unremarkable, a woman made of arithmetic.

But her hand had changed. The letters were smaller than they'd been ten minutes ago, cramped down against the rule lines as though the numbers themselves might give something away if written too large.

*You know I know,* he thought. *Or you will, soon enough.*

The inspection concluded at midday with tea neither party drank. Vael saw him to the door with courtesies polished to a shine, and Dain paused on the threshold, rain waiting beyond like an audience.

"One small matter," he said, mildly. "Your records-keeper. The tall woman at the high desk. I'll want her assistance. Cross-referencing historical entries—there are irregularities in the parish tithes going back decades, and guild archives may hold corroborating material."

Something moved behind Vael's eyes, quick and guarded. "Mistress Thornbury is our senior ledger-keeper. Her time is much in demand."

"All the more reason she'll understand the value of accurate records," Dain said pleasantly, and stepped out into the rain before the old man could find a refusal that didn't sound like one.

* * *

Tamsin watched the door close behind him and did not move for a long moment.

*Dain.* Dain Corvasser, here, in her counting house, wearing the gray-and-scarlet of the Office of Inquiry as though he'd been born to it—as perhaps he had been born to something; he'd always worn authority easily, even at fourteen, even carrying water and standing watch beside boys twice his age.

She had known his name was coming toward her for years, the way you know a debt is coming due. Inquisitors' names appeared in the broadsheets; hers was prominent enough lately. She had told herself the odds were long, the country wide, the company scattered to the four winds by death and desertion. She had almost believed it.

Her pen hovered over an entry for forty yards of moon-silk. The figures swam.

He had looked at her. Not at her face—at her hands, at the desk, at the ledger itself, with the particular patience of a man reading a document he already understood. And once, only once, when Vael had turned to point out the fire-brands in the archive room, Dain's gaze had come up and settled on her, and there had been nothing in it she could name. Not triumph. Not suspicion, exactly. Something older and sadder, like a man looking at a house he grew up in and finding strangers living there.

*He knows,* she thought. Or he suspected, which amounted to the same thing with a man like Dain, who had never needed proof of anything except what people were. He had always been able to read her. It had been the best thing about him, once, back when being read felt like being held rather than being hunted.

The prayer-token hummed faintly against her sternum, and she pressed her palm flat over her heart as though steadying a bird.

She should tell Vael. She should go up to his office right now and say: the inquisitor asked for me by function but meant me by name, and I knew him when we were children, and you should know that whatever comes next, I am the crack in this guild's hull. That was what honesty required. That was what the new covenant of truth she'd been building in her own head since the cipher pages demanded.

But honesty also required the other thing, the thing she hadn't yet done: telling him what the ledgers contained. What the first kings had paid to keep shut. And she couldn't do both at once, not yet, not with the ink barely dry on her own understanding.

Instead she finished the moon-silk entry, blotted it, squared the book, and went to find out what Dain Corvasser wanted from her—knowing already, in the place where the token hummed, that he would want the one thing she could not give.

* * *

He waited for her in the map room, alone, having dismissed the guild's escorting partner with a courtesy that permitted no argument. Rain streaked the tall windows. A chart of the river deltas hung on the wall, and beneath it a table bearing a decanter of wine and two glasses, which someone had set out for negotiations that would not happen here.

"Tamsin Thornbury," he said, when she entered. He did not offer his hand. "It's been a long time."

"Eleven years, four months." She kept her voice level. "Give or take."

"You counted."

"I count everything. You remember."

Something flickered across his face—there and gone, like fish under ice. "I remember."

He looked older than thirty-one. Not worn, exactly; carved. The softness of the boy she'd known had been planed away and what remained was all structure, jaw and cheekbone and the scar bisecting his left eyebrow that she did not ask about because she already knew the answer would have a body attached to it somewhere.

"You've done well here," he said, gesturing at the room, the guild beyond it, the whole improbable edifice. "Father always said you'd end up running something. He said you had the head for it and the rest of us had the backs for carrying you."

"He said that about everyone who could add."

"No," Dain said quietly. "About you."

Silence stretched between them, filled with rain. She refused to fill it first.

Finally he exhaled and took a folded paper from inside his coat—not a warrant, she registered immediately, not the stiff vellum of the Office's instruments, but common paper, folded twice, the kind of thing a man might carry to a private meeting. He set it on the table between them but did not push it toward her.

"I'm going to say some things," he said, "and I'd ask you to let me finish before you decide what they mean. Can you do that?"

"I can do anything," she said. "You remember that too."

A ghost of a smile touched his mouth and died. "The parish register of Marrowfield, year of your birth, records a caul presented for burning. The priest's notation reads: *the flame bent away from it.*" He said it flatly, without drama, and watched her the way she imagined he watched prisoners—not for guilt but for the shape of the truth underneath whatever they said. "In the Office's taxonomy that notation constitutes a Class Two marker. Sufficient grounds for seizure, examination, and—" he paused, choosing, "—processing."

The word landed in the room like a dropped stone. *Processing.* She thought of the neighbor from Ash Lane, of the cart, of the crowd that had learned to look elsewhere. Her hands stayed very still on the table's edge.

"That was twenty-nine years ago," she said. "In a parish that has since flooded. Records burn in floods."

"They do," Dain agreed. "When someone burns them." He turned the folded paper a quarter-turn with one finger. "Here is what happens next if I do nothing. Within the month, my superiors will conduct their own audit of this guild—they're already asking why I requested a courtesy inspection of a trading house, and curiosity is not a quality they reward. They will find the same parish record I found. They will not have grown up in the company. They will not have shared bread with you. They will come with writs and irons and a list of questions you cannot answer safely, and they will start with you because you touch every book in this building."

"And you're telling me this out of the goodness of your office."

"I'm telling you this," he said, and now something roughened in his voice, something she recognized from a muddy road fifteen years ago, from the night he'd held a dying friend's hand and lied to him about dawn coming, "because there is a version of the next month where everyone in this building lives. I've spent eleven years learning exactly how narrow that version is, and I am offering it to you because I knew you when you were nine years old and you gave me half your supper when mine was confiscated, and I have never once forgotten it."

She let the silence sit. Then: "Say it plainly, Dain. You always hated when Father made us talk in circles. Say what you want."

He pushed the paper toward her. It was a clearance certificate, blank in the particulars, already stamped, already sealed—the Office's own seal in gray wax, the signature block awaiting a name. A clean bill. *Examined and found free of mark or taint.*

"I can enter your name tonight," he said. "Cleared, examined by me personally, documented, filed. The record of the caul becomes a clerical error, corrected. No one revisits a corrected error—it embarrasses the correcting officer, and no one in the Office wants embarrassment. You keep your desk. Your guildmaster keeps his guild. The hunt passes over this house like weather." His eyes held hers. "All I require is the cargo lists."

The words hung there. She made herself look puzzled, and knew even as she did it that puzzlement was wasted on him.

"The current manifests," Dain went on, relentless, gentle, terrible. "Everything moved through this house in the last two years, and especially anything moved in the last sixty days. Names of senders, receivers, routes. You keep the true books—I know you do, everyone in the river trade knows the guild's ledgers never lie, it's practically a religious doctrine among smugglers. Give me the true lists, and I will lose them. Misfile them. A fire in a sub-basement, a clerical catastrophe, pages lost—my career takes a bruise, nothing more. But the names never reach the cull." He leaned forward slightly. "Do you understand what I'm offering? Not mercy for you. Mercy for all of them. Every gifted soul this guild has moved, hidden, sheltered—every name on those lists is a life currently hanging from a thread, and I am the only man in the Office positioned to cut the thread quietly instead of letting the machine pull it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I falsify nothing, I protect no one, and within the month the audit comes, and the audit does not contain me." He straightened. "I can't stop what's coming, Tam. I can only choose what passes through my hands on its way. That's the whole of my power now. It used to be bigger. When we carried steel, a man's choice was simple and visible. Now it's all paperwork, and the paperwork kills more than the steel ever did, and it does it politely."

She looked down at the certificate. Gray wax. Blank name. *Found free of mark or taint.* A document that would declare her a lie and call the lie safety.

"There's a word for this," she said.

"There are several. I've had time to learn all of them." He laughed, short and airless. "Betrayal. Extortion. Mercy. Take your pick; they all fit; none of them is wrong. I'm not here to feel righteous, Tam. I'm here because the alternative to me is a woman named Hesk with a warrant rack and a fondness for thumb-screws, and I have watched her work, and I will not put you in front of her while I still have hands to stand between."

"Why?" It came out of her before she could weigh it. "Truly. Not the bread. Not the childhood. People outgrow debts. Why spend your career—your neck—on this?"

For a moment the carved face cracked, and underneath it she saw him at fourteen, kneeling in the rain, holding a man's hand and lying about the light.

"Because I read the file," he said, "and I sat in my office all night, and I realized that if I do nothing—if I simply let procedure carry me along, the way it carries all of us—I will be the man who fed you to the fire. And I have sworn oaths to the crown, Tamsin. I have kept every one of them for eleven years. But I swore something else first, before any of it, back when we were children and didn't know oaths could be weapons. We cut our palms, all of us, the whole company. *No blade of ours turns on a shield-sister. No lie of ours lands on a shield-sister.* Do you remember?"

The token hummed against her sternum. She remembered. She remembered the mud, the blood, Wren-small herself with her hand in Dain's, the captain's voice making it solemn. She had thought that oath dead for years—oaths die, she'd learned that early—but it sat in her chest now, warm and awful, like an ember raked open.

"It's the only oath I ever kept without cost," Dain said. "I'd like to keep it a little longer. Help me keep it. Give me the lists."

* * *

She stood afterward in the ledger room with the door barred and the lamps unlit, because she could not bear lamplight just then, and laid both hands flat on the closed covers of the current manifest.

The true lists. Sixty days of impossible cargo. Three names she knew were gift-bearers, moved under forged provenance, sleeping tonight in safehouses because her arithmetic had gotten them past the tollgates. Wren's name was not on any list—she'd kept the girl off-paper entirely—but the routes were, and routes pointed, and pointing was enough for men like Hesk.

And Dain would misfile them. She believed that. That was the horror of it: she believed him, wholly, the way she had believed him at fourteen. He would burn his own integrity to keep those names from the fire, and it would even work, probably, for a while.

But the lists would exist in his hands. And hands passed things along—under orders, under torture, under the slow erosion of a career taking bruises. And somewhere below the city, behind bars made of words, something her mother's token had begun to sing to her about was waiting for the truth to walk out of hiding. She had pages in her strongbox proving the silent god was no god at all but a prisoner, that the crown's peace was built on a king's crime renewed every generation, that every charter, every oath—including the one Dain had just invoked, including the one she'd sworn with her own cut palm—might be a chain-link wearing the costume of loyalty.

If she gave Dain the lists, she bought the guild a month and fed the machine that built the Hollow.

If she refused, she gambled every life under this roof on her ability to outrun an audit she could not see.

Loyalty pulled one way. Truth pulled the other, and the token hummed between her breasts like a second pulse, and outside the rain kept falling on a city full of names.

Dain had asked her to help him keep an oath. She stood in the dark with her hands on the books and understood, at last, with total clarity, that she could not give him the lists—

—and that she could not, either, let him walk out of the guild unprotected, believing her silent compliance was purchased with a blank certificate.

There was a third thing. There was always a third thing; her mother had taught her that every false choice hid a real one underneath, the way a ledger hides theft between honest columns. But the third thing required the partners. It required the truth she had promised herself she would speak.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow I show them the pages.

Above her, muffled by floors and rain, she heard the front doors close behind Dain Corvasser, and the sound of them shutting was very like the sound of a cell.
