## 9. The Partners Vote

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its most important meetings in the counting room, because Oren Vael believed that any decision worth making should be made within sight of the numbers it would cost.

Tamsin had always loved the room for its order: the long table of blackened oak, scarred by a century of rings and knife-points; the wall of pigeonholes where every partner's seal hung on its hook like a sleeping bird; the high narrow windows that let in the grey light off Coinmaker's Row and nothing else. No tapestries, no trophies. Just the ledgers, ranked along the shelves like soldiers at rest, their spines stamped in gold that had faded to the color of old honey.

Tonight she stood before those shelves with a satchel against her ribs, and the weight of it was nothing—fourteen pages, copied fair in her own hand—and everything.

Seven partners were present. That was as many as could be gathered without rumor. Selke Marrow sat nearest the door, as she always did, her hands folded on the table's edge, her face composed into the particular patience of a woman who had raised four children through two plagues and a famine and no longer wasted expression on anything short of catastrophe. Beside her, the twins Ferro and Anselm Quill shared a single chair between them out of lifelong habit, though there were eleven other chairs empty in the room. Old Master Hobb had come up from the docks with salt still crusted in his beard. Yeva Solenne, who ran the guild's quiet trade in provenance and papers, sat with her back very straight, which for Yeva meant she was frightened. And at the head of the table, Oren Vael waited with his one good hand flat on the wood and his ruined one hidden beneath his cloak, the way he always hid it, as if an old wound were a debt he refused to let anyone else see on the books.

"Close the door, Tamsin," he said.

She closed it. The latch clicked, small and final, and she thought: *that is the sound of no way back.*

"You've asked for the floor," Oren went on. "That's not nothing. In thirty years I can count the times a keeper has called a full partners' session on her own authority, and I remember what each of them cost us." His eyes, pale as river-washed stone, held hers. "So before you open your mouth, understand what you're asking us to hear."

"I do understand." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "I'm asking you to hear something that will make every one of us a criminal under crown law. I've weighed that. I've written out the weights." She touched the satchel. "I keep accounts, Master Vael. This is one."

Selke made a soft sound—not quite a laugh. "Then read it, girl. Some of us have cargo to sign for in the morning."

Tamsin took out the pages and laid them on the oak, one by one, in the order she'd spent three sleepless nights arranging them. Fourteen sheets. Each copied exactly from ledgers older than the guild itself, entries in six different hands across a hundred and forty years, all of them coded in the cipher her mother had taught her without ever saying she was teaching it—the cipher of the Thornbury line, passed down ledger-keeper to ledger-keeper like a candle shielded inside a coat.

"This," she said, touching the first page, "is a payment record. Dated in the reign of Aldric the First. It reads, in plain figures, as a drainage contract—clearing the Hollow beneath the palace hill after the god-war, standard municipal work. But the cipher layer underneath says otherwise." She turned the page. "It says: *first payment to the wardens of the seal. The voice is bound but not ended. Keep the Hollow shut.*"

She let the silence sit. Rain had begun against the high windows, soft as fingers.

"The second entry is Aldric's son. Same contract, renewed annually. Third generation, same. For a hundred and forty years the crown has paid—through cutouts, through shell companies, through guilds like ours, gods forgive us—to maintain a prison beneath the capital. Not to bury a dead god. To keep a living one from speaking."

Yeva's breath caught audibly. Hobb's knuckles whitened around his pipe, unlit, forgotten.

"And this last page—" Tamsin's finger hovered over it. "This is the payment our own guild received eleven years ago. Before my time as keeper. Signed for by Master Vael's predecessor. We have been on the crown's list of wardens for over a decade. We are already part of this. We simply were never told what we were being paid for."

"That's a lie." Ferro Quill said it fast, too fast, and his brother put a hand on his arm. "Our ledgers don't lie. You've said so yourself a hundred times, Thornbury. If we'd taken crown money for something like that, it would be in the books in plain ink."

"It *is* in the books in plain ink," Tamsin said quietly. "As a drainage contract. The cipher tells you what the drainage was." She looked down the table. "I verified it three ways. The cipher matches the hand of every warden-payment across five reigns. And the cipher itself—" she hesitated, and the hesitation cost her something, "—the cipher is my mother's. It's been in her family longer than the guild has existed. Which means the people who built this code knew, generation after generation, and wrote it down where only other keepers could read it. They wanted it found. Eventually. By someone like me."

She had not meant to say the last part. It left her feeling flayed, standing in front of seven people with her inheritance spread out on the table like contraband—which, under the new proclamation, it was.

Oren Vael had not moved. Now he leaned forward, slowly, and drew the last page toward himself, reading it with his lips moving slightly, the way he read anything that mattered.

*Keep the Hollow shut.*

"The witch-hunt," he said at last. "Corvasser's commission. You think it's connected."

"I think the crown doesn't hunt gift-bearers because they fear us," Tamsin said. "I think they hunt us because we're evidence. Every gifted child born is proof the god isn't dead, and every year the prison holds, the lie gets more expensive. They're not burning witches, Master Vael. They're burning witnesses."

The rain thickened. Somewhere below, in the warehouse, a crate shifted and settled, and everyone in the room glanced toward the door at once—seven people who had spent the last month listening for boots on stairs.

Selke Marrow broke the silence first, as Tamsin had known she would.

"Burn them."

She said it without heat, the way she'd say *raise the toll* or *refuse the shipment*. She unfolded her hands and laid them flat.

"Burn the copies, burn the originals, burn whatever's left, and then burn the memory of them. Tamsin, I say this with respect: you've brought a lit lamp into a powder store and you're asking us to admire the light. Corvasser is already sniffing at our gates. He inspected us himself—he dined at our table, he smiled at us. Do you imagine a man like that doesn't know something's in our archives? He may not know *what*. The moment anyone outside this room learns we know, we stop being a guild and become a target. And not just us." Her voice roughened, just barely. "I have children. Two of them still sleep under my roof. You're asking me to vote for their deaths on the strength of fourteen pages of dead men's handwriting."

"I'm asking you to vote on the strength of the truth—"

"The truth!" Selke's composure cracked, just once, showing the fury beneath like firelight through a shutter. "The truth got Marta Ellery dragged out of her own kitchen last week while her neighbors watched through the windows. The truth is what they write on the warrants. Don't preach truth to me in a city that's arresting children for glowing."

"Marta Ellery is why we must—" began Anselm Quill.

"Marta Ellery is why we must be *invisible*—"

"Enough." Oren didn't raise his voice. He never needed to. "One at a time. Hobb. You've been silent longest, which means you've been thinking hardest. Speak."

Old Hobb turned his unlit pipe over in his fingers. When he spoke, it was slow, dredged up from somewhere deep.

"I carried cargo through the god-war's last years. Before your time, most of you. Before even yours, Vael." He nodded at Tamsin. "I saw what the cities looked like when the divine and mortal thrones were fighting openly. Ash to the second story. Rivers you couldn't drink for a generation. I swore after that I'd never again take sides in a war between things bigger than ships." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and hard at once. "But I'll tell you what else I saw. I saw what the first kings did to win it. And if what these pages say is true—if they didn't kill their god but chained him and have been feeding on the silence ever since—then every bad thing since has grown in that shadow. Every tax, every purge, every warrant. Root and branch, all of it, growing out of the Hollow." He set the pipe down with a click. "I'm too old to fight. But I'm not too old to be ashamed. I won't vote to burn it. I'll abstain before that."

"Abstention is cowardice dressed as neutrality," Selke said sharply.

"Abstention is an honest number in the column," Hobb answered. "You'd know that if you'd ever kept real books."

Yeva Solenne spoke next, her voice thin but level. "There's a middle road. There's always a middle road—that's the entire business of this guild. We don't burn the evidence and we don't use it. We move it. Quietly. Out of the archive, out of the city, into trusted hands beyond the crown's reach. If the day comes when it's needed, it exists somewhere Corvasser can't touch. Until then, we've endangered no one."

"And who are these trusted hands?" Selke demanded.

"Us. A handful. Compartmented. No one knows the whole route except—"

"—except whoever carries it," Tamsin finished softly. "Which makes that person the most hunted individual in the capital. Yeva, I appreciate the craft of it, but you're describing a dead-drop for a god. Sooner or later someone follows the courier home."

"Then it should be you," Ferro Quill burst out. He was on his feet now, and his twin rose with him, mirror-image, both lean and dark and twenty-six and furious. "You found it. You read it. You're already marked—don't look surprised, half this table has guessed about you for years, we just had the decency not to say it." Tamsin felt the floor tilt gently beneath her, the sensation of a long-concealed ledger opening in public. Ferro pressed on. "Use it, I mean. *Use* it. Copy it a hundred times. Send it to every gift-cell in the city, every sympathetic magistrate, every foreign envoy with a grudge against Aldric's throne. Make it *known*. The crown's power is the lie that the god chose silence. Shatter the lie and the warrants lose their sanctity. Half the guard will desert rather than enforce a prison built on perjury."

"With respect," said Selke icily, "half the guard will also arrest every courier by morning, and the other half will burn the guildhouse for the reward."

"Then we do it before they can—"

"Gentlemen." Oren's hand came down on the table, not hard, but the oak rang anyway. "Sit."

They sat. And the room turned, all of them, to the head of the table, to the old man with the hidden hand, and Tamsin realized she was holding her breath.

Oren Vael looked at the fourteen pages for a long time. Rain traced silver lines down the windows. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and even and terribly tired.

"I signed for that payment," he said. "Eleven years ago. My predecessor handed me the book and told me it was legacy revenue—a contract inherited from the old masters, paid punctual as sunrise, requiring nothing but our continued discretion and our continued existence. I questioned it once. I wrote to the crown's steward asking for the scope of works." Something moved behind his eyes, some old cold memory. "Three days later, two men from the palace visited my home. They were courteous. They admired my garden. One of them picked up my daughter's toy horse from the step and asked me, very kindly, whether I knew how quickly a man's whole household could be entered into *other kinds* of ledgers. Then they left. And I countersigned the next payment, and the next, and I told myself I was keeping my partners safe, and I let myself believe it, right up until tonight." He lifted his gaze to Tamsin. "You've done in a month what I lacked the spine to do in eleven years. Remember that I know that, whatever I say next."

"Master Vael—"

"I say we burn it." He said it plainly, without apology. "Not because it's false. Because it's true, and truth of this size kills everyone who touches it. Selke is right, though it pains me to align with her arithmetic. We are forty-three souls under this roof, plus dependents, plus the sixty families whose livelihoods ride on our charters. Against that: fourteen pages. Even if every word is true—even if the god himself rose up and confirmed it in a voice of thunder—we cannot *afford* it. That is not cowardice. That is accounting." He folded the last page closed. "A guild that dies for the truth leaves no one alive to tell it. Burn the copies. Seal the originals in the deep vault under triple lock, untouched, unmentioned, until such a day as the world changes on its own. That is my motion."

"And mine," said Selke.

"And ours," said the Quill twins together, "for the opposite. Full disclosure. Wide distribution."

Hobb grunted: "Abstain."

Yeva looked down at her hands. "Move-and-seal," she said. "My motion stands."

Four positions. Seven voters. Tamsin stood at the foot of the table with her heart going like a hammer in a locked room, and understood suddenly, viscerally, what she had done: she had taken the secret that had lived safely in her skull for ten years and nailed it to the guild's door, and now it belonged to everyone, and everyone would bleed for it differently.

"All those in favor of burning the copies and sealing the originals," Oren said. "Vael. Marrow. Against: Quill, Quill. Abstaining: Hobb. Solenne moves separately." He paused. "Thornbury. You called the session. You get a vote like anyone. How do you cast it?"

Every eye came to her. Selke's, weary and defiant. The twins', bright with borrowed courage. Hobb's, sorrowful. Yeva's, apologetic. Oren's, steady as a fixed star, giving her nothing and everything.

Tamsin looked at the fourteen pages. Her mother's cipher. Her mother's candle, shielded inside a coat, passed hand to hand down a hundred years of darkness by women and men who had never once been safe and had never once stopped writing. *They wanted it found,* she had said, and it was true, and it was also true that every one of those keepers had probably stood in a room like this one, before some equivalent of this table, and heard reasonable voices explain the arithmetic of survival.

"We are the Guild of Impossible Things," she said. Her voice shook and she let it. "Not the guild of profitable things, or safe things, or survivable things. Our charter says we handle what others won't touch. Well. Here it is." She laid her palm flat on the pages. "I vote to use it. Narrowly, carefully, Yeva's way if it comes to that—but it does not get burned, and it does not get buried alive in a vault to rot. I didn't dig my mother's grave deeper so we could throw this in after her."

"So recorded," Oren said quietly. "Motion to burn and seal: two. Motion to distribute: two. Motion to move and conceal: one. Abstention: one. Keeper's vote: against burning." He exhaled, long and slow, and Tamsin watched him do the sum and watched the sum come out wrong, split clean down the middle, a guild divided against itself for the first time in living memory. "No majority. Under charter, a split vote on matters of survival goes to... contingency custody. The matter passes to the keeper who raised it, who bears sole responsibility for its disposition, and sole liability for its consequences." His pale eyes met hers, and in them she saw, unmistakably, both mercy and sentence. "Congratulations, Keeper Thornbury. The guild cannot decide. Therefore the guild decides nothing. Therefore *you* decide everything. And if Corvasser comes through that door, it will be your name on the deed, not ours."

The rain hammered. Selke was already rising, gathering her shawl, her face a closed account. The twins argued in whispers. Hobb patted Tamsin's shoulder once, heavy as a mooring line, and said nothing.

Only Yeva lingered, and only long enough to murmur, close to Tamsin's ear: "Whatever you choose—choose fast. There's a rider at the sign of the Gilded Chain asking after our archive hours. Been there two days. Pays in new-minted crown silver."

Tamsin went cold. "New-minted?"

"Minted since the proclamation." Yeva's mouth was grim. "Someone's funding questions about us, and recently. Think on that tonight, keeper. Along with everything else."

She left. Tamsin stood alone in the counting room with fourteen pages and a decision and the sound of the rain, and far away beneath the city, faint as a heartbeat through stone, she almost fancied she could hear something waiting to learn what she would choose.

* * *

She did not go home. She went down.

Past the counting room, past the strongroom with its iron-banded door, down the spiral stair that smelled of tallow and cold rock, to the deep vault—Oren's contingency, triple-locked, older than the guild's charter. Her lantern threw her shadow huge and wavering on the wet stone. In her pocket, wrapped in wool, the prayer-token hummed against her thigh, patient as a second pulse.

She had voted to use the evidence. She had not yet decided how, or when, or at the cost of whom. But the vault was dry and dark and honest, and honesty, tonight, was the only company she could bear.

She set the lantern on the shelf. She unwrapped the token.

And in the silence of the deep vault, for the first time, it did not merely hum.

It *answered*.
