## 10. Fire in the Ledger Room

The night the archives burned, Tamsin was awake because of a number.

It was a small thing, as such things always were—a discrepancy of three coppers in the quarterly tithe paid to the Lamplighters' Wardens, an entry that had been corrected twice in two different hands, and neither correction quite matched the ink of its neighbor. She had learned long ago that fires started small. Not the kind that burned wood and paper, but the other kind: the ones that started in a margin, in a smudged figure, in a lie told by someone who thought no one would ever read that far.

She sat in the ledger room with a single lamp turned low, cross-referencing the tithe entries against the wardens' receipts, and she did not hear the footsteps on the stairs because there were none. Whoever set the fire had been careful about that too.

The first sign was the smell. Hot pitch, she thought at first—the guild used it to seal crates of impossible cargo, and the storerooms above always reeked of it in damp weather. But this was sharper, sweeter, wrong. Her mother had died in a house fire. Tamsin knew the smell of a burning building the way other people knew the smell of bread.

She lifted her head. The lamp flame leaned sideways, though the windows were shuttered.

Then the smoke came down the stairwell like something poured.

* * *

Later—much later, when she could bear to order the memory into sequence—she would understand how narrow her escape had been. The fire had not started in the ledger room. It had started three floors up, in the archive proper, where the guild kept ninety years of contracts, provenances, and payment records: the paper skeleton of every impossible thing the Guild of Impossible Things had ever done. The arsonist had chosen his hour well. The night watch was thin; Oren Vael was across the city settling a dispute over a bottled storm's delivery terms; and the rain that had fallen all week had finally stopped, leaving the old timber dry as tinder.

But Tamsin was in the building. Tamsin, who could not sleep until the numbers agreed with one another.

She came up the stairs two at a time into heat that hit her face like an open oven door. The main archive hall was already lost—flame moved along the shelving in a bright crawling tide, licking up the spines of ledgers older than the crown itself, and the smoke rolled black and greasy against the ceiling in a slow thunderhead. Somewhere a beam groaned. Glass burst somewhere else, farther off, and she heard shouting now, voices from the yard below, the clatter of the bucket chain being formed.

*Ninety years,* she thought, absurdly, standing in the doorway with heat peeling the tears from her eyes before they could fall. *Ninety years of proof.*

And then, underneath that thought, quieter and colder: *the cipher pages are still here.*

Her mother's cipher. The entries in the dead god's hand—or rather, in the first kings' hands, payments made century after century to "keep the Hollow shut," recorded in the guild's oldest ledgers in a code Tamsin had spent three feverish weeks cracking while Wren slept in the room below and Dain Corvasser walked the city streets looking for exactly what she'd found. She had copied them all, of course. Every deciphered line lived in a locked box under her bed, in her own careful hand.

But copies proved nothing. A copy was a story anyone could have written. What made the truth true was the original—the actual vellum, the actual faded ink, the actual ledgers whose age and provenance no forger could counterfeit. Burn those, and her evidence became a mercenary's word against the crown's. Burn those, and everything she had learned in the dead hours of the night was just so much ash-colored rumor.

The originals were in the restricted case. Third shelf, iron-banded, past the worst of the fire but not past all of it.

Tamsin did not decide to go in. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that frightened her more than the flames: there was no decision, no weighing of her life against the papers. Her body simply went, the way water goes downhill, the way her gift reached out without her permission to touch the nearest burning ledger and read its last moments—the hand that wrote it, the oath it sealed, the fear of the man who'd signed it a hundred years ago—all of it flooding through her in a half-second of terrible intimacy before the page curled and went dark.

She wrapped her cloak around her head and shoulders, dropped low where the air was still breathable, and ran.

* * *

The restricted case was a strongbox built into the wall, guild-made, with a lock that answered only to the master key or to a partner's blood-marked seal. Tamsin had neither. What she had was ten years of keeping these books, and a lock-pick hidden in her boot since she was nineteen, because a woman who hid a god-gift for a decade learned never to trust a door she couldn't open herself.

The iron was hot enough to raise blisters through her gloves. She worked the pick by feel, by sound, counting the tumblers the way she counted figures—*one, two, three, there*—while behind her the fire found the oilcloth covering the map tables and went up with a whump that pressed heat against her back like a hand. Sweat ran into her eyes. The first tumbler gave. The second stuck, then gave. The third—

The third gave just as the shelf above her head collapsed in a fountain of sparks, and she flung herself flat, dragging the case open one-handed, and pulled out the ledgers blind: three of them, heavy as paving stones, their leather already smoking at the corners. She crammed them inside her cloak against her body, felt the heat of them through her shirt like sleeping animals, and crawled for the door as the ceiling began to come apart in earnest.

She made the stairwell. She made the yard. She remembered little of either except the cold after the furnace-heat, and the buckets, and Oren Vael arriving at a run with his coat unbuttoned and his face gray, shouting her name in a voice she had never heard him use—raw, cracked, afraid.

"Tamsin." He caught her by both arms and shook her once, hard. "Are you hurt? Are you—"

"The ledgers." Her voice came out as smoke, rough and ruined. She coughed and tried again. "I got three. The Hollow entries. They're—"

"I don't care about the damned ledgers." He said it fiercely, and then he saw her face, and his grip gentled. "I care about you. Sit down before you fall down."

She sat down on an overturned crate, in the mud, in the rain that had chosen that moment to begin again—fat, indifferent drops hissing on the burning roof—and she held the smoking bundle against her chest and watched ninety years of the guild's history become a red glow behind black smoke.

It should have been worse, she thought numbly. It should have been all of it. If she hadn't been awake. If she hadn't smelled the pitch. If the lock had taken thirty seconds longer.

If someone hadn't wanted exactly the Hollow ledgers destroyed, and nothing else.

* * *

By dawn the fire was dead and the guildhouse stood gutted on its eastern side like a skull with one eye put out. The partners gathered in the counting room—the one room that still had four walls and a roof—and the meeting had none of the order of their usual councils. Selke Marrow arrived with soot on her sleeves and her youngest child's name on her lips, asking over and over whether anyone had seen Wren.

That was when the cold opened in Tamsin's chest, wider than any the fire had made.

"Wren." She stood up so fast the crate tipped behind her. "Where is Wren?"

"She sleeps in the east wing," Selke said. Her voice was carefully level, the way it always was when she was most frightened. "Third floor. Tamsin—the third floor is gone."

The east wing. The third floor. Tamsin's mind threw up the floor plan with horrible clarity: the narrow room under the eaves where they'd housed the girl as "apprentice copyist," the window that looked out over the coal-yard, the stairs that were now a black throat full of fallen timber. She was moving before anyone could stop her, out into the drizzle, around to the yard where the bucket chains still worked, and she seized the nearest man by the collar—he was a porter, half her size, soaked and shaking—and said, "Was anyone brought out of the east wing? Anyone? A girl, twelve years old, brown hair, she stutters when she's scared—"

"No one, mistress. No one come out that side at all."

The world went very quiet and very far away. Tamsin stood in the mud with three priceless ledgers under her arm and understood that she had made a choice in the fire without knowing she was making it—that she had crawled toward the iron case while a child slept above her, and if Wren was dead, then no deciphered cipher in the world would ever balance that account.

"Her gift," she said aloud, to no one, to everyone. "When she's scared, her gift flares. Someone would have seen light. Someone would have—"

"Tamsin." Oren's hand on her shoulder. "Look at the coal-yard wall."

She looked. Above the scorch-line, above the tumbled heap of half-burned crates the porters had thrown against the wall to break the fire's spread, one window hung open. The third-floor window. And on the wet slate below it, half-scraped away by the falling debris, were the marks of small boots sliding down a roof pitch no grown climber would have attempted.

"She got out." Relief hit her like a blow, and then, immediately, its twin: "She got out, and she didn't come back. She didn't come to us. Where does a terrified child go, Oren? Where does she run?"

He didn't answer. Neither of them wanted to say it: *to the bell tower cell, to the underground, to the people who worship the god you've been reading about in secret.* Wren knew things. Children always knew more than the adults guarding them believed, and Wren had ears like a wren indeed, and somewhere in the smoke and screaming she had heard something—or seen something—that had sent her running not toward safety but away from it.

Away from the guild. Away, possibly, from them.

* * *

They searched until midmorning. Tamsin led a party through the coal-yards and the tannery lanes, calling the girl's name until her ruined voice gave out entirely, and it was Selke who finally found the trail—not a trail, really, just a milk-seller who remembered a small girl running barefoot toward Tanner's Bridge an hour before dawn, "crying fit to bust, poor mite, wouldn't stop for nothing."

Barefoot. Toward the river districts. Toward the tower.

Tamsin stood on Tanner's Bridge in the gray rain and looked north at the distant bell tower rising over the rooftops like a raised finger, and she made herself think instead of feel, because feeling was a floodgate she could not afford. *Wren is fast and Wren is clever and Wren has survived worse than this.* The cell would take her in. The cell had been waiting for her, in a way—they all had, the underground gift-bearers with their candle-lit faces and their certainty that the token-bearer would come. At least there she would be among people who understood what flared out of her when she was afraid.

At least there, Tamsin told herself, she would be safe.

The thought rang false even as she thought it, and she examined why, standing in the rain with her hands white-knuckled on the bridge rail, and the answer arrived with the inevitability of a sum finally carried: *because I know what the cell believes, and I know what the crown does to cells like that one, and I know that Dain Corvasser has been pulling threads through this city for weeks and every thread leads to gifted folk and the people who hide them.* The bell tower was not a sanctuary. It was a gathering place, and gathering places could be found, and the fire—

The fire.

She turned it over now, finally, with the cold attention it deserved. The arsonist had known which ledgers mattered. The restricted case held forty volumes; the flames had gone to the Hollow-era shelves first, before spreading anywhere else—she had seen the burn pattern, she had *read* the burn pattern with her cursed gift before she could stop herself, and the fire's earliest appetite had been precise. And the watch had been thinned that night by a forged duty rotation, Oren had discovered at dawn, in a hand that imitated his own clerk's well enough to fool a tired sergeant.

Someone inside. Or someone with access inside. Someone who knew the ledgers, knew the floors, knew the watch schedule, knew which child slept where.

Tamsin stood on the bridge and let herself think the unthinkable, name by name, the way she audited any account: Partner Hesk, who had argued loudest against sheltering cargo that breathed. Partner Ivo, deep in debt to creditors who had Crown connections. Selke Marrow, who had voted to shelter the hunted and wept doing it, and whose family—whose four children—lived in a city where the penalty for a marked child was the pyre, and where a crown pardon could be bought, she'd heard whispered, by those who delivered value.

*Stop,* she told herself. *You're grieving and tired and you're doing what frightened people do, which is arithmetic with lives.* But the thought would not stop. It sat in her chest beside the grief for the archives, beside the terror for Wren, and it weighed exactly what a true entry weighs, which is to say: it weighed what it weighed, regardless of what she wished.

She had saved three ledgers from the fire. She had lost the rest. And now she had to consider that the fire might be only the first payment in a longer transaction—one that would end with the crown's men at the guild door, and a traitor holding the ledger open for them.

Behind her, on the bridge, she heard running feet, and turned to see young Petch from the counting room, breathless, hatless, holding out a folded scrap of paper.

"Mistress Thornbury—this was nailed to the guild's door. Nailed right through the paper. Watchman says a boy left it and ran."

She took it. The paper was cheap, the handwriting hurried, the spelling of a child taught letters recently and urgently:

*i sor who lit it. i am going wher thay cant find me. dont cum. i meen it. — W*

Below the words, drawn clumsily but unmistakably, was a small picture of a bell.

Tamsin read it twice. Then a third time, because the second reading had shown her what the first had missed: not the message, but the paper. Cheap rag-stock, yes—but the fold was a courier's fold, the kind used by houses that sent valuable things, and there, in the lower corner, pressed faintly into the fiber, was a wax residue. Royal scarlet. The same seal-wax smear she'd seen once before, on a client's payment, on the day this whole account began to tip.

Someone had given Wren the paper. Someone had helped the child write her warning—or written it for her—and let her run to the tower, alone, believing herself safe.

Or someone had written it themselves, and Wren's bare feet on the roof slates had been the only true line in the whole letter.

Tamsin closed her fist around the note and looked north again, at the bell tower, at the gathering storm of everything she loved arranged neatly inside a trap she could now see the shape of.

"Petch," she said, her voice a ruin, "find me a horse. And tell no one else. No one."
