## 11. The Traitor's Arithmetic

Selke Marrow had always been good at sums.

It was the first thing her father taught her, before letters, before prayer, before the names of the saints: that everything in the world could be counted, and that what could be counted could be traded, and that what could be traded could be survived. A bushel of barley against a winter's firewood. A daughter's labor against a debt forgiven. She had learned arithmetic the way other children learned lullabies, and it had never once lied to her.

So on the morning after the fire, she sat at her kitchen table with a slate and a stub of chalk and did the only honest thing left to do.

She counted.

Her husband, Aldous, slept badly in the next room. She could hear him turning, the bedsprings murmuring their small complaints. Her daughters—Miri, seven, and little Annet, four—slept the way children sleep after smoke has frightened them, tangled together like kittens, breathing in the shallow rhythm of dreams that smelled of ash even here, three streets from the guildhall. The fire had not touched this house. The fire had been paid *not* to touch this house.

That was the first entry on her slate.

*One guild archive, half destroyed.* She wrote it in her careful merchant's hand, though there was no one to read it but herself and whatever god bothered to look down through the smoke-hole in the roof—the silent one, presumably, the dead one, the one who watched everything and said nothing, which was the most useful quality a god could have.

The second entry took longer. Her chalk hovered over the slate while the kettle muttered on the stove and the gray light came up through the frost-ferned window.

What had she bought? Let the ledger speak plainly; a ledger that flattered its keeper was no ledger at all. She had bought two names struck from the Crown's list of the marked. Two exemptions, sealed with the Lord Inquisitor's own countersign, delivered to her by a soft-spoken man with ink-stained cuffs who called himself a clerk of the Office of Purity and drank his tea without sugar. Two names. Miri Marrow. Annet Marrow. Both born under the wrong stars—or rather, both born of a mother whose grandmother had once, in a village so small it appeared on no maps, been seen to make a well run sweet again with nothing but her hands and a song.

That was all it took. That was the whole inheritance. A sweet well, sixty years gone, and now her daughters' names sat in the Crown's rolls beside descriptions written by neighbors and gossips and men who owed them money: *bloodline suspect, gift likely latent, recommend watch.*

Selke had spent eleven years as a partner of the Guild of Impossible Things. She knew how the world priced impossibility. She had brokered the bottled storm; she had negotiated the terms for the borrowed year, which had required more actuarial imagination than anything else in her career. She understood better than anyone alive that the guild's whole business rested on one premise: that some things were worth any cost, if you counted rightly.

And so she counted.

The guild had ninety-one souls on its current rolls, counting partners, factors, and the hired muscle who guarded the vaults. Of those, perhaps thirty would be implicated if the Crown traced the sheltered cargo back through the ledgers. Thirty arrests, thirty trials—well, no trials, really; the trials had been discontinued as an economy measure. Thirty deaths, or thirty disappearances into the Office of Purity's cellars, which amounted to the same figure entered twice.

Against that: two girls asleep in the next room.

Thirty against two. Any fool could see which column won.

Except—and here was where her father's lessons failed her, here was where arithmetic turned in her hands like a knife—it was not thirty strangers against two daughters. It was Oren Vael, who had taken her on when every other house laughed her out of their counting rooms because she was a woman and a provincial and her accent made clerks smirk. It was Tamsin Thornbury, whose ledgers were the closest thing to holy writ Selke had ever believed in, who had sat across from her a hundred nights balancing books neither of them would show the tax assessors, who trusted numbers the way saints trusted prayer. It was Wren—the child, gods, *the child*, with her burned fingers and her terrible quiet—who had fallen asleep in Selke's own office chair twice during the fortnight before the fire, and whom Selke had covered with her own coat both times.

She looked at her slate. The columns would not resolve. She added them three times and got three different answers, which had never happened to her before, and she understood suddenly that this was what it meant to be damned—not fire, not brimstone, just a sum that refused to come out even.

A knock at the door.

Not loud. Not the Crown's knock, which was a kind of battering even when polite. This was a single knuckle, twice, apologetic. Aldous stirred. Selke wiped the slate with her sleeve, though there was nothing on it anyone could hang her with—a few figures, a few entries, the private liturgy of a traitor—and went to answer it.

The street outside was white with frost. The smoke still hung over the guild quarter like a curtain someone had forgotten to take down, and the smell of wet char rode the wind. On her doorstep stood a boy of maybe twelve, guild-liveried, one eye swollen shut and his lip split, holding out a folded paper as if it might bite him.

"Mistress Marrow." His voice cracked. "Mistress Thornbury says—you're to come."

"Come where?"

He swallowed. Blood showed at the corner of his mouth when he spoke. "The counting room. What's left of it." He hesitated, and then, in a smaller voice: "She knows, mistress. She said—I'm to tell you she knows, and you're to come anyway."

Selke looked at the boy's ruined face and thought: *they caught him running my errand, or someone's errand, or he was simply standing too close when the truth came out.* She thought: *one more entry.* And then she went inside and told her sleeping husband a lie about guild business, kissed each daughter's forehead—Annet's hair smelled of woodsmoke that wasn't hers—and walked out into the frozen morning to pay what she owed.

* * *

The guildhouse wore its wound openly.

Half the archive wing was open to the sky, rafters jutting up like the ribs of something slaughtered, black beams dripping meltwater onto the frost. Fire-guild men picked through the wreckage with the bored competence of people who did this weekly in a city built mostly of timber. The counting room itself had survived—Tamsin would have insisted on that, Selke thought; the woman probably stood in the doorway throwing sand—but its windows were broken and shuttered with planks, and the light inside came down gray and slanted, full of floating dust that might have been paper ash.

Tamsin Thornbury sat behind the great table with the ledgers stacked around her like fortifications.

Selke had prepared herself for many things on the walk over: fury, weeping, accusation, even the cold procedural horror of being arrested on the spot. What she had not prepared for was the sight of Tamsin simply *working*. Quill moving. Columns marching. As though the world had not ended, as though the fire were merely an expense to be recorded.

"Tamsin," Selke said.

"Don't sit down." Tamsin didn't look up. Her voice was level, which was worse than shouting; Selke had heard this voice exactly twice before, both times directed at merchants who had tried to falsify provenance, and both times the merchant had left the room looking like a man who'd been weighed and found short. "Stand there. I'll be finished with this entry in a moment, and then we're going to talk, and I want us both to remember exactly where everything is before we start."

The quill scratched. Somewhere above them, water dripped off a rafter into a bucket with a sound like a slow clock.

"You had Joss beaten," Selke said quietly. "The boy."

"I had Joss *questioned*." Now Tamsin looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, which meant she had done her weeping already, privately, the way she did everything. "By people who stopped when he told the truth. Which is more courtesy than the Crown shows, and more than I expected to feel like giving, so count yourself lucky I've any left." She set down the quill. She aligned it with the table's edge, precisely, the way she aligned everything. "Sit down, Selke. No—stand. Stand. I can't think straight when you're doing that thing with your face, the penitent thing, the thing you do to creditors."

"What thing am I doing with my face?"

"The face of a woman doing sums." Tamsin pushed a single sheet of paper across the table. "Because that's all this was to you, wasn't it? Not malice. Arithmetic."

Selke looked down. It was a copy of a delivery manifest, dated nine days past. Her own countersignature sat at the bottom in her neat hand, authorizing a cart of "salvaged parchment stock" to leave the guild vaults after hours—parchment that had never come back, that had instead, according to the Office of Purity's own records which Tamsin had somehow obtained, been delivered to the inquisitors along with a map of the archive wing's load-bearing walls and a note on which cistern fed which cellar.

"You used my own system against me," Selke said. "Double-entry. Every payment leaves a trace somewhere."

"You taught me that." Something moved behind Tamsin's eyes—not quite a smile, nothing near forgiveness. "Eleven years ago. My second week. You said, *girl, there's no such thing as a clean transaction, there are only transactions whose traces haven't been found yet.* I have thought about that sentence every day for a week."

"How much do you know?"

"All of it. Or enough." Tamsin stood, finally, and came around the table, and Selke saw that she was moving carefully, like a woman with an injury—grief as a physical wound. "Two exemptions. Sealed, countersigned, registered. Names of Miri and Annet Marrow, struck from the marked rolls as of eight days ago. Paid for with the archive, the cargo lists, and the location of our shelter houses—which is why Dain Corvasser's men hit the safehouse on Candle Lane yesterday morning, and why the Ferro family is now in the Office's cellars." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, and quieter was infinitely worse. "The Ferros. Their son is fourteen, Selke. He made flowers grow out of cracks in cobblestones. That was his whole crime."

Selke's knees felt strange. She found the edge of the table and held it.

"I know," she said. "I know what I did."

"Do you?" For the first time, heat. Tamsin stepped closer, and ten years of partnership collapsed between them until they were two women breathing the same ash-thick air. "Because here's what I keep coming back to, lying awake in what's left of my rooms. I keep thinking: she sat at Oren's table. She voted with us. She helped draft the shelter protocols—*you drafted the rationing schedules,* Selke, your handwriting is on the plans for keeping these people alive, and then you sold the same plan to the men killing them. So either you never believed any of it, or—" and here Tamsin's voice did break, slightly, one crack in the masonry, "—or you believed all of it, right up until the moment it asked you to pay."

"That's exactly it." Selke heard her own voice go flat and clear, the ledger voice, because it was the only voice she had left. "It asked me to pay. Everything asks. That's the whole world, Tamsin—everything is a price, and the only question is who receives the bill. The Crown sent me a bill. Do you know how it arrived? Not with soldiers. With a clerk. A tired little clerk who drank tea in my parlor and told me, very kindly, that my elder daughter's name was on a list, and that lists could be edited, and that the Office admired the guild's work and regretted the necessity of its duties. He said *regretted the necessity.* They've learned to say sorry, Tamsin. They've learned it beautifully."

"And Miri. Is she marked? Truly?"

"She's seven." Selke laughed, and hated the sound of it. "She makes the bread rise double when she helps bake. She hums to the hens and they lay in winter. Last month she put her hands on Annet's fever for a night and by morning it was gone, and I sat in the kitchen telling myself it was a strong constitution. That's how it starts. That's how it started for everyone on those lists. You don't get a proclamation. You get a child who is *slightly impossible*, and then you spend years praying she'll grow out of it, and then the criers come." She pressed her palms flat against the table, against the old scarred oak that had held every account she'd ever kept. "I ran the numbers. Ninety-one souls against my two. I ran them a hundred times and they always come out the same way, and I hate the answer, and I would run them a hundred more and they would *still* come out the same way, because that is what I am. I am the woman the sum makes sense to."

For a long moment Tamsin said nothing. Outside, the fire-guild men shouted something about a collapsed wall; a beam groaned and settled.

"And Wren?" Tamsin said at last, very softly. "Where does the child fall in your arithmetic? Because she's missing, Selke. Since the night of the fire. And the only person who knew which cellar she was moved to besides me and Oren was the person who drew the map."

The words landed like ice water. Selke stared at her. "I never gave them Wren. I swear it on my daughters—I never knew where you'd put her. You told no one. You said—" and then she stopped, because she remembered, she remembered perfectly, Tamsin in this very room saying *the fewer who hold a secret the heavier it gets per pair of hands.*

"I know what I said." Tamsin's face had changed. The anger was still there, banked now, crowded aside by something sharper. "Which means either Corvasser's people found her themselves, or—" 

"Or she ran." Selke pushed off the table, mind racing now along its old grooves, fast and clean. "Fire, soldiers, a child who's been smuggled and hidden and told for a month that discovery means death. Children bolt. Where would she bolt *to*?"

Tamsin was already pulling papers toward her, flipping through the salvage they'd pulled from the burned wing, and Selke saw it happen—the moment the partner became the traitor became, impossibly, the colleague again, because grief and fury could coexist with usefulness in a heart that had spent a lifetime counting. It was obscene. It was human. She felt Tamsin understand it in the same instant, and watched her hate that she understood.

"Bell towers," Tamsin murmured, not looking up. "Old shrines. Places with bells—she flinched from bells, she told me, they ring true and she doesn't." Her finger stopped on a scrap of scorched vellum, an inventory of deconsecrated properties the guild had once held liens against. "Saint Vellum's. Deconsecrated forty years. Tower intact. We hold the deed."

"You're going after her." It wasn't a question. "Into a tower, alone, with Corvasser's net already drawn over this quarter."

"With you," Tamsin said. She looked up, and her eyes were terrible and steady. "Because you're going to help me find her, Selke, and help me move her, and then you're going to decide—with your beautiful arithmetic—what your exemption is worth when it was purchased with a child's hiding place. That bill hasn't come due yet. But it will. And when it does, I want you standing in front of me when you pay it."

Selke nodded. There was nothing else in her throat.

Outside, over the smoking ruin of the guild they had built together, the bells of Saint Vellum's began—far off, faint, impossible—to ring.
