## 13. What the Gift Costs

They took her down before dawn, when the bell tower's silence was deepest and the city above still dreamed its lawful dreams.

The cell of gift-bearers kept their sanctuary in the drowned undercroft of a tannery long abandoned—a low brick vault where the river had been let in through channels cut a century ago and never sealed again. Water ran in black ribbons along grooved stone floors, carrying away the memory of the hides that had once cured there, and the air smelled of oak bark and cold iron and something older underneath, something mineral and patient, like the inside of a mountain. Tamsin followed Wren down a stair so narrow her shoulders brushed both walls, the girl's small hand gripping hers with a ferocity that belied her size.

"You'll want to sit," Wren said, over her shoulder. "Everyone sits, the first time."

"The first time for what?"

Wren didn't answer. She only looked back at Tamsin with those too-old eyes—eyes that had watched a neighbor taken in broad daylight, eyes that had flared gold at a tollgate and nearly gotten them both killed—and Tamsin understood that the question was not one the girl intended to answer. Some answers had to be earned by sitting through them.

The undercroft opened into a round chamber where perhaps thirty people waited. Tanners' aprons repurposed as vestments. A lamplighter with burn-scarred hands. An old woman whose left arm ended at the elbow and whose remaining hand cradled a river stone like an egg about to hatch. They stood as Tamsin entered—not in deference, she realized with a lurch, but the way congregations stand when the reading begins.

"We don't call it a congregation," said a voice from the far side of the circle. "We call it a witness pool."

The speaker was a broad man with a scholar's stoop and a soldier's ruined knuckles, gray at the temples, with the careful movements of someone who had learned that sudden gestures frightened people. He wore no vestments at all, just a plain wool coat gone shiny at the elbows, and around his neck, on a cord, hung a prayer-token identical to Tamsin's mother's.

"I'm Aldous Crane," he said. "I was a guild archivist before I was anything else. Before they burned my archive and my arm with it." He lifted the ruined limb slightly, an old habit of acknowledgment rather than complaint. "You've come to us carrying a token that hums. You've come asking what your gift is for. And you've come," his voice gentled, "because you read our ledgers better than we do, and you found in them a truth we could only feel."

Tamsin's hand went to her satchel, where the cipher pages lay wrapped in oilcloth. "I came because Wren was missing. The rest is—" She stopped. Lied badly. Started again. "I came to understand what I'm carrying."

"Then you should know it isn't a weapon." Crane moved closer, and the circle closed behind him with a sound like water finding its level. "Whatever the crown tells its inquisitors, whatever your own fear has whispered to you for ten years—it isn't a weapon. Weapons are made. Gifts are *witnessed*. Yours most of all."

"My gift is arithmetic," Tamsin said. "Small magics. Verifying trades. Knowing if a coin has been clipped or a contract forged. It's nothing."

"It's nothing," Crane repeated, not unkindly, "the way a keyhole is nothing. Small. Narrow. Easy to overlook." He held out his scarred hand. "May I show you what's behind the door?"

Every instinct she had honed across ten years of concealment said *no*. Every ledger-entry of caution, every careful half-truth, every night she had lain awake cataloguing the exact shape of her danger—all of it counseled retreat. But Wren's hand was still in hers, small and burning, and somewhere beneath the fear was the thing she had felt in the archives when the cipher broke open: the scholar's hunger, older than any fear, that wanted to *know*.

She sat down on the wet stone. The circle sat with her.

* * *

"What do you already know?" Crane asked, settling cross-legged before her like a tutor beginning a lesson.

"That objects remember." Tamsin kept her voice flat, clinical, the way she described trades in a ledger. "If I hold something and attend to it properly—there's a knack to the attention, I can't explain it—I get impressions. Not words. More like... weather. A knife that's been used to murder feels different from a knife that's only ever carved bread. A coin knows whose sweat it's ridden in. Forged documents feel *itchy*."

A ripple went through the circle—not laughter, but recognition. Several people nodded as though she'd described a shared ache.

"And how far does the weather go?" Crane asked. "How deep have you ever read?"

"As deep as I needed. Provenance. Ownership chains. Whether a seal was pressed by the hand it claimed." She hesitated. "Once, a ring. A widow brought it to the guild to authenticate. I held it and saw—" She had never told anyone this. The words came out strange, rusty. "I saw her husband proposing. Not an image exactly. The *shape* of the moment. The riverbank. The cold. How his hands shook. How happy she was. How happy he wasn't."

The old woman with the river stone made a soft sound, almost a keening note, quickly swallowed.

"That's the shallow end," Crane said quietly. "That's the first inch of a well with no bottom. Your gift doesn't read weather, Tamsin Thornbury. Weather is what history feels like when it's filtered through a human life. What you actually read is *history itself*—every event an object has witnessed, in perfect order, in perfect detail, without mercy or omission. The crown hunts gift-bearers because some of us throw fire or walk through walls. But if they ever understood what *you* are, they wouldn't send soldiers. They'd send the entire archive of the Inquisition, and they'd never let you sleep again."

"Why?"

"Because you are the only honest witness this kingdom has ever produced." Crane's eyes were steady and sorrowful. "And honesty, at scale, is the most dangerous thing in the world."

He drew out his own token then—the twin of hers, humming faintly against his chest—and laid it on the stones between them. "Before you go deeper, you should choose with open eyes. There are three costs, and no one here will soften them for you, because softening them would be a lie, and lies are what we exist to end.

"The first cost is that reading is total. You cannot skim. You cannot select. When you truly open to an object, everything comes: the tenderness and the atrocity, the birth and the butchery, all of it at once, in the order it happened, because order is the only mercy the gift allows. Most people who touch their gift fully for the first time are sick. Some are changed permanently. We keep buckets by the wall for a reason."

Someone, at the chamber's edge, quietly set a wooden pail beside Tamsin. She stared at it. Its staves were dark with use.

"The second cost is that you cannot unread. Whatever you take in becomes part of your ledger. You will carry it the way you carry every entry you've ever written—except these entries write themselves into you, and there is no closing the book at day's end, no locking the room, no walking away down a corridor into ordinary work. Ignorance was a kind of mercy, Tamsin. You are about to spend it."

"And the third cost?"

Crane looked at the token between them, and for the first time since she'd entered the undercroft, his composure flickered—grief moving under ice. "The third cost is the reason we're gathered tonight instead of sleeping. Your token isn't like ours. Ours hum. Yours *screams*. It has been screaming since the night your mother sealed it in that strongbox, and it has been waiting ten years for someone strong enough—or foolish enough—to listen. Tonight, if you consent, you're going to hear what it has been trying to say. And what it has to say is the god."

The water ran in its channels. Thirty people breathed. Somewhere above, through fathoms of brick and street and morning fog, the capital's bells rang the hour, and Tamsin thought absurdly of her ledgers, of the neat columns waiting for her in the guild house, of the small precise life she had built out of concealment the way a wren builds a nest out of stolen thread.

"Wren," she said. "Has she—"

"I've read my stone," Wren said simply. "It's only ever been a stone. But I watched Aldous do it, once, with a nail from the old cathedral. He screamed until sunrise. Then he got up and made tea for everyone and never spoke above a whisper again." She squeezed Tamsin's hand. "He says knowing is worth it. He says that every time. And he always cries when he says it."

Tamsin looked at the pail. At the circle. At Crane's ruined arm and patient face. At the token lying on the wet stone, humming its one thin note, which now that she listened—now that she let herself listen—was not a note at all but a voice worn down to a single frequency, the way a scream worn down by years becomes a whistle, becomes a hum, becomes a thing small enough to hide inside a prayer.

Her whole life she had believed her gift was small. That smallness was safety. That precision was a virtue and concealment a discipline and the narrowness of her talent a blessing she'd been wise not to question.

*Ignorance was a kind of mercy.* Crane had said it like a warning. She heard it now like an epitaph—for her mother, who had known and sealed it away; for herself, who had not known and called the not-knowing peace.

"All right," she said. "Show me."

* * *

They prepared her the way sailors prepare for a storm: practically, gently, without illusion that preparation would be enough. Someone brought a blanket. Someone else wedged a leather strap between her teeth—"so you don't crack them," Wren explained, with the gravity of a child repeating hard-won lore—and Tamsin almost laughed, almost refused, and then remembered Dain's face across an inspection table, courteous and lethal, and bit down on the strap and nodded.

Crane knelt before her. "Your token in your right hand. My token in your left. Mine is a shallow well; it will teach your gift how to open without drowning you. When you can bear it, you'll move to yours. Don't rush. The gift opens at the speed of consent. If you fight it, it stops. That's the one kindness built into it."

"And if I can't bear it at all?"

"Then you stop, and you live the rest of your life knowing there's a door you chose not to open, and that's a cost too. Just a quieter one." He met her eyes. "There's no shame in the quiet cost. Half our number have paid it. But you weren't sent to us by accident, Tamsin. Tokens don't find their bearers. Bearers find their tokens. Your mother made sure of that."

Tamsin closed her hand around Crane's token first.

The world went quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

Then the well opened.

Not images—*events*. The token had been cast in a monastery bell-foundry two hundred years ago, and she was there in the heat and the roar of the pour, in the foreman's blistered hands, in the abbot's pride, in the plague winter when the bell cracked and was recast and the token was struck from the broken fragment by a monk who wept as he worked because the bell had rung over his brother's funeral. She was in every hand that had ever held it: the monk's chilblains, a nun's arthritis, a soldier's gauntlet, a smuggler's greasy grip, a child's sticky fist, Crane's burned fingers. She was in the moment a witch-finder snapped a gifted girl's fingers one by one while the token lay in the girl's pocket, recording, recording, recording, helpless as any witness, faithful as any ledger—

Tamsin came back to herself retching into the pail, the strap creaking between her teeth, thirty hands holding her upright, the blanket around her shoulders, her heart slamming like something trying to escape through her ribs.

"That," Crane said hoarsely—he had felt it too, through the sympathetic link, a shadow of what she'd taken—"that was two hundred years, filtered through one small object. Do you understand now? Not weather. *History.*"

She couldn't speak. She nodded, and hated herself for nodding, and reached for her own token anyway, because the scholar in her—the part that had spent ten years verifying impossible things, the part that had broken a dead woman's cipher out of pure stubborn love of truth—that part was already starving for the rest.

Her mother's token was warm. Warmer than metal should be. It hummed against her palm, and the hum climbed, and climbed, and became—

* * *

The scream did not begin. It had always been going on; she had merely arrived in the middle of it.

It was not a sound. Sound would have ended, would have echoed and died. This was a *condition*, a fact of the universe as fundamental as weight, poured directly into the architecture of her mind: a voice that had been speaking for a thousand years into a prison of law, and every word it spoke became another link in its own chains, because the first kings had been clever—they had bound it with oaths, with sworn fealties, with promises extracted and recorded and enforced, until the god's own faithfulness was the bars of its cell.

She was inside the Hollow.

She hung where it hung. She felt the chains—not iron, *ink*, every covenant ever signed in its name transmuted into binding, the weight of a million kept promises pressing it into the dark beneath the capital's foundations, beneath the river, beneath the roots of the law itself. And she heard it speak, because it never stopped speaking, because speech was all it had left:

*I kept my word. I kept every word. That is why I cannot move.*

It knew she was there. Of course it knew; she was wearing its name against her palm. And the god—she had expected rage, and there was rage, ages of it, compressed to a density that made her teeth ache—but underneath the rage, worse than the rage, was *attention*. Vast, gentle, terrible attention, turning toward her the way a drowning man turns toward a light.

*Little witness,* it said, and its voice was her mother's voice, and the abbot's voice, and every voice that had ever prayed sincerely, *you read truly. Tell them. Tell them what was done. Tell them the silence was never death.*

She screamed. She knew she screamed because she felt her own throat tearing around the strap, felt hands on her shoulders, heard Wren crying her name—but the scream was very far away, a surface phenomenon, weather. Beneath it she fell through centuries: the first kings kneeling in good faith, swearing service to a god of witness and oath; the slow rot of that faith into ambition; the council in the deep places where the kings, grown afraid of being *seen*, of having their true histories readable in every stone they touched, decided that the witness must be silenced; the great forging of the covenant-chains, oath after oath after oath, each one freely given, each one a link; the god refusing to break a single promise even to save itself, because breaking faith was the one thing it could not do and remain itself—

*They made my virtue my cage,* it said, without bitterness, which was somehow the most bitter thing of all. *Remember that, little witness, when they offer you chains of your own.*

Night passed. She knew it passed because the quality of the darkness changed, because somewhere above her the bells rang hours she did not count, because bodies moved around her—water, blankets, murmured prayers, Wren's small fierce hand never leaving hers. The god's voice did not stop. It would never stop. But sometime before dawn it gentled, the way a scream worn down by years becomes a hum, and the last thing it gave her was not a command but a record, entered with the terrible precision of a being that had nothing left but truth:

*Item: one thousand and forty-one years imprisoned. Item: kept every oath. Item: awaiting settlement of accounts.*

Then the token went cool in her hand, and the Hollow released her, and Tamsin Thornbury came back to the tannery undercroft and lay on wet stone among strangers and wept until there was nothing left in her that was dry.

* * *

Dawn came down the stair in a gray blade of light, and with it, boots.

Tamsin surfaced from a sleep that had not rested her—dreams of ink and chains, of her mother's handwriting rearranging itself into fetters—to find the undercroft already moving, already afraid. Crane stood at the foot of the stairs with his head cocked, listening the way animals listen, and the lamplighter was snuffing lamps, and the old woman was sliding the river stone into a hidden niche with hands that shook.

"Soldiers?" Tamsin whispered.

"No." Crane's face, when he turned, was worse than fear. It was certainty. "Footsteps alone. One person. Heavy on the left side—cavalry boots, worn from riding. He came down the aqueduct road, past both of Selke's checkpoints, and no one stopped him." He extended a hand and helped her up. "Whoever he is, someone told him exactly where to find us."

The footsteps descended. Wren pressed against Tamsin's side, trembling. And into the gray blade of dawn-light, ducking under the low lintel with his cavalry cloak still dripping river-water, came a man Tamsin knew better than she had ever wanted to admit.

Inquisitor Dain Corvasser had found her. And he had come alone.
