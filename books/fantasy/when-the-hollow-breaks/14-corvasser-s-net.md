## 14. Corvasser's Net

Dain Corvasser had rehearsed this moment for eleven years. He had imagined it in the barracks at Vell's Crossing, in the inquisitorial dormitories with their whitewashed walls and their rules nailed to every door, in the long gray hours between executions when sleep would not come and a man had nothing to do but count his sins like coins. In every version of it, Tamsin Thornbury was guilty. That was the whole point of the exercise. You rehearsed until the guilt was real, until the girl from the mercenary company became simply a name on a list, a marked birth, a warrant to be signed.

He had not rehearsed her looking at him *that way*.

She stood in the bell tower's undercroft with river-water still beaded on her cloak and Wren pressed against her side like a burr, and she looked at him across the gray blade of dawn as if he were the one who had been hunted all these years. As if *he* were the one who had come down into the dark.

"You came alone," she said. Not a question. A ledger entry, entered and balanced.

"I did." His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "The cell below—how many?"

"Enough."

"That's not an answer, Tam."

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip-crack on ice. "*Don't* call me that. Not here. Not wearing *that*." Her eyes went to the silver-and-black of his inquisitor's badge, and something in them closed, door by door by door, until what looked back at him was a stranger holding his sister's face like a mask it had borrowed.

He had come alone because he had told himself it was mercy. Because Crane's people would scatter at the first sound of boots and be run down in the streets like rats, and a lone visitor might—might—mean parley. Because somewhere under eleven years of doctrine there was still a boy named Dain who had sworn oaths with mud on his knees beside a sharp-eyed girl who could tell a forged coin by the weight of its lie.

He put his hand on his sword anyway. Habit. Doctrine. Armor.

"Tamsin Thornbury," he said formally, because formality was also armor, "born under the sign of the Quiet God, marked at birth. By order of the Crown's Commission on Divine Contagion, I am required to—"

"Required," she repeated softly. "There's a word I'd have thought you'd be sick of by now."

Behind her, in the shadows of the undercroft, he could hear them moving—the god-touched, the gift-bearers, the contagion itself. A woman murmuring to something in her cupped hands. A boy whose breath fogged even though the cellar air was warm. And the child. The child from the tollgate reports, Wren, small and dark-eyed, staring at him with the flat animal hatred of someone who had already been caged once.

Dain had burned the report of what they'd found in the smuggler's wagon at Marrowgate. He had signed the transfer order for the two survivors to the Commission's cells in the capital, knowing what waited in the Commission's cells. He had done it with a steady hand, and then he had drunk alone until his hand stopped being steady, and in the morning he had written in his private journal, *the law is a wall and we are the mortar*, which was the kind of sentence a man wrote when he was drowning and wanted it to look like swimming.

"Clear the undercroft," he said to Tamsin. "Now, while I'm the only one here. When my writ arrives, it won't arrive alone. There are forty soldiers mustering at the Salt Gate as we speak, and Selke Marrow sold your tower to them with a map."

He watched the name land. Watched her flinch—not at Selke, he realized, but at the number. Forty.

"You're warning us," she said slowly. "The Inquisitor Corvasser is *warning* the contagion."

"The Inquisitor Corvasser is doing arithmetic," he said, and hated how true it was, and hated more that she smiled at it—a thin, terrible smile, the smile of the girl who used to balance the company's books around their campfire and find the errors in his share.

"So am I," said Tamsin Thornbury. "And you won't like my totals."

* * *

Tamsin counted in crisis the way other people prayed: fast, wordless, total.

Forty soldiers at the Salt Gate. Two checkpoints bought by Selke between here and the river. One stair up to the bell tower, one drain out to the drowned aqueduct, both of which Dain plainly knew about or he wouldn't have come down the aqueduct road himself. One exit that mattered, then: the old rope-gallery along the tower's north face, the one the ringer's children used for dares, rotted through in three places and invisible from the street unless you knew where the ivy grew thickest.

One exit, and forty soldiers, and perhaps twenty souls in the undercroft, and one inquisitor standing between her and all of it, wearing the face of her brother.

She made herself look at him properly. Eleven years since Vell's Crossing burned; eleven years since the company broke apart like a dropped plate and everyone walked away carrying different shards. He had been nineteen and righteous even then, Dain, righteous the way some men were tall—it was just how he was built—and she had loved him for it once, before righteousness curdled into uniform.

"You knew I was marked," she said. "You've known since the inspection. Since before. How long, Dain? How long have you known what I am and said nothing?"

His jaw worked. In the dawn-light leaking through the arrow-slit his eyes were the same brown as always, warm brown, campfire brown, and the fact that they were still warm after everything was somehow the cruelest thing in the room.

"Since Marrowgate," he said finally. "The provenance papers on your cargo. Nobody forges a guild seal with a left-handed slant on the wax fold. Only two people alive write left-handed under pressure, and I sat across a fire from one of them for six years." He exhaled. "I told myself I was mistaken for four months. Then I told myself I was gathering evidence. Then the evidence gathered itself, and I was out of lies."

"And now?"

"Now there are forty men coming and one of me, and I am asking you—" his voice dropped, and for a moment the inquisitor fell away entirely and it was only Dain, tired, soaked, afraid, "—Tam. Give me the child and the manifest of names, and walk out the front gate with me. You'll be processed, not condemned. Marked-but-registered. There's a category. I can get you into it. I've *built* categories for less."

"Registered." She tasted the word. "Caged with paperwork."

"Caged with paperwork and *alive*."

Wren's fingers dug into her sleeve. Small, trembling, trusting. Somewhere above them the great cracked bell hung silent in its frame, the bell that hadn't rung in a hundred years, the bell the underground swore would ring again when the Hollow opened. Twenty souls. One rotted rope-gallery. One brother-shaped obstacle with forty soldiers at his back and mercy in his mouth like a coin he wanted her to bite.

Tamsin had spent ten years being no one. She knew precisely how much cover was worth, because she had paid for hers in installments her whole adult life—every name unspoken, every gift smothered, every entry in every ledger carefully, murderously dull.

It was astonishing, really, how cheap it turned out to be. All it cost was everything.

"Dain," she said gently. "Look at me."

He looked.

And she let go.

She had never opened the gift wide. Never past a whisper, never past a fingertip's touch on an object's surface—verifying a bottled storm's vintage, tasting a borrowed year for tampering. What she did now was not a touch. It was a floodgate. She reached out along the undercroft, into the stone, into the *history* of the place, and she took Dain Corvasser's hand in hers without moving a muscle, and she showed him.

The tower bloomed open. A century of it. Ringers' hands on the bell-rope, oil and sweat and laughter. The plague year, when the bell rang day and night and the dead went out the north door. And beneath all of it, older than the tower, older than the kings, threaded through the very mortar: *oaths*. Thousands upon thousands of them, sworn to a voice in the dark, each one a bright filament running down through the city's bones into the deep, and at the bottom of all those filaments—

Dain made a sound like a man taking a blade between the shoulders. His knees buckled. He caught himself on the wall, gasping, and Tamsin released him, shaking, her nose bleeding freely, the taste of iron and starlight in her throat.

"What," he whispered, "what *was*—"

"The truth," she said. "That's all it was. Just the truth, all at once. Now you know why they hunt us. It isn't the power they fear, Dain. It's the reading. We see what things actually are. And your crown is built on a lie with chains on it."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound she had been dreading: boots. Many. On the aqueduct road, and spreading, fanning out toward the tower's base like spilled water finding every crack.

Dain heard it too. She watched him stand there with the whole choice laid bare in his face—duty on one side, dawn on the other—and she watched him understand, with terrible clarity, exactly what she was about to do.

"No," he said. "Tam, don't—"

"Wren," Tamsin said. "Rope-gallery. Now. Everyone. *Move.*"

* * *

Later, Dain would try to reconstruct the next ninety seconds and fail, the way a man fails to reconstruct the moment a bridge breaks.

What he retained were fragments. The undercroft erupting into motion—shadows peeling off walls, the fog-breathed boy scooping up a lame old man as if he weighed nothing. Tamsin hauling the child Wren toward the north wall while blood ran from her nose over her lip and she didn't wipe it. Himself, sword half-drawn, doctrine screaming *arrest them, contain them, the law, the law*—

—and Tamsin turning back, one hand on the sill of the hidden gallery, looking at him across the space between them where eleven years of silence used to live.

"The front stairs," she said. "Stand on them. Count to five hundred. That's all I'm asking. Five hundred, and then do whatever your law tells you."

"If I do that, I'm a traitor."

"If you don't," she said, "you're what they built. I know which one our mother would have mourned."

*I know which one our mother would have mourned.* Their mother—not theirs, never theirs, but the phrase was old mercenary shorthand, the company's mother-tongue, *our mother* meaning the woman who'd fed every orphan in the camp, meaning the standard, the conscience, the thing you measured yourself against when the pay was late and the orders were filthy. He had not heard anyone say it in eleven years. It went through him like a spear through water.

Boots on the aqueduct road, closer now. A whistle, twice, from the street—his own sergeants, asking where their inquisitor was.

Dain Corvasser drew his sword.

And pointed it—at nothing. At the empty south wall. "Contact in the undercroft!" he bellowed, loud enough to shake dust from the rafters. "Multiple hostiles breaking *east*! To me! TO ME!" And he ran for the south stair himself, roaring, drawing his own soldiers' eyes and feet and fury away from the north wall and the ivy and the rotted rope-gallery, away from the twenty shadows pouring out of the tower like smoke through a cracked window.

Away from her.

He led them on a chase through streets he chose with half his mind and all of his heart, east when west was truer, south when south was wrong, buying alleyways with lies the way he'd once been taught to buy bridges with gold. Behind him, distantly, a woman's voice among his sergeants: "Sir, the north face—there's movement on the north face—" and he shouted "Squadron, *wheel*!" and wheeled them wrong, and hated himself with a purity of self-hatred he hadn't managed since Vell's Crossing, and kept shouting.

Five hundred. He counted. He didn't stop counting until he stood alone in a burning-empty street with his men regrouping around him and the net full of nothing, and the last count came out of his mouth as steam on the cold air, and he understood that he had crossed a line so far behind him it was no longer visible from where he stood.

Inquisitor Dain Corvasser had let the contagion go. Had sung the hounds onto a false scent with his own blessed voice. There was no category for that. There was no processing, no registration, no quiet cell in a white corridor. From this morning forward there was only one road left to him, and it ran in the same direction as hers, and he was going to have to decide whether to walk it or burn it, and he did not know, he did not *know*—

A paper fluttered against his boot. Folded tight, weighted with a pebble, thrown from a rooftop by hands he never saw. He picked it up. Left-handed slant, pressed hard into the paper, ink gone blue-black at every downstroke because she still bore down too hard, she had borne down too hard since she was nine years old.

Three lines.

*Salt Gate checkpoint holds a list with your name on it too. They've had it since yesterday.*

*Selke sold more than maps.*

*Come find me before they find you.—T*

Dain read it twice. Around him his sergeants were waiting for orders, and the morning was tipping over into ordinary daylight, and somewhere beneath the city a god hung in chains of sworn words, waking by inches.

He folded the note into his breast pocket, over his heart, where the badge used to sit before he'd traded it for a worse one.

"To the Salt Gate," he told his men, and set off at a run toward the one place in the capital where his own arrest was already waiting for him.
