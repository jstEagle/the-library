## 15. The Broken Guild

The smoke reached her before the guildhall did.

Tamsin had been three streets away when the bell began—no orderly alarm but a broken, stuttering clangor, as though the hand pulling the rope kept losing its grip—and she had run with Wren's small hand locked in hers, past shuttered windows and faces that watched from behind glass and did nothing, and now she stood at the mouth of Cooper's Lane and could not tell where the building ended and the burning began.

The Guild of Impossible Things had never been beautiful. It was a squat stone warehouse with a slate roof that leaked in the spring and a sign whose letters had faded until it read only *GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THIN*. Oren Vael used to say that was honest advertising: everything inside was thinner than it looked. Now half of it was gone entirely. The eastern wing—the archive wing—was an open wound of blackened joists, fire still chewing along the beams with a low, patient sound like something eating. Water ran in sheets from the gutters, from the bucket chains, from the sky itself, which had clouded over since noon as if the city were ashamed.

Wren's hand tightened around hers. "Tamsin."

"I see it." Her voice came out flat and strange, a bookkeeper's voice, tallying damage instead of feeling it. *East wing lost. Archive lost. Originals lost.* She had saved the cipher pages; she had told herself all morning that saving them was enough. Standing here, watching ten years of careful paper curl to ash, she understood it was not enough. Nothing would be enough again.

They found the survivors gathered in the courtyard, in the rain, because no one wanted to be indoors anymore. Partners and clerks and two of the porters who hauled impossible cargo, all of them soot-streaked and dripping, arranged around the fountain in a loose half-circle the way people arrange themselves around a thing they are afraid to look at directly.

Oren Vael lay on the steps of the fountain with his head in Selke Marrow's lap.

Tamsin stopped walking. The rain ran down her face and she did not wipe it away.

She had seen men die before. Ten years a mercenary, five years a ledger-keeper for trades no sane insurer would touch; she had knelt beside dying friends in ditches and held their hands and lied to them about how bad it looked. But this was different. This was Oren, who signed his letters with a little flourish like a bird taking off. This was the man who had hired her at nineteen off a battlefield where she'd been counting corpses for coin, looked at her ledgers, and said, *You count the dead like they matter. Come count my debts.* This was the man who had put the guild between the Crown's hunters and a child, knowing exactly what it would cost, because some entries could not be left blank.

There was a spear-wound under his ribs. Someone had bound it with a tablecloth—she could see the embroidered corner, the guild's old crest, the key and the open hand—and the cloth had gone dark and then black, and the rain was not helping, and everyone knew it was not helping.

"Tamsin." His eyes found her across the courtyard. They were still clear. That was the worst part. "Come here, girl. Stop standing in the weather like a monument."

She crossed the stones. Kneeling hurt; she didn't notice until later. Selke made room, moving back on her knees, and Tamsin saw the other woman's face properly for the first time in days—saw the exhaustion there, and something else beneath it, something folded small and hidden, and filed it away because there was no time for anything else. There was never going to be time again.

"Report," Oren said.

It was such an *Oren* word that a sound escaped her, half laugh and half sob. "East wing is gone. The archive with it. We got out eleven of the hunted—ten. Ten, plus Wren. Corvasser's men took four partners in the raid. I don't know where they've been taken." She swallowed. "The cipher pages survived. The originals did not."

"The originals." He said it slowly, tasting it. "The ones that prove our pages are true."

"Yes."

"Then we hold a truth no one can check." He coughed, wetly, and Selke leaned forward and Tamsin saw her hands shake. "That's either everything or nothing. Depends what we do with it."

"You shouldn't be talking about strategy," Tamsin said. "You should—" *You should let someone help you,* she almost said, and knew how stupid it would sound. There was no helping this. There were perhaps six surgeons in the capital who might have tried, and every one of them worked for a crown that had just put a torch to her guild.

"Tamsin." Oren lifted one hand. It found her wrist with surprising accuracy, cold fingers closing around it, and she felt how much strength the small gesture cost him. "Listen to me now, because I'm going to stop being interesting very shortly and you'll have to remember it all yourself."

"Don't talk like that."

"I'm a dying man giving instructions. Talking like this is the whole job." A ghost of his old dry smile. Then it went, and what remained underneath was grave and level and more frightening than any expression she had ever seen him wear. "The vault under the counting room. You know the door everyone thinks is the wine cellar."

"The false door."

"There's a true one behind it. Third brick from the floor on the left side—press it twice, then once. It opens on the oldest vault the guild has. Older than me. Older than the charter." His grip tightened. "Your mother knew about it. Did you know that?"

The world tilted slightly. Rain. Stone. His cold hand. "What?"

"She came to us twenty-two years ago wanting something kept. Not sold, not traded. *Kept.* Wouldn't say what. Paid in advance, which told me it was serious—people who pay in advance are people who don't expect to come back." He was breathing harder now, each sentence costing him visibly, and he pushed through it. "We keep it still. Whatever it is. And there are other things down there. Records. The kind the Crown burns people for owning. Deeds and debts and proofs going back to the first kings' reigns. I never let anyone inventory that vault. Not even the partners. Some things you don't put in a ledger, because a ledger can be read."

"Oren—"

"Hear the terms." His voice cracked like a whip, the old command voice, the voice that had settled disputes between partners for thirty years, and the whole courtyard went silent around them. Even the fire seemed to lean in. "I'm naming you guildmaster. In exile. Which means the guild goes wherever you go, and right now that means nowhere, which is fitting, because the Impossible Things have always done their best work nowhere."

"I can't—" The words came out of her before she could stop them, raw and young. "I'm hunted. As of yesterday I'm *hunted*, Oren, I burned my own cover pulling Wren out of Corvasser's net. If you put the guild in my hands you're putting it in the hands of a woman with no name and no safety and a god's token in her pocket. That's not succession, that's a curse."

"That's a Tuesday," Oren said, and a few of the partners made sounds that were almost laughter, wet and ragged. "Girl. Look at me." She looked. His eyes were steady in a face gone gray as ash. "I've watched you keep books for ten years. Every entry true. Every debt honored. Every impossible trade balanced to the copper, even when balancing it cost *us*." He paused, and his voice dropped, went private, just for her. "Do you know why I never asked about your gift? All these years?"

Tamsin said nothing. Her heart was a fist in her chest.

"Because you never used it to win. I've seen gifted folk, Tamsin. They get drunk on it. They bend the world and call it fairness. But you—you used it the way a good clerk uses a ruler. To check the work. Never to cheat. Never once." His breath caught. "When the time came to choose what the guild would be—shelter or surrender—I didn't need to ask your vote. I already knew what you'd written in your heart, because you write everything down somewhere, and it's always true."

*Every entry true.* Her mother's phrase. Her mother's creed. He had known. All these years, he had known what she carried and whose daughter she was, and he had never once said it aloud, because saying it aloud was dangerous and Oren Vael did not make dangerous mistakes.

"The key," she said, and her voice broke on it.

He pressed it into her palm. It was iron, old iron, black with age, shaped like nothing so much as a bird mid-flight—or a flame, depending on the light. Warm from his hand. It weighed almost nothing and it weighed everything. "Third brick. Twice, then once. And Tamsin—" He pulled her closer, close enough that she could hear the rattle in his breath, feel it against her cheek. "Whatever your mother left down there. Whatever the first kings buried. When you find out what it is—"

"What?"

"Keep the books honest." He smiled, and this time it reached his eyes. "That's all a guild ever was. People agreeing to keep the books honest together. Everything else is decoration."

His hand slipped from her wrist.

For a moment he seemed to rest, eyes half-closed, listening to the rain on the stones. Then, without opening his eyes, in the tone of a man closing out the day's accounts: "Selke. The partners will need someone with a head for numbers while the guildmaster is abroad. See to it."

"I will," Selke said softly. "Rest now, Oren."

And Tamsin, kneeling there with a black key in her fist, watched the light go out of the only man who had ever given her a place in the world without asking her to explain herself—and understood, in the same terrible instant, what Selke had meant by *rest*, and what the folded thing in the older woman's face had been, and that grief and suspicion were going to live in the same room inside her for a long time to come.

* * *

They buried him at dusk, in the garden behind the chandler's shop on Bellows Street, because cemeteries were being watched and the guild had always been good at finding space where officially there was none. No priest—the priests were the Crown's now, most of them—but Wren stood at the head of the grave and sang, quietly, a song in no language Tamsin knew, a song that made the rain fall softer somehow, or made it seem to, and no one asked the girl to stop.

Afterward they gathered in the chandler's back room, fourteen of them, dripping and hollow-eyed, and Tamsin stood at the head of a borrowed table and understood that everyone was waiting for her to speak.

She had rehearsed nothing. So she told the truth.

"The guild is not ended," she said. "A guild is not a building. It isn't even its members, though I pray to whatever's left listening that we all survive the season. A guild is a set of promises. The Crown burned our roof. They cannot burn a promise unless we let them." She set the black key on the table, where it seemed to drink the candlelight. "Tomorrow night, I'm opening the old vault. What's in it belongs to all of us now. And after that—" She stopped. Thought of the cipher pages hidden in the lining of her coat. Thought of the Hollow beneath the capital, and the voice she had heard screaming through a prayer-token for one unbearable night. Thought of Dain, somewhere in the dark with his soldiers and his broken oath. "After that, we decide what the Impossible Things are *for*."

Silence. Then old Partner Hesk, seventy if he was a day, raised his cup of watered wine and said, "To Oren Vael, who paid all his debts," and fourteen voices answered, and Tamsin drank with them and tasted nothing at all.

* * *

She went to the vault alone, near midnight, because some doors you must walk through by yourself or they mean nothing.

The counting room was untouched by the fire—Corvasser's men had wanted the archive, not the money—and it smelled of ink and cold stone and absence. She lit one lamp. Found the false wine-cellar door, and the wall behind it, and counted bricks the way Oren had told her, and when her fingers found the third brick from the floor on the left it gave, gently, like a knuckle pressing back.

Twice. Then once.

Stone moved against stone with a sound like a long-held breath released, and a section of wall swung inward onto darkness that smelled of dust and time and, faintly, impossibly, of myrrh—her mother's scent, the oil she wore on holy days, rising up from below as if it had been waiting.

Tamsin stood on the threshold for a long moment, lamp raised, heart hammering.

*Every entry true,* she thought. *Even the ones no one was supposed to read.*

Then she stepped down into the oldest vault of the Guild of Impossible Things, and pulled the door shut behind her, and turned to see what her mother had left.

The lamp found shelves first—shelves of ledgers, hundreds of them, spines cracked with age, some labeled in scripts she did not recognize. It found sealed jars and wrapped bundles and one long shape under a cloth that she decided, firmly, not to look at yet. And at the far end of the vault, on a plain wooden table like an altar, it found a strongbox no bigger than a bread loaf, unremarkable, unlocked, with a single folded paper resting on top bearing eight words in her mother's handwriting:

*Tamsin. If you are reading this, break the seal.*

Her mother had been dead twelve years. The ink had waited twelve years. Tamsin picked up the paper with hands that were not quite steady, and unfolded it, and read:

*The god is not dead. I have seen where they keep him. The proof is in the ledgers behind you—look for the payments marked in red thread, and follow them down. And my darling girl, forgive me: I did not hide you from your gift. I hid you from what it is for.*

Below the words, in the same careful hand, was a sketch. A circle of seven marks. A door. And beneath the door, a single word in the old cipher, the one from the ledgers, the one Tamsin had already learned to read:

*HOLLOW.*

She stood in the dark among her inheritance—all of it, the whole weight of it, ten years of concealment ending in a cellar under a burnt guild—with the lamp trembling in her hand, and outside, far above, a bell began to ring.

Not the guild's broken bell. A city bell. The alarm pattern. The Crown's men were moving through the streets, and they were moving toward Bellows Street.

Someone had told them where the survivors slept.
