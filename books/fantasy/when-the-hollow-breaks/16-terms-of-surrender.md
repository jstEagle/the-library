## 16. Terms of Surrender

Dain Corvasser had learned, over eleven years of inquisition, that the worst rooms were always the quiet ones.

Torture chambers were loud—pleas, prayers, the wet percussion of flesh. Courtrooms were loud in their own way: lawyers circling, judges droning, the crowd's appetite humming under everything like flies. Even executions had their noise, the crowd's hunger and the condemned's last words and the drumroll of official procedure. But a negotiation room was silent, or ought to be. Two chairs. A table between them, bare except for what each party chose to put upon it. And the whole weight of what could not be said pressing down on the silence until it groaned.

He had requested the room himself. The scriptorium annex of the Bastion, third floor, its single window overlooking the cistern yard where the water caught the late light and threw it, rippling and broken, across the ceiling. He had chosen it because it was beautiful, and because he wanted her to see that beauty existed even here, even now, inside the machine that hunted her. A small mercy. Perhaps the last one he would be permitted to give her.

The guards had wanted to search her before admitting her to the Bastion. Dain had forbidden it.

"She'll come unarmed," he'd said, "because I'm asking her to."

And she had. Tamsin Thornbury walked into the scriptorium annex at the appointed hour wearing a mercenary's plain coat and a ledger-keeper's careful expression, and she carried nothing but herself. She paused just inside the door, taking inventory the way she always did—the exits, the window, him—and something in Dain's chest turned over like a page.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been told it's fashionable among the godly." He gestured to the chair across the table. "Sit, Tam."

"Don't call me that."

The words landed exactly where she meant them to. He let them sit there between them for a moment, then nodded once—an acknowledgment, not an apology; apologies were currency he no longer possessed—and she sat. Not relaxed. She never relaxed anymore. But she sat, and folded her hands on the scarred wood of the table, and waited.

She had always been better at waiting than he was. Ten years in ledgers taught a person that debts came due on their own schedule, whether you paced for them or not.

"I'm going to offer you terms," Dain said. "Not demands. Terms. There's a difference, and I need you to hear the difference before you decide anything."

"Terms are demands wearing nicer boots."

"They can be. They don't have to be." He set his hands flat on the table, mirroring hers, and made himself hold her eyes. Her eyes hadn't changed since they were children running messages for the company—dark, quick, always reading three lines ahead. What had changed was what lived behind them. Once it had been mischief, mostly. Now it was arithmetic. She was calculating costs in there, he realized. She was already weighing his soul against her people's lives, and she was doing it faster than he could speak.

"The crown knows about the Hollow," he said.

Something flickered behind her eyes—not surprise. Confirmation. "Does it."

"The Inquisition has fragments. Testimony from Selke Marrow, among others. Enough to know the guild holds documents. Enough to know those documents say the Silent God was never silent by nature." He kept his voice level, clerical, the voice he used for reading charges aloud. It was easier that way. If he let it become the voice of the boy who had shared her bread on the road to Vellmark, he would never get through this. "What the crown does not have is the documents themselves. Or proof. Or names beyond the ones we've already taken."

"Wren," Tamsin said quietly. "You mean Wren is safe."

"For now."

"And Oren Vael?"

Dain looked down at the table. "Dying," he said. "Or dead by now. I haven't been permitted to ask."

"Permitted." She laughed, and it was the ugliest sound he'd ever heard from her, because it wasn't bitter—it was precise. Surgical. "Listen to you. *Permitted.* Do you hear yourself, Dain? You used to complain about the company quartermaster counting our rations."

"The quartermaster wasn't feeding us to a war."

"No," she agreed softly. "He was only starving us honestly."

* * *

He had rehearsed this. He wanted that on the record, somewhere, in whatever ledger the gods kept of men's accounts—he had rehearsed every word, and still the rehearsal crumbled the moment she looked at him like that, like he was an entry she was trying to reconcile and the numbers refused to balance.

He took out the parchment.

It was heavy stock, cream-white, sealed twice—at the top with the crowned sigil of the Regent's office, and at the bottom with the iron wheel of the Inquisition itself. Two powers that had spent two centuries competing for jurisdiction over heresy, joined at last in a single document. He had watched the chancery clerks argue over the wording for six days. He had won most of the arguments. He wondered, sometimes, whether winning them had saved anyone or only made the cage more comfortable.

"This is a covenant of surrender," he said. "Drafted under the Regent's own seal. I'll read you the substance, and then you may read the whole thing yourself, line by line—I know how you are about fine print, and I want you to be. Every clause is negotiable within limits. Except one." He laid the parchment between them but did not push it across. Not yet. "Here is what it says.

"First: full amnesty. Every gift-bearer sheltered by the Guild of Impossible Things, named or unnamed, past or present, is pardoned. No registers. No branding. No compulsory service to the Inquisition. Their gifts become private matters, protected by law rather than prosecuted by it."

"That's impossible," Tamsin said. "That's the opposite of everything the Proclamation stands on."

"It is expensive," Dain said. "It is the most expensive thing the crown has ever offered, which is why it comes at the price it does. Continue listening.

"Second: the Guild of Impossible Things survives. Charter intact, assets unfrozen, partners reinstated—including the ones currently in my cells. Selke Marrow's family keeps their exemption and their freedom. The arson investigation closes without prosecutions."

"You'd pardon her too." Tamsin's jaw tightened. "The woman who burned your archives and sold out a child."

"I'd pardon her because she's already sold everything she has. Chasing her buys nothing and costs the amnesty its credibility. Mercy that's selective isn't mercy, Tam. It's patronage." He heard the inquisitor in his own voice and hated it. "Third: the Hollow evidence—all of it, every cipher page, every original, every transcription—passes to the crown's custody. Sealed, not destroyed. The truth is preserved. It simply becomes... classified."

"Buried."

"Held."

"Those are the same word with different rent." But she was leaning forward now, despite herself. He could see the temptation working on her the way water works on mortar—not violently, just patiently, finding every seam. He understood. That was the cruelty of it, and he had not designed the cruelty, but he was its instrument all the same: the offer was good. It was genuinely good. Half her people freed, her guild restored, the traitor unpunished, the truth preserved in a vault instead of burned in a square—and all it cost was—

"Fourth," Dain said, and here his voice finally failed him, cracked on the first word, and he had to stop and begin again. "Fourth. You renounce your gift. Publicly. At the Cathedral, before the Regent's court and the assembled clergy. You declare before witnesses that the gift of Tamsin Thornbury was vanity and delusion, that no divine inheritance exists, that the Silent God is dead and his token a trinket. You hand the prayer-token to the Patriarch, and he breaks it on the altar stone. And then—" he made himself finish— "you take the white veil. You enter the Order of Quiet Witnesses as their first lay sister. Protected, honored, pensioned. And silenced. Formally, legally, permanently silenced."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough that Dain counted his own heartbeats against it. Eleven. Twelve. Sixteen.

Tamsin did not touch the parchment.

"You've thought about this," she said at last. "A great deal."

"Every day since Vellmark."

"Since before Vellmark." She shook her head slowly. "You've been building toward this since the day you inspected the guild. Since the courtesy call, the tea, the questions about our cargo manifests. This was always where you were walking. Everything else—the arrests, the raid, Oren bleeding out on the guildhouse steps—that was pressure applied to make this moment arrive. To make this look like mercy." She looked up. "Was it?"

He didn't insult her by denying it.

"Yes," he said. "And no. The raids weren't mine to order. Corvasser doesn't command Corvasser, whatever the stories say. But yes. I argued for this shape. Amnesty purchased with one renunciation, because it was the only purchase the crown would approve. One sacrifice instead of a hundred. I told them—" his hands curled on the table— "I told them you'd understand the arithmetic. You're the ledger-keeper. I told them you of all people would see that one debt, paid in full, clears the books for everyone."

"And they believed that?"

"They believed it because it's true." He leaned forward. "Tam. Look at the numbers. Look at them the way you look at everything, coldly, honestly. On one side: your gift. Which has cost you ten years of hiding, cost you your mother's grave unmarked, cost you Oren, cost you half the archives, cost you your name in every registry in the city. On the other side: Wren walks free. The gifted walk free. The guild endures. Your mother's work is preserved in a vault where fire can't reach it. And you—" his voice gentled despite every discipline he owned— "you get to stop running. You get to sleep in one bed for the rest of your life. You get peace, Tam. Real peace, signed and sealed. When has anyone ever offered you that?"

"Never." She said it simply. "No one's ever offered me peace before. Only quieter wars."

"This is not a war."

"Everything is a war. Some of them just file better paperwork." She reached out at last and drew the parchment toward her—but she didn't read it. She laid two fingers flat on the seal instead, the crowned sigil, and looked at him with something worse than anger. Pity. "Read it back to yourself, Dain. Out loud. All four clauses. Tell me what they have in common."

He frowned. "Amnesty. Restoration. Custody. Renunciation—"

"What does every clause buy?" she pressed. "Every single one?"

He was silent.

"Custody," she answered for him, soft and merciless. "The gifted go into the crown's keeping. The evidence goes into the crown's vaults. My gift goes into the Patriarch's hands. My voice goes into your Order. Even the pardon is custody, Dain—you're not freeing anyone, you're moving everyone into a larger cell and calling it a room with a view. That's what this is. That's what it's always been. The Hollow beneath the capital, scaled up to fit a kingdom." She pushed the parchment back across the table, gently, the way one returns a borrowed tool. "The god hangs in chains made of oaths. You're offering me chains made of signatures. I've seen both up close. They weigh the same."

"It's not the same."

"It's exactly the same. It's better made, that's all. Better hinges." She stood, slowly, and Dain stood with her, absurdly, some old etiquette of the road rising in him unbidden—to stand when she stood, as if they were still two soldiers sharing a campfire and not a hunter and the hunted. "You asked me once, on the road to Vellmark, why I never swore the full company oath with the rest of you. Remember? You thought it was pride."

"I remember."

"It was fear," she said. "I'd already seen what oaths do. My mother's letters, the ones I didn't understand yet—I'd seen enough to know that a promise given carelessly becomes a chain someone else holds. So I held back. Just enough. Just the edge of it. And you teased me for years about being the girl who wouldn't commit." Her mouth curved, and it was almost the old smile, almost the one from the road, and it broke his heart more cleanly than any blade could have. "You committed, Dain. Completely. To a crown that uses your love for me as a crowbar. And now you're standing in front of me offering me the same bargain you took—certainty in exchange for self—dressed up in mercy because you can't bear to call it what it is."

"And what is it?" His voice came out rough. "Name it, if you're so wise in your ledgers. Name it plainly."

"Temptation," Tamsin said. "Dressed as peace. The oldest invoice in creation." She moved toward the door, and stopped beside him, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and river water on her coat—the aqueducts, the fugitive roads, the whole underground life she'd built in the space of weeks while he'd built her a cage of cream-colored parchment. "But I'll tell you what I will do, brother, since you went to the trouble of drafting terms."

He flinched at the word *brother*. He couldn't help it. Eleven years of iron discipline, undone by one word spoken without irony.

"The evidence stays free. The gifted stay free. And I am going beneath the capital to open the Hollow, and when the god wakes—and he will wake, Dain, the chains are already failing, your own archivists' ledgers prove it if you'd read them instead of sealing them—when he wakes, every oath sworn to him breaks with him. Including yours. Including the crown's claim to divine right. Including, I think, the Inquisition's authority to exist." She met his eyes. "I'm sorry. I know you came here believing you were saving me."

"I did come to save you."

"I know. That's what makes it unbearable." She touched the door. Paused. "There's a guard rotation change at the fourth bell. The cistern stair will be understaffed for a quarter hour. I noticed on my way in." And before he could decide whether that was a gift, a taunt, or a test, she added, quietly, "You'll have to choose what to report, Dain. Choose carefully. Whatever you write in your ledger tonight—that's the man you'll be tomorrow."

Then she was gone, and the door closed on the soundless click of a well-oiled hinge, and Dain Corvasser stood alone in the beautiful quiet room with a rejected covenant in his hands and her words turning over in him like pages in a wind.

At the fourth bell, he climbed the cistern stair himself. He found the understaffed post, the quarter-hour gap, the faint print of boot-heels in the dust of a service corridor leading down toward the old aqueduct gates—leading down, beneath the river, beneath the foundations, toward the Hollow.

He stood at the head of that stair for a long time.

In his breast pocket, against his heart, he carried the report he was supposed to file. Blank parchment. The ink horn in his other pocket felt suddenly very heavy, like a shackle, like an oath.

Somewhere below the city, a god was screaming inside a woman's skull, and she was walking toward it willingly, and he—sworn brother, inquisitor, keeper of the crown's cleanest lies—stood at a crossroads made of stairs, and began, at last, to descend.
