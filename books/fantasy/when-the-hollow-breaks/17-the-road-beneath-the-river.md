## 17. The Road Beneath the River

The aqueduct took them in without ceremony, the way rivers take in drowned men.

Tamsin stood at its mouth for a long moment before stepping through—an arch of black stone half-swallowed by silt, older than any guild charter, older than the kings who had built their palace above it. Water sheeted down its face in a curtain so thin it looked like glass. Behind her, the city coughed smoke into the grey morning. Ahead, the dark breathed cold against her skin.

Wren's hand found hers. The girl's palm was damp and small and gripping hard enough to hurt, and Tamsin found she did not mind.

"You first or me?" asked Brannoc Hale, the guild's one-armed quartermaster, shifting his pack of oilcloth-wrapped ledgers on his shoulder. He had come because Oren was dead and someone had to carry the accounts, and because, as he'd put it at the mouth of the tunnel, "I've been hauling impossible cargo thirty years; I'm not stopping now that the cargo is us."

"Token first," Tamsin said. She held up her mother's prayer-token on its cord. In the open air it had only ever hummed, a faint vibration like a struck string heard through a wall. Down here, beneath the river's weight, it pulled. Not humming now—*leaning*, the way a compass needle leans, if the needle wanted something.

They went in.

* * *

The drowned aqueducts were not drowned. That was the first lie the name told. The old engineers of the first kings had built three channels: one to carry water to the city, one to carry sewage from it, and one—the middle way—to carry nothing at all. A dry road beneath the riverbed, sealed and forgotten, known only to the guild's oldest maps. Oren Vael had shown Tamsin those maps two years ago, laughing, saying every guild worth its salt kept one route it never used so it would always have somewhere no one expected them to be.

She had not imagined she would need it like this.

Their party numbered eleven. Tamsin and Wren. Brannoc with the ledgers. Sefa Ilves, the younger partner who had voted to use the Hollow evidence rather than burn it, still wearing the scorch-marked coat she'd pulled Wren out of the fire in. Six others—porters, a locksmith named Quill who could open anything including, he claimed, conversations, a former tollgate clerk named Maren whose gift let her hear lies as a sour note in a voice, and three hired blades who had torn up their guild contracts the night of the raid rather than sign the crown's loyalty papers. They walked in single file through a corridor of wet black stone, boots splashing in an inch of standing water, lanterns hooded to show nothing but their own feet.

Above them, muffled through fathoms of earth and riverbed, the capital went about its morning. Criers. Carts. Somewhere far off, a bell. Tamsin tried not to think about which bell. Dain Corvasser's inquisitors had taken the bell tower cell three days ago, and every bell in the city had rung since with a wrongness she couldn't name.

"Tell me a story," Wren said.

It was what the girl did when the dark pressed too close. Tamsin had learned that in the first hours. Wren's gift flared when she was frightened—a shimmer in the air around her, heat-haze made visible—and the flare could be felt by anyone within a hundred paces who knew how to feel it. So they told stories, and Wren listened, and stayed small and quiet and unfindable.

"The bottled storm," Tamsin said. "Guild account 114. Year of the Salt Winter."

"I know that one."

"You don't know the ending."

"There isn't an ending. You said the client never came back."

"He didn't." Tamsin stepped over a fallen strut of ancient timber, wood gone soft as cheese with age. "But here's what I didn't put in the ledger. When we finally opened the vault to inventory it—guild law, everything counted twice a decade—the storm was gone. Bottle empty. And there was rain on the inside of the vault door. Only the inside."

Brannoc snorted. "You never told me that."

"You were recovering from the fever."

"I was recovering from *you*, more likely." But he said it warmly, and Sefa laughed—a short, startled sound, as if she'd forgotten laughing was allowed—and the story did its work. Wren's shoulders came down from around her ears.

That was the arithmetic of this road, Tamsin thought. Every hour of dark bought with a story, every mile paid for in fear and settled in small kindnesses. It was not so different from keeping books. You carried forward the debts you could not pay and trusted the next page to hold something better.

The token tugged them onward.

* * *

They rested at the second junction, where three channels met under a vaulting of carved stone so ancient the reliefs had worn into suggestion—a figure here, a kneeling crowd there, chains that might have been garlands. Tamsin held the token up to the carving and felt it shiver.

"It knows this place," Sefa said quietly.

"It remembers it," Tamsin corrected. "There's a difference. Things remember. My mother taught me—" She stopped. Swallowed. Her mother had taught her a great deal, most of it in the last year, most of it posthumously, through cipher and strongbox and the patient archaeology of ledgers. "My mother taught me that gifts aren't random. They're inherited. And inheritances have histories."

Maren, the lie-hearer, was chewing a strip of dried plum and watching Tamsin with frank curiosity. "And yours has a history? Your gift?"

"Everything does."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Tamsin agreed, "it isn't." She passed out water and hardened bread and did not elaborate, and Maren accepted the silence with a shrug that said *I'll get it out of you eventually*, and Tamsin found, to her own surprise, that she didn't entirely mind the eventual.

This was the thing she had never had, she realized, sitting on wet stone eating bread with ten people who knew what she was—or enough of what she was—and had not flinched. Ten years of hiding had taught her that concealment was safety, and safety was contentment, and contentment was enough. It had been a comfortable ledger. Balanced, precise, every entry in its column. And like all balanced ledgers it had hidden its losses in footnotes: no sworn brother, no table of her own, no one who had ever seen the whole of her accounts.

Now here she was, hunted, exiled, guildmaster of a broken house, sitting in a sewer-road beneath a river with a child asleep against her side and a woman who could taste lies asking after her soul. The columns didn't balance. Nothing balanced anymore. And yet—

"Tamsin." Brannoc, low. He nodded down the left-hand channel.

She heard it a moment later. Boots. Many. Echoing off stone in a rhythm too regular for water, too heavy for rats.

"Lights," she breathed, and three lanterns died as if pinched out.

The sound grew. Then, worse, stopped. Silence has a texture when forty men are holding their breath in a tunnel, and Tamsin felt it press against her eardrums like deep water.

A voice, distorted by echo but not by doubt: "Thornbury. We know you're in the middle channel. Corvasser's orders—you won't be harmed. You'll be *cleared*. There's a difference, and you're clever enough to know it."

Dain hadn't come himself. Of course he hadn't. He'd sent a herald instead, because Dain Corvasser understood precisely how much cruelty lived in courtesy.

"They cleared the tower cell," Sefa whispered against Tamsin's ear. "Cleared it with rope and axes. That's what clearing means now."

The herald waited. Boots shifted. Somewhere a soldier coughed and was elbowed silent.

Tamsin looked at the token in her hand. It leaned—not toward the voice, not toward the soldiers, but deeper, down the middle channel, toward whatever waited at the end of the road her mother had spent her whole life mapping in cipher. *Keep walking*, it said, in the wordless grammar of objects. *This is not your door.*

"We're not here," she said aloud, barely a breath, and Quill understood—he was already moving, finding the junction's third mouth, a channel so narrow the maps called it a maintenance crawl, choked with five centuries of silt. "Everyone in. Slowly. Wren, eyes on me. Story rules."

"*The borrowed year*," Wren whispered back, gripping her hand.

"The borrowed year," Tamsin agreed, and began to tell it as they crawled.

* * *

The borrowed year was the strangest account the guild had ever carried, and Tamsin had audited it three times without once understanding it until the night she'd understood everything else. A farmer had sold the guild twelve months of his life—actual, calendrical, verifiable—and the guild had held them in escrow for forty years waiting for a buyer. Tamsin had found the entry in her mother's hand, and beneath it, in the same cipher that hid the Hollow payments, a marginal note: *Time is also a debt. Ask who holds the calendar.*

She told Wren all of it as they crawled through the silt-throat of the tunnel, elbows and knees in cold mud, lanterns abandoned, guided by Brannoc's count of paces and the token's pull against her sternum. She told it badly, breathlessly, in fragments between squeezes through gaps that scraped her shoulders, and Wren hung on every word anyway, because the words weren't the point. The point was a voice in the dark that wasn't a soldier's voice. The point was being someone's daughter-of-sorts, someone's keeper of stories, someone's—

"Stop." Maren's hand on her ankle. "Listen."

Behind them, faint but distinct: the jingle of mail, the scrape of a lantern-cage, and then a sound that turned Tamsin's blood to well-water. A dog. Hounds could not be crawled away from.

"Onward," Brannoc said grimly. "Onward and downward. The maps say the middle channel drops to the old sluice gates past the fifth shaft. If we can reach the sluice chamber we can flood the crawl behind us."

"Flood it with what?" Sefa asked.

"With the river," Brannoc said, "which has been trying to get in for four hundred years. All we do is stop arguing with it."

They went faster. The token burned cold against Tamsin's skin, pulling so hard now the cord bit into her neck. The crawl widened, then dropped, then opened without warning into a chamber so vast her lantern-light refused to find its walls—a cathedral of water-stone where the old sluice gates stood like the legs of drowned giants, and the river overhead made a sound like slow applause, seeping through a thousand seams.

And there, on the far wall, lit by their returning lanterns: writing. Hundreds of lines of it, chiseled into the stone, running in columns floor to ceiling, in an alphabet Tamsin had seen exactly once before—in the margins of her mother's cipher pages, in the entries that recorded the first kings' payments to *keep the Hollow shut*.

Sefa stared. "What is it?"

"Names," Tamsin said. Her voice came out strange. She raised the token, and it hummed so fiercely it seemed the chamber itself might answer. "Oath-names. Every person who ever swore to him. This is a register. He kept—" She stopped, corrected herself, the scholar in her refusing even now to be imprecise. "*We* kept it. His faithful carved their names so he would know them. And the kings turned the wall into part of the prison. Every name on this wall is a link in his chains."

Quill ran his fingers along the lowest line of carving and whistled softly. "Then whoever wrote these names wrote themselves into his cage."

"Yes," Tamsin said. "Loyalty was the lock. That's what none of us understood. Not the kings' armies. Not sorcery. Oaths." She touched the token to the wall, gently, the way you touch a sleeping face.

The humming changed pitch.

Below them, in the sluice mechanism's throat, iron groaned. Brannoc was already at the winch, Sefa and the hired blades hauling beside him, and behind, far back up the crawl, the hounds gave voice—a high, thin music, closer than comfort allowed.

"Gate's moving!" Brannoc roared. "When it opens, everyone through and nobody looks back!"

Water came first—not a flood but a fist of it, black and roaring, punching through the crawl they'd just abandoned, carrying silt and one very surprised hound and the sound of soldiers shouting. Tamsin shoved Wren through the gap ahead of her, felt hands seize the girl on the other side, and turned for one last look at the wall of names.

In the lantern light, in the wash of the rising water, the carvings had begun—there was no other word for it—to *glisten*. As if the stone remembered it had once been alive.

The token sang against her collarbone, and for the first time in ten years of careful, balanced, hidden life, Tamsin Thornbury answered it.

"Not long now," she said, to a god, to a wall, to a mother. "We're coming."

Then she went through the gate, and the river closed the road behind them, and somewhere far above, in a city built on a secret, the bells all rang at once—with no one ringing them.
