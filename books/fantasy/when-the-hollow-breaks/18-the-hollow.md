## 18. The Hollow

The aqueduct ended where the city's memory ended, in a wall of dressed stone older than any kingdom Tamsin could name.

They had walked for two days beneath the river—Wren ahead with the lantern, the surviving loyalists strung out behind her like beads on a fraying cord: Maren the salt-witch, whose gift had kept their drinking water sweet; old Hollis, who had not spoken since the raid; and three others whose names she had learned only in whispers, because names were currency now and everyone was poor. Selke's betrayal had bought Dain a map of their route. Twice they had heard boots on the stairways above them, and twice the token against Tamsin's breastbone had grown warm as a held hand, and she had led them down side-channels that should not have existed, following warmth like a dowser follows water.

Now the channel simply stopped. Before them stood the wall, and in the wall stood a door.

It was not a grand door. That was the first wrongness of it—the first of many. It was low, iron-banded, scarred by centuries of use, the kind of door that belonged to a cellar or a tithe-house. Set into its face at eye height was a slot for documents, and around the slot, worn nearly smooth by generations of thumbs, ran an inscription so old the letters had softened into suggestion: *PAYMENT RECEIVED. ACCOUNTS KEPT.*

"Ledger-speak," Tamsin said, and her voice came out strange in the dark, too loud and too flat. "It's a counting-house door."

"It's older than counting-houses," Maren murmured. "Older than the river."

Tamsin reached out. She had learned, over these terrible weeks, to read objects when she chose to—not everything, not all at once, but in deliberate sips, because the deep draughts were the ones that had shown her the god screaming. She touched the door lightly, the way one touches a sleeping animal.

The door remembered being built. Not by masons—by clerks. Hundreds of them, working in shifts through years that predated the first kings' coronation, carving each band of iron with words she now understood were oaths: *I will keep the account current. I will deliver what is owed. I will speak no word of the arrangement.* Every oath sworn to the god of the Hollow, transcribed onto iron and stone, hammered into place like nails. The door did not lock anything out.

It locked something in.

"The kings didn't build a prison," she said slowly. "They built a strongbox. And they made the god's own worshippers forge the lid."

Wren set the lantern down. In its light her face was pale as paper and just as blank, waiting for whatever Tamsin would read next, the way she had waited all through the drowned dark—this child who believed, with the simple ferocity of the young, that Tamsin was the one the prophecies meant. The weight of that belief sat on Tamsin's shoulders like wet wool. She had stopped arguing about it days ago. Arguing only made Wren look at her more earnestly.

"The token," Wren said. "Does it want to go in?"

"I don't know what it wants." That was a lie, or near enough. For two days the prayer-token had been pulling—not tugging at its cord, but pulling at something behind her sternum, a slow tidal insistence, like grief remembering itself. Her mother had carried this thing her whole life without once opening the sealed box until her deathbed. Tamsin had always assumed fear. Now she wondered if it had been courtesy. Some doors you do not knock on unless you mean to answer what answers back.

She pressed her palm flat to the iron.

*Payment received,* the door thought, in the voice of ten thousand clerks long dead. *Accounts kept. Enter, keeper, and balance the books.*

There was no handle. There was instead a recess shaped precisely for a hand—and when Tamsin fit hers into it, the iron knew her grip, knew the calluses from quill and sword-hilt both, and the bands began to move. Not swinging open. Unwriting themselves, oath by oath, letters lifting off the metal like ash rising from a page, and behind them the door went soft as curtain.

Beyond lay darkness that breathed.

* * *

The Hollow was a cathedral, and the cathedral was a cage, and the cage was full of paper.

That was Tamsin's first coherent thought, standing on the threshold while the others crowded behind her: *full of paper*. The space opened above and below and around them beyond the lantern's reach—a vaulted dark like the inside of a mountain hollowed by patient hands—and hanging in it, everywhere, drifting slow as snowfall in reverse, were pages. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Scrolls and folios and single sheets, some vellum, some papyrus gone brown as autumn, some written on bark, on hide, on clay tablets with strings still knotted in the old accounting fashion. They turned in air that had no wind, rising and settling, rising and settling, and the sound they made—

The sound they made was breathing.

Not the pages. Something beneath the pages. Something the pages were wound around, layer upon layer, year upon century, the way a cocoon is wound around a living thing.

"Saints," Hollis whispered, and broke his silence at last, and even his blasphemy sounded like prayer.

Tamsin lifted the lantern higher. Its light climbed and climbed and found no ceiling, only more pages, and then—woven through them, thick as a ship's hawser where the pages were thread—a chain.

She followed it down. It descended from the unseen dark above in great slack curves, and it was not iron. Up close, where the light finally caught a link, she saw the truth and felt her knees try to leave her.

Each link was made of writing. Compressed, fused, aged into something harder than steel—but writing still. She could read it if she leaned close. *I swear my sword to your keeping. I swear my harvest's first fruits. I swear my children, my children's children, I will teach them your name, I will keep your festivals, I will never turn from you, I will—* Oaths. Thousands of years of oaths, every promise ever sworn to the silent god by every faithful mouth, drawn out of the world and hammered link by link into fetters.

And wrapped in those chains, bound at the center of all that drifting paper, hung the god.

He did not look like the icons. He did not look like anything the iconographers had dared. He was vast in a way the eye refused—one moment he seemed a figure the size of a man, gaunt and grey-bearded and patient; the next the scale lurched and he filled the vault entire, shoulders braced against the mountain's bones. His eyes were open. They had been open, Tamsin understood, for nine hundred years.

His mouth was closed. Stitched shut—not with thread but with a single ribbon of vellum, and on the vellum, in the first king's own hand, which Tamsin recognized from the cipher entries, was written: *THE ACCOUNT IS SETTLED.*

"Oh," Tamsin said. It was a very small word for the largest thing she had ever felt. "Oh, you poor magnificent creature. You never died. They couldn't kill you, so they made your own faith into your chains."

The loyalists were on their knees. Maren was weeping without noise. Wren stood closest of any of them, head tipped back, lips moving—reciting, Tamsin realized, the litany the bell-tower cell had taught her, the names of the god's forgotten aspects: Keeper, Witness, Hearer-of-Promises. And with each name the god's gaze shifted, fractionally, until it rested on Tamsin.

Being looked at by him was like being audited by the dawn.

She felt every entry of her life laid open—the small cruelties, the careful concealments, the decade of hiding her gift like a thief hides stolen silver, the ledgers she'd balanced with clean hands and dirty knowledge. But there was no condemnation in the looking. That was the worst of it. He looked at her the way she looked at accounts: seeking only what was true, recording only what was there.

"You're the ledger," she whispered, understanding arriving like cold water. "You've been the ledger all along. Every oath sworn to you was an entry in your own book, and the kings found the trick of turning the entries into bars." Her voice shook. "Loyalty. They used loyalty itself. People swore to serve you faithfully and their faithfulness became the chain—because a promise kept forever is a prison, isn't it? To them. To you."

The god's throat moved beneath the stitched vellum. From somewhere in the vast dark came a sound—not a voice, nothing so merciful as a voice—a pressure, a groan in the stone, in the teeth, in the fluid of the eyes. Tamsin had heard that pressure once before, for one unbearable night, when the token showed her everything. She knew what it meant. Nine hundred years of trying to say one word through a sewn mouth:

*I never asked them to stop speaking to me. Only to mean it.*

"He wants—" Wren turned, radiant, tear-streaked, certain. "Tamsin. Tamsin, he knows why you came. The token. Give him the token."

Tamsin's hand closed around the prayer-token through her shirt. Warm. Warmer than it had ever been, warm as a living thing, and it was humming now—not the faint buzz of before but a clear sustained note, and around them the drifting pages had begun to stir, to orient, thousands of documents turning like leaves toward a sun, all of them facing her.

She saw then what the pages were. Not random records. Contracts. Every covenant ever struck between mortal and divine—the god's half of every bargain, written out and waiting, unsigned, undelivered, held in escrow for nine hundred years because the counterparty had been bound before he could honor a single promise. Harvests never blessed. Battles never judged. Dying prayers never answered. All of it filed here, accruing interest, the greatest arrears in history.

The first kings hadn't silenced a dead god. They had defaulted on the oldest debt in the world, and hidden the creditor.

Tamsin had spent her whole life believing debts must be paid. It was the one law she had never broken, the spine of everything she was. And here, hanging in chains made of other people's kept promises, was the debtor to end all debtors—innocent of everything except being trusted.

Her eyes stung. She laughed, once, wetly. "Oren would have loved you," she told the god, meaning the man he might have been, the partner across a table. "A client who can't lie, can't run, and can't pay up. He'd have called you impossible."

The god's ruined mouth might almost have smiled.

Then the note of the token changed.

It dropped, the way a dropped glass drops—all at once and irreversibly—and the warmth went out of it, and every page in the Hollow stopped turning at the same instant. Silence absolute, silence with weight, and Tamsin knew, before the sound came echoing down the aqueduct behind them, before the torchlight bloomed in the low doorway, exactly what it meant.

Boots. Many boots. The ring of drawn steel.

And a voice she knew better than her own, calling carefully into the dark, pitched to carry and to wound:

"Tamsin. I know you're in there. Step out where I can see you, sister, and we'll talk about what mercy looks like."

Dain had found them. Dain was already inside the threshold.

And behind Tamsin, soft as falling paper, the god's chains had begun—to tighten.
