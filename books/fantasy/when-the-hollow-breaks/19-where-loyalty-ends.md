## 19. Where Loyalty Ends

Dain Corvasser had learned, over twenty years of service to the crown, that the truth was a thing you carried the way a soldier carries a wound: privately, quietly, and always on the side away from your officers. He had carried his for twenty-three years—since the night in the ash-yard behind the mercenary company's barracks when a hedge-witch pressed her thumb to a newborn's brow and read a mark there like a signature.

*Marked*, she had said. *This one will see what is written.*

He had told no one. Not his commanders. Not his confessors. Not the inquisitors he'd risen beside, whose careers were built on finding precisely the thing he was. He had built instead a fortress of diligence so impeccable that no one ever thought to look inside it. He hunted the gifted with the particular ferocity of a man drowning who has decided the water is fine.

And now he stood at the threshold of the Hollow with his sword drawn, and Tamsin Thornbury looked at him across thirty feet of black stone, and there was nowhere left to hide it.

The chamber defied every law of masonry he knew. It was vast as a drowned cathedral, its pillars rising into darkness where no ceiling should be, and at its center hung the god.

Dain had expected—what? A corpse? An idol? Something he could put a name to and file away. What he saw instead made his sword-arm ache with the effort of not lowering it. The god was bound in chains, yes—but the chains were writing. Lines of script, dense as ledger-columns, ran from the walls in ribbons of pale light, coiling around limbs the size of bridge-arches, wrapping a body that seemed less flesh than weather, less form than the idea of form. Every link was an oath. Dain understood this without being told, the way one understands grief without instruction. He could feel it in his teeth: the hum of ten thousand promises, sworn and kept and kept and kept, until the keeping itself became a prison.

"Corvasser," said the woman beside Tamsin—one of the loyalists, Marta of the aqueduct cells, her voice raw from running. "Behind us."

He heard them then. Boots on stone, many, disciplined. His own inquisitors, descending through the aqueduct mouth he'd come by, moving faster than they should have been because Selke Marrow's information had been very good indeed.

"Tamsin." His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "Step away from it. That is an order, and it is also a mercy, and I need you to believe both things are true."

She didn't step away. Of course she didn't. She stood in front of a god with a child at her hip and three exhausted survivors at her back, and she looked at him the way she used to look at him across the training yard when they were fourteen and he was cheating at knife-work—patient, unimpressed, certain he would thank her for it later.

"Dain." Just his name. No title. She had never once called him Inquisitor, not even now, not even with his soldiers coming. "You've known since the yard in Vellmark. You've known what I am for two years. And you came down here alone ahead of them anyway."

He hadn't known he'd done that until she said it. He had ordered his squad to follow at distance—"the Hollow is unstable; no torches past the third junction"—and only now heard the order for what it was.

"I came to arrest you," he said.

"You came alone first."

The boots were louder now. Echoing off stone that should not echo, sound behaving strangely here, as if the chamber itself were listening. Dain felt sweat slide cold between his shoulder blades. Twenty years of discipline held him upright. Underneath the discipline, something older stirred—the boy in the ash-yard, watching a witch's thumb hover over his own brow, feeling the world lean close.

"There is a bargain on the table," he said. "You know there is. Deliver the evidence, renounce publicly, and the crown spares the rest. I negotiated it myself. It is the best terms anyone has gotten out of the Lord Inquisitor's office in a decade. Take it."

"And the god stays chained."

"The god stays chained, the guild survives, Wren lives to grow up, and you live to grow old. That is the arithmetic, Tam. I have run it every way there is to run it." He heard his own voice fraying. "It is the best arithmetic there is."

"No," she said gently, terribly. "It's just the quietest."

Then she did the thing he would spend whatever remained of his life trying to understand.

She walked toward him.

Marta hissed a warning. The child—Wren, small and hollow-eyed and clutching a scrap of bell-rope—made a sound like a struck wire. Tamsin crossed the threshold-stone with her hands open and empty, and stopped an arm's length from the point of his sword, and said:

"Look. That's all I'm asking. Don't take my word. Don't take theirs. Look."

"I can't—" His throat closed. *I can't let you show me, because if you show me I will have to choose, and I know which way I'll choose, and everything I am will burn.* "Tamsin. If you use the gift on me, I am required by statute to—"

"Required." She almost smiled. "There's the word. Required. Sworn. Bound. Dain, look at him." She turned, and her open hand swept toward the hanging vastness, toward the chains of script that pulsed slow as a sleeping heart. "Every chain on him is an oath someone kept. Kings paid the guild for two hundred years to keep the Hollow shut—that's in my ledgers, I can show you the cipher, but you wouldn't sign off on paper, would you? Paper can be forged. So don't take the paper. Take me."

The boots reached the top of the final stair. Torches bloomed in the tunnel mouth behind them—six men, eight, the white-and-gold sashes of the Office of Inquiry bright as wounds in the dark. Their commander, a broad man named Hesk whom Dain had personally trained, called out: "Inquisitor Corvasser! Hold the traitor! Do not engage the anomaly!"

*Anomaly.* They had named a god *anomaly*. Somewhere above, some clerk had stamped a classification on the oldest living thing in the world, and the stamp had been approved.

Dain did not move. His sword stayed level. Eight of his people fanned out along the gallery with crossbows, and the chains of the god dimmed slightly, the way a candle gutters when a door opens, and Dain understood with sickening clarity that the oaths binding it included his own—his crown-oath, sworn at seventeen with his hand on the seal of the Office, *to guard the realm against all powers unaccounted*. He was one of the links. He was part of the cage. Every inquisitor who had ever taken the vow was a link, and the god had been listening to their fidelity for nine hundred years, and it must have been—

It must have been like drowning while everyone praised you for holding your breath.

"Last chance," Hesk called. "Both of them, sir. The girl too. Orders from the Lord Inquisitor's own hand."

Tamsin hadn't moved. She stood inside the arc of his blade with her hands open, and her eyes were wet, and she was not begging. That was the unbearable part. She was offering.

"Through me," she said. "One breath of it. That's all it takes—it comes through touch, through the token, but I can carry it to you. You'll feel what I felt. One night of his voice, Dain. Nine hundred years of it, compressed into one breath. And then you decide. Not before. I won't have it any other way—I'm not asking you to betray anything blind."

"She's using the gift," someone shouted from the gallery. "Corvasser, she's working the gift—"

"I know," Dain said.

His voice came out strange. Flat. Final. The word *knowing* sat in his chest like a key turning.

Because he did know. He had always known. He had known since the ash-yard, since the witch's thumb, since the first time he'd touched a dead man's ring and seen the argument that had killed him playing behind his eyes like theater—he'd known exactly what she was offering, and he had spent twenty years making sure no one ever offered it twice.

He sheathed his sword.

The sound of it rang through the Hollow like a dropped coin in a temple, and eight crossbows snapped up along the gallery, and Tamsin's breath went out of her in a rush, and Dain stepped forward and took her hands.

Her palms were rough with rope-burn and ink. The prayer-token was laced through her fingers, warm as a held bird. For one heartbeat they stood like children again, sworn brother and sister in a mud-yard company, before either of them had sworn anything to anyone but each other.

"Whatever you're going to do," she whispered, "do it fast. They'll loose on my mark or yours."

"It'll be mine," he said. "Now. *Now.*"

The gift came through the token like a river through a broken dam.

He had braced for images. What he got was *voice*—a voice so old it predated language and yet spoke in it perfectly, fluently, wearily, the way a man speaks after decades of shouting at a door that never opens. It poured into him: the first kings kneeling in this dark, swearing fealty with tears on their faces. The god accepting—not demanding, *accepting*—their oaths as a father accepts a child's promise to behave, meaning nothing by it, never dreaming the words could be turned. Then the kings rising, and the priests arriving with their styluses, and the slow horror of feeling each promise harden, thicken, *chain*: every oath sworn to him becoming a link, every act of mortal fidelity becoming fetter. Loyalty as leash. Devotion as dungeon. He felt the god understand it too late—felt the moment the last king walked out of the Hollow and the door of law sealed overhead—and then he felt the nine hundred years.

Nine hundred years of hearing prayers addressed to a silence the pray-ers believed was death. Nine hundred years of feeling every gift-bearer born as a splinter of himself scattered among mortals, hunted, burned, hidden, hidden, hidden. Nine hundred years of inquisitors' oaths tightening link by link, generation by generation, faithful men and women keeping their vows beautifully, impeccably, each kept promise another turn of the screw.

Dain fell to his knees. He was aware, distantly, of Tamsin catching his shoulders, of Wren crying out, of the gallery erupting into shouted orders—but all of it was far away, because inside his skull the god's voice was saying one sentence, over and over, in the tone of a man reading his own epitaph:

*I never asked them to keep their word. I asked them to mean it. There is a difference. There was always a difference.*

When he surfaced, his face was soaked and his nose was bleeding and Tamsin was holding him up, and somewhere above them Hesk was screaming for volley-fire.

"Inquisitor!" Hesk's voice cracked across the chamber. "You are relieved of command! By authority of the Lord Inquisitor's writ, stand aside!"

Dain rose. His legs shook. His hands did not.

For twenty-three years he had carried the truth on the side away from his officers, and he had thought that was cowardice, and maybe it was, but standing here now with the god's lament still ringing in his bones he understood it had also been preparation. Everything he was—every inspection passed, every hunt led, every night he'd lain awake hating himself for the precision of his own diligence—had been load-bearing. It had all been holding up this exact moment, waiting for him to arrive.

"Hesk," he said. "Do you remember Vellmark? The foundling house?"

Hesk faltered. "Sir?"

"You asked me why we burned the records instead of archiving them. I told you it was procedure." Dain drew his sword—not against the threshold, not against Tamsin, but turning, slowly, deliberately, to face the gallery. "It wasn't. I'll tell you what it was on the other side, if we get to an other side. Men—lower your pieces."

"Corvasser, you are *relieved*—"

"I know what I am." And gods forgive him, it felt like laughter, it actually wanted to be laughter. "I'm the only honest man in this room. Fire."

The first bolt he batted from the air—an impossible parry, the token's warmth flooding his arm, the god lending him a fraction of a fraction of its attention, enough. The second bolt took him through the meat of the left shoulder and spun him half around and he barely felt it. He was already moving, already shouting, driving forward into the gallery's narrow throat where eight crossbows became useless clubs and eight trained killers became, briefly, simply men who had trusted him, hesitating in the torchlight because twenty years of loyalty does not evaporate at a single order.

That hesitation was the whole battle. He fought inside it.

He broke Vessick's crossbow arm and threw him down the stairwell rather than onto his blade. He caught Hesk's cutlass on his quillon and headbutted him—undignified, effective, a mercenary's trick from a mud-yard company that had never appeared in any Office manual—and while the man reeled he shoved him bodily back into the others, a human ram. Behind him he heard Tamsin's voice rising in the Hollow, chanting something old, and the chains of script began to glow, link by link, oath by oath, waking.

"Burn it all!" someone screamed. "Fire the chamber! The writ permits—"

"No," Dain snarled, and drove his sword through the speaker's sash and the wall behind it, pinning cloth and terror but not flesh, and wrenched the man's torch from his hand and hurled it into the flooded channel below, where it died with a hiss.

A bolt hit him low in the back. He didn't fall. He noticed, with academic interest, that he probably should.

*Tam,* he thought—couldn't say anymore, blood where breath should be, but thought it clearly, the way you think a name across a great distance—*it's working. Whatever you're doing. It's working. Finish it.*

The chains were blazing now, whole columns of script burning off like fog under sun, and the god was *moving*, the first true motion in nine centuries, and the sound it made was not a roar but a long, shuddering exhale, the exhale of a prisoner who has just heard the lock turn, and the gallery inquisitors broke—not routed, not yet, but breaking, falling back up the stairwell, and Dain planted himself in the throat of the passage with his ruined shoulder and his ruined back and his sword held level, and made himself the last thing between his old life and his new one.

They came at him because he left them no choice, and he killed two of them because he left himself no choice, and he hated every second of it with a purity he had never managed to feel about anything else in his life. This was the arithmetic he'd never been able to solve: that mercy sometimes wore armor, that peace was sometimes just a quieter prison, that the only clean choice left to a man who had spent twenty-three years dirty was the one that cost him everything.

Vessick's dagger found him under the ribs. Hesk's boot caught his wounded knee and put him down. The last thing he saw from the ground was not the inquisitors regrouping, not the stairwell filling with reinforcements from above—

It was Tamsin Thornbury walking out of the light of a breaking god, unnamed and unbowed, with a child on her hip and her hand extended down toward him.

He couldn't take it. His arms wouldn't answer.

So he smiled instead—the mud-yard smile, the cheating-at-knives smile—and spent his last breath on the only honest entry he'd ever made:

"Told you... you'd thank me later."

Above them, the Hollow broke. Below him, Dain Corvasser—sworn blade, honest man, marked at birth, free at last—closed his eyes on the sight of the door swinging open, and did not see what came through it.
