## 21. A New Covenant

The city was burning in places, and singing in others, and Tamsin Thornbury walked through both with a god at her shoulder.

He had chosen a shape for the surface world: an old man in a grey coat, unremarkable, the kind of figure one's eye slid past in a market crowd. Only his eyes were wrong—riverstone veined with gold, and when he looked at something, it stayed looked-at. Tamsin had seen that quality before, in her mother's prayer-token. In herself, if she was honest, on the bad nights.

The bells of the capital had not stopped ringing since dawn. Somewhere to the east, the palace district was an orange smear against the morning sky; somewhere to the west, people were standing in their doorways saying *I am no one's sworn man* out loud, testing the words like children testing ice. The oath-bonds had come apart all over the city at once—the moment she had released them in the Hollow, every vow sworn to the silent god had dissolved into plain air, and ten thousand years of quiet obedience had ended with a sound only the gifted could hear: a sigh, vast and relieved, like a rope going slack after a lifetime of weight.

Ordinary folk heard nothing. Ordinary folk just felt lighter, and could not say why, and some of them wept into their breakfast without knowing the reason.

"It's strange," the god said. His voice was quiet, roughened, as though he had not used it in a very long time—which was true—and as though he found each word slightly surprising, which was also true. "I expected to be angry."

"You aren't?"

"I was angry for nine thousand years. Anger is a thing you do while bound." He considered a collapsed fruit-stall, its apples rolling free into the gutter, and stopped to right the stall's broken leg with two fingers, an absurd domestic gesture from a being who had once held up the sky of a nation's faith. "Now I am mostly curious. And tired. Bound things dream of freedom; free things dream of rest."

Tamsin said nothing. She had spent the walk cataloguing what she had lost, the way she would audit any account: her guild name, struck through. Her mercenary vow, released. Her identity as a sworn blade of the Guild of Impossible Things—dissolved, because every oath in the capital had been, in some deep ledger-entry neither king nor priest had ever read, sworn ultimately to him. She had known that would happen. She had done it anyway. But knowing the price in advance did not stop the hands from shaking afterward, and hers had not stopped shaking yet.

Wren walked on her other side, holding the hem of Tamsin's coat with one fist, the way she had held it through the tollgates, through the aqueducts, through the burning of everything. She had not spoken much since the Hollow. Gifted children, Tamsin was learning, processed catastrophe by watching everything and saying little, and Wren's eyes had been moving constantly—cataloguing the smoke, the singers, the soldiers who had simply put down their weapons in the streets when their oaths came undone and stood looking at their own hands as though the hands belonged to strangers.

Ahead of them, the Guildhall rose out of the smoke, half its roof gone, its great door hanging open like a slack jaw.

Oren Vael had died there yesterday. Or rather—he had died in the street outside it, buying Tamsin forty seconds with a duelist's precision and a guildmaster's stubbornness, and his body had been carried inside by partners who wept and saluted in the same breath. Tamsin had named him guildmaster in exile for six hours, and then he had been dead, and the title had fallen to her like a stone down a well.

The surviving partners were waiting in the shattered counting hall. Selke Marrow was not among them—Selke was in a cell beneath the hall, alive, because Tamsin had argued for alive, because even betrayal had comprehensible reasons and because someone would eventually have to do the arithmetic of what came next with clean eyes, and Selke's eyes, whatever else they were, had always been clean.

They stood among the wreckage: Master Ferren with his burn-scarred hands, young Ivetta with her arm in a sling, old Brannoc leaning on a spear he had not carried in thirty years, and a dozen more, ash-grey, exhausted, alive. On the wall behind them, the guild's ancient charter hung crooked, one corner scorched black.

Tamsin set her ledger on the counting table. The god stood beside her, grey and quiet, and let the silence stretch until it became unbearable, which she suspected was deliberate. He was teaching her, she realized. Letting her open this negotiation herself. *Witness*, he had said in the Hollow. *Not worship. Not service. Witness.*

"Partners," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You know what happened beneath us. You know the oaths are gone—all of them, everywhere, including ours. Including mine."

"We felt it," Ferren said. "Half the contracts in this city are void. Caravan guarantees, protection bonds, marriage-vows made in the old rite—" He shook his head. "There are clerks across the capital sitting in front of ledgers that have turned to nonsense overnight. Every entry that referenced an oath is now referencing nothing."

"I know," Tamsin said. "I've been thinking about that."

"She would," Brannoc muttered, but there was no venom in it. There was almost fondness.

"The crown will tell you this is chaos," Tamsin went on. "The priests will tell you it's apocalypse. Both of them are wrong. It's an *audit*." She opened her ledger to a fresh page. "For ten thousand years, the divine law of this land ran on obedience. You swore, therefore you obeyed. The god below enforced the oaths whether he wished to or not—that was his imprisonment, that was the mechanism of his chains. Every vow ever sworn fed the lock." She looked around the room. "That enforcement is finished. Not because he's cruel enough to abandon you, but because he's finally *free enough to*. What holds now is only what people choose to hold."

"Then everything falls," Ivetta said flatly.

"No," said the god.

Everyone turned. He had not raised his voice. He never seemed to need to.

"Everything does not fall," he repeated. "Everything *settles*. A building stands not because it fears the ground, but because it rests upon it. Your kings built their tower on my back and called the stillness law." He touched the scorched charter on the wall, gently, the way one touches an old scar. "This document—your guild's charter—I remember when it was drafted. Nine hundred years ago, in a room smaller than this one, by frightened people with good ink. Do you know why it has endured when kingdoms fell around it?"

"Because we honor our contracts," Ferren said.

"Because your charter was never an oath," the god said. "It is a *record*. It says: these are the terms we agreed to, here is the proof, here is the witness. Oaths bind the swearer. Records bind no one—they simply *remain*. Anyone may consult them. Anyone may contest them. Anyone may be corrected by them." He looked at Tamsin, and gold moved in the riverstone of his eyes. "Your guild has spent nine hundred years doing, in miniature, the only thing I have ever actually wanted done. Keeping true accounts. I did not teach humanity to swear. I taught them to *write things down*. The kings took the first skill and built chains from it. The second skill survived in spite of them—in warehouses like this one, kept by people like her."

The silence in the counting hall was very long.

"You're proposing," Tamsin said slowly, working it through as she spoke, the way she worked through any impossible trade, "that the guild take up what the crown and the temple have lost."

"I am proposing that the guild *admit* what it has always been," the god said. "The last honest institution in this city."

"They'll burn us," Ferren said. "What's left of them will come with fire, same as before."

"Some of them will try," the god agreed. "And some of them will discover that their soldiers' oaths no longer hold, and that their tax-collectors' ledgers have been public record since dawn, and that a covenant which rests on truth is far harder to burn than one which rests on fear. Fire destroyed half your archive. Did it destroy the truth in the pages that survived?"

Tamsin thought of the cipher pages, singed at the edges, clutched to her chest through the raid. No. The truth had survived. The truth was annoyingly durable that way. She had staked ten years of concealment on the opposite belief, and the whole war had taught her otherwise.

She turned to face the partners fully. Her hands had finally stopped shaking.

"Here are my terms," she said. "As guildmaster—if the title means anything anymore, which is itself the first question on the table."

"Say them," Brannoc said. "We'll vote. That's how this house works. Even now. *Especially* now."

"First: the gifted are received into the guild as witnesses, not weapons. Their gifts are used to verify—to read the true history of objects, of documents, of deeds—never to compel. We keep accounts of divine truth the way we keep accounts of grain and storm-bottles. Publicly. Openly. Any citizen may request verification, any citizen may contest it before three witnesses."

"Public ledgers," Ferren breathed. "Anyone could read our books."

"Yes," Tamsin said. "That's the point. Secrecy is how they hunted us. It's how *I* hid for ten years, and look what hiding bought. A secret truth is a hostage. A published truth is a foundation."

"Second," she continued, "the guild renounces enforcement entirely. We don't punish. We don't compel. We record. If a merchant cheats, we write it where everyone can see, and the market corrects him. If a magistrate lies under verification, we write that too, and the city decides what to do about its magistrates. We are not a new crown. We're the opposite of a crown."

"And third?" Ivetta asked.

"Third." Tamsin paused. This was the hard one, and she made herself say it plainly. "Third, we forgive Selke Marrow. Publicly, and on the record. Her family is exempt from the hunt by crown writ; that writ dies with the hunt, and when it dies, she comes home. Her betrayal is entered in the books beside everything else, honestly stated, and then we move forward, because a guild that keeps accounts must also know which debts are worth collecting and which are only worth recording."

"That's mercy," Brannoc said.

"That's arithmetic," Tamsin said. "Hate costs more than it returns. I've run the numbers."

Old Brannoc laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut. Then he pushed himself upright off his spear, wincing, and raised his hand. "I vote aye. All three terms. My father kept this guild's books before me, and his father before him, and none of them ever swore an oath they weren't also willing to have printed on the door. This is the oldest thing we've ever done, girl. It just looks new."

Ferren's hand went up next. Then Ivetta's. Then the rest, one by one, ash-grey faces turning toward her, until only the empty chair where Oren Vael had sat remained unvoted, and Tamsin looked at it, and decided that was its own kind of aye.

"It carries," she said quietly.

"It carries," Ferren confirmed. "Motion entered. Someone write it down before the roof finishes falling in."

"I'll write it," said Wren.

Everyone turned. The child had stepped forward, chin up, her small hand already reaching for the spare pen on the counting table. "I'm gifted. You said witnesses. I want to be the one who writes it down. First entry. So it counts."

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment—at the tollgate-child, the bell-tower-child, the child she had burned her own cover to save, who had watched everything and said little and had apparently been listening to all of it. Something in Tamsin's chest, which had been calcifying for ten years and shattering for two days, settled into a new configuration. Not healed. Set, the way a bone sets. Usable.

"Apprentice ledger-keeper," she said formally, "you'll need better handwriting."

"I'll learn," Wren said. "I learn fast. Ask anyone I've robbed."

A ragged laugh went around the counting hall—the first one in two days, thin and cracked and real.

* * *

They drafted the covenant through the afternoon, in the ruined hall, while volunteers nailed tarps over the missing roof and the smell of smoke gave way gradually to the smell of fresh ink.

The god sat apart, at Oren Vael's old chair, which nobody had objected to and everybody had noticed. He did not dictate. When Tamsin brought him passages to review, he read them with his finger moving under the lines like a schoolmaster, and changed surprisingly little, and what he changed he changed always in the same direction: toward less power for himself.

*The god shall hold no office within the Guild,* she wrote at his instruction. *The god shall make no commandments. The god shall be consulted as a witness of last resort, whose testimony, being divine, shall be entered in the public record alongside all testimony, weighted no more than any other honest account.*

"Weighted no more than any other," Ferren protested, reading over her shoulder. "He's a *god*."

"A god who was chained for nine thousand years by the weight of other people's certainty," the god said mildly. "Let me be one voice among many. I find I have developed a strong professional objection to being load-bearing."

By dusk, the document stood complete on the counting table: the Covenant of Witness, seventeen articles, written fair in Wren's improving hand, signed by every surviving partner, witnessed by a god, and headed—as Tamsin had insisted over mild objection—with the guild's founding words, the ones her mother had taught her before either of them knew what they meant:

*Nothing here is owed. Everything here is recorded.*

Dain Corvasser arrived as the last signature dried.

He came alone, out of uniform, in a plain grey coat that matched the sky, and he stopped in the doorway of the counting hall the way a man stops at a threshold he isn't sure he's permitted to cross. There was ash in his hair. There was a bandage on his forearm. Behind him, the bells had finally, finally gone quiet.

Tamsin set down her pen.

"Inquisitor," she said.

"Not anymore." His voice was flat. "The Office of Inquiry was dissolved this morning. By whom, nobody's quite sure—the chain of command dissolved along with everything else. Half the crown's writs are void. The hunt is..." He looked at his hands. "The hunt doesn't exist. The hunters don't either. We're all just men again, suddenly, and I haven't slept, and I've been walking toward this door for six hours because I couldn't think of anywhere else to go."

Wren had gone stiff beside Tamsin, one small hand curled into a fist. Tamsin rested two fingers on the child's shoulder—*witness, not weapon*—and stepped forward to meet her oldest friend on neutral ground.

"Dain—"

"Don't." He held up his hand. "Don't absolve me. I arrested children, Tam. I arrested the bell-tower cell. I read the god's history through your gift at the Hollow and it took me a full day to understand what I'd been doing my entire career, and I'm not asking for forgiveness, I'm asking for—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started over, quieter. "What do you do with a man who was loyal to a lie? Where does he go? What work is there for him?"

Tamsin looked at him for a long time. The god, in Oren's chair, watched them both with an expression she could not read and suspected was deliberately unreadable.

Then she turned back to the table, took the covenant, and laid a blank page beside it.

"There's one office left vacant," she said. "Verifier of contested records. It requires a man who knows exactly how lies are built, because he built them professionally. It requires a man the old regime trusted, so that defectors believe they'll be heard fairly. It pays poorly, the hours are terrible, and the first file on your desk will be Selke Marrow's, and you will have to verify her betrayal honestly even though she sold us to save her family, because that's the job. Nothing is owed. Everything is recorded." She held out the pen. "No oath required. Just your signature, and your word, renewed whenever you choose to renew it, and revocable the day you stop meaning it."

Dain stared at the pen.

"That's it? After everything—you're just—"

"That's it. Loyalty rebuilt on truth instead of obedience. It's the whole theme. I've had a very long week; I'd like the theme to resolve."

Something broke in his face—not grief exactly, and not relief, but the specific collapse of a man who had braced for judgment and been handed a job description instead. He took the pen. His signature, when he wrote it, was careful and exact, a soldier's signature, a bookkeeper's signature, and when he finished, Wren leaned over and added beneath it, in her small determined hand:

*Witnessed.*

* * *

Night came, and with it the first honest quiet.

The counting hall was empty now except for the two of them—Tamsin and the god—sitting across the table from each other over cold tea, like any two colleagues closing out a long day's accounts. Through the tarped gap where the roof had been, the stars were visible, and beyond them the dark bulk of the palace district, still smoldering faintly, and beyond that the sleeping city, lighter-hearted than it had been in ten thousand years and not one person in it knowing precisely why.

"You could have demanded anything today," Tamsin said. "Worship. Thrones. A temple on every hill. They'd have given it. Half of them wanted to."

"Yes." The god turned his cup slowly in his grey hands. "And in nine thousand years of bondage, I learned one lesson perfectly: whatever is given to me out of awe will eventually be taken back out of fear. Worship is a loan with ruinous interest. I would rather be one voice in a chorus than the whole song again." He looked up, and the gold in his eyes was gentle. "Besides. You freed me with a ledger, Tamsin Thornbury. Not with a sword. It seems fitting that I retire into one."

"My name's struck through," she said. "I released my own oath with the rest. I walked out of the Hollow unnamed. I'm not sure who I am anymore, honestly. Ten years I was the woman who hid, and then one day I was the woman who hid everyone else, and now—" She gestured vaguely at the covenant, the tarps, the smoking skyline. "Now I'm apparently a guildmaster with no guild charter and a god for a colleague, and I don't have a single sworn word left in my body. I feel like a building with the scaffolding removed, waiting to see if I stand."

"You stand," the god said simply. "I have seen ten thousand years of human beings, and I can tell you what I know: the oath was never what held you upright. You held the oaths. All of them. Even the false ones. Even the ones that cost you." He rose, and gathered his grey coat around him, and for a moment his outline flickered—riverstone, gold, something vast and patient wearing the shape of a tired old man. "Rest tonight. Tomorrow, the real work begins: the crown will want terms, the temple will want concessions, and every clerk in this city will want to know what happens to a contract written in good faith under a dead law. You'll need your ledgers."

"Where will you be?"

"Here." He smiled, and it was an old smile, worn smooth like river stone. "In the margins. Where the best truths always hide."

He went, quietly, through the broken doorway, into the night, and Tamsin sat alone in the counting hall with her cold tea and her covenant and the enormous, terrifying lightness of a woman with nothing left to hide behind.

After a while, she pulled a fresh ledger toward her—the good one, the one meant to be read by anyone—and opened it to the first page.

Her pen hovered. For ten years, every entry she had ever written had been coded, hedged, deniable, safe. Now the page lay open under the lamplight, blank and public and patient.

She wrote:

*Year One of the Covenant. Inventory of losses: one guild charter (burned), one guildmaster (buried with honors), one identity (released by choice). Inventory of gains: one god (freed), one apprentice (promising), one brother (returned), one city (awake, unsettled, ours). Balance outstanding: everything else.*

*Note to whoever reads this, which is everyone: we are not loyal because we swore. We are loyal because we stay, and check, and write it down. That is the whole covenant. The rest is bookkeeping.*

She blotted the page, and listened to the quiet, and somewhere above the tarped roof the stars were coming out over a city with no oaths in it—and on the doorstep below, she heard small feet on the stair, and Wren's voice calling up, breathless: "Guildmaster! There's someone at the gate—a whole delegation—from the palace—and they're asking for the woman who freed the god, and they say—" the child paused, catching her breath, grinning, "—they say they've brought *terms*."
