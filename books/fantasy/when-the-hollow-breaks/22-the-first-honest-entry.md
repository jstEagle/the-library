## 22. The First Honest Entry

The guildhouse smelled of lime and new-cut pine when Tamsin unlocked the doors for the first time in seven months.

She had chosen to do it alone, before dawn, before anyone could see her hesitate. The old doors had burned with the east wing—Selke's fire had eaten through them like a hungry thing given permission—and what stood in their place was pale, unscarred wood that still whispered of sap and sawdust. Someone had carved a lintel above them already: *Guild of Impossible Things*, and beneath it, smaller, in letters that could not be mistaken for anything but deliberate:

*Charter of Witness. Est. by consent.*

Not *by oath*. She had argued about that word for three nights with the surviving partners, and Oren's ghost had argued loudest of all, though he was nine months dead and buried beneath the pear tree in the courtyard where he'd once claimed he would never be caught resting.

*By consent*, she had insisted. Because oaths were chains now, and everyone knew it, and the whole city knew it, and pretending otherwise would be like hanging a sign that said *nothing has changed here* over a door through which everything had changed.

Tamsin stood on the threshold with the key still in the lock and let herself feel it: the fear, small and stubborn as a splinter, that opening these doors again was a mistake. That the crown would come back. That Dain—

No. She set that thought down the way she set down a ledger at the end of a long day, gently, deliberately, knowing she could pick it up again if she needed to.

Dain was dead. He had died in the dark beneath the river with his blade turned against his own order, buying her eleven minutes with his life, and the last thing he had said to her was not an apology or an accusation but a number—the combination to the vault door, spoken like a boy reciting a lesson he'd finally learned. She had written it into no ledger. Some entries belonged only to memory.

She turned the key. The new lock turned smoothly, obediently, without the old complaint of rust, and the doors swung inward onto a hall that was both the guildhouse she had served for ten years and something entirely new.

* * *

They had rebuilt around the bones of the old place. The great hearth remained, its stones smoke-blackened but whole, and the long counting table remained, scarred with a decade of knife-points and inkwells and one memorable incident involving a bottled storm that had gotten loose during an audit. But the walls were different. Where the old guildhouse had kept its records behind iron and secrecy—ledgers chained to shelves, cipher pages locked in drawers, knowledge hoarded because knowledge was leverage—these walls held shelves that faced outward, toward the light of the tall windows, and every shelf was open.

Anyone could walk in off Copperlane Street and read any book in this room.

That had been the hardest clause of the charter to win. The partners had fought it harder than they'd fought the question of whether to shelter gifted folk at all, which told Tamsin something true and uncomfortable about the guild she loved: it had always been easier for them to risk their lives than their secrets. Secrets were the real currency. Everything else was accounting.

But the god had been caged by secrets—by oaths sworn in darkness and payments recorded in cipher, by ten centuries of kings who understood that the surest prison is the kind built from other people's loyalty. And Tamsin had spent one unbearable night inside his memory, hearing his voice scream through the walls of law that bound him, and she had come out of that night knowing exactly one thing with perfect clarity:

Nothing gets imprisoned twice if the first imprisonment is public.

So the shelves faced the windows. So the ledgers lay open. So the guild's oldest cipher—the one her mother had used, the one that had unraveled the first kings' conspiracy entry by patient entry—was reproduced in plain script on a placard beside the hearth, so that any child could learn it and understand that codes are just truths wearing coats, and coats can be taken off.

"You're talking to the room again."

Wren stood in the doorway with two cups of chicory steaming in her hands and her braid coming loose the way it always did by midmorning, and she wore the guild's gray apprentice sash with the particular self-consciousness of someone who had never expected to live long enough to hold a job. Fifteen months ago she had been cargo—a terrified girl smuggled through tollgates in a grain cart whose gift flared whenever she was frightened, which was often. Now she could walk into a room full of strangers and keep her gift folded close as a letter, and she did the accounts faster than Tamsin did, though she rounded up too generously on charitable debts and Tamsin had decided not to correct her.

"Rehearsing," Tamsin said, taking her cup. "First impressions matter."

"It's me."

"I know. I'm rehearsing anyway." She looked out at the hall—at the shelves, the table, the placard, the pear tree visible through the courtyard window with its new grave-shaped shadow of roots—and felt the morning settle around her shoulders like a coat that fit. "Today we write the first entry."

Wren's eyes went wide. "In the *open* ledger? The one that goes on the stand?"

"The one that goes on the stand."

"What if we get it wrong?"

Tamsin smiled. It still surprised her sometimes, how easily smiling came now, how much cheaper it was than it used to be. For ten years she had rationed her own face, her own voice, her own gift, spending nothing that might betray her. Frugality had kept her alive; it had also made her poor. "Then someone will read it and tell us," she said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The stand stood near the door where the light fell best: a lectern of dark oak, older than the guild itself, salvaged from the ruins of the Hollow.

Tamsin had gone back down there once, in the spring, with Wren and a company of masons and three very nervous representatives of the Crown's new Office of Covenant. What had been a cathedral-sized prison was now a cathedral-sized absence—the chains of written law dissolved, the god gone up into the world like water into air, leaving behind stone walls scored with the ghost-letters of every oath ever sworn to him. The masons wanted to quarry the walls. The Office wanted to seal the site. Tamsin had wanted only to stand in the silence where the screaming had been, and listen, and find that the silence was not empty but attentive—the way a room feels after someone you love has left it.

They had taken the lectern from the antechamber where the first kings' clerks had once recorded their payments to *keep the Hollow shut*. Nobody objected. It seemed right that a piece of furniture built for secret accounting should spend its second life holding honest books.

At noon the doors opened, and the city came in.

They came slowly at first, the way people approach any animal they've been taught to fear. A fishwife who had known Tamsin's mother. Two clerks from the Chancery, sent officially to observe and unofficially, Tamsin suspected, to steal techniques. A woman with the storm-gift who had spent the hunt hidden in a root cellar and now walked upright in daylight for the first time in a year, blinking like something newly hatched. Children, inevitably—children always found the open door first, drawn by the novelty of a building full of books with no locks on them, and Wren stationed herself among them within the hour, showing them how the cipher worked, letting them sound out the words *the kings paid to keep him silent* with the solemn delight of children reading forbidden things that weren't forbidden anymore.

By late afternoon the hall hummed. Not with the old guild's murmur—deal-making, debt-hiding, the low music of leverage—but with something rougher and more alive: argument. Reading aloud. Questions asked of strangers. A man disputing an entry from 214 years ago with a woman who happened to have the gift of touching truth in old paper, and both of them laughing by the end of it, because being wrong in front of witnesses had turned out to be survivable, even pleasant, once the witnesses weren't looking for reasons to burn you.

Tamsin watched all of it from the counting table with her mother's strongbox open before her.

Inside lay the prayer-token, humming its faint warm note against the cedar lining, and beside it the original cipher pages she had carried out of the burning archive clutched against her ribs while half the guild's history went up in smoke. She had meant, months ago, to deposit them here among the open shelves. She found she couldn't yet. Perhaps she never would. Some inheritances were meant to be displayed, and some were meant to be carried, and she was learning to tell the difference—not by rule, but by the slow education of living.

Her mother had carried hers in secret for thirty years and died with it unspent. Tamsin had carried hers for ten and spent it all in one night, and what remained afterward had frightened her worse than the burning: the hollow where her mercenary vow had lived. She had sworn that oath at seventeen, kneeling in the mud outside a border fort, and it had structured her like a spine—*I am Tamsin Thornbury, sworn blade of the Guild of Impossible Things, and my word is iron.* When the god woke and every oath sworn to him came loose, hers had come loose too, and for weeks she had woken in the mornings reaching for the shape of that identity and finding only smooth emptiness, a socket where a tooth had been.

It had taken Wren, of all people, to name the cure. The girl had looked at her across a campfire in the smoking spring countryside and said, with the brutal economy of the young: *"You keep saying 'I was.' Try saying 'I am' and see what happens."*

What happened was this chapter. This charter. This open door.

* * *

Evening came, and the crowd thinned, and the last visitors left with borrowed books tucked under their arms—borrowed against nothing but their names, written in the register, trusted because trust was the whole experiment and someone had to go first.

Tamsin sat alone at the counting table with the new ledger open before her.

It was a beautiful object; she had paid for beauty deliberately. Bound in undyed calfskin, sewn with linen thread, its pages ruled in faint blue lines that would fade gracefully rather than fight the ink. On its cover, tooled in blind—that is, invisible until the light caught it—ran a single line of text: *To be read by anyone.*

She dipped her pen. Her hand, the steadiest hand in the guild for fifteen years, hesitated.

Because here was the thing no one had warned her about: honesty is heavier than secrecy. A cipher is easy. A cipher lets you write anything, because no one will ever check it against the world. But a page that says *this is true, come and see* makes the writer accountable to every reader, forever, and Tamsin had spent her whole adult life avoiding exactly that accountability, hiding behind precision the way other people hid behind silence.

She thought of Oren, who would have hated this ledger and then, after a decent interval of hating it, would have defended it with his life. She thought of Selke, who had sold them all for her family's safety and whom Tamsin visited once a month in the penitent's house outside the walls, bringing accounts to audit because Selke's mind was still the finest in the guild and punishment need not mean waste. She thought of Dain, who had chosen truth at the last possible moment, paying for it with everything, and who would never know that the covenant they'd built had a clause shaped exactly like his sacrifice: *no more oaths, only witness.*

And she thought of the god, wherever he moved now—in rain, in rumor, in the strange good dreams the city reported having—and of the terms she had negotiated with him on the ash-strewn night the war ended. He had wanted worship restored. She had offered him something better and stranger: attention. Gifted folk as record-keepers of divine truth, guild-ledgers reborn as public law, a standing covenant between the divine and the mortal built not on kneeling but on *noticing*. He had laughed—a sound like the first thaw—and said, *You drive a hard bargain, little keeper,* and she had said, *I learned from the best,* meaning her mother, meaning the kings, meaning him, all at once.

The pen touched the page.

**Entry the First — Guild of Impossible Things, Charter of Witness**

*Date: the fourth day of Harvestmonth, in the first year of the Covenant.*

*Opened by: Tamsin Thornbury, guildmaster by election of the partners, formerly ledger-keeper, formerly sworn blade, formerly (for one night and one night only) the token-bearer of prophecy, a title I did not ask for and have formally declined.*

*Present: myself, and Wren of Copperlane, apprentice, who was once entered in these archives as impossible cargo and is hereby re-entered as staff.*

*Purpose of this ledger: to record the guild's debts, holdings, and doings in plain script, in plain language, for any reader whatsoever. There is no cipher. There is no sealed section. If we ever add one, may whoever reads this line call us liars to our faces, loudly, in this hall.*

*Statement of account, being the true history of this guild as best I can render it: We trafficked in impossibilities for two hundred years and called ourselves honest because our numbers balanced. They did balance. That was never the same as being honest. We kept secrets that kept a god in chains, and we did not know it, and ignorance was our income. This is not an accusation against the dead, most of whom were good people working in the dark they were given. It is an entry, and entries exist so that the next reader does better than the last writer.*

*Assets: one rebuilt hall; one open archive; one pear tree of excellent temper; forty-one partners and apprentices, of whom six bear gifts, openly listed herein by name and nature, beginning with mine — Tamsin Thornbury, who reads the true history of objects, and who has used this gift today to verify the provenance of the lectern by the door, and reports that it remembers being carried down into the dark by men who whistled while they worked, and does not remember ever being ashamed before, and hopes to get used to it.*

*Liabilities: grief, ongoing. Also a debt to Inquisitor Dain Corvasser, deceased, of a kind that cannot be repaid and therefore will be carried forward indefinitely, accruing nothing, forgiven nothing, remembered everything.*

*Terms of trade going forward: we buy and sell impossibilities as before, but every transaction shall be entered here, in sight of God and street. We shelter the hunted as before, but our sheltering shall be announced, not denied. We keep faith as before, but faith henceforth means witness — seeing truly, recording honestly, and leaving the door open.*

*Which brings me to the last item, and the reason for this entry's length:*

*The door is open.*

*It will stay open. Not because we are safe — we are not, and the Crown's friendship is a weather, not a climate, and there are men in the Chancery tonight who would chain the god again tomorrow if they could find a chain he hadn't already broken. We are not open because it is safe. We are open because closed doors are how the Hollow happened, and I have stood inside the Hollow, and I decline to build another one, starting with this one.*

*Let the record show: on the fourth day of Harvestmonth, the Guild of Impossible Things reopened its doors, and the doors did not shut behind us.*

*Signed,*
*T. Thornbury, guildmaster.*
*Witnessed: Wren, apprentice, who adds — in her own hand, over my objection and with my eventual agreement — the following:*

*"We were never impossible. We were just locked away. Come in."*

Tamsin sat back and read it through twice, hunting for errors the way she had hunted them for fifteen years, and found none that mattered, and found several that did—places where the truth was rougher than the phrasing, where a kinder sentence had almost replaced an accurate one—and she corrected them, because the whole point was the correcting, done in the open, where anyone could watch a woman change her mind.

Wren blew out the lamps one by one, leaving only the hearth and the last of the dusk. Together they set the ledger on its stand, squared it, and weighted the pages with two smooth river stones so the evening breeze off Copperlane could turn them if it liked.

At the door, Tamsin paused with her hand on the new wood—pale, unscarred, whispering of beginnings—and looked back at the hall: the shelves facing the light, the placard by the hearth, the open book gleaming faintly in the fire-glow like a held breath about to become speech.

Somewhere above the rooftops, in the space between the stars where a freed god drifted like weather, something noticed her noticing it, and the night grew briefly, warmly attentive, the way a room feels when someone you love has come home.

Tamsin smiled, pulled the door wide instead of shut, and left it that way.

Outside, on the step, someone had left a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. She knelt and unwrapped it by hearthlight spilling through the doorway: a child's coat, worn thin at the elbows, smelling of river-smoke and fear—and pinned to its collar, a scrap of paper in a shaking hand.

*They say you take the impossible. Please. My daughter was born humming.*
