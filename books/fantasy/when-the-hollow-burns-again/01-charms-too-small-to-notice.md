## 1. Charms Too Small to Notice

The frost on Felix's stall had patterns in it, and every morning she spent a quarter hour deciding whether to melt them.

Today she let them stay. Frost sold well in Vaelmoor. Nobody wanted to buy warmth from a merchant whose table was bare and bright with it—warmth implied fire, fire implied a hearth, and a hearth implied a woman who could coax heat out of nothing, which was precisely the sort of woman the crown had been hanging from the Salt Gate for fifteen years. So the frost stayed, pretty and harmless, and beneath it, hidden in the lining of her gloves where no inquisitor's iron ever thought to look, Felix Iversen kept the smallest magic in the city.

She called them charms too small to notice. That was the craft of it, really—the not-noticing. A luck-knot was just a knot if you tied it right: three loops of grey wool, each one closed with a breath and a wish so faint it barely qualified as speech. A warming sigil was a smudge of tallow on a mitten's cuff, worked in while the buyer was still haggling over the price. She never sold anything that glowed. She never sold anything that hummed. If you held one of her pieces up to your ear, all you heard was wool.

"Two coppers for the mittens," she said now, to a fishwife with cheeks like raw meat. "Three if you want the good lacing."

"The good lacing."

"It doesn't unravel when the wind gets spiteful." Which was true, because Felix had asked the wind, politely, to leave the lacing alone, and the wind—bored, half-starved, like everything else in Vaelmoor these days—had agreed.

The fishwife paid three coppers and shuffled off into the white murk of Tanner's Row, and Felix tucked the coins into a box that had once held her mother's sewing needles and looked out at her market.

It was a poor market. It had been a poor market for fifteen years, ever since the Edict of Ash came down and the guildhalls were sealed and anyone caught selling *worked* goods was invited to reconsider their profession at the end of a rope. But people still needed luck the way they needed bread, and bread was scarce, and so every morning the same gray procession came through: the fishwife, the lamplighter with his cough, the widow Brann who bought a new knot every week because she was convinced the old ones wore out, though Felix had never told her they didn't. They came because their joints ached and their children cried at night and the winter had lasted so long that most of them could no longer remember a year with a summer in it.

Felix could remember. She'd been nine when the last true spring failed, old enough to recall green things growing without asking permission. Her mother had carried her through the crowd at the sealing of the guildhall, up on her shoulders so she could see the covenant stone behind the altar, and her mother's hands had been shaking—not with fear, Felix understood much later, but with something worse. Recognition.

*Don't look at it too long, little wren,* her mother had said. *Some doors remember being opened.*

Then the Edict, then the years of small careful work, then the fever, then the grave in the paupers' field where the ground was too frozen to dig proper depth, and Felix alone at a stall on Tanner's Row selling knots that were only knots unless you knew how to listen.

She was wrapping a set of ear-warmers for the lamplighter—he paid in lamp-oil, which was its own small illegality—when the crier came.

You heard him before you saw him. The royal criers wore bells on their boots so the crowd would part, and in Vaelmoor the crowd always parted quickly, because the crier only came to Tanner's Row for two reasons: tax assessments and names.

He stopped at the fountain, which hadn't flowed in eleven years, and unrolled his scroll, and the bells on his boots went quiet, and the whole market seemed to lean toward him the way wheat leans before a scythe.

"Hear ye," he said, in the flat voice of a man reading a list he'd read too many times. "By decree of the Crown and the Office of the Hollow Inquisition. The guild known as the Iversen Compact, keepers of the knot-craft, is hereby marked for the Hollow Hunt. Members are summoned to render account at the Salt Gate within three days. Those who do not render account will be sought. Those who are found will be burned by name."

He rolled the scroll up. The bells started again. He moved off down the row, and behind him the market did not scream or scatter, because markets in Vaelmoor had learned that screaming attracted attention. It simply went quiet, the way a house goes quiet when something large passes outside the door.

Felix kept wrapping the ear-warmers. Her hands did not shake. Fifteen years of practice had taught her hands better than that.

But inside, somewhere below the ribs, an old door she'd kept locked since she was nine years old swung open, and cold air came through it.

*Iversen.* Not the trade-name she used at market—Ivory, she called herself, Ivory of Tanner's Row, seller of woolens. The crier had said *Iversen*. Someone had given them the name. Someone had given them more than the name.

She finished the ear-warmers. She took the lamplighter's oil. She smiled at the widow Brann and sold her a knot, and tied it properly, three loops and a breath, and did not let her voice shake on the wish. Then she packed her stall with the unhurried precision of a woman who had rehearsed this morning in her head a hundred times without ever believing it would come, and pulled her hood up, and walked home through streets that suddenly felt full of eyes.

* * *

Her rooms were above a chandler's shop on Candle Lane, two floors of cold plaster that smelled of tallow and old smoke. She lit no lamps. She sat in the dark by the window with her gloves off, watching the street, and that was where Old Maren found her.

Maren had been coming to her for nine years—for salve for her husband's lungs, worked so gently it was barely work at all—and she climbed the stairs now with the laborious care of a woman whose knees had opinions, and did not knock, and stood in the dark doorway breathing hard.

"They came to my house," Maren said.

Felix turned. In the window-light her face must have shown something, because the old woman crossed the room and took her hands, and her grip was strong as a trap.

"Two of them. Gray cloaks, iron pins shaped like a flame. They had a book, girl. An old book, leather gone soft, writing in brown ink. And they asked me—" Maren's breath caught. "They asked me if I knew a signatory. That word. *Signatory.* Like it was a title."

"What did you tell them?"

"That I buy woolens from a woman named Ivory and know nothing else." Maren's thumbs pressed into Felix's palms, hard. "But they weren't asking me, child. They were checking me. Seeing what I'd say. Because they already have what they need—they showed me the flyleaf. There was a name written there in that brown ink, and a line under it, and the line wasn't ink. It was pressed into the page, like a brand."

Felix's mouth had gone dry. "Whose name?"

"You know whose name." Maren's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Iversen. Your mother's hand, girl, I'd know it anywhere—she wrote my marriage lines, thirty years back. And under it, the mark of the Hollow. And beside them, a man came after, tall, gray-eyed, wearing white under the gray. He didn't ask me anything at all. He just looked at my hands, like he was counting fingers. Then he said—" the old woman swallowed— "he said, *the daughter sells charms too small to notice. Tell her we've noticed.*"

The words settled into the cold room like snow through a broken roof.

"They have the ledger," Maren whispered. "Whatever it is, whatever she wrote in it—they have it, and they know about you, and they're letting you run, child. Do you understand? Three days. They gave you three days. Inquisitors don't give time unless time serves them."

Felix looked down at her own hands in the old woman's grip. Hands that had tied ten thousand knots, each one a small treason, each one a prayer too quiet for the crown to hear. Her mother's hands, the shape of them. Her mother's craft. Her mother's ledger, sitting now on some inquisitor's desk, open to a page with a brand burned into the vellum.

And under the fear, rising slow as water through a floor, another feeling—one she hadn't let herself feel in fifteen years, because hope was the most dangerous thing a marked woman could carry.

*Some doors remember being opened.*

"Maren," she said quietly. "Do you remember the old guildhall? On the cliff road. What's left of it."

The old woman stared at her. "That ruin? Nothing's left but stones and drafts."

"Good," said Felix Iversen, and reached for her cloak. "Drafts remember things too."
