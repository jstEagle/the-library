## 2. The Ledger of Signatories

The guild hall had burned twice in Felix's lifetime—once during the Purge, once for spite—and what remained of it crouched at the end of Tanner's Row like a beggar with his hands cut off. She reached it as the bells rang the third hour past midnight, her breath tearing out of her in white rags, the stolen ledger pressed against her ribs beneath a coat that no longer kept anything warm. Snow came down thick and silent, filling the doorless archway, drifting over the fallen lintel where someone had once carved *Iversen* in letters meant to outlast kingdoms.

She climbed through the gap that had been a window and stood in the great hall's skeleton, rafters open to the sky, stars burning cold above the blackened floor. Her boots crunched on glass and char. Fifteen years she'd walked past this ruin, selling her little charms two streets over, telling herself the guild's death was history, finished, buried. Now the crier's words followed her through the dark: *the Iversen guild, marked for the Hollow Hunt, by name.*

By name. Not by trade. By blood.

She found the ledger room by memory she didn't know she had—a narrow chamber behind the dais, its stone shelf still standing, its iron box still locked. The lock surrendered to a warming sigil traced quick against the hasp, metal groaning, ice splitting. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark: ledgers. Dozens. And one, bound in grey calfskin, unmarked except for three small burns pressed into the cover like brands.

Her mother's hand was on the first page.

Felix sat down on the frozen floor because her legs would not hold her, and read.

*The Covenant of the Hollow,* the ledger began, in ink faded to the color of old bruises, *binds warmth to warmth. As the Hollow gave its fire to the realm, so the realm gives back its keepers: three signatories, three relics held in trust, renewed each solstice while the pact endures.* Names followed—her mother's name among them, Maerin Ivensen, written in a younger, prouder script than Felix had ever seen. *Should a relic fail of renewal, should a signatory break faith or die faithless, the Hollow withdraws. Winter follows. Winter keeps.*

There were diagrams. A ring, drawn in fine lines, annotated *the Drowned One's token — held beneath still water.* A bone-needle, *the Traitor's penance — carried closest to the heart.* And a third mark that was not an object at all but a spiral of ink, labeled only *the blood of the last line, freely given.*

Felix stared at that last entry until the letters swam. Then she turned pages faster, hunting for dates, corrections, anything—and found them, marginalia in her mother's tightening hand across years: *renewal made. renewal late. renewal made under protest.* And then, in the final winter before the ban, a single line pressed so hard the nib had torn the page:

*They will make us swear we never knew it. Let them. But the pact starves without its keepers, and when it breaks, it will be my daughter who pays.*

* * *

The flashback took her the way cold water takes a swimmer.

She was nine again, kneeling on the kitchen floor of the house on Bellwether Lane while snow ticked against the shutters, watching her mother sew. Not charms—Felix knew every stitch of charmwork by then—but something else, something long, a seam being picked open in a garment that had no opening. Her mother's fingers shook. That was the frightening part. Maerin Ivensen never shook.

"You'll remember tonight," her mother said quietly, not looking up, "even if you pretend you don't."

"Remember what?"

"That I was a coward." The needle paused. "That I loved my life more than my word." She set the sewing aside and took Felix's face in both cold hands. "Listen to me, little wren. There is a place under the world that burns, and it is kind, and we were sworn to tend its kindness, and I let it go hungry. Fifteen years from now—or thirty—you will stand somewhere cold and learn what I did. When that day comes, I need you to know that I knew. I knew exactly what it cost, and I paid it anyway, because the price of keeping faith was going to be your life, and I chose mine."

"Mama, I don't understand."

"I know." Her mother kissed her forehead, and her lips were colder than the room. "Forgive me anyway. Forgive me and finish it. The ring beneath still water. The needle nearest the heart. And the last—" her voice broke, just barely, "—the last will find you on its own."

Then the kitchen was gone, and Felix was alone in the ruined hall with snow sifting through the broken roof onto the open ledger, landing on her mother's name and melting there like tears.

* * *

"She reads fast, your mother said. Pity it runs in the family."

Felix was on her feet before the voice finished, fire coiling up her arms, ready to spend fifteen years of hoarded power in one breath—and stopped, because the woman leaning in the doorway wanted her to stop.

Serah Vane was smaller than the rumors made her. Grey-eyed, plain-dressed, unhurried, with frost melting off her shoulders in slow drops, which meant she had walked through the storm without warding herself and hadn't bothered to notice. In one gloved hand she carried a lantern; in the other, nothing at all. She didn't need anything. Everyone in Vaelmoor knew what Vane's empty hand could do.

"The ledger stays," Felix said. Her voice sounded steadier than she felt. "You have your own copy. Burn me instead."

"I've read yours. Yours is better annotated." Vane stepped into the hall, and the snow seemed to lean away from her. "Maerin Ivensen. Third signatory. Died owing a debt to a god of hearths, and left the bill to a daughter who sells luck-knots at market." Something moved through the grey eyes—not mockery. Envy, maybe. Or memory. "Do you know what I did before the crown gave me a white cloak? I was hedge-taught. Little charms. Warmth-sigils. Nothing worth the pyre, until the ban made everything worth the pyre."

"Then you know what you're hunting."

"I know precisely what I'm hunting." Vane set the lantern on the dais, and its light crawled up the blackened walls. "Here is what happens next, Ivensen, so pay attention. Tomorrow I burn Aldous Renn of the Weftmakers. The day after, the Corrow sisters. By solstice there will be eleven names on the pyre list, and I will keep my promise to the crown about every one of them—unless."

"Unless."

"Unless you bring me the three relics. The ring, the needle, the blood. You give them into crown custody, the covenant dies unreforged, the winter stays forever—and I strike the guild rolls clean. Every mage in Vaelmoor walks free. Every pyre goes cold." Vane smiled, and it was almost gentle. "You can't reforge it alone anyway. You know that. Three relics, one keeper, no guild to stand witness. Your mother's failure, completed at last, tidily. And everyone lives."

"Everyone except whatever the Hollow warms."

"The Hollow has been dead fifteen years. Nobody mourns it." Vane picked up the lantern again. "You have until solstice eve. Bring the relics to the threshold below Harrowfell, or I start lighting fires alphabetically. Renn first. He has children."

She turned toward the broken window, then paused, half-lit, frost smoking off her shoulders.

"For what it's worth—I read your mother's confession. All of it. She chose wrong, and she knew it, and she wrote it down so you'd choose differently." The grey eyes found Felix's across the ruined hall. "I'm offering you the chance to be right, Ivensen. Take it."

Then she was gone, down through the falling snow, and the hall was quiet again except for the wind moving through the rafters like breath through teeth.

Felix stood a long time with the ledger open in her hands, her mother's torn page under her thumb, the three relic-diagrams ghosting in the lantern light. *Everyone lives,* Vane had said. Except the city, slowly freezing. Except the mages freed today, burned next year, when the crown found new reasons. Except her mother, whose debt would never be paid.

Except everyone, in the end.

But Renn had children. Eleven names. Solstice eve.

Felix closed the ledger, pulled her collar up against the cold that suddenly felt older than weather, and went out into the snow—toward the river, where a drowned friend's ring waited beneath still water, and where, if her mother's margins were honest, something else waited too: the truth of what her mother had actually hidden, all those years, in the house on Bellwether Lane.

The needle nearest the heart. She already feared she knew whose heart.
