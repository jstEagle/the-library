## 3. What the Winter Keeps

The bells of Vaelmoor rang wrong that morning—three strokes, then silence, then three again. Felix had grown up knowing the city's bells the way other children knew their mothers' voices: the dawn peal from Saint Aldric's, the market chime, the slow mourning toll when the river took someone. This was none of them. This was the Hunt's rhythm, and every mage in the city knew what it meant.

*They are burning the guild by name.*

She pulled her hood low and moved with the market crowd, a woman carrying nothing but a basket of tallow candles, unremarkable as frost. Behind her, smoke rose from the Weavers' Row in a column too black and too steady to be anything but pyres. She did not look back. Looking back was how they caught you.

The ledger's words were still inked behind her eyes, her mother's cramped hand reciting itself like a prayer she'd never been allowed to learn:

*Three relics bind the pact anew: the ring of the drowned, given freely; the needle of the traitor, surrendered without hate; the blood of the last signatory, spilled at the stone. Bring these to the Hollow's threshold on solstice eve, or the winter keeps us all.*

Two days. The solstice was tomorrow.

Her first stop was the river.

Maren had been dead eleven years, drowned beneath the ice of the Vael with Felix's name still warm on her lips—the last thing she'd said, some swore, though Felix had never dared ask anyone to repeat it. Her family kept a shrine-house on the Salt Quay, one of those narrow places where the drowned were remembered so the river wouldn't forget them. Felix climbed the frozen steps and knocked, and Maren's mother opened the door looking older than grief should have made her.

"You," the old woman said. Not surprise. Something worse. Recognition.

"Mistress Corren, I—"

"She told me you'd come." The woman stepped aside. "Eleven years ago, before the ice. She said, *if the winter never breaks, my daughter will knock on your door.* I thought it was fever-talk." Her hands shook as she lifted a small box from the mantel. Inside, on a bed of river-silt gone to dust, lay a silver ring worn thin as thread. "She gave it back to me the day before she went out on the water. Said it wasn't hers anymore. Said it belonged to the winter."

Felix reached for it, and stopped. *Given freely.* The ledger hadn't been poetry. The relic would know the difference between a theft and a gift.

"I can't pay you for it," Felix said. "If I pay, it isn't freely given."

Maren's mother pressed the box into her hands and closed Felix's fingers over it with both of her own, cracked and cold as driftwood. "You think I want coin? Girl, I've watched this city freeze for fifteen years while priests tell me my daughter's death was the price of order. Take it. And when you stand wherever you're going—" her voice caught, "—tell the Hollow Maren Corren loved someone stubborn enough to come back."

Felix held the ring against her sternum like a second heartbeat and ran.

* * *

Serah Vane watched the Weavers' Row burn from the balcony of the Inquisition House, and felt nothing she would have called pleasure. Pleasure was imprecise. What she felt was closer to arithmetic: nine guild members taken this week, six burned, three fled beyond the walls where the cold would do the crown's work for free. The ledger of signatories sat open on the table beside her, its pages soft as cloth from two centuries of handling, and she read the names again the way she read everything—looking for the flaw.

*Iversen, Aveline. Signatory by descent. Covenant-keeper presumptive.*

Aveline Iversen had been dead four years. Which left the daughter, who had slipped through the ruined hall like smoke while Vane stood in the doorway offering her a bargain, and who had apparently decided that running was wiser than listening.

Vane could respect that. She had run once herself, long ago, from a house in the low country where her gift had shown itself early and hungry. She knew what it cost to be something the law had outlawed. That was precisely why she could not allow the covenant to be reforged—not because she hated mages, but because she understood them better than any of them understood themselves. Power that answered to blood would always, *always*, answer to blood first. She had watched the old pact strangle a kingdom's warmth for fifteen years on the word of dead women. She would not watch it reborn.

"Bring me the Iversen girl alive," she told Captain Doss. "And if the choice is between damaged and lost, bring me damaged. She knows things about that ledger no living person should."

Doss hesitated. He was a decent man, which made him useful and tiresome in equal measure. "The pyres, my lady? The remaining names—"

"Burn them tonight. All of them." She turned back to the fire-glow over the rooftops. "If the girl is gathering relics, let her arrive at the threshold with nothing left to save but herself. Despair makes people reasonable."

* * *

Uncle Tam lived above a shuttered dye-house in Tanners' Walk, in a room that smelled of vinegar and fear. He didn't open the door until Felix spoke the family words through the wood, and even then he opened it only wide enough to show one eye and the glint of a knife.

"You shouldn't have come here."

"The ledger names you, Tam. You signed the covenant's witness-roll. You owe the needle."

"I owe the needle to staying *alive*." But his eye flicked past her shoulder, down the stairwell, and she understood—he'd already sold her name once, or meant to. The bone-needle hung at his throat on a leather cord, yellowed, ancient, the kind of small ugly thing that outlives empires.

"They're burning people, Tam. The crier read the names this morning. Yours is on the roll too—they just haven't reached Tanners' Walk yet."

That got the door open. He pulled her inside, barred it, and stood breathing hard in the dark. "I signed because your mother asked. I witnessed because refusing her was like refusing weather. And then the ban came, and the Hollow went silent, and I told myself the needle was just a needle." His hand closed around it. "It isn't, is it. It hums."

"It does."

He looked at her then, really looked, and whatever he saw—her mother's jaw, maybe, or the frost-light of her suppressed gift bleeding through her skin—made him laugh, short and broken. "Surrendered without hate," he said, reading her expression. "I know the terms. I helped copy them, God help me." He lifted the cord over his head. For a moment his fist stayed clenched around the needle, knuckles white, and Felix braced for him to bolt, or bargain, or weep.

Instead he set it in her palm and folded her fingers shut, the same gesture Maren's mother had used, and Felix thought: *this is how the old ones passed things. Hand to hand. Warmth to warmth.*

"Your mother forgave me before I ever asked," Tam said. "Don't you dare carry it for her."

She left him barring the door behind her, and did not see the paper he burned in his stove after she'd gone—the paper with Serah Vane's seal on it, the one promising him leniency for the name of his niece.

* * *

By dusk the bells had stopped entirely, and the silence was worse.

Felix ran the last streets with the ring at her throat and the needle sewn into her cuff, past shuttered windows and snow going grey with ash. From the direction of the Chandlers' Hall came light—not candlelight, nothing so kind—and screaming that cut off mid-note, and she forced herself not to stop, not to turn, *not to count the names*. Grief was a luxury with a deadline. The Hollow waited at the city's edge, past the old wall, past the fields where nothing grew, in the sunken vale the maps called a quarry and the oldest maps called something else.

Her own blood was the easy relic. It had been promised since before she was born.

She reached the threshold as the last light failed, and the world changed texture. The vale below was not a quarry. It was a wound in the earth shaped like a throat, and from it rose heat—the first true heat Felix had felt in fifteen years, rising like breath from something enormous and sleeping. Snow fell upward at the rim. Ice grew in patterns too deliberate to be natural, spiraling inward like a written invitation.

At the center stood the covenant stone, taller than a man, older than the kingdom, its surface crawling with names.

And standing before it, alone, unhurried, her white cloak dusted with falling-upward snow, was Serah Vane.

"You're late," Vane said, almost gently. "Though I suppose grief makes poor timekeeping."

Felix's hand went to the knife at her belt before her mind caught up. "The bargain—"

"Was real when I offered it." Vane stepped aside from the stone, and Felix saw what she'd been doing: fresh chalk lines, precise as surgery, circling the ancient carvings. "But bargains are for equals, and you've spent the day learning what I learned years ago—that the covenant doesn't need a reforging. It needs an *heir*. Someone to hold the terms. Someone strong enough to keep the winter disciplined instead of merely endured." Her pale eyes fixed on Felix, and there was no cruelty in them, which was somehow far more frightening. "Give me the relics. Speak the rite naming me keeper of the renewed pact. Do that, and every mage in Vaelmoor lives—including you. Refuse, and I'll take the relics from your body and name myself anyway, and your friends will have died for a girl too proud to save them."

The heat from the Hollow breathed against Felix's face. In her pocket, the ring seemed to pulse. At her wrist, the needle hummed.

Her mother's voice, from the flashback that would not leave her: *A covenant is not a cage, little fox. If ever it becomes one, break it rather than feed it.*

"And if I say no?" Felix asked.

Vane smiled, thin as the ice on the Vael. "Then we find out which of us the winter prefers."

Behind them, at the rim of the vale, torchlight bloomed—Doss and his men, arriving with the last hours of the solstice, and somewhere among those torches, Felix knew, the ashes of everyone whose names she'd failed to reach.

She walked toward the stone.
