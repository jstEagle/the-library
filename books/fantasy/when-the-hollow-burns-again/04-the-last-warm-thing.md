## 4. The Last Warm Thing

The covenant stone rose out of the ice like a drowned ship's keel—black, veined with silver, older than the city that had forgotten it. Felix knelt before it on solstice eve with the three relics laid out in the snow: a ring gone green with river-water, a bone-needle snapped at the eye, and a shallow clay bowl of her own blood, already freezing at the edges.

The Hollow breathed around her. Not wind—the Hollow did not make wind—but a slow exhalation of cold that came up through the ground itself, through the soles of her boots, through the marrow of her. Fifteen years she had felt it as a pressure behind every small charm she sold, the sense that all her little magics were candles lit in a house where something vast was starving.

Behind her, boots crunched snow. She did not turn.

"You came," Serah Vane said.

"You knew I would." Felix kept her eyes on the stone. "You let me gather them. A ring from Maren's grave. A needle from my uncle's hand—I had to take it from his *hand*, Vane. He's still alive. I want you to know that. Whatever else happens tonight, he's alive because I was faster than you."

"I know," Vane said quietly. "I counted on it."

Felix turned then. The inquisitor stood at the edge of the firelight her own lantern threw, and for the first time Felix saw her plainly—not the gray figure from the burning-squares, not the voice that read names off ledgers, but a woman perhaps fifty, frost in her eyebrows, eyes red-rimmed from years of looking into pyres. Her hands were bare. No gloves, in this cold. Mage's hands, scarred from burns of their own making.

"Your bargain," Felix said. "The relics for the guild. For every mage you've hunted. You swear it, and I reforge the pact, and you let them live."

"I swear it," Vane said—and then, as Felix reached for the ring, "on one amendment."

There it was. Felix had known it would come; she had carried the knowledge down the mountain like a stone in her belly. Still it landed like a blow.

"The old terms named an heir," Vane said. "A line empowered to hold the Hollow's warmth and dispense it. Your mother held it. Her mother before her. When the covenant broke, that authority didn't die—it *waited*. It waits in that stone right now, and whoever reforges the pact chooses its shape." Her breath fogged and hung strangely in the air, refusing to disperse. "Name me. Name my line. And I will swear whatever oath you like—the mages live, the hunt ends, the winter ends. All of it. You have my word."

"Your word," Felix repeated. "The word of the woman who burned Aldric Fen by name while his apprentices watched."

"Aldric Fen made charms that killed," Vane said, and there it was—not cruelty in her voice but something worse, something worn smooth as river stone. Conviction. "I have burned forty-one people, Felix Iversen. Do you think I don't count them? Every night. I count them, and I tell myself they are fewer than the ones who would freeze if magic stays wild, if power stays in the hands of anyone strong enough to take it. Give the warmth to one accountable line and the counting stops." She stepped closer. Snow did not melt where she walked; it had stopped melting around her years ago, Felix realized. "You've seen what your gift costs to hide. I've seen what it costs to rule. Name me heir, and go home. Open your little stall. Sell your luck-knots. Live."

And there it was—the temptation, offered not as a knife but as an open door. Felix looked at her own hands above the relics. She thought of fifteen years of hiding, of sigils inked beneath her sleeves, of flinching at every crier's bell. One word and she could walk away warm. Walk away *safe*.

She thought of her mother's ledger, and the last entry in her mother's hand, the one Felix had read a hundred times in the ruined hall:

*The pact failed because we kept the warmth for ourselves. Forgive me. Whoever finds this—do not keep it.*

"No," Felix said.

Vane's face did not change. "Think—"

"I've thought for fifteen years." Felix picked up the ring. "That's what makes me faster than you."

* * *

The rite was simpler than the ledger promised. Blood on the stone. Ring upon the blood. Needle through the ring's band, pinning promise to promise. The words were not incantation but plain speech, which somehow made them heavier:

*"Hollow below, hearth above. What was hoarded, be unhoarded. What was bound, be given. Let the warmth flow to every hand or no hand, and let no line hold it again."*

The stone drank the blood. The silver veins kindled—not gold, not fire, but the color of bread baking, of a bath drawn hot, of every small domestic warmth the winter had stolen—and Felix felt her gift rise in her like water answering a drain.

It did not hurt. That was the terrible part. It left the way sleep leaves, gently, completely, and she understood in the last moment of having it that her magic had never truly been hers—it had been a cup dipped into the Hollow's river, and now the river was being given back to everyone.

She said the last line herself, alone, unbound by any text: *"And I surrender mine first."*

The warmth went out of her like a name going out of memory.

She heard Vane scream.

Felix turned. The inquisitor stood rigid, both hands raised toward the stone, fingers hooked as though she could catch the old terms out of the air and force them back into shape—*heir, heir, name me heir*—and the Hollow answered her. Not with warmth. With the winter she had served. Forty-one burnings' worth of cold came home to her at once, all the frost she had spent, all the names she had fed to the fire, and the snow rose up around her in a slow spiral, taking her piece by piece—boots, coat, hands, the red-rimmed eyes still fixed on Felix with something that was not hatred. It might have been relief.

Then there was only a lantern lying in the snow, still burning, and a shape of packed ice that the wind was already softening.

Felix stood alone at the covenant stone until dawn.

* * *

Spring did not come all at once. It came the way truth comes—drip by drip, undeniable.

By the second week of thaw, the eaves of Vaelmoor sang with meltwater. By the third, the canals cracked open and the barges moved again, and people stood on the bridges just to watch brown water flow where white had been. There was no magic in the city anymore. Felix tested it once, privately, pressing her thumb to her wrist where the warming sigil used to sit, and felt nothing but skin. The guild mages wandered the streets like the newly pardoned, touching doorposts, staring at their empty hands, some weeping, some laughing, most doing both.

Felix rented a shop on Candlemakers' Row. Small. One window. She sold nothing but candles—tallow, beeswax, one expensive bayberry pair she kept for herself—and she learned quickly that an ordinary flame, in a city that had spent a generation freezing, was worth more than any charm she'd ever inked.

On the first evening of true spring, she lit the window candle herself, with a match, the ordinary way, and watched the flame stand up straight in the glass.

The bell over the door rang. A girl came in, maybe ten, cheeks pink from the thaw-wind, holding a coin in her fist.

"Do you have anything," the girl asked, very seriously, "that keeps the dark away?"

Felix smiled, and reached for the shelf.
