## 1. The Keeper's Watch

The trouble with guarding a door nobody opens is that you start talking to it.

I know how that sounds. Ten years of it, though, and you'd talk to a door too. Especially this door. The Hollow Gate doesn't look like much from the outside—a seam of black stone in the mountain's shoulder, twice my height and three times my width, veined with silver that might be mineral or might be something older and less willing to explain itself. No handle. No hinges. Just stone, and cold coming off it like the memory of winter.

"Morning," I said to it, because I say that every morning, and because if I ever stopped, I think I'd have to admit what I was really doing out here.

The gate didn't answer. It never does. But the wind through the pass made its usual low moan against the stone lip, which is close enough to conversation as most people get in the Greywatch.

I tightened my coat, checked the lamp at my hip out of habit rather than need—dawn was bleeding grey over the eastern peaks—and started the patrol. South rim first, then the scree field, then down along the old pilgrim road to where the barricades stood, then back up. Four miles of nothing, walked every day for a decade. My boots knew the path better than my thoughts did, which on most mornings was a mercy.

Here's what the crown pamphlets say about the Hollow Gate, if you can find one that mentions it at all: sealed by royal decree after the Vaelmoor Accords, maintained by a hereditary keeper, closed for the safety of both realms. What they don't say is that "hereditary keeper" means one woman with a rusted key ring, a ledger nobody reads, and instructions consisting entirely of *walk the perimeter, log the weather, touch nothing.*

The touching nothing part has always been the hard one for me.

Not because I'm curious. Well—not only because I'm curious. Because some mornings, standing near the gate, something under my skin wakes up and leans toward it like a plant toward light. A warmth in the palms. A hum in the back teeth. Small things. Deniable things. Things I have spent ten years pretending not to notice, the way you pretend not to notice a loose tooth: prodding it only when you're sure no one's watching.

Because here is the other thing the pamphlets don't say: keepers are chosen for more than their stamina. There used to be a word for what runs in my family, before everyone agreed to stop using it. The gift, the old women called it. A little thread of the other country, woven into certain bloodlines back when the two worlds still traded more than silence.

Nobody in Ashford knows. Nobody in Thornfell knows. That's the arrangement I've made with my own life: I keep the gate, and I keep myself shut just as tight, and between the two of us—me and the door—we hold very still, and the world stays simple.

* * *

Doss arrived at midmorning, as he always does, announcing himself from two hundred yards off because a smuggler who sneaks up on an armed keeper deserves whatever befalls him.

"Wren Eriksen!" His voice carried up the switchbacks ahead of his mule. "You look terrible!"

"You say that every time."

"And every time you've had another month of talking to rocks for company, so every time it's truer." He came around the last bend leading Brindle, his mule, who regarded me with the flat disdain only mules and cats have mastered. Doss himself was all elbows and grin, forty-some years old, a face like a dropped pie, and eyes that missed nothing—which is precisely the problem with befriending smugglers.

He unpacked while I made tea on the little brazier in the watch hut. This is our ritual, ours and the mountain's: once a week, sometimes twice, Doss comes up the smuggler's road with salt, iron nails, dried apples, news, and lies, and I trade him what the crown pays me in coin and what the mountain gives me in silence. He thinks I don't know he marks the goods up threefold. I let him. A man needs his dignity, and a keeper needs somebody who remembers her name.

"Ashford's talking," he said, casual as weather, which meant it wasn't casual at all.

"Ashford's always talking."

"No, this time it's got its mouth full." He turned the tin cup in his hands, not drinking. "New criers. Crown men, come up from the capital. Preaching about purity, and the old gift, and how anyone born with a spark of the fae in them is a threat to Thornfell's health." He said it lightly, the way you'd report hail damage. His eyes were somewhere else entirely. "Lists being drawn up, they're saying. Names."

My hands were steady. I made sure of it before I spoke. "Superstition sells. Always has."

"Mm." He drank finally. "Your ledger due any inspections soon?"

"Crown hasn't sent an inspector in six years. They've forgotten the pass exists."

"That so." He looked at me across the steam, and for one long moment I thought—he knows. It's a ridiculous thought. Doss has known me since I was nineteen and green, and in all that time I've given him nothing but competence and complaints. And yet there it was, that flicker, the way his gaze snagged on me like cloth on a nail.

"Well," I said, too loudly, "if they ever do remember, they'll find a gate sealed tighter than a tax collector's heart and a keeper with nothing interesting about her whatsoever."

"Nothing whatsoever," Doss agreed. But he said it the way you agree with someone standing on a frozen lake: carefully, and while watching the ice.

* * *

That afternoon I did the north rim alone, and the wind came down hard off the high snows, and I let myself do the thing I do—the small, forbidden accounting of my own body. Palms: quiet. Teeth: quiet. That place behind my breastbone where the warmth lives, banked down to embers: quiet, quiet, quiet.

Ten years of quiet. You get good at it. You get so good you stop noticing the cost, the way you stop noticing a wall you've leaned against your whole life—until someone asks you to describe the room, and you realize you've been facing the wall the whole time.

I ate supper alone. I logged the weather in the ledger. *Clear. Wind south by west. No traffic.* Under *remarks* I nearly wrote something true, then wrote *gate stable* instead, because the ledger goes in the archive in Ashford someday, and archives are where secrets go to be found.

* * *

It happened that night. It happens rarely enough that I can count the times on one hand and have fingers left over, and each time I've told myself it meant nothing—a trick of the light, a draft, indigestion of the soul.

I went out to check the gate before bed, because I do that, because it's mine, because ten years of walking the same four miles at the same hours builds a kind of prayer out of routine even if you don't believe in anything to pray to. Frost furred the barricade ropes. The stars over Vaelmoor's side of the mountains were the fierce, hard kind, the kind that make you feel like the last lit window in a dark city.

And the gate was humming.

Not audibly. I want to be clear about that—I heard nothing. It was lower than hearing, deeper, a vibration in the ground that climbed up through my boot soles and into my shins and stopped there, waiting, like a dog that knows better than to jump.

I should have walked away. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone honest: Wren Eriksen, keeper of record, knew better, and stayed anyway.

I put my bare hand on the stone.

Cold—cold like a well shaft, cold like something that has been holding its breath. And then the thing behind my breastbone opened its eyes.

Warmth poured out of me before I could clamp down, out through my palm and into the black stone, and the stone answered. The silver veins lit. Not bright—no blaze, nothing that would carry down to the valley—but a slow pulse along every seam, like blood returning to a numb limb, like a great engine remembering it once had a purpose. And through my hand, faint as a voice through a wall, I felt it: not words, not quite. An attention. Something on the other side of ten feet of rock, turning toward me the way you turn toward a knock in the night.

*Oh,* said the gate, in whatever language gates use. *There you are.*

I snatched my hand back and stumbled three steps and sat down hard on the frost-stiff grass, heart slamming, breath smoking in the cold air. The veins dimmed. Went dark. The gate was just stone again, dumb and seamless, keeping its counsel the way it had for ten patient years.

But I had felt it. And worse—it had felt *me*. All these years I'd assumed my little thread of the other country was a dead inheritance, a keepsake in a drawer. It isn't dead. It's *recognized*. There is a lock in my chest and the Hollow Gate is its key, or the other way around, and tonight the tumblers moved.

I sat in the frost a long while, until my legs stopped shaking and the sky began to pale toward the false dawn. Then I got up, brushed off my coat, and did what I have trained myself to do with every dangerous truth for ten years: I folded it small, put it deep, and told no one.

In the ledger, under remarks, I wrote: *Gate stable.*

Then I sat back down, looked at those two words for a long minute, and struck them through.

Underneath, in smaller letters, because I am apparently done lying to ledgers if not yet to myself: *Something answered.*

* * *

Doss came back three days early.

I heard Brindle's bell before I saw him, which was wrong already—Doss doesn't travel at night on the high road, not unless something's chasing him or he's carrying something worth the risk. When he came into the lamplight of the watch hut his grin was fixed in place like a picture hung crooked, and he didn't complain about the cold, and he didn't insult my cooking, and that frightened me more than the gate's silver veins had.

"Pack a bag," he said.

"Hello to you too."

"Wren." He set both hands flat on my table, and I saw his fingers weren't steady. "The criers reached Thornhollow yesterday. There's a list. I saw it—got a look, don't ask how, it cost me and we'll leave it there." He swallowed. "Your name's on it. Second page. *Eriksen, W., keeper, Hollow Gate.* They're sending soldiers up the pass within the week."

The hut was very quiet. The brazier ticked. Somewhere outside, the wind found the gap in the shutter it always finds, and whistled its thin note.

"Second page," I said, because my mouth needed to say something small and stupid while the rest of me rearranged itself around the end of my life as I knew it.

"They start with the big names in the towns. Work outward." He wouldn't look at me now, which was its own message. "Wren. I came up the fast way, through Kettle Gap. Nobody knows I'm here. You've got maybe four days before the road below fills with crown livery, and after that the only ways off this mountain are the ones only you know."

I looked at my ledger, open on the table. *Something answered.* Two days ago that was the largest secret on the mountain. Now it wasn't even the largest secret in the room.

Ten years. Ten years of walking my four miles, banking my ember, keeping my side of the bargain with a world that had just decided the bargain was off. I thought I would feel terror, and I did, cold and clean as the gate's stone. But underneath the terror, shamefully, unmistakably, was something else—something that lifted like a bird off water.

If they were hunting the gifted, then the gift was no longer a thing to hide. It was a thing to spend.

"Help me saddle nothing," I said. "I walk light. But Doss—" I took the ledger, tore out the last three pages, and pushed them into the brazier, watching the flame take *something answered* and curl it to ash. "You're going to tell me everything you saw on that list. And then you're going to show me which roads the soldiers think are closed."

Doss stared at the fire, and his crooked grin came back, and this time it reached his eyes, which were bright with something that in a better life might have been tears.

"Atta girl," he said. "Knew you'd stop talking to that door eventually."
