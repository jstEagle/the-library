## 2. Proclamation in Ashford

I had been down from the mountain exactly four times in ten years, and every one of those trips had taught me the same lesson: the lowlands were lovely, and the lowlands were a trap.

Ashford was the loveliest trap of all. Market day, and the town square had dressed itself for it—stalls draped in dyed wool the color of poppies and eggplant, a fiddler working his way through something cheerful near the well, the smell of frying onions and horse dung braided together into that particular perfume that only market towns manage. Children wove between cart wheels. An old woman argued cheerfully with a fishmonger about the honesty of his trout. Ordinary life, thick as porridge.

I moved through it with my hood up and my hands in my pockets, which was how I always moved through crowds. Hands in pockets meant no accidental touches. No brushing against iron railings or cold stone or anything else that might make the thing under my skin sit up and take notice.

Because here is what nobody in that square knew: the woman buying apples beside me could light a candle with her breath if she wanted to. Not well. Not dramatically. A candle, maybe two, on a good day, when the air was right and I wasn't frightened. Ten years of practice had taught me to keep it small, keep it quiet, keep it buried so deep that even I sometimes pretended it wasn't there.

The gatekeeper's post at Hollow Gate came with a stipend, a cottage, and a rulebook written by men who had never stood close enough to the seal to feel it hum. Nowhere in that rulebook did it say *and if you happen to have the old gift yourself, best keep it in your fist and never speak of it.* It didn't need to. Everyone knew. The gift had been dying out for generations, they said, thinning like an old bloodline, and the crown found that convenient. A little magic in the world's history books. None of it in its streets.

I haggled for the apples—a woman who can't haggle on market day might as well wear a sign—and I let myself enjoy it, the way I always let myself enjoy these trips. That was the danger of the lowlands. They made you forget you were prey. Warm bread, warm sun, warm human noise. I'd bought a twist of honey candy for Doss—he pretended to be above sweets and always finished them before we reached the pass—and I was weighing whether I could afford a bolt of blue ribbon purely because I wanted it, when the criers began.

There were three of them, spaced around the square, and they didn't shout the way criers usually shouted. There was no *oyez*, no flourish. One of them simply climbed onto the fountain's rim, unrolled a paper sealed with the crown's black stag, and read in a flat voice, the way a man reads a list of flood warnings.

"By order of His Majesty, and the King's Council entire: let it be known that the old gift is an abomination before crown and covenant. Those bearing it are declared enemies of the realm. Any soul who harbors, aids, conceals, or fails to report a bearer shall share their punishment. Let all bearers present themselves to the king's soldiers within seven days, that they may be—"

He paused there. I remember the pause more clearly than anything else about that morning. He looked at the paper a moment too long, as though the next word needed to be climbed over.

"—*examined.*"

Examined. The word sat in the square like a stone dropped into still water, and I watched the ripples move outward through the crowd. The fiddler stopped playing. Somewhere a child laughed, and the laugh sounded suddenly obscene. People didn't scream. That was the terrible part. They did what people do—they went still, and then they went busy, and then they started looking at each other.

I looked at them looking. The fishmonger's eyes flicked to the old woman he'd been arguing with, and away. Two men by the farrier's stall drifted closer together, heads bent. A mother pulled her daughter's hand out of a stranger's reach without seeming to notice she'd done it. Ten years of neighborliness, undone in the length of one royal proclamation. Fear is efficient like that. It doesn't waste time on ceremony.

I should have left then. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger heaven keeps of my stupidities: *she should have left then.* But my feet had gone stupid, and besides, I told myself, I was nobody. I was a gatekeeper with a clean record and a stipend and ten years of being aggressively unremarkable. Lists were for other people.

Then they brought out Tam Arden.

*

You have to understand who Tam Arden was, because he matters to this story, though he never appears in it again after that morning. He was the cooper. He'd made barrels in Ashford for thirty years. He had a wife named Bess who sang off-key in the chapel choir, and three daughters, and a bad hip he complained about the way some men complain about weather—constantly, fondly, as though the hip were an old friend. When I was a girl passing through with my father, Tam had given me a whistle he'd carved from applewood, and told me that a trade was a kind of promise: you make a thing well, and the world owes you back.

They dragged him out of his own shop. That's what I mean about the efficiency of fear—no one had reported him, as far as anyone ever knew. But someone had examined him. Two soldiers and a man in grey robes with a chain of dull iron links, and the man in grey held up something like a tuning fork, and when they walked Tam past the fountain, the fork *sang*. A thin, high note, barely audible over the crowd, and Tam flinched at it the way a man flinches at a whip already falling.

"That's not—" Tam said, loud enough for the whole square. "I make *barrels*. Thirty years. Ask anyone. Ask Bess, ask the—"

Nobody asked anyone. The soldiers put him face-down on the cobbles and bound his wrists, and Bess came running out of the crowd screaming his name, and a soldier caught her by the shoulders—not cruelly, that was the worst part, almost gently—and said something to her I couldn't hear. She stopped screaming. She just stood there while they loaded her husband into a wagon like a sack of meal, and the man in grey marked something in a ledger, and the wagon rolled.

And the square went back to being a market. Slowly, badly, but it went back. The fiddler picked up his tune halfway through a bar. The fishmonger called out prices. People stepped around the spot where Tam had lain as though it were a puddle.

I stood there with my apples and my honey candy and my hood up, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the fork would sing for me too. Louder than it sang for Tam, probably. Tam's gift must have been a whisper—enough to season wood, maybe, enough to keep his barrels sweet. Mine was small, but it was real, and it had been answering the Hollow Gate's hum for ten years like a dog answering a distant bell. The man in grey wouldn't need to hold the fork near me. He'd probably hear me coming up the road.

My name was on a list I couldn't see. Maybe it wasn't—I had no proof, only the animal certainty that crawls up your spine when the net comes down and you realize the mesh has been sized for you all along. Keepers were licensed. Keepers were examined. Of course we were. Who better to check than the people already stationed at the crown's most sensitive door? We'd be first. We'd be *convenient.*

I paid the apple woman. My hands were steady, which surprised me; inside, everything was moving like scree in a rockslide. I bought the blue ribbon too, and I've never been able to explain why except that some part of me understood, even then, that I was saying goodbye to the woman who wanted ribbons, and I wanted to mark the occasion.

*

Doss wasn't hard to find. Doss was never hard to find; he arranged his life on the principle that hiding was for people with something to hide, which was either profound wisdom or the most successful con of his career. He was holding court at the back of the Drover's Rest with a pint and an audience of two drovers, telling a story I recognized as one of mine, improved. In his version, I'd fought the rockslide on the Grey Stair hand to hand.

"Wren!" He lit up. Doss always lit up; it was his best feature and his most expensive one. "Tell them about the ice bridge. Tell them what you said when it went."

"I said a word Mother would wash my mouth for."

"She said a word," he confirmed solemnly, "that melted the remaining ice." He waved me onto the bench beside him, and I sat, and I smiled, and I waited until the drovers drifted off toward their pints' refills before I leaned close.

"Doss. Listen to me. Have you heard the proclamation?"

"Heard it? I watched them read it in Harrow Cross yesterday." He said it lightly, but his eyes had gone careful. Doss's eyes were always three moves ahead of his mouth. "Bad business. Bad for trade, especially. You know what happens to smuggling roads when the crown gets religious? Every pass gets a checkpoint, every checkpoint gets a bribe, and every bribe doubles by spring."

"Doss."

"And before you ask—no, I haven't heard anything about the mountains. Nothing specific." He turned his pint slowly on the table, a full rotation, watching it. "But I'll tell you what I know, since you've got that look. The grey-robes came through Harrow Cross with wagons. Empty wagons, Wren. Going up-country. And a clerk showed me a paper by accident—or showed it to me very much on purpose, with clerks it's hard to say—that had a column of towns on it, and Ashford was third, and the fourth name was a place you'd know."

The tavern noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater.

"Hollowford," I said.

"There's a reason you're the clever one." He finally looked at me straight, and whatever he saw in my face made him set the pint down. "Wren. What's wrong?"

Nothing was wrong. That was the joke of it. Nothing was wrong, nothing had ever been wrong, I was a law-abiding keeper with a license and a stipend and a decade of service, and if I sat in this tavern until the soldiers reached Hollowford, the fork would sing, and I would go into a wagon like a sack of meal, and Bess would stand in a square somewhere not screaming.

"They're going to examine the garrisons," I said. "The passes. Everyone posted remote—they always start remote, it's in the crown's playbook, you clear the edges before you touch the center. Doss, I need to be gone from the lowlands by tomorrow night, and I need roads that don't exist."

For a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—something moved behind Doss's eyes that I chose not to see. I have thought about that moment many times since. He was frightened, my friend. Frightened men do arithmetic. I saw him do it and I forgave him for it in advance, which is either love or foolishness, and I've stopped trying to work out which.

"The Smuggler's Knee is snowed under till thaw," he said quietly. "But the old drover's cut behind Ravenfell—if you go now, tonight, you beat the first checkpoint by a day. And Wren?" He gripped my wrist, hard, his thumb finding the pulse point like he was checking I was still alive. "Don't go home first. Whatever you're thinking—cottage, keepsakes, the cat, don't. Go up. Go now."

"I don't have a cat."

"You have something. You have that face people get when they're about to do something sentimental and fatal." He pushed his untouched pint across to me. "Drink. Then walk out the back and don't stop walking until the ground is higher than your head."

I drank. It tasted like nothing. I hugged him—briefly, hard, his chin rough against my temple—and he held on a half-second longer than our friendship strictly allowed, and neither of us said anything true, because we were both men of the mountains in that way, and men of the mountains say true things only at funerals.

*

I walked out of Ashford as the light was going long and gold, and the town did what towns do: it closed itself around its fear and kept smiling. At the gate I passed the notice board where the proclamation had been nailed up, its black stag seal gleaming, and beneath it, fresh ink, a second notice: a reward schedule. *Information leading to the examination of a bearer.* Information. As though we were lost property. As though someone might find us behind a hedge and earn a coin.

Tam Arden's wagon was still visible on the east road, a speck now, crawling toward wherever the empty wagons went. I thought about the applewood whistle. I thought about a trade being a kind of promise—you make a thing well, and the world owes you back—and I wondered what the world owed a woman who had spent ten years keeping a door shut, and what it charged her for the keeping.

The road climbed. Behind me, Ashford's smoke rose straight and domestic into the evening sky, and ahead the mountains stood up black against the last light, wearing their snow like a dare. Somewhere up there was a smuggler's cut that didn't exist, and beyond it the high passes, and beyond those the Hollow Gate, humming its long note to no one, waiting for a keeper who was no longer permitted to exist.

I pulled the blue ribbon from my pocket, looked at it a moment—the one frivolous thing I'd ever bought myself—and tied it around my wrist where I could see it. A reminder. Not of what I was leaving.

Of what they'd made me leave it for.

Then I turned my back on the lowlands and started to climb, and I did not look at the road behind me again, because I knew—with the same animal certainty that had curled in my spine all day—that before I reached the tree line, I would hear hooves on the road below, and they would not be riding away from Ashford.

They would be riding toward the mountains. And one of the riders, I'd have sworn it even then, wore a coat I recognized.
