## 3. Smuggler's Roads

The thing about knowing every smuggler's road in the northern Thornfell passes is that you also know exactly how easy it is to be followed on them.

I'd learned that from Doss himself, six years ago, hunched over a fire above the Greyfen switchbacks while he drew trails in the ash with a burnt stick. "There's no such thing as a secret path, Wren," he'd said, grinning through his beard like the observation was a gift. "Only paths whose secrets haven't been sold yet."

At the time I'd laughed. Now, three days into my flight from Ashford with a proclamation bearing my description nailed to every market post between there and the mountains, I thought about that burnt stick more than I cared to.

I moved at night and slept in daylight folds of stone that only a keeper would know—hollows beneath slabs where the wind couldn't find you, where frost came late and left early because old fae warmth still leaked up through the rock from before the sealing. My gift liked those hollows. It sat under my skin like a cat in a sunbeam, purring faintly whenever I settled into one, which was exactly the wrong instinct for a fugitive to indulge. I told it to be quiet. It mostly listened. It had been listening for ten years; it knew the drill.

The roads themselves were good to me. The Miller's Stair, which didn't appear on any surveyor's map because the millers who built it hadn't wanted company. The Widow's Cut, narrow enough that I walked it sideways with my pack scraping both walls. The long scree run below Cragfell, where I let myself slide half a mile in a controlled avalanche of my own making and emerged at the bottom breathless, dusty, and—for one whole minute—almost laughing.

That was the trick of fleeing for your life, I discovered. There were hours when it felt like an adventure, and those hours were worse than the terrified ones, because afterward I hated myself for enjoying them while a neighbor named Marta Hale sat in a crown cell somewhere for the crime of lighting her stove with a snap of her fingers when her hands were too cold for flint.

By the fourth night I'd made the tree line above the Hollow valley and allowed myself a fire, small and shielded, in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel. I had dried mutton, hard bread, and a view of the pass I needed come morning: the Kestrel Notch, which threaded between two ridges and came out above the old gate road, three days' walk from Greyfen, where rumor said the gifted were gathering like sparks before a wildfire.

Rumor was a commodity Doss dealt in. That should have worried me sooner than it did.

I heard the whistle before I saw him—two low notes and a rising third, the signal he used on the lake roads when he wanted to meet without being shot by whoever he was meeting. My whole body went tight as a bowstring. Nobody else knew that call. Nobody living.

"Doss." I stayed behind the boulder, hand on my knife, feeling foolish even as I did it. "Come slow and keep your hands where I can see them."

"Charming welcome," he said, stepping into the firelight with his palms raised, his grin already deployed. He looked the same as always—broad, weathered, gray coming into his beard like frost claiming a field by degrees. "Ten years I've known you, and this is the thanks for climbing four ridges in the dark."

"How did you find me?"

"I've known every hole you hide in since you were nineteen and sick on my boat." He lowered his hands and crouched by the fire, uninvited, helping himself to warmth. "You went to ground like you always do. I just knew which ground."

It sounded right. It was even mostly true. But something cold slid down the back of my neck anyway, and I've learned over a long career of guarding things not to ignore the parts of my mind that go cold without permission.

"They took Marta Hale," I said. "And the Cooper girls. And old Fennick, who never had two drops of magic in him but whose grandmother did, which is apparently enough now."

"I know." His grin faded. For a moment he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with ridges. "Wren, there are soldiers in Ashford asking after you by name. Not by description. By *name*. Somebody gave them your name, and somebody gave them more than that, because they know you're headed for the high passes."

My mouth went dry. "How do you know where I'm headed?"

He wouldn't look at me. That was the answer, really, delivered before any words were. Doss had never once in ten years failed to look me in the eye when he was lying—he considered it part of the craft, the courtesy of a fair contest—and so the fact that he stared into the fire like it owed him money told me everything a confession would have.

"You didn't climb four ridges in the dark," I said quietly.

"Listen to me—" He turned, and his face was doing something awful, something caught. "They offered a full pardon. Full. And coin besides. I've got eleven people who work my roads, Wren, and half of them have families, and if they hang me the roads die with me and everyone starves honest or turns thief desperate, and—"

"And so you sold me."

"I sold them your *road*, not you!" It burst out of him, and the echo came back off the boulder like an accusation. "I told them the Kestrel Notch, because nobody goes through the Notch, it's suicide in this season, I figured—I figured you'd hear them coming, you're a keeper, you hear everything in these mountains, and I figured—"

"You figured you could sell me and save me in the same transaction." I stood. My legs were steadier than I expected them to be. "That's very nearly honest, Doss. It's the most honest arithmetic you've ever done. One of me weighed against your pardon, and you rounded down."

"Wren—"

"How many?" I was already kicking dirt over the fire, already reaching for my pack. "How many soldiers?"

"A dozen. Maybe more. Led by some crown man, a hunter. Kane." He said the name carefully, the way you'd set down something sharp. "They'll reach the Notch by dawn. Wren, please. Let me get you out another way. The lake road—"

"The lake road is watched, and we both know it, because you're the one who watches it." I swung the pack onto my shoulders and looked at him for what I genuinely believed might be the last time, and I found, to my disgust, that my eyes were burning. Ten years of friendship. Ten years of him pulling me out of rivers and me pulling him out of debtors' cells, and it had all apparently been held together by nothing sturdier than convenience. "Go home, Doss. Spend your pardon. I hope it buys you warm nights."

"It bought me nothing," he said, low. "It bought me this mountain and this conversation and the look on your face. That's what it bought."

But I was already moving, because grief is a luxury and dawn was coming whether I mourned it or not.

* * *

Here is what I knew that Doss either didn't or hadn't thought to mention: the Kestrel Notch has a twin.

The mapmakers never recorded it because the mapmakers never found it—a chimney of broken rock a quarter mile east of the main pass, invisible until you're standing in front of it, runnable if you know the line and lethal if you don't. My father had shown it to me before he died, back when showing your daughter secret ways through mountains was the sort of thing fathers did instead of telling them they were loved. I could cross it in the dark. I intended to.

The trouble with intentions in the high passes is that the mountains hold votes on all of them.

I was halfway up the chimney, wedged into a slot with my cheek against stone older than kingdoms, when the flare went up behind me. A soldier's light, red and harsh, blooming over the shoulder of the ridge where the Notch began. They'd reached it faster than Doss predicted—or they'd started earlier. Voices carried thin and strange across the cold air, and then a horn, twice, and I understood with a lurch that I wasn't hearing a search party finding an empty pass.

I was hearing them find someone.

I should have kept climbing. Every survival instinct I owned voted for it, loudly. Instead I hung there on the rock like a fool and listened, and what I heard was a boy's voice, high and cracking, saying words I couldn't make out, and then the flat command of a soldier saying words I could: *hold him, hold the little wretch, he came out of the Notch itself—*

He came out of the Notch itself. Which meant the boy knew my secret road, which meant the boy was either a ghost or a miracle or, most likely, another fugitive who'd stumbled onto the chimney by grace rather than knowledge. Either way, the soldiers had him, and I was twenty feet above their torches with a clear line down the slot, and my gift—the quiet, obedient, ten-years-hidden thing under my skin—woke up all at once and pushed against my ribs like it meant to go down there with or without me.

"Not tonight," I breathed at it. "Not for a stranger. We survive first. That's the whole covenant, surviving first."

The gift did not agree. It flared along my arms, warm as summer stone, and the frost on the rock around my hands melted with a hiss I could feel more than hear, and below me a soldier shouted that there was witch-light on the cliff.

Wonderful. Ten years of discipline undone by one starving child I'd never met.

I did the only sensible thing, which was the stupid thing: I climbed down toward the torches.

* * *

He was small. That was my first coherent observation once I'd dropped the last few feet into the shadow of the boulder field and gotten an actual look at the commotion. The boy couldn't have seen fourteen winters; he was all elbows and hollow cheeks, hair the color of old straw cut short and ragged, wearing a coat that had belonged to someone much larger and much deader. His wrists were manacled. He wasn't struggling. He was watching the soldiers with the flat, patient attention of a fox watching a henhouse door, and there was something clutched against his chest inside his ruined coat that he was protecting with his chin, the way a mother protects a sleeping infant.

Something in that bundle was humming.

No—not humming. Singing, in a register below sound, a vibration I felt in my teeth and in the healed-old ache of my knee and in the deep place where my gift lived. The soldiers clearly felt nothing. My gift felt *everything*. It rose in me like water climbing a wall, straining toward that bundle, and I had to bite the inside of my cheek bloody to keep whatever light it wanted to shed from spilling out of my eyes.

"Hold still, lad," the sergeant was saying, not unkindly, which was somehow worse. "Proclamation says the gifted come in gentle or otherwise. You'll be fed, same as the others."

"There's no others," the boy said. First words I'd heard from him, and his voice was rough as a crow's, worn down by disuse or screaming. "You say that to everyone you catch. There's no others. There's just holes."

The sergeant had no answer for that, which told me he'd at least noticed what his job actually was. He glanced at the manacles, then away, and in that glance the whole camp of his conscience was visible and losing ground.

Which is when I stepped out of the dark, and did the second stupid thing of the evening, and the reason I'm alive to narrate it at all: I let my gift off its leash.

Just a little. Just a finger's width. I put my palm against the frozen scree at the edge of the boulder field and *pushed*, the way I'd push a door I wanted to open slowly, and the entire slope shifted—one groaning, sliding shrug of loose stone, nothing dramatic, nothing that would light any witch-hunter's senses on fire, just gravity deciding to be generous. Rocks poured down the cut above the soldiers in a rattling brown flood. Torchlight scattered. Men swore and scrambled for cover, and the sergeant dragged the boy back by his chained wrists—which, fine, saved him from the rockfall, I'll take allies wherever I find them—and in the chaos I came out of the shadows low and fast, knife out, and cut the question mark of my presence directly into the situation.

Nobody died. I want that on the record, because later events would suggest I'm the type. I put my knife to one young soldier's collarbone close enough to part threads, told the sergeant in my best keeper's voice—the one that had faced down drunk smugglers and one memorable bear—that the mountain was unstable, the night was cold, and his prisoner had nothing worth the effort of escorting anywhere, and that if any of them wished to argue the point, the rocks and I were happy to continue the discussion.

The gift helped. Not with violence—with *presence*. It rolled off me like heat off a forge, and men who've been taught to fear witches will fill that heat in with whatever frightens them most. By the time I finished speaking, three of the six had backed toward the Notch of their own accord, and the sergeant was looking at me with the expression of a man renegotiating his entire understanding of his pension plan.

"Go," I said.

They went. Soldiers are practical people at heart; it's why kingdoms use them.

Then it was just me and the boy in the settling dust, and he looked up at me with eyes too old for his face and said, "You shouldn't have done that. They'll remember your shape."

"They remembered my shape already. It's on a proclamation." I sheathed the knife and knelt to deal with his manacles, which were good iron and badly locked. My gift slid into the mechanism like a key it had been waiting years to try, and the lock opened with a click so soft it was almost apologetic. "You came out of the Kestrel chimney. Who taught you the line? Nobody lives who knows that line."

"Nobody taught me." He rubbed his wrists, watching me with that fox patience. "I just go where the stone lets me. It doesn't let everyone." He tilted his head, and something flickered across his face—not quite fear, not quite wonder. Recognition, maybe, of the kind you get when a stray dog meets the one person who won't kick it. "You're the one it sings to."

"The what sings to?"

But he was already turning, digging into his coat, drawing out the bundle he'd guarded with his chin. Cloth, wrapped around cloth, wrapped around something the size of a fist. When the last layer fell open, moonlight caught on a shard of pale stone veined with silver, and every hair on my body stood up, and my gift rose in me so hard and so sudden that I staggered backward a full step.

The shard sang. There was no other word. It sang a single low note that I felt in the roots of my teeth and in the scar tissue of the old burn on my palm, and the note was *familiar*, was heartbreakingly familiar, was the exact pitch and timbre of the Hollow Gate on the one occasion ten years ago when I'd pressed my whole hand flat against its sealed surface and felt it press back.

"That's impossible," I whispered. "That stone was shattered. I *felt* it shatter."

"Did you?" the boy asked, terribly gently, and wrapped the shard back up with the care of someone who'd been carrying it a long way and had a longer way to go. "I'm Callum," he added, as if we hadn't just survived an ambush together and traded an impossibility. "Where are we going?"

"We," I said, "are not going anywhere. I'm going to Greyfen, alone, and you are going to—"

He simply looked at me. Half-starved, manacle-marked, clutching a fragment of the covenant stone in a coat full of holes, and he looked at me with those old eyes, and somewhere behind me Doss's betrayal still burned like a coal in my chest, and I heard myself hear the word *alone* and find that it had gone stale.

"There's a camp," I sighed. "Greyfen. Gifted folk. If anyone can tell us what that stone is—what it *means*—it's them." I pointed east, toward the black shoulder of the ridge and the long cold miles beyond. "Try to keep up."

Callum tucked the singing shard against his heart, smiled the smallest smile I have ever been given by another person, and followed me into the dark.

Behind us, far down the valley, a red flare rose and hung in the sky like a wound, and I understood that the soldiers had not gone home.

They had gone for reinforcements.
