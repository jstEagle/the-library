## 4. The Fugitives of Greyfen

I knew someone was coming up the goat track before I saw them, because the goats told me.

Goats are honest creatures. They have no talent for diplomacy and no interest in pretending a stranger is a friend. When the herd on the eastern shelf went stiff-eared and silent all at once, I set down the fish I was gutting, wiped my hands on my trousers, and picked up the sword I hadn't been officially allowed to carry in six years.

"Brann," I said to the boy mending nets by the fire pit. "Visitors."

"How many?"

"The goats haven't said yet."

He grinned. Brann is fourteen and thinks everything I do is either hilarious or heroic, which tells you how little he knows about the difference. The rest of Greyfen—thirty-one souls as of that morning, thirty-two if you counted the goat named Duchess, whom several people did—went about their business with the studied casualness we'd drilled into them. Which is to say: they hid badly and looked terrified.

The stranger came around the bend of the track at midday, leading a half-starved boy by the hand.

I'll be honest with you, since no one else will be: I expected a soldier. We'd had word from Ashford that the crown's criers were reading names off lists now, that the hunt had teeth and a budget. What I got instead was a woman in a gatekeeper's coat worn thin at the elbows, mud to her knees, with a bruise blooming along her jaw like a bad sunset. Behind her shuffled a boy who looked like he'd been assembled from twigs and stubbornness.

She stopped ten paces out—the correct distance, I noted, for a conversation between armed people—and looked at me looking at her.

"I'm looking for Greyfen," she said.

"You've found its doormat," I said. "State your business."

The boy behind her made a small sound. Not fear exactly. Recognition. His eyes had gone to the sword at my hip, then to my face, and something in his expression said *royal guard* the way a brand says *cattle*.

"Sera Voss," he whispered.

Well. That was one way to skip introductions.

---

Here is what you should know about me, since apparently the whole camp would learn it within the hour anyway: six years ago I was Captain Sera Voss of the Royal Guard, sworn shield to the Crown of Thornfell, and I was very good at my job. So good that when I discovered my own lieutenant was selling the locations of gifted children to men who paid by the head, I did the arithmetic every honest officer does eventually. Report it, and watch it vanish into some minister's drawer while the lieutenant kept selling. Or act on it myself.

I broke his arm in three places and his nose in one, in front of four witnesses, two of whom were the parents of a child I'd just pulled off a slaver's wagon. The Crown called it assault on an officer. The Crown was not wrong, technically. The Crown has never once been wrong, technically. That's rather the problem with crowns.

They took my commission, my colors, and my right to bear arms within city walls, and they let me walk because the alternative was a trial where those four witnesses might say inconvenient things aloud. I walked north until the roads ran out, found this fold in the mountains where the fog sits thick nine months of the year, and started collecting people the kingdom wanted gone.

Greyfen isn't much. A bowl of grey stone and black pines between two ridges, a stream that runs cold enough to hurt, a dozen shacks built from driftwood and denial. But the fog hides smoke, the ridges hide us, and the passes beyond hide the road south from anyone who doesn't know them. For six years, that's been enough.

Now there was a gatekeeper standing on my doormat asking to be let in, and a boy who knew my face, and I had a feeling the arithmetic was about to get complicated again.

---

We put her in the questioning hut, which is a grand name for a shed with one stool and no second exit. I sat across from her with the sword across my knees, which is not subtle, but subtlety is for people with time.

"Name."

"Wren Eriksen." She said it steadily, but I watched her hands. Hands tell you what voices won't. Hers were folded tight, knuckles white, the way you sit on fear when you've had practice.

"Occupation."

"Keeper of the Hollow Gate. Ten years."

There it was. The word moved through the camp faster than the fog comes off the ridge—I could feel it, the way you feel weather change. *Gatekeeper.* By nightfall every soul in Greyfen would know that the woman in the shed had spent a decade wearing the crown's livery, drawing the crown's pay, guarding the crown's most important door.

"You understand my difficulty," I said.

"Because I worked for them."

"Because you worked for them *well*, by your own account. Ten years. That's not employment, that's devotion." I leaned forward. "Half the people in this camp lost someone to the kind of diligence you're describing. Old Marta's sister was taken at a waystation checkpoint. The Tam boys watched their father hanged for harboring. And here you come down the mountain wearing the uniform of the thing that hunts them, asking for soup."

"It's not a uniform anymore." Her voice stayed level. Impressive, or rehearsed. "And I didn't ask for soup. I asked for Greyfen. Your name came out of Callum's mouth before I even knew whose camp this was."

The boy. Of course. He'd been hovering near the shed door since we brought her in, radiating the particular anxiety of someone who has vouched for a person and is waiting to find out if the vouching was fatal.

"He says you saved him," I said.

"He was starving in a ravine. Anyone would have—"

"Doss wouldn't have." Callum's voice came through the door slats, thin and certain. "Doss sold you. I heard him do it. You still came back for me."

Silence in the shed. Wren Eriksen looked at her hands.

"Tell me about Doss," I said.

So she did. The smuggler friend, ten years of shared watches and shared whiskey, the one person she'd trusted with the shape of her life if not the secret in her blood. And when the proclamation came down from Ashford, when the lists started and the soldiers started, Doss had weighed his old friend against his own neck and found the scale wanting. Sold her location to a patrol captain for a pardon and, I'd wager, a purse.

"And you escaped how?" I asked. "A keeper against three soldiers?"

"Two. And Callum threw a rock."

"A very good rock," Callum said through the door.

I looked at her a long moment. Here is the thing about suspicion: it's cheap. Any fool can refuse to trust; I could stand in this shed until spring finding reasons, and every reason would be true. She had served the crown. She had helped keep a gate sealed that half the old stories say should never have been sealed. She had hidden her gift for a decade, which means she was good at hiding things, which means everything she said was suspect, including the things that sounded true.

But necessity doesn't care about my reasons. Necessity is a creditor that always collects.

"Here's my situation, Keeper," I said. "Three weeks ago, a peddler told me the crown has hired hunters. Real ones. Not garrison soldiers—hunters. Men who can smell the gift. If that's true, Greyfen has perhaps a month before someone walks up that goat track with a writ and a wagon. My people can't fight that. They can't outrun it either, because half of them don't know these mountains, and the passes in winter kill the ignorant faster than any hunter." I stood, and her eyes tracked the sword without flinching, which I'll admit I respected. "You claim to know the mountain roads."

"Better than anyone alive. I've run them blind, in fog, in snow. It's why I'm alive."

"Convenient."

"It's why I'm *here*," she said, and for the first time something cracked in the level voice—not weakness, but the exhaustion underneath it. "Captain Voss. I don't want your trust. I've seen what trust costs lately. I want your people to live. I know the ways out of these mountains that the maps don't show, because I helped close them. Every smuggler's cut, every shepherd's stair, every frozen waterfall you can climb behind. That knowledge is sitting in my skull doing nothing, and your people are going to die without it. Do the arithmetic."

God help me, she'd used my own word.

---

I called the council that evening, which is a grand name for eleven people crammed into Marta's kitchen arguing over salted fish.

I'll spare you the full transcript. It featured Marta saying the gatekeeper should be turned out into the snow, the Tam brothers saying worse, old Fenwick saying nothing loudly, and Brann—fourteen, immortal, insufferable—saying she seemed nice. Callum stood by the fire the whole time with his fists clenched and finally said, in a voice that cracked in the middle:

"She carried me for two days. On her back. Up a ravine. With soldiers behind her. She didn't have to. Nobody would have known."

Nobody would have known. There's the test, isn't it? Character is what you do when the ledger stays closed. I broke a man's arm in front of witnesses because I couldn't stomach the closed ledger anymore. This woman had carried a stranger uphill with the law at her back, for no witness but the stranger himself.

"She also lied to her best friend for ten years," Marta said.

"So does everyone in this room," I said. "Every day, to every neighbor, to every tax collector. Lying about what you are to survive isn't the crime. The crime is what's being done to people who have to lie." I let that sit. "Here's what we're going to do. She stays. In the far hut, under watch. She eats what we eat, works what we work. And she teaches me the passes—all of them, drawn, checked twice. If she's true, we gain a road out of a burning house. If she's false, she's one woman in a camp of thirty-two, and I've handled worse odds with a broken nose."

Fenwick finally spoke. "And if the hunters come?"

"Then we'll know the roads," I said, "because of her. Whatever else she is, that part I believe. Liars don't usually lie with their feet bleeding."

---

She drew the first map that night, by tallow light, in my hut because I wasn't letting her out of arm's reach yet. And I'll confess something I told no one: watching her work was the first time in six years I felt something other than dread about our prospects.

Her hand moved like she was remembering, not inventing. No hesitation at the junctions, no fudging where a cartographer would fudge. She marked the deadfalls, the cornices that lie, the stretch of the high pass where the wind sings through a split in the rock loud enough to cover footsteps—"good for moving people, bad for hearing them coming"—and a shepherd's stair behind the frozen falls at Raven Cleft that I'd lived two valleys from for six years and never once suspected.

"You closed these," I said. "You said you helped seal the ways."

"Some of them. The big ones. The gate itself." Her pencil paused. "It was supposed to protect Thornfell."

"From what?"

"From Vaelmoor." She said the name carefully, the way you handle a blade by the flat. "That's the story, anyway. The covenant, the sealing, the long peace. Ten years ago I believed it completely. I gave everything I had to it." She looked up at me, and in the tallow light her face was older than her years. "Ask me sometime whether I still believe it. Just—not tonight."

Everyone has a locked room in them. Mine holds a lieutenant's broken nose and the sound the Crown's ministers made dismissing me. Hers held something bigger, clearly, and heavier, and I decided—wisely, I thought—that I wouldn't pick that lock tonight either.

"One more question," I said instead. "Callum. Where did you find him, really? Boys don't materialize in ravines carrying rocks and grudges."

Something crossed her face—quick, guarded, gone. "He found me. Ask him someday whether he wants to tell you more. Just—not tonight."

Fair trade. I let it go.

---

Two mornings later, the fog lifted early—a bad omen; clear air shows smoke—and I stood on the eastern ridge scanning the valley road with Fenwick's battered spyglass, and that's when I saw them.

Riders. Four, maybe five, moving up the valley at an easy trot. Too organized for bandits, too well-mounted for tax collectors. At their head rode a man in dark traveling leathers with a grey cloak pinned at the shoulder, and even at that distance something about the way he sat his horse made my stomach drop through the mountain.

I'd seen that seat before. Years ago, in the palace yards. That was a king's rider. That was a *hunter*.

I climbed down into camp fast enough to pull something in my knee and didn't care. Wren Eriksen was splitting wood in the yard—under watch, technically, though the axe argued otherwise—and I grabbed her shoulder and turned her toward the ridge.

"Those your roads," I said. "All of them. Every pass, every cut, every stair behind every frozen waterfall. How fast can you get us out?"

She shaded her eyes, looked, and went very still. Then she said, quietly, in the voice of a woman reading her own death warrant:

"That's Aldric Kane."

"Who's Aldric Kane?"

She lowered her hand and looked at me, and I saw the fear in her finally, plain and unhidden, and somehow that frightened me more than the riders did.

"He's what happens," she said, "when the gift decides to serve the fire instead of the burned. Get your people ready, Captain. We leave within the hour, and we take the road he won't expect—because I built it, and only I remember it's there."
