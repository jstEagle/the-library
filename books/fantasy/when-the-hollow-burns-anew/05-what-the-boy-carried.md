## 5. What the Boy Carried

There are three rules to carrying something that hums.

One: never let it touch your skin for too long, or it starts to feel like yours. Two: never let anyone hear it sing, because people do terrible things for songs they can't have. Three: never, ever tell the truth about where you got it, because the truth has teeth and it bites the hand that feeds it.

I'd kept all three rules for two years. I want that on the record before you judge what happens next.

* * *

Greyfen smelled like wet wool and woodsmoke and fear that had been simmering so long it had gone stale, like soup left on a dead fire. The camp sat in a bowl of rock below the tree line, tents patched with sailcloth and sacking, a cookfire that smoked more than it cooked. Maybe forty people. Maybe fewer, if you didn't count the ones who'd stopped really being there—the ones who stared at their hands like they were waiting for them to explain something.

Sera Voss ran the place the way a dam holds back a river: quietly, constantly, with the understanding that everything behind her would drown if she blinked wrong. She was broad-shouldered and gray at the temples and had a scar that went from her jaw down into her collar like a lightning strike someone had tried to erase. When she looked at Wren Eriksen, she looked at her the way you look at a stranger who claims to know your mother.

"A gatekeeper," Sera said. "Ten years at the Hollow Gate."

"Ten years," Wren said.

"And you never once thought to mention, in all your patrols, that there was a camp of hunted people two valleys over?"

"I mentioned it to no one. That was rather the point of me."

I liked her immediately. There's a kind of person who answers hard questions with the truth laid flat as a plank across a gap, and you either trust them instantly or not at all. I trusted her. Which was a problem, because rule three said I couldn't.

They gave Wren a tent at the edge of camp—edge being the polite word for *where we put people we haven't decided about yet*—and gave me a spot nearer the fire, because I was fourteen and skinny and looked like a strong wind might be a personal tragedy. A woman with flour on her forearms handed me a bowl of barley porridge that tasted mostly of smoke and slightly of hope.

And then it happened.

The shard woke up.

It did this sometimes—usually at night, usually when I was almost asleep, a faint vibration against my sternum like a moth trapped in a jar. But this time it came in daylight, in front of everyone, and it wasn't a moth anymore. It was a bell.

The sound was low enough that I told myself nobody else could hear it. I told myself that right up until the flour-armed woman stopped stirring. Right up until a man by the far tents turned his head like a hound catching scent. Right up until Wren Eriksen, halfway across the camp with her bowl untouched, went still as a deer that's heard the bowstring.

She turned. She looked straight at me. And the shard sang *louder*, which was the worst thing it could possibly have done, because it sang the way it always sang when it was happy, and happiness is not a sound you can hide.

"You," she said, crossing the camp in six strides, "have been carrying something old."

"It's nothing." Rule three, deployed badly. "A rock. My grandmother's. It's a family rock."

"Your family rocks sing?"

"They're very musical people."

Wren crouched in front of me, and up close her eyes were the color of the ice on the high passes, and there was something in them that made my chest hurt—not fear exactly. Recognition. Like looking at someone wearing your own coat.

"Callum," she said—I hadn't told her my name yet, which meant Sera had, which meant everyone knew my name now, wonderful—"I know what that sounds like. I've stood next to the thing it came from every day for ten years. You don't have to be afraid of me."

"That's what my gran said about the soldiers," I said, "right before the soldiers took her."

Something moved through her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Then we'll do this slowly," she said. "When you're ready."

The trouble with *when you're ready* is that the world doesn't wait for it.

* * *

That night I sat at the edge of the firelight turning the shard over in my hands. It was the size of a hen's egg, black as cooled tar, shot through with veins of silver that moved when you weren't looking directly at them. Gran had pressed it into my palms two winters ago, the night before the soldiers came to our village, and she'd held my fingers closed around it and said, "This is the last piece of the promise between the worlds. They broke the promise, boy, and everything since has been the sound of it breaking. Take it where it can be mended or die trying, and either way, don't let them take it from you."

Gran had a gift for making things simple and impossible at the same time.

I'd walked south until the roads ran out, then west until the mountains started, following a feeling I couldn't name—a pull, faint and constant, like the tide going out. The pull had gotten stronger every day. And today, in a camp full of strangers, it had sung itself hoarse the moment one particular woman walked past.

I didn't understand it yet. But I understood that understanding was coming whether I wanted it or not, and rule two existed precisely because of moments like the one that arrived the next morning.

Because Sera Voss found me.

Not gently, either. I was washing my face in the stream when her shadow fell over me, and I looked up into a face carved out of the same stone as the mountains, and she said, "Walk with me," in a voice that made *walk with me* sound like an order of execution with a scenic route.

We walked up out of the bowl, along the ridge, until the camp was a scatter of grey dots below us and the wind was the only other company. Then she stopped and held out her hand, palm up, and said, "Show me."

"Show you what?"

"The boy who carries a piece of the covenant stone. The boy whose little treasure sang loud enough yesterday that half my camp felt it in their teeth. Don't insult both of us, Callum. I've interrogated men twice your size and made them cry about their mothers."

"I like my mother. She's dead, but I liked her."

For half a heartbeat, something flickered in Sera's face—not softness, exactly. More like the memory of softness, filed somewhere she hadn't visited in years. "Then show me," she said, quieter, "and maybe I won't make you cry about her."

So I took out the shard.

I'd like to say I hesitated nobly, weighed my duty against my fear, made some principled stand. What actually happened is that my hands shook and my eyes stung and I set it in her palm because I was tired, so tired, of being the only person in the world who knew what I carried. Two years of sleeping sitting up. Two years of flinching at every boot on gravel. Two years of a secret that got heavier every mile, even though it weighed less than an apple.

Sera held it up to the light.

The silver veins in the stone brightened. Not glowed—*brightened*, the way coals do when breathed on, and a sound came off it, thin and clear, a single sustained note like a wet finger on a glass rim. And then, distantly, from down in the camp, another note answered it.

Wren's voice? No. Something under Wren's voice. Something in Wren.

Sera's hand didn't shake. I'll give her that. But her breath went out slow and controlled, the way it does when someone is spending real effort on not reacting, and she said, "Where did you get this?"

"My gran. She was a keeper's daughter, back before the sealing. She said it broke off the covenant stone when the gate was shut. She said—" I swallowed. "She said it was waiting for a mending hand."

"And it sang yesterday. When Eriksen walked past."

"It sings whenever she's close. Louder the closer she gets. Last night it wouldn't stop until she was asleep in her tent, and even then it sort of... purred."

"Purred."

"Like a cat. A very holy cat."

Sera looked at the shard for a long time. Then she looked east, toward the mountains, toward the Hollow Gate none of us could see from here but all of us could feel, the way you feel a storm before it shows on the horizon. And when she spoke again, her voice had changed—it had dropped its armor. Underneath the disgraced royal guard, the camp commander, the woman who held back the river, there was just a person, and the person sounded almost afraid.

"Do you know what this is, boy? Do you know what you've been carrying in your shirt pocket like a lucky coin?"

"A piece of the old promise. Gran said—"

"Your gran undersold it." Sera closed her fist around the shard, and the note cut off, and somehow the silence was worse. "The covenant between Thornfell and Vaelmoor wasn't a treaty. It wasn't ink and wax. It was a working—a binding woven into living stone, older than the crown, older than any kingdom that stands today. When the gate was sealed ten years ago, the stone shattered. Everyone said it was destroyed beyond mending. Everyone built their lives on that being true." Her eyes came back to me. "You are holding the proof that it was a lie."

The wind came up the ridge and pushed against us like it wanted to hear better.

"There are stories," Sera went on, slower now, choosing each word like a step on rotten ground. "Old stories, from before the purges, from before anyone sensible stopped repeating them. Stories about a reforging. About how if the stone could ever be made whole—if a keeper's hand could set the pieces back and speak the binding true—the gate wouldn't have to be a wall anymore. It could be a door." She turned the shard over in her fingers. "But the stone chooses who works it. It was never going to sit quiet for just anybody. It needed someone it *recognized*."

"And it recognizes Wren."

"And it recognizes Wren." She exhaled through her nose. "Which means our new arrival isn't just a runaway gatekeeper with useful knowledge of the passes. She may be the only living soul who can reforge the covenant—and end the hunt that's killing everyone in that camp below us. Including, if we're unlucky, you and me."

I should have felt hope. That's what you're supposed to feel, hearing that the thing you've suffered for has a purpose. And I did, a little, way down deep, like a coal banked under ash.

But mostly what I felt was terror. Because I knew something Sera didn't yet. I knew what the shard cost. I knew how it drank from me a little more each year, how my hair had gone white at the left temple at thirteen, how sometimes my hands went numb for hours and I couldn't remember why. Gran had carried it for thirty years and died of it—or died around it; it was hard to say where the carrying ended and the dying began. The stone was a promise, yes. But promises take payment, and the shard had been taking mine in installments since I was twelve years old.

If it recognized Wren—if it *wanted* her—then sooner or later it would start drinking from her instead of me.

And I didn't know yet whether handing her that burden made me a messenger or just a man passing a debt downhill.

* * *

We told her that evening, by the fire, with the whole camp pretending not to listen and failing completely.

Sera did the telling, because Sera believed bad news landed softer from a commander than from a boy. She laid it out plain: the shard, the singing, the recognition, the possibility of a reforging. She kept my name out of it—kept Gran out of it, kept the white streak and the numb hands out of it—and I loved her a little for that, in the way you love someone who lies kindly on your behalf.

Wren listened with her arms crossed and her weight on her heels, and when Sera finished, she was silent long enough that the fire popped twice.

"You're saying I'm not just prey," she said finally. "I'm a tool."

"I'm saying you might be a key," Sera said.

"Keys get used, Sera. That's the entire job of a key."

"Everything gets used. The question is by whom, and for what, and whether you get a say." Sera leaned forward, firelight carving her face into planes and shadow. "Forty people in this camp are alive because they ran. Forty people will keep running until they drop, because there is nowhere left to run *to*. If there's a chance—one chance—to unmake the hunt itself, to reforge the thing that keeps both worlds breathing, then I'm going to put it in front of you. But I'm not Halloway. I'm not the crown. Nobody here will force your hand. You have a say, Wren Eriksen. That's more than anyone's offered you in a month, and it's more than most people ever get."

Wren looked into the fire. Then she looked at me, sitting cross-legged at the edge of the light with the shard hidden inside my shirt, warm against my breastbone like a second heart.

"Can I see it?" she asked me. Not Sera. Me.

Rule two screamed in my head. People do terrible things for songs they can't have.

But she'd been the first person in two years to ask instead of demand, and I was fourteen and lonely and so tired of rules that existed only to keep me alone. So I drew it out on its cord and held it out across the fire, and the moment it crossed into her reach, it began to sing.

Not the thin note from the ridge. This was fuller, layered, a chord instead of a tone, and the silver veins lit until the stone looked like a piece of night sky cupped in her hands. The whole camp went quiet. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath. And Wren—Wren looked down at it with an expression I'll carry to my grave, because it wasn't wonder and it wasn't greed. It was grief. It was the face of someone meeting a friend they'd buried.

"It knows me," she whispered.

"It's been calling for you," I said. "I think maybe it's been calling longer than I've been alive. I think maybe Gran sent me after an echo."

Sera watched the two of us across the flames—the fugitive keeper and the boy with the white-streaked hair and the humming burden—and I saw her arrive at the same arithmetic I'd been avoiding for two days. One shard. One hand it recognized. One gate. One chance.

"We leave within the week," Sera announced to the camp, in the voice that ended arguments before they started. "High passes, smuggler roads, the Hollow Gate. Anyone who wants to come, comes prepared. Anyone who wants to stay, stays and prays the hunters stay lost." She paused, and the pause had weight. "Because they won't."

Later, when the fire had burned down to red eyes in the dark, Wren crouched beside me and said, "Thank you for trusting me with it."

"Don't thank me yet," I said. "You don't know what it costs."

"No," she agreed. "But I'm about to find out. Same as you did."

That night the shard purred against my chest, content as it had ever been, and I lay awake listening to it and understood, with the terrible clarity that visits boys my age roughly never, that my part in this story was nearly done. The stone had found what it wanted. Whatever came next belonged to Wren Eriksen and Sera Voss and the mountains and the gate.

I just hadn't counted on how fast *next* could arrive.

Because at dawn, a scout came down off the eastern ridge at a dead run, and the word he brought spread through Greyfen faster than the cookfire's smoke:

Riders. On the high trail. Crown colors.

Hunters had found us.
