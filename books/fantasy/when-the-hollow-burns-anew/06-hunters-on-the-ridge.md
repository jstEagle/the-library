## 6. Hunters on the Ridge

I had been a soldier long enough to know that panic is a smell. It comes off people like smoke off wet wood—acrid, sour, impossible to hide—and by the time the scout finished shouting, Greyfen stank of it from the cookfires to the latrine trenches.

"Positions," I said, and my voice came out flat and hard, the old parade-ground voice, the one that had once made raw recruits stand up straighter and now made gifted children flinch. "Everyone to your posts or I'll post you myself."

They moved. Not fast enough, but they moved.

Greyfen was never meant to hold. That was the whole point of it—a camp, not a fortress. A fold in the mountains where a rockfall had chewed a hollow out of the ridge, screened by scree and pine, with two ways in: the goat track from the east, which we could see coming for a mile, and the chimney, a crack in the cliff face on the west that only someone who knew it was there would ever find. We'd hidden in it for three years. Three years of cold tents and thinner soup and children learning to bank their magic the way other children learned to bank a fire.

Three years, and now riders on the high trail with crown colors snapping in the wind.

"How many?" I asked. Tammet, the scout, was nineteen and breathing like a bellows. I put a hand flat on his chest, mostly to make him stop vibrating. "How many, Tammet?"

"Thirty." He swallowed. "Maybe more behind the ridge. Light horse, and—" his face did something complicated, "—there's a man walking ahead of them, Captain. On foot. Alone."

"Walking ahead of cavalry. That's either very brave or very stupid."

"It's neither." That was Merric, our oldest, sixty if he was a day, with hands that shook unless he was doing something with fire in them, at which point they were the steadiest hands in Thornfell. His voice had gone quiet in the way I hated. "If he's walking ahead of the horses, he doesn't need them. Gifted men don't need horses."

"Gifted men don't hunt their own kind, either."

"They do when there's coin in it," Merric said. "Or fear. Or both wearing each other's clothes."

I knew the name before anyone said it. Everyone in the camps knew the name. Aldric Kane—the Hound, some called him, though not within earshot of anyone who might repeat it to the wrong person. Once he'd been the most celebrated battle-mage of the border wars. Then something happened—I'd heard six stories and believed none of them completely—and he'd handed his gift over to the crown's service like a man returning a borrowed coat. Now he hunted us. He was said to be able to feel magic the way a hound feels a hare under snow.

And we had forty-one gifted souls in this camp, including a boy with a shard of covenant stone singing against his chest like a struck bell.

"We break camp," I said. "Now. Nothing stays. Tents down, fires drowned, everything portable goes into packs. The children go up the chimney first with Halley and Bren. The rest of us buy them the hour they need."

Nobody argued. That was the mercy of having been a royal guard; argue with me about rations all day, but when the voice went flat, they moved. Except—

Except Wren Eriksen, standing at the edge of the crowd with her arms crossed and her jaw set, looking at the eastern ridge like she could measure the riders' progress by sheer spite.

"A word, Captain," she said.

I wanted to say no. I wanted to say *you've been here nine days, gatekeeper, you don't get a word.* But she'd led us through the passes without losing a single soul, and she'd done it while half my people watched her sideways, waiting for her to sell them out the way every keeper had sold them out since the sealing. She hadn't. So far.

I followed her a few steps aside, into the shadow of the rockfall.

"You can't fight them," she said. No preamble. I was beginning to understand that Wren Eriksen didn't believe in preambles. "Kane especially. If he feels even a flicker from the camp before you're clear, he'll call the horses up and you'll be caught between him and the trail with children on your backs."

"I'm aware of how fighting works, thank you."

"Are you?" Her eyes were gray as slate and just as warm. "Because I've stood a gate for ten years, and I know what a gifted hunter does to a defended position. He doesn't assault it. He makes it come apart. Stone remembers being stone, Captain. Fire remembers being free. Kane will ask the mountain to shrug, and your mountain will shrug, and everyone standing on it will be at the bottom of the slope."

I looked at her. Nine days, and she'd never once used her gift where any of us could see. But she spoke about it the way a fisherman speaks about water.

"So what's your proposal? Surrender?"

"My proposal is that you get everyone moving *now*, and I go meet him on the trail."

The laugh came out of me before I could stop it, short and ugly. "You. Alone."

"I'm the only one here who knows how keepers mask their gift. Ten years, Voss. Ten years of hiding what I am from a garrison trained to sniff out exactly what I am. I can stand in front of Aldric Kane and be nobody." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice—fear, maybe, or memory. "He won't be looking for a keeper anyway. Keepers aren't on the list. Keepers wrote the list."

"And if he looks closer than you'd like?"

"Then I run," she said, "and you use the time."

*

We used the time.

That is the sentence I want carved somewhere, if anyone bothers to carve anything over me. Not *Sera Voss died well* or *she was loyal* or whatever nonsense gets put on stones for soldiers. Just: *we used the time.*

By the time the first horn sounded off the eastern ridge—two notes, then silence, the crown hunters' signal that quarry was sighted—the chimney was full of children. I stood at its mouth and pushed them up into the dark one by one, small bodies and frightened eyes and, in two cases, small fists full of things they refused to leave behind: a wooden bird, a river stone, somebody's mother's thimble. Callum went near the last, the shard-boy, and he paused with one foot on the lowest ledge.

"The stone's loud," he said. His voice cracked in the middle, fourteen years old and trying so hard. "It hasn't been this loud since—since the waygate. Can he hear it?"

"Then muffle it," I said, which was useless advice, and we both knew it, and he climbed anyway because I told him to, and that is the entire tragedy and glory of being fourteen: you climb because the adult says climb.

Merric stayed with me at the mouth of the hollow, along with Tammet and six others, and we made ourselves the loudest thing in Greyfen. That was the plan, such as it was: be seen, be felt, be *wrong*—make Kane's hound-sense fix on the seven of us while forty-one souls went up a crack in the mountain behind us. Merric lit fires that burned blue and green and cast shadows that didn't match anything. Tammet threw his little tricks, sparks and whispers, the cheap conjuror's magic they'd dragged him out of his village for at fifteen. I had nothing but a sword and twenty years of drill, but a sword makes noise, and noise is a kind of bait.

The horn sounded again, much closer. And then the riders came around the shoulder of the ridge, and the world got very simple.

Thirty-odd horses in column, crown blue and silver, moving at an easy canter because they knew they had us. Ahead of them, on foot, walked a man in a gray cloak with his hood back, and I will give the stories this much: they undersold him. He wasn't tall or broad or scarred in any cinematic way. He was ordinary—mid-forties, sandy hair going thin, the face of a tax collector or a respected uncle. The only remarkable thing about Aldric Kane was that every living thing he passed seemed to lean away from him. The pines bent. The horses' ears flattened. Even the wind coming down off the ridge went around him rather than through.

He stopped fifty yards from the mouth of the hollow and looked past my drawn sword, past Merric's impossible flames, past all of it, directly into the camp as though the rockfall were glass.

"Keeper's work," he said. His voice carried without effort, the way voices do in dreams. "This hollow was sealed once and unsealed recently. Interesting." He tilted his head, listening to something none of us could hear. "But no keeper here now. Seven gifts, all small. Where are the rest?"

"Nowhere," I called back. "You've found all of us. Congratulations. Shall I surrender, or do we do the part where you monologue?"

Something moved across his face that was not quite amusement. Behind him, the column began to spread into a line, unhurried, professional.

"Captain Sera Voss," he said, and my stomach dropped through the mountain. "Discharged for striking a superior officer. They still tell the story in the barracks. You hit Colonel Ashe hard enough to loosen two teeth." He considered me the way a man considers a chess problem. "You're wasting yourself in a hole in the ground. The crown pardons useful people."

"The crown harvests useful people. I've heard what happens to the pardoned ones. They go into wagons and they don't come out."

A pause. Wind moved through the pines; Kane didn't move at all, and somehow that was worse.

"You've been listening to frightened people," he said. "Frightened people invent stories."

"Frightened people recognize teeth when they're inside them."

He sighed—an actual sigh, weary, almost human—and raised one hand, and that was the end of talking.

What happened next, I have tried to describe a dozen times and I only have soldier's words for it, which is like trying to catch a river in a bucket. Kane asked the mountain to move, and the mountain obeyed. The rockfall above the hollow groaned—a sound I felt in my back teeth—and then the whole eastern lip of our shelter let go and came down in a slow, thunderous avalanche of stone and pine, burying the cookfires, the supply cache, the tent I'd slept in for three years. Dust rolled over us like weather. Merric screamed something defiant and threw a lance of white fire at the line of riders, and a hunter on the left end screamed back, and then it was simply war, ugly and close, arrows and fire and my sword ringing off a dismounted trooper's breastplate while Tammet went down beside me with an arrow through his shoulder and kept making sparks, gods love him, sparks and sparks and sparks.

And through it all, Kane walked forward. Unhurried. Untouched. Arrows curved away from him like shy birds. He looked at Merric and Merric's fire guttered out—not extinguished, *taken*, swallowed into Kane's hand like water into sand—and Merric fell to his knees looking emptied, robbed, hollowed.

I put myself between them, because that was the job, because it had always been the job, because a captain stands where the blow falls or she isn't worth the title.

"Enough," I said. "You want the camp. Take it. There's no one else here."

"No," Kane agreed mildly. "There isn't. There hasn't been since before I arrived. Forty-one signatures leaving this hollow over the past hour, going up instead of out." He looked at the cliff face, at the chimney, invisible to any eye but one that saw as his saw. "Clever. Cruel, sending them up a dead-end crack with winter coming. But clever."

My heart was a fist beating on a locked door. *Not a dead-end,* I thought, with everything in me, with all the force of a wish I'd never admit to. *Please be through by now. Please be over the saddle.*

"There's a boy," Kane said, conversationally, horribly. "Small gift, barely kindled. But he carries something old. Something that sings. I've been following its voice for two hundred miles, Captain Voss, and it has been getting louder every single day." He stepped toward me, and the pressure of him was physical, like wading upstream. "Tell me where it is, and I'll let the old man live. The boy with the arrow in him too. I'm told I have a reputation, but reputations are mostly arithmetic. Two lives for thirty-nine."

Overhead—far overhead, a speck against the pale sky, so high it might have been a bird—a flicker of movement at the top of the cliff. A hand. Then another. Small hands, hauling small people onto the saddle of the ridge, into the pass beyond, out of reach.

I smiled at Aldric Kane with blood on my teeth from a split lip I didn't remember earning.

"You're arithmetic," I told him. "Count again."

His eyes narrowed, and for just a moment—less than a heartbeat—he looked past me, up, at the ridge, and something in his expression faltered. Not doubt. Not yet. But the space where doubt would eventually build its house.

Then he lifted his hand, and the mountain moved again, and I stopped having thoughts and started having only motions: parry, strike, fall, roll, run.

*

We lost them in the high passes because Wren Eriksen had promised us we would, and she was right, and I hated how right she was.

She met us above the saddle—her and Halley and the children, scattered among the rocks like survivors of a shipwreck, which is precisely what they were. She counted us in with those slate-gray eyes, and I watched her find Callum, find the old man, find Tammet slung between two others, and only then did her shoulders come down from around her ears.

"Forty-one?" she asked.

"Forty," I said. Tammet was alive; I'd decided that counted. "Where were you? You were supposed to be drawing him off."

"I was drawing him off." She nodded back the way we'd come, down the southern flank of the ridge, where the afternoon light was already failing. "For an hour I walked the low trail in plain view, masked as a grave, quiet as a stone. He rode past me twice." Her voice did something strange on the second word. "Twice, Voss. He looked right at me the second time and called me *goatherd* and moved on. Ten years of hiding, and it turns out hiding is the only thing I'm genuinely excellent at."

Behind her, the smoke of Greyfen rose into the darkening sky, a gray pillar where our home had been. Three years of tents and soup and children's laughter gone in forty minutes. Somewhere down there were our blankets, our stores, the little shrine Merric had built, the names we'd scratched into the rockfall of everyone we'd lost. Ash. All ash.

I sat down on a rock and discovered that my legs had resigned without informing me.

Grief came first, and it was enormous and dumb and shapeless, a whale beaching itself in my chest. Then fury came, and grief made room for it the way the sea makes room for anything. Fury at Kane, at the crown, at Halloway whose name we didn't yet know, at the world. And underneath the fury, quieter and more dangerous, a grudging, growing, deeply unwelcome thought:

*Kane tracked the shard for two hundred miles. He nearly found it today. He'll find it again.*

Which meant that running was over. Running had been the strategy, and the strategy was dead in the ashes of Greyfen. You cannot outrun a hound forever. At some point you have to decide what the hound is chasing you *toward*.

I looked up at Wren Eriksen, standing against the dying light with soot on her cheek and her arms crossed, watching the smoke rise with an expression I couldn't read—or could, and wished I couldn't. Guilt wears the same face on everyone, given time.

"The boy," I said. "The thing he carries. You knew what it was before you came to us. Didn't you?"

She didn't answer, which was an answer.

"Hunters will follow that song to the ends of the earth," I said. My voice came out low and rough. "So the ends of the earth is where we take it. No more hiding. No more holes in the ground." I stood, and my legs held, barely. "From tonight, we don't run *from* anything. We go to the gate, Eriksen. Your gate. And God help everyone, we finish whatever it is you came here to finish."

Wren went very still. Down the valley, the last light caught the snow on the high passes, and somewhere among the rocks a child started crying and was hushed by another child.

"That road goes through everything I spent ten years sealing shut," she said quietly.

"Then you'd better start telling me," I said, "what's on the other side."

Her face, in that moment, opened like a door onto a dark room—and whatever stood inside it, she had been keeping it locked a very long time.

* * *

They reached the frozen passes by nightfall, and the mountain closed behind them like a hand.
