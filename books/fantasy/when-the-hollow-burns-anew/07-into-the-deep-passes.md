## 7. Into the Deep Passes

The high passes above Greyfen don't kill you quickly. That's the trap. They let you think you're winning for a day, maybe two, and then they take your fingers, or your toes, or—if you're particularly careless—your sense of humor, which in my experience is the first thing to go and the hardest thing to survive without.

We'd been climbing since dawn. Behind us, the smoke from Greyfen still smudged the southern sky like a bruise that wouldn't decide on a color. Sera Voss had counted forty-one survivors out of a camp that had held better than ninety. She didn't talk about the number. None of us did. We just climbed, and the wind did the talking, and the wind was a bore who only knew one subject.

"Keeper," Sera said, falling into step beside me somewhere above the treeline. She used the word like a knife she was still deciding whether to sharpen. "How far to shelter?"

"Smuggler's cache at the Col of Nine Stones. Half a day, if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then it's half a day regardless. Weather up here doesn't negotiate. It just informs you of the terms."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped her before she strangled it. Good. I'd take one strangled laugh a day from Sera Voss and consider myself rich.

I'll admit something now that I would have denied under torture at the time: I liked being needed. Ten years of solitary patrols, of knowing things nobody asked me about, and here were forty-one people whose lungs continued working largely because I knew where the snow bridges were rotten and where the wind carved shelter out of nothing. It was a corrupt pleasure. I hoarded it shamefully.

"Listen," I said, loud enough for those nearest to hear, because the trick with teaching is you never teach just one person—you teach the air around them and hope it sticks. "Rule one of the deep passes: the mountain is always lying to you. That smooth white slope? It's hiding a crevasse with teeth. That gentle blue shadow? It's a cornice waiting for an idiot to walk on it. Assume everything beautiful up here wants you dead, and you'll mostly live."

"You're a charming guide," muttered Tam Vair, the farrier whose hands had been burned in the raid and whom I'd been pretending not to worry about for six hours.

"I'm an alive guide. Charm is extra."

* * *

The Col of Nine Stones earned its name honestly: nine standing rocks, leaned together by some shepherd's great-grandfather into a rough beehive of shelter, floored with old pine boughs gone to dust. Not comfortable. But when the wind came down off the glacier at dusk sounding like the end of all arguments, forty-one people crammed inside it felt less like refugees and more like a very bad-tempered honeycomb.

That was where Callum started dying.

I want to be precise about this, because at the time none of us had the right words, least of all me. He didn't look sick. He looked *dimmed*. The boy had come out of the ambush half-starved and strange, carrying his secret wrapped in oilcloth against his chest—that shard of the covenant stone that sang when I came near, a thin silver thread of sound I felt in my back teeth. Since Greyfen he'd been quieter each day. Paler. As if something were drawing him down through a straw.

He sat against the stones near me, chewing on the heel of bread I'd pushed at him, and mid-chew he simply tipped over sideways like a dropped puppet.

I got to him fast. So did Sera, and so did Mother Hollis, the camp's herbwoman, whose hands were steadier than her opinions. His skin was cold in a way cold shouldn't work—not weather-cold but *absence*-cold, as if the warmth had been called elsewhere and had gone obediently.

"It's the shard," Hollis said. "It's drinking him."

"Then we take it off him," said Tam.

"No." Callum's eyes opened, fever-bright and furious, and for once the boy sounded like what he was becoming: someone with a burden older than himself. "It doesn't leave. It *can't*. If it's taken—" He didn't finish. His hand went to his chest, to the oilcloth bundle, the way a drowning man goes for a rope.

Sera looked at me. And I knew—I knew before I knew—what everyone else was also beginning to know, because the shard had started to sing. Loudly. A rising silver note that made the nine stones hum sympathetically, that made the hair on my arms lift, that made every gifted person in that shelter turn to stare at me with expressions ranging from awe to the oldest fear of all.

They were looking at me the way villagers look at lightning.

"It's answering her," Hollis said quietly. "It's been answering her since Greyfen. It's pulling from the boy because it wants the keeper's hand, and it can't reach her properly, so it's going through him instead. Like water forced through too small a pipe."

"Then how do we unblock the pipe?" Sera asked, never taking her eyes off mine.

And there it was. The moment. Ten years of hiding, ten years of keeping my gift folded small beneath my breastbone like a letter I was ashamed of—and the answer was to open the letter in front of forty-one frightened people who had spent the last weeks learning exactly why people like us get hunted.

My mouth said, "There might be another way."

My magic said otherwise. It rose before I could stop it, the way it always flared near the gate's stone, except now it wasn't the gate's stone—it was the shard's song calling it, and my whole life of careful concealment came apart in about four heartbeats. Light. I won't pretend it was dignified. My hands glowed like I'd plunged them into a bank of coals, warm gold threaded with green, the color of new leaves on old wood, and the shard's singing smoothed from that awful strained note into something low and contented, and Callum gasped underneath me like a man surfacing from deep water.

Forty-one people watched their keeper—the gatekeeper, the woman who had spent a decade guarding the wall between them and everything they'd lost—burn quietly with the same fire the crown wanted to cut out of them.

Nobody screamed. I want that recorded. In my experience of disasters, nobody screaming is rarer than snow in summer, and it happened.

Callum's color came back in a slow tide. His eyes found mine, and he didn't say thank you, because Callum didn't waste words on things he considered obvious. What he said was: "Told you it likes you."

"Telling me isn't the same as asking my permission," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle, which ruined the delivery entirely.

* * *

We lost the next two days to a storm that Sera described, with grim accuracy, as "the sky having a grudge." We couldn't move; the passes in whiteout are a elegant method of suicide. Instead we stayed in the col, and I did the thing I'd spent ten years perfecting the art of avoiding: I was visible.

It went badly at first. People find it hard to relax around a lit lantern when they've been living by dark. Bren the weaver took her meals on the far side of the shelter. The twins—fugitive sisters, maybe fourteen, all elbows and suspicion—watched me eat like I might sprout something. I understood. Fear of your own kind is a habit, and habits built by soldiers and criers don't dissolve because of one warm glow.

So I did what I do instead of talking about feelings: I taught.

"Rule two," I announced the morning after Callum's collapse, while the storm leaned on the stones and everyone pretended not to listen. "Snow tells you stories if you know its grammar. A slope that whispers is stable. A slope that's gone silent has shifted—shear layer underneath. Never cross the quiet ones."

"How do you make snow whisper?" one of the twins demanded, arms crossed, daring me to be foolish.

"You lie down and put your ear to it, obviously."

"You're mad."

"I'm alive, which historically requires occasional madness. Rule three: if you're lost, follow the wind downhill at dawn, not at dusk. Morning wind runs out of the valleys, evening wind climbs. Smugglers call it the mountain breathing out and in. You move when it breathes out, and the mountain carries you home."

By the third telling, both twins were sitting close enough that I could see the frost in their eyelashes, and the elder one asked—so casually it nearly fooled me—"And rule four?"

"Rule four is never travel with a smuggler you haven't paid double," said Doss's ghost in my head, and I didn't say it out loud, because betrayal is a wound I wasn't ready to show anyone, even as a joke. Especially as a joke.

On the second night, Callum crawled over and sat against my side, radiating heat again, the shard quiet and satisfied between us. Around us the camp had arranged itself into sleep, and Sera sat awake at the entrance with her sword across her knees, doing her statue impression.

"Why'd you hide it?" Callum asked. "Ten years. You could've been with people like us."

I thought about the honest answers: that hiding is easy and belonging is terrifying; that a secret is a room with only one door and you can guard it alone; that I'd sealed the Hollow Gate believing I was saving Thornfell, and had spent a decade paying for the belief in solitude. I gave him the truest one I could manage aloud.

"Because a hidden thing can't be taken from you," I said. "Turns out that's wrong, mind. It can be taken. It just takes longer, and you do most of the taking yourself."

Callum considered this with the gravity he gave everything. "That's a stupid system," he concluded.

"Yes," I said. "Yes, it is."

Across the shelter, Bren the weaver shifted in her blankets. Then, deliberately, she picked up her bedroll and moved it closer to mine—not touching, just nearer, the way you'd edge toward a fire you'd decided to trust after all. The twins followed suit within the hour, with the elaborate unconcern of teenagers committing an act of kindness.

I lay there listening to forty-one people breathe, and the hollow place behind my breastbone—ten years' tenant, rent-free—was, for the moment, quiet.

* * *

The storm broke on the third morning like a dish hitting a floor: sudden, total, done. We came out into a world scoured blue and white and unbearable gold, and I nearly forgave the mountains everything.

We made the high shoulder above the Deep Passes by noon. From there you can see a long way, and I should have known better than to look.

Smoke. Not one column—many, spaced along the valley floor below the southern approach, disciplined, evenly spaced. Campfires in a chain. Soldiers' fires; smugglers learn to read smoke the way sailors read water.

Sera saw them a heartbeat after I did. "How long before they're up here?"

"Two days if they know the routes. Four if they don't." I studied the chain, and my stomach performed a slow, professional descent. "Those aren't search parties, though. Look at the spacing. They're not hunting us anymore, Sera. They're *herding*. Someone's pushing us along a specific line—east, toward the old waygates."

"Why would Halloway's men want us anywhere?"

"That," I said, watching the smoke rise straight as plumb lines into the clean cold sky, "is exactly what frightens me."

Behind me, faint as a heartbeat, Callum's shard began to sing eastward into the pass—and this time, for the first time, I could swear it wasn't calling me forward.
