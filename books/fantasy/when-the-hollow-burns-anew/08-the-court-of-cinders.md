## 8. The Court of Cinders

I had been inside the Hall of Ascension exactly twice before that night. The first time was when I received my commission, all polished marble and trumpets, and the second time was when they stripped it from me—metaphorically speaking, since I'd never actually been stripped of anything except dignity, and there wasn't much left to take.

The third time, they sent a carriage for me.

That alone should have told me something. The Crown does not send carriages for hunters it trusts. It sends carriages for hunters it wants to keep where it can see them, the way you keep a wolf you're not yet certain won't eat the hand that feeds it.

The carriage smelled of lavender and old money. I hated it immediately and completely, which is my usual response to both.

* * *

Lord Edmun Halloway received me in the eastern wing, in a room I had never heard of despite serving the crown for nineteen years—a room with no windows, which in a palace famous for its glass was rather like finding a toothless lion in a pride of lions.

"Kane." He didn't look up from the papers spread across a table of black wood. "You lost them at Greyfen."

"I dispersed them at Greyfen," I said. "Whether they're lost remains to be seen."

He did look up then. He had one of those faces that would have been handsome if it ever did anything, a face built for portraits rather than people. "You burned their camp."

"They were given the opportunity to surrender. They declined. Fire was the next item on the agenda." I kept my voice level, the way you keep your hand level when you're showing a card player you have nothing—or everything. Nineteen years teaches you that these are, functionally, the same posture.

"And the gifted among them? How many did you take?"

"Seven fled into the high passes. Two dead in the fighting. One—" I hesitated, because this was the part I had been chewing on for six days, like a bone with marrow I couldn't quite reach. "One woman surrendered. Unmarked. No visible gift. She asked me to spare her children."

"And?"

"I took her into custody."

"Where is she now?"

"The garrison at Coldbrook."

Halloway made a small note on his papers. Not her name—I hadn't given him one. Just a mark, a tally, as though she were grain in a storehouse. "Coldbrook will be handled. You'll ride south with me tomorrow. The court wishes to review your campaign."

"The court has never once reviewed any hunter's campaign. We file reports. Reports go into drawers. Drawers stay closed. That's the arrangement."

"The arrangement," Halloway said, setting down his pen with the care of a man laying down a knife he intends to pick up again later, "has changed."

* * *

They fed me first, which I took as either courtesy or an insult, depending on whether the food was meant to be a last meal. It was pheasant. Nobody has ever been executed after pheasant; the logistics don't allow for it. So I ate, and I watched the court move around me—the inner circle, the men and women whose names appeared in dispatches I was never cleared to read—and I noticed something I should have noticed months earlier.

None of them spoke about the hunt.

Oh, they spoke around it. There was talk of roads and tariffs and a border dispute in the south that I knew for a distraction because I'd manufactured half its evidence myself last spring. But the gifted—the hundreds of them, the lists, the arrests, the wagons that rolled out of provincial garrisons heavy and came back light—not one word. A kingdom at war talks about nothing else. A kingdom conducting something else entirely goes very quiet.

"You've noticed," Halloway said, appearing at my shoulder with two glasses of wine. He handed me one without asking. "Good. I'd begun to worry your instincts had gone soft out in the mountains."

"Noticed what, my lord?"

"That we are not hunting them." He drank, watching me over the rim. "You assumed we were. It's why your reports read so strangely—all pursuit and capture and no accounting. You kept waiting for orders that matched a hunt. They never came, and you filed the discrepancy instead of acting on it. That restraint is the only reason you're here tonight rather than somewhere considerably less pleasant."

Somewhere in my chest, an old instinct stirred—the one that tells a man the ground under his feet has been hollow all along and has finally decided to mention it. I'd felt it before. I felt it now, stronger.

"Then what are we doing with them?"

"Come and see," said Lord Halloway, "and try not to embarrass either of us."

* * *

We went down. That's the thing I keep returning to, the detail my mind snags on like cloth on a nail: we went *down*. The Hall of Ascension, seat of Thornfell's glory, and beneath its marble feet there were stairs I had never known existed, descending past cellars past cisterns past anything a palace has any honest business containing, until the air itself changed—thicker, warmer, tasting faintly of copper and lightning.

The corridor opened into a vaulted hall that must have been older than the palace above it. Older than the kingdom, possibly. And filling that hall—

I am a hunter. I have seen cruelty dressed as law and law dressed as necessity and necessity dressed, once memorably, as a birthday celebration. I am difficult to shock. I record this fact so you'll understand when I say: I stood in the doorway of that hall and could not make my legs work.

Cages. Rows of them, iron and pale wood, arranged with the tidy geometry of an orchard. And in the cages, people. Dozens. More than dozens. Some sat motionless with the terrible stillness of the deeply exhausted. Some wept, quietly, the way you learn to weep when noise has consequences. And from every cage, thin as spider silk and faintly luminous, ran lines of—light? Thread? I have no better word. They rose from the prisoners' chests and wrists and temples and converged overhead into conduits of copper and glass, and through those conduits something *flowed*, visible to the eye like heat shimmer made solid, pouring toward the far end of the hall where a machine squatted in the dark like a fat iron god.

A woman in the nearest cage looked up at me as I passed. Her eyes found my coat, my insignia, and something in her face shut like a door. She had been a person who hoped once. I watched the hope leave in real time, and I want to be honest with you, whoever finds this account: that small death did more damage to me than everything that followed.

"What is this?" My voice came out wrong. Smaller than I intended.

"The harvest floor," Halloway said, with the mild pride of a man showing off his orchards. "Though the technical term the engineers prefer is 'extraction gallery.' Farmers and their terminology."

"Harvest." I said the word back to him and tasted it. "These are people, my lord."

"These are *assets*, Kane, and before you reach for your sword—do notice the forty royal guardsmen between you and it—let me show you the rest, because you of all people have earned an explanation. Your conscience has always been your most inconvenient organ."

He walked, and because the alternative was standing alone among the cages, I walked with him. The threads of light hummed faintly as we passed beneath them. One of them, I realized, was coming from a child. A boy of perhaps nine, asleep or made to sleep, his small chest rising and falling while the light drained from him in a steady silver stream.

I stopped walking. Halloway let me stop. He was patient with me the way you're patient with a dog hearing thunder.

"Vaelmoor is dying," he said.

The non sequitur hit me sideways. "What?"

"The fae realm. Our neighbor beyond the Hollow Gate. You know the histories—covenant, sealing, all that. What the histories don't tell you is that the covenant was never merely a wall. It was a *tether*. Their magic sustained our land's blessing—our harvests, our long lives, our luck, if you believe in luck. And ours anchored theirs. When the gate sealed ten years ago, the tether snapped. Vaelmoor began to starve. And Thornfell—" he gestured upward, at the palace, the city, the whole complacent weight of the kingdom—"began, slowly, to follow. Crops fail a little earlier each year. Children come smaller. The court physicians call it a dozen fashionable ailments. I call it what it is: withdrawal. Both realms are addicts cut off from the same supply."

"And this—" I looked at the machine, at the threads, at the boy.

"This is the solution discovered by minds greater than mine, funded by purses deeper than yours will ever see. Gifted blood carries residual covenant-magic. Extracted, refined, piped—it can be directed. Sent through the mountain stone toward the gate itself, pushed across the seal like water forced through a crack. Enough of it, delivered long enough, and Vaelmoor stabilizes. Thornfell stabilizes. The covenant is restored by proxy, no gate required, no fae required, no truth required." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who believes himself a savior. "Your hunters aren't hunting, Kane. They're *gathering*. Every gifted soul you've brought in these past months has bought another season of light in Vaelmoor and another season of wheat in Thornfell. You haven't been betraying your gift. You've been spending it. Wisely, at scale, for the first time in history."

I looked at the boy again. The thread pulsed with his heartbeat.

"How much do they take?" I asked. My voice had gone very flat, which anyone who knows me knows is worse than loud. "From each of them. Per day."

"Enough."

"How much, my lord."

Halloway sighed, a tutor wearying of a slow pupil. "In the early trials, more than was sustainable. We adjusted. The current extraction rates allow most subjects to remain... productive... for years."

"*Years.*"

"You keep repeating my words as though repetition changes them." He turned to face me fully, and for the first time all evening he looked tired—an honest tiredness, which frightened me more than the machine did. "You think I sleep well? You think I don't count them at night? I grew up in the famine years, Kane. I buried two sisters before I was ten. If feeding a machine a few hundred comfortable prisoners keeps two kingdoms alive, I will carry that arithmetic to my grave gladly, and I will answer for it before whatever gods are listening, and I will make the same choice again. Now. Can you say the same?"

And here is the trouble with doubt: it doesn't arrive like an argument. It arrives like weather. Halloway's arithmetic was sound. I checked it, right there, standing in the humming dark, because I owed him that much honesty. Two kingdoms against some hundreds of souls. Wheat against suffering. Any commander I ever served under would have signed.

But the boy's chest rose and fell, and the thread drank with every breath, and I thought: *we were told they were criminals. We were told they were dangers. Nobody told me I was a shepherd delivering sheep to the shear, and nobody asked the sheep.*

"My lord," I said carefully, "the gifted in the high passes. The ones who fled Greyfen. If they're assets..."

"Then they must be gathered." He resumed walking, and I fell in beside him because my legs had decided, without consulting me, that this conversation needed to end soon. "Which brings me to your new assignment. The operation moves to the mountains. To the Hollow Gate itself—we can increase throughput dramatically at the source. You'll have command of the escort companies. Wren Eriksen, the keeper. She's on the priority list."

"The keeper is gifted?"

"She sealed the gate. What do you imagine seals a gate *with*?" He glanced at my face and, misreading it as reluctance, softened by a degree. "You've done well, Kane. Better than well. This is the center of things now. Be glad you arrived in time to see it."

We rode up out of the dark in silence. At the corridor's mouth, the ordinary palace resumed—lamplight, marble, a servant polishing a banister who would never know what lay beneath his knees. The world above wore its face again.

I paused at the top of the stairs.

"My lord. The prisoners at Coldbrook. The woman and her children."

"Already transferred," Halloway said mildly. "This morning. Efficient, isn't it?"

It was. That was the whole horror of it—how efficient, how orderly, how *reasonable* the machinery was, and how none of the reasonableness ever consulted the people inside the cages.

* * *

My rooms that night had four walls, a fire, and a window overlooking the city, and I trusted none of them. I sat awake with the lamp unlit, doing sums.

Here is what nineteen years of hunting taught me, the lesson beneath all the other lessons: everyone believes themselves the hero of their own ledger. The smuggler steals to feed his sister. The traitor turns to save his sons. Even the monsters I chased through the borderlands had ledgers, and in every one, the columns balanced.

Halloway's ledger balanced. Two kingdoms weighed against hundreds of captives, and the kingdoms heavier.

But ledgers only balance if you agree on what counts as a life. And I had watched a boy's heartbeat feed a machine, and I had watched hope die in a woman's eyes, and somewhere in the frozen high passes seven fugitives were running from me—from *me*—through snow I knew would kill the slow ones, and every one of them was a column in Halloway's book marked *asset*, and every one of them had a name I had never bothered to learn.

I thought about the woman at Greyfen asking me to spare her children. I had said yes. I had even meant it. Then I had put her name on a transfer list, because the list was the procedure, and procedure was the armor I'd worn so long I'd forgotten it was armor at all and called it skin.

Toward dawn, I did three things.

I wrote out my resignation, read it, and burned it—resignation would only remove me from the board, and I had just learned the board was larger and filthier than I'd dreamed.

I drew a map of the Hollow Gate approaches from memory, marking the escort routes, the guard rotations, the tunnels only the old keepers knew, and hid it inside the lining of my left boot where no inspector ever searches, because inspectors search boots but rarely *my* boots, a superstition I have cultivated deliberately for two decades.

And I sat by the window and watched the sun rise over a capital that did not know what burned beneath its feet, and I understood that Aldric Kane, loyal hound of the crown, had died sometime in the last twelve hours in a hall full of cages, and that whatever stood up in his place would be hunted by both sides before this winter ended.

Below, in the courtyard, the wagons were loading. Mountain supplies, rope, chain, copper coil. The whole apparatus moving toward the Hollow Gate—toward Wren Eriksen, whoever she truly was, and whatever remained of the people who had escaped my fire.

I finished dressing. I picked up my sword, which suddenly weighed what it had always weighed, and had never felt heavier.

Time, I decided, to go to the mountains—and to find out, before Lord Halloway's machines got there, whether the keeper of the Hollow Gate could be persuaded to open it for someone other than the crown.

Because if the gate could be truly opened—covenant reforged, tether restored honestly, freely, without a single cage—then everything beneath the Hall of Ascension was not salvation.

It was just theft, wearing a crown.
