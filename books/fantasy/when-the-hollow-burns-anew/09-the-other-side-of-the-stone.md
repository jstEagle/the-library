## 9. The Other Side of the Stone

The waygate found us before we found it.

I want to say I led us there—that my smuggler's knowledge of the Thornfell high country carried the day again—but that would be a lie, and this story has enough of those already. The truth is that Callum's shard started pulling him like a fish on a line three days out of Greyfen, and by the fourth morning he was walking in his sleep with his eyes open, boots crunching over frost, arms held straight out in front of him like a boy wading into a lake.

"Should we stop him?" Sera asked, watching him go.

"He'd just start again," I said. "He's been doing it since the ridge."

"I know. It's unsettling."

"It's more than unsettling." I'd been watching the shard through his shirt all morning. It glowed now. Not the faint coal-warmth it had given off since the harvest fields—this was a steady pulse, slow as a heartbeat, and every time it flared, the frost on the nearest stones melted and refroze in patterns too regular to be natural. "It's a compass needle that's decided where north is, and it's dragging your whole camp after it."

Sera chewed on that for a while. She did her best thinking while chewing, jaw working like she was grinding the problem down to something swallowable. "If it leads us somewhere bad?"

"Then we'll be somewhere bad together," I said. "Which is how we've done everything else so far."

She almost smiled. Almost. Sera Voss rationed her smiles like a besieged town rations bread, and I'd learned not to waste them.

* * *

We came over a shoulder of grey granite at midday and saw the waygate sitting in a bowl of dead grass like a thing that had been waiting to be remembered.

I knew what it was immediately, though I'd never seen one outside the archives at Ashford—and never believed the drawings. A ring of standing stones, maybe twenty feet across, each carved with script so old the letters had gone soft-edged, worn back toward suggestion. Lichen the color of old pewter climbed them. Snow lay in the carvings like mortar. And in the center, flat on the ground, was a disc of stone that should have been ordinary except that it wasn't: the air above it bent, very slightly, the way air bends over a fire.

Callum walked straight to it and knelt, and the shard in his shirt blazed through the wool like a lantern behind cloth.

"Callum," I said carefully. "Hey. Kid."

He turned and looked at me, and for a moment I wasn't sure anyone was home. His eyes were fixed somewhere past my shoulder, past the mountains, past everything. Then he blinked, and he was nine years old again and terrified, and he scrambled up and backed against the nearest stone.

"It wouldn't stop singing," he said. "I'm sorry. It wouldn't stop."

"It's all right." I crouched to his level, which is a trick you learn fast when there are children in a fugitive band—you get good at making yourself small without looking small. "What's it saying?"

He hesitated. He always hesitated before answering questions about the shard, because answering them meant admitting how much he understood, and understanding things was what had gotten his family killed. But he'd been getting braver since the passes, since the night I'd used my gift openly to pull the cold out of him, since the camp had stopped flinching when I raised my hands.

"It's not words," he said finally. "It's more like... you know when you're asleep and someone says your name and you can't wake up but you know they're calling? Like that. Except it's not my name. It's—" He frowned, hunting for it. "It's yours."

The whole camp went quiet. Forty-odd people, ragged and frostbitten and haunted, all suddenly listening.

"That's nonsense," said Tam, the tanner, who said everything was nonsense and then did it anyway.

"Is it?" Sera looked at me. Her eyes had that flat guard's assessment in them, the look that weighed a person for threat and usefulness in the same glance. "Wren. When we found you at the gate, ten years sealed, and the first time you touched the stone it lit up under your hand—was that keeper's training? Or was that something else?"

I could have lied. I'd had ten years of practice lying about exactly this. But lying had gotten expensive lately; every secret I kept seemed to cost someone else something.

"I don't know," I said, which was true, and which was somehow worse than any lie.

* * *

Sera posted watches and made everyone make camp fifty yards off, because Sera did not trust anything that sang, and honestly neither did I. But I couldn't sleep, and around what might have been midnight—I say *might*, because up in the high country the stars wheel around like they're showing off and time goes soft at the edges—I gave up pretending and walked down to the stones alone.

The cold was the clean kind, the kind that hurts in an honest way. My breath hung around me like a thought I hadn't finished. I stepped onto the central disc, and the bending air closed around me like water, and the shard-song that Callum had been hearing for days finally reached me too.

It wasn't music. It was closer to memory—the feeling of a smell that means home, or a voice you haven't heard in years saying something kind. It came up through the soles of my boots and settled in my chest, right where my gift lived, and my gift answered.

I hadn't let it answer anything in ten years. Keeping it leashed was the first discipline they taught gatekeepers: your magic is a key, and keys don't get to decide which doors they open. But there was no one here to see, no Doss to notice, no archive inspectors, no crown. Just me and a dead waygate and a song.

I put my bare hand on the stone.

The world opened.

I don't have better words than that, and I'm supposed to be a woman who makes her living with words. The disc didn't become a door; it became a window, or maybe I became the window, and through myself I saw—

Vaelmoor.

I had seen drawings. I had heard the old keepers talk, the ones who remembered the last crossings before the sealing: towers of living glass, rivers that ran silver because the water remembered being starlight, forests where the trees grew in colors Thornfell had no names for. I had assumed half of it was embellishment, the way travelers embellish, the way exiles make their lost countries into jewels.

It was worse than the stories. It was more beautiful, and it was dying.

I saw a valley of glass towers, and half of them were dark—not broken, dark, the way a lamp is dark when the oil runs out. I saw a river that ran silver for a mile and then thinned to a trickle of plain brown water, as if the silverness itself had been drunk. I saw a forest of blue-leaved trees, and the blue was fading at the edges of the wood, leaf by leaf, going grey the way ash goes grey. And everywhere, everywhere, there was a dimness, a thinning, like watching a fire burn down to its coals and knowing no one is coming with more wood.

And I saw them. Figures moving among the dark towers. Tall, slender, wrong in the way that beautiful things are wrong—faces too long, eyes catching light that wasn't there. Fae. Real ones. Some of them moved slowly, heavily, like people wading upstream. One stood at the edge of the dying forest with her hand pressed against a grey tree, and even across the impossible distance between worlds I could see her shoulders shaking.

Ten years I had guarded the Hollow Gate believing I'd saved Thornfell from a war. Nobody had ever told me what the sealing cost the other side. Nobody had ever asked.

My hand came off the stone. I sat down hard in the frost, and my heart was going like a drum, and my breath came in white rags, and I sat there in the dark shaking like something newly born.

Because here is the thing I couldn't stop thinking, the thing that has stayed with me since, that will stay with me until I die: the fae woman crying against the tree had looked less like a monster than anyone I'd met in Thornfell that year.

* * *

"You saw it."

I hadn't heard Sera come down. That frightened me more than the vision had—a former royal guard shouldn't be able to sneak up on a trained keeper, and yet she'd done it twice now, and both times I'd been so far inside my own head I might as well have left a sign.

"Yes," I said.

"And?"

"And I think we've been lied to. All of us. For a long time." I told her what I'd seen. I watched her face while I talked, and Sera's face is a fortress, but fortresses have gates, and I watched hers open a crack when I described the woman in the forest.

"The covenant," she said slowly. "The one the gatekeepers maintained. The histories say it kept the fae *out*."

"The histories say a lot of things." I hugged my knees, staring at the faint bend in the air above the disc. "Maybe it did once. Maybe it was a wall. But walls need two sides, Sera, and whatever covenant used to run through these stones—it's failing. You can see it failing. Whole world, running dry."

"And the shard?"

"Callum's shard is a piece of the old covenant stone. If the stone broke—if the covenant shattered instead of just fraying—then a piece of it would still remember what it was supposed to be. Still sing. Still..." I trailed off, because the next part arrived in my head fully formed, the way the worst realizations do, dressed and ready. "Still call to whoever could mend it."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere up the slope, one of the sentries coughed. The stars turned overhead, indifferent and enormous.

"You're saying a nine-year-old has been carrying the most important object in two worlds inside his shirt," she said, "and we've been using it to boil water."

"We didn't know."

"No. We didn't. That's rather the theme of my life lately." She rubbed her face with both hands, and for a second she looked every year of her age and several she hadn't earned yet. Then the guard came back into her spine, vertebra by vertebra, and she said: "Nobody touches the stones tonight. Double watch. And Wren—"

"Yes?"

"If it calls you again, you wake me first. Whatever you see on the other side of that thing, you don't go looking alone."

I promised. I even mostly meant it.

* * *

It called again before dawn, and I woke Sera first, and I'm counting that as keeping my promise.

The shard was blazing now—Callum came stumbling down the slope wrapped in a blanket, wide awake for once, drawn like iron to a lodestone, and none of us had the heart or the sense to stop him. The whole camp gathered at the rim of the bowl in the grey pre-light, forty faces ghost-pale, watching the dead waygate come alive.

Because that's what it was doing. The lichen on the standing stones had begun to glow, a green-gold shimmer crawling along the old carvings, lighting letter after letter that hadn't been read aloud in a century. The air above the central disc stopped bending and started *shining*, like heat-haze filled with dawn.

And then something came through.

I want to be precise about this, because precision matters and because I'll be asked about it for the rest of my life. The figure didn't step out of the disc so much as arrive within it—one moment empty shining air, the next moment a person standing there, as if they'd always been there and we'd only just noticed. Tall. Thin. Robes the color of deep water. Skin like moonlight on snow, and eyes that caught the pre-dawn dark and gave back stars.

Every instinct Thornfell had ever taught me screamed. Every story I'd been raised on said *monster*. Ten years of guarding a sealed gate against the horrors of Vaelmoor rose up in my throat.

Then the figure took one step, staggered, and went down on one knee in the frost, and I saw how gaunt the face beneath the hood was, how the robes hung off a frame that had known real hunger, and every instinct I owned shut its mouth at once.

"Don't," Sera said sharply, because I was already moving.

"I know," I said, and kept moving.

I stopped an arm's length from the fallen envoy. Up close, the fae was neither the jewel of the stories nor the horror of them—just a person, exhausted and desperate and a long way from home. Their eyes found mine, and widened, and fixed there as though I were the marvel.

"You," the envoy said. The voice was strange, layered, like two people speaking in unison, but the accent was Thornfell's northern burr, learned from somewhere, long ago. "You carry the old blood. The gatekeeper's line." A hand, long-fingered and trembling, rose and pointed at my chest, right at the place where my gift lived. "It sings in you. Even muffled, even buried—it sings."

"My name is Wren Eriksen," I said. "Who are you?"

"Ilyen." The envoy bowed their head, still kneeling, still shaking. "Envoy of the Court of Cinders, last envoy of Vaelmoor, and I have crossed the deep places and the dying woods to find a keeper's hand." Those star-caught eyes lifted to mine, and what was in them was not menace. It was the look of a drowning person finding a rope.

"Wren Eriksen," Ilyen said, "Vaelmoor is dying. Not fading—dying. Rivers, forests, the great houses, the children. The covenant that fed our world from yours was broken, and what remains of it is being *drunk* by someone on your side of the mountains, and we cannot stop it, and we cannot survive another winter." The layered voice cracked, both speakers at once. "I beg you. I beg all of you. Help us reforge the covenant—or when it fails, it will not be one world that ends."

Behind me, forty fugitives held their breath. Beside me, Callum's shard burned like a captured sunrise. And in my chest, my hidden, hunted, ten-years-buried gift rose up and answered the envoy's song, loud and clear and unmistakable, in front of everyone.

There was no taking it back now. There was no hiding anymore.

Ilyen smiled—faint, exhausted, hopeful—and said the words that turned my stomach to ice:

"Then we must go to the Hollow Gate. Before the one who broke the covenant finishes drinking it dry."
