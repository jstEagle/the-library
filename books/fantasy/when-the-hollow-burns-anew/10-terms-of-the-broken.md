## 10. Terms of the Broken

I have interrogated prisoners, negotiated with warlords, and once spent three days in a snowdrift listening to a sergeant explain his theory about goats. None of it prepared me for the fae.

Ilyen sat on a flat stone at the edge of camp with her ankles crossed and her hands folded in her lap, as if she were waiting for a coach that was already four hours late. She had been there most of the morning. Nobody could decide whether to feed her, arrest her, or worship her, and she seemed equally unbothered by all three options. Her beauty was the kind that made you suspicious—the way a perfect stillness in the woods makes you suspect wolves. Silver-pale hair, eyes like frost over deep water, skin that caught the grey mountain light and did something unfair with it. And around all of it, faintly, like heat shimmer off cold stone, an ache. That's the only word I have. Being near her hurt a little, the way an old scar hurts before rain.

"So," I said, planting myself in front of her with my arms crossed, because if I didn't give them something to do they'd drift to my sword hilt, and I wasn't planning on needing the sword yet. "You crossed a forbidden waygate into a kingdom that's currently hanging people like us from trees. Why don't you start with that."

"Because you would not believe me until you had finished being angry," Ilyen said. Her voice had music buried in it somewhere, the way a brook under ice does. "And anger finishes faster when it is fed." She tilted her head. "You are the one called Voss. The disgraced guard."

"One word of that is accurate."

"The one who chose her people over her oath."

I felt that one land somewhere under the ribs where I keep things I don't look at directly. "Careful," I said. "The last person who read me that accurately ended up owing me money."

Something moved across her face—not quite a smile. Fae, I'd learn later, ration their smiles like besieged cities ration grain. "Then we will trade truths carefully, you and I. But first—" her gaze went past my shoulder, to where Wren stood at the camp's edge with Callum pressed against her side like a burr, "—the keeper should hear this too."

"She's not a keeper anymore," I said. "She kept gates. Past tense."

"Once a hand has held the covenant," Ilyen said, rising in a motion that seemed to skip some of the usual steps of standing, "the covenant holds the hand. She knows this. Ask her why she flinches when she touches old stone."

I didn't ask. I'd seen Wren touch old stone. I filed that away with everything else I was choosing not to say out loud, which was becoming a substantial ledger.

We gathered by the fire—my people, what was left of Greyfen: eleven souls, two of them children, all of them watching our fae visitor the way cats watch a door that opens by itself. Wren came slowly, Callum trailing her, and the boy had the shard wrapped in wool against his chest. Even through the wool, even at ten paces, I could feel it now. A hum. Like standing near a struck bell after the sound should have ended. It hadn't done that before the waygate. Things were accelerating, and I hate acceleration. Acceleration is how ambushes work.

"Tell us," Wren said. No preamble. Ten years alone at a gate will strip a person of small talk.

Ilyen looked into the fire for a long moment, and when she spoke, she spoke like someone reciting something she had known so long she'd forgotten it was knowledge at all.

"In the beginning there were two lands, and between them a wound in the world—the Hollow. Not made. Found. The old ones sealed it not to divide but to *shape* the crossing, the way you dam a river not to kill it but to make it drinkable. They built the gate, and they bound it with a covenant: that each realm would remain whole within itself, and the passage between would be tended, honored, and never forced. Three things anchored the binding. A keeper's hand, of the blood that tends thresholds. A shard of the covenant stone itself, carried among the people, so the promise would live in flesh and not merely in walls. And a truth, spoken at the gate's heart whenever the covenant must be remade—because bindings made of lies rot, and the old ones knew it."

"And the truth?" I asked. "What truth? Whose?"

Ilyen's eyes came to me, and I understood suddenly why fae envoys historically required interpreters and occasionally sedatives. "That is the cruelty of it, Captain Voss. The rite does not require *a* truth. It requires *the* truth—the one lie or silence that broke the covenant's holding. The wound in the binding must be named aloud, at the gate, by whoever carries it. And the naming costs." She paused. "Truths spoken at the Hollow are taken into the stone. The speaker gives up whatever sheltered them. Sometimes that is comfort. Sometimes it is memory. Once—" and here her voice went very quiet, and the fire seemed to lean in, "—it was a name. The woman who spoke it forgot her own daughter's face and greeted her as a stranger every day for forty years, and the covenant held, and both realms lived, and everyone agreed it was worth it. Everyone except her."

Silence. One of the kids threw another stick on the fire with excessive ceremony.

Wren had gone very still. I've watched soldiers receive death sentences with less stillness than that. I told myself it was just the weight of the story. I was getting good at telling myself things.

"And your realm is dying," Wren said. "Because the covenant broke."

"Because it is breaking," Ilyen corrected gently. "A binding frays before it fails. Vaelmoor dims a little more each season. Our rivers forget their courses. Our children are born lighter each generation, as if the land can spare less of itself for making them. We sent envoys through the wild ways—most did not arrive. I did, barely." She spread her hands, and I saw for the first time that they were too thin, the knuckles sharp. Whatever glamour dressed her, it was running low on fuel. "Someone on your side of the mountains is tearing at the binding deliberately, and pulling what comes loose through machines of iron and grief. I have felt the drain. It flows toward your capital's inner circle, and toward one house above all others."

"Halloway," I said.

"You know the name."

"I know the name. I also know names get thrown around camps like knives in a bar fight. But yes. Halloway." I'd served under men who took Halloway's coin before my disgrace, back when I still thought royal gold meant royal purpose. It fit. Cruelty always fit somewhere, once you found the joint.

"So we go to the Hollow Gate," Wren said, in the flat voice she used for facts, "and we speak the truth, and reforge it."

"That is the shape of it."

"And the truth might be anything," I said, and I heard my own voice harden, because I could see where this road went and I wanted to stand in the middle of it with a barricade. "Might be. Whoever broke the covenant—whoever's silence or lie started all this—they pay. What if it's not one person? What if it's a family? A guild? What if the truth belongs to a dead man and the living inherit the bill?"

"I cannot say," Ilyen said. "The stone chooses its debtor."

"Wonderful," I said. "A magical debt collector. Just what this expedition needed."

But Wren wasn't looking at Ilyen. She was looking at the fire, and her hands were folded in her lap in exactly the posture of a woman sitting on something, and I want it noted, for the record, by whoever keeps records of fools, that I saw it. I saw it and I thought: *she's frightened, anyone would be frightened, the price is monstrous.* I did not think: *she's frightened because she recognizes the bill.*

Callum raised his hand, actually raised it, like this was lessons, and something in my chest cracked sideways despite everything. "If the truth gets taken," he said, "does it—does it fix what it broke? Or does it just... go somewhere? Get stored?"

Ilyen regarded him with more gentleness than she'd shown any of us. "It becomes the mortar, little carrier. It does not erase the harm. It makes the next thing possible. There is no going back through a covenant. Only forward, differently."

The kid nodded like he'd gotten a satisfying answer, which told me more about his childhood than I wanted to know.

* * *

That evening I found Wren checking the picket lines, which was her habit—she couldn't fight, couldn't hunt, so she guarded us the only way she knew, counting heads and reading weather. I fell in beside her. For a while neither of us said anything, which is the language Wren and I speak most fluently.

"You believe her," I said finally.

"Yes."

"You believe all of it. Machines, drains, Halloway, the dying world."

"Yes."

"Then you understand what she's actually saying." I stopped and turned to face her. Snow was starting again, fat lazy flakes that lied about how serious they were. "This isn't a quest, Wren. It's a trial. We're walking a thousand miles to a place where something is going to reach into somebody's chest and pull out the worst thing they've ever done, and use it as mortar. And nobody knows whose chest."

"I understand."

"Do you?" I grabbed her shoulder, maybe harder than necessary. "Because there are twelve people behind that fire who have decided you're the answer. Callum thinks you walk on water. Tam's kids follow you around like ducklings. If the stone decides the debt is yours—"

"It won't be mine," Wren said.

It came out fast. Too fast. The kind of fast that means rehearsed.

I let go of her shoulder. The snow kept falling. Somewhere down the slope, one of the horses complained about the weather, voicing my own position.

"Okay," I said slowly. "Say more about that."

"There's nothing to say. I sealed nothing. I broke nothing. I was a girl who took a posting and kept it." Her chin was up, her voice steady, her eyes anywhere but mine. I'd spent twenty years reading recruits and criminals and I am telling you, that performance would have fooled no one in the history of performances.

"Wren." I kept my voice level. That took effort; effort leaves marks. "I followed you into the deep passes because you knew roads I didn't. I put my people's lives in your judgment every day since Greyfen burned. Every single day. You want to carry secrets, that's your right. But secrets that touch the thing we're walking toward—that's not privacy anymore. That's weight distribution on a rope bridge. I need to know what's on the bridge."

For one heartbeat—one—I thought she'd tell me. Her mouth opened. Something moved behind her eyes like a fish under ice.

Then it closed.

"If the truth were mine, Sera," she said quietly, "I'd speak it. You have to believe that."

"I don't have to believe anything," I said. "That's the luxury of being wrong as often as I've been."

I walked away. Classy, I know. Twenty years of soldiering and my conflict resolution style remains *walk away before you say the true thing*, which is ironic given the subject matter, and I'm aware of it, thank you.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat up with the second watch and a flask of birch-bark tea that tasted like punishment and watched Ilyen sit alone at the camp's edge, dimming slightly as the hours passed, like a lamp running out of oil. Around midnight she spoke without turning her head—fae hearing, apparently, extends to grumpy ex-guards brooding at thirty paces.

"You doubt her," she said.

"I doubt everyone. It's a professional discipline."

"And yet you would die for her."

"I'd die for the boy," I said. "And the kids. And Tam, because he's a decent cook. Wren I'd follow into trouble, which is different and worse."

Ilyen considered that. "In Vaelmoor we have a saying: the bridge does not fear the flood, only the crack it cannot see." She turned those frost-water eyes on me. "You have seen the crack, Captain. You simply do not yet know what you saw."

"Don't do that," I said sharply. "The cryptic oracle routine. If you know something, say it plainly. We're not courtiers, we're refugees."

"What I know is not mine to tell," she said. "That is the oldest law of envoys, older than your kingdom. But I will tell you this plainly, since you asked: the covenant demands truth, and truth has a gravity. It pulls toward the light. Everything that hides strains harder the closer it comes to the gate." She rose, unfolding upward in that impossible economical way. "Watch her at the gate, Captain. Not before. Before, she is only a woman carrying something heavy, and she has carried it well enough to save your lives twice over. That counts. In my country, it counts a great deal."

She drifted off toward wherever fae sleep, presumably standing in a puddle for aesthetic reasons, leaving me with my tea and my thoughts, neither of which improved.

Here's the thing I kept circling: she was right, and I hated it. Wren had saved Callum on the passes with magic she'd hidden for ten years, spent openly at last, and it had nearly killed her and she'd done it anyway without hesitation. Whatever she was carrying, she wasn't carrying it *instead* of us. She was carrying it alongside us. There's a difference, and I've bet lives on smaller differences.

But Ilyen had said one other thing that gnawed at me, and it took until the grey edge of dawn to surface: *everything that hides strains harder the closer it comes to the gate.* Wren flinched at old stone. Wren had gone white when Ilyen described the truth-taker. Wren had said *it won't be mine* like a password she'd memorized.

I poured out the tea. Some things even punishment-tea can't wash down.

At first light, the scouts came down off the ridge at a run, and the morning stopped being about any of this. Riders on the southern flank—crown colors, six of them, moving deliberate and unhurried, the way men move when they're herding rather than hunting. Behind them, far off, a thin dark line of smoke rose from the valley we'd meant to pass through, sitting in the sky like a signature.

They weren't chasing us. They were steering us.

And the direction they steered us, of course, was north. Toward the Hollow Gate.

Toward home, Wren whispered, when I showed her the ridge—and there was something in her voice that had nothing to do with home at all.
