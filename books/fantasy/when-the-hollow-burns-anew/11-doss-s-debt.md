## 11. Doss's Debt

There are two kinds of men who follow a woman into the mountains to beg forgiveness: the kind who bring flowers, and the kind who bring something useful.

I brought ledgers.

Three of them, wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to my back like a penance I could carry, because I'd spent four days telling myself that paper was heavier than it looked. Route schedules. Patrol rotations. The crown's entire marching order for the high passes, copied out in my own cramped hand by candlelight while my conscience gnawed on me like a wet dog with an old bone. If they hanged me for what I'd done to Wren Eriksen—and Sera Voss looked like a woman who kept rope for exactly such occasions—then at least the ledgers would outlive me. Paper's patient that way. It doesn't care whose neck paid for it.

I found them at dusk, three days north of where I'd last seen them, in a hollow below a ridge the locals called the Shepherd's Tooth. Smoke, thin as a secret, rising from behind a screen of boulders. I'd have missed it if I hadn't spent twenty years learning to read smoke the way priests read scripture. Smugglers and fugitives hide their fires the same way. It's one of the few honest things we share.

I stood at the edge of the camp with my hands visible—empty, palms out, the universal language of *please don't put an arrow in me*—and waited.

It didn't take long. The big one, Marta, came around the boulders with a wood axe held low and casual, which is how you hold an axe when you want people to know you're not casual about it at all.

"Doss," she said.

"Marta." I gave her my best smile, the one that had talked toll-keepers out of tolls and magistrates out of warrants. It felt obscene on my face. "You look well."

"You've got nerve coming here."

"I've got ledgers, too. Nerve just happened to be closer to hand."

Her eyes flicked past me, counting whatever she counted—exits, accomplices, the exact weight of my guilt measured against the reach of her arm. Then she jerked her chin toward the fire. "Voss will want to do this herself."

"I rather expected she would."

* * *

Sera Voss did not disappoint.

She came out of the firelight like weather, and the whole camp came with her—a dozen faces peering from bedrolls and shadow, and among them, sitting closest to the flames with a boy asleep against her shoulder, Wren Eriksen.

That was the worst part. Not Voss, with her guard's stance and her hand resting on a knife she clearly knew how to use. Not the suspicion written on every face like a debt notice nailed to a church door. The worst part was Wren looking up at me with no hatred at all. Just a kind of tired recognition, the way you look at a storm you'd already heard coming.

"Doss," she said quietly. Callum stirred but didn't wake.

"Wren." My voice cracked on the second syllable, which was mortifying, so I plowed through it. "Before your captain decides anything permanent, I'd ask you to look at what's in this oilcloth. Then you can decide everything permanent you like."

I unwrapped the ledgers and laid them on a flat stone between us. Three books. Patrol schedules for the northern passes, garrison strengths at the way-stations, supply convoy timetables marked with dates through midwinter. Everything I'd bought my pardon with—or rather, everything I'd been willing to sell before I understood the price tag wasn't mine to pay.

Sera didn't touch them. She looked at me instead, the way a butcher looks at meat, assessing cuts.

"Kane's schedules," she said. "The hunter who burned Greyfen."

"The same. He bought my silence with a pardon and my information with a threat. I gave him Wren's route. I told him which pass she'd take, when she'd take it, who she'd be traveling with." I swallowed. "He was going to kill my sister. Or worse—he was going to make sure I watched him not kill her, forever, whenever he wanted a favor. I'm not telling you this to excuse myself. There is no excusing myself. I'm telling you so you understand I know precisely what I am."

"And what are you?" One of the others—a lean man with burn scars, Greyfen stock by his look—spat into the dirt. "A rat. Rats come back for the grain."

"That's fair," I said. "Though in my defense, rats don't usually bring the grain back."

"Doss." Wren's voice cut through, quiet and level. Callum had woken; she shifted him behind her shoulder without seeming to move at all. "Why now? What changed?"

And there it was. The question I'd been walking four days to answer, rehearsing versions of it on every switchback, none of which survived contact with her actual face.

I could have lied. God knows lying was my native tongue; I'd spoken it before I spoke Thornfell common. I could have said I'd heard what Halloway was doing with the harvested gifted, though I only half-knew it, rumor braided with rumor. I could have said Kane had double-crossed me, which was true in spirit if not in letter.

Instead I said: "A child asked me for bread."

The camp went still.

"Day after the ambush," I said. "I was back in Ashford, spending my pardon, drinking to convince myself it tasted like anything. A girl came to my table—eight, maybe nine years old, selling matches nobody buys in summer. And she had the gift. Small thing. She warmed her own hands with it when the wind came through the door, just a flicker, the way you'd rub your palms together. Habit. Nobody noticed except me, because I knew what to look for—I'd spent ten years watching Wren do the same trick over her tea and pretend she hadn't." My throat closed. I pushed through. "And I thought: there's a list. There's a list with names on it, and someone wrote Wren Eriksen's name on it because I handed them the ink. And that girl is going to be on it too, someday, because people like me keep handing over the ink."

I looked at the ledgers on the stone.

"So. That's why now. Not honor. Not courage. A child warming her hands." I spread my arms, palms up again. "Hang me if you need to. But read these first. There are convoys moving through the passes every eight days carrying supplies to a crown facility in the eastern valleys—Halloway's operation. Whatever he's building, these schedules tell you when it's blind."

* * *

They argued. Of course they argued. I sat outside the ring of firelight like a debtor waiting on the magistrate, listening to my own trial conducted in murmurs I wasn't meant to hear and mostly couldn't.

Marta wanted me gone—walked to the pass edge and sent off with nothing, or walked off the edge entirely, opinions varied. The scarred man, Bren by name, wanted the middle option, which was to say he wanted me hanged but worried about the noise. An old woman named Grethe argued the ledgers were worth more than my corpse, which I appreciated even if she delivered it like an appraisal of livestock.

And Sera Voss wanted blood. I could hear it in the flatness of her voice when she finally spoke loud enough to carry.

"He sold her. He sold all of us, the moment he sold her route, because she was coming to us. Greyfen burned because of men like him—men who chose their own skin and called it survival." A pause. "I buried nineteen people at Greyfen. I'm not sentimental about rats."

Then Wren's voice, lower, and I had to strain for it.

"Sera. Listen to me. I've known Doss since I was seventeen. He taught me the passes. He fed me through two winters when the gatekeeper's wage wouldn't cover bread. He is a coward, a liar, a drunk, and a thief, and he sold me, and I have spent four days being angrier about it than I have ever been about anything." A breath. "And he came back. Nobody made him. Kane's pardon was safe. Ashford was safe. He walked four days into the mountains carrying ledgers to the people with the best reason in Thornfell to kill him. That's not courage—that's not what I'd call courage—but it's the shape courage leaves in a man when most of it's been carved away. I say we use him. And I say we watch him like a hawk does, and the moment he flinches wrong, Sera, he's yours."

Silence. Long enough that I started composing my own eulogy, just in case. *Doss Merren: he talked too much and it finally caught up with him.*

Then Sera came around the fire.

She stopped an arm's length away, close enough that I could see the Greyfen ash still under her fingernails, and studied me the way she probably once studied enemy positions from a ridge.

"Here's how it will be," she said. "You eat last. You sleep apart, down-slope, where Marta can reach you in six steps. You speak when spoken to. You give us everything in those ledgers, twice, and Bren checks your figures. And if we take a road because you drew it for us, and there's an ambush on that road—" she leaned in, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, "—there won't be a trial next time. There won't be a discussion. Do you understand me?"

"Perfectly," I said. "It's the fairest offer I've had all year."

"It isn't an offer."

"No," I agreed. "But it's fair."

She turned away, then stopped. "Wren argues for you. Remember that. Every kindness you get in this camp comes through her, and every time you fail, it lands on her. Weigh that."

"I will," I said, and meant it more than I've meant most things.

* * *

They let me near the fire after the ledgers were read. Not beside anyone—Grethe's spot, once she'd retired—but near enough for warmth, which in the mountains is a currency richer than gold.

Callum came and sat across from me after a while. The boy with the shard. I'd seen it glinting at his throat when his collar shifted, a sliver of grey stone that seemed to hold light the way a well holds sky. He watched me with the frank, unnerving stare of children and cats.

"You're the one who betrayed her," he said.

"Word travels fast."

"She said you were the one who taught her the stars."

I blinked. "Did she."

"For navigation. When the clouds cover the peaks, you can't trust landmarks, but the stars don't move." He hugged his knees. "She taught us that on the passes. She said a smuggler taught her, and he was the best liar she ever knew, but he never lied about the stars."

I looked into the fire for a while. It's a good trick, looking into fires. Gives your face somewhere to be.

"Well," I managed. "There you have it. My life's single honest enterprise, and it's astronomy."

"What's astronomy?"

"Stars. Knowing their names and habits. Like knowing people, except stars don't betray you."

"Everything betrays you," Callum said, with the terrible simplicity of the orphaned. "People just do it slower than stars."

I had no answer for that, so I dug in my pack—the one possession they hadn't confiscated, because it smelled too much of hard travel to interest anyone—and found what I was looking for. A twist of dried apple, the last of the store I'd carried out of Ashford. I tossed it to him.

"Bribery," I said. "I'm told I'm good at it. This is the honest version."

He caught it, considered it, ate half, and pocketed the rest with the prudence of a child who has learned food is a story with an ending. Then he said, "Wren cried. After the ambush. When she thought we were sleeping."

"Callum—"

"I'm not saying it to hurt you. I'm saying it so you know what it cost. She doesn't cry easy. Sera says soldiers cry easy and survivors don't." He shrugged. "So now you know the price. That's all."

He got up and went back to Wren's side, and I sat there with the fire eating my face and the arithmetic of forgiveness turning over in my head like coins I'd never be able to count. Because that's what it was, I realized. Forgiveness isn't a door that opens or shuts. It's a ledger—of course it was a ledger, everything in my life was a ledger—and every entry had to be paid down line by line, in bread and honesty and stars correctly named, and some lines would never clear no matter how long I lived. Wren's tears were a debt I couldn't repay. The girl in Ashford warming her hands was a debt I could only pay forward, blindly, to strangers.

Fine. I'd always been better at installment plans than redemption anyway.

* * *

We broke camp before dawn, as fugitives do, and I learned my place in the machine of them: last in line, first suspected, useful as a mule and trusted slightly less. Bren walked beside me for the first hour, checking my figures aloud, and I discovered he had a smuggler's mind under the burn scars—he caught my one deliberate error inside ten minutes, a falsified convoy date I'd included to test whether anyone actually read the things.

"Clever," he said, marking it. "Stupid, but clever. Don't test us again."

"Noted."

Near midmorning we crested a saddle and the eastern valleys opened below us, and I saw what the schedules had been hiding in plain sight. Down in the grey distance, where no facility had any business being, smoke rose—not cookfire smoke, not forge smoke, but the steady industrial breath of furnaces running day and night. And along the valley road, small with distance, a convoy crawled eastward. Right on schedule. My schedule.

Sera stood at the saddle's crest with a spyglass pressed to her eye, and I watched her shoulders change. Watched the fury in her go still and cold, which in that woman was far worse than hot.

"There are cages on those wagons," she said quietly. "Ilyen. Can you see?"

The fae envoy drifted up beside her—still strange to my eye, all silver and shadow, like moonlight given opinions—and looked long through the glass. When she lowered it, her face had gone the color of the winter sky.

"They are children," Ilyen said. "Some of them are very young."

Nobody spoke. The wind moved the dead grass. Beside me, Wren stared down at that distant smoke with an expression I recognized from mirrors, from the night I'd signed away her route to save my own skin: the look of a person watching the bill arrive for something they'd helped order.

Sera turned, and her eyes found me first, and I braced for the rope.

Instead she said, "Your schedules, Doss. The convoy's route. Every checkpoint, every rest halt, every hour it's blind." Her jaw tightened. "Because we're not letting that wagon reach wherever it's going."

I already had the page open in my head. Rest halt at the Miller's Bend crossing, two hours past dusk, guards rotated, sightlines broken by the river gorge.

"Miller's Bend," I said. "Tonight. And Sera—you'll want to hurry. They don't wait long, and they don't stop twice."
