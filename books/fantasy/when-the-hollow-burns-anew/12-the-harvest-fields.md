## 12. The Harvest Fields

Miller's Bend was the kind of place smugglers loved and honest people never thought about twice: a crook in the river where the gorge pinched shut and the road narrowed to a single wagon's width, hemmed by rock on one side and black water on the other. Doss had called it "the purse-string" once, back when I still trusted him to mean anything he said. Cut it, and everything spilled out.

We cut it.

Sera split us into three knots before full dark—herself and the strongest fighters at the road's throat, Tam and two of the Greyfen archers up on the rocks with bows that had seen better decades, and me with Callum tucked into a hollow beneath an alder, close enough to see and far enough to run. I'd argued for giving him a knife. Sera had argued for tying him to a tree. We compromised on neither, which is how most things between us got settled.

"You stay here," I told him, crouching in the dark. "You don't move unless I come get you. You don't shout unless something's about to kill you."

"What if something's about to kill *you*?"

"Then shout very loudly."

He almost smiled. It was a thin, cold night for smiles, but he managed one, and I found it did more for my nerves than the knife would have.

The shard hung against his chest under his shirt, and even from a handspan away I could feel it—that faint wrongness in the air, like the moment before lightning. It had been humming all day, low and constant, since we'd turned onto the convoy road. I hadn't said anything. Neither had he. Some things you both hear and agree not to name.

The wagon came at half past dusk, exactly as Doss's stolen schedule promised, which was its own small insult—of course Doss's information was good. He'd always been good at the parts of his trade that mattered. Lanterns swung at the wagon's front and rear, six guards walking, four more on horses, and the driver hunched over the reins like a man who wanted very much to be somewhere else. The wagon itself was long and high-sided, canvas stretched over hoops, and it moved slowly, carefully, the way you'd carry glass. Or eggs. Or people.

I remember thinking: *it's supplies*. Weapons, grain, coin for garrisons. I remember thinking it because I needed to think it, because the alternative was the hum in my bones and the way the shard's song had climbed all afternoon, sweet and terrible, like a choir tuning toward a note no throat should hold.

The purse-string closed.

Tam's first arrow took the lead rider's horse rather than the man—Sera's orders, always the horses first—and the second took the lantern out of the rear guard's hand, and then the dark came down over Miller's Bend like a lid. I heard Sera's voice cut through it, hard and clear: *"Down! Blades down or die holding them!"* She meant it. The Greyfen people had spent a season being hunted; they had no appetite for mercy and less patience for men who chose crowns over conscience.

It was over fast. Two guards ran. One didn't get far. The rest knelt in the mud with their hands laced behind their heads while Sera walked the line of them like a schoolmistress deciding who'd done their sums.

I went for the wagon.

The canvas was tied down with proper knots—crown-issue, military—and my fingers were shaking so badly I fumbled the first one twice. Callum appeared at my elbow without having been summoned, because of course he had; telling that boy to stay put was like telling the river not to bend. He didn't speak. He just held the lantern up and kept it steady while I worked, and when the last knot gave and I dragged the canvas back—

I stopped being able to think at all.

Cages. Rows of them, narrow as coffins, stacked two high along the wagon's length. And in the cages, people. Thin, gray-lipped, eyes too bright, wrists wrapped in bands of dull metal that pulsed with a light I knew—I *knew*—because I'd felt its cousin every time my own gift flared against the Hollow Gate's stone. Copper and salt and something older underneath. The bands were drinking them. You could see it happening, the way you can see a candle drowning in its own wax: each prisoner dimmer than the empty air around them, their magic siphoned off through the bands into conduits that ran the length of the wagon and terminated in a black iron box bolted to the driver's bench, stamped with a seal I recognized from ten years of crown correspondence.

A stag under a sunburst. Lord Halloway's mark.

"Wren." Callum's voice, very small. "Wren, there's children."

There were. A girl no older than eight, curled at the bottom of her cage with her knees drawn up, her band glowing steady and obscene. A boy maybe twelve, awake, watching me with the flat, patient look of someone who has stopped expecting anything from anyone.

Something in my chest went very quiet and very cold, and underneath the quiet, something else began to burn.

* * *

Getting them out was its own kind of nightmare. The bands wouldn't unbuckle—they weren't buckles, they were seals—and in the end it was Callum who solved it, or the shard who solved it through him. He pressed the stone against the first band and it shrieked, a sound like a nail dragged across the world, and cracked open, and the girl inside gasped like a diver surfacing. After that we worked down the rows, shard to band, band to cage, while Sera's people formed a ring around us and the kneeling guards watched with expressions I couldn't read and didn't try to.

Seventeen prisoners. That was the count. Seventeen people stacked in a supply wagon like barrels of salted fish, bound for wherever Halloway's machines were waiting.

Fourteen adults. Three children.

And one old woman in the last cage, whose face, when the band fell away and the lantern light reached it, stopped my heart in my chest and then started it again, badly.

"Keeper," she whispered. Her voice was ash. "Oh, you foolish, stubborn girl. Look at you."

Maren.

My hands were on the cage bars before I knew I'd moved. Maren, who had taught me letters on the gatehouse floor with a stick in the dust. Maren, who had taught me to fold my gift small and keep it folded, who used to say *a flame hidden is a flame saved, Wren-girl*, who had vanished from Thornfell eleven years ago and been mourned by exactly one person, quietly, at a sealed gate in the mountains. They'd told me she'd gone south to family. I'd believed it because believing it was easier than asking questions I couldn't afford answered.

"They took me at Corrow Ford," she said, when I'd gotten the cage open and her half into my arms. She weighed nothing. When had she come to weigh nothing? "Two years past. Maybe three. The days don't—" She coughed, wet and deep, and I felt the shape of what the cough meant before she said it. "The days don't sort themselves anymore."

"Don't talk. We're getting you out. There are healers at—" At where? Greyfen was ash. Our road led up into passes where a dying woman would be dead before the second ridge. I heard myself say it anyway, because the alternative was unsayable. "There are healers."

"There are no healers for this, child." She touched my cheek with fingers like winter twigs. Her eyes, though. Her eyes were still hers, sharp and warm and faintly amused, the eyes of a woman who had spent forty years keeping secrets and enjoying the work. "But listen. Listen to me, because I haven't the time to say it twice and you've never been good at listening twice."

"I'm listening."

"The bands. The iron box. Where do you think it all goes?" Her grip tightened, surprising strength in it. "Not to the capital. Not to Halloway's houses. Follow the conduit, Wren. Follow it north. Into the mountains. To the Gate."

The word landed in me like a stone dropped down a well.

"That's impossible. The Gate is sealed. I sealed it. There's nothing to *drain*—"

"There is if someone's found a way to crack the seal a hair's width at a time. To bleed it." She smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all night, worse than the cages, because it was Maren's teaching-smile, the one she wore when a student finally stood at the edge of a truth. "You thought you were the only one who ever touched that stone? You thought the covenant was yours to end and no one else's to profit from? Someone has been drinking from the Hollow for years, girl. Slowly. Carefully. Every gifted soul they harvest buys another cupful of Vaelmoor's life, poured down some lord's throat."

The world tilted. I gripped the cage bars to keep my feet.

Vaelmoor. The fading country beyond the Gate—the reason for the covenant, the reason for my ten gray years of patrol and silence. I had sealed the passage believing I was saving Thornfell from war, and I had known, in the buried place where I kept the things I refused to look at, that sealing it would starve the other side. But this—this was different. This was not starvation by neglect. This was a tap. A spigot. Someone had made Vaelmoor's dying into an industry, and the wagons, the hunts, the purge, the seventeen souls in these cages—all of it, all of it, was freight for that tap.

"Wren." Sera's voice behind me. She'd seen Maren's face, or mine, or both. "What is it?"

"It's my teacher," I said, and my voice came out strange and level, which frightened me more than screaming would have. "And she's just told me where the wagons go."

* * *

We couldn't take the wagon. Too slow, too conspicuous, and the iron box hummed with harvested magic that made everyone's skin crawl and Callum's shard sing loud enough to give away our position to any hunter within a mile. So we stripped what we could carry—blankets, a sack of hard bread, the prisoners themselves, walking those who could walk—and Sera gave the kneeling guards a choice that wasn't one: walk south and tell your masters whatever keeps you alive, or walk south and tell them nothing at all. Then Tam put an arrow through the iron box, which screamed and died and left a smell like burnt sugar, and we melted into the hills with seventeen ghosts.

Maren lasted until the third rest halt, near dawn, in the lee of a fallen oak. I sat with her head in my lap and fed her snowmelt drop by drop, and she talked, because she was Maren, and talking was how she fought.

"You'll want to hate yourself," she said. "For the sealing. For not looking. Don't waste the hour."

"You don't know what I—"

"I know exactly what you did. I helped you decide to do it." Her laugh was a ruin. "You think you crept up to that stone alone one night? Who taught you the rite, Wren-girl? Who told you the words? I was there. I've carried it longer than you have." She coughed again, and I held her through it, and when it passed she went on quieter. "We were wrong. Or we were right, and rightness curdled. Doesn't matter now. What matters is the reforging. You've the hand for it. I always said so."

"Maren—"

"And you've the shard-bearer already, I see." Her eyes flicked to Callum, sitting watchful a few feet away, and something passed across her face—recognition, grief, relief, I couldn't sort them. "Good. Good boy. Keep her honest."

"I will," Callum said solemnly, as though accepting a commission.

She smiled at that, a real one, and then looked up at me, and her voice dropped to the register she'd used for exactly two lessons in my whole childhood: the ones that mattered.

"The truth at the Gate, when you speak it—it has to be the whole weight. Not the tidy version. Not the version where you were young and meant well. All of it." Her fingers found my wrist. "Promise me you won't flinch. Flinching is how Halloway wins. He's built his whole machine on other people flinching."

"I promise."

"Liar," she said fondly. "But you'll get there."

She died twenty minutes later, as the sky went gray behind the eastern ridges, mid-sentence, in the middle of telling me about a smuggler she'd loved in the years before I was born—a story I will now never hear the end of, which feels like the truest cruelty death manages. Sera stood at a respectful distance with her helmet under her arm, and when it was done she said nothing, which from Sera was a eulogy.

We buried her under the oak with stones for a marker and Callum's shard laid briefly against the earth, so that, as he put it, "the ground could remember her properly." I don't know if the shard does that. I let him believe it does. Some beliefs are load-bearing.

* * *

We walked all that day, and I did not cry, and I did not sleep, and by the time we made camp in a hollow above the treeline I had stopped feeling like a person and started feeling like a drawn bow.

Sera found me at the fire's edge, sharpening a knife I didn't need sharpened.

"Talk," she said.

"About what?"

"About whatever plan is forming behind your eyes, because I've commanded soldiers going into a breach and I know the look. You're not grieving anymore. Grieving ended sometime around noon. Now you're aiming."

She was right, which was irritating. I set the knife down.

"Halloway's operation runs north," I said. "Every wagon, every harvest, all of it funneled toward the Hollow Gate. He's bleeding Vaelmoor through a crack in my seal, and he's using the purge to stock the pump. Which means the purge isn't fear, Sera. It isn't policy. It's *logistics*. My neighbors, the criers, the lists—they're a supply chain."

"I know."

"You don't—not yet, not all of it." I pulled the map from my coat, the one Doss's schedules had let me annotate, and spread it on my knee. "Here. Here, and here. Three convoys in the next fortnight, all converging on the mountain roads. He's moving the heart of the operation to the Gate itself—I'd stake Maren's grave on it. Which means that when we go to reopen the covenant, we won't be sneaking past his soldiers."

"No," Sera said quietly. "We'll be walking into his granary."

"He's expecting fugitives. Runaways. A keeper who's spent ten years hiding." I looked up at her, and the cold thing in my chest had stopped burning rage and become something steadier, something with edges. "He is not expecting us to come for the machinery."

Sera studied me for a long moment. Firelight moved on her scarred face, and whatever she saw in mine seemed to settle some question she'd been carrying since Greyfen burned.

"All right, Keeper," she said. "Show me where we hit him first."

Behind us, unnoticed, Callum slept with the shard against his heart—and even in his sleep, even through his shirt, it glowed. Faintly. Steadily. Pointing north, like a compass needle made of light, toward the Hollow and everything waiting there.
