## 13. Kane's Choice

I have been called many things in my thirty-four years: prodigy, traitor, hound, monster. The last two came from the same mouths on the same day, which tells you something about the consistency of public opinion. But the word that has followed me longest, the one I've worn like a coat I stopped noticing, is *loyal*. Aldric Kane, loyal hunter of the crown. Loyal to Halloway. Loyal to the hunt.

It took a barn full of caged children to teach me what that word was actually made of.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. A bad habit of mine—Halloway always said so. *Kane, you narrate your own disasters before they finish happening.* He said it with a smile, back when he still bothered smiling at me. Back when I was useful.

Three days after Greyfen burned, I stood in a freezing courtyard outside a facility with no name and read a ledger I was never meant to see.

* * *

Let me explain how a man becomes a hound, since apparently I'm in the mood for confessions.

The gift found me at nine years old—the old gift, the fae-touched thing, sparks of knowing that let me feel where a person had been, warm traces on cold air. My father, a pious man with a respectable position and one son too many, did not celebrate. He reported me. That was the law then, gentler than the law now: report the gifted, and they'd be "examined." Most were released. Some were not.

I was examined for eleven weeks. What they learned, chiefly, was that I was more frightened of the examiners than of my own magic, and that fear could be shaped. A man named Corren—dead now, thank all the quiet gods—took an interest. He taught me to use the gift as a tracking sense instead of hiding it, and to hate it a little while doing so, because hatred keeps you obedient. By twenty I was wearing the crown's grey coat. By twenty-five I was the best hound they had, and everyone said it like a compliment.

Here's the arithmetic that kept me sleeping at night, such as it was: every gifted fugitive I dragged in was one more dangerous witch off the streets. Dangerous to whom was a question I'd learned not to ask, the way you learn not to press a bruise.

Then Greyfen burned, and I watched Sera Voss—a woman I'd once trained beside, before her disgrace, or possibly because of it; accounts differ—throw herself between crown hunters and children who could barely light candles with their little gifts. And the bruise started talking back.

* * *

The facility sat in a fold of hills three days south of the mountains, disguised as a quarantine station for fevered livestock. Halloway sent me there with forty men and simple orders: intercept the prisoners freed from the harvest fields by persons unknown—Wren Eriksen and her band, though Halloway didn't say the name with anything so human as anger; he said it the way a merchant notes a discrepancy in inventory—and recover the assets.

Assets. I want that word to sit uncomfortably with you, because it sat increasingly uncomfortably with me.

The trouble began when we arrived a day early and found the place understaffed, underfed, and drowning in its own noise. Not alarm noise. Machine noise. A deep grinding hum that rose through the ground itself, felt in the teeth before the ears, coming from beneath the quarantine barns.

My lieutenant, Brask—a solid man, the kind whose loyalty is indistinguishable from incuriosity—wanted to report to the steward and wait. But my gift was already pulling. Warm traces everywhere, hundreds of them, layered and fresh, seeping out through the barn walls like heat through a cracked window. Traces of people. Many people. Very young people, if the traces were any measure, and they always were; that was the whole point of me.

"I'll inspect," I said.

"Steward says no inspections without writ."

"The writ is in my head, Brask, and it says I outrank the steward." This was true in spirit and false in letter, a combination I had refined into an art form over ten years of service.

The first barn held machines. I don't have better words than that, and I've tried. Copper coils tall as men, arranged in rows, humming with that tooth-deep grind, each one fed by conduits running down through the floor toward the hill's heart. And threaded through every coil, faint as breath on glass, the shimmer of harvested gift. Magic siphoned, filtered, stored. I put my hand near a coil and my own gift flinched away like a dog from a whip.

The second barn held the source.

* * *

Cages. Row upon row of cages, the kind built for hunting dogs, except these were large, and most of them held children.

I want to be honest about the next few seconds, because honesty is the only currency I have left. My first feeling was not horror. It was calculation. That's what ten years of service does to a man: his soul reaches for the ledger before it reaches for grief. *Why would Halloway keep children here? Are they breeding them? Training them? Is this a reserve, a larder—*

Larder.

The word arrived and everything behind it collapsed at once, all the careful architecture of justification I'd built over fifteen years, floor by floor, like a building being demolished in reverse. Because I understood then—not suspected, *understood*, in the way my gift understands, whole and instant—that the machines above were drinking from the cages below, and that the cages below were only the newest stock, and that Greyfen's burned ruins and Ashford's dragged-away neighbors and every name on every list I'd hunted for a decade had been flowing through copper coils toward the mountains, toward Vaelmoor's fading heart, feeding a dying world so that a handful of men in the capital could go on pretending the covenant hadn't shattered.

The hunt wasn't protection. It never had been. It was harvest logistics.

A girl in the nearest cage looked up at me. Eight years old, maybe nine, with the hollow-eyed patience of someone who had learned that screaming changes nothing. Her gift flickered around her like a dying lamp—something small and green and growing-shaped, a hedge-witch's magic, the kind that makes beans sprout and cuts heal faster. The kind that hurt nobody in the entire history of the world.

She looked at my grey coat and pulled her knees closer and waited for whatever happened next.

And Aldric Kane, loyal hound of the crown, stood in a barn that smelled of straw and copper and unwashed children, and discovered that he had been on the wrong side of a war for fifteen years and nobody had even bothered to tell him there was a war.

* * *

I didn't do anything dramatic. I want that recorded. No torches thrown, no gates torn open, no speech. Dramatic gestures get people killed, chiefly oneself, and I had just developed a strong preference for staying alive long enough to be useful.

I walked out of the barn. I walked past Brask, who asked if everything was in order, and I said yes, because my voice worked by pure mechanical habit even while the rest of me was rubble. I found the steward's office, borrowed his records while he fetched wine for men who outranked him, and read for an hour.

The ledgers were meticulous. Halloway demanded meticulousness; sloppiness offended him more than cruelty ever had, which should have told me something years ago. Shipments in. Yield per subject. Depletion curves. Notes on which ages produced cleanest flow, written in the same tidy hand that once annotated tactical maps I'd studied with something approaching reverence.

There was a schedule, too. Transfers to the mountain operation. Departure dates, routes, escort strengths. Including one line, dated four days hence: *Consolidation of assets at Hollow Gate site, per Lord Halloway's directive. Terminal extraction authorized.*

Terminal. I read that word three times before its meaning stopped sliding off me.

They were going to drain the gate itself. And everyone in those cages was going to be emptied to the last drop doing it.

* * *

That night the fugitives walked into us.

Not on purpose—they couldn't have known our position; even I had to admire the terrible luck of it. Wren Eriksen's ragged column, maybe two dozen freed prisoners and half a dozen armed misfits, came down the western hollow road in the dark hours and walked straight into my picket line. By dawn I had them boxed in a shallow ravine with crossbows on the ridges, and Brask asking, cheerfully, whether we'd take them alive.

Eriksen stepped out front. Ten years guarding the Hollow Gate had made her lean and weather-colored, a woman shaped by stone and wind, and she stood in the trampled snow with her hands empty and her chin up like someone who had rehearsed dignity until it fit. Behind her, Sera Voss had a sword half-drawn and murder arranged very neatly on her face. Between them, the boy—Callum, the ledgers called him, carrier of the shard, asset of unusual significance—gripped something wrapped in cloth against his chest.

I rode forward alone. That surprised them; I could see it. Hounds travel in packs.

"Aldric Kane," Eriksen said. "The crown's best."

"Former best," I said. "I've recently been reassigned."

"To what?"

"To a decision I haven't finished making."

Sera Voss spat into the snow. Eriksen just watched me, and her watching had a quality I recognized, because it was mine: the flat, patient reading of a tracker. She was tasting the air around me, figuratively or otherwise, and what she tasted seemed to confuse her. I imagine I confused everyone that morning. I certainly confused myself.

Brask rode up beside me, crossbow relaxed but present. "Orders, Captain?"

And here is the moment the chapter of my life turns, and I wish I could tell you it came with trumpets or at least a decent metaphor, but the truth is my hands were shaking inside my gauntlets and the girl in the cage kept superimposing herself over the snowbound faces in the ravine until I couldn't tell them apart, and I heard myself say:

"New orders. We're riding for the capital."

Brask frowned. "The capital? Lord Halloway expects—"

"Yes. He does." I turned in the saddle so the whole ravine could hear, hunters and hunted alike. "Lord Halloway's harvest operation is moving to Hollow Gate within four days. There are children in cages under this hill being drained through machines to feed Vaelmoor's corpse, and when they're used up at the gate, the ledgers describe their disposition as *terminal*. Those are his directives, signed in his hand. I have the pages."

Silence, of the kind that has weight. Somewhere on the ridge a crow complained about all of us.

Brask's face went through several small weathers. "You're lying," he decided finally, which was sensible of him, and then, less sensibly: "You've turned."

"I've read," I said. "It comes to much the same thing, from where you're standing."

He drew. Of course he drew. Forty men, one traitor captain—it was simple mathematics, and Brask loved simple mathematics. What he hadn't counted on was that half those men had trained under me for years and knew exactly how fast I was, and hesitated precisely that fraction of a second longer than he did.

I put a bolt through his shoulder—not his heart; I'd done enough arithmetic for one lifetime—and shouted the order I'd been composing in my skull since the barn: *"Stand down!"*

For three endless heartbeats, forty crossbows aimed at a ravine full of people and one disintegrating captain, and nobody breathed.

Then Halver, my second on the ridge for six years, lowered his weapon. Then the man beside him. Then, in ones and twos like frost melting wrong-way-round, the rest—some slowly, some fast, one young fellow dropping his crossbow entirely as if it had bitten him. Perhaps a dozen held their lines, glaring, and Brask clutched his bleeding shoulder and swore at all of us, but the shape of the morning had changed. Mutiny is contagious once someone else goes first. All courage is.

Sera Voss stared at me across the snow with an expression I'll be chewing on for years: fury and disbelief wrestling, disbelief winning narrowly. "You expect us to trust you," she called.

"No," I said honestly. "I expect you to run, west and hard, because Halloway's regulars are two days behind me and they have no such crisis of conscience. I expect you to take the mountain road to Hollow Gate before his wagons get there." I pulled the ledger pages from my coat—copies; I'd had a long night and good handwriting—and weighted them with a stone and left them in the snow halfway between us. "And I expect you to survive long enough to make these worth carrying."

Eriksen picked them up. She read the top page, and I watched ten years of gatekeeper certainty die on her face, replaced by something older and colder. She looked at me the way you look at weather that has finally turned.

"Why?" was all she asked.

Because of a girl in a cage with a bean-sprouter's gift. Because I had spent fifteen years being shaped by men who thought fear was a tool and souls were yield. Because somewhere along the way loyalty had quietly become the lie I told about obedience, and obedience had become the habit I mistook for honor.

"Because somebody has to testify," I said, "and dead men make poor witnesses. Which is why I'm going to the capital, to put these pages in front of a king who may or may not already know what his inner circle feeds him." I gathered my dozen wavering loyalists with a gesture. "Ride. Now. And Eriksen—"

She waited.

"Halloway knows about you specifically. You're not on a list anymore. You *are* the list."

* * *

We rode east through falling snow, twelve men who had followed a captain off a cliff, and I will not pretend it was noble. Half of them muttered about pensions and the other half about hanging, and I let them mutiny verbally as much as they liked, because a soldier arguing about his wages is a soldier not thinking too hard about treason.

By the second day the muttering stopped, because the pursuit found us.

Halloway's response to my defection was, in its way, the final proof of everything in those ledgers: no negotiation, no summons, no attempt at explanation. Just riders on our trail—grey cloaks, no colors, the private retinue he wasn't technically permitted to maintain—and a message delivered by arrow into the chest of poor young Fenwick as he topped a rise, which read, in effect, that questions about Lord Halloway's operations would be received only in person and posthumously.

So now I write this by firelight in a shepherd's hut with my remaining eleven men asleep around me and Halloway's hounds close enough that I can smell their fires on the wind—or perhaps that's my gift being poetic, which it does sometimes when it thinks I'm being stupid. Tomorrow we ride for the capital with proof that the crown's inner circle has been farming children to feed a dead world's ghost. The king may listen. More likely Halloway owns enough of the court that I'll be dead before I finish the first sentence, and these pages will matter only if a smuggler-turned-fugitive and a disgraced guard reach Hollow Gate in time to do something with the truth I couldn't.

Either way, the choice is made. Fifteen years a hound; one barn to unmake it.

If anyone finds this and I'm not alive to explain: I was not a good man. I was a man who finally read the ledger.

And far to the west, past the dark hills, I could feel it even from here—the Hollow Gate, ancient and sealed and about to become the most important place in two worlds—with Wren Eriksen racing toward it, and Halloway's machines racing faster.

Four days, the transfer order said.

We had three.
