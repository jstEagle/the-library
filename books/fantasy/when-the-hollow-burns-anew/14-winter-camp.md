## 14. Winter Camp

The storm found us two hours past the last ridge and did not let go.

I want to say I was brave about it. I want to tell you I stood at the back of the column with my chin up and the shard warm against my chest, singing its little song of doom, and thought noble thoughts. What actually happened is that the wind picked me up like a rag doll some giant had grown bored of, threw me into a snowdrift, and left me there to contemplate my life choices while Sera Voss hauled me out by the collar of my coat like a mother cat retrieving a particularly stupid kitten.

"Walk," she said.

"I was walking."

"You were decorating." She shoved me forward into Wren's wake. "Stay in her tracks. Her tracks are smarter than you are."

It's hard to argue with that. Wren Eriksen moved through whiteout like it was a hallway in her own house, reading the mountain the way other people read letters — pausing here, tilting her head there, once stopping dead for a full minute until the wind shifted and she said, "There," and turned us ninety degrees for no reason I could see. Twenty minutes later we passed the frozen corpse of an avalanche path, a scar of broken pines as wide as a road, exactly where we would have been walking if she hadn't turned us.

That's when I stopped complaining. Mostly.

The bothy appeared out of the storm like something dreamed rather than built — a low stone hut with a turf roof, half-swallowed by the hillside, the kind of place shepherds used before the high pastures emptied out. Wren knew it was there the way she seemed to know everything up here. She'd been a smuggler's road-warden for ten years; the mountains were her parish. She got the door open, got a fire going from wood that must have been stacked by someone who died before I was born, and then stood in the middle of the floor, dripping, and said:

"No one goes outside tonight. No one travels tomorrow unless the sky clears. We're snowbound."

Sera posted watch at the window anyway, because Sera Voss has never in her life accepted a sentence that ended in *no one has to*. But even she, after an hour of squinting into a wall of white, sat down on the bench with her sword across her knees and admitted defeat to the weather.

And that's how eleven fugitives came to be trapped in a stone box the size of a barn, with nothing to do but look at each other.

You'd think after weeks on the run together we'd have run out of strangers. We hadn't. We were a camp, not a family. Camps have rules: don't ask, don't tell, don't get close enough that losing someone costs more than losing your own life. Everyone in Greyfen had lived by those rules so long they'd worn grooves in them.

But there's something about a fire, and a storm, and nowhere to hide, that wears grooves smooth.

* * *

It started with Brenna, because everything loud started with Brenna. She was the oldest of us, a hedge-witch with hands like tree roots who'd been caught selling charms to farmers' wives — small magics, warming spells, cures for colic. The kind of gift that should never have been worth a hunt.

She was roasting something over the fire that claimed to be a hare and probably was, technically, in the way that a ruin claims to be a house. And she said, apropos of nothing, "First time I ever used my gift, I was six. Healed my brother's broken arm. My mother kissed me and then made me swear never to do it again where anyone could see." She turned the hare. "Forty years I kept that oath. Forty years. And then some crown clerk decides oaths don't count anymore."

Nobody said anything. That was the rule. But Tamra, the twins' mother — quiet Tamra, who spoke maybe ten words a day — said, "My daughter warmed a bird's egg back to life. A bird's egg. They burned our house for a bird's egg."

The fire popped. Snow hissed against the shutters. And something in the room changed, the way ice changes just before it cracks.

Wren was sitting cross-legged near the door, mending a strap with the intense concentration of someone hoping very hard not to be part of a conversation. Sera looked at her across the fire. Then at the rest of us. Then, with the expression of a commander deciding to lose a battle on purpose, she said:

"One story each. Since we're stuck here. Not names if you don't want them. Just — how you got here."

"That's a terrible idea," said Merric, one of the twins' uncles.

"It's the only idea," Sera said. "We've been running together for three weeks and half of you still flinch when I walk behind you. If we're going to die on this mountain or at that gate, I'd rather we know whose hands we're dying in." She paused. "I'll go first."

That surprised everyone, including, I think, Sera.

* * *

"I was royal guard," she said. "Twelve years. I held the king's banner at Corren Ford and didn't drop it even after the arrow went through my shoulder. I was good. I was *very* good." She said it without pride, the way you'd read out a list of debts. "And then the purge orders came down, and one of them was for a girl in my own barracks. Pip Aldous. Nineteen. Could make plants grow — that was all, she could make things grow, and they wanted her in a cage for it."

Sera turned her sword over in her lap, not looking at any of us.

"I was ordered to arrest her. I wrote a report instead saying she'd deserted. I gave her a horse and my own purse and sent her south." A muscle worked in her jaw. "They caught her in Duncarrel anyway. And when they asked how a girl with no money and no papers got a horse, someone remembered who'd signed the tack ledger." She looked up. "So that's my disgrace. Not that I have a gift. That I tried to save someone with one and was too slow and too clever by half. They stripped my rank in front of the whole garrison. Called it mercy, letting me keep my head." Her mouth twisted. "I ran north because north was the only direction they weren't looking. And then Greyfen happened, and here we are."

Silence. The kind that isn't empty.

Brenna reached over and put her root-hand on Sera's knee, and Sera — Sera Voss, who once made a crown hunter cry with a single raised eyebrow — let her.

* * *

I'll tell you what I told them, though I hated every second of it.

"My village was called Lowmere. You won't have heard of it. There wasn't much to hear." I kept my eyes on the fire. "My parents weren't gifted. That's the stupid part. I was — I *am*, apparently, but they weren't, and nobody knew I was either, except the shard started singing when I turned twelve and I couldn't make it stop, and I couldn't exactly show anyone, could I?"

The shard pulsed against my sternum, gentle, like it knew it was being discussed.

"So I hid it. In a hole under the floor. And I got good at lying, which is a terrible skill to be proud of. For two years I lied to everyone I loved every single day." My voice did something embarrassing. I pushed through it. "And then the hunters came through asking questions, and a neighbor — a woman who'd known me since I was born, who'd given me honey cakes — she saw me digging up the hole. Just once. One time. And she told them."

Tamra made a soft sound. Her twins, Fen and Isla, pressed in against her sides, nine years old and already veterans of too many nights like this one.

"They took my parents first. Asked them where the singing was coming from. My father wouldn't tell them, so they—" I stopped. Started again. "I ran. I've been running since. I met Wren on the smuggler's road when Doss sold her out, and she could have left me. I was nothing to her. Half-starved boy in a ditch. She didn't leave me."

I looked at Wren then, because I had to, because the story needed it, and she was watching me with an expression I'd never seen on her face before — something raw and unguarded, quickly buried under a wry tilt of her mouth.

"Somebody had to feed you," she said. "You were all elbows and hunger."

"And now?" Brenna asked gently. "What are you now?"

I thought about it honestly, which was new for me.

"Still hungry," I said. "Less alone."

* * *

After me it loosened, the way a knot loosens once you find the right end to pull. Tamra talked about her husband, dead two winters before the purge, and how she'd promised him she'd keep the girls safe, and how the promise had become the whole architecture of her spine. Merric talked about his gift — he could hear lies, actual lies, taste them like copper — and how he'd spent thirty years pretending deafness to survive in a town full of liars, and how exhausting it had been, and everyone laughed, really laughed, for the first time since Greyfen burned. Even the twins told stories, small ones, about a dog they'd had named Turnip, and everyone agreed solemnly that Turnip sounded like an excellent dog.

Then someone — I think it was Brenna, with the tactlessness of the old — turned to Wren and said, "And you? Ten years keeping that gate. What was that like, hiding in plain sight?"

Wren went still in a way I'd learned to recognize. It was the stillness of someone standing at the edge of a truth with a long drop below it.

"Lonely," she said finally. "It was lonely. That's the honest answer. Ten years of being competent and useful and completely unknown. I had a friend — Doss — and I never told him what I was. I had a duty, and I never told anyone why I loved it. Do you know what it's like to guard a door to another world and have no one to tell about it?" She shook her head. "I told the gate. Pathetic, isn't it. Ten years of talking to a rock."

"It's not pathetic," I said, before I could stop myself. Everyone looked at me. "It's — you were keeping a covenant alive by yourself. Somebody had to talk to it."

Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice — guilt, I'd know later, guilt with teeth — but she smiled at me, and it reached her eyes, mostly.

"Thank you, Callum."

Sera was watching Wren too, and I saw her file the fish-under-ice moment away somewhere behind her ribs. Sera noticed everything. It was her best and worst quality.

* * *

Later, when most of the others slept, I couldn't. The shard hummed against my chest, restless, and the storm screamed at the shutters, and I lay there doing arithmetic I hated: how many of us would reach the gate, how many wouldn't, whether I'd be worth whatever it cost to get me there.

A floorboard creaked. Wren sat down beside my bedroll, legs folded, and offered me a strip of dried apple from her pack like it was contraband.

"Can't sleep either?" I took the apple.

"The storm's talking to the mountain," she said. "Old habit. Listening."

"What's it saying?"

"That we're lucky. This bothy sits above the deep drift line. Any lower and we'd be digging ourselves out till spring." She chewed her apple, looking at the banked fire. "Your parents. I'm sorry. About what I said earlier — feeding you, elbows. I joke when I shouldn't."

"I know," I said. "It's how you hide. Same as my lying. Different tools, same wall."

She looked at me sideways. "How old are you, exactly?"

"Fourteen."

"Fourteen," she repeated, disgusted. "Fourteen, and you say things like that." But she didn't deny it, and that was its own kind of honesty. "For what it's worth — I'm glad Doss sold me out. Don't tell Sera I said that. But if he hadn't, I'd never have met you, and I'd have gone to that gate alone, and alone is how I've done everything for ten years, and I'm tired of it."

I didn't know what to say to that, so I said the truest thing I had.

"You're not alone now. None of us are. That's the whole point of tonight, isn't it? Sera's plan. One story each." I pulled my blanket up. "We're not a camp anymore. Camps break apart. Families don't."

Wren was quiet a long moment. When she answered, her voice was rough at the edges.

"When did you get wise?"

"Had good teachers. One talks to rocks."

She laughed — actually laughed, quietly, so as not to wake the others — and ruffled my hair in a way that would have been humiliating if I hadn't secretly loved it, and went back to her bedroll by the door, because she always slept between us and the entrance, always, even now, especially now.

I slept better than I had in two years.

* * *

Morning came grey and silent. The storm had spent itself in the night, and the world outside the bothy was blinding, blue-shadowed, beautiful — and wrong.

Wren saw it first. She always saw it first. She was standing at the window with her breath fogging the glass, and her voice, when she spoke, was flat and cold in a way that killed the warmth of the whole night dead.

"Sera. Smoke."

Sera was beside her in two strides. I crowded in under their arms and looked out at the valley below, where the pass curved toward the Hollow Gate road.

Fires. Not campfires — a column of them, strung along the valley floor like festival lights, except nothing about them was festive. Wagons. Tents. The glint of armor. And rising above the largest tent, snapping in the clean morning wind, a banner I recognized from the proclamations nailed to every door in Ashford.

The crowned stag of House Halloway.

"They're not hunting us anymore," Sera said slowly. "Look at the layout. That's not a search pattern. That's a *garrison*. They've fortified the road to the gate."

Wren's hand rose to the glass, and beyond it, toward the mountain where the Hollow Gate slept under snow and stone. Her face, in the window's reflection, was the loneliest thing I had ever seen.

"They're not waiting for us to come to the gate," she said. "They've brought the harvest to it."
