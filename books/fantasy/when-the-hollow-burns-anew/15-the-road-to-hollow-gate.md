## 15. The Road to Hollow Gate

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in the high passes above the Hollow Gate. I spent ten years listening to it. It's not the silence of emptiness—it's the silence of something holding its breath. Ten years of my life I stood inside that held breath and called it duty.

Now I was walking back into it with a found family, a singing rock, a fae envoy who ate like a starling, and an ex-royal guard who looked at me like I was a map she wasn't sure she could trust anymore.

The truth had come out two nights ago—my truth, the one I'd buried so deep I'd almost convinced myself it was a stone and not a body. *I sealed the gate. I started Vaelmoor's dying.* Sera hadn't spoken more than six words to me since. She gave orders around me now instead of to me, which was somehow worse than being shouted at. Shouting at least meant she still thought of me as someone worth shouting at.

"Water," she said now, passing down the line of us on the frozen trail, handing out skins without meeting my eyes.

"Thank you," I said.

She grunted. Four words total for the morning. We were setting records.

Callum trudged ahead of me, the covenant shard wrapped in wool against his chest. He'd grown taller in the months since I'd pulled him out of Doss's ambush—or maybe he just walked taller now, which amounts to the same thing when you're fourteen. He glanced back at me, then at Sera, then at me again, doing the arithmetic children do when the adults they love are breaking each other.

"It'll be fine," he said quietly.

"Don't jinx us," I said. "I've read about our luck. It doesn't end well."

That earned half a smile, which I treasured unreasonably, the way you treasure anything warm when you're walking through snow that never quite melts even in summer.

Ilyen drifted at the rear, hood up against a sun that seemed to offend her. The closer we got to the gate, the more alive she became—and the more afraid. Fae are bad at hiding fear; it comes off them like heat off a hearthstone. Her fingers kept moving, tracing patterns in the air, old wards or old prayers, I couldn't tell which.

"How much farther?" Sera called back.

"Two days if the weather holds," I said. "One and a half if we take the Keeper's Cut."

Sera stopped walking. "The tunnel under the ridge."

"The same."

"The one only gatekeepers know."

"That would be why I mentioned it," I said, keeping my voice level. "Since I'm the only one here who knows it."

She looked at me then, finally—a long, measuring look, the kind you give a blade before deciding whether to pick it up. "Fine," she said. "Lead."

It wasn't forgiveness. But it was forward motion, and on a mountain in winter, forward motion is all the mercy anyone can afford.

* * *

We took the Cut at midday. It's a smuggler's road, carved by hands older than Thornfell itself, running beneath the spine of the ridge like a marrow in a bone. My father's maps had shown it to me before I understood what maps were for. Walking it again after ten years felt like reading a letter from someone dead.

I knew every bend. Every drip of meltwater through the ceiling. Every place where the dark got thick enough to chew. I put my hand on the wall as I walked, the way I used to, and my gift rose lazily to meet the stone—familiar, easy, a dog greeting its master.

Then I felt it.

At first I thought it was the shard, singing through Callum's wrappings. But no. This came from deeper. From ahead. A vibration in the rock itself, low and rhythmic, like a heartbeat heard through a hospital wall.

"Stop," I said.

Everyone stopped. Sera's hand went to her sword. "What is it?"

"Do you feel that?"

They pressed their palms to the walls. Callum first—he was closest to the shard, and whatever the shard was, it was attuned. His face went pale in the lantern light.

"It's the gate," he said. "It's... hurting."

"Not hurting," Ilyen whispered. She had gone very still, her eyes wide and black. "*Bleeding.* Someone is drawing from it. Now. Heavily." Her composure, that porcelain calm the fae wear like armor, cracked straight down the middle. "Wren. How close are we?"

"A day from the gate proper," I said slowly. Working it out. Hating what I was working out. "But this vibration—it's not coming from the far side. It's coming from *this* side of the threshold."

Sera understood before I finished saying it. I watched it land on her face, the way understanding lands on soldiers: fast, hard, and unwelcome.

"He's moved the harvest there," she said. "Halloway. He didn't stay in the capital. He brought the whole operation to the gate."

"Why would he—" Callum started.

"Because the gate is the deepest well in either world," I said. My voice came out flat, the way it does when the alternative is shaking. "Ten years ago it was sealed because both sides feared what it carried. All that magic, dammed up behind my sealing, nowhere to go, growing denser every year like water behind a failing weir. If you wanted to drain Vaelmoor completely—if you wanted to drink the last of a dying country—you wouldn't do it from cages in some valley facility." I swallowed. "You'd tap the source. And you'd seal it behind you when you were done, so nobody could ever refill it."

The tunnel was very quiet. Somewhere above us, wind worried at the ridge like a dog at a bone.

"He means to kill it," Ilyen said. Not a question. "The gate. Vaelmoor. Everything." She laughed—one short, awful note. "And I crossed half a world to ask politely for help."

"We're past polite," Sera said. She turned to me, and something in her face had shifted—not forgiveness, but purpose, which for Sera was always the more generous emotion. "How many men does it take to run a harvest operation?"

"Hundreds. Maybe more, with the machines. Kane said they'd built engines—" I stopped. "Kane. If he made it to the capital with that evidence, Halloway will have pulled everything to the gate. He knows the endgame is here now. He'll be dug in like a tick."

"Then we don't have two days," Sera said. "We have however long it takes him to finish tapping the well. How fast can we move?"

"As fast as the boy can walk," I said, and immediately regretted it when Callum squared his shoulders.

"I can keep up," he said.

"I know you can. That's not why I said it." I crouched to his level, the way I had on the night I first learned what he carried. "Listen to me. That thing in your shirt has been drinking from you since Greyfen. On open ground near the gate, it's going to sing louder than it ever has. When it does, everyone around you is going to feel it—including whatever machines Halloway has built. You're not cargo, Callum. But you're also not a soldier. When I say run, you run, and you don't look back, and you don't argue."

He looked at me with those too-old eyes. "You're going to say run, aren't you," he said. Not a question either. "Everyone always says run. Nobody ever says *stay and fight beside me*."

The words went in under my ribs where armor doesn't reach. Because he was right. I had been telling people to run my whole life—run from the gift, run from the gate, run from the truth—and look where it had gotten us. A drained world. A hunted people. A camp full of fugitives led by a woman who couldn't stand the sight of me.

"Then here's the new plan," I said. "Stay and fight beside me. Right up until I say run. Deal?"

Something loosened in his shoulders—the smallest release, like a knot giving way one fiber at a time. "Deal."

Sera, who had heard all of it and pretended she hadn't, turned back up the tunnel. "Move," she said. "Fast as we can. Rest when the mountain makes us."

* * *

We came out of the Cut at dusk onto the shoulder of the mountain, and I made everyone stop and look, because some things you should not be permitted to see by accident.

The Hollow Gate sat in its saddle of black stone two miles below and east, and where there should have been darkness—or moonlight, or nothing—there was light. Wrong light. It crawled over the rocks in slow veins of blue-white, pulsing in time with the heartbeat we'd felt through the tunnel walls, and above it hung a haze that wasn't cloud. Scaffolding climbed the cliffs on either side of the gate like ivy. Watch fires dotted the approaches in neat military rows. And at the center, planted against the gate itself like a spear in a wound, stood a structure I recognized from Kane's descriptions: the harvest engine, swollen to ten times its size, pipes and cables running from its heart into the seam of the sealed gate.

Even from two miles away, I could feel it pulling.

My gift flared in my chest, involuntary, answering the pull the way iron answers a magnet. Beside me, Ilyen gasped and dropped to one knee, hands pressed flat to the frozen ground.

"What's wrong with her?" Callum asked.

"She can feel it more than we can," I said. "Every drop they're taking out of Vaelmoor, she feels leaving." I knelt beside the fae, put a hand on her shoulder. Her skin was cold as river stones. "Ilyen. Look at me. Breathe."

"They're taking the rivers," she whispered. Her voice had gone strange, layered, the way it did when she forgot to hold her human shape together. "The white stag's herd. The orchards at Silver Ford. I can feel them going out like lamps. Wren—" Her hand shot up and gripped my wrist with strength no starving creature should have had. "If he finishes before the rite, there will be nothing left to forge a covenant *with*. Nothing on the other side but hollow air."

"Then we don't let him finish," I said.

Below us, a bell began to ring. One of the watch fires flared, and figures spilled from the tents like ants from a kicked hill. They'd seen us. Of course they'd seen us—we were standing on a ridge silhouetted against the glow of their own stolen light like players on a stage.

Or.

Or they had seen exactly what they were meant to see, and someone very smart had put us on this stage deliberately.

"Sera," I said quietly. "How much do you want to bet Halloway knows we're coming?"

"I'd bet the camp on it." She was already moving, dragging Ilyen up, waving the others toward a fold in the ridge. Down-slope, away from the light. "He wants us to come to him. Saves him the trouble of hunting us across the mountains."

"Then why hasn't he sent soldiers up this ridge already?"

Sera paused. I watched her run the same calculation I was running, and arrive at the same ugly answer.

"Because he doesn't need to catch us on the ridge," she said. "He needs to catch us at the gate. Where the shard goes, we go. And the shard is going to the gate whether we like it or not." Her jaw tightened. "He's not trying to stop us, Wren. He's *herding* us."

Wonderful. Ten years as a gatekeeper and I'd never once considered that a gate works in both directions—including the trap kind.

"There's another way in," I said. "Not the main approach. Not the pilgrim road he'll have mined and manned." I pointed east, to where the mountain fell away in broken shelves toward a black slot in the rock, half-hidden by shadow. "Doss's tunnel."

The name landed among us like a dropped knife. Doss, who had sold me to the crown. Doss, whose guilt had bought us the harvest schedules and cost him—well. What it had cost him, none of us liked to say aloud yet.

"You're sure it still runs under the gate complex?" Sera asked.

"If he's moved the operation to the gate, he'll have found it," I admitted. "But finding it and knowing where it exits are different things. Smuggler's roads lie about themselves. There are three branchings, and only one goes anywhere useful. He'll have guarded the obvious ones."

"And you know the un-obvious one."

"I sealed this gate, Sera." I heard my own voice steady itself, and realized I meant the words in a way I hadn't meant anything in years. "I know its bones better than Halloway ever will. He can build his engines and stack his soldiers until the mountain groans. He's drinking from a well I built the walls of." I turned to face her fully, and she let me, which was its own small miracle. "Let him think he's herding us. Tomorrow night, we come up through the floor of his stronghold, and he learns what it costs to make prey of a keeper."

For a long moment Sera said nothing. Wind pulled at her cloak. Below us the wrong light pulsed and pulsed, patient as disease.

"You sealed the gate," she said at last. Quietly. Not an accusation anymore—or not only one. "So you're the one who knows how to unseal it."

"Yes," I said.

"And the price of that."

"Yes," I said again, and didn't elaborate, because the truth of it was still coiled in my chest where I hadn't yet found the courage to look at it directly.

Sera studied me for a breath longer. Then she nodded once, sharp as a blade going home into its sheath, and turned down-slope.

"One night's rest," she said. "Then we go through Doss's road. And Wren—" She didn't turn around. "Whatever it costs tomorrow. Don't decide it alone."

We descended into the dark, five of us and one shard, toward the place that had been my whole life and was now the enemy's heart. Behind us the gate's stolen light throbbed against the clouds like a wound that wouldn't close.

And deep beneath my ribs, quiet as a held breath, my gift pulsed in time with it—as if it already knew what tomorrow would ask of me, and was saying its goodbyes.
