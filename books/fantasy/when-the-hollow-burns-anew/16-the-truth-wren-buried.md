## 16. The Truth Wren Buried

There is an art to confessing, and I have never learned it.

I've had ten years to practice. Ten years of lying awake in a keeper's hut above the Hollow Gate, composing speeches to no one, rehearsing apologies for crimes that had no witnesses and no victims—except, as it turned out, an entire world. You'd think a decade of rehearsal would make the words come easy. They don't. Words are like knives: the longer you keep them sheathed, the more you bleed when you finally draw them.

We made camp in the ruins of an old watch-shrine two days down from the waygate, close enough that at night the shard in Callum's pocket still hummed through the bedrock like a tuning fork. Ilyen had slipped back through the stone to carry word to her people, promising to return before we reached the Hollow Gate. Sera had set watches, checked the perimeter twice, argued with Bram about salted meat versus fresh risk, and done all the thousand small things she does to keep forty frightened people alive. And all the while I had been circling the moment like a crow over a battlefield, telling myself *tomorrow*, and then tomorrow again, because tomorrow is the native country of cowards.

But tomorrow had run out. In the morning we'd break for the gate, and Halloway's machines were already there, and if I died on that wall with my secret still in my chest, I'd be killing her twice—once with the lie, once with the truth arriving too late to matter.

So after the evening meal, when Callum had fallen asleep against Bram's shoulder with the shard's faint glow leaking between his fingers, I found Sera on the shrine's broken steps, honing a knife she'd already honed to a razor's patience.

"Walk with me," I said.

She looked up. She has eyes the color of wet slate, and they do the thing slate does: show you your own face, warped. "Trouble?"

"Of a kind." I almost smiled. "The kind I bring myself."

She followed me up the goat track behind the shrine, past the last of the sentries, to a shelf of granite where the wind came off the peaks clean and cold enough to scour a person down to their bones. Below us the valley was a puddle of ember-light—cook fires, watch fires, forty small reasons not to sleep. Above us the stars were thick and indifferent, and somewhere beyond them, dimming, Vaelmoor bled out its light into Halloway's engines.

Sera leaned against the rock and waited. She's very good at waiting. It's one of the things that frightens people about her, and one of the things I'd come to trust, which is exactly why what I was about to do felt like breaking something that couldn't be mended.

"I need to tell you why the gate was sealed," I said.

"You told us. The old covenant failed. The keepers sealed it during the war scare."

"That's what the histories say." I turned my back on the campfire glow, because I didn't want to see anyone's face while I did this, even hers. Especially hers. "I should know. I wrote part of it."

The wind filled the silence. She let it. Of course she did.

"Ten years ago," I said, "there were rumors. Fae raiders crossing in the night. Livestock found drained white. A child in Thornmere who woke with frost on her windows in high summer and marks on her skin nobody could read. The crown wanted the gate shut. The fae courts denied everything—or rather, the three factions denied three different things, which amounted to the same noise. The keepers were ordered to perform a closing rite."

"And you performed it."

"I *led* it." My voice came out flatter than I intended, which was a mercy. "I wasn't just the youngest keeper, Sera. I was the strongest. Old Magda held the north anchor-stone and her hands shook too badly; Tam could barely hold his gift steady on a good day. So when the rite needed a hand strong enough to bind the seal, it used mine. I pressed my gift into the covenant stone and I pulled the gate shut, and I remember thinking—I remember this precisely, because I have taken it out and looked at it every night for ten years—that I was saving everyone. That I was the hero of my own story."

Sera said nothing. The knife was still in her hand. I noticed that, the way you notice the position of furniture in a room where someone has just died.

"The war scare was real," I went on, "but it was small. A few raids. Some frightened children on both sides. What nobody understood—what *I* didn't understand, though I had no excuse, I'd been raised on the old texts—was that the covenant wasn't just a lock. It was a circulation. Magic moved between the realms the way blood moves through a heart. The gate didn't merely connect Thornfell and Vaelmoor. It kept them both alive. And I closed it."

I heard her breath change. Not a gasp. Sera doesn't gasp. Just a slow, controlled intake, the sound of a soldier deciding how much ammunition a situation requires.

"You're saying the sealing killed Vaelmoor."

"I'm saying it started the dying. The realm would have faded slowly regardless—the covenant was old and fraying—but I cut the artery. Everything Ilyen showed us through the waygate, the grey orchards, the rivers running thin, the courts going dark one by one—" I made myself turn around, made myself look at her. "That's mine. Every year of it. I did that believing I was a hero."

* * *

Here is what I expected: rage. Here is what I got, which was worse.

She was quiet for so long that the wind seemed loud, and when she finally spoke, her voice was level and terribly careful, the voice of a woman counting.

"How long have you known?"

"That the sealing began the decline? Since the waygate. Since I saw Vaelmoor through it."

"No." Her eyes didn't move from my face. "How long have you known *and traveled with us*. How long have these people—people who are hunted, people whose families are in cages right now because of what the closed gate made possible—how long have they been following a woman who built the cage door and locked it?"

The question went in under my ribs the way only true questions can. "Since the harvest fields," I said. "Since Magda."

Magda. My old mentor, dying among the freed prisoners, her gift sucked out of her through Halloway's pipes. Even now I couldn't say that name without my throat doing something humiliating.

"You've known since the harvest fields," Sera repeated. "Three weeks. Three weeks of watching you lead us through passes no one else knows. Three weeks of letting Callum worship you. Three weeks of me—" She stopped herself. Whatever the sentence had been going to be, she swallowed it whole, and I watched her do it, and I understood that the swallowed half of the sentence was the part that mattered most.

"I was trying to find the right time."

"There isn't one." She pushed off the rock and took two steps away, then two steps back, pacing the width of the ledge like something caged. "That's the thing about lies, Wren. There is never a right time to bury them, so people like you just keep digging. Was there ever going to be a right time? After the reforging? After I'd spent whatever trust I had left getting you to that gate? Or were you going to take it to your grave and let me find out from Ilyen, or from the stone itself, mid-rite?"

"I'm telling you now. Before the gate. Before anything."

"Yes. When you need me." She laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Do you know what I keep thinking about? Greyfen. The night Kane burned it. You stood next to me at the treeline and you said, *I know how keepers think, I can predict their patrol patterns.* And I believed you, because it was true, because you're competent, because competence is such a comfortable place to hide. But you also knew something else that night, didn't you. You knew that everything Kane serves—the hunt, the harvest, all of it—grows out of the soil you tilled."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed, softly. "It probably isn't. Fairness is for people who haven't been lied to."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened inside me on that ledge, because a confession that stops halfway is just a lie with better posture.

My first feeling was not guilt. It was anger. Hot, animal, childish anger—the fury of a child caught out, the fury that says *you don't understand what it was like, you weren't there, you don't know what fear does to a nineteen-year-old girl holding a god's worth of power and a kingdom's worth of panic.* I had rehearsed this conversation a hundred times and in every rehearsal Sera listened, and grieved, and forgave. In none of them did she look at me the way she was looking at me now, like a commander reassessing a soldier's entire service record.

And beneath the anger, colder and heavier, the guilt I had carried for ten years rearranged itself into a new shape. Because I had always framed my secret as a burden I bore alone—a private penance, noble in its loneliness. Standing there with Sera's trust coming apart in front of me like wet rope, I finally saw it plainly: my lonely penance had been a luxury. Every year I kept silent, other people paid my debts. The fugitives at Greyfen. The gifted in the cages. An entire fading world. None of them had ever been consulted about whether my secret was heavy enough to justify the interest accruing on it.

"You're right," I said.

Her pacing stopped.

"About all of it. There was no right time because I didn't want one. I wanted absolution without the bill. I wanted to be the woman who saved Callum on the pass and led us through the deep snow, and I wanted that woman to be complete, and she isn't. She's the same woman who sealed the gate. Both hands. Same hands." I held them up. They were shaking. "I'm not telling you this because I need you for the rite, Sera, though I do. I'm telling you because if we die on that wall and your last thought of me is the lie, then I've stolen something from you that not even Halloway managed to steal."

For a long moment she just looked at me, and I braced for the verdict.

What she said was: "Callum carries the shard."

It took me a second to follow the swerve. "Yes."

"He's eleven years old. He watched his village burn, he starved across half of Thornfell, he slept with that thing under his tongue for months because he was afraid soldiers could smell it. And when it started singing near you—when it nearly pulled him apart on the ridge—he gave it to you without hesitation, because he decided you were safe. We all did. That's what you're confessing to, Wren. Not a mistake ten years ago. Mistakes happen. Wars start over mistakes. You confessed to ten years of letting people decide to love you while wearing a mask." Her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and then flattened again, controlled. "That's not a sin against Vaelmoor. Vaelmoor I can understand. Realms fall. People are smaller than history. But the lying—" She shook her head slowly. "The lying was a choice you made every single morning for three weeks, and each morning it got easier, and you know it did."

She was right. That was the worst of it. Each morning it had gotten easier, the way scar tissue gets smoother, until I barely felt the shape of the thing underneath.

"What happens now?" I asked.

"Now?" She looked out at the valley, at the scatter of cook-fires, at the sleeping shapes of forty people whose lives she carried in her spine every day. "Now nothing changes. We leave at dawn. You take us through the passes, because you're the only one who knows them, and we reach the gate, and we fight, and you attempt the reforging, because you're the only one who can. Nothing changes." She turned back to me, and her slate eyes were bright and hard and terribly tired. "That's what should frighten you. I'll trust you with my life tomorrow, Wren, because strategy demands it. But you've taught me the difference between trusting a person and trusting their usefulness, and I used to think you knew that difference too."

"Sera—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and it wasn't unkind, which somehow made it worse. "Not tonight. Maybe not on this mountain. Ask me again when we're standing in front of the gate and I've seen what your truth costs. If we're both still alive, maybe I'll have an answer." She picked up her knife, slid it home in its sheath, and moved toward the track down. At the edge of the ledge she paused, not turning around.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I believe you'd have taken it to your grave. That's the saddest thing about you. Not the sealing. Anyone might have sealed it, given your age and your orders and that much power in one pair of hands. But you would have carried it perfectly, silently, forever, and called it strength."

Then she was gone, boots crunching down the frozen track, and I was alone on the granite with the wind and the stars and the distant ember-glow of the people I had, with one evening's honesty, just unmade myself among.

* * *

I stayed up there until the cold worked through my coat and into the marrow of the lie itself, and I'll tell you the strangest thing I learned on that ledge: it hurt less than I expected. Not because it didn't hurt—it hurt like a wound hurts, clean and shocking—but because for ten years the secret had been rotting in the dark, and now it was out in the open air, and even infected things breathe easier in the open air.

Below, the camp slept. Above, past the stars, a whole world was waiting to learn whether the woman who had begun its dying could end it.

And far to the south, along roads I knew too well, torches were moving. I didn't see them yet. But I felt them the way you feel weather in an old scar—Halloway's columns, Kane's hunters or what remained of them, the whole machinery of the hunt grinding toward the same gate we were racing toward, all of us converging on the one place in either world where the truth was finally going to have to matter.

Dawn came grey and merciless. Sera wouldn't meet my eye as we broke camp, but she assigned me the point position anyway—front of the line, the dangerous post, the post you give someone you trust with your life and nothing else.

"Lead on, keeper," was all she said.

I led.
