## 17. Storming the Hollow

I had commanded soldiers before. I had ordered men and women over walls and into arrow-storms, and I had learned to do it with a flat voice and a still face, because that was the job: you spent your fear so they didn't have to spend theirs.

But I had never led forty fugitives, half of them children of one age or another, against a fortified harvest operation run by the second-most-powerful man in Thornfell, armed mostly with smuggler's tricks and spite.

"Remember," I said, crouched on the ridge above the Hollow Gate as dusk came down purple and cold, "we're not here to win a battle. We're here to ruin Halloway's evening. Anything past that is showing off."

Someone laughed. Good. Armies march on their stomachs, but they fight on their jokes.

Below us, the hollow burned—not with covenant light, not anymore, but with the ugly orange glare of Halloway's machines. He'd thrown up timber scaffolding around the gate like a man building a gallows for a mountain, and the harvest engine sat at its heart, a black iron heart of pipes and cages, drinking from the stone. Even from this distance I could feel it in my teeth, that wrong pulling sensation, like the world being sipped through a straw.

Wren stood beside me at the ridgeline, staring down at the place she'd guarded for ten years. Her face did something complicated and then put itself away.

"You all right?" I asked.

"I'm looking at my life's work wearing a cage," she said. "Ask me again after."

Doss appeared out of the dark the way he always did—no warning, just suddenly there, smelling faintly of pipe smoke and bad decisions. He'd been gone two days scouting the tunnels beneath the gate, the old smuggler's roads he claimed to know better than his own sins.

"Well?" I said.

"Well," Doss said, dropping onto a rock like his knees had voted for retirement without consulting him, "there's good news and there's news I'll tell you after we've eaten, because morale."

"Doss."

"Fine. The tunnel under the west foundation is open. Runs right under the harvest yard, comes up behind the engine works. Halloway's boys don't know about it because Halloway's boys aren't criminals, God love the honest." He paused. "The bad news is the tunnel's old. Older than me, and I'm held together with habit. The crown bricked part of it years back, and somebody—I'm not naming names, but her initials are W.E.—did some kind of sealing-work on the far junction. Stone's fused. Magic-fused."

Everyone looked at Wren.

"I sealed every approach to the gate when I closed it," she said quietly. "I didn't know there *was* a tunnel under the foundation."

"There isn't," Doss said. "There's a tunnel-shaped regret. But—" he waggled a hand, "—you made those seals. What you seal, presumably you can unseal. Question is whether you can unseal it fast, and whether unsealing it makes enough noise to wake the whole garrison."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "If I hold the seal open instead of breaking it—if I keep it open by hand, so to speak—it should be quiet. It will just cost me everything I've got while I'm doing it."

"How long can you hold it?" I asked.

"How long does the assault need?"

I looked at her, and she looked back, and I understood we were negotiating over who got to spend themselves first. That was going to be a problem between us until one of us ran out. Maybe longer.

"An hour," I said. "Maybe less if Kane keeps his promise."

"And if Kane doesn't come?"

"Then an hour is what we have, and we make it worth an hour."

* * *

We went down the mountain in the last grey light, forty ghosts on the smuggler's paths, Wren calling each turn before we reached it because she'd walked these slopes alone for a decade and knew the stones by name. Callum stayed glued to her side, the covenant shard wrapped in cloth against his chest. He'd stopped complaining about the weight weeks ago; now it hummed loud enough that all of us could feel it, growing louder the closer we came to the gate, like a dog straining toward home.

At the mouth of the tunnel Doss stopped us all with a raised hand.

"Rules of the road," he said. "Nobody touches anything. Nobody lights anything. If you hear dripping, it's water. If you hear *humming*, it's the seals, and you walk faster. And if the ceiling talks to you, that means you've been down here too long, and you should see someone about it when we're done being heroes."

"That's your briefing?" Sable muttered behind me.

"It's more than most people get," Doss said, and grinned at her, and led us into the dark.

He was different underground. I kept expecting the smuggler to fade and the frightened, guilty man—the one who'd sold Wren's location to buy his pardon—to surface, but the opposite happened. The tunnels were his country. He moved through the black like a fish through current, murmuring left and right and duck, and forty people who had never trusted him followed him anyway, because competence is its own kind of proof.

Wren walked at the rear with her palms pressed to the tunnel wall, and where she touched it, the old fused stone glowed faintly and *unclenched*, seam by seam, like a fist remembering how to be a hand. I could see what it cost her. Sweat despite the cold. Breath coming shorter. By the third junction she was leaning on Callum's shoulder, and he bore it without a word, the way you carry someone when you're fourteen and finally bigger than your grief.

The last seal lay just below the harvest yard. Through the grate above us I could see firelight, boots, the shadow-skeletons of Halloway's scaffolds.

"Now or never," Doss said softly. "Sera—when she opens it, they'll hear the yard floor shift no matter how gentle she is. You'll have maybe thirty breaths between the opening and every soldier in the hollow knowing exactly where we are. So: thirty breaths."

"Thirty," I agreed. I turned to the others. "You know the plan. Free the cages first. Machines second. Kill nobody who puts down their weapon—we're not them. Go."

Wren knelt and laid both hands flat on the seal.

I will say this for her: she made it look almost easy, which I knew was the cruelest lie in the world. The stone sighed apart. The grate lifted on hinges that hadn't moved in ten years without so much as a squeak, because even the iron seemed afraid to interrupt her. Cold night air poured down into the tunnel, tasting of ash and machine-oil.

"Go," Wren whispered, white-lipped. "*Go.*"

And we went.

* * *

It went well for ninety seconds, which for us was practically a festival.

We came up behind the engine works and took the yard guards before they finished being surprised. Sable got the cage-block gates open and started streaming prisoners out—hollow-eyed, stumbling, some of them so young my throat closed up and I had to swallow it and keep moving. Rennik's crew went for the machines with hammers and firepots. The great engine screamed when they hit it, an actual scream, metal and magic dying together, and the whole hollow shuddered as though something vast had been pinched awake.

Then the garrison came, and it stopped being a raid and became a fight.

I haven't got much to say about the next twenty minutes that's fit for polite company. We were outnumbered three to one, and it didn't matter, because we weren't fighting for a wage. The freed prisoners fought too, once Sable pressed knives into their hands—fought like people who had seen the inside of the cages and intended to be nobody's harvest again. I lost track of Wren. I lost track of everyone. There was a moment with a sergeant's sword coming down at me and my own blade a half-second slow, and then a firepot took the man's legs out from under him and Doss was there, hauling me up by the collar.

"You're welcome," he shouted over the din.

"You're supposed to be *holding the tunnel!*"

"Tunnel's held! She's holding it! I came up to see the show!" He grinned, wild and young suddenly, twenty years shed somewhere in the dark. "Somebody's got to make sure you don't die stupid!"

That was when the yard floor above the tunnel entrance cracked.

Later, I pieced it together: the engine's death-throes had shaken the whole foundation, and the old tunnel—the tunnel-shaped regret—had taken the strain. The collapse began at the far end and rolled toward us like thunder rolling down a valley, and I heard Wren scream from somewhere near the mouth of it, felt her gift flare through the stone like a struck bell, holding, *holding*—

Because she was still holding the seal. Because forty people were still in that tunnel, and if it shut, they shut with it.

"Hold!" I roared, to everyone, to no one. "Get to the tunnel mouth—*move!*"

They poured into the dark, fugitives and freed prisoners both, and Doss was already there ahead of me, standing at the collapsing junction with his arms spread like a man holding back a tide with his coat.

"What are you *doing?*" I screamed.

"Prop work!" He wasn't grinning anymore. His hands were pressed to the sagging stone, and the stone was glowing where he touched it, and I understood with a horror like falling that Wren's seal was running through him somehow, that he'd taken her burden into his smuggler's hands without asking anyone's permission, least of all mine. "It needs weight at the junction! Go, Sera! *Go!*"

"I am not leaving you—"

"Sera." He said it quietly, which was somehow worse than shouting, because I could hear him fine under the roar. "I sold her to them. One name, one pardon, and I've been choking on it since the day I did it. This—" he nodded at the groaning dark around him, "—this is me coughing it up. Get them through. All of them. Tell Wren—" and here his voice did crack, finally, honestly, "—tell Wren the debt's paid. Tell her I said so."

The ceiling came down another foot. Behind me, Callum grabbed my sleeve, and beyond him a hundred faces waited in the shaking dark, and Doss looked at me with the steadiest eyes I'd ever seen on a coward, and I made the command decision that has kept me awake every night since, and would keep me awake forever:

I turned and led them out.

The tunnel held. It held for every last soul—forty of ours, sixty of the freed—and as I came out the far side into moonlight and turned back, I saw the junction glow gold through the settling dust, one bright seam in the collapsed dark, like a coin dropped into a pocket of night.

It faded slowly. Like something being let go.

Wren knelt at the tunnel mouth with her palms flat on the stone, and she didn't cry, and neither did I, and neither did anybody, because grief like that doesn't come out as tears—it comes out later, as the way you stand, and the things you build, and the debts you refuse to leave unpaid ever again.

"Wren," I said.

"He paid it," she said. Her voice was scraped raw. "He didn't owe it. There was no ledger. I forgave him months ago and he knew it and he paid it anyway." She bowed her head until it touched the stone. "Stubborn smuggler."

"Yeah," I said, kneeling beside her, putting my hand on the cold rock over the place where the gold had been. "Found family's like that."

* * *

Kane came at dawn.

We saw him on the ridge road, one man on a lathered horse, riding badly, slumped in the saddle, and half the camp reached for weapons before I waved them down. He fell off the horse rather than dismounted, and Sable and I caught him between us, and he weighed nothing, a man emptied out.

He'd been hurt days ago, by the smell of it—a sword wound in his side, bound crudely, gone bad. His face was grey. His hands, when he fumbled at his coat, shook like an old man's.

"Halloway," he rasped. "Proof. Ledgers. Harvest schedules. Names. Every gifted taken, every shipment, every—signature. His signature. Ten years of it." He pressed a bloodstained satchel into my hands like a man handing over a newborn. "The king doesn't know. I checked. Gods help me, I checked. The hunt is Halloway's. The crown's been lied to along with everybody else."

"Why bring it to us?" I said. "You could've ridden to the capital. Been a hero. Bought back your honor with a bow and a speech."

Kane laughed, which turned into coughing, which turned into something close to weeping.

"Because honor's not a purchase," he said. "It's a payment plan. And I'm behind." His eyes found Wren, standing at the edge of the group, the shard-light already rising around her and Callum as they turned toward the gate. "The reforging. It's true, then. You're really going to try."

"We're going to do more than try," I said.

"Then go." He tried to straighten and failed, and Sable lowered him gently to the ground. "Halloway knows you're here. Everything he has left is converging on the gate—he's going to drain it dry before you can reach the heart. You have hours. Maybe less." He looked up at me with the eyes of a man who had finally decided what he was willing to be. "Go make my treason worth something."

I slung the satchel across my body and turned to face the Hollow.

Above us, past the ruined yard and the dead engine, Halloway's machines were waking again—every pipe, every cage, every stolen spark in his black arsenal pouring toward the gate in a single greedy river. And beneath it all, deeper than the machinery, older than the theft, the Hollow Gate began to burn.

Not orange. Not the color of cages.

Gold.

Wren was already walking toward it, Callum at her side with the shard singing against his chest, and the light rose off them both like dawn arriving early.

"Stay close," I told the others, drawing my sword. "Whatever happens next—stay close."

We went up toward the burning.
