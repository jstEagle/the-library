## 18. The Reforging

The heart of the Hollow Gate was a chamber I had entered exactly once before in my life, ten years ago, on the night I helped kill it.

It looked smaller now. That was the first thing I noticed, and the pettiest, and I clung to the pettiness because the alternative was screaming. The vaulted hollow beneath the arch, the walls of veined grey stone that the old keepers called the Root of the Door—all of it seemed shrunken, the way a childhood home shrinks when you return as an adult and find the ceilings low and the magic gone out of it. Except here the magic hadn't just gone out. It had been *piped out*, siphoned through copper veins bolted crudely to the ancient stone, running in gleaming rivers toward the machine at the chamber's center.

The harvest engine sat where the covenant altar used to be. I remembered the altar: a low basin of black glass where the old stone had rested, warm as a sleeping bird. In its place squatted Halloway's abomination—a lattice of iron and copper and glass cylinders, hissing steam, drinking light. And inside those cylinders, suspended like specimens in a physician's cabinet, swirled colors that had no business existing outside a living person. Greens. Violets. A particular shade of gold I recognized because I'd seen it in my own palms on dark nights when I thought no one was watching.

People's gifts, bottled. People's souls, decanted.

"There she is," said Lord Edmun Halloway, from atop the engine's platform, as if greeting a late guest to dinner rather than a fugitive he'd spent a season trying to hang. He was smaller in person than his portraits. That was the second petty thing I noticed. His voice, though, was exactly as advertised—smooth, reasonable, the voice of a man who has never once doubted that his arithmetic is correct. "Wren Eriksen. The keeper who ran. I confess I expected you weeks ago."

"I got lost," I said. "Terrible signage in your kingdom lately. All the criers shouting about murdering people with gifts, very distracting."

Behind me, Sera Voss made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Good. If we were going to die in this room, we were going to die annoying him.

Halloway descended the platform steps with the unhurried grace of a man whose guards outnumbered us eight to one. They ringed the chamber in crown grey, pikes leveled, and among them—moving stiffly, one arm bound in blood-soaked linen—I saw Aldric Kane, weapon in hand but pointed nowhere in particular, his face the color of old ash. He'd made it. Doss had bought him the road with his life, and Kane had spent that coin carrying a satchel full of Halloway's own ledgers. I could see the satchel's strap cutting into his shoulder even from across the chamber.

"You're bleeding," Halloway observed mildly. "All of you. This is what comes of idealism."

"This is what comes of your machines," Sera said. Her voice was flat and cold and absolutely steady, which anyone who knew her would recognize as the most dangerous register she owned. "I've seen the cages, Halloway. Children."

"Patients," Halloway corrected, and God help me, he believed it. I watched his face as he said it and there was nothing behind the word but sincerity. "Vaelmoor is dying. You know this—all the old texts say it, all the keepers' records confirm it. When Vaelmoor dies, the magic bleeds out of Thornfell too. Our fields fail. Our gifted children burn out or go mad. I have merely built a pump between a flooding cellar and a dry field." He spread his hands. "I am not a monster. I am an engineer."

"An engineer," I repeated. "With cages."

"With *efficiencies*," he said, and turned to me, and smiled, and I felt the smile like frost down my spine. "But you didn't fight through half a mountain to discuss ethics with me, Keeper. You came for the gate. And here is the tragedy of your evening: so did I. The engine is nearly finished with the last reserves of the old covenant. When it draws the final thread—" he gestured at the copper-veined stone around us, and the whole chamber gave a low, tectonic sigh, like something vast rolling over in its sleep— "the passage closes forever. Vaelmoor gutters out on its own side of the mountain. Thornfell keeps every drop harvested. Everyone I love prospers."

"And everyone you don't," Sera said, "goes in a jar."

"The arithmetic is unkind," Halloway agreed. "Arithmetic usually is."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the moment everything you've feared arrives: it doesn't feel like a climax. It feels like Tuesday. Your hands are cold. You notice absurd things—the way steam curls off the engine's valves, the fact that one of the guards has mud on his boot, the memory, unbidden and useless, of Doss telling you once that the trouble with noble houses is they mistake their ledgers for their souls. *He wasn't wrong,* I thought. *And he still died for it.*

Callum stood pressed against my side, and through his coat I could feel the shard. Even through wool and linen and a boy's ribs, I could feel it—a heat, a hum, a hungering. Since the waygate it had been singing almost constantly, and now, in the presence of its murdered siblings woven through Halloway's copper veins, the song had become a keening only I and the boy could hear.

"It knows them," Callum whispered. His face was pale as the snow on the passes. "The pieces. It wants—" He stopped, swallowed. "It wants to go home, Wren."

"I know," I said. "Soon."

"Keeper Eriksen." Halloway had paused halfway down the steps, head tilted, studying me with fresh interest. "You have something. I felt it when you entered—the engine registered a resonance. What do you carry?"

"Snacks," I said.

His eyes went to Callum. To the boy's coat. And I watched the arithmetic complete itself behind his face, quick and terrible, because Halloway had spent months harvesting the gifted and he knew better than anyone alive what a fragment of covenant stone felt like when it came home.

"Guards," he said quietly. "The boy."

Everything happened very fast after that, and also, somehow, very slowly, the way things do when your blood decides the world should move at its pace instead of yours.

Sera moved first—of course Sera moved first; she'd been coiled since the tunnel—and took two guards' attention and one pike-thrust meant for her throat onto her shield arm, roaring something that was either a war cry or a very creative insult. Kane turned his weapon at last, not on the guards but on the engine's nearest copper vein, and fired, and the chamber filled with a shriek of escaping magic that sounded, horribly, like a choir. Callum grabbed my hand and ran with me toward the platform, because that had always been the plan, the only plan, the plan we'd bled across three mountain ranges to reach: get the shard to the altar's place before Halloway's engine drank the last of the old covenant dry.

A guard cut across our path. I didn't think. My hands came up and my gift came with them—ten years of hiding it, choking it down small, and now I let it fill me to the fingertips, gold and furious—and the guard stopped mid-stride as the stone beneath his boots blazed with warmth, with *life*, the old gate's dormant power waking at my touch. He stared at the glowing floor. Then he stared at me. Then, wisely, he decided to be somewhere else.

"You could have done that the entire journey," Callum panted.

"Not the entire journey. There was that week I was too tired."

We reached the platform. The engine loomed over us, and up close it was worse than an abomination—it was a *desecration*, the black-glass basin of the old altar bolted into its guts like a heart sewn into the wrong chest. Colors churned in the cylinders. The keening rose. And beneath my feet, through the platform, I felt the Hollow Gate itself—not dead, as I'd told myself for ten years. Not sealed. *Drowning.* Holding its breath. Waiting for someone to remember how it breathed.

"Callum," I said. "Give it to me."

He fumbled the shard out of his coat. Up close I could see what ten years of carrying it had cost him—the fine tremor in his hands, the bruised shadows under his eyes, the way the shard's light moved beneath his skin like a second pulse. He'd been a battery for a dying world since he was seven years old. He held it out, hesitated, and then—because he was Callum, because he was the bravest person in that chamber including the disgraced royal guard currently holding off four soldiers—he pressed it into my palm and closed my fingers around it with both of his small, scarred hands.

"Don't drop me," he said, which I understood eventually to mean *don't waste it.*

"I won't," I said.

Then the world exploded. Or a portion of it did—one of the cylinders burst under Sera's shield, showering the platform in liberated light, and through the spray of gold and green I saw Halloway climbing toward us, past caring about dignity now, a pistol in his aristocratic hand and murder in the set of his mouth.

"Put it down, girl," he shouted over the shrieking engine. "You'll kill us all! The rite requires a covenant that no longer exists! It requires truths that died with the old world! You cannot forge what you don't understand—"

"Watch me," I said, and set the shard in the basin.

* * *

The old texts describe the reforging in careful, scholarly language. *The keeper shall lay the fragment upon the root-stone and speak the truth of the severance, and the stone shall hear, and answer.*

The old texts can go hang themselves.

The moment the shard touched the black glass, everything I was became a conduit. Light poured up my arms—not pain, exactly, though pain was certainly invited to the party—more like being *read*. Like the stone was a book and my life was the page it needed, and it flipped through me looking for the thing it required. The rite demanded a truth spoken. Ilyen had warned us. *The truth of the severance, spoken whole, or the forging fails and the speaker is unmade.*

There was only one truth it wanted. I had known there was only one truth it wanted since the night by the waygate, and I had spent the whole march to this mountain not saying it, hoping desperately the stone would accept some lesser confession. A lie of omission. A truth with the sharp edges filed off.

The stone flipped to the page. It waited. The whole chamber waited—the engine's stolen fire, the fighting guards, Sera with her ruined arm, Callum white-faced at my elbow, Halloway frozen on the steps with his pistol half-raised, all of it suspended in the shard's rising blaze like flies in amber.

"Wren." Sera's voice, raw, from somewhere below. She knew. Of course she knew; I'd told her on the road, and I had watched her trust crack like river ice, and she had come anyway. "Say it. Whatever it costs—say it."

So I said it.

"Ten years ago," I said, and my voice carried impossibly far in that stone throat, "a keeper named Wren Eriksen stood at this gate with her mentor and sealed it from the Thornfell side, alone, against her orders, because she believed she was saving her kingdom from a fae war. She was nineteen years old and certain and wrong. The war she feared was already ending. The sealing did not prevent it—the sealing *caused* the wound. Cut Vaelmoor off from its own heart. Every winter since, that country has dimmed because of what I did in this room. Every child in a cage out there, every gift gone grey, every family torn apart by the hunt—the hunt exists because magic has been failing since the night I turned my hand against the door and told myself it was mercy." My eyes burned. My hands, braced on the basin's rim, glowed like banked coals. "I did not come here to undo another man's crime. I came to undo mine. That is the truth of the severance. I spoke the seal. Let me speak its ending."

Silence. Even the engine's shriek seemed to hold its breath.

Then the shard answered.

Light detonated upward from the basin, silent and total, and in that light I felt every stolen fragment answer from Halloway's copper veins—the bottled gifts, the piped souls, the harvested years—all of them turning toward me like sunflowers toward dawn. The copper veins began to glow. To strain. The engine screamed, a real scream now, metal and magic tearing at each other, because everything it had swallowed wanted out and the covenant stone, reforming above my palms piece by molten piece, was calling them home.

"No," Halloway said. It was the first honest sound I'd ever heard him make. "No—you will *not*—"

He raised the pistol. I watched his finger whiten on the trigger, and I found, strangely, that I was no longer afraid. Terror had been transfigured somewhere in the speaking—burned away like fog in sunlight—and what remained underneath was purpose, clean and hard as forged steel. The stone assembled itself in my hands, shard and fragments and the old basin's black glass all flowing together, and it was warm, warm as a living thing, warm as the bird-sleep warmth I remembered from before, and it *knew* me, and it was waiting for the last instruction, the final term.

The pistol cracked.

The shot never landed. The reformed stone flared, and the bullet struck a wall of morning that had no business existing underground, and Lord Edmun Halloway stood blinking in the light of everything he had spent his life measuring and never once understood.

"What," he whispered, "is happening?"

"Arithmetic," I said. "Yours was unkind."

I placed both hands on the covenant stone, and I gave it the only thing left it asked of me, and the asking was gentle, which somehow made it worse—but that, as they say, is the next chapter of this particular ruin.

The gate opened its eye. And the last thing I saw before the light took everything, everywhere, all at once—

—was the far wall of the hollow splitting open along an ancient seam, and through the widening crack, thin as a knife-edge, a wind was blowing *in*: sweet, wild, and smelling of a country I had doomed, coming home to collect its debt.
