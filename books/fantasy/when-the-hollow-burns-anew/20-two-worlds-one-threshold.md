## 20. Two Worlds, One Threshold

I have stood guard over a lot of things in my life. A king I no longer believed in. A camp full of frightened children. A woman who turned out to be the reason half of Vaelmoor was dying. But I had never stood guard over a miracle before, and it turns out miracles are worse than sieges, because at least in a siege you know who's coming and what they want.

The morning after the reforging, the Hollow Gate burned with a light that had no heat in it—gold shot through with green, like sunlight falling through leaves—and on the far side of it, Vaelmoor was visible for the first time in ten years to anyone standing where I stood. Not a rumor. Not a story told by smugglers with more scars than teeth. An actual country, dim as a coal banked under ash, but there.

And people were coming through it.

Ilyen arrived first, as Wren had said she would, walking through the light with her hands open like a woman approaching a horse she didn't know. She was taller than I expected and thinner than was comfortable to look at, reed-grey robes hanging off her the way flags hang off a defeated army. She stopped three paces from Wren, looked her up and down—the giftless keeper, hollowed out by her own sacrifice—and said something in a language that sounded like water going over stones.

Then she switched to trade-tongue, accented but clear. "You are smaller than the songs will make you."

"They always say that," Wren said. Her voice was rough. She'd been crying, or close to it, and she wasn't hiding it, which from Wren was its own kind of miracle. "Welcome to Thornfell. Mind the mud."

Ilyen looked down at the churned ground where an army had recently tried very hard to kill us all. "We have mud," she said gravely. "Ours is older."

I decided then that I liked her.

* * *

The days after the battle were the strangest of my life, and I once spent a week disguised as a tax collector to get close enough to arrest a corrupt magistrate, so the bar is high.

They came through in ones and twos at first. Fae, actual fae, stepping over the threshold into Thornfell with the cautious air of people testing ice. Some were tall and strange and beautiful in ways that made your eyes slide off them. Most were just—people. Tired people. Hungry people. A world's worth of refugees from a slow death, blinking in our grey northern light and asking, in careful trade-tongue, whether the water was safe to drink.

It fell to me to organize them, because apparently I'm the sort of person who ends up organizing things. Sera Voss, disgraced royal guard, leader of fugitives, now chief quartermaster of a border that hadn't existed a fortnight ago. I set up cook fires. I rationed what we'd salvaged from Halloway's stores—which, I'll note, was stolen magic converted into grain and medicine, so every loaf we broke was a small act of justice. I posted watches, though against whom I couldn't have said. There was no enemy left on this side of the gate. Just two peoples staring at each other across a fire and trying to remember how to be neighbors.

Callum appointed himself translator-in-chief, mostly because he was the only one of us unafraid to walk up to a seven-foot fae herdsman and ask him his name. The shard was gone—given up into the stone with everything else—but the boy carried something else now, something quieter. He walked differently. He'd been a boy carrying a burden; now he was a boy carrying a purpose, and the second one fit him better.

"You should eat," Wren told me on the third day, appearing at my elbow with a bowl of something the fae cooks had made. It smelled like honey and pine and was almost certainly better for me than anything I'd eaten in years.

"I'm eating," I said, which was a lie, and she knew it, because she took my ledger out of my hands and put the bowl in their place.

"You've slept four hours in three days."

"I've had worse campaigns."

"That's not the flex you think it is." She sat down next to me on the supply crate, close enough that our shoulders touched, and for a while neither of us said anything. Below us, the camp spread along the mountainside—tents and cookfires and fae lanterns burning green among our orange ones, and the Gate above it all, steady as a lighthouse.

"Sera." She said my name carefully, the way you pick up something sharp. "Are we all right? You and me?"

That was the question, wasn't it. I'd had three days to turn it over, and I still didn't have an answer I liked.

Here's what I knew. Ten years ago, Wren Eriksen sealed the Hollow Gate believing she was saving Thornfell from a war. She was young, she was lied to, and she was carrying a secret that would have gotten her killed if she'd spoken it. And her sealing began the slow murder of another world—a whole country dimming toward death while she stood watch at the door of it, keeping everyone out, keeping herself alone.

She'd told me too late. That was the wound. Not the sealing itself—I could forgive the girl she'd been; I'd been that girl once, following orders I shouldn't have. It was the years of silence. Every mile we'd walked together, every night she'd taught Callum the passes, every time she'd looked at me and called me friend, she'd known what she'd done and let me trust her anyway.

But here's the other thing I knew. When it mattered—when the truth could have stayed buried forever, when confessing cost her everything, when she had nothing left to gain—she'd spoken it anyway. On the eve of the assault, with Halloway's army coming, she'd handed me the knife and shown me where to cut.

"Ask me again in a month," I said finally.

Wren winced. "Sera—"

"No. Hear me." I turned to face her. "I'm not punishing you. I want that understood. You paid the price, all of it, more than anyone asked. But trust isn't a coin you hand back once it's dropped. It has to be rebuilt, and rebuilding takes time, and I won't pretend otherwise just because the ending was heroic. You deserve better than a lie, even a kind one."

She was quiet a long moment. Then she nodded, slowly, and something in her face eased—not relief exactly, but something adjacent to it. Respect, maybe. "A month," she said. "Fair."

"And in the meantime," I went on, "you're going to help me organize these supply lines, because Ilyen's people brought seed-craft I don't understand and mine are about to mutiny over the barley rations."

Wren laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and picked up the ledger. "Yes, Captain."

"Don't call me that."

"Yes, Quartermaster."

"Worse."

* * *

The king's men came on the fifth day.

I saw the banners first—Thornfell blue and silver, a column of fifty riders winding up the mountain road—and my hand went to my sword before my brain caught up. Old habits. But Kane rode at the head of them, pale and bandaged and upright through sheer spite, and beside him rode a herald in royal colors carrying a scroll the size of a bread loaf.

Kane dismounted badly. He'd taken wounds at Halloway's fall that would have killed a less stubborn man, and he walked like a man arguing with his own legs. He stopped in front of me, and for a moment neither of us spoke. The last time we'd met, I'd put a blade to his throat and meant it.

"Halloway's dead," he said.

"So I heard. Consumed by his own machine. Poetic."

"The king has seen my evidence. All of it. The harvest records, the ledgers, the—" He stopped. Swallowed. "He wept, Voss. I never thought to see it. Then he signed the rescission. The hunt is ended. Every warrant voided. Every survivor pardoned." He held out the scroll. "There's a formal proclamation. The herald wants to read it at the Gate. Something about symbolism."

"He can read it," I said, taking the scroll, "but if he uses the word 'magnanimous' I'm throwing him off the mountain."

Something that might have been a smile crossed Kane's ruined face. "He was warned."

The proclamation was read that afternoon, at the threshold, with fae and humans crowded shoulder to shoulder to hear it. The herald did use the word magnanimous. I restrained myself. Mostly. The gist of it was simple enough: the hunt was over, the gifted of Thornfell were free, and the crown acknowledged—these words clearly costing the herald physical pain—that grave wrongs had been done in its name, wrongs which the crown pledged to make right.

When he finished, there was silence. Then old Marta, the hedge-witch who'd hidden in Greyfen since the first purge, started to cry, and then a lot of other people did too, and the herald stood there holding his scroll looking like a man who'd rehearsed for a speech and gotten a funeral instead.

Wren found me afterward at the edge of the crowd. "You heard?" she said.

"I heard. Pardons all around. We're citizens again." I snorted. "Ten years ago I'd have given my sword arm for those words. Now they feel like someone apologizing for a fire after the house has burned."

"It's a start."

"It's a start," I agreed. "Question is what we build on it."

* * *

What we built on it, it turned out, was a market.

Ilyen had been negotiating with the herald and anyone else in a sash for days, and she was better at it than any diplomat I'd ever seen, mostly because she treated every negotiation like a conversation between equals rather than a performance. By the end of the second week, the first true crossing was scheduled: fae healers coming through to work in Thornfell's plague-struck lowland towns, and in exchange, grain and iron and—this was Ilyen's particular request—seeds. "Your plants grow loud here," she explained. "In Vaelmoor everything whispers. We would like some loud things."

So on the appointed morning, the Gate became what it had always been meant to be. Not a wall. A door.

I stood watch while they came through—healers in moss-green robes, traders with carts that folded up impossibly small, a delegation of stonemasons who took one look at the scarred mountainside and began arguing excitedly about repairs. And on our side, the first Thornfell folk to cross: farmers, merchants, one extremely nervous priest, and a contingent of children who broke ranks immediately and ran laughing into a country their grandparents had called a nightmare.

Callum stood at the threshold with Wren, watching them go, and I caught the expression on his face—wonder layered over grief layered over hope, the whole geology of him visible for once.

"They're just people," he said.

"Just people," Wren agreed.

"I thought they'd be..." He gestured vaguely. "More."

"Everyone thinks that," I said, coming up beside them. "About anyone different. It's the oldest lie there is, and the cheapest." I nodded toward the healers, who were already being mobbed by our wounded. "Look at them. Half of them are limping. One of them is asleep standing up. They're a dying country's last strength, and they came anyway, to mend people who spent a decade hunting their kin." I shook my head. "If that doesn't shame us into being better, nothing will."

Wren glanced at me sideways. "Was that a speech, Captain?"

"It was a briefing."

"It was definitely a speech."

* * *

On the evening of the thirtieth day, Wren found me on the ridge above the Gate, where I went when the camp got too loud. She sat down without asking, which was new. The old Wren would have hovered at a polite distance until invited.

"A month," she said.

"I keep count."

"I know you do." She pulled her cloak tighter against the wind. Below us the Gate burned its steady gold-and-green, and the camp glowed around it like embers around a hearth. "So. Am I forgiven?"

I made her wait. Not out of cruelty—out of honesty, because the answer deserved more than a reflex.

"Here's what I learned in the guard," I said. "Loyalty isn't obedience. Anyone can follow orders. Loyalty is staying when staying costs you. And truth—" I looked at her. "Truth is the same thing, said out loud. You stayed. You spoke. Late, but you spoke, and late truth is worth more than timely lies. So yes." I exhaled. "Forgiven. Not forgotten—I'll carry the anger a while yet, that's just how I'm built. But forgiven."

Wren's eyes were bright. "That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it. Tomorrow I'm back to calling you a menace to supply discipline."

"Noted." She was quiet a moment, watching the light. "Sera... what happens now? To us. All of us. The hunt's over, the war's over, the covenant's reforged. People are going to start going home."

"And some of us don't have one," I said. "Greyfen's gone. My commission's ash. Your post—" I caught myself. "Sorry."

"No, it's true. Keeper no more." She said it lightly, but I heard the hollow under it, the place where ten years of purpose used to be. "I don't know what I am now."

I did, actually. I'd known for weeks. I'd watched her teach Callum the passes, watched her sit with the freed prisoners from the harvest fields, watched her learn fae healing-songs from Ilyen's people with the fierce humility of someone making up for lost time. I'd watched her stand at the threshold every single morning, not guarding it anymore, just—being with it.

"You're someone who builds doors," I said. "That's what you are. Always have been. You just spent ten years building them shut."

She stared at me. Then she laughed, wet-eyed, and bumped her shoulder against mine, and we sat there in the wind like a pair of soldiers off duty, watching two worlds learn each other's names.

Below us, a fae child and a Thornfell child were teaching each other a clapping game, getting it wrong in two languages, laughing anyway.

"There's your new order," I said. "Starts small."

"Most good things do."

* * *

Later that night, Callum came running up the ridge path with a lantern and an expression I'd learned to dread, because on him it meant either wonderful news or catastrophe, and historically it had been both at once.

"Sera! Wren! You have to come see!"

"If this is another goat situation—"

"It's not goats!" He grabbed my sleeve, tugging. "It's the king. He's here. At the Gate. Right now. And he's asking—" Callum paused dramatically, which is a habit I was going to break him of if it killed me. "He's asking for the woman who reforged the covenant. By name."

Wren and I exchanged a look.

"Well," she said. "Let's go meet the man whose warrants used to have my name on them."

We went down together, the three of us, toward the burning bridge and the waiting king—and whatever he wanted, I had a feeling it was going to change everything again.
