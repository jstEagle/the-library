## 21. The Keeper No More

The offer came on a Tuesday, which felt about right. Kingdoms, in my experience, do their most insulting work on Tuesdays.

A courier climbed the mountain road to the gate with a satchel of documents and an expression of a man who had been told the recipient was dangerous and had decided to be disappointed about it. He found me sitting on the old keeper's step, mending a kettle with a hammer and more optimism than skill, and he looked from me to the ruined harvest engine still smoldering in the valley below to me again, as if checking that we were the same person.

"Wren Eriksen," he said. Not a question. A man reading a list.

"That's what it says on the kettle."

He blinked. I let him. It's one of the few pleasures left to a woman without magic—watching officials recalibrate.

He produced a scroll sealed with enough wax to caulk a boat. "By order of His Majesty, you are offered restoration of your post as Keeper of the Hollow Gate. Full honors. Full stipend. A garrison of twenty to serve under your command." He paused, clearly expecting this to land like a coronation.

I looked at the gate behind him.

It had been three weeks since the reforging, and the Hollow Gate no longer looked like anything the word *gate* had prepared anyone for. The old seal—the one I had helped forge ten years ago out of fear and bad counsel and stone that sang like a struck bell—was gone. In its place stood an archway of living granite threaded with veins of light that pulsed slow and warm, like something breathing. On quiet mornings you could hear it hum. On loud mornings, when the first fae traders came through with their carts of glassfruit and mothsilk, it hummed louder, almost smug.

Through the arch, Vaelmoor shimmered: silver grass, pale towers, a sky the color of dawn that had decided to stay. And crossing back and forth beneath it, every day now, people. Human shepherds trading wool for starlight-salt. Fae children daring each other to touch Thornfell dirt. An entire border town assembling itself around the threshold like iron filings around a magnet, because where two worlds touch, someone will always arrive to sell soup.

"Twenty soldiers," I said.

"To guard the gate," the courier said. "As before."

"As before." I set down the kettle. "You mean to stand armed men at a door that's finally open, and call it keeping."

"The covenant requires—"

"I've read the covenant. I wrote half of it. With my hands. While screaming." I said it mildly, and watched his ears go pink. "There's nothing in it about garrisons. There's nothing in it about guards at all. There's a threshold, and there are two peoples, and there is a promise that neither will drain the other ever again. That's the whole document. It fits on a napkin. Ilyen checked."

"The fae envoy does not speak for the crown—"

"No," I agreed. "The crown speaks for the crown, and look how that's gone." I stood, brushing stone dust off my knees, and took my time about it, because some refusals deserve ceremony. "Tell His Majesty thank you for the offer. Tell him the post of Keeper is hereby retired, along with the woman who held it. Tell him if he wants the gate watched, he should send teachers instead of soldiers, because what this door needs guarding against is ignorance, and I'm hiring."

The courier looked at me the way men look at weather they've been warned about but never believed in.

"And what," he said carefully, "should I tell him you're doing instead?"

I smiled. It had been three weeks since I'd had a gift, and I was only just learning what my face could do without one. Turns out: plenty.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about losing your magic: it doesn't feel like loss. It feels like silence after a bell has been ringing so long you'd forgotten the ringing wasn't the world.

For ten years my gift had been a small thing—a warmth in the palms, a knack for hearing stone, enough to keep a gate sealed and a secret kept. Then I poured all of it into the covenant stone, every last ember, and woke up the next morning ordinary. Mortal in the specific, unglamorous way. My hands were just hands. When I pressed them to the gate's arch now, I heard nothing but wind.

I expected grief. What I got, mostly, was appetite. I ate like a horse. I slept like the dead. Sera said I looked better than she'd ever seen me, and then looked alarmed at having said something kind, and then punched my arm to restore the natural order.

But some nights—and I'll admit this once, here, where no one can make me repeat it—some nights I wake reaching for the warmth in my palms and find only cool linen, and I lie there listening to the gate hum through the walls of the cottage we're building, and I think: *that was mine. That small warm thing was mine, and I gave it away, and I would give it again, and isn't it strange that both of those sentences are true.*

Then morning comes, and with it the children, and there's no time for strange.

Because that's what I'm doing instead. The school.

It started the way most of my disasters start: with Callum asking a question I couldn't answer. He'd been at the gate every day since the reforging, shard long surrendered into the stone, hands always hovering near the arch like a boy standing next to a fire he'd personally lit. The fae children had started coming through—three of them at first, then nine, then a whole ragged parade of pointed ears and impossible eyes—and they didn't share a language with the Thornfell kids beyond shouting and rocks, which is a language, but not a curriculum.

"How do you say *hello* in Vaelmoor?" Callum asked me.

"You don't. You say it in Old Covenant. Everyone did, before the sealing. It's the tongue both realms used at the gate."

"Teach me."

"I—" And there it was, the hole where my confidence used to live. "Callum, I was a smuggler's friend and a secret keeper. My teaching experience is explaining to Doss why we can't sell moth eggs."

"You taught me the passes," he said. "You taught me which berries lie. You taught me how to walk soft and listen hard." He shrugged, fourteen years old and already better at argument than I'd been at thirty. "That's teaching."

So I started with four children under the arch—two human, two fae, one shared alphabet scratched into slate—and by the end of the month there were forty, and by midwinter there were so many that Sera built benches out of the dismantled harvest engine, which I maintain is the single most satisfying act of carpentry in recorded history. Halloway's machines, melted and beaten flat into school desks. If the man's ghost objects, it can take it up with the ghost of everyone he caged.

Ilyen sends tutors over from Vaelmoor twice a week. They teach the fae tongue, star-reading, and a form of mathematics that appears to involve arguing with numbers until they apologize. I teach everything else: letters, history (both versions, loudly), the passes, the berries that lie, and the Old Covenant tongue, which turns out I remember far better than I admitted, because a girl who spends ten years alone with a humming wall picks things up.

And Sera—

Sera stands at the edge of the yard with her arms crossed and her sword worn plain, and watches.

* * *

The king's second offer came in spring, delivered this time not by a courier but by a nervous minor lordling who climbed the mountain in court boots and regretted every step.

"He asks again," the lordling panted. "Keeper of the Gate. He's added a title. *Warden of the Two Realms.* There's a house attached. In Ashford. With a garden."

"A garden," I repeated.

"With roses."

"Sera," I called across the yard, where she was pretending not to listen while sharpening a blade that did not need it, "the king is offering me roses."

"Tell him you're allergic," she said.

"I'm not allergic to roses."

"You would be. For the principle of it."

The lordling looked between us like a man watching a tennis match played with knives. "My lady, the position comes with real authority. The border settlements are growing unruly. Traders cheat each other. There was a knife fight in the market last week. The crown believes a firm hand—"

"The crown believes," I said, "that authority is a thing you hand a person along with a house. But authority grows or it doesn't, like anything else. Look." I gestured at the yard, and made myself see it as he might: forty children of two worlds sitting in rows on melted-machinery benches, a fae tutor demonstrating the letter *vael* with her four hands, Callum at the front helping the little ones, wearing the serious frown of a boy who has decided to be responsible and is still negotiating terms with himself. "Nobody appointed any of this. It grew. That's what makes it hold. Your king can keep his garden. I've got one. It screams at recess."

"The king will be... disappointed."

"The king has been disappointed by me before," I said. "He'll survive. I'm told it builds character. Ask him about the hunt sometime—he learned *so much*."

The lordling left with his scroll unsigned and his boots full of mountain mud, and I went back to my chalk, and that was the end of it. Mostly. The third offer arrived by raven, which I fed and did not answer, on the principle that even kings deserve one silent rejection before the formal one.

* * *

Not everyone wanted the school. Let's be honest about that, because the ballads aren't.

There were folk in Thornfell who remembered twenty years of stories about fae kidnappers and saw pointed ears in their market and reached for pitchforks on instinct. There were fae in Vaelmoor—Ilyen calls them the Grey Court remnant—who thought opening the gate to humans was like opening your veins to leeches and said so, elegantly, at length. Twice that winter, someone painted *GO HOME* on the schoolhouse wall in tar. Once, someone loosed a crossbow bolt through the yard during lessons; Sera caught the shooter herself, three miles down the pass, and brought him back tied to his own donkey.

What she did with him became legend among the children, mostly because she refused to tell them, which meant they invented versions. In the best one, she made him sit through a full day of lessons. In the truest one—which Callum got out of her eventually, because Callum gets everything out of everyone eventually—she sat him down at a bench beside a fae child named Merrin and made him learn the alphabet alongside her, and by the end of it the man was crying, though whether from shame or boredom remains disputed.

"We don't hang them," Sera told me afterward, on the step, watching the yard empty out at dusk. "That's the difference, isn't it? Halloway hung his problems. We educate ours. Slower. Louder."

"More expensive."

"Infinitely." She rolled her shoulder, the old wound aching the way it did before snow. "You know I'm not a teacher."

"I know. You're the shield. It's in the charter."

"There's no charter."

"There will be. Ilyen's drafting one. She wants a line about 'mutual flourishing.' I told her to add a line about snacks or the children would riot."

Sera huffed—not quite a laugh, but the geological event from which laughs are later quarried. Then, quieter: "Ten years ago, if someone had told me I'd end up guarding a school..."

"You'd have arrested them for sedition."

"I'd have *wanted* to arrest them for sedition." She turned the sword over in her lap, unneeded, habitual. "Wren. The king's going to keep offering. Men like that don't understand *no*, they just understand *not yet*. Eventually he'll send someone with more than roses."

"Then I'll refuse more elaborately."

"And if he stops asking nicely?"

I looked out at the gate. Evening light came through the arch from the Vaelmoor side, gold and strange, laying a long bright stripe across the schoolyard like a path someone had laid down and forgotten to finish. Through it, faint, I could hear Ilyen's people singing the evening song—the one that used to be sung at the old gate, before I sealed it, before everything dimmed. They sing it facing Thornfell now. An apology in reverse. Or maybe a welcome. I've stopped trying to sort those apart.

"Then Sera Voss stands at the door," I said, "and explains our attendance policy."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way she does when a decision settles into bone, and got up to check the yard gates, because shields don't stop working just because the conversation got sincere.

* * *

Summer came, and with it the first anniversary preparations, and with *those* came the moment I want to write down properly, because I am a novelist by accident and a record-keeper by necessity, and some things should outlive the people who saw them.

It was the day of the first examinations—not examinations, really, I lied to the children and called them "demonstrations"—and Callum stood up in front of the assembled school to recite the Covenant Tongue creation verse. He was fifteen now, tall in the way that happens overnight, all elbows and certainty. The shard was long gone from him, given back to the stone where it belonged, but he still carried himself like a boy who knew what it was to hold something heavy, and the younger ones arranged themselves around him the way iron filings arrange around a magnet.

He began. His accent was terrible. His heart was not.

And halfway through the second line, the gate answered.

Not much. A flicker in the light-veins, a deepening of the hum, the arch glowing the way a stove glows an hour after the fire. Just enough that every child in the yard turned to look, and every fae tutor went still, and Ilyen, visiting, pressed all four hands to her chest and murmured something in Vaelmoori that translated, roughly, to *oh, it remembers him.*

Callum faltered. Looked at me, panicked, fourteen years of orphan terror rising in his face in a single heartbeat.

"Keep going," I said. "It's listening. Give it the good part."

He gave it the good part. His voice steadied. The gate hummed along like a grandmother humming at a recital, slightly out of tune, entirely delighted. And when he finished, the children cheered, and the gate flared gold, and Callum Eriksen-Voss—no, he's not ours by paper, there's no paper, there's just the name he started using in winter camp when someone asked and he answered without thinking, and none of us corrected him, because that's how families get made, in the answering without thinking—Callum bowed, deeply, formally, in the style of the Vaelmoor court he'd learned from Ilyen's tutors, and ruined it completely by grinning.

Afterward, walking back up from the well, he fell into step beside me.

"You looked proud," he said. Suspicious. Accusatory, almost.

"I was proud."

"You're not supposed to be. You're supposed to be stern. You're the headmistress."

"I'm also the person who found you half-starved in a smuggler's tunnel eating candle wax, and now you're reciting creation verses to a living gate. Pride is the minimum response. I'm restraining myself from bragging to strangers."

"You brag to strangers constantly. Ilyen says you tell the story of the candle wax to every new trader."

"Ilyen should mind her four businessess." I bumped his shoulder. He bumped back, harder, nearly sending me into the well, because affection in teenage boys is measured in blunt force. "Go on. Merrin's waiting. You promised her the star-reading lesson."

He went. Of course he went—he always goes toward whoever needs him, that boy, that stubborn luminous boy, and I stood a moment at the well with the rope in my hands and watched him cross the schoolyard under the arch's warm light, and felt the hollow place where my gift used to be do the thing it does now instead of aching.

It fills.

Not with magic. With this. With chalk dust and terrible accents and a shield at the door and a boy who belongs here because he decided to, in a family that exists because we chose each other over and over, in a kingdom that keeps offering me titles I keep refusing because the truth is I already have the only post I ever wanted, and it came with no stipend, no garrison, no garden.

Just roses, actually. Sera planted roses along the schoolyard wall in the spring. She claimed it was defensive landscaping. Nobody believed her. Nobody needed to.

I was carrying the water bucket back when the rider appeared on the southern road—dust plume, official livery, riding fast. Sera saw him the same moment I did, and her hand went to her sword out of pure habit, and Callum, mid-lesson, looked up.

Another offer, probably. Another title. Another Tuesday.

Or something else entirely.

I set down the bucket, wiped my hands on my apron, and went to meet whatever the world was bringing up my mountain this time.
