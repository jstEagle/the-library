## 22. When the Hollow Burns Anew

A year ago, if you had told me I'd spend the longest night of the year stringing lanterns on a gate that had nearly killed me, I'd have asked which of us you thought was the fool.

And yet here I was, three rungs up a ladder that leaned against stone older than any kingdom, tying a fae-glass lantern to an iron hook with numb fingers while Sera Voss stood below with her arms crossed and her opinion of my ladder safety written across her whole face.

"You're going to fall," she said.

"I've fallen off worse things."

"That's not the comfort you think it is."

The lantern caught when I let it go—fae-glass doesn't need a flame, just a whisper of intent, and mine came easier these days even without the gift that used to live behind my ribs. It kindled soft and blue-white, one more star in a constellation we were building along the Hollow Gate's great arch. Behind me, through the open passage, the mountains rang with music in two languages.

I climbed down and looked at what we'd made.

For ten years this gate had been a wound sealed shut, cold as a dead man's promise. Tonight it burned. Not with the hungry fire of the old covenant, the kind that demanded truth like a tax collector demands coin, but with festival light—hundreds of lanterns strung from Thornfell's watchtowers, down the pass, through the arch itself, and out the other side into Vaelmoor, where Ilyen's people had lit their own answer: silver lamps in the shapes of moths and rivers, hung from trees that had spent a decade grey and were, this spring, cautiously green again.

Two strings of light meeting at one threshold. When Doss was alive he'd have called it the most expensive bonfire in history and then found some way to sell tickets to it.

"He would have loved this," Sera said, reading my silence the way she always did.

"He'd have tried to charge admission."

"And made a fortune."

"And been insufferable about it forever." I laughed, and the laugh caught somewhere in the middle and turned into something else for a moment, and Sera put her hand briefly on my shoulder—the whole of her comfort, exactly the right amount—and then let me have my grief back, private as ever.

A year. Long enough for the scar tissue to stop aching when the weather changed. Not so long that I didn't still turn around sometimes expecting to see him leaning on the tunnel mouth, hat crooked, wearing the expression of a man who knows exactly how much trouble he's in and has decided to enjoy it anyway.

He died holding a door open. Of course he did. It was the only door he'd ever truly cared about.

* * *

The children found me before noon, as they always did.

That's the thing about founding a school at the edge of the world: the school finds you back. We'd started with Callum and four local children whose parents wanted them to learn letters. Now there were thirty-two of them, and eleven were fae, which meant my mornings involved teaching human arithmetic next to children who could hear the color of numbers, and my afternoons involved explaining, again, why we did not fly over the refectory wall no matter how much easier it made lunch.

Today was no ordinary day, though. Today was the Night of Two Moons—or rather, the anniversary of the night Vaelmoor's second moon had come back. The night the covenant reforged and the dimming stopped and a whole country breathed in after ten years of holding its breath.

So the festival had spilled everywhere, and the children had spilled with it, and I found myself drawn to the sound of Callum's voice carrying over the crowd near the eastern buttress.

"—and then the stone sings, right, but not with your ears. You hear it here." He tapped his chest. "Like remembering something you never actually knew."

He was taller. That's the thing nobody warns you about grief and joy living in the same house—you miss entire seasons of a boy growing. He was fourteen now, or close enough that he'd stopped correcting people about it, and he wore his shard on a cord outside his shirt where everyone could see it, because secrets were for the old world and Callum had decided, with the absolute conviction of the young, that he was done with the old world.

Around him sat a half-circle of the younger ones, human and fae both, faces tipped up at him like sunflowers. In his hands he held a river-smooth piece of pale stone—a teaching copy of the covenant shard, one Ilyen had shaped for him, dead and harmless, good for lessons and nothing else.

"Show us the singing," said Pip, a fae girl of about seven who had never known the sealed years and therefore asked the questions adults were too polite or too frightened to ask.

"It only sang for Wren," Callum said, and grinned at me over their heads, traitor that he was. "Go on. Hold it."

Thirty-two excuses for a riot turned as one to look at me.

I should explain something. When I gave my gift to the stone—when I poured ten years of hidden light into that hungry seam and let it go—I assumed I'd be hollow afterward. Empty. That's what the old texts implied, what Ilyen feared, what I'd accepted somewhere between terror and resolve: that the price of two worlds was whatever was left of me.

What I hadn't expected was that the hollow would fill with everything else.

I took the teaching stone from Callum's hands. It sat dumb and cool in my palm, exactly as it should. But the children leaned in, and Pip reached out and touched it, and Callum said, "Listen," and there—

There it was. Faint as a memory of a memory. Not magic. Not anymore. Just resonance, the way a struck bell keeps a ghost of its note in the wood it rests on. The covenant remembered me the way I remembered it.

"They can all feel that?" I asked quietly.

"Every one of them," Callum said. "That's the lesson. The gate isn't a thing you guard anymore. It's a thing you belong to. All of you."

Pip looked up at me with the terrible directness of small children. "Did it hurt? Giving your gift away?"

The honest answer was yes, in the way that pulling your own heart out and setting it on an altar hurts, in the way I still sometimes woke reaching for a warmth that wasn't there. But there was a truer answer underneath, and I'd learned the hard way—ten years of hiding, a betrayal, a confession that shattered a friendship—that truer answers underneath are the ones that kill you if you bury them.

"Yes," I said. "It hurt like anything. And I'd do it again before you finished asking me to."

"Why?"

"Because—" I looked at the circle of faces, fae and human mixed without a single one of them thinking it strange, because to them it wasn't strange, because we had made it not strange, and the ache in my chest was the good kind, the full kind. "Because I used to think my gift was the only part of me worth anything. Turns out it was just the loudest part. There was plenty left. There was all of you."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a judge. "Sera says you're stubborn."

"Sera," I said, "is not wrong."

* * *

Dusk came early to the pass in winter, and the festival bloomed with it.

I'll try to describe the Night of Two Moons properly, because I want to remember it exactly, even the parts that embarrassed me.

On the Thornfell side: long tables down the switchback road, loaded with mountain food and fae food and the strange delicious children both cuisines produced when they met—honey-baked river eel, spiced root bread, little candied pine things that Ilyen swore were traditional and which I suspected she'd invented specifically to watch humans make that face. The king himself had sent a delegation, which was court-speak for *we're watching you*, but even the crown's watchers had loosened up by their third cup of mulled wine, and one of Kane's old investigators was currently losing gracefully at dice to a Vaelmoor tinker.

Kane himself wasn't there. He'd sent a letter instead, formal and stiff, from the capital where he was busy being uncomfortably honest in front of tribunals—Halloway's network was mostly dismantled now, though the harvesting machines' records kept surfacing new horrors, and Kane had appointed himself the man who read every page. *Someone must witness it,* his letter said. *I was complicit. Witnessing is the least of my penances.* At the bottom, in smaller writing, as if embarrassed: *Tell the boy the shard exercise was correct. He was right. I was wrong. He may quote me.*

Callum did quote him. Repeatedly. For weeks.

On the Vaelmoor side, the celebration ran deeper and stranger. A year of returning color had done what a century couldn't: the courts had stopped counting grievances and started counting grandchildren. Ilyen crossed the gate at moonrise wearing robes that shimmered between silver and green, and when she saw me she pressed her hand to her chest in the envoy's greeting and then abandoned all protocol entirely and hugged me, which she had been doing more of lately, and which I had decided to allow.

"Keeper," she said.

"Not anymore."

"Founder, then. Teacher. Wren." She smiled, and it was the smile of someone who had watched a world go grey and gotten its colors back one season at a time. "My people sent gifts for your school. Books. Seeds for trees that will take fifty years to grow—we are patient; you should learn to be."

"We're working on it."

"And where is the fierce one?"

Sera was, at that moment, at the head table being ceremonially presented with a Vaelmoor blade by a delegation of the very border wardens she'd once fought as a disgraced exile. She accepted it with a nod that would have frozen a smaller river. Later I saw her showing it to Pip, letting the girl hold it with both hands, saying something that made Pip laugh so hard she fell off the bench.

Disgraced royal guard. Fierce one. Shield of a school. Sera wore all her names now, and none of them fit badly, and when our eyes met across the firelight she raised the new sword in a small salute—*to us, still here*—and I raised my cup back, and neither of us needed to say the rest, because we had spent a year saying it in smaller ways.

That's what reconciliation looks like when it's real, I think. Not one grand speech. A thousand small returns.

* * *

Near midnight, the crowds drifted toward the gate itself for the lighting of the last lanterns, and for a while I stood alone at the threshold—which is not the same as standing alone, because the threshold was never empty anymore.

Here is what happens now, every night, that would have been unthinkable in my keeping years: folk cross. Traders with Vaelmoor silk heading to Ashford markets. Human healers going to study under physicians who work with living light. Fae children coming to my school in the morning and waving goodbye at dusk, crossing home through the arch with lantern-light gilding their horns. The gate hums underfoot, gentle as a sleeping animal, and asks nothing of anyone except that they be honest at the crossing—and it can tell, everyone learns quickly, so nobody bothers lying at the gate. It ruined smuggling forever. Doss would never have forgiven it.

I touched the stone. Warm. For ten years I'd known this arch as the coldest place in Thornfell, and now it held heat like a hearthstone, the way it must have in the deep past, before fear built walls inside the wall.

"Wren." Callum came up beside me, breath fogging, the shard glinting at his throat. Below us the last lanterns were being passed hand to hand up the line—human hands, fae hands, no one marking which was which. "They're going to sing the crossing-song. Both sides together. You should—"

"In a minute. Go on. Your students will stage a mutiny."

He hesitated. Then, in the too-careful voice he used when he'd been thinking about something for weeks: "I've been working on the resonance lessons. The ones with the teaching stones. Ilyen thinks I could teach the second-year class. Properly. If you'd—"

"Callum." I looked at him—this half-starved boy who'd once pressed a stolen shard into a stranger's hands in a snowdrift and changed the shape of two worlds—and felt the fullness press up behind my eyes. "You don't need my permission. You never did. But yes. Second-years. Yours."

He grinned, and it was Doss's grin, gods help us both, crooked and delighted and already planning mischief, and then he was gone down into the light and the noise, and I stayed at the top of the steps a moment longer.

The song started below—first the Thornfell voices, then the Vaelmoor ones joining a beat behind, weaving together the way they'd learned to over a year of festivals and arguments and shared harvests. The lanterns went up the line like a wave, and the arch caught them and multiplied them, and above us both moons rose clear over the pass: the old white one, and Vaelmoor's second, silver-green again, no longer a fading rumor but a fact in the sky.

Once, I'd guarded this gate against the world. Then I'd fled from it. Then I'd nearly died on its stones giving it everything I had. And all of that—the loneliness, the running, the betrayals given and received, the truths spoken too late and exactly on time—all of it had been the long road to this: standing unhunted, unhidden, un-hollow, at an open door between two worlds, listening to them sing to each other.

I used to think the hollow in me was where my gift had lived. I know better now. The hollow was the hiding. The hiding was the hollow. And it was full—full of a boy teaching light to children, a shield-sister laughing by the fire, a smuggler's grin echoing down a year, an envoy's hug, a gate gone warm, a whole sky with both its moons.

Wren Eriksen, Keeper of the Hollow Gate, dead ten months and glad of it.

I went down into the light to join the song.
