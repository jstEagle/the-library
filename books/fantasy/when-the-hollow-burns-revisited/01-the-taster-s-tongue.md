## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There were three hundred and sixty-one ways to kill a king of Amberdale, and August Amberdale — no relation, whatever the heralds' jokes might say — had committed every one of them to memory by way of the tongue.

Not the whole tongue, of course. Just the aftertaste. That was the trick nobody told you about poisons: they lied on the way down and told the truth on the way out. Almond was easy; everyone knew almond. But there was almond and then there was *bitter* almond, which meant peach kernels steeped in wine, a favorite of jealous wives because it could be blamed on a careless cook. There was copper that meant mineral salts, and copper that meant blood-root, and copper that meant somebody had stored the pheasant in a bad pewter dish, which wasn't murder at all, merely stinginess.

August sat at the small side table in the king's breakfast room with a silver spoon, a crystal glass of water, a wedge of lemon, and a lifetime of practice, and tasted everything before His Majesty touched a bite of it.

"Smoked trout," said the under-steward, Ferren, setting the plate down. He was a young man with the anxious dignity of someone who had recently been promoted past his competence and was hoping no one would notice. "From the Lirre. The palace fishmonger swore on his mother it came in this morning."

"His mother is dead," said August.

"He knows that. I think that's why he chose her."

August took a sliver of trout, let it sit on the tongue, and waited. Salt. Smoke. A faint sweetness at the back, which was correct for Lirre trout in early summer. And then — there — a whisper of tin at the very end, so faint you'd miss it if your livelihood didn't depend on not missing it.

"Hmm," said August.

Ferren went the color of good pastry. "Is it—"

"It's fine." August held up a hand. "The fish was packed in a tin-lined crate. Somebody wanted to keep it cold on the road. Tell your fishmonger his mother's honor is intact, but if he packs it in the crate longer than a day, the tin starts to whisper, and I start to frown, and nobody wants that."

Ferren exhaled like a man released from prison and went to tell the kitchen, where August had no doubt the story would become *the taster nearly died saving the king from poisoned fish*, which suited August fine. Reputation was armor. The better they thought August was at this job, the less anyone looked closely at anything else.

The rest of the breakfast went without incident. Honeyed porridge — clean, though the honey had a grassy edge that suggested the beekeeper was stretching it with clover. Bread. Butter. Small beer. Quince preserve, which August tasted twice purely for pleasure, because even a taster was allowed one vice, and quince was August's.

King Aldous arrived midway through the porridge, gray-faced and heavy, the way he arrived everywhere these days. He was not an old man, but he was beginning to look like one, and the winter that wouldn't end had aged him another five years all by itself.

"Amberdale," he said. "Anything trying to kill me today?"

"Not yet, Majesty. It's still early."

The joke was old. The king laughed anyway, the small tired laugh of a man who needed something to laugh at, and sat, and ate the porridge August had approved, and did not notice — he never noticed — when August slipped the last spoonful of quince preserve onto his own plate and ate it standing up, like a thief.

That was the job, really. Not the drama of it, not what the ballads pretended. No masked assassins, no vials in candlesticks. The truth of a food-taster's life was that you spent thirty years learning to be afraid in very small, very specific ways, and then you died of old age or you didn't, and either way the king never learned your first name.

August had been doing it for eleven years. Long enough that the fear had worn down into something more like habit. Long enough that August had gotten comfortable.

Comfortable, as August's mother used to say, is what a mouse calls the warm spot inside the cat.

* * *

The night market ran in the crook of the old tannery wall, where the lantern-light didn't quite reach and the city watch had decided, generations ago, that some corners were cheaper to ignore than to patrol. By day it was a shuttered row of empty stalls. By night it smelled of fried onions, wet wool, tanned hides, and — to anyone with a tongue like August's — a faint green shimmer of things that shouldn't have existed at all.

Charm-sellers worked the far end. Everyone called them charm-sellers the way they called the fortune-teller's cards *entertainment*: a polite fiction, a legal fig leaf. A rabbit's foot was superstition. A knotted red thread was folklore. A dried sprig of rowan sewn into a hem was just what grandmothers did.

Magic, actual magic, was punishable by burning. This had been law since before August was born, renewed with enthusiasm by Lord Chancellor Veyre, who liked to say that fire was the only honest form of debate. They burned people in Copperwick Square on the first Friday of every month, weather permitting, and it drew a crowd like a hanging, and mothers brought their children, and afterward everyone agreed it had been very regrettable and also probably necessary.

So the women and men at the far end of the night market sold luck. Only luck. Nothing else. Wink wink.

August went as a buyer, mostly, because buyers attracted less attention than sellers, and because August genuinely liked the other hedge-witches, scattered and careful as they all were. There was Old Nan Pell, who sold charms against nightmares and was possibly eighty and possibly immortal. There was a boy called Finch who could quiet a crying infant with a hum and swore up and down it was "just a trick" while making urgent eye contact. And there was tonight's customer, who wasn't there yet.

The baker's widow, Marta Voss, found August by the onion stall, the way she'd found August the last three times, which was by pretending to browse and failing to be subtle about it. She was a big woman with flour permanently in the creases of her knuckles and the particular grief-shaped tiredness of someone whose husband had died in the frost-fever that was going around, and whose bread had stopped rising properly ever since, and who had decided — correctly, as it happened — that these two facts might be related.

"Taster," she said, too loudly, then winced. "I mean. Good evening."

"Evening, Mistress Voss. Lovely onions."

"I don't want onions."

"No," August agreed gently. "Shall we walk?"

They walked. Past the tallow-candles and the secondhand boots, past Finch humming at a squalling baby in a sling until it hiccupped into silence (a trick, definitely just a trick), out to the mouth of the alley where the noise of the market thinned enough for talk.

"My loaves," Marta said, once she was sure no one was near. "They come out heavy. Wrong. Hal says — my brother-in-law Hal, he's not right about most things, but he said grief gets in the dough. And I thought, well, that's nonsense, and then I thought, but what if it isn't."

"Grief does get in the dough," August said. "It gets in everything. It's very undiscriminating."

"So you can—" She lowered her voice further, down to almost nothing. "You can do something."

August looked at her. This was the moment, every time, where a sensible person said *I sell rabbit bones and folk remedies, mistress, nothing more*, and walked away, and stayed invisible, and lived.

Three years now August had been practicing magic. Small magic only. Charm-sized. A warmth-charm here, a finding-charm there, a taste of blessing stirred into a sickroom broth. Small enough to pass as superstition, small enough that it never left a ripple the witch-finders' hooks could catch. August's whole life was built out of these small deniable kindnesses, laid brick by brick like a wall, and the wall's purpose was simple: to make sure that no one, ever, looked too closely at the woman with the famous tongue who never seemed to catch so much as a stomachache.

"Sourdough starter," August said quietly, "is a living thing. Like grief. Both need feeding. Both get sour if you neglect them." Marta blinked. August reached into a coat pocket and produced a small cloth bundle, tied with green thread. Inside was a smooth river stone, no bigger than a plum, which had spent the last week sitting in August's windowsill absorbing exactly the sort of patient warmth that sourdough and widows both required. "Put this near your crock. Don't tell anyone what it is. If anyone asks, it's a lucky stone, and it was your husband's, and you're keeping it for the children."

"It wasn't his."

"Now it was."

Marta held the stone like it might go off. Then her face did something complicated and crumpled, and she pressed a coin purse into August's hands and said, thickly, "He used to score the loaves with a leaf pattern. His mother's pattern. And I can't seem to—"

"You will," August said. "Feed the starter. Talk to it. People will think you're mad, and the bread won't care."

She laughed — a wet, surprised laugh, the best kind — and squeezed August's arm hard enough to bruise, and went home through the dark with her lucky stone, lighter by one grief-weight and several coins.

August stood in the alley mouth and watched her go.

This was the part nobody wrote ballads about. The walk home. The counting of the small kindnesses, which never added up to anything, which couldn't be told to anyone, which would burn you alive if they were discovered and changed precisely nothing in the world if they weren't. Somewhere out there, kings were saved by tasters and never learned their names, and widows' bread rose because of a warm rock, and neither fact would ever appear in any history.

*One person at a time,* August's mother used to say, back before the laws tightened, back when she still dared teach her child anything at all. *You can't save the world, little spark. You can feed it. One bowl at a time.*

August's mother had fed a great many bowls. And when the witch-finders finally came for her anyway — because small magic was still magic, and deniable was not the same as invisible — August had learned the real lesson, the one under the lesson: kindness keeps you human, but it doesn't keep you safe. Only smallness does that. Stay small. Stay useful. Stay forgettable.

August pulled up a coat collar against a wind that was far too cold for summer — third summer running now, and the elders grumbling that the seasons had gone wrong, and the court physicians blaming miasmas and the Chancellor blaming witches — and turned toward home through streets gone strangely quiet.

The tenement on Candlemaker's Row had four floors and one lamp lit in its stairwell, which meant either the landlady was awake or someone else was. August climbed past the third floor, keys out, thinking about supper, thinking about whether quince counted as a food group.

And stopped.

There was a smell in the stairwell that didn't belong. Under the tallow and the damp stone: iron. Fresh blood, plenty of it, and beneath that a stranger thing August had no name for — something like struck flint and cold honey and a forest after lightning, a scent that made the back of the tongue ache the way a high note makes glass ache.

August stood very still on the stairs, keys in hand, invisible life balanced perfectly on the head of a pin.

Someone coughed. From the landing above. Twice, wetly, deliberately — the cough of a person who wants to be heard.

Every instinct August owned, honed over eleven years of royal breakfasts and three years of careful secrets, said the same thing in perfect unison: *walk away. Walk away now, walk fast, don't look back, stay small.*

August climbed the last flight anyway.

There was a man slumped against August's door.
