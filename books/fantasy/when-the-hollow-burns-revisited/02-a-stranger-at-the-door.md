## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The trouble with a life built on being forgettable, August had learned long ago, was that it worked even when you wished it wouldn't.

They had spent the whole of the king's breakfast that morning tasting for him—quail eggs poached in cream, a plum compote, honey-cakes from a new baker whose name the steward couldn't be bothered to remember—and cataloging aftertastes the way other people cataloged grievances. Bitter almond ghosting under the compote: not poison, just overcooked pits, but worth noting. A metallic edge on the honey-cakes: copper pan, carelessness, nothing lethal. The quail eggs were clean as a hymn.

"Nothing," August reported to the king, and His Majesty nodded and ate, and did not look at August once, which was precisely how August liked it. Three years of this. Three years of being furniture that happened to have a tongue.

It was only later—walking home through streets gone gold with late afternoon, past the fishmongers shouting down their prices and children chalking hopscotch grids on the cobbles—that August let themselves think about the night market, and the widow Baker, and the little luck-charm sewn into oilcloth in their coat pocket like a guilty conscience.

*Small things*, they reminded themselves, as always. *Keep it small.*

A charm to keep bread from burning. That was all. Anyone caught doing magic in Amberdale went to the stake—that law had teeth now, real ones, and the witch-finders wore grey coats and never smiled—but a charm to keep bread from burning? That wasn't magic. That was superstition. Grandmothers knotted red thread against the evil eye; midwives whispered to fevered foreheads; nobody burned grandmothers. Not yet. The trick was staying on the grandmother side of the line forever, and August had gotten very good at tricks.

Their tenement stood on Candlerow Lane, four stories of leaning brick where the stairwell smelled permanently of onions and wet wool. August climbed it with the ease of habit, thinking about supper—a fried egg, maybe, if the egg hadn't turned—and pushed open the door to the landing.

And stopped.

There was someone sitting against their door.

Not leaning. *Collapsed*, in the way that people collapse when standing has become a rumor they can no longer afford to believe in. He was slumped with his back against August's doorframe, legs splayed across the landing boards, and his coat—the good kind, courier's cut, waxed canvas made for hard roads—was dark with blood from collar to hem. His face was the color of tallow candles.

August knew dead men. They'd seen enough of them in three years of palace service, enough to know the difference between dying and dead, and this man was firmly in the first category, though he seemed to be arguing with it.

"Don't," he said, when August took one step backward. "Don't go for the watch."

"I wasn't—" August began, which was true; they'd been considering the window.

"You were." The man laughed, and it cost him something, because the laugh turned into a cough and the cough came up red at the corner of his mouth. "Everyone goes for the watch. It's what makes you all so easy to find."

"I don't know who you think I am," August said carefully, "but I'm nobody. I taste food. For the king. It's very dull."

Something flickered in the man's eyes—not disbelief exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has been searching for a particular door in a city of ten thousand doors and has finally found it, and finds it smaller than he'd hoped.

"Amberdale," he said. "August Amberdale. Taster to the crown. Third floor, Candlerow Lane, the room with the crooked shutter." He recited it like a litany, like something he'd repeated to himself on a long road to keep from losing it. "You're not nobody. Nobody gets written down."

*Written down.* August's stomach performed a slow, cold roll. Written down where? By whom?

"Listen to me." The man's hand shot out and caught August's wrist—his grip shockingly strong for a body that looked half-spent—and pulled them down to a crouch beside him. Up close he smelled of horse sweat, road dust, and blood, and beneath all of it something stranger: a sweetness, like ripe pears left too long in the sun. Like Emberfall smelled, though August had only ever caught that scent twice, both times by accident, both times small.

"The Hollow is waking," the man said.

"I don't know what that means."

"No. But your blood does." He was fumbling at his chest with his free hand, working at the lacings of his coat, and August should have run—they knew that, some sensible animal part of them was screaming *run*—but there is a particular paralysis that comes over a person when a dying stranger says *your blood*, and August had never claimed to be brave, only careful, and careful people freeze beautifully.

He drew out an oilskin packet, wrapped tight, tied with waxed cord, stained dark along one edge. He pressed it into August's hands with both of his own, folding their fingers closed around it, and his hands were shaking and freezing and leaving red prints on August's knuckles.

"The Hollow is waking," he said again, slower, as if speaking to someone simple or someone terrified, and possibly those were the same thing. "The stone is cracking. The winter—it isn't weather. You've felt it. Midsummer frost. The river going quiet. The fae are dying, taster, and when they finish dying, the covenant dies with them, and then—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Then the city learns what it owes."

"I don't owe anything," August said. "I've never even *been* anywhere. I taste eggs."

The man smiled. It was a terrible smile, bloody and gentle at once.

"That's exactly why it's you."

Boots on the stairs.

August heard them before they saw them—heavy boots, iron-shod, climbing with the unhurried rhythm of men who owned every staircase they touched. Four floors below. Maybe three. The whole building seemed to flinch around the sound; somewhere above, a door closed softly, and a family became very interested in being silent.

The dying man heard them too. August watched him hear them—watched something settle in his face, not fear exactly, more like arithmetic. He had done this sum already, on the road, in the blood. He knew the answer.

"They followed me from the river gate," he said quietly. "I thought I'd lost them at the tanneries. I was wrong about the tanneries."

"Then we get you inside," August whispered. "I can hide you. There's a cellar hatch behind my stove—"

"And spill their blood on your floor next to mine?" The man shook his head, a small, deliberate motion. "No. Listen. The packet—don't open it where there's light. Don't open it near water. And don't—" His grip tightened on August's wrist, painful now. "Don't give it back. Whatever they promise. Whatever they take. They'll take something. They always do. Don't give it back."

"Give it to *whom*?"

But the man's eyes had drifted upward, past August's shoulder, toward the stairwell, where the boots were louder now. Two sets, maybe three. And voices—low, clipped, the conversational tone of men discussing dinner plans while they hunted.

"Witch-finders," August breathed. The word came out of them without permission, an old reflex, a child's word for the thing that had emptied whole streets of the quiet practitioners August had grown up among. Grey coats. Iron nails in their boot heels—iron, because the old stories said it bit the fae-touched, and the Chancellor's men believed every story that gave them an excuse.

"Third floor," one voice said below, flat and certain. "The taster's rooms."

*Taster's rooms.*

Ice went down August's spine and pooled there. Not a sweep. Not a general terrorizing of Candlerow Lane, which would have been bad enough but survivable, deniable. They were coming here. To this door. Which meant someone had told them. Someone had written August down after all.

The man's hand found August's face—cold fingers against their cheek, turning them back to look at him.

"Names," he said. "On the map. Oath-kin. Find them." His breath was failing between words now, each one bought at cost. "Hesk. The river-warden. Find—" A cough wracked him, silent this time, all spasm and no sound, which was worse somehow than any noise could have been. "My daughter. Wren. She'll come for the map. Tell her—" He stopped. Started again. Stopped again, as if the sentence were too heavy to lift.

"Tell her what?"

"Tell her I finished it." His eyes were bright and fixed on August's with an intensity that had nothing left to spend on anything else. "Tell her I didn't waste it."

The boots were on the second-floor landing now. August could hear the scrape of an iron heel on brick, could hear one of the men say something about blood on the stairs—*there's blood, look, fresh*—and the world narrowed to a horrible clarity: the oilskin packet heavy in August's hands, the dying man's grip on their wrist, the crooked shutter, the smell of onions rising from the floors below, all of it sharp as cut glass.

"Inside," the man said. "Go inside. Now."

"Not without—"

"*Now.*" And there was authority in it still, whatever was draining out of him; a courier's authority, the authority of a man whose whole life had been carrying things that must arrive. "You carry it. That's all. You carry. Let me do the rest."

August didn't want to know what *the rest* meant. Every instinct that had kept them alive for thirty years and invisible for three of them screamed that knowing would be the end of them.

They went inside anyway. They closed the door until only a crack remained, because they were a coward, they told themselves, and cowards need to see.

What happened next happened fast, and slow, both at once.

The boots reached the third-floor landing. Two witch-finders came around the stairwell—grey coats, faces like locked doors, the younger one carrying a lantern though it was barely dusk—and they stopped at the sight of the man slumped against August's door. For a moment nobody moved. The lantern flame leaned and steadied.

"Well," said the older one. "Courier."

The man looked up at them from the floor. And August, watching through the crack, saw him smile that terrible bloody smile again, saw him reach up—one last expenditure, spending the last coin in his purse—and pull something from his collar. Not a weapon. A pendant, small and dull, carved bone or river-stone, and he held it up so the lantern light caught it, and he said something too quiet for August to catch.

Both witch-finders recoiled. Just a step. Just a flinch. But August saw it, and understood in one lurching instant that the man had done it *on purpose*—had shown them whatever that pendant was, had made them afraid, had chosen his final act the way a cook chooses a final course.

The older witch-finder recovered first. What he did then was quick and professional and August shut their eyes at the last moment, so they heard it rather than saw it, and heard the man's body settle against the door—against *August's* door—with a weight that would live in their memory for years.

Silence. Then the younger one, unsteady: "Did he—was that—"

"Burn the pendant. Search the landing. Knock the doors." The older voice was flat as slate. "Someone on this floor is harboring."

August stood in their own kitchen, in the dark, holding an oilskin packet full of treason, listening to a dead man's blood seep under their door.

They should burn it. That was the sane thought, arriving on schedule: burn the packet, wash the blood, answer the knock with the bland face of a man who tastes eggs for a living and knows nothing of hollows or covenants or daughters named Wren. The witch-finders would search. They'd find nothing. August would remain what they had always been: nobody. Furniture. A tongue.

The knock came. Heavy. Iron-ringed.

August looked down at the packet in their hands. In the thin light leaking under the door, the oilskin seemed almost to breathe—or perhaps that was the blood spreading beneath it, reaching the toes of August's shoes.

And then two things happened, nearly together, and either alone would have changed everything.

The knock came again, harder, and the older voice said: "Taster. Open the door. We know you're home."

And the packet, warm now—impossibly, body-warm, warm as a living thing—in August's hands, shifted. Along its sealed seam, in ink that had not been visible a heartbeat before, letters were surfacing. Rising through the oilskin like fish through dark water. Old letters, covenant-script, the kind found on boundary stones and in books no shop would sell.

August couldn't read them yet. But they could feel them, the way a tongue feels a poison it hasn't named yet—knows it's there, knows it will matter, knows the swallowing comes next whether you choose it or not.

The dying man had called it *your blood*. August had thought he was raving.

The door shuddered under a third blow.

August slid the packet inside their shirt, against their skin, where it lay hot as a coal and beating—actually beating, faintly, like a second heart learning its rhythm beside their own—and went to answer the door wearing the blandest face they had ever worn, while underneath it, in letters of old ink and older grief, their invisibility quietly ended.

* * *

Later—much later, when the witch-finders had torn through the rooms and found nothing but a tidy kitchen and a man's collection of harmless spice jars, when the body had been dragged away and the blood scrubbed from the landing by August's own hands because no one else would, when Candlerow Lane had gone dark and pretending to sleep—August sat at their table by one shielded candle and unwrapped the packet.

Inside was a map. Folded, worn soft as cloth at the creases, marked with a route stitched in thread and annotated in a cramped, patient hand. And across its surface, surfacing slowly as the candlelight touched it, the covenant-script woke—line after line of it, glowing faintly gold, rearranging itself, reading *him*. Reading August. Choosing, apparently, to be legible.

At the top, in the same cramped hand, someone had written a single line in plain trade-tongue:

*If you can read this, the map has already decided about you. I'm sorry. So was I.*

August sat very still for a long time, listening to the second heartbeat against their ribs, thinking of a daughter named Wren who was going to come looking for this map, and wondering—with a dread that felt horribly like excitement—what she would do when she found out who had it.

Outside, far off toward the hills, something that was not thunder rolled through the frozen air.

The Hollow was waking. And it knew August's name.
