## 3. What the Map Refuses to Say

The dead man's blood had dried brown by the time August got the door barred, and it flaked off the oilskin in little rust-colored scales when they unrolled it across the kitchen table.

August had buried the stranger's body before dawn—not buried, exactly. There was no time to dig, and no shovel in a tenement the size of a breadbox. Instead they'd wrapped him in his own ruined cloak, carried him down three flights with legs that shook like a foal's, and laid him in the coal cellar of the empty flat below, behind a wall of stacked briquettes that no one would move until winter proper came and by then, August prayed, this would all be someone else's problem.

Someone else's problem. That was the family prayer, really. The Amberdales had been saying it for three generations, quietly, over small charms and smaller kindnesses, and it had kept every one of them off the pyre so far.

August washed their hands. Washed them again. The stranger's blood was under their nails no matter how they scrubbed, and underneath the blood there was something else—a smell, faint as a memory: cold stone and honey and something green growing where nothing should grow.

*The Hollow is waking,* he'd said. And then he'd held on to August's wrist hard enough to leave bruises, as if August were a railing on a ship going down.

Now the map lay on the table, and August sat across from it with a candle, a pot of barley tea gone cold, and the particular dread of a person who has found something in the road that is very obviously not theirs.

It looked, at first glance, like any courier's route-map. August had seen hundreds—the king's kitchens ran on them, grease-stained charts of roads and river-fords and which toll-keepers could be bribed with sausage. This one had the same roads. The same river. But drawn along the spine of the hills north of the city, where the map should have shown only sheep pasture and an old quarry, there was a smudge of ink that wasn't ink at all.

August tilted the candle closer.

The smudge moved.

Not much. Not the way a rat moves, or a flame in a draft. It shifted the way a word shifts when you look away from it and back—there and not-there, a suggestion of lines beneath lines, like writing pressed through from the other side of the paper.

"Oh, no," August said aloud, to the empty room, because the room deserved to know. "No, no, *no.*"

This was covenant-script. August knew it the way a kitchen boy knows heraldry—from a distance, from warnings. It was the script of the old binding, the one the priests called superstition and the Chancellor called treason. Learning it was a fineable offense. Writing it was a hanging offense. Carrying it, if you were unlucky, was a burning one.

Three years. Three years of tasting the king's breakfast and keeping their charms small enough to fit inside a thimble—a knot tied left-handed against nightmares, a salt line under the door that might just be housekeeping, a luck-charm sold to the baker's widow that was, technically speaking, mostly just a nice knot and some good intentions. Small magics. Deniable magics. The kind that a witch-finder could squint at and call folk custom, because the alternative was paperwork.

And now there was a dead courier in the coal cellar and a piece of outlawed scripture on the table, moving under its own power.

The sensible thing—the *Amberdale* thing—was to burn it.

August fetched the candle anyway. Held it over the corner of the oilskin. Watched the flame waver and steady.

And the smudge shifted again, and this time August saw—or thought they saw—a shape in it. A curve like a doorway. A vertical line like a standing stone. And beneath both, faint as breath on glass, a single word trying very hard to be read.

August set the candle down with hands that were not entirely steady.

"All right," they told the map. "You have my attention. That's not the same as having my agreement."

They did what any sensible person raised on three generations of deniable magic would do. They made tea. Fresh tea, because decisions made on cold barley tea were how families ended up in coal cellars. And while the water heated they turned the map over, checked the seams, held it up to the light, and finally—out of desperation more than inspiration—pricked their thumb on the paring knife they'd used to slice cheese, and touched the bead of red to the smudge.

The map drank it.

There was no other word for it. The drop sank into the oilskin like rain into parched earth, and the smudge *bloomed*—lines unfurling across the hide in slow silver-green strokes, a whole hidden country waking up between the roads. Hills August had walked all their life suddenly had doors in them. The old quarry had a staircase. And running down from the hills, through the city itself, through the palace district, a thread of light that ended—

—at the palace. At the kitchens. At, unless August badly missed their guess, the taster's own corridor.

The word beneath the doorway resolved at last, written in a hand like frost forming:

**OATH-BLOOD ONLY.**

"Well," said August, after a long moment. "That's flattering and terrifying in roughly equal measure."

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing they had never told anyone, the secret folded smaller than any charm: August had always been able to taste things other people couldn't. Not poison—well, yes, poison, that was the trade—but *more*. The sadness in meat from an animal that had suffered. The loneliness in water from a well nobody visited. Once, terribly, the grief in a funeral cake, so thick August had excused themselves and wept in the scullery for an hour over someone else's grandmother.

They'd assumed everyone tasted the world a little. That was the polite theory, the deniable theory, the theory that let you keep your head down.

But a map that opened only to oath-blood—and had opened to *them*, instantly, greedily, like a lock recognizing its key—that was not a theory you could keep polite for long.

Oath-blood. Blood of the people who had sworn the old covenant. Blood of the people who had bound human city to fae realm, back when such things were done openly, back before some betrayal or other (the histories disagreed; the priests disagreed; the Chancellor, notably, never discussed it at all) had cracked the whole arrangement down its spine.

August looked at their thumb. A tiny bead of red still welled there, ordinary, unremarkable, the thumb of a person whose greatest official duty was eating eggs in front of a nervous king.

"You," August informed it, "are going to be the death of me."

But even as they said it, they felt it—the thing underneath the dread, warm and unwelcome as sunlight through a sickroom window. Inheritance. For twenty-nine years August had been a small person living a small life built out of small kindnesses, each one deniable, each one alone. And now here was proof, in silver-green ink, that the smallness had been a choice someone else's generation made for them. That somewhere up the family tree there had been people who stood in doorways instead of hiding beside them.

The thrill of it was obscene. August felt it rising anyway, like hunger.

They were still staring at the thread of light ending at the palace kitchens when the candle guttered—no draft, no reason—and a smell crossed the room that didn't belong: woodsmoke and wet feathers and something animal and furious, like a fox that has been wronged personally.

August looked up at the window.

Nothing. Rooftops. Chimney pots. The grey beginning of another unnatural morning, frost furring the gutters in high summer.

But on the rooftop opposite, half-hidden behind a chimney stack, something moved. A shape. A watcher. Gone before August could fix their eyes on it.

"Right," said August to the empty street below, drawing the shutter closed with great deliberation. "I'm being haunted. Wonderful. Add it to the list."

The list, currently: dead courier. Outlawed map. Mysterious inheritance. Haunting. And at the bottom, underlined twice, *tell absolutely no one.*

Wren crouched behind the chimney stack and hated the little light in the tenement window with her whole body.

Her father's map. His *map*. She knew the way it was rolled—end-cap first, always, because he said a courier who rolled a map wrong deserved to lose it—and she knew the oilskin's particular shine, worn soft at the corners from nine years of his hands. She'd watched him wrap it in oilcloth and sew it into his coat lining two days before he rode north. She'd kissed his cheek and told him to stop chasing faerie stories and come home for her birthday.

He hadn't come home for her birthday. He'd come home in a box, officially of "exposure," unofficially of whatever actually happened to couriers who asked too many questions about the hills.

And now his map was sitting in a candlelit window in the Tanners' Row tenements, unrolled on the table of some stranger who was *reading* it. Reading it. Wren had tried to read it for a year. She'd held it to firelight, moonlight, lamplight, once memorably lightning. The ink stayed ink. Her father had laughed when she complained—"It'll open when it opens, little wren"—and now it had opened for a stranger in a shabby kitchen who pricked their thumb like it was nothing, like it cost nothing, like they hadn't bled for it at all.

Wren's knife was in her hand. She didn't remember drawing it. It was a good knife, her father's second-best, and she had spent the last day and night telling herself a simple story: go down, knock on the door, take back what was hers, ask questions after.

The story had a flaw, which was the witch-finders.

They'd come through Tanner's Row at dusk, four of them in their ash-grey coats, knocking on doors with questions about "a man of middle years seen traveling north." Wren had flattened herself into a rooftop gutter and listened while the neighbor woman lied beautifully about seeing nothing, and the neighbor woman's lie had bought the block an hour of peace, and Wren had understood with cold clarity that her father's map was worth men in ash-grey coats. Worth killing for. Worth dying for—her father had demonstrated that part already.

Which meant she couldn't just kick the stranger's door in. Kicking the door in would bring the coats. And the coats would take the map, and take the stranger, and probably take Wren, and none of those outcomes returned her father to her.

So she watched instead. She was good at watching; her father had trained her to it without meaning to. And what she saw, in glimpses through the shutter-crack, unsettled her worse than a thief would have.

The stranger wasn't studying the map like a thief studies loot. They studied it like a cook reads a recipe—leaning close, muttering, touching it gently, apologizing to it at one point, *apologizing*, which no thief alive has ever done. And when the map bloomed silver-green, the stranger flinched like it had bitten them, then went still in a way Wren recognized because she'd seen it in mirrors since the funeral: the stillness of a person whose life has just split into before and after.

*Oath-blood,* the stranger's lips shaped, and Wren's grip tightened on the knife until the handle bit her palm.

Her father had said those words too. Late at night, deep in his cups, tracing the blank smudge on the map with one finger: *It wants oath-blood, little wren, and I've traveled with mine thirty years and it won't so much as blink at me. Someone in our line broke something. Or maybe just diluted it.* He'd said it like a joke. He hadn't sounded like a joke.

So either this stranger had stolen the map *and* somehow been born with the key to it—which was a coincidence Wren didn't believe in—or the map had chosen them, the way her father had always half-hoped, half-feared it would choose somebody, someday, and finish what it was drawn to finish.

Either way, they had her father's map. Either way, they owed her answers.

Wren settled in behind the chimney stack, arranged her father's spare cloak around her shoulders, and began, methodically, to wait. She'd waited out sieges of weather and one memorable siege of geese outside the grain market. She could wait out one thumb-pricking stranger.

Below, the shutter closed. The light stayed.

Above, the frost crept down the chimney stacks like fingers, and somewhere far to the north, past the hills, past the quarry, past the edge of every map her father had ever trusted—something old turned over in its sleep, and dreamed of a taste it remembered.

*

August did not sleep. Sleep required believing the world was a certain size, and August's world had grown several new countries overnight.

Instead they sat with the map until the candle drowned in its own wax, cataloging. It was what they did instead of panicking; the kitchens had taught them that. When the king's breakfast arrives and the third dish smells wrong, you do not panic. You name what you smell. Bitter almond, perhaps, or oleander under the honey. Naming is the first step to surviving.

So: name it. One, a dead man in the coal cellar who had known August's name without being told—he'd said *Amberdale*, gasped it, as if reading it off a list he'd been carrying for miles. Two, a map that drank blood and showed doors in hills that had no doors. Three, a watcher on the roof who smelled of woodsmoke and grievance. Four, frost in midsummer. Five, five, five—

Five was the hardest one, so August saved it for last and made the tea do the heavy lifting.

Five was that the thread of light on the map ran *from* the hills *to* August's own corridor, and threads like that don't run toward random strangers. Threads like that run toward anchors. Toward keystones. Toward the person the whole arrangement is balanced on, whether that person has agreed to anything or not.

"No," August told the map firmly. "Whatever you're thinking, I decline. I am a food-taster. My ambitions include a slightly better mattress and possibly a cat."

The silver-green lines glowed gently, and did not, in any way that mattered, listen.

August blew out the candle, rolled the map—end-cap first, some instinct insisting on the courtesy—and sat in the dark listening to the frost tick against the shutters like a patient guest.

Somewhere out there was a witch-finder who wanted this map burned and its bearer with it. Somewhere out there was a watcher on a roof who wanted it back. And somewhere north of the city, in a hollow between hills, something was waking up and asking, politely but persistently, for oath-blood.

August looked at their pricked thumb in the dark.

The first name on the map, glowing faintly now beneath the roads like a fish under ice, read: **HESK.**

"Hesk," August repeated. It tasted, when they said it aloud, of feverfew and old guilt and a garden long untended. "Of course it does."

They got their coat.
