## 4. Wren

Wren had been watching the taster's tenement for three days, and in that time she had learned four things.

First: August Amberdale kept terrible hours for someone who tasted poison for a living. Up before dawn to reach the palace kitchens, home well after dark, and always — *always* — pausing at the corner of Candle Lane to buy roasted chestnuts from Old Marta as if a man who might die by breakfast deserved a treat.

Second: he was kind. This irritated her more than anything. He'd stopped on the second day to help a cart-driver whose wheel had jammed, and he'd done it without looking around first to see who might be watching. Nobody kind survived long in this city. Kindness was a door left unlocked.

Third: there was magic in that house. She'd seen the small glow behind his shuttered window past midnight, green-gold and soft as foxfire, the same color her father's charms used to make before he'd taught himself to smother them. Her father had died with that light in his hands. Or so the witch-finders' report claimed. The report also claimed he'd resisted arrest, which was a lie, because her father couldn't resist a stray cat begging at the window, let alone six men with iron pikes.

Fourth: the taster had her father's map.

She'd seen it through the gap in his shutters on the third night, spread across his table, candlelight pooling on oilskin she would have known anywhere — the burn mark in the lower corner shaped like a moth, from the night she'd knocked a lamp onto it when she was nine. He'd smoothed it flat with both hands like it belonged to him. Like it hadn't cost her everything.

So on the fourth morning, when August Amberdale came down his crooked stair with chestnut-warmth still on his breath and turned toward the palace, Wren stepped out of the doorway where she'd waited since before the gulls woke up, and put a knife against his ribs.

"Don't," she said. "Don't shout, don't run, don't do anything clever. You're a food-taster. I've watched you for three days. You are not clever."

August looked down at the knife, then up at her, and did the single most infuriating thing available to him.

"You have your father's eyes," he said quietly. "He described them. Grey, he said, 'like the river before it decides.'"

Wren's grip tightened. "You knew him."

"I knew a courier who brought me honey-cakes every solstice and complained about my handwriting." Something moved behind the taster's face — grief, maybe, or guilt, and both of them fit badly, like borrowed coats. "I'm sorry. About the stairwell. About—"

"You took his map."

"He gave it to me."

"Liar."

"Search me, then," August said, spreading his arms carefully away from the blade. "Go on. If I'm carrying anything of his besides a debt I can't pay, take it."

She searched him. It was mortifyingly quick — a pat-down performed with one hand while the other kept the knife honest — and it turned up nothing but a lint-covered peppermint and a copper token for the bathhouse. Which meant the map was upstairs. Which meant she'd have to go upstairs. Which meant, some treacherous part of her brain observed, that she was now committed to a whole conversation.

"The roof," she said. "There's a wash-yard behind the tannery nobody uses because it smells like regret. Walk. Slowly. And if you signal anyone—"

"I won't."

"Everyone says that."

"Yes," August agreed mildly, "and yet here you are, believing me enough to walk behind me instead of opening my throat. That's trust, Miss Courier. Ugly little thing, isn't it?"

Wren decided right then that she hated him.

* * *

The wash-yard smelled exactly as advertised. Between two leaning walls, rainwater had pooled in the hollows of old flagstones, and a single straggling pear tree grew out of a crack in the masonry, stubborn as a debt collector. Wren backed August against the wall beneath it and put the knife away only far enough to talk.

"Start at the stairwell," she said. "Everything. Leave nothing out, or I'll know, because I'll ask twice."

So he told her. The knocking. The blood. A stranger folding down against his door like wet paper, pressing oilskin into his hands with the last strength he had. Boots on the stairs. *The Hollow is waking,* her father had said — and then he hadn't said anything ever again, because he'd died holding a stranger's wrist in a tenement hallway, alone except for a terrified food-taster who couldn't even give him a clean death.

"He wasn't afraid," August said. "That's what I keep coming back to. Men are usually afraid at the end. He was angry. And relieved, almost — like a man handing off a load he'd carried too many miles." His voice caught on something. "He said, *tell my daughter the map chose right.*"

Wren turned away. The pear tree offered her a single hard green fruit at eye level; she stared at it until her eyes stopped stinging.

"That's not how maps work," she said finally. "Maps don't choose."

"This one does. I've read it. Well — I've tried to read it." He laughed, short and unhappy. "Half the script rearranges itself when I look too long. It's written for oath-blood, whatever that is. Your father bled on it once, I think — there's a stain near the border that makes whole passages legible. I haven't figured out why mine works."

"Yours works?"

"A line here and there. Enough to be haunted by it."

She made herself turn back and look at him properly — the flour-dusted cuffs, the ink stains, the face of a man built entirely out of harmless things. Her father had trusted this stranger with the family's whole ruin. Either Aldous Vane had been a fool at the end, or he'd seen something under all that harmlessness.

"Here's what happens now," Wren said. "We go up. You give me the map. Then you go back to tasting the king's soup and forgetting my father's name, and I finish what he started."

"And what did he start?"

"The Hollow." The word came out harder than she meant it to. "Three years he hunted it. Three years of couriers' routes chosen to pass near every standing stone in the province, every barrow, every crack in the world where the old things used to live. Do you know why?"

August shook his head.

"Because Emberfall is dying." Saying it aloud felt like pulling a splinter. "My mother was fae. Did he mention that? Half of him, half of me. And the winter that's sitting on our city like a lid — it's not weather. It's *bleed-through*. The veil is thinning where the covenant fails, and the cold comes through it, and on the other side—" Her voice snagged. She pushed through. "On the other side, children are guttering out. Little lights, my mother called us. Emberfall's children don't freeze to death. They just get dimmer and dimmer until they're not there anymore. Two went out last spring. My cousin's boy. A girl named Sorrel who used to braid reeds into my hair when I visited."

The yard was quiet except for the drip of rainwater and, distantly, the tannery doing its regrettable business.

"My father found the first name on the map eight months ago," she went on, when she could. "An old woman, a physician, struck off the rolls. He wrote to me — coded, courier-fashion — saying he thought he could see the shape of it now. Where the stone was. What it needed." She jabbed a finger at August's chest. "And then he walked into your building with witch-finders behind him, and now the shape of it is in the hands of a man who buys chestnuts."

"They're excellent chestnuts," August said.

"Do not make jokes at me."

"I'm sorry. It's — when I'm frightened, I cook. When I can't cook, I joke. It's the same instinct. Something has to be made bearable." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Keep it. The map, I mean — no. Listen. Come up and *keep* it. But hear me first."

"Make it fast."

"Three nights I've sat with that map, and it's shown me exactly enough to terrify me. Names. Oath-kin, scattered across the city like seeds after a harvest. A covenant-stone split down its heart, binding this city to Emberfall, and failing. And a deadline I can't read but can feel, the way you feel a storm coming in your teeth." He met her eyes, and for the first time the harmlessness fell away and she saw the man underneath — tired, resolute, more frightened than he wanted her to know. "Your father didn't give me the map because I'm special. He gave it to me because I was *there*, and dying men don't shop for options. But the map opened for me anyway. So either it knows something about my blood that I don't, or your father knew something about me that I don't. Either way — I can't unopen it. And neither, I think, can you."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. The pear tree dropped a leaf between them, early and yellow, like the season itself had given up waiting.

"Fine," she said. "Upstairs. And if you've so much as creased it—"

"I iron it," August said. "Gently. With a cool iron and a cloth over it, the way you'd press an old letter. He would have approved."

"That is the strangest sentence anyone has ever said to me, and I once interviewed a man who claimed to be his own twin."

* * *

They were halfway up the stair — Wren first, knife palmed, August behind her narrating which steps creaked — when the shouting started.

It came from the mouth of Candle Lane, that particular official barking that the city knew like it knew church bells: **WITCH-FINDERS. CLEAR THE STREET.** Then boots, many boots, and the ugly music of a door being taken off its hinges two houses down.

Wren froze on the landing. Below, through the stairwell's slatted window, she watched them pour into the street — six, eight, a dozen, iron-grey cloaks, pikes, and a brazier on wheels with a cage above it that she refused to look at directly. They kicked in the door of the chandler's shop. An old woman's scream cut off mid-syllable.

"They're not here for you," August breathed. He had gone white as his flour. "Look — they're going house to house. It's a sweep. Someone reported charm-work on this block, and it wasn't me, I swear on my own tongue—"

"It was me," Wren said numbly. "Three days watching your window. Someone watched me watching. Oh, Da. Oh, you clever, stupid man — even dead you're leading me into traps."

A fist hammered the door of the tenement below them. A voice like gravel: *"Open in the Chancellor's name!"*

August grabbed her sleeve. Not toward the roof — she'd assumed roofs, roofs were her country — but down, past their own floor, to a narrow door she'd taken for a cupboard. Inside: not a cupboard. A passage. A servant's corridor, old as the building, running behind the walls like a vein.

"There's a network of these behind the whole row," he whispered. "Palace kitchens connect to half the city, if you know where the doors are. Everyone watches faces. Nobody watches service corridors." He glanced back at the pounding, then at her, and something almost like humor flickered through the fear. "Invisibility has its uses. Come on."

They went. Behind them, the tenement door burst open, and witch-finder boots thundered up a stairwell that was already empty, hunting a taster who had spent three years learning exactly which cracks in the world a body could slip through.

* * *

The corridor let out through a laundry-chute room into a cooper's yard behind the chandler's row, smelling of wood shavings and, mercifully, not of tannery. From there, a drain-gap, a wall, and a stretch of rooftops Wren navigated on instinct while August navigated on prayer and surprising agility. They came down at last in the bone-yard behind Saint Verity's almshouse, among femurs and femurs of soup-stock, breathing like bellows.

Wren put her back against the wall and laughed — one sharp, involuntary bark that surprised them both.

"You have escape routes," she said. "You, the chestnut man."

"I have *habits*," August corrected. "Habits and routes are different. Routes imply planning. Habits imply that a person has spent three years lying awake wondering how they'd get out of their own house if it ever became necessary." He slid down the wall until he was sitting among the bones, and pulled the oilskin map from inside his shirt, where it had apparently lived since the stairwell, warm from his skin. He held it out to her, flat across both palms. "Yours. It should never have been mine. Take it home, take it to Emberfall, do whatever your father intended."

Wren looked at the map. The moth-shaped scorch. The stain of her father's blood. Three years of his life folded into oilskin and handed, at the end, to a stranger.

And under that — the memory she'd been holding at arm's length since the wash-yard: *tell my daughter the map chose right.*

If it had chosen right, then it had chosen August too. Her father had never once, in her whole life, been wrong about people. Infuriating people, sometimes. People who bought chestnuts. But never wrong.

"If I take it and walk away," she said slowly, "you starve to death on riddles. And I spend the rest of my life wondering if I buried my father's work along with him." She dropped to a crouch in front of him and held out her free hand. "So here's how it goes, Taster. We read it together. We find your oath-kin. We reach the Hollow before whatever deadline is chewing through that script gets there first. And when it's done — when Emberfall's children stop going out like lamps — *then* we settle what you owe my father's ghost. Understood?"

August took her hand and shook it, solemn as a treaty.

"Understood," he said. Then, because he was who he was: "For the record, this is the worst hiring interview I've ever conducted. Knife to the ribs. No references."

"You'll notice you got the job anyway."

The map lay open between them on the bone-yard's cold stones, and as the grey noon light touched it, the script stirred — faint, reluctant, like a sleeper turning over — and a new line surfaced along the western margin, ink-dark and patient:

*First, seek the physician the crown discarded. Mother Hesk keeps her fire in the house of the forgotten.*

Wren read it twice. Beside her, August had gone very still.

"I know that hospice," he said quietly. "Leper ward, by the fen gate. I deliver broth there on Thursdays." He looked up, and for the first time since the stairwell, the fear in his face had a direction. "Thursday is tomorrow."

"Then tomorrow," said Wren, folding her father's map closed with more gentleness than she'd planned, "we knock on the door of the forgotten, and we pray the forgotten are feeling charitable."
