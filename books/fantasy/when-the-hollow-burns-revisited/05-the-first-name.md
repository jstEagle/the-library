## 5. The First Name

The map had spent the night doing nothing at all.

I knew this because I had watched it do nothing for most of that night, sitting cross-legged on my bed with the oilskin spread across my knees and a candle guttering beside me, waiting for it to confess. Maps, in my experience, were honest things. They told you where the wells were, where the walls were thin, where a person might slip out of a city when slipping out became necessary. This one lay there like a dead fish, all coastlines and hill-shading, and not one word of covenant-script on it anywhere.

Either Wren's father — the courier, the dying man whose grip I could still feel on my wrist, cold as a key — had carried an ordinary map to his death for no reason at all, or the script came and went on its own schedule.

Or, said the small voice in the back of my head, the voice that sounded uncomfortably like Mother Hesk from twelve years ago, *it shows itself to whom it chooses, child, same as everything worth having.*

By morning I had made three decisions, which by my standards was a productive week.

First: I would not burn the map. My hands had actually hovered over the stove, oilskin folded small enough to swallow between the coals, and then I had thought about the stranger's eyes, and about the frost creeping up the inside of my window in July, and I had put the kettle on instead, because a coward with tea is still a coward but at least she's warm.

Second: I would go to the leper hospice at Saint Verre's, because if half of what the map's first name implied was true, I needed someone who understood oaths, and the only oath-keeper I'd ever known who wasn't burned or banished or both was a disgraced royal physician currently pretending to be too contagious to bother with.

Third: I would stop reading the map on an empty stomach, because hunger makes everything look like doom.

* * *

Saint Verre's sat on the east bank of the Alder, where the river went sluggish and grey and the city stopped pretending to care about anything downstream of the tanneries. It was a long, low building of yellow brick with a chapel at one end and a vegetable garden going gloriously to seed at the other, and it had the particular silence of places where people have agreed not to complain.

The gate was a rope bell and a sign that read, in a hand that had clearly been written by someone with opinions: **IF YOU HAVE COME TO PRAY AT US, THE CHAPEL IS THAT WAY. IF YOU HAVE COME TO PITY US, KINDLY PRAY INSTEAD.**

A woman answered the bell before I'd finished ringing it, which meant someone had been watching me pick my way down the lane for some time. She was sixty-something, built like a doorstop, with flour-white hair cropped close and forearms that suggested a life of lifting patients who did not wish to be lifted.

"You're not sick," she said.

"Good morning to you too."

"Sick people walk differently. They apologize with their shoulders." She looked me over the way a butcher looks at a carcass, and her eyes snagged — just for a heartbeat — on my hands. On the faint silver scarring across the fingertips, old and fine as spider silk. Taster's hands. Physician's eyes would know them. "And you're not from the Chancellor's office, because they send handsome people."

"I need to speak with Mother Hesk."

Something closed over her face like a shutter. "No Mother Hesk here. There's a Sister Anselm who does the poultices and mispronounces Latin, if that would serve."

"It might," I said, "if Sister Anselm once wrote a monograph on the pharmacology of fae-touched wounds that the College of Physicians burned in her absence, along with, I'm told, her chair."

The woman studied me a long moment. Behind her, somewhere deep in the hospice, somebody was singing — badly, cheerfully, in a language I didn't know and yet somehow recognized, the way you recognize the shape of a word seen through fog.

"You have ten minutes," she said, "and if you use the word *witch*, our arrangement ends and so, briefly and painfully, does your visit."

* * *

Sister Anselm — Hesk, Hesk, it was Hesk, and I would keep straight faces until the door was barred — led me through a ward where a dozen patients lay in clean beds, their bandages white as the sheets, their faces turned toward windows that faced the garden rather than the tanneries. She paused at each one. Not to fuss. To *look*. A touch here, a name there, a joke for the old man by the wall who cackled like she'd paid him for it.

I had forgotten what that looked like. Medicine, done by someone who liked the patient more than the theory.

Her cell was the size of a generous cupboard: a bed, a desk drowning in papers, a shelf of jars labeled in a personal shorthand that probably violated several laws of pharmacy and at least two of grammar. She shut the door, sat, and pointed me at the only stool.

"Now," she said. "You know my name. You carry yourself like someone walking on a frozen pond. And you've the hands of a palace taster, though no palace taster has come here since the purge, and none of them ever came alone." She folded her arms. "So. Whose errand are you?"

I put the map on the desk between us.

She didn't reach for it. That was the first thing. Everyone else who had seen the map — which is to say, me and a dead man — had wanted to grab it. Hesk looked at it the way you look at a doctor's letter you already suspect contains your own name.

"Where did you get this?"

"A courier brought it to my door last night. He was dying. Witch-finder bolt, I think — I've seen the aftertaste of those on men who survived them, and he had it worse than any survivor." I kept my voice level. Practice helps. "He said the Hollow was waking. He put this in my hands and he held onto my wrist until he was gone, and I would very much like to tell myself I had no choice about taking it, but I took it."

"Yes," Hesk said quietly. "They always hold on. The ones who carry it. It's less an instruction than an inheritance." Then her gaze flicked up, sharp as a lancet. "And the script? What does the script say to you?"

"There isn't any script. It's just a map. Rivers, hills, the old pilgrim road up past the—"

"Read the spine of it. The ridge line, west to east. Aloud."

I bent over the oilskin, feeling foolish, tracing the hills with one scarred fingertip. "Greyfell. Coldharbour Tor. The Shepherd's Rest. And — "

And there it was. Under my finger, rising off the parchment like breath on cold glass: a line of letters in no alphabet I knew and every alphabet I knew, curling gold-green, smelling faintly of honey and iron. I read it before I could be afraid of it.

"*Hespera Maren Kell, called Hesk, oath-sworn of the stone.*" My voice came out strange. "Then there's another name under it. Smaller. Newer." I stopped.

"Don't stop now, girl, you're the first entertainment I've had since spring."

"It says — " I squinted. The letters shifted like fish. "*August Amberdale, taster, oath-blood unsworn.*"

The room got very quiet. Somewhere below, the bad singer had reached a note that could strip paint.

"Well," said Mother Hesk, into that quiet, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard from anyone in three years of careful living: wonder. "It's never added a name before. In forty years of carrying the wretched thing's memory around in my head, it has never once *added*."

* * *

She made tea while I reassembled my understanding of the world. It was terrible tea, stewed to the color of tannin and regret, and she gave me the cup with the exact authority of a woman administering medicine, which I suppose she was.

"The covenant," she said, settling onto her bed because there was nowhere else to sit, and launching in with the briskness of a woman who had lectured apprentices and missed it terribly. "Two hundred years ago, give or take a plague, human and fae swore a shared oath at a stone in the hills above this city. Not a treaty — treaties are paper, and paper burns, and both parties knew a great deal about burning. An oath. Spoken into the stone itself, witnessed by the earth. The stone holds the oath the way a keystone holds an arch. Humans got the lowlands and the law; the fae got Emberfall and the deep roads; both got open passage, open trade, and — this is the part everyone forgets — *open obligation*. Their fortunes tied. Their winters shared."

"And now?"

"Now the stone is cracked. Has been cracking for years — slowly at first, like ice before the thaw, except there's been no thaw, have you noticed?" She gestured at my coat, still damp at the cuffs from river-fog that should have been summer-warm. "The winter isn't weather, girl. It's a symptom. When the covenant fails, Emberfall doesn't simply die. It dies *outward*. Its dying leaks into ours. First the frost, then the failing crops, then the sickness in the king — yes, I've heard, even backwater leper houses hear things — and then, eventually, both realms go dark together, hand in hand, like the sentimental old fools they are."

I wrapped both hands around the awful tea. "The courier said the Hollow was waking."

"Waking, aching, breaking — the couriers all used different verbs. There were nine of us sworn to the stone once. Physicians, wardens, a stonemason, a woman who claimed to be a midwife and was almost certainly a spy for somebody. Our job was to tend the covenant. Small maintenance. Oath-keeping, literally." Her mouth twisted. "When Veyre rose, the first thing his witch-finders did wasn't burn the hedge-charmers and luck-sellers. Those were theater, kindling for the crowd. No — they hunted the *oath-sworn*. Quietly. One by one. Because Veyre understands what the crowd doesn't: that magic isn't the wound. Magic is the stitch. Kill the stitches and the whole seam opens."

"Why?" I asked, though a part of me had already guessed, and the guess was cold. "Why would he want the covenant broken?"

Hesk looked at me over her cup for a long moment, and I watched her decide how much truth I could carry.

"That," she said, "is a question I'll answer when we're further from open windows. Ask me instead the question you actually came with."

"How do we mend it?"

"*We*," she repeated, savoring the word. "Good. Honest. Terrified, by the smell of you, but honest." She set down her cup. "A cracked covenant-stone can be reforged — the rite exists, I know it, I've known it for forty years like a stone in my shoe. But it requires oath-blood, freely given, at the stone, at the right moment. The solstice eclipse, which is — " she did arithmetic on her fingers, and I watched hope acquire a deadline right in front of me, like watching bread go stale in fast motion — "nineteen days away. And it requires the oath-kin. All of them. Every name on that map. The covenant won't take a mending from strangers. It takes it from family, and family, Miss Amberdale, means everyone named, present and willing."

"Nineteen days."

"Nineteen days, and a witch-hunt between us and the hills, and a list of names most of which are, I grant you, probably dead." She said it without flinching, the way you say a prognosis. I remembered suddenly, sharply, why the palace physicians had hated her: she was never wrong, and she never softened being right. "But the map has added *you*, which means the map thinks at least some of us are alive. And the map, in my considerable experience, is a better judge than I am."

* * *

We talked for two hours, though I'd been granted ten minutes, which tells you something about Mother Hesk's relationship with rules. She drew the rite out of memory in fragments, quizzing me on tastes and poisons with sudden ferocity — "Bittercress and griefroot together, what do you taste? *Think*" — and each time I answered correctly she grew, if anything, more troubled.

"You taste like a born anchor," she muttered, the third time. "Years of dosing yourself, building tolerance, taking poison in small measures so others wouldn't have to. That's not immunity, child, that's *practice*. That's the shape of the thing itself." Then she caught my expression and changed the subject with a physician's ruthlessness.

It was full dark when she walked me to the gate. At the rope-bell she stopped me with a hand on my arm — a strong hand, steady, and I realized she was frightened and had been the entire time, and had simply declined to let it interfere.

"One more thing, and you'll listen, because it matters more than names and rites and deadlines." Her voice dropped. "Veyre's finders aren't searching randomly anymore. Two of the old oath-kin were taken this month. Taken quietly, no fires, no spectacle — which means they weren't being made an example of. They were being *questioned*. He knows about the map, August. He knows bearers are gathering. Whatever else you do, however clever you are, understand that you are already being hunted, and the only advantage you have left is that he doesn't yet know *where* we all are."

"And when he finds out?"

"Then we had best be at the Hollow before him." She squeezed my arm once, hard, and let go. "Come back tomorrow nightfall. Bring the map, bring your appetite for terrible tea, and bring—" she hesitated, and for a moment the brisk physician looked simply old, and tired, and hopeful in a way that hurt worse than fear, "—bring whoever else believes you. Oath-work is not done alone. That's rather the entire point."

I was three steps down the dark lane when the bell behind me rang again — fast, urgent, wrong.

I turned. Downstream, along the river road, lanterns were coming. Not two, not four. A dozen at least, moving in formation, unhurried, the way men move when they are certain.

Witch-finders. Walking toward Saint Verre's, toward a hospice full of people who couldn't run, toward a disgraced physician whose name was written on the map folded against my heart — written, I now understood, in a hand that could add new names at will.

Names that could also, presumably, be read.

Mother Hesk appeared at the gate, and to her eternal credit, she did not look afraid. She looked annoyed, in the manner of a woman whose dinner has been interrupted, and mouthed one word at me through the bars:

*Run.*

I ran — but I ran along the riverbank, into the dark, toward the city, already knowing with a taster's certainty that whatever came next would leave a taste in my mouth I would never wash out.

Because in my hurry, checking the map one last time by moonlight as the lanterns swung closer behind me, I had seen something new written beneath my own name, fresh as breath on glass, in letters of gold and green:

*The second name is Tam of the Weir. Bring him the river's greeting.*

*He will test you.*

*Do not fight the water.*
