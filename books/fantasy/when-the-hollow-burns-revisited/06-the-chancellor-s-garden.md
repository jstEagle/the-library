## 6. The Chancellor's Garden

The summons arrived on a Tuesday, which August had always found to be an unlucky day for other people.

It came in the form of a palace page with polished buttons and the particular blankness of a boy who had learned early that carrying messages was safer than reading them. He stood in the doorway of August's tenement room with a scroll sealed in green wax—the Chancellor's color—and waited while August read it twice.

*By order of Lord Chancellor Veyre, August Amberdale, Taster of the King's First Table, is commanded to attend the state banquet for the Ambassador of Sarnholt, three days hence. Attendance is not optional.*

"Well," August said to the page, "it's very kind of him to clarify."

The page did not smile. Pages who delivered summons from the Lord Chancellor were selected specifically for their inability to smile; it was practically a hiring requirement. He turned and clattered down the stairs, and August stood in their small room with its single window and its carefully unremarkable contents, and felt the floor tilt very slightly beneath their feet.

Three years of being nobody. Three years of tasting the king's breakfast with a face like unsalted porridge, of selling luck-charms to widows at the night market for coppers and never once letting the charm actually *do* anything beyond what a hopeful heart could accomplish on its own. Three years of small, deniable kindnesses, each one folded up smaller than the last, until August had become a person shaped like a shadow on a wall—present, harmless, forgettable.

And now the Lord Chancellor wanted them in his garden.

"He doesn't want *you*," August told their reflection in the chipped mirror above the washbasin. The reflection looked skeptical, which was fair. "He wants a taster. Any taster. You happen to be the one whose name is on the roster." The reflection continued to look skeptical. "It's a coincidence."

August had survived three years by believing in coincidences the way some people believed in the gods: loudly, publicly, and without any real conviction whatsoever.

* * *

The trouble with being summoned to taste at a state banquet was that everyone would be watching.

At the king's breakfast, August was furniture. A ritual, like the ceremonial pouring of water or the folding of napkins into swans. Nobody looked at the taster; they looked *through* the taster, at the food, waiting to see if the taster fell over. It was, in its way, perfect cover for a person with a lockbox under the floorboards containing hedge-witch supplies.

But a state banquet in the Chancellor's garden was theater. Every dish presented, every cup sipped, every reaction catalogued by two hundred courtiers who had nothing better to do than catalogue each other. And at the center of the garden, seated beneath the trellis where the roses had frozen black in midsummer—a wrongness nobody seemed willing to mention—would be Lord Chancellor Veyre himself.

August spent the intervening days doing what they always did before danger: cooking.

It drove Wren half-mad, when she came through the window she refused to use the door for, to find August calmly braiding dough for a honey loaf as though the world weren't coming apart at the seams.

"You're summoned to the Chancellor's garden," she said, perched on the sill like a crow with opinions, "three days after a witch-finder raid on my street, and you're *baking*."

"Baking is thinking," August said. "You wouldn't understand. You brood; I braid."

"I don't brood."

"Wren, you sharpened that knife for four hours yesterday. I timed it. That's brooding with a whetstone."

She scowled, but she took the heel of the loaf when it came out of the oven, and ate it in the way of someone determined not to enjoy anything ever again, going back for seconds almost immediately. Her father's map lay between them on the table now—it lived there, most days, weighted with the pepper box—and they had spent two evenings failing to coax the third name out of its stubborn covenant-script. But tonight August's mind kept sliding back to the green wax seal. To the frozen roses.

"Something's wrong at the palace," August said. "The gardens are dying. Midsummer frost, black roses, and now this banquet. Ambassadors don't come to look at dead flowers. Veyre wants something from this dinner."

"Maybe he wants people to stop noticing the winter."

"Ambassadors notice everything. It's why you invite them—to control what they notice first." August wiped flour from their hands and looked at the map instead of at Wren, because saying the next part aloud was easier without witnesses. "If I'm called to serve in front of the whole court, either I'm being honored, or I'm being displayed. And Chancellors don't honor food-tasters."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then, in the tone of someone making an enormous concession: "I'll be on the wall."

"There won't be a wall inside the garden."

"Then I'll find one. There's always a wall." She said it the way her father must have said *there's always a road*, and August felt the familiar ache of it—that this girl had inherited not just a map but a whole geography of stubbornness.

* * *

The Chancellor's garden, on the night of the banquet, was beautiful the way a painted portrait is beautiful: accurate and entirely without life.

August walked in behind the kitchen procession, dressed in the taster's formal grey—grey, the palace had decided generations ago, so that the taster would match the ash if things went poorly, which August had always considered a grim bit of institutional humor—and tried to look like furniture with better posture.

Torches burned in iron brackets along the hedges, though the flames gave less light than they should have, as if the cold itself were drinking. Beyond the hedges, the famous rose garden spread out under a sky far too dark for midsummer. Every bloom August could see was black or brown or hanging heavy with frost, and yet the tables had been arranged so that guests faced inward, toward the lit pavilion, and no one had to look at the evidence.

Lord Chancellor Veyre sat at the high table like a man who had grown there.

August had seen him perhaps a dozen times in three years, always at distance, and had built up a portrait from fragments: narrow, neat, precise. Up close, the portrait gained details August didn't want. His hands were the hands of a much younger man, smooth and unspotted, set against a face lined like old parchment. He wore no rings. His eyes moved over the garden the way August's own eyes moved over a plate of food—not admiring it, exactly. *Assaying* it.

*Tasting it,* August thought, and suppressed a shiver.

The dinner began, as these things did, with a parade of dishes designed to communicate national wealth through butter. August tasted each one thirty heartbeats before it reached the high table, standing at the sideboard with the other tasters—a fish-kitchen veteran named Corvo with forearms like ship's cable, and a nervous new girl from the pastry, Bette, whose hands shook so badly August quietly shifted the sweet course onto their own tray. Small kindnesses. Even here. Especially here.

And all the while, August listened.

Court gossip was a current, and tasters stood hip-deep in it. From the servers' murmurs August gathered that the Sarnholti ambassador had arrived with forty retainers and a suspicious quantity of fur; that the king had not appeared in public in eleven days; that the witch-finders had made nine arrests that week, including, oddly, a candlemaker whose only crime seemed to be making candles that burned too cheerfully. Nine arrests. The hunt wasn't closing in. The hunt was *expanding*, sweeping wider and wider, as if searching for something specific and increasingly desperate about it.

"And the taster," said a voice at August's elbow, silk over gravel.

Veyre stood there holding a glass of pale wine he did not drink from. Up close, his youthfulness was worse—there was a stillness to him, a lack of the small human fidgets, as though he'd practiced his way out of having a body.

"My lord Chancellor." August bowed to the correct depth for a servant addressed unexpectedly, which was shallow enough to be humble and deep enough to be obedient.

"They tell me you've served the king's table three years without incident." Veyre's voice was mild. Everything about him was mild, the way ice is mild until it closes over a river. "No illnesses. No accidents. Remarkable health, Master Amberdale."

"I take care of myself, my lord."

"I'm sure you do." He turned the wineglass slowly by its stem. "There was a courier, some weeks ago. Died in the Lowmarket tenements. The witch-finders believe he carried contraband. Papers. Maps." The glass stopped turning. "You live in the Lowmarket."

"The Lowmarket is large, my lord. Most of it is in it."

Something flickered across Veyre's face that might, in a human face, have been amusement. "Serve well tonight, taster. Your health is a matter of considerable interest to me."

He drifted away toward the ambassador, leaving August with a mouth gone dry and a heart conducting its business somewhere around the back of the throat.

*He knows.* Or he suspected, the way a fisherman suspects where the fish are without needing to see them—he simply dragged the whole river and watched what flopped. The summons hadn't been coincidence. It had been display. *Look at me, whoever has the map. Look how closely I'm watching your world.*

August tasted the next five courses in a haze of controlled terror, and it was during the sixth course—the quail, roasted with cherries and served under a silver dome that Corvo lifted with a flourish—that everything went wrong at once.

The smell hit first.

Most poisons announced themselves subtly, to a trained tongue: bitter almonds, a metallic edge, a sweetness that sat wrong. This did not announce itself subtly. This *shouted*. Underneath the cherry and the fat and the salt lay something August had encountered exactly once before, years ago, in a market cup of wine meant for a moneylender—henbane, but henbane cut with something older, something that smelled less like a poison and more like a *place*, like cellar stone and cold rivers and the underside of bridges where the world's warmth doesn't reach.

The witch-finders' poisons. Crafted, not brewed.

And August had already swallowed the tasting portion before the recognition landed.

Time did the thing it does in emergencies, stretching like warm sugar. August heard Corvo say something cheerful about the quail. Saw the dish traveling toward the high table on Bette's trembling tray. Did the arithmetic—Veyre's plate was second from the left, the ambassador's first, and the poison was in the *cherry glaze*, which meant it had been laid under the ambassador's portion, and someone in the kitchen was trying to kill a diplomat and blame it on—

Then the arithmetic stopped mattering, because August's tongue went numb, and then their throat, and then the numbness moved inward like winter claiming a garden, orderly and thorough and utterly indifferent.

*Ah,* August thought, with the strange clarity of the nearly dead. *So this is what it feels like.*

They had built their immunity the way they'd built everything else: in small doses, over years, deliberately. Hedge-witch charms to blunt the worst of it, yes—but mostly it was the swallowing. Every toxin the palace kitchens might produce, taken in grains and drops, night after night, alone in a tenement room, so that no king would ever die on their watch. Their blood was a library of every poison the kingdom knew.

Except this one had been written by someone else. Something else. The cold spread down their arms and up behind their eyes, and the torchlight thinned to pinpricks, and distantly August registered that they were still standing, still holding the tray, because three years of invisibility meant the body had learned to keep performing even when the soul was busy dying.

*Fight it,* said some deep-buried instinct, the same instinct that had pressed a map into a stranger's hands. *You're oath-blood. Fight it.*

August didn't know how to fight anything. They knew how to *endure*. So they endured—swallowed against the closing throat, breathed past the freezing lungs, and did the only thing they had ever done with poison, the only thing they knew.

They took it in. Deliberately. Dish by dish, taste by taste, as if grief itself were a meal that demanded finishing.

And their body—clever, patient, three-years-cultivated body—did what it always did. It broke the poison apart. Catalogued it. Filed it among the others.

But this poison was not altogether of this world, and as August's blood dismantled it, something answered. A heat bloomed up through their chest, unfamiliar and vast, and the numbness cracked like river ice in spring. Light—actual light, faint and gold-green, the color of the memory-pool August had drunk from below the Hollow—flickered once along the veins of their wrists.

Only once. Only for a heartbeat. But Bette was standing right beside them, and Bette screamed.

* * *

Afterward, August could never reconstruct the next hour except in fragments, the way one remembers a storm by its damage.

Bette screaming. The tray falling—August catching it, because even half-dead and glowing, apparently, the body insisted on keeping the quail off the floor. Corvo's arm barring the approach of the guard. The ambassador's table in uproar, the poisoned dish discovered, a whole cherry-glazed conspiracy unfolding under torchlight. Voices. Boots. The witch-finders' captain arriving with his grey cloak and his gray face.

And Veyre, rising from the high table, unhurried, crossing the garden through the chaos as though the chaos had been arranged for his convenience.

He stopped in front of August—who stood swaying, tray abandoned now, wrists clasped behind their back to hide the fading gold-green tracery—and looked at them for a long, long moment.

"Fascinating," Veyre said softly.

"My lord." August's voice came out rougher than intended. "The ambassador's dish was poisoned. I recommend the kitchen staff be—"

"Yes, yes. Treason in the kitchens, we'll root it out by morning." He leaned close, and his eyes—those assay-balances—moved from August's face down to their wrists, where the last traces of light were guttering out like the fae children in the scrying glass. "You should know, Master Amberdale, that ordinary men do not glow."

"I believe," August said, "that was the torchlight."

"It was not the torchlight."

The garden held its breath. Somewhere beyond the hedge, a nightbird sang—a wrong note for the season, thin and strange, and both of them heard it.

Veyre smiled. It was the single worst thing August had ever seen him do, because it made him look human, and they understood suddenly that the smile was the disguise, worn outward, the way August wore dullness.

"Take the taster home," he said to the captain, straightening. "Unharmed. Watched." And then, quieter, meant for August alone: "I'll want to know what's in your blood eventually, Amberdale. Everything interesting ends up on my table. Yours will too."

* * *

They let August walk out of the garden under escort, through corridors smelling of panic and spilled wine, past servants whispering behind their hands, out the great gates and into streets gone blue-black with unnatural night. The guards peeled off at the Lowmarket boundary, satisfied—or ordered to seem satisfied—and August walked the last stretch alone, shaking now that it was safe to shake, breath clouding in midsummer air.

Wren dropped off the bakery roof half a block from the tenement, landing soft as a cat with a grievance.

"I saw the light," she said, no preamble, no hello. "From the wall. In the garden. You *glowed*, August."

"Torchlight."

"Don't you dare give me the torchlight."

"It was extremely good torchlight," August said, and then their knees gave their notice, and Wren caught their arm, and steered them the rest of the way home with a grip that was half support and half arrest.

Inside, August sank onto the stool by the cold hearth while Wren built a fire with the brisk violence she brought to all domestic tasks. The shaking got worse. The gold-green flicker came back faintly at their wrists—just traces now, embers under skin—and August stared at it and understood, finally, what the map had known all along.

Oath-blood wasn't ancestry. Or not only. It was *preparedness*. Some blood learns to carry what isn't safe to carry. Theirs had been learning for three years, one grain of poison at a time, without ever being told what it was practicing for.

"Veyre saw," Wren said. Not a question.

"Veyre saw."

"Then he knows what you are."

August looked at the fire, at the frost furring the inside of the window glass, at their own faintly shining hands. The taster had become the tasted; the invisible had been weighed, assayed, and found worth watching. There was no folding back down to nothing after this. There was no smaller.

"No," August said quietly. "He knows what I'm *for*. And tomorrow morning, so will every witch-finder in the city."

Outside, boots were already moving through the streets—more of them than usual, moving with purpose, spreading through the Lowmarket like frost through a garden.

Wren blew out the candle.
