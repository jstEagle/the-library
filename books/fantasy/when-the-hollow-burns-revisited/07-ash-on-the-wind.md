## 7. Ash on the Wind

Wren had grown up learning to read crowds the way other children learned to read letters. A market crowd had a grammar: the haggling pitch of voices, the shuffle of feet, the small courtesies of elbows and apologies. Her father had taught her that. *You can hear trouble before you see it, little wren,* he used to say, haggling over chestnuts or maps or whatever the day's coin required. *Trouble always clears its throat first.*

The crowd in Copperkettle Square on that grey morning had gone silent.

Silence in a market was a scream wearing a mask.

She found August at the edge of it—of course she did; since the raid on their block three nights ago they'd been inseparable in the way of two people who each suspected the other might bolt and neither could afford to let happen. August stood very still near a spice-seller's stall, and their face had the particular bloodless look Wren was beginning to catalogue as *August has just realized something terrible.*

"What is it?" she asked, though she already knew. She'd known since the bells started ringing wrong—a flat, arrhythmic clangor she hadn't heard since she was seven, when they burned a hedge-mage in her grandmother's town.

"Charm-seller," August said quietly. "The one from the night market. Old Sefa. She sold luck-knots for bakers' windows."

Wren followed their gaze through the crowd, to the square's center, where witch-finders in their slate-grey coats had built a pyre out of market stalls—broken crates, a cart, someone's entire stock of dried lavender, which meant the smoke would smell sweet and that was somehow the worst thing yet.

"They can't," Wren said. "She sells *knots*. String and knots. That's not—that isn't even illegal, that's superstition, the edicts say superstition gets a fine—"

"The edicts changed yesterday." August's voice was flat as slate. "I heard it from a kitchen girl at dawn. Possession of charms is now grounds for execution. Not fines. Not trial." They swallowed. "Execution. As an example."

"An example of what?"

August didn't answer, because at that moment the witch-finders brought Sefa forward, and the whole square learned together what the example was.

* * *

Wren had seen death before. Her father's, most intimately—the door of their rooms broken open, men in grey, her mother screaming, and Wren shoved out a window with the family's spare cloak and nothing else. She had seen the aftermath of death in hospices and ditches. But she had never seen a killing done this way: slow, public, ceremonial, with the city watch holding back the crowd not to protect Sefa but to guarantee the audience.

Sefa did not struggle. That was the part that broke something in Wren and left it broken. The old woman climbed the pyre like she was climbing onto a market cart, grumbling, patient, and when the witch-finder captain read out her crimes—"trafficking in forbidden arts, corrupting the faithful"—she laughed. Actually laughed, a dry crackle that carried impossibly across the silent square.

"Corrupting," she repeated, loud enough for everyone. "I sold bread-charms, you peacock. I made sure the loaves rose and the babies slept. If that corrupts you, you were rotten already."

The captain struck flint to oil without another word. He didn't want a conversation. Conversations could go wrong. Fire was simpler.

Wren turned away at the first smell—and then forced herself to turn back. That was the promise she made to herself, nails biting into her palms: *I will look. I will look at all of it.* Because looking away was how this happened, wasn't it? Years of looking away while the edicts tightened one notch at a time, everyone telling themselves a fine wasn't a rope, a rope wasn't a pyre, until here they stood in a sweet-smelling square watching an old woman die for string.

Beside her, August had not looked away once. Their lips moved slightly, and Wren caught fragments—an old kitchen blessing, a charm for the dead's safe passage, murmured under their breath like a prayer smuggled past the guards in plain sight. Even now, even here, August couldn't stop doing the small kind thing. It was, Wren was learning, simply who they were. It infuriated her and steadied her in equal measure.

When it was done, the witch-finders didn't disperse. They nailed a notice to the square's proclamation post, and a crier read it in a voice trained to carry:

*"By order of his lordship Chancellor Veyre: the harborers of witches share the witch's fate. Those who know of magic and do not report it burn beside it. Report. Report. Report."*

*Harborers,* Wren thought. She and August were standing shoulder to shoulder in front of half the city. If anyone recognized either of them—if anyone remembered the raid, the stranger bleeding out on a tenement landing—

"We should go," she murmured.

"No," August said. "Not yet. Watch the crowd."

Wren watched. And slowly, horribly, she saw what August wanted her to see. Fear, yes—fear everywhere, fear thick enough to chew. But threaded through it, here and there: nodding. A fishwife saying to her neighbor, loud enough to be heard, *"Well, if they're burning them anyway, better now than later."* A shopkeeper spitting toward the ash-pile. A boy, no older than twelve, memorizing faces—not the witch-finders' faces. The weepers'. The ones who'd cried out. Reporting was going to be rewarded; you could smell it, the way you smelled rain coming.

"It's not fear," Wren said slowly, as they drifted away from the square with the crowd's current, anonymous among hundreds. "It's permission. He's giving them permission."

"Veyre." August said the name like tasting something. Wren had learned that about them—they tasted words sometimes, especially dangerous ones, rolling them around like wine. "Three years I've tasted his food. Three years, and I've watched him. Do you know what I never once tasted in him? Hatred of magic. Chancellors who hate things eat badly—rage sours the stomach. Veyre eats beautifully. He outlawed magic the way a man buys up all the wells in a drought. Not because he hates water." August's jaw tightened. "Because he wants to be the only one who decides who drinks."

They stopped in the shadow of a cooper's awning, out of the current of the crowd. Up close, Wren could see the exhaustion carved into August's face—three days of almost no sleep, deciphering the map by candle stub, jumping at every boot on the stairs. She probably looked the same. Neither of them had mentioned the fight, the knife, the truce sealed in terror during the raid; some things didn't need revisiting. The knife had said what it had to say.

"My father knew," Wren said. It came out before she'd decided to say it. "My father spent eleven years hunting for the Hollow. Eleven years, while the winter got worse and the fae lights on the river guttered out one by one. I used to think he was obsessed. Mad, maybe. I said so to his face once." Her voice cracked and she hated it and pushed on. "But look at what just happened. An old woman burned for *bread-charms* while the kingdom freezes in midsummer. Why now? Why this hard, this fast?"

August was quiet a moment. Around them the market tried to resume itself—stalls reopening with the shaky brightness of people determined to pretend the last hour hadn't happened—but the day's heart wasn't in it. Ash still drifted on the wind, settling on fruit and fabric and everyone's shoulders alike, so that the whole crowd wore the same grey dusting, a congregation no one had agreed to join.

"Because the covenant is failing," August said at last. "The map says so. Hesk will confirm it—I'm sure of it. The winter isn't weather, Wren. It's the fae realm dying, and its dying bleeds through into ours. And if Veyre knows that—if his family has known it since the betrayal—then the crackdown isn't about magic at all."

"Then what's it about?"

"Making sure nobody can fix it." August's hands, Wren noticed, had curled into fists inside their sleeves. August, who apologized to furniture. August, whose fury was so unfamiliar to them that it seemed to frighten them more than the witch-finders did. "Think about it. If the Hollow can be reached and the stone reforged, what happens? The fae live. The two realms stay bound. The old arrangement holds—the arrangement where power is *shared*, where humans and fae swore one oath at one stone. But if the covenant dies, and the fae die with it, then what's left is us. Alone. Human, and ruled by whoever's standing closest to the throne when the last light goes out." August met her eyes. "He doesn't want to save the city. He wants to inherit the ruins."

The wind shifted, carrying more ash, and Wren thought absurdly of snow. Of the frost that had killed the palace gardens—Tam had told them, Tam with his river-warden's knowledge and his careful human disguise—in *midsummer*, flowers blackening overnight as if a year had died ahead of schedule. The world was ending politely, gradually, in a language most people couldn't read. And Veyre was burning anyone who might translate.

"Sefa knew things," Wren said suddenly. The thought arrived whole, the way her father's best deductions used to. "A charm-seller in the night market, in *that* district, where every hedge-witch and half-touched soul in the city comes to trade? She heard everything. Who's oath-kin. Where the old names went to ground. If the witch-finders took her, they took more than an old woman. They took an archive."

"And if they made her talk—" August went pale again.

"She didn't." Wren surprised herself with how certain she sounded. "Did you see her? She *laughed* at him. Eleven years my father taught me that people in this work have two ways of being caught: the ones who break and the ones who were ready. Sefa sold luck-knots knowing the penalty grew every season. She was ready. Whatever they wanted from her, she kept it behind her teeth." She paused. "But they'll find others who aren't ready. And every name she ever touched is now a thread they're pulling."

* * *

They walked most of the afternoon, separately and then together again, shaking tails that probably weren't there and one that definitely was (a limping man with too-shiny boots, lost easily enough near the tanneries, where everyone's nose surrendered). By dusk they'd reached Mother Hesk's hospice, moving through the leper's gate where the watch feared to follow—superstition cut both ways, and Hesk wielded it shamelessly.

The old physician listened to their account of the burning without interrupting, grinding herbs with a pestle in slow, even circles, her ruined physician's hands steady as a clock.

"You understand what today was," she said finally. Not a question.

"A message," Wren said.

"Worse. A *deadline*." Hesk set down the pestle and fixed them both with eyes gone milky at the edges but missing nothing. "I've seen crackdowns before, children. This one has a shape. Veyre isn't hunting magic—he's hunting the map. He doesn't know who carries it, but he knows it exists, and he knows it surfaces when the covenant grows desperate. Sefa was a knot in his net. He cut her out in public to make the rest of the net feel it." She began wrapping a bundle of herbs, brisk and precise. "Which means your father's map, girl, is currently the most dangerous piece of parchment in the kingdom. And the two of you are the most dangerous pair of nobodies."

"Then we should move faster," Wren said. "The second name. The river-warden. We go tomorrow. Tonight, if—"

"Tonight you sleep," Hesk said, in the voice of a woman who had ordered kings to bed and been obeyed. "Dead couriers deliver nothing." Then, softer, seeing Wren's face: "The fire will still be cold in the morning. Grief keeps. Purpose keeps better. Sleep, child. Both of you."

Sleep, it turned out, was not on offer. Wren lay on a pallet in the hospice's back room listening to the labored breathing of the dying poor around her, and did arithmetic instead of dreaming. Eleven years of her father's searching. A map that only woke for oath-blood. A chancellor who wanted the fae dead. A solstice coming, Hesk had said, when the eclipse would open the Hollow's door one last time before the stone went dark forever.

How many mornings did that leave? How many more squares, more pyres, more sweet-smelling smoke?

She gave up on sleep near midnight and found August in the hospice's herb garden, sitting on a bench under the dead apple trees, turning something over in their hands. Wren sat down beside them. Below the garden walls, the river glimmered—or rather, didn't. There had been fae-light in that river once, Tam said. Now it was just water, reflecting a sky with no stars willing to commit.

"For Sefa," August said, opening their hand. On the palm lay a small knot of red thread, singed at one end. "Someone pressed it into mine in the crowd. I don't know who. One of hers, maybe—one of the widows she baked charms for." August closed their fingers around it. "I keep thinking about what she said. *If that corrupts you, you were rotten already.*"

"She was right," Wren said.

"I know. That's what frightens me. Because Veyre heard the same words and drew the opposite lesson." August looked out at the starless river. "Tomorrow, then. The river-warden. The second name."

"Tomorrow," Wren agreed. Then, because grief had burned down to something harder and hotter than sorrow, she added: "And August? When we get to the Hollow—when we reforge this thing—I want it on the record that I intend to be *extremely* unpleasant to Veyre about my father."

August huffed something that was half laugh, half sob. "Noted. Though between us, I'd settle for getting there alive."

Below the wall, in the black water of the river, something flashed. Briefly—a flicker of gold-green light, like a candle glimpsed underwater, there and gone. Both of them leaned forward. The river lay dark again.

But it had been *there*. Emberfall, guttering, drowning, reaching up one thin finger of light through the surface—as if to ask, in a voice almost too weak to carry:

*Hurry.*

Wren stood, pulling August up with her. "First light," she said. "We go at first light."
