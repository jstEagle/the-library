## 8. The River Oath

The second name on the map was written in water.

I don't mean that poetically—or rather, I do, but it was also literal. When I unrolled the oilskin in Wren's rented room above the cooper's shop, with the shutters drawn and a single stub of candle burning low so the script wouldn't wake its own glow, the name *Tam of the Weir* shimmered like the surface of a pond someone had just skimmed a stone across. The letters rippled. They settled. They rippled again, patient as tide.

"That's unnerving," Wren said from the doorway, arms crossed, a knife she hadn't offered to put away riding her hip. "Names shouldn't do that."

"Most names don't." I traced the air above it without touching. Three days had passed since Mother Hesk had sent us down to the river district with a vial of willow-bark tincture, a warning about the Chancellor's riders, and the particular blessing of a woman who expected never to see us again. "Hesk said the second oath-kin keeps the drowned piece of the stone. The river wardens' families have held that post since before the city had walls."

"And he's going to hand it over because you flash a map at him?"

"I imagine," I said, "that it will be more complicated than that."

Wren snorted. Everything with Wren was a snort or a knife; I was learning to read the gradations between them. "Everything with these people is more complicated than that. My father spent eleven years chasing this thing and it killed him."

"It didn't kill him," I said quietly. "The witch-finders did."

She looked at me the way she always did when I corrected her grief—like she was deciding whether to forgive me this month. Then she shrugged her coat off the peg. "Fine. But we're going at dawn, and if the river man tries anything strange, I'm throwing you in first as a distraction."

"You're a marvel of tactical thinking."

"I have my moments."

* * *

The river district at dawn smelled of wet rope, woodsmoke, and the particular green-mud sweetness of the Aldwater in summer—which wasn't summer anymore, whatever the calendar insisted. The frost had followed us out of the palace gardens and into the streets. It lay along the wharves in silver seams, and it frosted the fishermen's nets stiff, and when I breathed deep I could taste it: cold iron at the back of the tongue, and under it something fainter, like a song remembered from a room you left years ago.

The fae realm's dying breath, Hesk had called it. It got stronger near water. That, I would learn, was no accident.

We found Tam at the old weir upstream of the mill race—a long, low structure of timber and stone that had stood, according to the fisherfolk, since the world was young and slightly damper. He was knee-deep in the shallows, mending a fish-pass with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, and my first thought was entirely unworthy of the quest: *he doesn't look like half a god's worth of anything.* My second thought, arriving about four heartbeats later and considerably more useful: *he doesn't look human either.*

It's hard to say how I knew. His ears were round. His skin was brown as river clay and weathered like everyone who worked water for a living. But there was a stillness to him, a quality of attention, that humans don't have. Humans look *at* things. Tam looked *through* them, the way water looks through a net—taking in everything, keeping nothing, holding the whole shape of the river in one unhurried gaze.

He saw us before we reached the bank, of course. He'd probably heard our heartbeats from the mill.

"If you're from the Guild of Dredgers," he called, voice carrying flat and clear over the water, "the toll's unchanged and no, I won't reconsider the eel quotas. The eels have opinions too."

"The eels can take it up with the council," I said. "We're here about a stone."

His hands stopped moving. Just for a moment—the space of one breath—and then resumed, but slower now, deliberate, each pass of the cord through the wicker a kind of counting. "Lots of stones by a river."

"One in particular. Split down its heart. Old enough to remember when this river had two banks in two worlds." I held up the map, and even folded as it was, I felt it hum against my palm like a struck bell remembering its note.

Tam waded out of the water. Water streamed off him, and where it fell on the frost-rimed stones, the frost *melted*—just a little ring of green around each drop, tiny and impossible and gone in seconds. He looked at me for a long time. Then at Wren. Then back.

"You taste things," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm the king's food-taster."

"Mm." Something moved behind his eyes—not quite a smile, not quite sorrow. "That's what they're calling it now. Come down to the water at moonrise. Bring nothing made of iron. And bring your courage, taster, because the river's going to want to know why it should trust you, and it doesn't accept letters of introduction."

"What if I don't show up?" Wren asked.

"Then your father's map stays a dead man's paper," Tam said, not unkindly, "and the winter finishes what it started. The river is patient, but it isn't *waiting* forever. Nothing is."

He turned and walked back into the shallows, and this time the water rose to meet him, folding around his waist, his shoulders, and then there was only a ripple and a circle of melted frost spreading out like a benediction.

Wren stared at the spot for a while. "I hate him already."

"You hate everyone already."

"Yes, but I usually need a reason first. This one came pre-installed."

* * *

Moonrise over the Aldwater turned the whole river to hammered pewter, and I stood on the bank with my boots off and my charms tucked against my skin and tried very hard not to think about how many ways a person can drown.

Wren waited up on the levee with strict instructions and a knife she'd made me swear to reach for. She hadn't liked being excluded from the rite. She'd liked even less my pointing out that the map answered to oath-blood, and hers, apparently, ran thin—"thin" being Tam's word, delivered via a boy sent up from the weir with a message scratched on a shingle: *The daughter may watch. Only the bearer swims.*

The shingle also said: *Undress to your shift. Leave your charms on the bank. All of them. Yes, even the little one in your boot. Especially that one.*

So there I stood, barefoot on frost-slick stone, feeling naked in a way that had nothing to do with clothing. Every charm I owned was a small act of defiance I'd spent three years perfecting—the luck-knot against mischance, the salt-ring sewn into my hem, the little hazel bead in my boot that was technically nothing at all, just a bead, just superstition, except it wasn't, none of it was, and leaving them all on the bank felt like walking into the dark without my own name.

"You don't have to," Wren said. Her voice was rough. Grief does that to some people; it had sanded Wren down to grain and grainwood. "There are other oath-kin. Hesk said—"

"Hesk said the names come in order, and the order matters." I flexed my toes against the cold. "And I've been swallowing other people's poison for three years, Wren. One more dish."

"That's an awful way to live."

"It's the only way I know how." I smiled at her, and was surprised to find the smile mostly true. "Watch the bank. If I'm not up in a quarter hour, avenge me."

"On who?"

"That's the spirit."

Then I walked into the river.

The cold hit me like a slammed door. The Aldwater in midsummer had never been warm—it came down off the same hills where Emberfall's border thinned into ours—but tonight it carried winter in its bones, real winter, the covenant's winter, and I felt it reading me as I went deeper. Shin. Knee. Waist. The current pulled with intent now, not the blind shove of water but something with *manners*, something that wanted me in a specific place, and I let it take me because fighting a river is how rivers win arguments.

Tam stood on the far side of the pool, hip-deep, moonlight silvering him to the collarbones. His eyes were wrong now—or right, depending on your ledger—pupils blown wide and dark as depthless water, irises gone the green-gold of light through leaves.

"The rite is old," he said, and his voice had doubled, human speech laid over something older and slower, like a hymn sung under a waterfall. "Older than the law that burns your kind. Older than the king's crown. When the covenant was forged, the stone was set in three places: one in the earth, at the Hollow. One in the blood, in the lineages of both peoples. And one in the water, because oaths spoken on land dry out, taster. Only what passes through water is kept honest."

"And the water decides?"

"The water remembers." He spread his hands. "It remembers every promise ever sworn on this river, and every promise broken. It has been holding the drowned shard of the stone for two hundred years, and it is *tired*, and it grieves, and grief makes rivers run cold and deep and hungry." His doubled voice dropped. "It will hold you under until it knows you. Not your face. Not your name. What you carry. If you carry only fear, it will keep you—kindly, in the way of rivers, but it will keep you. If you carry what the map thinks you carry—"

"What does it think I carry?"

"Taste," Tam said, "and find out."

The current took me then, properly, and pulled me under.

I have nearly died twice in my life. Once at nine, from a bad batch of elderberry wine that taught me my tongue was different; once at twenty-three, when a nervous cook mistook foxglove for comfrey and I drank the queen's tisane knowing, *knowing*, that this dose was past what my blood could bargain down. Both times, dying felt like falling. This felt like drowning properly—like being *kept*. The water closed over me and did not let go, and it pressed in at my ears and eyes and mouth with the patience of centuries, and somewhere far above, distorted into bells, I heard Wren scream my name.

Panic arrived on schedule. I fought it the way I'd learned to fight panic at the tasting table: by working. Catalog. Breathe—no, don't breathe, hold, count. Taste.

Because that was the trick, wasn't it? That was the only gift I had ever owned. So I tasted.

The water tasted of mud and weed and the mineral dark of the riverbed. Under that, cold iron—the winter's signature. Under *that*—

Grief.

Not mine. Not anyone's, exactly. A grief the size of a realm, pooled and slow, the way groundwater pools. I tasted children of light guttering like candles at the end of their wicks. I tasted orchards going gray mid-bloom, and songs losing their last notes one singer at a time, and doors closing between worlds that had stood open since before the word *door* meant anything to anyone. I tasted two hundred years of promises kept honestly by water while everything they were promised *for* rotted on the shore.

And underneath the grief, so faint I nearly missed it, a question. The river's question. It didn't have words. It had a shape, and the shape was: *Why should I keep holding? Who is worth holding on for?*

I couldn't answer. I had no breath to answer with. My lungs were burning now, black flowers blooming behind my eyes, and the rational animal in my skull was screaming that I was dying, that Tam had watched a fool walk into a river and die, that Wren was going to bury a second person she—

No. Work. Catalog.

The river tasted of grief. I knew grief. I'd been eating small doses of it my whole life—the widow's luck-charms, the cook's quiet dismissals, three years of kindnesses so small they passed as superstition, invisible even to me. I had swallowed poison for strangers every morning and called it a job. I had never once been permitted to matter, and I had done the work anyway, dish after dish after dish.

So I did the only thing I knew how to do.

I opened my mouth underwater—I don't know how else to describe it, I opened something—and I *took the taste in*. Deliberately. The way I took in arsenic at the king's table, the way I took in foxglove at twenty-three: not fighting it, not refusing it. Tasting it fully. Acknowledging it. Letting the grief be exactly as large as it was, without pretending I could fix it, without flinching away.

*I taste you,* I told the river, in the only language I had, which was no language at all. *You have held this a long time. It is heavy. It is real. I am not worthy of it and I will not pretend to be. But I will carry what I can, one mouthful at a time, if you'll tell me where the stone sleeps.*

The water went very still around me.

Stillness, underwater, is a strange thing—you feel held instead of crushed. In that stillness I tasted the river's answer arrive like a change of season: surprise first, ancient and rusty, the shock of a thing that had not been *met* in two hundred years meeting something at last. Then something that might, in a person, have been laughter—grief's twin, the thing grief becomes when it finally sets down its bags.

The river let go of my lungs gently, the way you set down something breakable, and pushed me up.

I broke the surface gasping, and the gasp tasted of mint and morning and, absurdly, honey—summer flavors in a frozen world, the river spending its hoarded memories to give me back a sweet breath. I clung to a boulder in the middle of the pool, coughing, alive, my shift plastered to me and my heart hammering out a rhythm I felt in my teeth.

On the bank, Wren was halfway into the water before Tam caught her arm, and she rounded on him with the knife already drawn, and he raised both hands and said, mildly, "She's fine. Look. She's laughing."

I was, I realized. Laughing and crying in equal measure, which is the specific flavor of having survived something you volunteered for.

Tam waded out to the boulder, and in the shallows beside it the moonlight bent oddly, and I saw it: beneath three feet of impossibly clear water, resting in a cradle of woven roots, a fragment of pale stone the size of a paving slab, veined through with gold light that pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like a heartbeat.

"The river accepts you," Tam said. "Which means I do, which means—" he grimaced, rueful, suddenly and completely human again, "—which means I'm in. Gods help us all. The shard comes with me, and me with you, and fair warning: the third name on that map is worse than the second."

"Worse than a drowning test?"

"Oh, much." He looked north, toward the hills, toward the Hollow, and his face did something complicated. "Because the third name belongs to a man who's known about the failing covenant for thirty years. And he's Lord Chancellor of this city."

The gold pulse in the drowned stone flickered—as if the heartbeat itself had stumbled at the name.

Veyre.

I thought of the banquet hall, and the poisoned dish, and the way the Chancellor had watched me eat it. And I understood, all at once and completely, that we had never been hunting him at all.

He had been hunting us.
