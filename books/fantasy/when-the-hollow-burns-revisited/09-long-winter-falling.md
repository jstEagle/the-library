## 9. Long Winter Falling

The frost came for the roses on the morning of Highsummer.

August stood at the kitchen garden gate with a cup of the king's morning tisane cooling in their hands and watched the head gardener—a broad, sun-brown man named Petch who had never in twelve years of service worn anything heavier than a linen shirt—walk the rows of his ruined beds with his hat held to his chest, like a mourner at a funeral.

The roses had been heavy with bloom on Midsummer's Eve. August knew this because they had tasted them: petals folded into the royal honey-cake, sweet and faintly peppery. Now every bush in the palace garden stood sheathed in white, blossoms blackened mid-unfurling, leaves brittle as old parchment. The frost lay in perfect, deliberate lines along the hedgerows, as though someone had drawn it there with a careful hand.

"Midsummer," Petch said, when August reached him. Not a complaint. Just the word, set down between them like a stone.

"I know," August said.

"Frost. In Highsummer." He looked up at a sky that was, admittedly, doing its best. The sun hung pale behind a gauze of cloud, giving light without warmth, like a coin seen through fog. "My father gardened here forty years and his father before him, and neither of them ever—" He stopped, pressed his lips together, and put his hat back on with the air of a man deciding that grief could wait until he'd saved what could be saved. "They're saying it's witchcraft. Down in the servants' hall."

"Are they," said August, in the flattest voice they owned.

"Petch! Petch, the glasshouse—"

He went. August stood a moment longer among the killed roses, then carried the tisane inside, because the king was waiting, and whatever else was dying, breakfast kept its schedule.

* * *

The king's wasting had begun, by the physicians' later reckoning, three days after Midsummer—though August had noticed it sooner, because noticing was the whole of their trade.

His Majesty had always eaten like a man genuinely fond of his food, which made August's work easier and their conscience lighter. Now he sat before a tray of quail in rosewater and pushed it about the plate like a child confronted with homework. His hands, once steady enough to carve at state dinners, had developed a fine tremor. His skin had gone the color of candle tallow, and there were shadows under his eyes deep enough to store things in.

"The quail is excellent," he said faintly, moving a drumstick two inches to the left.

"Then perhaps Your Majesty might honor it by eating some."

That earned August the ghost of a smile. The king had a habit of talking to his food-taster as though the taster were a person; it was one of the small, dangerous kindnesses of his reign, and August had spent three years trying not to be moved by it.

"I dream of winter," the king said abruptly. "Every night now. A winter with no floor to it. I stand in snow and beneath the snow there is more snow, and beneath *that*—" He stopped himself, embarrassed, and reached for the honeyed water. He drank half and set it down with a wince, as though it had hurt him. "Everything tastes of ash lately, Amberdale. Even the honey."

August's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

Because they knew. They had tasted it themselves that very morning, standing over the breakfast tray in the little tasting room with its row of silver spoons: honey from the palace apiary, and beneath its sweetness something else—not poison, nothing so merciful as poison, which has an author and an antidote. This was absence. The taste of a thing forgetting how to be sweet. It was the same hollowness August had found in the well-water last week, and in the bread, and yesterday in a plum so beautiful it ought to have sung.

The realm was losing its flavor.

* * *

Mother Hesk had taught them the theory, back in the hospice, in her crooked handwriting on scraps of ledger paper: *The covenant is not a wall, child, it is a circulation. What flows between the realms feeds both. Stopper the flow and you do not merely starve the fae—you begin to starve everything that ever drank from them.*

Everything, August thought now, watching the king's physician lean close and peer into His Majesty's eyes with the expression of a man reading a bill he cannot pay. Everything drinks.

There were four physicians at the king's bedside, and by afternoon there would be seven, and they would bleed him, and purge him, and argue about humors while the true sickness went about its business unchallenged, because none of them could name it. How could they? Its name was written on an oilskin map hidden under August's floorboards, in script that only revealed itself to oath-blood, and the cure lay in a split stone in the hills above the city, past witch-finders and checkpoints and the Lord Chancellor's increasingly attentive eye.

"You look pale, Amberdale," said Physician Graye, encountering August in the corridor outside the sickroom. She was the senior of them, dry and shrewd, and she had taken to studying August with an attention August did not care for. "Tasting disagree with you?"

"I'm told it disagrees with everyone eventually."

"Hm." Her eyes were sharp as lancets. "The king says everything tastes of ash. Do you find it so?"

August considered lying, and then considered that Graye was the only person in the palace asking the interesting question. "I find," August said carefully, "that things taste less like themselves than they did. The flour. The fruit. The water." They let that sit. "You could ask the bakers. They'd tell you the ovens are sulking. Ask the brewers—the mash won't take. Whatever's wrong with the king, Physician, I don't believe he's alone in having it."

Graye was silent a long moment. "Half the city's children have stopped growing," she said at last, quietly. "Not ill, precisely. Just... paused. Like plants in a cold frame waiting out a frost that doesn't lift." She rubbed her eyes. "And the Chancellor's men tell me it's sorcery. That someone is *doing* this to us." Her voice curdled slightly on the word. "As if any hedge-witch alive could reach into every loaf in the city."

"And can they?"

"No." She said it flatly, with the certainty of forty years of medicine. "Whatever this is, it isn't a hand at work. It's a *body*. Something systemic. And bodies don't sicken out of spite—they sicken because something's failing in them." She fixed August with a look. "If you learn anything in your peculiar line of work, Amberdale, you'll send for me. Day or night."

She swept off before August could decide whether they had just acquired an ally or a liability.

* * *

Wren arrived at the tenement that evening smelling of chimney smoke and river mud, which meant she'd come across the roofs again, which meant she was frightened, which meant she would deny being frightened, so August didn't ask. They put soup in front of her instead—barley and onion, tasting today of barley and onion and a whisper of nothing underneath—and waited.

"They burned another one," Wren said, when the bowl was half empty. "Down in Tanner's Row. An old man who sold birth-charms. Horseshoes and red thread, August. Red thread." Her jaw worked. "Tam sent word the wardens pulled two more fae-blooded families out of River Ward last night. No charges. Just gone. And the criers are announcing that the Chancellor will address the city from the palace steps tomorrow. He's going to—" She stopped. Started again. "Everyone knows what he's going to say."

"That magic is killing the king."

"That magic is killing *everything*, and only he can save us from it." Wren shoved the bowl away, appetite gone. "It's mad. It's mad, and it's working. Tam says people who traded with river-fae all their lives are smashing their own windows where the glamour shows. People who owe their *children* to fae midwifery."

August got up and fetched the map.

They had taken to keeping it wrapped in oilcloth inside a hollowed loaf, on the theory that no witch-finder in history had ever searched a bread bin twice. They unwrapped it now and spread it on the table, and the covenant-script stirred faintly in the candlelight like fish under ice—the words rearranging themselves just enough to remind August that the thing was not paper, not really. It was a promise, wearing paper's shape.

"Look at it," August said. "Hesk said the map shows the circulation. Look where the lines run."

The map's rivers and roads were inked in ordinary brown, but threaded through them ran lines of covenant-script, and those lines did not follow geography. They followed *food*. The wheat routes. The waterways. The drove-roads the beekeepers took to the heath. Every artery by which the land fed the city pulsed with the faint gold shimmer of fae-oath, and every one of them, August saw anew, ran thinner than it had a month ago. Some had gone dark entirely.

"It's not attacking us," August said slowly, hearing the truth assemble itself as they spoke. "Nothing is attacking us. The covenant is failing, Wren—it's been failing for years, maybe decades—and everything it carried is stopping flowing. The sweetness. The growth. The king's health, the children, the harvests. We're a body going cold, and Veyre—" Their voice caught on the name, and they made themself finish. "Veyre is standing over the bed telling everyone the neighbor did it."

Wren stared at the thinning gold threads. Then, in a smaller voice than August had ever heard her use: "Emberfall's children first. That's what my father always said. The fae children guttered first because they lived closest to the flame." Her hand hovered over the map, not quite touching it. "But we're next. We were always next. He spent ten years trying to make people understand that, and now it's happening, and they're burning charm-sellers for it."

* * *

The Chancellor spoke from the palace steps at noon the next day, and August heard it from the tasting room, through a window open to a sky the color of dishwater.

They hadn't intended to listen. But Veyre's voice carried—of course it carried; the man had been born with a pulpit where other people had throats—and it came over the garden walls and through the killed roses and into the room where August stood among the king's untouched breakfast, and it was very simple, and that was the horror of it.

*"For too long, hidden hands have fed on this kingdom's suffering..."*

Below the window, Petch and the under-gardeners had stopped working to listen. Across the yard, scullery maids clustered in the doorway, faces upturned. Somewhere a crier repeated each sentence as it fell, so that the words arrived doubled, echoed, inevitable.

*"...and I say to you: the age of hiding is ended. Every charm, every hex, every whispered word of sorcery poisons our wells and blights our fields and lays its finger on the brow of your anointed king..."*

August looked down at the tray. Honeyed water, tasting of nothing. Bread whose warmth was already leaving it, the way warmth left everything now. Somewhere beneath their feet, beneath the foundations and the clay and the old buried bones of the city, a stone lay split down its heart, and everything the world used to be was leaking out of the crack.

*He knows.* The thought arrived with the certainty of a poison identified by aftertaste. Hesk had planted it weeks ago—*the Chancellor hunts the map's bearers, child, hunts them specifically, and ask yourself how he knows whom to hunt*—and August had been carrying the thought around like a splinter, unwilling to look at it straight. They looked at it now.

Veyre knew the covenant was failing. Had known, perhaps, for years—for decades, if Hesk's suspicions about the man's suspicious longevity held anything. And rather than mend it, he had chosen to harvest it. Fear was a crop, and it grew in any soil, and a frightened city needed a strong hand far more than it needed a healed stone. Let the fae die. Let the land go quiet. So long as the blame could be laid on magic, the laws against magic could grow harsher, and the harsher the laws, the safer a chancellor sat—who happened to be the last man in the kingdom with blood old enough to hold the stone, hoarding the possibility of it like everything his traitor ancestor had hoarded before him.

Magic wasn't killing the realm. Magic was the only surgeon available, and the Chancellor was arresting surgeons.

*"...and therefore, by my hand and the king's authority, a general edict..."*

August's hands had gone cold. Not with fear—or not only fear—but with the specific chill of scale, of standing at the edge of something too large. For three years their whole world had been sized to fit a pocket: a luck-charm here, a fever-charm there, kindnesses small enough to hide. Even the last weeks—the map, the names, the terrible arithmetic of it—had felt, in some cellar of the heart, like a private matter. My trouble. Our trouble.

But there was no private corner of this. The frost was in the roses and the king's blood and the children who had stopped growing and the brewer's mash and the well-water and the honey that had forgotten how to be sweet. The whole world was a dish going cold at the table, and August was the taster, and for the first time in their life the poison was not in one dish.

It was in the meal itself.

A knock at the tasting-room door—two taps, quick, then one. Wren's signal. August opened it and found her flushed from running, eyes bright and hard, with Tam's river-damp handkerchief knotted around her wrist.

"Tam found the third name," she said. "And August—" She caught her breath. "The eclipse. The solstice eclipse. It's not six weeks off. It's *nine days*. Whatever we're going to do at that stone, we do it in nine days, and the Chancellor's just declared martial law."

Behind her, out the window, Veyre's voice rolled on over the frozen gardens, smooth as poisoned honey, promising to save them all.
