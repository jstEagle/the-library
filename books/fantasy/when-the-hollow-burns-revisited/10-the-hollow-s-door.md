## 10. The Hollow's Door

The map had a spine.

Wren had carried her father's work in her hands since she was old enough to hold a quill, and she had never once thought of it as having a spine, but that was what Tam called it, crouched on the riverbank with the oilskin spread across his knees and his bandaged side leaking through the linen. "See here." His finger traced the road that wound up out of the city and into the hills. "Every route your father drew bends toward this line. Every name, every waypoint. It's not a map of places. It's a spine. You follow it the way you'd follow a body up a hill—head first."

"Cheerful," Wren said.

"I'm half river-folk. We're cheerful about corpses or we'd never get any work done."

August said nothing. They hadn't said much of anything for two days—not since the ambush on the descent from Tam's river-warden post, not since Hesk had turned back down the switchbacks with three witch-finders' hands closing around her arms and shouted at them to *run, you fools, run*, in the voice of a woman who had spent thirty years ordering kings to bed and expected to be obeyed even now. August had run. Wren had run. But Wren had watched August's face while they did it, and what she saw there was not fear. It was arithmetic. The same terrible sum Hesk had been doing aloud before she'd shoved them toward the high road: two names left on the map, one door, one eclipse, and no time to buy anything back.

Wren knew about leaving people behind. Her father had done it every time he walked out the door with his satchel and his maps, promising he'd be home by the thaw, by midsummer, by her next birthday. She had learned young that love could be measured in departures. She had promised herself she would never be the one who left.

And now here she was, climbing.

* * *

The hills above the city were wrong, and everyone pretending they weren't made it worse.

It was midsummer. Wren knew this because her father had died in midsummer, four years ago, and she marked the season the way other people marked saints' days. Midsummer in the hills meant bees drunk on clover, meant dust and thistle-down, meant the smell of hot pine resin bleeding from the trees. What it meant now was frost. Frost in silver feathers along the bracken. Frost furring the sheep they passed—the living ones huddled in gray clumps, breathing steam, and two others lying stiff in the ditch with their eyes open, which Tam covered with his cloak without being asked.

"The winter's climbing," August said quietly. They had stopped at a spring to drink, and the spring had a skin of ice on it thin as window-glass. August broke it with one finger and held the fragment up to the light. "It started in the low quarters. Now it's up here. It's not spreading like weather spreads. It's spreading like—" They hesitated.

"Like something dying," Tam finished. "From the feet up."

Nobody argued. There wasn't anything to argue with. For three days the sky over the city behind them had been the color of dishwater, and ahead of them, when the wind gusted, Wren kept catching glimpses of something else—a shimmer above the northern peaks, like heat-haze in reverse, like light seen through the wrong end of a spyglass. Emberfall. The fae realm, pressing close against the skin of the world because the thing holding the worlds apart was failing.

Her father had spent eleven years looking for the Hollow. Eleven years of taverns and archives and bribed clerks and cold camps, and he had died in a stairwell with the answer pressed into a stranger's hands, and Wren had hated that stranger for a full week before she'd understood that the stranger was just the last pair of hands available. The map hadn't chosen August because August was special. It had chosen August because August was *there*, and willing, and apparently had oath-blood in their veins, whatever that meant, and because the dead do not get to be picky.

She still caught herself hating them sometimes. Usually at dawn. Usually when August was asleep sitting up against a tree with their chin on their chest, looking so ordinary—a food-taster, a person built entirely out of smallness—and usually the hate lasted until August woke, and looked at her, and asked if she wanted the last of the bread without making it a big generous gesture, just handed it over like it was nothing, like sharing was simply what you did. Then the hate got embarrassed and went away for another day.

"You're staring," August said now, without opening their eyes.

"I'm deciding whether to hate you today."

"How's the tally?"

"Holding steady. Don't push it."

Tam snorted from where he was re-wrapping his wound, and winced, and swore in river-cant, which Wren was learning meant roughly the same things swearing meant everywhere else.

They climbed.

* * *

They found the door on the third evening, almost by accident, which Wren supposed was how all the important doors worked.

The map's spine ended at a fold in the hills—a combe, green once and white now, ringed with standing stones so old the frost had worn their carvings down to suggestions. At the combe's head, where a waterfall should have been, there was instead a wall of living rock, smooth and dark and utterly blank, except that it wasn't blank at all. As the light failed, Wren began to see that the stone was covered in lines—thin channels cut into the rock in patterns that spiraled inward toward a seam down the center, a vertical crack barely wide enough to slide a knife into.

"It's a tongue," August said.

Wren looked at them. "What?"

"A taste-map." August stepped closer, and Wren watched them do the thing they did—the taster's stillness, the total attention, the way the rest of the world seemed to politely step back. "These channels. They're not decoration. Look at the way they branch—like the veins in a leaf, like the cracks in drying glaze. Someone carved this so it could be *read*. Not with eyes." August glanced back at her, and there was something almost apologetic in their face. "With the mouth."

"Oh, that's foul," Wren said. "We're going to lick the haunted rock."

"We're going to lick the haunted rock," August agreed gravely, "and I'm going to try very hard not to die, and you're going to stand there judging me, and that's the plan."

"That's the whole plan?"

"The good plans are short. It's the long ones that go wrong."

Tam laughed, then pressed a hand to his bandage. "He's right. River rule: the shortest prayer is the one the water hears."

August stood before the stone for a long moment. The wind came down the combe and smelled, impossibly, of woodsmoke and honey and something green and growing that had no business existing anywhere within fifty miles of this frozen hillside. Wren watched August breathe it in, watched their lips part slightly, the way they went when they were cataloging an aftertaste.

Then August leaned forward and touched their tongue to the lowest channel of the stone.

Nothing happened. For three heartbeats, nothing happened, and Wren felt the disappointment arrive like a stone dropped into her stomach—of course, of course it wouldn't work, of course the blood in August's veins was the wrong blood, her father's map leading them all up a mountain to lick a—

The channel filled with light.

Not firelight, not faerie-glow from any story Wren had been told as a child. It was a slow amber seep, like honey pressed from the rock itself, running upward against gravity along the carved veins, branching, splitting, finding its tributaries, and each channel it filled gave off a scent. Wren's eyes watered. She smelled her mother's kitchen—bread and burnt sugar and the specific smoke of the tallow candle her mother had always let gutter too long. She smelled river-mud and her father's coat. She smelled the hospice where Hesk had hidden, carbolic soap and lavender. She smelled things she had no memory of and grieved anyway: a harvest festival, someone's wedding, a child's hair.

"What is it doing?" she whispered.

"Remembering," August said. Their voice had gone strange and soft. "Or asking me to." They moved to the second channel, and licked it, and Wren saw their whole body flinch—but they swallowed whatever they tasted, and the channel lit, and the amber climbed higher. Third channel. Fourth. August worked their way up the stone like a reader working down a page, and with each line their face changed—grief, recognition, disgust, laughter, once, sudden and startled, as though the stone had told them a joke eleven hundred years old. Sweat stood on their forehead in the freezing air. By the tenth channel their hands were shaking, and Wren stepped forward without deciding to, and put her hand flat between August's shoulder blades, and left it there.

"I've got you," she said. "Keep reading."

"You don't know what it's asking."

"No. But I know you're answering. So keep answering."

August kept answering. Channel after channel, taste after taste, and the amber light rose up the stone like sap rising in spring, and the scents came faster now, a flood, a lifetime, a dozen lifetimes, until Wren couldn't tell which memories were hers and which belonged to the stone and which, maybe, belonged to August, who had spent three years swallowing poison in small doses for strangers and had never once told anyone what that cost.

At the top of the stone, at the seam, August paused. The last channel was different—it forked, and one branch ran off into blank rock and faded, unfinished, and the other ran straight down into the crack.

"This one's broken," August said. "Half the taste is missing."

"Maybe that's the point," Tam said quietly. He had come to stand beside them, gray-faced, holding his side. "A covenant takes two peoples. Maybe the stone only ever answered half a question."

August looked at the forked channel for a long time. Then they bent, and tasted both branches—one, and then the other—and Wren heard them make a small sound, not quite a word, and the entire face of the cliff lit from within like a lantern.

The seam opened.

* * *

Inside, the air was warm.

That was the first thing, and it undid Wren more than anything else—the warmth, after weeks of cold, rising up out of the earth like breath, smelling of loam and deep water and summer that had never gotten the news. Steps led down into the hill, cut smooth and worn hollow in their centers by feet older than the city. Amber light followed them down the walls, seeping from the same vein-work that had lit the door, and Wren walked with her hand on the wall and her heart going like a drum and the ridiculous, childish certainty that they were walking down a throat.

Tam lit no torch. There was nothing to light it with, and besides, the walls glowed. "Stairs," he murmured, counting under his breath out of some river habit. "Forty. Sixty. Ninety." At a hundred and twenty the stairs ended and the passage opened, and Wren stepped past August into the chamber beyond, and forgot, for a moment, how to breathe.

The cavern was vast—a dome of rock ribbed with veins of amber light, so that overhead it looked less like stone than like the underside of a leaf held up to the sun. And at its center, on a floor of black glass that mirrored everything, stood the covenant-stone.

Wren had imagined it a hundred times, in the years since her father first whispered the word *Hollow* over his maps. She had imagined a monolith, a throne, a tower of light. It was none of those things. It was a stone the height of two men, rough-hewn, unremarkable, the kind of boulder a farmer would curse at and plow around. Except that it was split. Split clean down its heart, from crown to root, the two halves leaning apart like a book fallen open, and the gap between them was dark—not empty-dark, but *hungry*-dark, a darkness that pulled at the eye and made Wren's teeth ache and her scars itch, a darkness she understood, in her bones, was the reason for the frost on the sheep and the ice on the spring and the children of light guttering in a realm she'd never seen.

The mirror-floor showed it all doubled: the split stone, and above it, reflected in the black glass, something that was not quite the ceiling. A flicker. A shimmer of green and gold, like a forest seen underwater, like a sky that belonged to somewhere else. Emberfall, pressing close. Dying close.

"Well," Tam said softly. "There it is."

"There it is," Wren echoed, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she didn't care, because her father had walked eleven years to stand where she was standing and had died one stairwell short, and somebody ought to say it out loud in his place. "Papa. We found it. You found it. I just—" She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "I just did the walking."

August stood beside her and didn't say anything, which was, Wren was learning, one of the things August was actually good at. They just stood there in the amber light, small and ordinary and sworn to something enormous, until Wren had finished crying the way you finish a chore—thoroughly, and with resentment, and glad when it's done.

It was August who noticed it first. Of course it was.

"The halves aren't just apart," they said slowly. They had drifted closer to the stone, close enough to touch, and were studying the split faces with the taster's narrowed eyes. "Look at the edges. They're not chipped. They're not broken the way rock breaks." They reached out—hesitated—looked back at Wren and Tam as if asking permission, which nobody had ever done before, least of all August, and then laid their palm flat against the nearer half.

Their face went white.

"August—"

"They're *worn*," August said. "Both faces. Smooth, like—" They swallowed. "Like the inside of a cup. Like something has been poured out of here, a little at a time, for years. For decades." They lifted their hand and stared at their palm as though it might have answers written on it. "This didn't crack on its own. And it hasn't just been failing. It's been *drunk from*."

The darkness between the halves breathed. Wren felt it move past her, cold, deliberate, patient, and somewhere far above them, faint through miles of frozen hill, she heard the wind change.

Tam's hand went to his knife. "Then whoever's been drinking," he said, "knows we've found the cellar."

Behind them, far up the stairwell, the amber lights along the walls began, one by one, to go out.
