## 12. The Traitor's Heir

Wren had imagined this moment a hundred times on the road: the last name on her father's map finally legible, the final piece of his life's work laid open like a gutted fish. In most of those imaginings, the name belonged to a witch-finder captain she could put a knife to, or a fat guildmaster with a soft throat, or some gray functionary of the Chancellor's whose death would mean something.

In none of them had the name been *Veyre's own*.

She heard August say it aloud — "That's him. That's the Chancellor" — and the words went through her the way a cold current goes through wading boots, sudden and total, up to the knee and rising.

They were still at the water's edge below the Hollow's threshold, in that drowned half-light where the river ran slow and green-gold and the fae realm bled through the stones. Tam stood shin-deep in the shallows, which Wren had learned was simply where Tam preferred to stand, as though solid ground were a rumor they'd declined to confirm. Hesk was not there. Hesk was two hills away buying bread and lies for their passage east, and Wren thought, absurdly, *she should be here for this*, because Hesk was the only one among them who had ever treated the Chancellor as anything other than weather.

"Say it again," Wren said. "Slowly."

Tam looked at her with that terrible patience river-folk have, the patience of someone who has watched things float past for centuries and learned that grabbing at them is how you drown. "The map names its oath-kin by bloodline," they said. "August carries the taster's line. I carry the warden's line. You—" a small incline of the head, respectful, "—carry the courier's. And the fifth line, the one the covenant swore to watch above all others, is the king's line. The oath-keeper's line. Except it stopped being that a long time ago."

"The betraying king," August said quietly. They were sitting on a flat stone with the map across their knees, and their hands were very still, the way they always went still when something mattered. Wren had catalogued that stillness over weeks of travel: stillness before bad news, stillness before courage. "The one who hoarded the stone."

"The same," said Tam. "The blood didn't stop when he did. It thinned and it hid and it married sensibly, and it climbed, because that blood has always known how to climb. Four generations back it took the name Veyre."

Wren laughed. She couldn't help it; it came out of her like something escaping a burning house. "No."

"Yes," Tam said, gently, which made it worse.

"No. He *outlawed* magic. He burns children in the market square for selling luck-charms—"

"And every night," Tam said, "he goes down beneath his own palace, into the old foundations, to a splinter of the covenant-stone his ancestor stole away and hid, and he puts his hands on it, and he drinks." The river lapped at their ankles. Somewhere overhead, a bird gave up on singing mid-note; even the birds were leaving as the cold deepened. "The stone doesn't heal, exactly. But it holds. It holds grief, it holds time, it holds a body together long past its natural hour. He has been holding himself together with it for forty years, Wren. Every year of his life past sixty is a year he took from the covenant. Every candle he extends is a child of Emberfall guttering out."

The map trembled. Wren realized it wasn't trembling on its own; August's hands were shaking, just slightly, and the parchment shook with them.

"That's why the winter started when it did," August said. Their voice was flat, careful, a taster identifying a poison by aftertaste. "It's not just the stone cracking. He's been sipping from a wound for decades, keeping it open. Keeping it from sealing itself, because if it sealed—"

"He dies," Wren said.

"He dies," Tam agreed. "And so he cannot allow the covenant to be reforged. Not by oath-kin, not by anyone. He needs the wound, and he will salt it forever rather than let it close."

* * *

Wren turned and walked up the bank because standing still had become unbearable, and she paced the length of the shingle twice, boots crunching on frost-killed reeds, before the words found their way out of her.

"My father spent eleven years looking for the Hollow." Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, too calm. "Eleven years. Do you know what that costs a courier? The routes he gave up. The favors he burned. He sold our house. He sold my mother's rings, after she died, and he wept doing it, and then he kept going, because he believed — he *believed*, the stubborn, hopeless man — that if he could find where the stone lay, someone could fix what was broken, and the children in Emberfall would stop dying, and the winter would end, and his life would have meant something other than carrying other people's letters." She stopped. Turned. "And the whole time, the man who broke it was sitting in a marble chair above us all, drinking the wound like wine, writing laws against magic so no one would ever come looking near his cellar."

"I know," August said.

"Do you?" Wren crossed back toward them, and her hand had found her knife without asking her permission, the way it sometimes did. "Because here is what I want, August. I want you to say it plainly, so I know we understand each other. The name at the bottom of your map is the name of a man who killed my father. Not with a knife. With eleven years of hope, drained slow. And tomorrow or the day after or however long it takes Hesk to get back, we are going to walk down off these hills and finish this, and I want to know: when we see Aldous Veyre, are we reforging a covenant, or are we killing a man?"

The silence stretched. The river filled it, that low green murmur that Wren had decided she hated, because it never took sides.

"Wren," August began.

"Don't *Wren* me in that kitchen voice. Don't tell me about arithmetic. I've watched you do the arithmetic all week — Hesk against the solstice, Tam's blood against the miles. I can do sums too. Here's mine." She held up her hand and folded fingers down as she spoke. "One Chancellor. One splinter of stolen stone. One knife. Against that—" she gestured at the sky, at the bruised color pressing down over the hills, at the light in the west that flickered wrong, like a lamp running out of oil, "—against everything else. It's cheap, August. He is *cheap*. We could pay for everything he's done with one evening's work and still make the eclipse."

"You couldn't," Tam said.

"Excuse me?"

"You couldn't pay for it with his death," Tam said, "because his death wouldn't buy anything. That's the part nobody wants to hear. Kill Veyre tonight, and the splinter under the palace goes dark, and the stone under the Hollow is still split, and the winter is still falling, and the eclipse still comes, and there are still three of you and one rite and no more time than there was this morning. His death costs you nothing and buys you nothing. It is not even a trade. It is just a fire to warm yourself by while the house burns."

"Then let me be warm," Wren snapped, "just once, before I freeze."

* * *

August hadn't moved from the flat stone. Wren realized, with the particular irritation one feels at people who refuse to be provoked, that August had been listening to all of it with that stillness, turning the words over the way they turned a suspect dish over, checking the underside.

"Can I tell you about the first poison I ever swallowed?" August said.

"What?"

"It was when I was fourteen. Old Taster Merrin — my predecessor, my teacher, though teacher makes it sound kinder than it was — he puts a cup in front of me and says, *almond, but not quite.* And it wasn't. Bitter almond cut with something sweeter, and underneath it all a taste like pennies, and I knew — I knew before he told me — that it was meant to kill somebody important. And he watched me drink it, and I drank it, because that was the job, because a taster who hesitates is a taster who hangs. And afterward he told me the person it was meant for. A magistrate. A bad one. The kind who took bribes to look away while grain merchants starved whole streets." August looked up, and their eyes in the green river-light were very tired. "I asked him the same question you're asking me. Why do we save men like that? And he said — and I hated him for years for saying it — *the tongue doesn't get to choose whose food keeps it alive.*"

"That's not an answer," Wren said. "That's a shrug with pretensions."

"It took me ten years to understand it, so I'll forgive you the delay." A ghost of the gentle humor moved through August's face and was gone. "Here's what I've got instead. You're right. Everything you said is right. He killed your father the way frost kills — impersonally, thoroughly, without ever learning his name. He deserves every ugly thing you want to do to him, and I will not pretend otherwise, and I will not insult you by telling you to rise above it. But, Wren — the covenant doesn't need him dead. It needs him *unmade*. There's a difference, and it matters, because the splinter under the palace isn't just his life. It's a piece of the stone. A piece of the oath. If we kill him and the splinter dies with him, we forge a covenant with a hole in its heart, and holes are how the first betrayal happened in the first place."

"The stone has to be whole," Tam said quietly. "All of it. Even the parts that were used against it."

"So what," Wren said, and her voice cracked despite everything, "we take the splinter *back*? From under his palace? From behind whatever guards a man posts around the thing keeping him alive?" She laughed again, harsher this time. "You're proposing a burglary of the Lord Chancellor's basement. That's your grand alternative to murder."

"Yes," August said simply. "More or less."

"And if he's there? If we break in and he's standing over it with his hands on it, drinking his extra years, and there's a moment — there's always a moment, August, I've lived enough of them to know — when it's him or the plan? When stopping him and saving the mission are different things? What does your tongue choose then?"

August was quiet for a long time. The river murmured. High above, through the crack in the hills where the Hollow opened its mouth, the faint gold light of Emberfall pulsed once, weakly, like something breathing in its sleep.

"I don't know," August said at last, and Wren blinked, because she had expected philosophy and gotten honesty, and honesty was harder to fight. "I have swallowed a great many things in my life and pretended to be unmoved by all of them. I don't know what I'll choose, in that room, with him in front of me and your father's map in my coat. I hope I choose the covenant. I believe I will. But I won't lie to you on a riverbank and promise a certainty I don't carry." They folded the map along its old creases, carefully, the way one handles something already broken once. "What I can promise is this: if it comes to the choice, I won't make it alone. Whatever happens in that room — you'll be beside me, and you'll have your say, and if I reach for the covenant when justice is owed, you may call me a coward to my face and I will stand there and take it."

Wren looked at them for a long moment. The little taster, who had survived three years of outlawed magic by being small and deniable, offering to share the one decision Wren most wanted to make alone.

"That's a terrible bargain," she said. "You're giving me veto power over vengeance."

"I'm giving you company in it," August said. "Which is worse."

Despite herself — genuinely despite herself, and she would deny it later — Wren felt something in her chest loosen by a single degree. Not forgiveness. Nowhere near forgiveness. But the rage had somewhere to sit now, instead of rattling around inside her like coins in a dead man's pocket.

"Fine," she said. "Company. But if he begs, I get to enjoy it."

"Noted," said August, "and filed under *deniable kindnesses.*"

Tam, who had been standing in the shallows through all of it like a heron with opinions, finally spoke again. "There's something else," they said, and the tone of it — the *tone* of it, that low grave note Wren had learned meant the water had gone deeper than expected — turned them both around.

Tam had bent and lifted a handful of river-water, and in the cup of their palm the liquid gleamed with that same green-gold light as the pool beneath the Hollow, and it was moving. Shivering. Forming letters, or trying to.

"The river carries what the stone leaks," Tam said. "Grief, mostly. Lately, warnings." They raised their palm so the light caught it, and Wren leaned close, and the letters resolved — human letters, court-hand, the crisp script of a man who signed death warrants before breakfast.

A summons. Bearing the Privy Seal of the Lord Chancellor.

"*August Amberdale, taster of His Majesty's household,*" Tam read, "*is commanded to appear before the court at the Palace of Vell, within three days, to answer questions touching upon sedition, theft of crown property, and consorting with persons attainted of witchcraft.*" They lowered their hand and let the water run through their fingers. The light winked out. "He knows the name. He knows the bloodline. He's known since the banquet."

Wren's knife was in her hand again. She honestly hadn't felt it move.

"He's not summoning you to answer questions," she said.

"No," August agreed softly. They were looking east, toward the city, toward the marble chair and the cellar beneath it, and their face had gone into that final stillness, the one that came before courage. "He's setting a table."

And Wren understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the winter, that August was already deciding what to bring.
