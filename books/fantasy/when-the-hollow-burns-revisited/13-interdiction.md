## 13. Interdiction

The descent from the hills should have been the easy part.

Going up, every step had been a negotiation with the mountain — scree that slid like bad manners, wind that found the gap between collar and neck and made itself at home. Coming down, gravity was at least on our side. I told myself that. I have found that when you start negotiating with gravity for allies, you are already losing an argument you didn't know you'd entered.

Hesk walked ahead of me, her physician's satchel thumping against her hip, and every fifty paces she stopped to look at something growing out of the rocks — lichen, mostly, gray and unambitious — and pronounced judgment upon it.

"Reindeer moss," she said. "Edible. Barely. Tastes like forgiveness that's gone off."

"Everything tastes like something to you," Tam said from behind, leaning on his warden's staff as though it were an old friend and he an old friend of its family.

"Everything *is* something," Hesk said. "That's rather the whole trouble with the world."

Wren brought up the rear, which was where she had insisted on walking since the hospice, and where she had said perhaps forty words in two days, all of them functional. *Pass me the rope. The map says south. Don't step there.* She watched the way back the way you watch a door when you're expecting bad news through it.

I was watching the sky instead. Midsummer was three days off. The sun sat fat and golden above the western ridgeline, and around its edges — only when I wasn't looking directly, only in the corner of the eye — the light had begun to flicker. Not like cloud. Like a candle in a draft. Emberfall showing through the seams of the world, thin as coin gold beaten too far.

The realm behind the sky was guttering, and we were its errand boys.

"August." Hesk had stopped again. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes very calm and your hands go very still and everyone pretends not to notice because you're the one holding the map." She held out her palm. "Give it here. My eyes are older but they're better at reading a man's handwriting, and whoever drew this wrote like a drunk spider."

I passed her the oilskin. We crouched in the lee of a boulder, and she spread it flat while Tam stood over us, dripping hill-water from his boots onto the parchment and apologizing for it in three languages, one of which I was fairly sure wasn't human.

The map's covenant-script had opened fully to me now, oath-blood answering oath-ink, and what it showed made my stomach do the small cold drop I usually associated with an unfamiliar aftertaste. A spine of marked stones ran from the city to the Hollow's mouth, and the descent route wound along a fold of hills called — in a hand much newer than the rest — the Shepherd's Stair. Narrow switchbacks. Good cover. One road down.

"One road down," Hesk murmured, tapping it. "Which means anyone who wants us doesn't need to find us. They only need to wait."

"You're a very cheering companion," Tam said.

"I'm a physician. Cheer is outside my practice."

Wren dropped to a crouch beside us without a sound, which she managed to do even on gravel, a talent I intended to ask about someday if we all lived long enough for pleasant conversation. "Smoke," she said.

We all looked at her.

"Back along the ridge. Two fires, maybe three hours old. Someone camped on the high ground overnight." Her jaw was set in that line I was learning meant *I am not afraid, I am simply going to be extremely angry about whatever happens next.* "Witch-findings don't make cooking fires. They make signal ones."

* * *

We came off the Shepherd's Stair in the last hour of daylight, moving quick and quiet, and I want it recorded that we were quiet. Tam muffled his staff with a strip of his own sleeve. Hesk tied her satchel shut so nothing clinked. Even Wren's knives were silent, which somehow frightened me more than noise would have.

The hills opened below into a shallow bowl of gorse and bracken, and beyond it the river valley, and beyond that — faint with distance and evening haze — the roofs of the city, looking terribly small and terribly far, the way home does when home has started hunting you.

And across the bowl, in the bracken where the shadows pooled deepest, something moved that was not wind.

"Wren," I breathed.

"I see it."

What happened next happened in the order that things happen when people who are not soldiers meet people who very much are: too fast, badly, and all at once.

A horn sounded — one flat note, no flourish, the note of a man doing his job — and the bracken stood up. That is the only way I can describe it. Forty yards of harmless brown scrub resolved into cloaked figures with iron-banded staves and the white hoods of the Chancellor's witch-finders, and the evening went from quiet to ringing.

"*Down!*" Hesk shouted, and then she did the last thing I expected and the first thing, thinking back, I should have known she would do.

She stepped forward.

Not away. Forward, into the open bowl, arms wide, satchel swinging, an old woman with white braids and a leper's bell still hooked at her belt presenting herself to forty yards of drawn steel like a hostess greeting guests who've arrived early.

"Gentlemen!" she called, in the voice I'd heard her use once on a dying man who wanted to argue about dosage. "You'll want me alive. I'm Mother Hesk, formerly physician to the crown, and I can tell you precisely which of your captain's men has been visiting the wrong sort of apothecary for his stomach complaints, and precisely how much he's been paying."

The line of hoods wavered. A big man with a captain's knot at his shoulder pushed forward, uncertain, greedy. Greed, I have learned at a hundred breakfast tables, is the most reliable poison of all; it always takes.

"Hesk," Wren snarled, reaching for her. "What are you—"

"Hesk, *no*—" But I was already being dragged, and Tam was already staggering, because this was the second thing that happened all at once:

A crossbow bolt came out of the gorse to our left, low and fast, and took Tam through the side.

He made no cry. That was the river in him, I think — water takes wounds the way water takes stones, with a kind of terrible acceptance. He folded against his staff, and I caught him, and we went down together into the bracken, and his blood on my hands was warm and *wrong*, carrying a taste I could feel without my tongue, iron and riverweed and something older underneath, something like the smell of rain on stone in a country I have never visited.

"Go," he said. It came out mostly air. "The stair forks ahead. Left fork is the false path — it looks right, it isn't, it dead-ends at a fall. Right fork is ugly but true. Take the ugly truth, taster. It's the only kind worth taking."

"I'm not leaving you in a field full of witch-finders."

"You're leaving me," he said, and his half-fae eyes caught the strange flickering light, "with the best company I could ask for, which is a job to do. Get to the stone. Wren — *Wren* — get him up the stair."

Above us, Hesk's voice rang clear over the bowl, buying each syllable with its weight in gold: "*—and I'll want that in writing, Captain, before I say another word—*"

Wren got her shoulder under Tam's arm on one side and mine under the other, and we ran.

* * *

The fork came sooner than the map promised, the way forks do. Left: a broad, gentle path sloping confidently down through the trees, the kind of path that gets people killed by agreeing with them. Right: a scramble of root and rock, ugly as sin, dropping steeply into dark.

"Left's faster," Wren said.

"Tam said left lies."

"Tam also said *go*, and we did, and I'd like it noted I hate both halves of that sentence." She shifted his weight — he was walking now, barely, one boot dragging, his breath coming wet at the edges, and every wet breath put a new taste of dread on my tongue that I couldn't spit out. "Right, then. Ugly truth."

We took it, and the ugly truth did exactly what ugly truths do: it hurt the whole way down. Roots grabbed ankles. Rocks rolled. Twice Tam's legs simply stopped being consulted, and twice we lowered him to sit on a ledge while I pressed cloth to his side and felt, through my palms, how much of him was leaving.

He grinned at me the second time. River-wardens' grins are a specific genre: unhurried, slightly damp, entirely unconvinced by urgency.

"You're counting," he said.

"What?"

"Your fingers. On the dressing. You count when you're working out sums nobody wants done." He tapped my wrist, once, gentle. "Physician's girl back at the hospice told me. Said you tally everything like dishes at a table. Cost and portion."

"I don't—"

"You're doing it now. Hesk for the stone. Me for the hours. You're weighing whether we're affordable." His grin faded into something kinder and much worse. "Stop it. It's not arithmetic. It never is. You'll do the sums your whole life and the answer will always be *everything costs more than you have*. The trick is paying anyway."

"Tam—"

"Eclipse is day after tomorrow," he said. "Spend me well."

Below us, faint but unmistakable, a horn sounded again. Then a second. Then, from somewhere behind and above — back up the stair, on our trail, closer than any horn had a right to be — a third.

They hadn't gotten Hesk's surrender and moved on. They'd gotten Hesk's stall and *followed*.

* * *

We reached the valley floor after full dark, and I will not pretend I remember it clearly. Memory, when it's working hard, edits. What stayed with me: the river's smell, sudden and merciful. Wren swearing steadily under her breath in her father's courier cant, a whole vocabulary of profanity arranged by severity. And Tam's weight going from heavy to heavier, the difference between a man who is wounded and a man who is failing, which I knew in my hands the way I know almond on the back of the tongue.

There was a warden's hut at the river bend — low, turf-roofed, half-swallowed by ivy. Wren kicked the door; inside, a hearth, a boat, and a stack of oiled canvas. We got Tam down by the cold fireplace, and I did what I could, which was more than most and less than enough. I packed the wound. I made him drink willow-bark and something Hesk had left in my pack labeled, in her handwriting, *for bleeding, despair, and stubbornness — dose generously.*

His eyes found mine in the firelight Wren coaxed from the hearth.

"They'll bring her to the city," he said. "The Chancellor keeps his prisoners close. He'll want her to open doors, and she won't, and then—" He stopped, because physicians' bluntness is one thing and kindness is another, and he was choosing kindness. "You have a choice, taster. It's a bad one. They always are."

"I know."

"Say it anyway. Choices get smaller when you keep them in the dark."

So I said it, and saying it made it real, which is the cruelty of language: "If we turn back tonight, three of us against the finder-capt's whole company, we might take Hesk back before they reach the walls. And we miss the eclipse, and the covenant fails, and everyone in two worlds pays for it forever. If we go on, Hesk spends whatever she has in a Chancellor's cell, alone, and I live with it, and the worlds live."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the river kept up its patient argument with the stones.

"That's not the choice," Wren said quietly.

We both looked at her. She was sitting with her knees drawn up, knife across her lap, sharpening it in slow strokes, and she wouldn't look up from the blade.

"It's not *your* choice," she said. "It's hers. She bought us out on that hill. You think she did it so you could un-spend her? You think she stood in front of forty hoods and offered up her medical gossip like a market crier so we'd waste it?" The whetstone paused. "My father taught me: when someone gives you their time, you owe them the *finish*, not the refund. She finished her part. Ours is the stone."

"And Tam?"

"Tam," said Tam, from the floor, "would like everyone to stop discussing him in the third person while he's lying here perfectly able to hear." A breath, wet but steadier than before. "She's right. Both of them. Go on. Leave me the boat and the bitter tea. Wardens die slow, it's practically our charter."

Nobody laughed. It was that kind of joke, the bravest kind, and it deserved better.

I sat down on the hearthstone and did the thing I do, the only thing I have ever known how to do: I tasted the moment. Fear, yes, thick as burnt oil. Grief for Hesk, sharp and green, like the rind of a fruit I'd never allowed myself to buy. Underneath, shamefully, unmistakably, purpose — the clean hot taste of a road with one direction, which is the flavor of every dish you'd order again.

Arithmetic. Tam had said stop. But some sums you don't solve; you just carry them, the way I carried the map, the way Hesk carried her satchel, the way Wren carried her father folded up in oilskin.

"We leave before dawn," I heard myself say. "Boat downstream to the weir, then east on foot. Hollow's mouth by tomorrow's dusk. We scout it in the dark, and when the eclipse comes—" My voice did something embarrassing. I let it. "When the eclipse comes, we're standing there."

Wren nodded once and went back to her knife. Tam closed his eyes, breathing.

And that was when the dogs started.

Deep-throated, many, coming down the near slope with the particular music I'd learned to dread in three years of listening for it — the sound of iron-banded men who had found a trail and liked it. Horns answered horns across the valley. Torches bloomed along the ridgeline like a rash.

"Not possible," Wren said, on her feet. "We broke the scent at the water. Nobody tracks that fast unless—" She stopped. I saw her arrive at it the same moment I did, and I watched her face go through fury and land somewhere worse, somewhere quiet.

"Unless they were never tracking us," I said. "Unless we were never the prize."

The Chancellor didn't need to hunt the map's bearers to the Hollow. He needed only to herd them — let Hesk buy us loose, let us run, and run us straight down the one road to the one door only my blood could open. Veyre had been playing ferryman the whole time, and we had paid our fare in the only coins we had.

Torches came down the valley on both flanks now, unhurried, converging on the warden's hut with the confidence of men closing a purse-string. Through the smoke-hole, above the black hills, the sky flickered — Emberfall's dying light showing through the seam, guttering, guttering.

Wren looked at me across the fire, knife bare in her hand, and asked the question with her eyes.

But it wasn't hers to answer, and we both knew whose it was. The torches reached the bend in the river, and the lead rider raised a white hood against the night, and I stood up from the hearthstone with my heart beating double-time — one beat for this world, and, for the first time, one I could swear I felt answer from the other —

and I walked out the door with my empty hands held high, and said, "Take me to the Lord Chancellor. He wants an oath-blood to open the Hollow. I'm the only one left."
