## 14. The Chancellor's Bargain

The cell was better than I expected, which is the sort of sentence that should worry a person.

It had a window—narrow, barred, set high enough that I could see only sky and the top of the palace wall, but still. A window. There was a cot with an actual straw mattress instead of bare boards, and a bucket that had been emptied recently, and someone had left me a blanket that smelled faintly of lavender, as though captivity came with housekeeping standards.

I lay on the cot and took inventory of myself the way I'd once taken inventory of poisons: what hurts, how much, where exactly. Ribs bruised from the ambush, not broken. Wrists raw from rope, now replaced by iron cuffs that at least allowed my hands to reach each other. A cut above my ear that had been bandaged by someone who knew their work, which told me Hesk's capture hadn't been entirely wasted—the witch-finders' surgeon had learned *something* from her over the years, even if he'd helped hunt her.

And underneath all of it, humming like a struck bell that wouldn't quite stop ringing, was the grief.

Tam's wound. Hesk gone into whatever darkness they kept captured oath-kin in. Wren somewhere out there in the blizzard, alive or not, free or not—I didn't know, and not knowing sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Three days, I judged, since the ambush. Three days of bread and water brought twice daily by a guard who wouldn't meet my eyes. Three days of listening to the tower's sounds the way I listened to kitchens: the shift change at dawn, the squeak of one particular boot on the stair, the way the wind changed its mind about direction around midday. A taster learns to read rooms through sound. It turns out cells can be read the same way.

On the fourth morning, the boot on the stair belonged to no ordinary guard.

* * *

Lord Chancellor Veyre did not knock. He had the door unlocked from outside and swung it open himself, which for a man of his station was either a courtesy or a message, and I suspected both.

He looked older than his portraits. That surprised me. Portraits flatter everyone, but they usually flatter toward power—broader shoulders, firmer jaw. Veyre's portraits gave him a face like a drawn blade. In person, the blade had been left out in weather. His skin had the gray undertone I'd learned to read in the king's courtiers, the color of people who were being slowly spent by something. His eyes, though. His eyes were sharp as the day they'd been forged, and they went to my wrists first, then my face, then—lingering—to my hands.

Taster's hands. Everyone always looked at them eventually.

"August Amberdale," he said. Not a question. He knew my name the way I knew the aftertaste of arsenic: thoroughly, professionally, and with a certain grim respect.

"Lord Chancellor." I sat up on the cot. My cuffs clinked, and I made no apology for it. "You'll forgive me if I don't curtsy. The irons complicate the footwork."

Something flickered across his face—not quite amusement, but the ghost of a man who might once have laughed. "You're composed."

"I've been poisoned for a living for three years. Composure is the job description." I tilted my head. "Though I confess this particular posting wasn't in my expectations. Usually when I'm summoned to a lord, there's food involved."

"There will be food," Veyre said, and pulled a chair from the corner—he'd had one placed there, I realized; this meeting had been staged down to the furniture—and sat facing me, close enough that our knees nearly touched. Close enough to smell him. And under the cedar oil and the wool and the faint mineral tang of scrying-glass residue, I caught it.

A taste in the air. Old, sweetish, wrong. The same note I'd found in the pool beneath the Hollow, the same note that hung around the cracked covenant-stone like breath around a dying fire.

He'd been drinking from the stone. For years. Decades, maybe. Tam had said the map's final name was Veyre's own, and I hadn't wanted to believe it, because believing it meant the man who outlawed magic had been mainlining the deepest magic in the world while children burned in market squares for selling luck-charms.

But smells don't lie. Tastes don't lie. It was the first honest thing about him.

"You have something I want," he said.

"The map's in pieces, and half of them are memorized by people you haven't caught."

"I know precisely where your friends are." He said it mildly, folding his hands. Too mildly. A threat dressed as small talk. "Wren Okafor is sheltering with the hospice faithful in the tannery district. Your river-warden is fevered but mending—their kind mend quickly, even half-blooded. And Mother Hesk is in a cell much less comfortable than this one, two floors below, though she'd never complain. She's too proud. Always was."

"Always was," I repeated. "You speak like someone who knew her."

"I knew everyone worth knowing, back when knowing people meant something other than cataloging them for arrest." For a moment—one moment, brief as a candle guttering—something tired moved behind his eyes. Then it was gone, shuttered, and the Chancellor sat where the man had been. "Here is my offer, taster, and I'll make it only once, so attend."

He laid it out with the precision of a man reading terms of trade.

The map. Freedom—for me, and for Wren, and for Tam, and even for Hesk, pardons signed and sealed, their names scrubbed from every list. In exchange: August Amberdale would walk through the door of the Hollow, because only oath-blood could open it, and would stand aside while Lord Chancellor Veyre reforged the covenant in his own name, on his own terms, with himself as its anchor.

"And the fae?" I asked. Because that was the question, wasn't it. Everything else was furniture.

"They will be permitted to remain," he said, "in Emberfall. Behind the stone. Sealed, bounded, bound. They will keep to their realm and we will keep to ours, and the winter will end, and the city will be safe, and no child will ever again be burned in a square for a charm against sour milk." He leaned forward. "You want to save them. I am offering to save them. All of them. Every guttering light in that realm, preserved behind a wall, forever. Is that not mercy?"

"It's a cage," I said.

"It's a border. Borders are how peace is made between peoples who frighten each other." His voice stayed level, reasonable, infuriatingly sane. "You've seen what fear does, taster. You've watched a woman burn for selling charms. Would you rather more pyres? Or a wall?"

* * *

I asked to think. He granted it with a graciousness that itself felt like a move in a game, and then—and this I hadn't expected—he said, "Before you decide, you should see what you're deciding about," and produced from his cloak a sphere of dark glass the size of a grapefruit.

The scrying glass.

He set it on the cot beside me and turned it with one finger, and the black surface swirled, and then cleared, and I was looking at Emberfall.

I had seen it before, in the memory-pool beneath the Hollow, but that had been Emberfall remembered—Emberfall whole, Emberfall in its first spring, rivers of light and orchards that fruited in colors without human names. This was Emberfall now. This was Emberfall dying.

The light was thinner. That was the first thing. Where the memory had shown radiance, the glass showed radiance *failing*, like a lamp running low on oil, like the last hour of a long feast. The great trees stood bare. The rivers ran slow and dim, more memory than water.

And the children.

They were made of light, the fae children—or light was made of them, I couldn't tell where one ended and the other began—and they guttered. That was the only word. They flickered the way candles flicker in a draft, brightening and failing and brightening, and some of them failed longer than they brightened, and one small one near a ruined archway flickered and did not come back, and the others gathered around the place where it had been the way human children gather around a dead bird, not understanding, touching the ground where it had shone.

I put my hand against the glass. Of course I did. You always do, the first time. The glass was cold as a cellar wall.

"How many?" I asked. My voice came out rougher than I intended.

"Fewer each season." Veyre stood looking at the glass too, and here was the thing I couldn't reconcile, the thing that kept me staring at him instead of the dying lights: he looked *sad*. Genuinely sad, in the specific way of a man watching something he has decided cannot be saved. "I have watched this for eleven years, taster. Eleven years of this glass on my desk while I signed arrest warrants for hedge-witches and charm-sellers, telling myself the law was necessary, telling myself the fae were a danger, telling myself—" He stopped. Pressed his lips together. Started again, quieter. "Telling myself stories. Yes. I know what I am. Do you imagine I don't?"

I stared at him. "Then why—"

"Because knowing what you are doesn't unmake you." He said it flatly, like a diagnosis. "My ancestor hoarded the stone and broke the covenant, and every generation of my blood has paid interest on that theft, and I have paid most of all, because I am the one who found out what we'd done and chose to keep doing it anyway. Because stopping felt worse than continuing. Because the alternative was admitting that everything I built—the laws, the order, the quiet streets, the *safety*—was founded on letting another people die so mine could sleep soundly." He turned the glass again, gently, and the little dead child's place slid out of view. "So yes. I will tell you the truth, since no one tells you anything in this city: I am a thief's heir keeping the theft. And I am offering you a share of it, because you are the only person alive who can open the door, and I would rather share a sin than fail alone."

There it was. The confession. Full, damning, sincere.

And it was also, I realized as I sat very still and let my taster's mind do its work, the most poisonous dish I had ever been served.

* * *

Because here was the thing about poison: the deadliest ones don't taste like anything. Arsenic is subtle. Certain alcaloids are almost pleasant. The killers that matter are the ones that arrive disguised as nourishment, and this confession—this weary, honest, self-aware confession—was arsenic in warm milk.

He had shown me the dying children. He had confessed his guilt. He had offered pardons for everyone I loved. Every single element of it was designed to move me, and every single element of it was true, and that was what made it lethal. Lies you can resist. Truth, weaponized, gets under the skin.

He wanted me to open the Hollow because pity had opened my mouth and he meant to pour the bargain straight down my throat.

I thought about the widow I'd sold the luck-charm to, back in the first chapter of all this, back when my life was small and deniable. She'd known the charm was probably nothing. She'd bought it anyway, because hope is a thing people purchase even when they suspect the seller. And I had taken her coin and given her a dried sprig and a few spoken words, and I had slept fine afterward, because the kindness was real even if the magic was small.

Veyre was selling the opposite. Real magic, enormous stakes, and no kindness in it anywhere—only a cage with the bars painted to look like mercy.

"Let me ask you something," I said. "When the covenant is reforged—with you as anchor, the fae sealed behind the stone—what happens to the children in that glass?"

"They live. Behind the border. Safe."

"Do they?" I leaned forward. "Chancellor. I taste things for a living. Let me taste your plan and tell you what's in it." I held up one cuffed hand, fingers spread, counting off. "One: a covenant is a living thing. It isn't a wall, it's a marriage. Seal the bride in a room and call it a marriage, and see how long the vows hold. Two: the stone cracked the first time because one side took and took and gave nothing back. Your plan is that, again, with better paperwork. Three: you are dying. Whatever you've been drinking from that stone has kept you upright past every natural year you owned, and anchors wear out. What happens to your sealed covenant when you fail? Who opens the next door? Another thief's heir? Four—"

"Enough."

"—four," I finished, because I was going to finish, "the children in that glass aren't afraid of us. Look at them. They're just *going out*, like lamps nobody trims. They don't need a wall between us. They need someone on our side of it who gives a damn. And you have spent eleven years proving you give a damn only when it costs you nothing."

His jaw tightened. "And your plan? Reforge it openly? Bind yourself to it? Do you know what that means, taster? Do you understand what you'd be?"

"No," I admitted. "Not entirely. But I know what you'd be, and I know what the hospice faithful would be, and I know what Wren would be, and none of those are cages." I paused. "Also—and forgive me, this is the professional in me—if you wanted my cooperation, showing me the dying children was a mistake. Because now I've seen them. Now they're in my head, flickering, and I can't unknow them, and every instinct I have says *help them*, not *wall them off where I can stop looking.* You showed me the truth hoping it would frighten me into your arms. It did the opposite. Compassion isn't a lever, Chancellor. You can't stand on it."

For a long moment neither of us spoke. The scrying glass dimmed on the cot between us, Emberfall sinking toward its own evening, and the little lights guttered on, stubborn, ridiculous, refusing.

"You sound like her," Veyre said finally. "Hesk. She used to argue like that. As though decency were an argument that could be won." He rose, and the tiredness came back into his face, and this time he didn't fight it. "You'll say no, then."

"I'll say no."

"To the pardon. To freedom. To your friends walking free."

"To the cage wearing their pardon as wrapping paper, yes." I stood too, as much as the chains allowed, and met his eyes. "Here's my counteroffer, and I'll make it only once, so attend: open Hesk's cell. Give her paper and ink. Let me send word to Wren. And then get out of our way before the eclipse, because whether you help or hang us, the covenant is getting reforged, and the only choice you actually have left is which side of it history finds you standing on."

He studied me for a long time. Something moved behind his eyes—calculation, mostly, but underneath it, faint as the last warmth in a cooling cup, something that might have been envy. Or longing. Hard to tell the difference in a man who had spent decades alone inside his own justification.

"You realize," he said softly, "that I could simply execute you."

"You could," I agreed pleasantly. "But you won't. Because I'm the only key you've got, and because—" I smiled, and it was the smile I used at banquets, the one that said *I have already tasted this course and survived it* — "because you didn't come down here to kill me. You came down here hoping I'd say yes. And men who hope can be reached."

He picked up the scrying glass. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"The eclipse is in six days," he said. "I'll have your answer by then, one way or the other. Think about the children, taster. You'll find they're very persuasive." And then, quieter, almost to himself: "That's rather the problem."

The door locked behind him. The boot descended the stairs—that same deliberate tread—and the tower settled back into its sounds.

I sat down on the cot and pressed my palms flat against my knees and breathed until my hands stopped shaking, because they had been shaking the entire conversation and a good taster knows the audience should never see it.

Six days. Wren was in the tannery district. Hesk was two floors below. Somewhere in this tower there was ink, and somewhere in this city there was a kitchen I knew better than any man alive, and somewhere in my chest there was a heartbeat that the stone below the hills had already, in some sense I didn't yet understand, begun to answer.

I looked at the empty place on the blanket where the scrying glass had rested, and the cold of it still lingered in the straw.

"Hold on," I murmured, to the guttering children, to the flickering lights, to anyone listening on either side of any wall. "Hold on. I'm coming."

And far below the hills, faint but unmistakable, through six feet of earth and three years of hiding and every lock in the Chancellor's tower—

something answered.
