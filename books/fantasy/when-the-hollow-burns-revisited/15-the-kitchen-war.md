## 15. The Kitchen War

Wren had spent her whole life learning the city's back ways—the courier's inheritance, the map under the map. She knew which gutters could hold a person's weight and which would dump them into a tanner's yard. She knew every door in the palace district that pretended to be a wall.

She did not know kitchens.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly. As if I'm stupid."

Tam, propped against a rain barrel with his bandaged side and his usual expression of someone tolerating the weather, closed his eyes. "You're not stupid. You're impatient. Those look alike from a distance."

"I'm both. Say it again."

Mother Hesk spread the stolen plans across three overturned crates in the hospice's laundry yard, weighting the corners with candle stubs and one indignant cat. The plans had come from a laundress who owed Hesk a debt older than the Chancellor's office, and they were drawn in the confident hand of someone who had never once carried a full scullery bucket up four flights.

"Now," Hesk said, "August left us instructions before they took them. In their way."

"The riddle?"

"The riddle." Hesk's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly grief. August's notes were written on the backs of menu scraps—there was no paper in the tower cell, only the drafts of the Chancellor's banquet menus that the guards discarded—and they were signed, absurdly, *The Cook*. Wren read them for the fourth time.

*Guests allergic to shellfish sit at the Chancellor's left. The wine steward waters the third cask but not the seventh. The night guard on the pantry stair takes his supper at the ninth bell and sleeps until the tenth, and he is fond of figs.*

"It's a shopping list," Tam said.

"It's a siege plan," Wren said, "written by someone who has been invisible so long they forgot other people can't see through walls."

Hesk tapped the largest scrap. "Here. The execution yard shares a wall with the pastry kitchen. And this—" her finger moved to a corridor marked only as *the warm passage*, unglamorous and unmapped in any official plan— "runs behind the Chancellor's wing from the buttery to the chapel. August has walked it every day for three years. Carried dishes down it. Counted the steps."

Wren looked at the warm passage and felt something turn over in her chest. Three years of carrying other people's food, memorizing their allergies, their habits, their weaknesses—not because August wanted power over anyone, but because noticing was how you survived. And now every small noticing was a key.

"They turned being overlooked into a weapon," she said slowly, "just by remembering everything."

"They turned *kindness* into a weapon," Hesk corrected. "You don't learn the wine steward cheats unless you've been kind enough that he tells you. That's the part Veyre will never understand, and it's going to ruin his entire morning."

Tam pushed himself upright, wincing. "Then let's not keep him waiting."

* * *

They went in as servants, because the city was still wrapped in solstice blizzard and nobody questioned an extra pair of hands hauling anything heavy in that weather. The hospice faithful came in ones and twos over two days—a leper-brother with a cart of linens, two sisters of the laundry, old Fen with his delivery of salted fish that no one inspected too closely because everyone hoped he'd leave faster. By the eve of the execution, six of Hesk's quiet people were folded into the palace's working life like stitches in a hem, and not one guard had thought to count them.

Wren went in carrying a wedding cake.

It was Hesk's idea and Wren's disguise: some minor courtier's daughter was marrying in the chapel, and a cake the size of a millstone gave a girl in a servant's cap a perfect reason to stand in the warm passage for as long as she liked, being in the way and beneath notice. Wren had iced it herself, badly, under Hesk's supervision. It said *JOY* in lopsided letters. She had come to hate it personally.

"Nine bells," Tam murmured. He was inside as well—of course he was, half-dead and furious about it—leaning on a serving cart with his river-warden's gray eyes downcast, the very portrait of a man whose entire soul consisted of spoons. "Pantry guard's eating his figs."

Wren pressed her ear to the hairline crack in the passage wall that August's notes had described with the tenderness of a love letter. On the other side: the scrape of a stool, the sigh of a man settling into a nap, the faint sticky rhythm of someone chewing who should not be chewing on watch.

"Fen's at the grate," she breathed.

"Then we move." Tam took a small vial from his sleeve—river water, blessed by nothing, but drawn from the submerged oath-stone fragment and cold as memory. "For the locks. It likes hinges. Don't ask me why; stones have opinions."

They went along the warm passage in single file, past the buttery, past the chapel, past the great ovens where the night bakers dozed in the flour-warm dark. At the second junction, according to the notes, there was a door that stuck, and beside it a bell-pull that rang in the kennels, and Wren understood suddenly why August had written all of it down in menu code: *the soufflé always falls when the kitchen boy whistles.* Everything in this palace was connected by threads only the servants held, and the Chancellor had built his fortress on top of a web without ever looking down.

At the far end, the passage opened through a service panel into the antechamber of the Chancellor's justice hall. Beyond that door: the execution yard. Beyond that: August.

Wren set down the cake.

"JOY stays here," she said, and drew her knife.

* * *

Later, Wren would remember the sound first.

Not the crowd—though the yard was packed, the Chancellor wanting witnesses, wanting a lesson, wanting magic burned publicly and thoroughly before the solstice. Not the drums. What she remembered was the specific, domestic clatter of the executioner's equipment being laid out on a table, the sound of poison being decanted with a ladle, as casual as porridge.

They came out of the service panel into the yard's shadowed colonnade just as August was brought out.

Wren had prepared herself for worse and it was worse anyway. August walked between two witch-finders with their wrists bound and their chin up, which was so entirely wrong—August was a person who made themselves small, who folded inward like a letter—that for one hot second Wren forgot the entire plan and simply wanted to kill something. Then she saw why the chin was up. August was reading the room. Even now. Even here. Eyes flicking to the executioner's table, the cups, the brazier, the row of little stoppered flasks—counting, cataloguing, tasting the air like a man reading a menu for the exits.

*"August Amberdale,"* the herald read out, *"convicted of the forbidden arts—"*

"Now," Hesk whispered.

What followed was not, strictly speaking, a battle. It was a kitchen war, and it was fought with the weapons of the overlooked.

Fen, in the yard since dawn as a fish-seller licensed for executions (the Chancellor believed in feeding crowds; crowds behaved better fed), upset his cart at the precise moment the herald reached *high treason*, and three hundred people discovered simultaneously that they were standing in herring. In the ensuing moral crisis, the front ranks stopped watching the scaffold entirely.

The two sisters of the laundry, positioned at the yard's water pump, chose that moment to discover that the pump was broken, and to scream about it, and to draw the two nearest guards into a dispute about whose fault it had been for the last eleven years.

And Brother Aldous, gentle Aldous, who had tended lepers for forty years and had a voice like a soft bell, stood up on a barrel near the Chancellor's viewing box and began, in a clear carrying tenor, to sing a hymn. Not a forbidden hymn. A perfectly ordinary grace, the sort sung before meals in every honest kitchen in the city. But he sang it directly at Lord Chancellor Veyre, and he did not stop, and there is no law against grace and no easy way to arrest a blessing in front of a crowd.

Veyre stood, white-faced, shouting for order, and order did not come, because the herring was spreading.

Through all of it, Wren went over the colonnade rail and dropped into the yard proper, Tam a gray shadow behind her, and cut August's bonds while the executioner was still staring at the fish.

"Hello," Wren said. "Your cake's inside. We couldn't carry it."

"You *made* me a cake?" August's voice was hoarse but the dryness was intact, and Wren could have wept with relief.

"Hesk made it. I iced it. There are criticisms."

But the executioner had found his wits, and he was quick—he caught August's shoulder with one huge hand and with the other swept up the cup of state poison, black and thick as tar, and forced it against August's lips before either of them could move. "*Drink, witch,*" he snarled, "in front of everyone—"

The whole yard saw it happen.

Saw August swallow. Saw them stand very still for a moment, eyes closed, like someone listening to distant music. Saw them lick their lips consideringly.

"Hm," August said.

"What," said the executioner.

"That's wolfsbane cut with hemlock and sweetened with honey to hide the bite." August opened their eyes, and there was something in them Wren had never seen—not fear, not even defiance. *Professional disappointment.* "Whoever compounded this rushed the hemlock stage. Also, you've used the good honey, which seems wasteful."

The silence lasted exactly as long as it took three hundred people to understand what they had witnessed. The man had drunk the killing cup. The man had reviewed it.

"He's *blessed*!" someone shouted—wrongly, wonderfully—and Brother Aldous's grace swelled triumphant, and the crowd's fear curdled into something far more dangerous to Lord Chancellor Veyre: laughter.

"Run," Tam said, shoving August toward the colonnade. "Debate your seasoning later."

* * *

They ran, and the palace itself fought for them.

Every door they needed was unlocked or unlockable; every guard they met was facing the wrong way, drawn off by some small catastrophe—a fire alarm in the linen stores, a runaway in the stables, a sudden plague of jam. Wren realized, breathless, halfway down the warm passage, that they hadn't planned half of these. The palace's invisible people had simply seen which way the wind blew and started pulling threads. The head cook had unlocked the buttery. The kennel-master had loosed the dogs nowhere in particular. Three years of August's small kindnesses, and everyone August had ever been decent to, paying it forward at once.

"They think we're heroes," August panted, scandalized.

"They think you're theirs," Wren said. "Which is worse. For Veyre."

They burst out of the warm passage into the pastry kitchen, past the great sleeping ovens, past JOY the cake sitting forlorn on its trolley—

—and stopped, all four of them, because the yard gate ahead was opening, and through it came witch-finders in steel, and behind them, wrapped in furs despite the indoor heat, leaning on a cane topped with amber glass, came the Lord Chancellor himself.

He looked at August. He looked at the empty poison cups still sitting on the yard table beyond. He looked, briefly, like a man doing arithmetic and getting an answer he refused to accept.

"Interesting," Veyre said softly. "Very interesting. Take them—all of them. And this time, witch, we will find what *doesn't* agree with you."

Tam moved first, hurling the river-water vial—not at the Chancellor, at the floor, where it burst and steamed and *sang*, a low ringing note like a struck bell made of winter, and every candle in the kitchen guttered blue, and the witch-finders hesitated, because men who burn magic for a living know its smell when it rises up unexpected.

In that hesitation, Hesk threw open the flour store.

The cloud that followed was total, white, and blinding, and through it Wren heard the Chancellor coughing with immense dignity, heard guards colliding with trolleys, heard Aldous somewhere still singing grace. She grabbed August's sleeve and pulled, and they ran—out the yard gate, into the solstice blizzard, into the hills, where a split stone waited and an eclipse would not wait for anyone.

Behind them, faint under the wind, Veyre's voice rose above the coughing, and it was not shouting anymore. It was calm, which was far more frightening:

"The Hollow, then. We'll finish this where it started."

Wren ran harder.

The snow was deepening, the light was failing, and somewhere ahead in the white whirl of the world, the last road home was already open—and they were not the only ones on it.
