## 16. Flight to the Hollow

The world had turned to salt.

August had never seen snow fall sideways before. Now it was all they could see—a white roar that erased the hills, the road, the sky, everything except the three of them floundering through it like moths in spilled flour. Somewhere behind them, Veyre's riders were doing what riders do best, which was ride, and August's legs were doing what a taster's legs do best, which was nothing at all.

"Left!" Tam shouted against the wind, one arm bound tight across his chest where the witch-finders' blades had found him on the descent from the Chancellor's wing. The bandages under his coat were already rust-brown at the edges. "The river remembers this valley—follow the memory!"

"That's not *directions*, Tam, that's poetry!"

"Everything I say is directions if you're half a river!"

Wren appeared out of the white on August's other side, hood back despite the cold, her father's map wrapped in oilskin and strapped beneath her coat where it couldn't be taken. She'd stopped arguing about who should carry it somewhere around the second hour of running. "There's a shepherd's bothy ahead. I can smell peat smoke."

"You can smell peat smoke through a blizzard?"

"I can smell through anything. It's the eyes that fail me." She grabbed August's sleeve and hauled them leftward. "Move, taster. Your legs are pretending to be decorative again."

"They've always been decorative," August wheezed. "It's my tongue that does the work."

* * *

The bothy was a stone hunch barely bigger than a bread oven, but it had a door, a hearth, and half a stack of cut peat some dead shepherd had left as a kindness to strangers, which struck August as exactly the sort of small deniable generosity they'd built their whole life on. Wren got a fire going with the efficiency of someone who had slept outdoors more often than anyone her age should have. Tam sank onto the bench by the wall and did not get up again, which frightened August more than the riders had.

Hesk, though—Hesk stood in the doorway a long moment, looking back at the storm they'd crossed, and August watched something go out of her face. The old physician had spent eleven days in Veyre's cells between her capture and their rescue, and the cells had done what the witch-finders hadn't: they'd made her small. Not broken. Just... banked, like a fire left overnight.

"Sit," August said gently. "I'll see to your feet. They're frostbitten; don't argue."

"I wasn't going to argue." Hesk lowered herself onto the bench beside Tam. "I was going to say you've gotten better at reading people. Before, you'd have waited for permission."

"Before, I could afford manners." August knelt and began working off the old woman's boots with hands that only shook a little. "Before, I was nobody. Turns out nobody was a full-time job."

"It's a job everyone should hold for at least a decade," Hesk said. "It teaches humility." Then she coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it ended there was blood on her lip, thin and bright as currant jelly, and August knew its taste without tasting it.

They didn't speak about it. Some things you catalog and set aside, the way you do with poison, so your hands stay steady.

* * *

By midnight the storm had settled into a steady scream rather than a howling one, and Tam had finally lost consciousness against the wall, breathing shallow but even, his unbound hand resting in a basin of meltwater where the water kept curling toward his fingers like a cat. Wren took first watch at the window slit, knife drawn, watching the white dark for lantern-light.

August sat beside Hesk's bedroll, and Hesk patted the blanket next to her in a way that meant *sit down properly, we're going to talk.*

"You know I'm dying," Hesk said. Not a question. Physicians never wasted words on questions they'd already answered.

"The cough says lungs. The color of your lips says the blood isn't following. The way you held your ribs on the climb says at least two cracked, probably three." August kept their voice level. Three years of tasting for a king taught you to deliver bad news in a pleasant tone. "You shouldn't have been able to walk out of the city."

"I shouldn't have been able to do a great many things lately. I find that once you've decided to die anyway, the body stops taking votes." Hesk smiled, and it was her old smile, dry as good bread. "Which brings us to business. We have until dawn, perhaps midmorning if the storm holds the riders back, and I have thirty years of oath-lore in my head and no time to teach it properly. So we won't do it properly. We'll do it the way I taught anatomy to field surgeons in the plague camps—fast, dirty, and remembered by fear."

"Fear?"

"You'll remember everything I tell you tonight because you'll be terrified of forgetting it. That's the trick, August. Fear is just love with nowhere to go." She said it lightly, but August heard the weight under it, and looked away.

"Then let's begin," they said, "before I run out of places to put mine."

* * *

She started with the stone itself.

"The covenant-stone is not a symbol. Everyone thinks it's a symbol—the historians, the priests, certainly the Chancellor. It is an *instrument*. A lock has two keys because two parties must agree to open it; a covenant-stone has two oaths because two peoples must agree to keep it. When your king betrayed Emberfall—" Hesk caught herself. "When *his* king betrayed Emberfall. You keep inheriting other people's crimes tonight, child, I'll try not to add to the pile."

"Too late," August murmured. "Go on."

"The stone was split deliberately—not broken in rage, but *split*, like a ledger divided into columns. Human column. Fae column. Each side keeps its own book. To reforge it, the books must be balanced. That's the rite. Balancing." She pressed a hand flat against her bandaged ribs and breathed through the pain with the discipline of a woman who had screamed professionally and considered it a tool to be rationed. "The old rite used a monarch from each side. King and queen, oath and counter-oath, each swallowing the other's grief—literally drinking it, a cup poured from the memories of the realm, each taking what the other could not carry."

"And now there's no fae queen, and our king is dying, and the man who wants the throne would rather let Emberfall starve than share a table with it."

"Now there's you." Hesk's eyes were bright and feverish and utterly certain. "A person who has spent nine years drinking small poisons so that others might eat safely. Do you understand what that *is*, August? Not metaphorically. In the old terms of the art?"

"A habit," August said. "A stupid, careful habit."

"It's an *anchor*. The covenant needs a living balance-point, something that can hold grief in one hand and hope in the other without spilling either. There are maybe three people alive with the constitution for it. Two of them are in this room, and I'm leaving." She said it the way she'd once announced diagnoses, kindly, completely, without room for bargaining. "So. The rite."

* * *

She taught until the candle guttered.

The order of the oaths: grief first, then gratitude, then the binding word, which was not a word at all but a taste—the memory of a shared meal, any shared meal, held in the mind at the moment of sealing. "That's why it was a feast, originally," Hesk said. "The first covenant was signed over dinner. All this pomp came later, like embroidery on a patch."

The cup: not gold, not silver. Stone, hollowed, or failing that, cupped hands. "The realm will provide the vessel if you provide the willingness. It always could. That was the whole point."

The danger: "Grief is heavier than poison. Poison wants to kill you quickly and be done. Grief wants to *stay*. You must swallow and swallow and swallow and never once mistake it for yours. You are a vessel, not a grave. The moment you start believing the sorrow belongs to you—that you deserve it—it pools. And pooled grief drowns the vessel. Tasters know this already, I think. You've spent years telling yourself *this harm is not mine, it passes through me.*"

August thought of every breakfast, every banquet. Every swallow followed by the little litany: *not mine, not mine, passes through.* They had thought it was a coping mechanism. It had apparently been training.

"How long?" they asked quietly. "If I do this. How long do I last?"

Hesk was silent a moment. "I don't know. The stone may hold you the way you hold it—mutual preservation. Or you may simply endure, the way bridges endure. Used. Weathered. Present." She reached over and took August's chin in her fingers, physician's grip, tilting their face to the firelight. "But listen to me. Whatever happens—you will not vanish. That's the old fear talking, the fear that kept you small for nine years. Anchors don't disappear, August. They're the thing everything else ties to."

August's eyes stung, which was inconvenient, because they'd been planning not to do that until after the solstice. "And if I refuse? If I run?"

"Then the winter finishes what it started, Emberfall goes out like a guttering candle, and this city follows within a generation, because the realms share a root whether Veyre likes it or not." She released their chin. "But you won't run. I've watched you since Mother Aldith's hospice. You have never once in your life chosen yourself when someone hungry was standing in front of you. It's your worst quality and your only greatness."

"That's a terrible eulogy."

"It's not a eulogy. It's a blessing. I'm getting to that. Stop interrupting your elders."

* * *

Dawn came gray through the storm, and with it, Hesk gave the rest.

She gave August her physician's ring—iron, plain, worn smooth—"so the rites carry a witness's authority, and so some part of me is on your hand when you need steady fingers." She gave Wren the contents of her satchel, itemized aloud from memory because the satchel itself was mostly gone: which poultices for Tam's wound, which teas thinned blood and which thickened it, the exact pressure points that slowed bleeding in a body with fae blood ("different from ours; the heart sits a finger's width leftward of where you'd expect, nature showing off"). She gave Tam a list of names, spoken twice, of healers in the river towns who still remembered the old ways and owed her debts.

"And to all three of you," she said, when the storm outside had begun to slacken and the light was strengthening toward the terrible clarity of a day for traveling, "I give the only inheritance worth anything. Attention. Pay attention—to the road, to the enemy, to each other. Most people die of inattention. The rest die of attention paid to the wrong things." Her breath rattled a little. "Also I leave you my opinion that you're all far too young for this, which you will disregard, as the young do, and prove wrong, as they rarely do."

"Hesk—" Wren started, and the old woman raised one eyebrow with such exact former-authority that Wren shut her mouth.

"When we reach the Hollow," Hesk said, "you will want to leave me somewhere warm and press on quickly. Don't. Let me see it. I've read about the Hollow for forty years and I intend to be insufferable about the real thing for as long as I have a thing to be insufferable with. Grief carried forward, children. Not left behind. Left-behind grief becomes haunting. Carried grief becomes company."

August bowed their head over her hand, and Hesk pressed it briefly to their brow—an old blessing, older than the magic laws, older probably than the stone itself—and said words in a language August didn't know but recognized anyway, the way you recognize the shape of a taste from childhood.

"What does it mean?" they asked.

"'May you be fed by what feeds others,'" Hesk translated. "It's a table-grace. My grandmother said it over every meal. I spent sixty years thinking it was about food." She smiled. "It was never about food."

* * *

They moved at midday, into a world scoured clean and blinding. The storm had passed and left the hills like something newly made—every fold of land edged in white, the sky a hard enamel blue, the silence enormous. And behind them, small on the southern ridge, movement: riders. Six, perhaps eight, black dots crawling along the skyline like flies on a wedding cake.

"Two hours," Wren said, squinting. "Three, if they lose the trail at the frozen ford."

"They won't lose the trail at the ford," Tam said grimly, swaying in the saddle of the shaggy hill pony August had traded a luck-charm for at the last farmstead. His color was bad. "Rivers tell tales. Even frozen ones."

"Then we don't take the ford." August checked the map's spine against the ridgeline, Hesk's lessons fresh and terrifying in their mind. "There's a path here—the Shepherd's Cut, it goes straight up through the cliffs. Steep, narrow, brutal. But it puts us above the Hollow's door by dusk."

"Dusk," Wren said. "The eclipse begins tomorrow at moonrise. We're early."

"Early is better than dead," said August.

"Debatable, but fine."

They climbed. It was exactly as brutal as promised—switchbacks of blue ice, ledges barely wide enough for the pony, wind funneled through the cut with enough force to peel tears sideways off your face. Tam rode when he could and walked, leaning on Wren, when he couldn't. Hesk, wrapped and lashed into a litter of coats and rope between August and Wren, insisted on narrating geological features until her voice gave out, then narrated them in glares instead.

Halfway up, on a ledge with a drop so sheer it seemed rude, the riders' horns sounded below—found, then, faster than hoped—and August made the arithmetic of sacrifice again, the same bitter sums as the night Hesk was taken: speed against safety, the many against the few, the stone against everything.

Except this time the sum came out different, because Hesk, lashed to the litter, looked up at August with fever-bright eyes and said, "Faster, child. I believe I mentioned I intend to be insufferable."

They made the top as the sun went down.

And there—the whole world opened. Below them the hills fell away into a great bowl of shadow, and at its center, visible even in the dying light, a ring of standing stones around a dark cleft in the earth, rimmed with frost that did not glitter white like the rest of the mountains but burned faintly, impossibly, ember-red, as though the ground itself remembered warmth. Above the cleft, high and thin in the darkening sky, hung a smear of light that was not cloud and not star: another country's sunset, leaking through a wound.

Emberfall. Dying. Visible.

"Oh," Hesk whispered, from her litter, in a voice like a girl's. "Oh, it's *beautiful.*"

Behind them, up the cut, the horns sounded again—close now, close enough to hear the riders' voices on the wind—and the red frost at the Hollow's mouth pulsed once, slowly, like something waking.

Like a heartbeat answering August's own.

"Down," August said. "All of you. Down, quickly—it knows we've come."
