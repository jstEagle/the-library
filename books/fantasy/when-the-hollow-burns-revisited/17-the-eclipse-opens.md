## 17. The Eclipse Opens

Wren had climbed a great many rooftops in her life, but never a hill that felt like it was watching her back.

The blizzard had thinned as they gained the high pastures, snow giving way to a strange granular light that fell sideways and refused to settle. Above them, the sky was doing something Wren had no name for. The sun still hung in the west—should have hung there—but around its edge the blue of day was flaking away like old paint, revealing not night behind it but something else entirely: a wash of green-gold, deepening by degrees, as though the sky were a pond and someone had thrown a stone into the far side of it.

"That's Emberfall," August said quietly.

"I know what it is." Wren's breath came out in white ribbons. "I've been dreaming about it since I was six."

She hadn't told them that. Any of them. But up here, with the hills humming under their boots like struck bronze, secrets seemed to be leaking out of everyone. Tam walked half a step behind, one hand pressed to the bandages wound around their ribs, gray-faced but upright. Hesk rode the mule they'd borrowed from the hospice, wrapped in three blankets, refusing with immense dignity to admit she could no longer sit a horse. And at the center of the little procession, carrying her father's map in an oilskin sleeve against her heart, Wren tried not to look at the sky again.

She looked anyway.

The green-gold had spread to cover a quarter of the heavens now, and in it—if she squinted, if she let her eyes go soft—she could see shapes. Towers, maybe. Or trees. Rivers of pale fire winding between them. Once, unmistakably, something small and bright crossed the whole breadth of that other-sky in a long slow arc, like a moth crossing a window, and Tam made a sound low in their throat that was either grief or prayer.

"They're still there," Tam said. "Still burning."

"Then let's not keep them waiting," Wren said, because if she said anything softer she would cry, and there would be time for crying later or there would not be time for anything at all.

The map's spine led them along a ridge of black rock where no snow lay—not melted, simply *absent*, the stone bare and dry as if winter had agreed, long ago, to keep off this particular stretch of the world. Wren's father had drawn this ridge in ink so fine it looked like thread. She remembered him hunched over his desk by candle-end, tongue between his teeth, drawing a line and whispering *there you are* the way other fathers whispered endearments.

She had hated the map for months. Hated it for taking him, hill by hill, year by year, until all that came home to her was his cough and his cold feet and his stories about a place he'd never reached. She had hated August for carrying it when he couldn't.

She found, climbing this impossible ridge under a bruised and gilded sky, that she didn't hate it anymore. The map had simply known something she hadn't: that some inheritances aren't burdens. Some are instructions.

*There you are,* she thought, at the top of the ridge.

Below them, cupped in the hills like water in a hand, lay the Hollow.

It wasn't a valley, exactly. It was more like a place where the land had once held its breath and never quite let it out—a bowl of dark grass ringed with standing stones, and at its center, plain and enormous and wrong, the covenant-stone.

Wren had imagined it a hundred ways: a jewel, a throne, a pillar of glass. It was none of those things. It was a boulder the size of a chapel, squat and grey and ordinary-looking, except that it was split cleanly down its middle from crown to root, a crack wide enough to drive a cart through, and out of that crack came light. Not the green-gold of the wounded sky. A warmer light, hearth-colored, pulsing with a rhythm she recognized before she understood why.

It pulsed like a heartbeat.

And it was slowing.

"Oh," Hesk said softly, from the mule's back. Her physician's voice, the one she used for diagnoses. "Oh, the poor thing."

They went down into the Hollow together, boots silent on the dark grass, and the closer they came to the split stone the more Wren felt it—not heard, felt, in the bones of her jaw and the roots of her teeth: a second heartbeat, vast and labored, keeping time with the light. The air tasted of iron and honey. Of blood and bread. Of both realms at once, mixed the way her father had always sworn they were meant to be.

August stopped an arm's length from the crack and laid one palm flat against the stone.

"It knows us," August said. Their voice had gone strange—layered, almost, as though two people were speaking in unison and neither quite knew it. "It's glad we came." A pause. "It's sorry."

"Sorry for what?" Wren asked.

The stone's light guttered. Somewhere above them, faint as a memory of bells, something in Emberfall cried out—or would have, if realms could cry. August snatched their hand back as though bitten.

"Sorry," August repeated, "that it can't hold on much longer."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the standing stones, which was a generous word for what it was: Hesk dozing upright against the mule, Tam rebuilding the fire three times before it would take, Wren walking the ring with her knife loose in her hand because twenty years of couriers' instincts don't retire just because the world is ending.

August sat apart, cross-legged before the split stone, doing nothing at all—which for August meant everything. Their lips moved sometimes. Counting, probably. Tasting the air the way they'd taught Wren to taste suspicious broth: first with the tip of the mind, then all at once.

Wren came to stand beside them.

"Do you feel it?" August asked without looking up. "The eclipse. It's close."

She did feel it—a pressure behind her eyes, a thinning, as though the world were a drumhead drawn too tight. In the sky above the hills, the sun had begun, impossibly, to dim at its edge, and the green-gold realm showed through more boldly now, its rivers of pale fire flickering in sympathy with the covenant-stone's failing heart.

"My father spent eleven years looking for this place," Wren said. "Eleven years, and he got within thirty miles of it once. Turned back because I had a fever and he wouldn't risk the road with me sick." She watched August's profile, careful and still. "He died four miles from the last waystation. Did you know that? The witch-finders didn't even want him. He was nobody. He just happened to be carrying something somebody else wanted."

August said nothing. August was good at saying nothing; Wren had learned that about them early, and learned eventually that it wasn't coldness. It was room. They left space where other people filled it.

"He'd have liked you," she said at last, grudgingly. "He liked people who did brave things quietly."

August's mouth curved. "I don't know that I've done anything bravely. Mostly I've done things terrified, and quietly."

"That's what brave means," Wren said. "Nobody tells you."

The eclipse took another bite of the sun. And that was when Tam, feeding the fire, went rigid.

"Riders," they said. "North rim. A lot of them."

Wren had them counted before Tam finished speaking: torchlight bobbing along the ridge like a string of feverish beads, forty at least, maybe sixty, moving in the disciplined columns of palace guard rather than the ragged scatter of witch-finders. At their head, a single lantern of white glass.

"No," Hesk breathed, waking. She knew that lantern. Every physician in the palace had known it. "No, he shouldn't be able to ride. He shouldn't be able to *stand*—"

"What do you mean?" Wren demanded.

Hesk's face in the firelight was terrible with old knowledge. "Veyre has been dying for years. Whatever he's done to himself with that stone, it eats him faster than it feeds him. For him to come here, tonight, in this cold—" She shook her head. "He's spending everything. He thinks this is his last chance."

Down in the Hollow's bowl, the riders began their descent.

* * *

They came in perfect order, torches banked low, hooves muffled with cloth—and Wren, watching, thought bitterly that Veyre's guard treated this sacred ground with better manners than they'd ever shown a single market square. The Lord Chancellor rode at their center on a white gelding that shied repeatedly from the split stone and had to be urged forward with spurs. Even horses, then, could tell when something was being drained.

Veyre himself looked, from a distance, remarkably well. Tall, silver-haired, immaculate in a traveling coat the color of dried blood. It was only as the party halted twenty paces from the stone that Wren saw the truth Hesk had named: the way his gloved hands trembled on the reins; the hollows of his cheeks, powdered over; the fact that he dismounted only by taking two guards' shoulders as a ladder.

He should have been in a bed. He had chosen instead to die here, if it came to that, in sight of the thing he'd spent a lifetime bleeding.

"Somehow," Veyre called, and his voice carried beautifully—he'd always had that gift, Wren thought, a voice built for courtrooms and gallows alike—"I expected fewer of you. And yet more trouble." His gaze traveled over their small defiant campfire, their mule, their hospice blankets, and settled with unpleasant precision on August. "The taster. Of course it's the taster. You were invisible for three years, Master Amberdale. Remarkable discipline. I nearly awarded you a medal."

"You could have," August said, rising, brushing off their knees. "You ate my cooking every Tuesday."

A few of the guards laughed before they could stop themselves. Veyre's smile thinned but held.

"And the courier's daughter," he went on, turning to Wren, and something in the way he said *courier's daughter* told her he had read every page of her father's file, knew his name, his route, perhaps even his last words. "Your parent was a thief, you know. That map belonged to the crown. To *me*, by inheritance of blood." He gestured, and two guards advanced, unhurried, professional. "Return it, and I'll permit you all to walk down this hill alive. What happens after the eclipse is no business of mine. I am not a cruel man. I'm simply the last one willing to do what must be done."

"The stone is dying," August said. "Not sleeping. Not resting. *Dying.* You've had your hand in its chest for thirty years, Chancellor, and you're calling it stewardship."

"I've kept this city fed through three failed harvests," Veyre said, mild as milk, and Wren realized with a lurch that he believed it. That was the horror of him. He believed every word. "When the fae realm finishes its collapse, the winter will break on its own. Grief does that. And what remains of Emberfall will drift beyond the sky like ash on wind, and humanity will hold one city instead of sharing half of one. Someone had to be strong enough to let them go. My family has always paid that price."

"Your family *caused* it," Wren snapped.

Something flickered behind Veyre's eyes—the first honest thing on his face all night. "My family," he said softly, "made a hard choice, once, and has spent every generation since being punished for it by people who weren't there. You'll forgive me if I've stopped asking permission to survive."

He turned from them then, dismissive as closing a door, and walked toward the split stone. The guards parted for him. The white lantern-bearer followed, and in its light Wren saw what Veyre carried in his free hand: a rod of black iron, forked at the end, its tines fitted—she understood with a cold drop in her stomach—to grip the edges of a crack. To pry.

To widen.

"He means to break it open fully," Hesk whispered. "Take everything left in one draught. It will kill the stone outright—it may kill Emberfall outright, mid-eclipse, with the veil this thin—"

"Then we're done talking," Wren said.

She had her knife out before anyone could answer, which in hindsight was not strategy, but she was a courier's daughter raised on rooftops and hard riding, and there are moments when the body decides before the mind convenes. Tam caught her arm.

"Wren. Look at the sky."

She looked.

The eclipse had reached its edge. The last sliver of sun was going out like a pinched wick, and as darkness poured across the hills, the sky *opened*. There was no other word. Where daylight had been, Emberfall now hung entire: towers of woven light, forests whose leaves were small flames, rivers running silver upward, and everywhere—everywhere—the people of it, the fae, thousands upon thousands of them, gathered in their burning streets, looking *up*. Looking, Wren realized with her heart slamming, at the same eclipse. Looking back.

Among them, near the front of a crowd gathered at a riverbank, stood a figure holding a child aloft so the girl could see the human hills. Both of them were dimmer than they should have been. Both of them were still burning.

"No more hiding places," August murmured beside her. Wren couldn't tell if it was awe or grief or both braided together.

Across the Hollow, Veyre set the tines of his iron rod against the crack in the stone, and the stone's heartbeat stumbled—caught—stumbled worse. The guards leveled their crossbows at the five of them, casual as men covering a construction site.

"Last chance, taster," the Chancellor called, without turning around. "Stand with me or stand aside."

August stepped forward into the torchlight, and Wren saw their fear plainly—saw it in their white knuckles and careful breathing—and saw, beneath it, the thing that had been growing in them since a stranger died against a tenement door. August looked at the split stone. At the sky full of watching fires. At Veyre's trembling hands.

"No," August said. "But thank you for asking politely."

And then the eclipse closed its fist, the Hollow went dark but for the stone's own failing light, and Veyre leaned his whole dying weight against the iron rod, and the covenant-stone—cracked, starving, ancient—began, at last and terribly, to scream.
