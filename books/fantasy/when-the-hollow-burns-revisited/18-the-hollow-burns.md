## 18. The Hollow Burns

The eclipse began as a bite out of the sun, and the whole hillside held its breath.

I had tasted that breath before—the held breath of a kitchen the moment before a dish is carried out to someone who might kill the cook for insulting them. Every eye fixed on the sky above us, where Emberfall was bleeding through into our world: towers of green glass, rivers of slow silver light, and shapes moving in the deep meadows there that I did not have words for and suspected no human language had bothered to invent. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. It was also dying, and you could see it dying, the way you can see a swan is sick long before it stops floating gracefully.

The split covenant-stone stood at the center of the Hollow like a heart cut in half by a careless surgeon. Two great halves of pale stone, leaning apart, the gap between them wide enough for a thin man—or a thick lie—to pass through. Frost crawled up its faces even now, in midsummer, under an eclipsed sun. The grass around it grew in rings, each ring whiter than the last, as though the ground itself were going gray from the inside out.

Wren stood at my left shoulder with her knife drawn, which was not unusual; Wren drew her knife the way other people cleared their throats. Tam leaned against me at my right, still gray-faced from the wound they'd taken on the descent, one hand pressed to a bandage Hesk's instructions had told Wren how to tie. Hesk herself was gone—taken, traded for our escape, and every hour since I'd been carrying her absence in my chest alongside everything else, the way a waiter carries too many plates and refuses, refuses, to drop any of them.

And across the Hollow, flanked by a dozen witch-finders in ash-gray cloaks, stood Lord Chancellor Aldous Veyre.

He looked older than he had at the banquet. That was the first thing I noticed, and the second thing I noticed was that I was noticing it, because a food taster learns to read faces the way farmers read weather, and what Veyre's face said was *famine*. He had been draining himself to feed on the stone for decades, Tam had told us, and now here he was at last, come to drink from the source.

"August Amberdale," he said, and my name in his mouth sounded like something he was deciding whether to swallow or spit. "You've cost me a great deal."

"I get that a lot lately," I said. "It's the map. Everyone wants the map."

"The map." He smiled. It was a good smile. It had been practiced on important people for forty years. "Do you know what I admire about you, taster? You survived in my palace for three years. Under my nose. Serving me. Smiling at me. Charming small poisons past my guards in your fingertips." He spread his hands. "Invisibility is a talent. I respect talent."

"He does this," Wren muttered to me. "At the executions. The bit where he compliments them first. My father called it the gravy speech."

"The *what?*"

"You pour it over everything so nobody notices there's no meat underneath."

Despite everything—the eclipse, the frost, the witch-finders' crossbows, the dying otherworld flickering overhead like a lamp running out of oil—I felt the corner of my mouth twitch. Grief and terror do strange things. Sometimes they make room for a laugh the way a packed cupboard makes room for one more jar if you shove.

Veyre turned from us. Whatever we were to him, we had stopped being the interesting part of the evening. He walked toward the split stone with the unhurried gait of a man entering his own dining room, and the witch-finders parted for him, and the frost seemed to lean away from his boots as though even winter didn't want to be blamed for this.

"Stop him," Tam said hoarsely. "August. If he takes the stone's power without paying the price—"

"What price?" Wren demanded. "What happens?"

Tam looked at me. Just at me. And I realized, in the way you realize the dish has gone wrong only after it's already left the kitchen, that Tam had known since the river. Maybe since before the river. Known what the covenant would ask for, and known who it would ask.

"Nothing happens," I said quietly. "That's the point. Nothing happens to anyone unless somebody anchors it. That's what the oath always was. Somebody agreeing to hold the door."

"Then hold it faster," Wren said, "and let's go home."

But I wasn't watching Tam anymore. I was watching Veyre reach the stone.

* * *

He put both hands on the split halves—one palm on each face of the broken covenant—and he spoke a word that was old when the city walls were young. I felt it in my teeth. I felt it in the fillings Hesk had once told me off for neglecting.

The stone woke.

Light poured up through the crack between the halves, not white but the color of the fae sky above us, green-gold and grieving, and it ran up Veyre's arms like water running up a wick. For one heartbeat he looked transfigured. Younger, even. The famine in his face filled in; the decades drained into him backward. He laughed—a real laugh this time, unpracticed, delighted, the laugh of a man who has just been handed the bill for everyone else's dinner and found someone else's name signed at the bottom.

Then the stone read his signature.

I have spent years learning what poison does to a body, catalogued by aftertaste: the almond of bitter cyanide, the metal shavings of arsenic, the sweetish rot of hemlock. What happened to Veyre had no taste I knew, which frightened me more than any flavor ever had, because my tongue is the most educated part of me and it went to school on that light and came back with nothing.

The power poured *into* him and kept pouring, and he had built nothing inside himself to hold it. No vessel. No vow. Forty years of taking from the stone and never once giving back, and inheritance without obligation turned out to be exactly what it sounds like: a feast with no stomach for it. The light filled him up and then it filled him up some more, and I watched a man become a cup being overfilled at a wedding, and I understood—with the awful clarity that comes to tasters at the exact moment they realize the king's breakfast has been tampered with—that the covenant-stone was not a treasure.

It was a *duty*, wearing the costume of a treasure, and it had been set out like poisoned wine for whoever came greedy enough to drink.

Veyre screamed. The witch-finders screamed. The light screamed, in a voice made of two peoples' worth of broken promises, and then—

Then it was over, quicker than that. Quicker than an egg boiling. One moment there was a Lord Chancellor of the city, and the next there was a shape of green-gold light in the approximate location of a man, and then not even that. His cloak settled onto the frost like a dropped tablecloth. His ring rolled three feet and stopped against a white ring of grass, spinning lazily, indecently cheerful.

Nobody moved. Even the crossbows drooped.

"Well," said Wren, into the silence, "he always did take more than he could eat."

A sound came out of me that was half laugh and half something else, and I pressed my sleeve to my mouth, and Tam gripped my arm hard enough to bruise, and the witch-finders—who had followed a man, and now followed no one—began, very sensibly and all at once, to run.

* * *

Except one stayed.

The captain. Ash-gray cloak, scarred jaw, the kind of face you forget immediately and then recognize forever afterward. She had lowered her crossbow during Veyre's ending and now stood holding it like a shovel she'd been caught using for the wrong job, staring at the empty cloak, the ring, the stone.

"The children," she said.

We all looked at her.

"In Emberfall." Her voice cracked down the middle like dry bread. "My grandmother was half-blooded. There are children of light in the deep meadows and they're guttering, I've seen them through the scrying glasses, and he told us—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, smaller. "He told us magic was the disease. Not the cure."

"The disease pays better," Wren said, but without much edge in it. Wren's edges had been wearing soft all week, the way a knife wears soft when it's finally being used for its proper work.

The captain looked at the stone. Then at me. And I saw her do the arithmetic that everyone on this hillside was doing, because the stone was still glowing, still open, its light reaching toward us across the frost like a hand extended over a table, waiting.

"It wants," she said. "Doesn't it. It wants an anchor."

"Yes," said Tam.

"And it will take the next person greedy enough to grab it?"

"No," said Tam. "That's the trick of it. It took him because he grabbed. It doesn't want grabbing." They looked at me again, those river-colored eyes, and there was apology in them, and there was also—this was worse—there was *hope*, and hope aimed at me is a heavier thing than any crossbow bolt ever fired. "It wants giving."

The wind coming down off the hills smelled of snow and, underneath the snow, faintly, of summer. Two seasons arguing about whose hill this was. Above us Emberfall flickered—actually flickered, the whole otherworld guttering like a candle in a doorway—and somewhere behind the green glass towers I swear I heard a sound like a very large number of small voices all inhaling at once.

I thought: *I am going to be sick.*

I thought: *Good. That means you're paying attention.*

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about spending three years swallowing poison in small doses for a king. You learn the taste of duty. Not the romance of it—the actual taste. Duty tastes like bitter almonds and cold sweat at four in the morning. Duty tastes like smiling at a chancellor while your blood does quiet, careful battle with what he paid to have put in his wine. Duty is not a grand burning thing. Duty is showing up to the table. Every day. Dish after dish. Taking in the harm so it doesn't reach someone else, one measured, deliberate, survivable mouthful at a time.

Three years of practice. My whole invisible life, it turned out, had been training for exactly one job, and the job posting had finally arrived, and it said: *must have strong stomach, must tolerate grief in large quantities, must be willing to work evenings, weekends, solstices, and eternities.*

"August." Wren had turned to face me fully. Her knife was still out, but pointed at the ground now, forgotten, which for Wren was roughly equivalent to bursting into tears. "Whatever you're calculating in there. Stop it."

"I'm not calculating," I said. "I'm plating."

"You're *what?*"

"When a dish comes back to the kitchen untouched, you don't send it out again hoping. You remake it. From scratch. With what you have." I looked at my hands. Taster's hands. Unremarkable hands. Hands that had carried a hundred small kindnesses past a hundred sharp eyes and never once been seen doing it. "I have what I have. It turns out what I have is the exact thing required. Someone who knows how to take poison in doses."

"That's not—" Wren's voice broke and reassembled itself into fury, because that's what Wren's voice did instead of crying. "You don't get to volunteer. That's not how this works. We're a *we* now. I didn't spend a year hunting you across this city just to watch you eat yourself."

"Wren." I took her knife hand. She let me, which frightened me more than the stone did. "Your father carried that map for fifteen years. Carried it knowing it might get him burned, and carried it anyway, because the children in Emberfall were dying and he couldn't stand it. You think I don't know why the map chose your family? It chose people who give things back."

"That's sentimental nonsense."

"Yes," I agreed. "Almost all true things are."

Above us, the eclipse reached its fullness. The sun went dark as a covered dish, and the Hollow lit up entirely with the stone's green-gold light, and every shadow on the hillside swung around like a compass needle finding north. In that light I could see the crack in the world clearly at last—not just the split in the stone but what it had split: two realms that had once shared a single heartbeat, now beating separately, both failing.

The stone's light reached toward me. Not demanding. Asking. The way a starving guest asks, trying to pretend they aren't hungry, trying to be polite about needing everything you have.

And here is the last thing I'll say about fear, since I've apparently become a person who says things. I was terrified. Properly terrified, down in the marrow, in the place where even immunity can't reach. But terror, I have learned, is just interest. Terror is the body saying *this matters*. And I had spent three years being invisible precisely so that nothing would matter, so that nothing could touch me, so that I could go on making my small deniable kindnesses alone in the dark—and I was so tired, friends. So tired of mattering to no one.

The stone pulsed. Once. Twice.

Like a second heartbeat, looking for its first.

"Look after Wren," I told Tam. "She bites."

"I heard that," said Wren.

"Look after Tam," I told Wren. "They drown people."

"They *test* people," Tam protested.

"And somebody," I added, because the captain of the witch-finders was still standing there with her dead chancellor's cloak at her feet and her grandmother's blood in her cheeks, "should tell the city the truth about what actually causes winters. When it thaws. Which it's about to."

I stepped forward. The frost parted around my boots like a crowd making room.

The stone's light rose up to meet me, warm as a kitchen, terrible as a truth, patient as hunger—

and I opened my mouth, and began to swallow.
