## 19. One Dish at a Time

The stone was split down its heart like a loaf someone had torn in anger rather than hunger, and it wanted feeding.

That was the only way I could understand what was happening, and I have made a long career of understanding things through food. The covenant-stone stood in the center of the Hollow—a hollow indeed, a great bowl of hills cupping a floor of blue-veined rock—and it was hungry. Not the way Veyre had been hungry, gnawing at it for decades like a man stealing coins from his own treasury. Hungry the way a hearth is hungry. It wanted to be fed so it could give warmth back.

The trouble was what it ate.

"Grief," Hesk had told me on the flight up the hills, her voice failing between words like a candle guttering. "The fae realm is dying of grief, boy. Someone has to swallow it. A king never could—kings think grief is beneath them. But you." Her hand had found my wrist, physician-firm even now. "You've spent years eating poison one crumb at a time. What's one more meal?"

I had laughed then. I don't remember why. Terror does strange things to the humor.

Now Wren stood at my left shoulder with her knife drawn for no practical reason whatsoever—there was nothing left to stab, except possibly me—and Tam knelt at my right, pressing their ruined side, river-light leaking from under their hands where the wound refused to close entirely. Above us, through the Hollow's open mouth, the eclipse had begun. The sun went the color of an old copper coin, and across the sky, faint as breath on glass, another country flickered into view: towers of woven light, rivers the color of dawn, and small bright things moving in those rivers that might have been children.

They were guttering. Even from here I could see it. Candles in a draft.

"August." Wren's voice was steady in the way of a bridge cable—steady because everything depended on it. "You don't have to do this. We could try Tam's fragment first. We could—"

"We could die more slowly and less usefully, yes." I took off my coat. That seemed important, ceremonial somehow, though if I'm honest it was mostly because I didn't want to ruin the only good coat I owned. Three years as a king's taster on a taster's salary; you learn to protect your investments. "Hold this for me. If I survive, I'll want it back. If I don't, sell it."

"August—"

"Wren." I turned and looked at her properly. She had her father's eyes, I'd realized somewhere on the road—same sharp brown, same look of a person perpetually reading a map no one else could see. He had spent nine years walking toward this stone and died at the door of it. His daughter had carried him the last mile. "Your father gave me the map because my blood would open it. But I think—" I touched the oilskin memory of it, still folded against my heart. "I think he knew. I think he chose me not just for the blood. For the tongue."

She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. She just nodded once, hard, the way she did when she'd stopped arguing and started helping, and took my coat over her arm like it was a flag.

Tam looked up at me from the ground, water-bright eyes full of something I recognized as pity, which I could have done without.

"For what it is worth," they said, "the river has tasted you already. It said you were *brackish*. Sweet underneath."

"That is the nicest thing anyone has ever said to me before asking me to die."

"You are not going to die," Tam said. "You are going to become a door. Doors do not die. They just get very tired."

* * *

I put my hands on the stone.

It was warm. That surprised me—I had expected cold, all this winter coming off it—but it was warm the way a forehead is warm, feverish, working too hard at staying alive. And through my palms I felt it notice me. Not the way witch-finders notice you, all suspicion and boots. The way a starving creature notices the hand that feeds it: hope and shame in the same flinch.

*Feed me,* it said, in a voice that wasn't a voice but a taste at the back of my throat. *If you can.*

"If I can," I agreed, out loud, because talking steadied me and because I have always narrated my tastings, some habit from the palace—*bitter almond, no, bittercress, false alarm, sire*—and because I did not want the last thing I ever said to be silence.

I opened myself the way I opened myself to a suspect dish. You cannot brace against a poison and taste it truly at once; you must go soft, receive, catalog. So I went soft.

And the realm's grief came in like the first course.

It tasted of frost-killed gardens. That was the opening note—green things dying in their beds midsummer, the king's roses blackening, and beneath that, wider, whole orchards of light going out in Emberfall, mothers of both peoples standing in doorways with empty arms. It tasted of the original betrayal too, old and vinegared: a human king hoarding the stone, oaths curdling in the mouth, the fae turning away in hurt that had calcified over centuries into this winter, this long slow dying breath. It tasted of every charm-seller burned in the market square, ash on the wind, ash in the lungs of everyone who watched and did nothing.

I swallowed.

It went down wrong, the way grief always goes down wrong, catching behind the sternum, sitting there like a stone of its own. My knees hit the rock. Somewhere far away Wren shouted my name.

"One dish at a time," I told the stone, or myself, or the dead courier whose map had led us here. "That's all anything is. One dish at a time."

I swallowed again.

Here is what nobody tells you about poison-tasting, about the immunity I built crumb by crumb over three years: it isn't that the poison stops hurting. It's that you learn the shape of the hurt so well it becomes almost familiar. Almost a friend. Bittercress on a Tuesday. Almond on a feast day. You make room for it at the table. And the grief of two realms, I discovered, has a shape too, and my tongue—the cursed, blessed, deniable, invisible tongue that had kept me alive this long—knew how to find it.

So I fed the stone the only way I knew how. Dish by dish.

The first dish: the winter itself, cold and mineral, like licking a frozen gatepost, which I did once as a child and never told anyone about. Swallowed.

Second course: the fear. Every mother in Emberfall watching her light-child dim. Every human farmer watching frost eat the barley in July. Fear tastes of tin and spit. Swallowed.

Third: the rage. The fae's rage at the betraying bloodline, hot as a soup left too near the fire, scorching going down—and tangled in it, horribly, Veyre's own rage, decades of him drinking the stone dry and calling it governance. I held that mouthful longer. Rage is hard to swallow without choking on it yourself. But there was grief under it, the way there's stock under every sauce, and I found the grief and drank that instead and let the rage pass through me like a flavor I decline to finish.

Somewhere in the fourth dish I lost track of time. The eclipse hung above the Hollow's mouth like a lid on a pot, holding the heat in. I became aware, distantly, that Wren had come to kneel beside me, one hand flat on my back between my shoulder blades—not pushing, not pulling, just present, the way you sit with someone at a sickbed. Tam was singing. River-songs, low and wordless, and I understood they were timing my swallows to the song, pacing me the way you pace a drinker, keeping me from gulping what must be sipped.

Sipped. Yes. Small doses. Three years of small doses had been practice for this, though whoever had been arranging my life had neglected to mention it, which I intend to file a complaint about if I find the office responsible.

Fifth dish: loneliness. This one nearly broke me, because it was mine. All those years of charms disguised as superstition, luck-knots sold to widows, blessings whispered so quietly they could be denied. A whole life built out of small deniable kindnesses, and the loneliness of that—of being kind in secret until kindness felt like a symptom—came up the stone's channel along with everything else, and I recognized it, and I wept while I swallowed it, and the stone sealed each tear into itself like a cook sealing a pastry with egg-wash, neat and final.

"It's working," I heard Hesk say, though Hesk had been dying on the hillside and should not have been speaking. Perhaps the stone let her speak. Perhaps doors open both ways. "Look—the frost. Look at the frost."

I couldn't look. I was on the ninth dish, which was forgiveness, and forgiveness tastes of bread, unexpectedly, plain bread, warm, and I had to swallow it in pieces because I had never believed I deserved any and it stuck in my throat like dry crust.

Tenth dish: hope. Hope tastes of citrus and salt, which sounds wrong until you've had it.

Eleventh: the future, which has no taste at all yet, which tastes of an empty plate waiting, and I swallowed the emptiness too, gladly, because an empty plate means someone intends to cook.

* * *

I don't know how many dishes there were. The couriers of great houses count courses by the dozen; I counted by endurance. At some point the grief stopped arriving in dishes and started arriving as one continuous river, and I stopped swallowing and simply *opened*, and the stone drank around my heartbeat the way a vine drinks around a trellis.

Because that was the thing I finally understood, kneeling there with two realms' sorrow running through me: the stone didn't want a meal. It wanted a *metronome*. Grief unmeasured drowns. Grief given rhythm—one heartbeat, then another, one dish, then another—becomes endurable. Becomes music, eventually. The covenant had never needed a king's power. It had needed someone willing to keep time.

My heart beat. The stone beat. For a moment the two rhythms argued, syncopated, ugly.

Then they matched.

Warmth came up through the rock like an oven coming to temperature, and the split in the stone began to draw closed—not quickly, not dramatically, but with the patient inevitability of bread rising, of ice melting, of a wound closing from the inside out. The last seam sealed directly beneath my palms, and I felt it take my pulse and keep it, a second heartbeat answering mine through the stone, and I thought, absurdly, *well. Now I'm a clock.*

Above us, the eclipse released the sun.

Light poured down into the Hollow, ordinary golden daylight, and through it I watched the other realm's flickering image in the sky steady. The towers of woven light stopped guttering. The dawn-colored rivers ran brighter. And the small bright things moving in them—the children—flared, all together, like ten thousand candles relit by one careful hand.

The winter broke over the hills with a sound I will hear for the rest of my life: a groan, a crack, a rush, like the whole landscape exhaling after holding its breath since spring. Snow slid from the slopes in long white sheets. Beneath it, green. Actual green, the shocking tender green of things that had waited under frost for months and decided, against all advice, to live anyway.

I fell over sideways.

Wren caught me before I hit the rock, which was fortunate, because I had no plans left beyond falling and had executed them thoroughly. My hands were shaking. My whole self was shaking. Everything hurt in the specific way of having eaten something enormous, and also in the general way of having eaten two realms' worth of sorrow, which I note for the record is not covered by any physician's training, Hesk's included.

"August." Her face above mine, wet-eyed, furious with relief. "You absolute idiot. You wonderful, impossible—"

"Careful," I rasped. "Flattery. On an empty stomach."

She laughed, and it cracked halfway through into something else, and she pressed her forehead to mine and stayed there while the thaw ran down the hillsides around us in a hundred silver threads.

Tam crawled over, still leaking river-light but leaking less of it, and pressed their ear to the sealed stone, and then to my chest, comparing notes. "In tune," they pronounced. "Mostly. You're a little flat on the left side."

"I've been told. Many times. By choirmasters and physicians and one particularly rude goat."

Hesk was sitting up against the stone, color in her face for the first time since the ambush, looking at me with an expression I'd seen her wear over patients she'd given up for lost. "The fever breaks," she said softly. "It shouldn't. By every law of the art, the fever breaks and takes the patient. But here you are. Complaining about goats."

"Habit."

She reached up and gripped my hand, physician-hard. "Listen to me, boy. The stone keeps your heartbeat now. Which means you keep the realm's grief. It will not leave you alone in a room forever—it will visit. Some mornings it will sit on your chest like a cat. When it comes, you will not fight it and you will not drown in it. You will measure it. One dish at a time. Promise me."

"One dish at a time," I promised. It was, after everything, the only vow I had ever made that I understood completely.

Above the hills, the last of the image faded gently from the sky—the other realm withdrawing to its own side of the door, as polite realms do—but not before one of the small bright things came near the surface of the picture and paused there, and I would swear, on my professional palate, that it looked directly down at the Hollow, at the stone, at me, and dipped once, the way a diner nods to the kitchen.

Then the sky was only sky, blue and rinsed and smelling of rain and green things.

Wren helped me up. My legs worked, in the sense that they moved when instructed and complained throughout. Together the four of us stood at the mouth of the Hollow and watched the winter run off the world, and I felt the stone's second heartbeat inside my ribs, keeping time, patient as a kettle.

"Well," I said at last, when none of the appropriate solemn words arrived. "I don't know about either of you, but I have not eaten anything in two days that I actually *wanted*."

Wren stared at me. Then Tam started laughing—that bubbling river-laugh—and Hesk snorted, and Wren shook her head helplessly, and we started down the hill toward the city, four battered oath-kin, while below us the thaw waters sang and somewhere in the waking streets a thousand kitchens, unaware that anything had changed, began to think about supper.

But at the edge of my hearing—new hearing, perhaps, or maybe it had been there all along and I'd only just learned to listen—the river was murmuring my name. And far off, from the direction of the city, came bells. A great many bells. Ringing the way bells ring when a king has died in the night.

Or when someone else has taken the throne.
