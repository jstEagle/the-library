## 20. After the Thaw

The morning the winter broke, Wren woke because the birds had come back.

Not gradually, either — not the polite trickle of a normal spring, one thrush at a time, testing the air. No: the whole hillside above the Hollow erupted at once, as if every bird in the river valley had been holding its breath for three years and had finally, collectively, decided to let it out. Wren lay on her bedroll in the mouth of the cave and listened to the racket with her eyes still shut, and for one long, disoriented moment she thought she was dreaming of her father's farm, where the mornings had sounded exactly like this.

Then she remembered. And she opened her eyes to green.

The frost that had sheathed the hills since midsummer was running off them like a fever breaking. Water sheeted down the rock faces in silver threads; the blackened heather was lifting itself back up, stem by stubborn stem, shaking off its coat of ice; and down in the valley the city lay spread out beneath a sky so blue it looked embarrassed about all the gray it had made them wear.

Wren sat up too fast and banged her head on the low stone of the cave ceiling, which felt appropriately humbling for the day after a miracle.

"Ow," she said, to no one.

"Careful," said Tam's voice from deeper in the cave, hoarse but alive. "The world just got fixed. It'd be poor manners to crack your skull on it."

She found him propped against the wall where they'd left him, his bandaged side spotted but not soaked, his river-gray eyes fixed on the light spilling through the entrance. He looked worse than he had before the ambush and better than he had any right to look after it. Half-fae healing, Hesk would have said, if Hesk were here. Hesk was not here. That was a grief for later, or maybe not later — maybe for right now, folded into everything else, the way you fold salt into bread dough so no one can pick it out again.

"It came back," Wren said. It wasn't a question. She could see it. But saying it aloud made it true, and she wanted very badly for it to be true.

"The river first," Tam said. "I felt it an hour before dawn. Like a knot untying somewhere under my ribs." He smiled, and it wobbled at the edges. "My mother's people are singing. I can't hear them properly yet, but I can feel the vibration. Like standing near a bell."

"And August?"

Tam's smile went careful. "Still sleeping. Still breathing." A pause. "Still August. Mostly."

*Mostly* hung in the cave air between them, and neither of them reached for it. There would be time enough to learn what *mostly* meant.

* * *

They walked down into the city that afternoon, because staying away had become impossible.

The roads were mud and miracle. Farmers stood in their fields staring at shoots that had no business existing in what should have been late autumn — barley green as emeralds, apple trees shouldering out blossoms alongside their dead fruit, the whole landscape confused and delighted about it, like a dog that has heard its person come home after ten years and cannot decide whether to bark or weep. Some of the farmers did both.

The city gates stood open. Nobody had ordered them open; there simply hadn't been anyone left willing to close them. Inside, Aldermere was waking the way the hillside had — all at once, indecorously, with noise. People were in the streets. Not the furtive, hooded, quick-eyed crowds of the winter, moving between market and hearth like mice between walls, but actual people, faces upturned, doors flung wide, someone in Tanner's Row beating a pot with a spoon in a rhythm that could charitably be called music.

And the river — Wren stopped at the bridge over the Liss because everyone else had stopped at the bridge over the Liss, and because stopping was the only reasonable response.

The fae light was back.

It rose off the water the way heat shimmer rises off summer cobbles, except it wasn't heat, it was *light*, gold-green and slow, drifting upstream in ribbons like the river was exhaling something it had held underwater for three years. Children leaned over the parapets trying to cup it in their hands. An old woman was crying into her apron. A fisherman swore — loudly, joyfully, with tremendous elaboration — that he'd seen a lantern-fish leap, which hadn't happened in living memory, and half the bridge believed him immediately and the other half pretended to disbelieve him while edging closer to the water.

"They're really there," Wren said quietly.

"All along," Tam said. "Just... starving." He rested his hand on the bridge rail, and for a moment the light gathered toward his fingers like moths. "They know. Word travels fast when you're made of song. They know what was done at the Hollow." His eyes found hers. "They know whose heartbeat is keeping the door open."

Wren looked downstream, toward the hills, toward the cave mouth where August Amberdale slept off the single largest meal anyone had ever eaten.

"Then they can thank them by never making me attend another fae negotiation," she said, and Tam laughed, and the laugh hurt, and it was good that it hurt.

* * *

The proclamation went up on the third day.

By then the old king was dead — the wasting had taken him two nights after the thaw, gently, in his sleep, which was more than most kings get — and his daughter had been crowned in a ceremony notable chiefly for how quickly it was organized and how few robes were involved. Queen Isolde, it turned out, had spent the war winters quietly stockpiling grain and grudges, and both in considerable quantity.

Her first act was read from the palace steps by a herald with a magnificent voice, and Wren heard it from the crowd, pressed shoulder to shoulder with bakers and tanners and a suspicious number of hedge-folk who had emerged from whatever cracks they'd been hiding in:

*"Be it known: the laws proscribing the gentle arts are struck from the book entire. Let it be further known that the crown acknowledges its debt to those who kept small lights burning through the long dark — and let no witch-finder's badge be worn in this city again, save in a museum, with a placard explaining our shame."*

The crowd roared. Somewhere near Wren, a woman in baker's clothes began to laugh and couldn't stop, and Wren recognized her — the widow from the night market, the one August had sold luck-charms to for pennies, back when pennies and luck-charms were all either of them had. The woman caught Wren looking and grabbed her arm.

"He did it," the widow said. "Your friend, the taster. He did it." Her eyes were wet and fierce. "I knew there was something kind in those charms. You can't fake kindness in a charm. It goes sour otherwise. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that," Wren agreed solemnly, though until this year she had not been everyone, and had believed charms went sour because magic was poison, full stop. Her father had died believing the map mattered more than the man carrying it. She was still learning the arithmetic the other way round.

That evening the new queen sent for them — "them" being Wren, Tam, and a hollow-eyed August who had to be steered around furniture — and received them in a room much smaller than any throne room, with tea instead of wine, which told Wren more about Isolde than the coronation had.

"You're the courier's daughter," the queen said to Wren, before any formalities. "The one who finished the route."

"He started it," Wren said. "I mostly yelled at people along the way."

"A sound method of governance. I intend to use it extensively." Isolde looked at each of them in turn — at Tam, half-fae, standing in a royal audience without flinching; at August, who still moved like someone listening to a second heartbeat under their own; at last back to Wren. "The Chancellor's trial begins next month. I'm told you witnessed what he attempted at the Hollow."

"I witnessed it," Wren said. "So did August."

"We'll testify," August said quietly.

The queen studied them. "You understand," she said, not unkindly, "that your testimony will require explaining what you are now. What the covenant made you. There are still people in this city — good people, frightened people — who will hear 'living bridge between realms' and reach for the old fears."

August smiled. It was a tired smile, but it reached their eyes, and Wren noticed — not for the first time — that the eyes themselves had changed. There were colors in them now that hadn't been there before. Gold, faintly, at the edges. Ember-colored.

"Your Majesty," August said, "I spent six years tasting poison for strangers. I promise you, explaining myself at a trial is well within my tolerance."

Isolde laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of her — and Wren decided, then and there, that the new queen might do.

* * *

Chancellor Veyre's trial took nine days, and Wren attended all of them, sitting in the public gallery with her hands folded and her jaw set, watching the man who had burned her father's world try to explain why he had drained a covenant-stone for forty years rather than share it.

He looked smaller in chains. That was the thing nobody warned you about. Veyre, who had loomed over the city like weather, who had turned fear into infrastructure — in the dock he was just an old man with excellent posture and dying faith in his own arguments. He testified that he had acted to protect the realm from fae predation. He testified that the covenant was slavery dressed as partnership. He testified, at length, with the fluency of a man who had rehearsed his innocence for decades.

And then August testified.

Wren had expected eloquence. August was not eloquent, exactly, but they were precise, and precision in a courtroom cuts deeper than eloquence ever manages. They described the scrying glass. They described Veyre's offer — *the map and freedom for the opening of the Hollow* — delivered while fae children guttered in the glass like candles in wind. They described the moment the stone had rejected him.

"Inheritance without obligation," August said. "The stone asked him for service and he tried to take it by force. It wasn't cruelty, exactly. It was..." They paused, searching, head tilted as if tasting the word. "Indigestion. The covenant is a meal. It has to be swallowed. He wanted to plate it and sell tickets."

A ripple of bewildered laughter went through the gallery. Even the judges' mouths twitched. And Wren watched Veyre's composure crack — not at the accusations, not at the evidence, but at being made ridiculous, which was the one poison he had no immunity to.

The sentence was read on the ninth day: stripping of office, seizure of estates, imprisonment for the term of his unnatural life. Which, the court noted grimly, might now be considerably shorter, since the stone he'd been siphoning had closed to him forever.

Outside the courthouse, the sun was shining, absurdly, generously, and a charm-seller had set up a stall directly across the street, operating openly, legally, with a hand-painted sign reading CHARMS — SMALL KINDNESSES, FAIR PRICES. Wren stood on the courthouse steps and cried, briefly and furiously, and let Tam pretend not to notice, and bought a luck-charm she didn't need.

"For the supper house," she said, daring him to comment.

"What supper house?" said Tam.

"Exactly," said Wren.

* * *

She went up to the Hollow alone, on a gray-gold morning a week later, with her father's map wrapped in oilcloth and a shovel borrowed from a hospice gardener who had refused payment twice and then accepted a charm against blisters.

The walk took half the day. The hills were loud with returning things — bees already, impossibly, working the heather; a red hawk riding the thermals; water everywhere, chuckling down gullies that had been silent ice for three years. Wren talked as she walked, because she always talked when she walked this road, because silence on this road felt like leaving him behind.

"—and the queen's herald mangled my name at the trial, called me 'Mistress Renwick,' which isn't even close, and August elbowed me, and I nearly lost it right there in front of the whole court. And Tam's walking again, properly walking, he says the river does half his healing if he sleeps with his feet in it, which is disgusting and effective, which describes Tam entirely. And the supper house is real now, Da. We signed a lease. It's at the foot of these hills, actually, the old toll-house, you'd hate it, the roof leaks, we're fixing the roof—"

The Hollow's mouth opened ahead of her, dark and quiet in the hillside. The split stone stood beside it, sealed now — whole, seamless, humming faintly, warm to the touch the way a sleeping animal is warm. Wren pressed her palm flat against it, as she did every visit, and felt the answer come: a second pulse, deep and slow, meeting hers.

August's heartbeat. Keeping the door.

Inside the threshold, where the light fell just so, she knelt and unwrapped the map.

Her father's handwriting covered every inch of it — thirty years of notes, corrections, crossings-out, little arrows pointing at nothing, marginal complaints about the weather and the food and the state of the roads. *Third inn in Marlow's Cross has lice.* *The script shows only to oath-blood, confirmed AGAIN.* *If anything happens to me, find the taster.* Wren had hated that last note, once. Now she understood it was the bravest sentence he ever wrote: a dying man admitting he couldn't finish, and trusting a stranger to.

His work was finished. Every name on the map accounted for — Mother Hesk, whose grave on the hospice hill already grew flowers out of season; Tam, tending the river; the final name, Veyre, answered for. The route complete. Delivered.

Wren dug at the Hollow's mouth, in the soft earth where the first green shoots were coming through, and buried the map with the care she'd have used on an urn. She didn't say a eulogy. He'd have hated a eulogy. She said, "There. Told you it mattered," and patted the ground twice, the way you pat a shoulder, and stood up, and let herself cry for exactly as long as she needed, which was longer than she'd planned and shorter than she feared.

When she turned around, August was standing at the cave mouth.

Wren hadn't heard them approach. She suspected she never would again; August moved now like something that belonged to the landscape. They were thinner than before the reforging, and paler, and their eyes had that gold at the edges, and they were holding two steaming bowls with a loaf of bread tucked under one arm like a besieging army's provisions.

"You bury it, and then you starve yourself walking home," August said. "Typical courier discipline. Sit down. Soup."

"It's not soup weather."

"Every weather is soup weather. This is the first law of the covenant."

Wren laughed despite herself and sat on the sun-warmed stone, and took the bowl, and tasted it.

And there it was — she'd heard August describe it at the trial, the thing they carried now, the change — a taste beneath the taste. The soup was barley and leek and good salt, and underneath it ran something else entirely: honey and woodsmoke and river-stone and the smell of the air before rain, the flavor of Emberfall threaded through the human dish like a golden wire. August cooked everything that way now. They couldn't help it. Both realms lived on their tongue, in every bite, forever.

"Well?" August asked, watching her face with an anxiety that was almost funny, coming from the person who had swallowed a realm's grief whole.

"It tastes like being alive," Wren said. "Both kinds."

August's face did something complicated and bright. "That's the review I'm putting on the sign."

They ate together in the Hollow's doorway, passing the loaf back and forth, watching the hawk turn circles over the green hills. Below, far off, the city smoked gently from ten thousand chimneys, and the river flashed gold-green on its way to the sea, and somewhere in it all a supper house with a leaky roof was waiting for its cook and its doorkeeper to come home.

"Tam says the fae want to send an envoy," Wren said eventually. "Formal relations. Trade. Whatever fae consider trade — songs, probably, and weather."

"Mm."

"He also says the envoy arrives next week. And that they've requested to meet 'the Door-Keeper and the Bridge' specifically, and that the Bridge apparently promised them dinner."

August went very still. "I promised them *what*?"

"You fed their children's grief back to them with a spoon, August. You're never escaping hospitality now." Wren grinned and stole the last of the bread. "Welcome to belonging. It's terrible. There's always somebody arriving."

Far below, at the edge of hearing, the bells of Aldermere began to ring — not for mourning, not for war. Just ringing. Because they could.

August sighed, and stood, and brushed the crumbs from their knees, and held out a hand to pull Wren up.

"Right," they said. "One dish at a time, then."
