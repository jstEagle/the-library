## 21. The Taster's Table

The king's chamberlain arrived on a Tuesday, which August would later decide was the first mistake. Tuesdays were for inventory. Anyone who had ever run a kitchen knew you did not interrupt a person mid-inventory unless the building was on fire.

"The crown," announced Chamberlain Odell, standing in the doorway of what had been, until three weeks ago, a derelict granary at the foot of the Hollow road, "wishes to offer you a title."

August looked up from a crate of river onions that Tam had hauled up from the shallows that morning, still dripping and smelling gloriously of mud. "A title."

"Baron Amberdale of the Thawed Marches. Lands in the eastern hills. A seat at court. A household staff of forty." Odell said it all with the flat reverence of a man reciting someone else's grocery list. "The new king wishes to honor the one who ended the winter."

August set down an onion. It was a good onion—fat, sweet-smelling, with just a whisper of fae light along its roots where Tam's river had touched it—and August found they could not stop looking at it while a barony was being offered to them like a plate of cold mutton.

"Forty staff," August said.

"At least."

"Who would train them?"

"Trained staff come with the title."

"They'd come with *court* training," August said. "They'd flambé things I asked them to simmer. They'd call my stock 'jus.' They'd die of shock when I licked a spoon."

Odell blinked. "You... lick spoons?"

"It's how I know the food is safe," August said. "It's rather the whole point of me."

The chamberlain opened his mouth, closed it, and visibly reorganized his argument. August watched him do it with the same attention they once gave to suspicious aftertastes—the faint bitterness of a bribe being reformulated, the metallic tang of wounded pride.

"The king," Odell tried again, "is prepared to be generous."

"And I'm prepared to be grateful," August said. "But I have a supper house opening in nine days, a roof that leaks over table six, and a baker two streets down who has already threatened to outbid me for Wren's services if I don't get her a proper door hinge. A baron cannot fix a door hinge. Believe me. I've seen barons. They stand near doors and look important."

"You're refusing the crown."

"I'm declining the crown," August corrected gently. "Refusing sounds so final. Declining leaves room for me to send the palace a pie at solstice."

They sent the pie anyway, later, with a note: *From your former taster, who checked it twice.* The king reportedly laughed for the first time since his father's death, which August counted as the most useful thing a declined title ever produced.

* * *

The supper house had no name for its first week, mostly because August and Wren could not stop arguing about it.

"'The Hollow Spoon,'" Wren said, arms crossed, leaning against the doorframe she had installed herself—crooked, but crooked with conviction. "People will remember it."

"People will remember it because it sounds like something you'd find in a ditch," August said.

"Better than 'The Taster's Table,' which sounds like something you'd find in a *palace*. Which is exactly what we're not."

"We're not a ditch either."

"Says the person who served eel soup to a fae knight last night and called it rustic."

"It *was* rustic. He cried. Fae knights cry beautifully, by the way—it's very disconcerting."

In the end the sign was painted by a hospice volunteer named Merrit, who had terrible handwriting and no opinions, which made him the only qualified party. It read, in wobbling letters: **THE TASTER'S TABLE**, and beneath, smaller: *all dishes checked twice.*

Wren claimed the door the way she claimed everything—fiercely, immediately, and without asking. She kept a ledger of names behind the counter, because in the early days there were still folk who came to stare rather than eat. Humans who wanted to see a fae up close. Fae who wanted to see if the humans would flinch. Wren's job, as she described it, was to make everyone flinch equally and then feed them.

"If you're here to gawk," she'd say, with her father's courier bluntness, "there's a viewing platform on the hill. If you're here to eat, sit down. If you're here to ask whether the fae are real—" she'd pause, savor it, "—smell the bread."

Because that was the thing. Every dish carried a trace of both realms now; it was simply what food was, this close to the Hollow. The barley had a low golden hum to it. The honey tasted faintly of Emberfall's orchards, which grew, as far as anyone could tell, nowhere and everywhere. Tam brought watercress from the river bend where the fae light pooled at dusk, and even the plainest soup came out of the pot carrying a warmth that had nothing to do with the fire.

August had worried, in the beginning, that it would feel like a trick. That they were seasoning with wonder the way a bad cook seasons with salt, covering for thin stock. But the opposite proved true. The wonder didn't cover anything. It *revealed*. A good broth was more itself with a thread of Emberfall in it, the way a song is more itself in a stone room. Human hunger met fae sweetness somewhere in the middle of every bowl, and neither side had to pretend to be the other.

The first night, a widow who had lost two sons to the witch-finders' purges sat down across from a fae child whose wings had been frost-bitten during the Long Winter and never quite recovered their shine. Neither spoke to the other. Both ordered the plum tart. By the second helping they were arguing, quietly, about whether the plums had been better before the thaw or after, and August, watching from the kitchen pass, had to step away and chop something violently for a few minutes, because some sights undo a person.

That was the arithmetic August had never learned at court: belonging wasn't granted. It accumulated. Dish by dish. Table by table. Nobody had decreed this supper house into existence. It had simply grown, the way the green had grown back over the hills—stubbornly, unevenly, in places nobody planned.

* * *

Tam came up from the river every third morning, carrying whatever the water offered.

This was not a figure of speech. Tam genuinely asked the river, and the river genuinely answered, though its answers ran heavily toward fish, watercress, and the occasional drowned boot, which Tam would present with great ceremony and then apologize for. ("The river has a sense of humor," Tam explained once. "It's not a *good* sense of humor.")

On Thursdays Tam worked the pass beside August, sleeves rolled, hands smelling of cress and cold stone. Half-fae, passing human for thirty years, Tam cooked the way someone cooks who spent three decades afraid to be noticed doing anything: precisely, modestly, without flourish. But give Tam a fae guest and something loosened. The knife work turned musical. Once, cooking for a delegation from Emberfall's orchard-clans, Tam hummed an old river-song under their breath and the soup came out tasting of the song, and the delegation ate in silence for ten minutes and then asked, very politely, if they could have the recipe, and Tam had to explain that the recipe was *humming*, and there followed a long diplomatic discussion about whether humming could be written down.

August kept a chalkboard menu because the menu changed with the river's mood, and because writing prices in chalk meant they could be argued with, which half the clientele considered the evening's entertainment.

*Plum tart — 3 coppers.*
*Eel soup (rustic) — 2 coppers.*
*Bread with Emberfall honey — 1 copper, extra if you dip twice, Wren is watching.*
*Whatever the river gave us — ask Tam.*

The last line caused trouble only once, when a merchant from the capital demanded to know what "whatever" meant before ordering. Tam looked at him for a long moment and said, "Patience, mostly," and the merchant, to his credit, sat down and waited, and was rewarded with a pike so perfectly cooked he wept into his napkin and left a silver piece on the table, which Wren chased him down the street to return because the price was *two coppers* and she did not run a charity, except on the nights she did, which were more nights than she admitted.

* * *

There were hard evenings too. August would have been lying—not to mention professionally negligent—to claim otherwise.

Some guests came carrying grief the way others carried umbrellas. A woman whose sister had burned in the market square, back when Veyre's witch-finders ruled the streets, sat alone every Friday eating bread and honey and saying nothing to anyone. August never pressed. Instead, on Fridays, the bread was always warm, and the honey was always the darkest, sweetest jar, and after four months the woman began arriving early enough to help Wren stack chairs, and after seven she laughed at something Tam said, briefly, like a door opening onto a lit room and closing again—but open.

The fae had their own silences. Some of Emberfall's children, thawed but marked, came down from the hills and could not bear the smell of roasting meat—it reminded them of the burnings they had watched through the veil, helpless. So August learned to cook those nights without smoke, all braises and steaming and cold dishes dressed in river herbs, and no announcement was ever made, and the children came, and slowly the smell stopped being a wound and became, again, just dinner.

"You feed people like you used to taste for them," Wren observed one night, drying glasses. "Looking for what'll hurt."

"That's not—"

"It is. It's the same skill. You just aim it kinder now." She hung the glass on its hook. "Your poison-tongue finally found honest work."

August thought about that for a long time afterward. Three years of swallowing small doses so the king could eat safely. Three years of taking harm in measured sips so that someone else's table could be joyful. And here was the strange grace of it: none of it had been wasted. Not the immunity, not the loneliness, not even the fear. It had all been practice. For the Hollow. For the grief they'd swallowed whole beneath the split stone. For this—a room where harm was noticed, named, and quietly kept off the plate.

Belonging chosen, not inherited. August hadn't coined the phrase—that had been Mother Hesk, in one of her letters from the hospice, written in a physician's hand that marched across the page like a well-organized army. But August had adopted it the way one adopts a stray: completely, and with mild embarrassment.

* * *

On the supper house's hundredth night—which Wren insisted on celebrating despite August's protests that round numbers were a tyranny—Merrit painted a second line under the name on the sign. He did it without asking anyone, at dawn, standing on Wren's crooked ladder.

When they all came down to look, the sign read:

**THE TASTER'S TABLE**
*all dishes checked twice*
*all seats belong to whoever sits in them*

Wren stared at it for a long moment. "He's improved."

"He's added philosophy."

"I said what I said." She folded her arms, and August recognized the expression—the one that meant Wren was moved and would sooner swallow glass than admit it. "Fine. It stays."

That night the room filled past capacity. Fae orchard-keepers shared benches with palace clerks who had walked out from the city on rumor alone. A witch-finder—one of the old ones, pardoned under the new laws, still bearing the ash-smell of his former trade—came in, sat in the corner, ordered soup, and ate it with his back to the wall, waiting to be hated. Nobody hated him. Wren refilled his cup with the brisk contempt she reserved for everyone, which he later told August was the kindest thing anyone had done for him since the thaw.

And late, very late, when the lamps had burned to stubs and Tam had gone down to sleep by the river and Wren was counting the till with her usual suspicion that money was personally conspiring against her, August stood alone in the empty room and listened.

The floor hummed. Faintly. The way the covenant-stone hummed, deep under the hill, its second heartbeat keeping time with theirs. The whole building sat on the Hollow's foot like a child on a parent's lap, and the stone seemed—August would have sworn to this, had sworn it to Hesk in writing—to *like* having a supper house above it. All those years it had held oaths and betrayals and the weight of two peoples' grief. Now, above it: plum tart. Arguments about eel. A crooked door hinge. Chalkboard prices.

Perhaps that was what a healed covenant sounded like, August thought. Not trumpets. Cutlery.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when the knock came.

Not the ordinary knock—Wren's knock, three sharp raps meaning *trouble at the door, probably a drunk, possibly a critic*. This was softer. Almost apologetic. Two taps, a pause, one more.

August wiped down the last table, crossed the room, and opened the door.

A girl stood on the step. Sixteen, perhaps seventeen, travel-worn, with river mud to her knees and a satchel clutched to her chest the way Wren had once clutched her father's map. Her eyes were red-rimmed. Her accent was from far downriver, past the city, past anywhere August's tongue had reached.

She looked at August the way people had once looked at the king—with hope, which is heavier than any crown.

"Are you the taster?" she asked. "The one from the stories? The one who ate the winter?"

"I'm the one who eats," August said. "The rest got embellished. Are you hungry?"

"Yes." Then, quickly, before the kindness could settle: "But not—not only. I have something. My grandmother sent me. She said—" the girl faltered, steadied herself the way couriers and cooks and all practical people do, by getting to the facts. "She said you'd understand. She said to tell you the stone isn't finished."

August felt it then, faint and unmistakable, through the floorboards, through their own chest: the second heartbeat, quickening.

"Come in," August said, holding the door wide. "Tell me everything. But first—"

They stepped aside, letting the lamplight and the warmth and the smell of tomorrow's rising bread spill out onto the dark road.

"—first, you eat."
