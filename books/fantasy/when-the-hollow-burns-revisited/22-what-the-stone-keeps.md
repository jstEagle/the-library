## 22. What the Stone Keeps

A year is a strange unit of measurement for someone whose trade was once measured in mouthfuls.

August had spent three years thinking in sips—a drop of arsenic here, a grain of monkshood there, the body's ledger kept in grains and scruples. Then they had spent one impossible night swallowing a realm's grief by the bowlful, and since then time had refused to behave. It moved in seasons now. In harvests. In the slow green creep of things returning.

But a year—that, August could hold. A year ago this night, the eclipse had closed its eye over the Hollow, and August had sat down at the split covenant-stone as though to a table set for one, and begun to eat.

They went up every year now. Wren called it a pilgrimage. Tam called it maintenance, in the tone of a river-warden who took personally the health of all waterways, including metaphysical ones. August privately thought of it as visiting a relative—one who lived alone, kept no fire, and had been waiting a very long time for company.

The evening of the anniversary, they climbed alone.

* * *

The hills above the city wore their autumn colors like an old woman's shawl—rust and gold, patched at the elbows with gorse. Frost would come soon enough; everyone felt the season turning early, the way you feel weather in an old scar. But the frost that came now was honest frost, the ordinary kind, the kind that killed lettuce and not kingdoms. August had learned to tell the difference the way they'd once learned poisons: by aftertaste. The old winter had tasted of grief. This cold tasted only of apples.

The supper house had done brisk business all day. Wren had run the door with her usual tact, which was to say she had thrown out two drunk tanners, seated a fae grandmother and her human grandson at the same table without comment, and charged a visiting magistrate double because she didn't like his face. The kitchen had gone out to the last table. Now the ovens cooled, the lamplight burned low, and August had pressed a kiss to Wren's forehead, accepted Tam's solemn nod from the river road—"Tell it I said hello," Tam said, meaning the stone, meaning it entirely—and started up through the darkening bracken with a lantern and a basket.

You did not visit the Hollow empty-handed. That was the first rule Hesk had taught them, back when Hesk was still alive to teach anything: *an oath is just hospitality that outlives the guests.* Bring bread. Bring salt. Bring something sweet, because whatever else the fae were, wherever else they had gone, they had been creatures who understood that sweetness was a debt worth keeping.

So August climbed with a basket: a loaf baked that morning, a twist of grey sea-salt from the river flats, and a jar of plum preserves made by a baker's widow—the same widow who had once bought a luck-charm off a nervous food-taster in a night market, in another life. The widow's plums had won a ribbon at the harvest fair. She sent up a jar every month now, addressed simply to *the Bridge,* which was what people called August when they weren't calling them by name, and which August had never quite gotten used to and suspected they never would.

The Hollow's mouth opened in the hillside where it always had—a seam of dark stone half-swallowed by hawthorn, wide enough for two to walk abreast if they were friends, narrow enough that enemies had to go single file and think about their choices. The witch-finders' bonfires had long gone cold. The new king had repealed the magic laws in spring, standing in a square that still smelled faintly of ash, and had said six words that Wren claimed were too few and August claimed were exactly enough: *"What was burned will be rebuilt."*

Inside, the passage sloped down through earth that breathed warmth. That was the strangest thing about the Hollow now—it wasn't cold anymore. The first months, the depths had been winter-sore, aching like a bruised joint. But the stone had sealed around August's heartbeat, and hearts, whatever else you say about them, are warm.

The covenant-stone waited in its chamber below, whole again, or whole the way a healed bone is whole—with a memory of the break running through it like a vein of silver. August set down the lantern, laid out the bread and salt on the flat shelf of rock that served as altar, table, and listening-post all at once, and sat.

"Hello," they said, the way you'd greet anyone you loved but hadn't seen since Tuesday. "It's me."

And beneath them, patient as tide, the second heartbeat answered their own.

It had taken months to learn to hear it properly. At first it had been terrifying—a pulse in the bedrock that quickened when August's did, slowed when they slept, stuttered once, memorably, when Wren had nearly fallen off a roof (she insisted she hadn't fallen; she had been *descending with intent*). Hesk's notes, carried down from the hospice in Tam's careful hands, explained it in a physician's dry hand: *the anchor does not hold the covenant. The covenant holds the anchor. You are held, child. Try to let that be a comfort instead of a cage.*

Some days it was a comfort. Some days it was a cage with very good lighting. Most days it was simply true, the way a name is true—you carried it whether or not you examined it.

Tonight August sat with their palm flat against the stone and let the two heartbeats find each other. Thud. Thud-thud. Thud. Thud-thud. A rhythm like a waltz someone had only half learned.

"I brought plums," August said. "The good ones. And news, if you want it. The king ate at our table last week—incognito, or what passes for incognito in a man with a crown-shaped birthmark. He had the venison pie and asked for the recipe. Wren told him it was a family secret. We don't have a family secret. She just likes watching royalty suffer."

The stone said nothing, in the way it always said nothing, which was somehow also a way of saying everything. August talked anyway. They had learned that this was the rite—not the old blood-and-oath ceremony, not the drowning tests or the taste-riddles, but this: showing up, bringing bread, telling the truth out loud in a place where truth could be kept.

That was what Hesk had meant. An oath was hospitality that outlived the guests.

"So," August said, when the news ran dry. "A year."

The stone hummed under their hand, and for a moment—just a moment—August tasted something on the air that wasn't air: honey and woodsmoke and rain on hot stone, the flavor of Emberfall remembered, the flavor of every oath ever sworn in this chamber rising up like steam off soup. Somewhere beyond the sealed threshold, the fae realm turned on its own axis, lit by its own suns, children of light guttering no more. They did not come through. The door stayed shut. But they knew. On this night, of all nights, they knew.

"Same time next year?" August asked, and felt the heartbeat answer—thud, thud-thud—which was either yes or the world's most committed waltz.

They gathered the crumbs for the birds, banked the lantern, and began the climb back toward the lamplight of the supper house, feeling lighter than a person carrying a year-old burden had any right to feel.

* * *

The lights were still on when August came down the hill.

That was odd. Wren closed the door at midnight sharp and defended that policy like a fortress gate. Tam kept the river hours and was usually asleep before the lamps were trimmed. Yet the windows of the supper house glowed amber against the dark, and—August squinted—there was someone standing outside the door. Small. Hooded. Hovering at the threshold the way people do when they want to knock and are afraid to, or know how to knock and are afraid of what comes after.

August's heart did something complicated. Part of it—the part that remembered witch-finders' boots on stairwells, bloodied couriers dying against tenement doors—wanted to reach for the old instincts, the small deniable magics, the shadows. But the larger part, the part that had been fed for a solid year by Wren's terrible jokes and Tam's river-steady loyalty and a dining room full of humans and fae arguing amiably about seasoning, simply walked down the hill and opened the gate.

"You're late," said the stranger. "Or early. Grandmother said midnight, but she was always vague about which midnight."

Up close, the hood hid nothing much: a girl, perhaps seventeen, with her mother's practical hands and her grandmother's impatient eyes. There was flour on her sleeve. There was something else, too—something August's trained tongue catalogued without asking permission: the scent of oilskin and old ink, of long roads and longer silences.

"Your grandmother," August said carefully.

"Mara. She keeps a crossing-house on the eastern marshes." The girl lifted her chin, and August saw the fear underneath the bravado, recognized it the way you recognize your own handwriting. "She says the crossings are waking. All of them. Not just yours. Little doors, all along the old lines—she thinks the covenant you reforged didn't seal the realms apart. It sealed them *together*. Properly, this time. With an anchor instead of a king." She swallowed. "She says the doors need keepers. And she says—" the girl faltered, then steadied herself, getting to the facts the way practical people do, "—she says you'd understand what it costs. And what it's worth."

From inside the supper house came the unmistakable sound of Wren pretending not to eavesdrop, which involved dropping several pots.

August stood in the doorway a long moment. Behind them: the warm room, the cooling ovens, tomorrow's bread already proofing under cloth, a life built not on small deniable kindnesses but on large undeniable ones. Before them: a frightened girl with a map-shaped bulge in her coat and a question she'd carried a hundred miles.

Once, August would have counted the risks. Once, invisibility had been the whole art of survival—keep your charms small, your head down, your heart locked. Once, a stranger bleeding on the doorstep had been the beginning of terror.

But that was before. Before they'd learned that the stone keeps what you give it, and gives back what you need. Before found family forged under fire and grief carried forward instead of left behind. Before belonging, chosen daily like bread broken and shared.

"What's your name?" August asked.

"Nell," said the girl. "Nell Marsh."

"Well, Nell Marsh." August stepped aside, holding the door wide, letting the lamplight spill gold onto the dark road and the smell of tomorrow's rising bread drift out into the night. "It happens I know a place where travelers eat free on their first night. It happens I've got questions of my own, and a table big enough for all of them. And it happens—"

Somewhere far below, deep in the hill, patient as tide, the second heartbeat answered theirs: thud, thud-thud. Yes. Come in. There is room.

"—it happens the door was never really mine to keep shut. Come in from the cold. Tell me everything."

Nell Marsh hesitated on the threshold for one breath more—every traveler does; it's tradition—and then crossed it.

August closed the door behind her against the coming frost, and put the kettle on, and reached for a third bowl.
