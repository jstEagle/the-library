## 1. The Valley That Swallows Magic

There was a saying in Hollowvale that the mountains had closed their hands around the valley long ago and never quite let go — that the ridgelines north and south were knuckles of some sleeping giant, curled inward to keep the world out. Astrid Lockridge had grown up beneath that image, had traced it with her finger on frost-covered windows, and had never once believed it was only a story. The valley did feel like a fist. It felt like a hand clenched shut around everything inside it, pressing magic flat the way a stone presses flowers.

She had learned early that this was the valley's great mercy and its great prison both. Beyond Hollowvale's borders, magic lived the way fire lived — hungry, spreading, consuming whatever it touched. Here, at the ward-stones, it simply stopped. Travelers who came over the pass from the east said they felt it like stepping into cold water: the hum behind their teeth went silent, the second sight dimmed to ordinary seeing, charms cooled in their pockets like dead coals. They came to Hollowvale to be plain people. They stayed because plainness was safe.

Astrid had come to it as an infant, carried over the same pass in her mother's arms, wrapped in wool against a wind that her mother had walked through without flinching though every charm she owned must have been dying on her body one by one. Twenty-three years later, Astrid still did not know what her mother had fled. She knew only that they had arrived poor, bought a croft on the valley's western lip where the land rose toward the ward-stone ridge, and settled into lives so deliberately small that no one ever looked twice.

Smallness was an art. Her mother had taught it the way other mothers taught prayers: how to keep your fence mended but not finer than your neighbor's; how to let your roof-moss grow just thick enough to look neglected; how to laugh at the right moments at market and never too brightly; how to answer questions about your family with pleasant vagueness — *my mother passed some years back; we came from the east; no, I don't recall exactly which town.* Astrid had practiced these lessons until they sat in her bones like marrow. She was good at being small. She was so good at it that sometimes, in the grey hour before dawn, she could not find herself underneath it at all.

* * *

The morning the ewe began to die was the kind of morning Hollowvale did best: mist lying in the hollows like spilled milk, the sun not yet strong enough to burn it off, the whole world hushed and dripping. Astrid went down to the fold with two pails swinging from her yoke and stopped at the gate because something in the air felt wrong.

The sheep were standing wrong. Flocks have a weather of their own, a pressure and drift, and hers were pressed hard against the far wall of the pen, ears flat, leaving a clear space around the shape lying in the trampled grass by the water trough.

It was Maren. The old ewe, grey-faced, one eye clouded white since three winters past, the ewe who had been the first creature Astrid had ever spoken to with anything like honesty.

Astrid set down the pails. She unlatched the gate slowly, because fast movement would send the flock into a panic that would cost Maren strength she clearly did not have, and she crossed the wet grass and knelt.

Up close, the ewe's breathing told the story before anything else did. It came shallow and quick, with a catch at the top of each breath like a stitch pulling tight. Her flank heaved. Her good eye rolled toward Astrid and then away, toward nothing, and her legs made small paddling motions against the ground as if trying to rise and forgetting halfway through how.

"Ah," Astrid said softly. "Ah, old girl."

She put her hands on the ewe's neck, feeling for heat, for swelling, for the flutter of a pulse under the skin. The fleece was damp with dew and colder than it should have been. Something in the gut, then, or the blood — something turning bad in the dark interior places where no hand could reach. She had seen it before. She had buried four animals this way in her years on the croft, each time after long hours of warm cloths and drenches and whispered encouragement that changed nothing, because there are illnesses that are simply doors closing, and no amount of love holds a door against them.

She should fetch Brammel's tinctures. She should send word to the Hendersons, whose ram sired Maren's lambs, because they'd want to know. She should do all the ordinary things a stockwoman does when death comes into the fold, and she would, in a little while. But first — first there was the thing she did with no person watching, the thing that lived at the center of her like a swallowed coal.

Astrid glanced across the fold, across the mist-hung pasture, up at the grey bulk of the house with its moss-thick roof and its shuttered windows. Empty. The nearest neighbor, Tilda Marsh, was half a mile off and deaf besides. The world gave her the narrow privacy it always gave her, and she took it, the way she always did — guiltily, gratefully, with a glance at the sky as if the sky itself might be counting.

Then she opened the door inside herself.

It is difficult to describe what that is like, and Astrid had long since given up trying, even in her own thoughts. It was less like speaking than like listening turned inside out. All her life the beasts' voices had been there, faint as fish beneath ice — the sheep's low constant murmur of grass-and-water-and-weather, the dog's bright loop of now-now-now, the hens' flickering alarm-thread. In Hollowvale those voices came to her thin and muffled, worn down by the ward until they seemed almost like imagination. She had spent twenty-three years telling herself they were imagination. She had never once believed it.

She let herself listen truly, and Maren rose up in her mind like a shape seen through deep water: an old ewe's soul, simple and vast at once, all patience and rain and the smell of lambs in spring, and threaded through it now, a darkness. Not pain exactly. Pain the ewe could carry; pain was weather. This was a hole. A place where Maren should have been and was not, growing larger with each breath.

*I'm here,* Astrid said, in the way that was not words, or not only words. *I'm here, old girl.*

The ewe's clouded eye found her. And Maren — because Maren had always been the cleverest animal on the croft, and perhaps because dying loosens whatever holds a mind to its manners — pushed back at her, deliberate, a thought shaped with care:

*Cold,* the ewe said. *Going cold from the inside.*

"I know." Astrid's voice cracked on it. She pulled off her shawl and tucked it around the ewe's shoulders, knowing it was useless, doing it anyway. "I know you are."

*The little ones. Who'll count the little ones?*

Every spring, when the lambs came, Maren stationed herself at the pen's corner and watched the new ones with an attention Astrid had never seen in any other sheep, as though keeping a ledger of them. *Counting,* Astrid had once joked to the flock, *Maren's counting again.* She had never expected the joke to land. It had landed. Maren had looked at her with an expression she still could not describe, and after that, something between them had been understood that needed no further discussion.

"You've counted eleven springs of them," Astrid said aloud, her thumb moving along the ewe's jaw. "More than any ewe I've known. More than I could count myself, near enough."

*Lambs don't remember,* Maren said, and there was no bitterness in it, only the plain fact of it, which was somehow worse. *Grass doesn't either. Only you.*

"I'll remember," Astrid promised. "I'll remember all of it. Every lamb. The flood year, and the fox spring, and how you bit Brammel's son for pulling your ear." A wet sound escaped her that was meant to be a laugh. "He deserved it."

*Good,* said Maren. *Good. Cold now. Going where the grass—*

The thought frayed. Astrid pressed her forehead to the ewe's skull, between the horns, and stayed there while the breathing slowed, and slowed, and the great patient presence thinned like mist burning off, and then was not there, and the fold was full only of dew-smell and the nervous shifting of forty other sheep and the enormous silence where one voice had been.

She stayed kneeling in the wet grass long after. Long enough that her knees soaked through her skirts and her hands went numb around the shawl-wrapped head. The flock crept back to their corners and began, cautiously, to graze. The mist burned off the far fields. Somewhere down the lane a cart rattled toward market, and its driver would see the Lockridge woman in her fold, motionless, and think nothing of it — a woman sitting with a dead ewe, grieving the way farmers grieve, briefly and privately and without fuss.

That was the cruelty of it, Astrid thought. Not that she had to hide the gift. That hiding it worked so perfectly well.

* * *

She buried Maren by the drystone wall where the old ewes went, under the rowan her mother had planted the year they arrived, its berries red as wounds in the autumn. Digging gave her hands something to do that wasn't shaking. She spoke the burial words her mother had taught her — plain words, farmer's words, *earth to earth, wool to soil, thanks for the springs* — and did not use the Old Tongue even here, alone, with only a rowan tree for witness. Habit ran deeper than solitude. Habit was the whole architecture of her life.

While she shoveled, her mind did what it always did on such mornings: it circled the question she had been circling for twenty-three years, worrying it like a tongue against a broken tooth.

Her mother had died when Astrid was nine. Fever, the herbwoman said, the kind that takes the old and worn-out quickly. Astrid remembered the last days only in fragments: her mother's hands, cracked and steady, braiding Astrid's hair; her mother's voice, roughened, saying *listen to me, girl* with an urgency that frightened her more than the fever did; and the rules, recited over and over, rules Astrid could still recite now like catechism.

*Never speak to them where a soul can see. Never let them answer where a soul can hear. If anyone asks why the beasts come to you, say you're quiet and patient and they like that. If anyone asks where you learned it, say nowhere. If the wardens ever knock, you know nothing, you heard nothing, and girl — above all things — above all things —*

But her mother had never finished the sentence. She had gone somewhere very far away in the middle of it, eyes fixed on the window and the ridge beyond it, and by evening the fever had her wholly, and the sentence had gone with her. *Above all things.* Above all things what? Never tell? Never leave? Never listen? For fourteen years Astrid had carried the unfinished commandment like a stone in her shoe, a small perpetual bruise she could not locate or remove.

She had built her whole life in the shadow of it. She kept to the croft. She went to market on Thursdays, smiled at the right moments, bought her salt and thread, sold her cheese and wool, and came home. She was friendly in the way that deflects questions. She was useful in the way that discourages curiosity — everyone wanted her calm hands around a difficult birth or a fractious horse, and no one wanted to examine too closely why the horses gentled for her, why her hens never flew the pen, why her dog had never once barked at night unless something was genuinely wrong. Quiet gifts read as luck in a farming valley. Luck was acceptable. Luck could be crossed with a superstition and forgotten.

What could not be forgiven, what got women burned in the towns beyond the pass — Astrid had heard the stories the way everyone heard them, in fragments, over ale, told with a shiver that was half pleasure — was beast-speaking. Soul-talking. The gift that marked you as court-touched, Undercourt-claimed, a thing that trafficked in the parts of a person that ought to belong only to the sky. She did not know if the stories were true. She knew only that her mother had crossed a mountain range with a baby in her arms to bring them both to a place where magic went to die, and that her mother had never once, in all the years before the fever, spoken to so much as a barn cat within Astrid's hearing.

Which raised the question Astrid did not let herself ask on most days, and asked on days like this one, standing over fresh-turned earth with a shovel in her hands: *where had Astrid's gift come from, if not from her?*

Gifts ran in blood. Everyone knew that. Charmsingers begat charmsingers, storm-readers begat storm-readers, and beast-speakers — the stories agreed on this much — beast-speakers came by it one way only: inheritance, or bargain, and bargains were paid in souls.

Her mother had been many things — tired, careful, kind in her brusque way, endlessly watchful at the edges of things. But she had not been touched. Astrid would have staked her life on it. There had been nothing in her mother that listened sideways, nothing that reached. She had been the most thoroughly closed person Astrid had ever known, closed like a fist, closed like a valley.

So where —

The shovel rang against a stone. Astrid straightened, pushed hair from her face, and refused the thought, the way she refused it every time, tamping it down with work the way you tamp soil over a grave.

Above all things, her mother had said, and never finished, and Astrid had made a life out of the silence where the rest of the sentence should have been.

* * *

Evening came down the valley the way it always did, blue and slow, smoke rising straight from the chimneys of the farms below because there was no wind — there was rarely wind in Hollowvale; the mountains saw to that. Astrid fed the dog, Bristle, who leaned his whole warm weight against her leg in the particular way he saved only for the day Maren died, having watched from the fence line that morning with his ears low. He could not tell her, not truly, not through the ward's muffling — the beasts' speech here was like hearing a song through a wall, melody without words — but he could lean. She scratched his ruff and told him she was all right and knew that he knew she wasn't.

She ate bread and hard cheese standing at the window. Below her, the valley lay in its blue dusk, forty hearths, forty families, a whole small world folded between the mountain's fingers, and every soul in it certain that magic was a story told about elsewhere.

She should sleep. Tomorrow there was cheese to press and the far meadow to cut and Brammel's tithe-list to check before quarter-day. Ordinary life stood waiting for her, patient as a well-broken horse, and tomorrow she would climb back onto it and ride it another day, another season, another year, until the years themselves became the wall she hid behind.

Instead she stood at the window until full dark, looking west, toward the ridge and the ward-stone that crowned it — a leaning grey fang of rock older than the valley's memory, which herders touched for luck and children were warned away from. From here it was only a paler blackness against the black hillside.

And it was there — as she stood in the dark with Bristle's warm flank against her knee and Maren's grave raw behind the barn — that Astrid felt it. Faint, so faint she might have dreamed it, might have invented it out of loneliness and grief the way she had accused herself of inventing everything for twenty-three years.

From beyond the ward, from the wild country on the far side of the ridge, something answered her listening. Not the muffled fish-under-ice murmur of the valley's beasts. Something else. Something that pressed against the ward from outside like a palm laid flat against a windowpane, curious, searching — and then, unmistakably, unmistakably, it spoke her name.
