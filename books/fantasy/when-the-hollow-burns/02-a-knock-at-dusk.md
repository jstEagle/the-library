## 2. A Knock at Dusk

The knock came at the hour when Hollowvale's shadows grow teeth.

Astrid was in the byre with her hands inside a ewe — literally inside, arm buried to the elbow in warm wool and afterbirth, easing a lamb that had decided to arrive sideways and stubborn. The ewe's name was Marigold, though no one but Astrid knew it, and she was saying, *easy now, easy, you great foolish girl, he's nearly turned* — words that meant nothing to any human ear within a hundred miles, and everything to the animal beneath her hands.

Marigold sighed the deep, groaning sigh of her kind, and the lamb slid free into Astrid's palms, wet and astonishing and furious about being born.

"There," Astrid murmured, wiping his nose clear with a handful of straw. "That's the whole world, little one. Disappointing, isn't it."

She was still laughing softly at herself when the knock came — three small raps against her cottage door, spaced like a heartbeat skipping.

No one knocked at dusk. No one knocked at all, really; Hollowvale's people called ahead across fences or simply let themselves into one another's kitchens the way water finds its level. And no one walked the lane to her croft after sundown, because everyone knew the beast-woman kept strange hours and stranger company, and because the lane past the millpond went dark early this time of year, the hills standing up black against a sky gone the color of a bruise healing.

Astrid rose slowly, wiping her hands on her apron. The dogs were already awake.

That was the first wrong thing. Bram and Sorrel slept like stones through thunder, through foxes in the henhouse, through the warden's annual inspection. But now they stood pressed against the far wall of the byre, hackles lifted in twin ridges down their spines, and they were not barking. They were silent in the particular way of animals who have decided that silence is safer than voice.

"Hush," Astrid told them, though they hadn't spoken. "Hush, what is it?"

Bram looked at her with his amber eyes and answered in the wordless way dogs answer, a shape of feeling rather than language: *wrong, outside, wrong.*

The hens had gone quiet too. The goat, Juniper, stood at the fence line staring toward the lane with her rectangular pupils blown wide as coins. Even the bees in the white hive behind the kitchen had changed their note — Astrid heard it without meaning to, the way she heard everything, the hive's hum dropping half a tone, gathering itself like a held breath.

Everything alive on her croft was listening to something dead at her door.

Astrid took the iron poker from beside the hearth — not for the thing knocking, but for whatever ordinary danger might be hiding inside an extraordinary evening — and crossed the yard. The light was failing fast. Mist was coming off the millpond in long gray banners, and the first star had opened above Torvane's shoulder, the mountain that held the valley's northern wall like a hand cupped around a candle flame.

She opened the door.

A child stood on her step.

It was perhaps seven years old, though hunger and road-dust made age impossible to read. Mud had dried in layers on its face and clothes, cracked like old paint, and beneath the mud the skin was the brown of river-washed oak. The hair had been cut raggedly, recently, with something duller than a knife. The eyes were what stopped Astrid's breath: enormous, dark, and utterly unafraid, set in a face so exhausted that fear had presumably been burned away as fuel days ago.

The child held one small fist against its chest, knuckles white, clutching something Astrid could not yet see.

"Hello," Astrid said carefully.

The child tilted its head. Then it spoke — a rush of syllables that fell over Astrid like water over stones, liquid and layered, vowels that bent in places her tongue had never bent. It was not valley speech. It was not trader's cant from the southern passes, which she'd heard twice in her life from wool merchants. It was not anything. It sounded, absurdly, like the birdsong of some forest she had never walked in, if birdsong could grieve.

"I don't understand you," Astrid said, and hated how flat her voice came out, how much like the warden's voice, the voice of people who meet strangeness with walls.

The child tried again. Slower this time. And again, watching her face with those unblinking dark eyes, hunting for comprehension the way Astrid had seen starving animals hunt for roots. On the third attempt, one word surfaced that Astrid almost recognized — almost, the way a dream almost means something — and then sank again beneath the flood of the unknown tongue.

Behind her, in the byre, Marigold began to low. Not pain. Dread. The whole croft was a single clenched muscle now, every creature tuned to the same frequency of alarm, and Astrid realized with a cold slide of understanding that they weren't afraid of the child.

They were afraid of what the child carried.

"May I see?" she asked, though the question was meaningless. She crouched slowly, bringing herself down to the child's height, and extended one open hand — palm up, fingers loose, the gesture that worked on every frightened creature she had ever met, from sparrowhawk to stallion.

The child stared at the hand. Then, with the deliberateness of someone making a decision far older than seven years, it opened its fist.

The shard sat in the small grimy palm like a splinter of midnight.

It was the size of a man's thumb, irregular, faceted along edges that seemed to shift when Astrid wasn't looking directly at them — glass, or obsidian, or something that only pretended to be either. And it *hummed*. She felt it before she heard it, a vibration climbing through the frozen air between them, through the packed earth of the yard, up through the soles of her boots. A trapped-wasp sound, angry and small and endless. The light coming off the western hills bent very slightly toward it, the way water bends toward a drain.

Every hair on Astrid's arms rose. Not from cold.

*Magic.* Here, in the valley where magic came to die. Her mind reached for the thought and flinched back from it three times before it would hold still. Nothing enchanted survived Hollowvale's borders — that was the whole architecture of the place, the reason her grandmother's generation had built their lives inside these hills like eggs inside a shell. Wards older than memory ringed the valley, fed by something no one spoke of plainly, and anything arcane that crossed them curdled and went quiet. She had counted on it for twenty-three years. It was the lock on her cage and the roof over her head both.

And here was a child holding a piece of singing darkness that the wards had apparently failed to stop — or that had come from somewhere the wards did not reach.

The dogs whined. Juniper bolted the length of the fence, hooves striking sparks of panic from the frozen ground. In the hive, the bees' hum climbed toward a pitch that hurt something behind Astrid's sternum.

"It hurts them," Astrid said quietly. To the child, though the child couldn't understand; to herself, because saying things aloud was how she thought. "Whatever that is, it hurts everything that can hear it."

The child's expression didn't change. But its fist began to close again, slow and protective, drawing the shard back toward its chest — and Astrid saw, in the last gray light, the state of the small hand holding it. The fingers were burned. Not freshly; the burns were weeks old, healed badly, shiny scar tissue across the palm and up the inside of the wrist in a pattern that looked less like accident than like *gripping*. The child had been holding this thing for a long time. Had held it through whatever journey had brought it here, through mud and miles and whatever terror moved behind those unblinking eyes, and had not once put it down.

Something in Astrid's chest turned over, heavy and unwilling, like a stone rolling in a streambed.

She had built her life out of not-seeing. Twenty-three years of careful, small kindnesses — the lame ewe, the mistreated cart-horse, the sparrow with the broken wing — each rescue contained, each compassion kept at the safe distance where it cost her nothing but coin and feed. She loved her animals precisely because they asked nothing of her that she could not give without risk. People were different. People noticed things. People talked to wardens.

The child on her step was mud and scars and a language of grief, and it had walked out of the wide dangerous world carrying a piece of it in its burned fist, and it had knocked on *her* door, of all the doors in the valley, and Astrid understood with sickening clarity that the world had just reached through her careful walls and taken hold of her apron strings.

"You'd better come in," she said.

* * *

Inside, the cottage did what cottages do: it shrank the enormity of things down to human scale. Firelight. The smell of yesterday's bread and today's lamp oil. The child stood dripping melted mud onto her clean floor and looked around the single room with an expression Astrid couldn't parse — not wonder exactly, not wariness. Recognition, maybe. As if it had been expected somewhere like this, somewhere with a hearth and a kettle and a woman who smelled of straw and birth.

Astrid put water on to heat and cut bread and hard cheese, and the child ate the way small animals eat when they've learned that food can vanish — fast, watchful, one eye always on her hands. While it ate, Astrid studied the shard where it lay on the table between them, because the child had set it down — deliberately, she noted, watching her reaction — and the moment it left the small burned hand, every creature on the croft exhaled at once. She felt it go through the walls. The dogs lay down. The bees descended back into their winter murmur. The relief was so physical it was nearly audible, a valley-wide loosening.

So. The thing was terrible to beasts and bearable to humans. Or bearable to *her*, at least, sitting three feet from it, feeling its hum tickle the backs of her teeth like a word she was refusing to say.

She should burn it. That was the sensible thought, the warden-approved thought. Iron tongs, the forge, done. But when she imagined reaching for it, her hand remembered the hum, and something deeper than sense — something that lived in the same place her gift lived, that dark root under twenty-three years of silence — whispered that destroying it would be like cutting a songbird's throat to stop the noise. Whatever sang inside the shard, it was *alive*. Or it had been, once.

The child finished eating and pushed the crust to the edge of the board, saving it, and Astrid's chest did the painful turning-over thing again.

"What's your name?" she asked, knowing it was useless, asking anyway because silence between two creatures is its own kind of scream.

The child looked at her. Looked at the shard. Looked at her again — and then did something that raised every hair Astrid owned: it tapped the shard once, gently, with one scarred finger, and then pointed at itself, and then made a shape in the air with both hands, a shape like wings folding, like something descending.

Then it said a single word. One word out of all that flooding unknown language, and this time Astrid caught it, caught it and *held* it, because the child said it slowly, shaping its mouth around the sounds with exaggerated care, teaching her, the way she had spent her whole life teaching sounds to creatures who had no human words:

"*Sess-ka.*"

And then the child touched its own chest with both hands, and said "*Wren*" — which needed no translation, a name is a name in any tongue — and then pointed at the shard, and then out the window, north, toward Torvane, toward the mountain, and repeated, softer, with a crack running through it that no language owns exclusively:

"*Sess-ka.*"

Sister. The word arrived in Astrid's mind fully formed, unasked-for, the way her gift delivered everything — not reasoned out but simply *known*, dropped into her the way seeds drop into furrows. She didn't know how she knew. She only knew that she did, with the same certainty she knew when a mare was foaling wrong or a dog was lying about a bone: the shard was a sister. Someone had been broken into pieces, and this was one piece, and a child had carried it through the dark to the one door in the valley where a woman lived who could hear what the world said without words.

Outside, full night had arrived. The fire popped. The shard hummed its trapped-wasp song, patient as a debt.

And far off, at the edge of hearing — so far at the edge that Astrid would spend the rest of the night telling herself she'd imagined it — something answered the hum. A long, thin cry from beyond the northern hills, high and hungry and *searching*, and every dog in Hollowvale began, one by one, to howl.
