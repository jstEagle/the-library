## 3. What the Shard Remembers

The child slept in the loft above the byre, wrapped in Astrid's oldest blanket, and the shard slept with her — or seemed to. Astrid did not trust it to sleep. She had set it on the table between two candle stubs and watched it for an hour the way she might watch a snake coiled in a bread basket: still, glossy, wrong.

It was no larger than a knucklebone of glass, that shard, its edges worn smooth as river ice, but it caught the candlelight strangely. Light went *into* it and did not come back out the same. It held a color beneath its surface that Astrid had no name for — not gold, not green, something like the flash you see behind your eyes when you press your palms too hard against them. And it hummed. Not aloud. In the teeth. In the soft bones behind the ears. A trapped-wasp note that never quite resolved into silence.

Her beasts had told her what they thought of it without a single word being wasted. The hens had gone mute at dusk, all eleven of them pressed into the far corner of their coop like conspirators. The dog, Bracken, would not cross the threshold of the kitchen. He had stood in the yard with his ears flat and his lip lifted off his teeth, and when Astrid had asked him — gently, silently, the way she asked everything — *what is it, what do you smell*, he had only looked at her with an expression she had never seen on a dog before.

Pity, she had decided finally. It was pity. As if he knew something about the shard that she did not, and grieved that she would have to learn it too.

Now the house was quiet except for the hum and the tick of the stove and the wind working at the eaves, and Astrid sat across from the thing and made herself look at it properly.

She had spent twenty-three years learning what fear felt like in her own body so that she could name it and set it aside, the way she set aside a lamb's dead twin or a fox-killed hen. Fear was a tool like any other. But this was not quite fear. This was the feeling of standing at the edge of a well in the dark, knowing there was water below, hearing it breathe.

*You could leave it,* she told herself. *Bury it in the midden. Let the frost take the child too, if it comes to that.*

But even as she thought it she knew the shape of the lie. She had carried the child up from the road herself, mud-caked and shivering and speaking that language that slid through Astrid's ears like water through a sieve — almost comprehensible, almost *familiar*, which was worse than strange. She had fed her barley soup and watched her eat with the desperate, untrusting speed of a creature that had learned meals were thefts to be completed quickly. No one who had done those things buried children in middens. Her mother had seen to that, God rest whatever was left of her.

Astrid reached out and turned the shard over with one fingertip.

The hum changed pitch. It rose, quickened, like a wasp waking in a jar, and the light inside it swirled, and Astrid felt the touch travel up her finger and into her wrist and further, further than flesh should carry anything —

— and the kitchen was gone.

* * *

She stood in a hall made of iron.

Not iron-trimmed, not iron-banded. Made of it, walls and vaulting and floor, every surface dark and mirror-smooth, so that she saw herself reflected a hundred times over, a thin woman in a wool dress with straw in her hair, multiplied into a congregation of the same frightened face. The air tasted of coins and cold. Somewhere below the floor, vast and slow, something breathed.

Torches burned along the walls without flickering, and their flames were the wrong colors — pale violet, a green like pond ice — and they cast no heat. At the far end of the hall rose two thrones. One stood empty, its seat draped in chains that hung slack as sleeping snakes. The other was occupied.

Astrid could not make herself look directly at what sat there. Her eyes slid away from it the way a horse's eye slides from a shadow it has decided not to see. She caught only impressions: a crown like a ribcage; hands folded with terrible patience; a stillness deeper than stone.

And before the throne, small and straight-backed, stood a girl.

She was perhaps fourteen. She wore a shift of grey wool that had once been fine and was now threadbare at the elbows, and her hair had been cropped short with something dull, unevenly, the way you shear a sheep you do not intend to keep through winter. Her wrists bore marks — old ones, healed brown, and new ones, red. She did not cry. That was the first thing Astrid understood about her, the thing that lodged under her ribs like a fishhook: the girl was terrified down to the marrow, and she did not cry, because crying had already been taken from her as a luxury she could not afford.

Around the girl's throat lay a collar of black glass, and within the collar something moved. Light. Soul-light, if such a thing existed, if such a word meant anything — and Astrid knew, watching, that it did, that everything she had ever half-believed about the invisible architecture of a person was true and had a price and a market.

Figures moved around the girl. They were tall and grey-robed and their faces were smooth as the backs of spoons, and they worked with the unhurried competence of shearers or farriers. One of them held a pair of scales. Another held a knife that was not a knife — a sliver of darkness with an edge — and with it, delicately, the way you'd pare an apple in one long unbroken ribbon, it began to take.

Astrid heard the sound. That was the worst of it. There was a sound, a thin high singing, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, and each time the blade passed through the girl the note dropped lower, flatter, poorer. The girl swayed. Her eyes stayed fixed on some point above the thrones, on nothing, on the memory of a sky.

And the thing on the throne spoke. Astrid did not hear words so much as feel them, weights laid one by one upon a scale:

*Portion the third. Reserve the voice. The rest to the vaults.*

The grey figures nodded. The ribbon of light came away whole, glowing, and was poured — actually *poured*, like honey, like molten tin — into a vessel of black glass. And beside the throne, on a shelf cut into the iron itself, Astrid saw rows upon rows of such vessels, hundreds, thousands, each one holding its captive swirl of light, each one labeled in characters that hurt to look at, and she understood with a lurch that she was looking at a treasury. A granary. A larder.

Souls, portioned and shelved.

The girl on the floor lifted her head. And though the vision had no logic to it, no reason that a dead woman's memory of a court should turn and find Astrid standing among its reflections, the girl's eyes found hers — found her exactly, in all hundred of the mirrored walls at once — and the girl's cracked lips moved, and no sound came out, because her voice had been reserved, but Astrid read the shape of it anyway, the way she read the shapes of mouths every day of her life:

*Who sees me?*

Then the shard screamed.

Not the hum. A scream, thin and furious and full of grief, and the iron hall shattered like a mirror struck with a hammer, and Astrid came back to herself on the floor of her own kitchen with her cheek against the cold boards and blood in her mouth where she had bitten her tongue.

* * *

The candle stubs had burned to puddles. Outside, the sky had gone the deep bruised blue that comes an hour before dawn. How long she had lain there she could not say. Her hand was still curled around the shard, and it had gone cold now, sullen, its light banked like a fire damped for the night.

Astrid got up in stages. Knees first, then a hand on the chair, then upright, swaying, listening to her own heart bang around in her chest like a trapped bird. She wet her face at the basin. The water was so cold it felt honest. She drank a cup of it and then another, and only when her hands had stopped shaking did she let herself think about what she had seen.

*A girl in an iron court.* A girl whose soul was being pared away in portions, itemized, shelved. *Reserve the voice.* Astrid touched her own throat without meaning to. Twenty-three years of silence, of swallowing every word that mattered, of speaking only to beasts because beasts could not betray her — and somewhere beneath a mountain, a girl a few years older than the child asleep in her loft had had her voice *reserved*, set aside like a jar of preserves, like something with a use.

And the child. The child who had appeared at her door speaking the Old Tongue, clutching this fragment of somebody's soul like a talisman, like a stolen coal from a fire she'd run through.

Astrid climbed the ladder to the loft slowly. The child lay curled in the blanket, small as a hare, her breathing quick and shallow even in sleep. Close up, in the grey pre-dawn light, Astrid could see the family resemblance she'd missed — the same narrow jaw, the same dark lashes, the same stubborn set to the mouth. The girl in the vision was this child grown seven years older. Or this child was that girl grown seven years younger. Sister. The word assembled itself in Astrid's mind without permission, and once it was there it would not leave.

She crouched at the edge of the loft and did the thing she had sworn on the ladder not to do: she touched the child's shoulder.

The girl woke the way feral things wake — all at once, silent, already moving back against the wall, one arm thrown up, the other hand snatching for the shard that wasn't there. Her eyes were huge and black in the gloom and they fixed on Astrid with a ferocity that would have been funny in anything smaller than a cornered wolf.

"It's all right," Astrid said, and hated how loud her own voice sounded after the silence of the vision. "No one's here but me. No one will be."

The child didn't understand the words. But she understood the tone, maybe, or the fact that Astrid had crouched low and kept her hands open and empty. Her arm lowered by degrees. Her breathing slowed from panic to wariness, which was, Astrid suspected, as close to peace as this child ever came.

They regarded each other in the dark. Then the child said something — a stream of that liquid, sliding language, urgent, questioning, her eyes going past Astrid's shoulder toward the stairs, toward the kitchen, toward the shard. She pointed. She said the same phrase again, harder, and jabbed her finger toward the door as if Astrid were simple, as if the answer were obvious: *go back, go now, we have to go back.*

"I can't understand you." Astrid kept her voice low and even, the voice she used for spooked ewes. "I'm sorry. I wish to God I could."

The child's face crumpled with frustration. She pressed both fists against her eyes for a moment — a gesture so young, so ordinary, that Astrid's chest ached — and then she dropped her hands and tried again, slower, pointing at her own chest: a word. Pointing at Astrid: a question. Astrid shook her head. The child tried another word, and another, cycling through sounds, hunting for something Astrid might catch, and most of them slid past meaningless.

Until one didn't.

The child pointed at herself, then out the window, north and east, toward the mountains that walled the valley's end. Then she pressed her fist flat against her breastbone, hard, twice, and said:

"*Sessa.*"

Sister.

Astrid knew the word. She did not know how she knew it — it rose in her from somewhere below memory, from the same drowned place the child's language came from, the place her mother's lullabies had been sung from in a tongue the valley forbade. *Sessa.* Sister. The syllables fit together in Astrid's mind like a joint long disused, clicking home.

"You're looking for your sister," Astrid said quietly.

The child couldn't have understood the words. But she understood the recognition — she saw it land, saw the meaning arrive in Astrid's face — and something in her broke open. She crossed the loft in a scramble and threw her arms around Astrid's neck, and she was shaking, shaking like a wet lamb, and she was saying *sessa, sessa, sessa* into Astrid's collarbone in a small cracked voice, over and over, a prayer, a report, a debt she was declaring.

Astrid held her. It was awkward; she had held few enough human children in her life, and none like this, all elbows and desperation and the smell of woodsmoke and road dust. But she held on, and after a while the shaking slowed, and the child pulled back and looked at Astrid with an expression of terrible, exhausted trust, and pointed again — at the window, at the mountains, at the direction of the iron hall that lived inside the shard.

And Astrid, who had built her entire life out of the careful avoidance of exactly this moment, sat down on the floor of her own loft and understood that the walls of her small safe world had just developed a door.

Because she had seen the sister. She had seen the iron hall and the shelf of lights and the thing on the throne saying *reserve the voice*, and she could not unknow it. Knowledge, her mother had always said, was the one debt that collected interest. Every hour she did nothing, it grew.

Below them, in the kitchen, the shard began to hum again — louder now, insistent, the trapped wasp beating against the glass. And beneath the hum, faint but unmistakable, Astrid heard something new: a rhythm, a pulse, like footsteps. Like many footsteps, very far away, walking patiently toward the valley.

Bracken began to bark in the yard.
