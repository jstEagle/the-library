## 4. The Warden's Question

Brammel had been warden of Hollowvale for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned one truth above the others: the valley did not forgive carelessness. It swallowed it. Magic died at the borders like a moth against glass, and anything that fed on magic — witches, beast-speakers, soul-mongers, the pale agents of courts beneath mountains — starved here. That was the whole design of the place. His grandfather's grandfather had helped raise the ward-stones, and every warden since had kept them fed and kept the ledger of what crossed them.

So when Tam Osgood came down from the high pasture at first light, hat in hand, saying he'd seen a light out past the standing stones — a light that *moved wrong*, that bent toward itself like water finding a drain — Brammel did not laugh. He never laughed at such reports. Laughing was how wards got neglected.

"A light," he said, pouring the boy cider because his hands were shaking too badly to hold the cup himself.

"Greenish. Low to the ground. And there was singing, Warden. Or — not singing. Humming. Like a wasp caught in a jar, but big. Big as a barn." Tam drank, wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "And my dog wouldn't go near it. You know Hark. Hark would chase a bear off the flock. He put his tail down and crawled under me like I was a door."

Brammel set down the jug. Outside his window, Hollowvale lay in its morning grays — the terraced fields, the low stone houses with their smoke rising straight in the windless air, the dark line of pines along the ridges where the ward-stones stood their silent watch. A good valley. A safe valley. The safest place he knew of in all the world, though he'd never traveled far enough to test the claim, and privately suspected no one who lived inside a ward ever could.

"Show me where," he said.

* * *

The ground beyond the standing stones was soft from two days of rain, and soft ground keeps accounts. Brammel crouched over the prints for a long while while Tam hovered behind him, offering commentary Brammel didn't ask for.

The tracks were small. A child's, or nearly — bare feet, narrow heels, toes dug deep with each stride the way prints dig when someone is running uphill through mud. They came from the north, from the scree below the old pass, which was impossible, because the old pass had been closed by rockfall before Brammel was born and no one came over the mountain. No one. That was rather the point of living in the bottom of a bowl ringed by stone.

But the prints said otherwise, and so did the other thing.

Around the stones themselves, in a rough circle perhaps three strides across, the grass was dead. Not trampled, not frost-burned. Dead the way grass dies when its roots have been pulled out of the world — gray, brittle, lying flat as if pressed under glass. Brammel touched one blade and it crumbled. There was no smell of rot. There was no smell at all, and that absence frightened him more than any stench would have, because the valley was a place of smells: loam and sheep and woodsmoke and the river's cold iron breath. This little circle smelled of nothing, and nothing, in Brammel's long experience, was the scent of magic dying.

Or of magic arriving and being strangled on the ward's teeth.

He stood, knees cracking, and looked north up the scree. Somewhere up there, over the rockfall, was the world. The wide world, with its courts and its hunters and its wars over things Brammel had only read about in the warden's ledgers — souls, mostly, and who owned them, and what they were worth weighed against silver or against sin.

"Someone came over the mountain," Tam said. "A child, by the prints."

"Someone came over the mountain," Brammel agreed. "And whatever they carried killed the grass where they stood."

He walked the circle twice more, then followed the small footprints as far as they went — down through the bracken, along the sheep-track, until the mud gave way to the hard-packed lane and the account ended. But he knew these lanes the way he knew his own hands, and the track ran downhill, and downhill from the standing stones lay exactly one dwelling before the village proper.

The Lockridge cottage sat alone above the millpond, wrapped in its usual quiet. Smoke from the chimney. Geese muttering in their pen. A woman's figure moving behind the frosted window, and even at this distance Brammel felt the familiar tightening in his chest that Astrid Lockridge always produced in him — not suspicion, exactly. Not after twenty-three years. Something more like the ache of an old question never answered.

She had come to the valley as an infant, in her mother's arms, on the last wagon of the season. Her mother had paid the settling fee in old coin and asked no favors, taken the worst field on the south slope, and grown the best herbs anyone had ever tasted. She had died eight years back, quiet as snowfall, and Astrid had stayed on alone with her animals, keeping to herself, speaking softly, watching everything.

Brammel had wondered, once or twice a year for two decades, why a woman like that never married, never took apprentices, never went to market day in the lower villages. He had decided long ago that some people were simply made of privacy, the way some stone was made of flint, and he had let it be.

Now there was dead grass in a circle below her house, and a child's footprints running toward it, and a boy beside him swearing his dog had whined all night facing south.

"Warden?" Tam said.

"We'll pay a call on Mistress Lockridge," Brammel said. "Be polite. We're neighbors first and officers second. Always remember that."

"Yes, Warden." A pause. "What if she's done something?"

Brammel looked at the gray circle of grass, and at the smoke rising straight and calm from her chimney, and said what he had said a hundred times in nineteen years of wardenship, and hated slightly more each time he said it:

"In Hollowvale, we don't punish what might be. We ask questions, and we watch, and we keep the stones fed. That's all the law asks. That's all the valley needs."

He only wished he believed it as thoroughly as he said it.

* * *

She opened the door before he knocked.

That was the first thing. He had raised his hand to the wood and the door swung inward and there she was, Astrid Lockridge, sleeves rolled, flour to the wrist, looking at him with those level gray eyes as if she'd been standing behind the door waiting for him since dawn. Perhaps she had. Tam swore later that Hark had whined all night facing south; perhaps animals weren't the only ones in the valley who heard things coming.

"Warden Brammel." Her voice was low and even. "And Tam Osgood. You look chilled. Come in, there's bread just out of the oven."

It was a small cottage and it smelled wonderfully of life — bread, tallow, hay, the medicinal sharpness of dried herbs hanging from the rafters, and underneath it all the warm animal smell of creatures well kept. A cat regarded them from the dresser with open hostility. In the back room, something heavy shifted and settled — a horse, maybe, brought in from weather. Everything about the place said *ordinary*. Everything about it said *this woman has nothing to hide*, said it so fluently, so completely, that Brammel — who had spent nineteen years learning to hear what wasn't said — noticed the effort of it the way you notice a singer holding a note a half-beat too long.

They took tea at her table. She served them without fuss, and Tam, young and hungry, ate three slices of the bread and complimented it twice. Brammel held his cup and warmed his hands and let the silence do its work, because silences were tools too, and Astrid Lockridge, he happened to know, could outwait almost anyone.

Almost anyone.

"You slept well?" he asked at last, mildly.

"Well enough." She didn't blink. "Why do you ask?"

"There was a light seen on the high pasture in the night. Greenish. Moving wrong, by the account. And singing, or humming." He watched her hands around her cup. Steady. "And this morning there's a circle of dead grass below the standing stones, and small bare footprints running from it down toward the millpond."

For the space of one breath — Brammel counted breaths the way other men counted coins — nothing in her face moved at all. Then she frowned, a small efficient frown, and said, "Dead grass? Below the stones? I haven't been up that way since shearing." And she poured him more tea, and the frown became puzzlement, and puzzlement became the mild offended wonder of an honest woman hearing strange news.

It was very good. It was, Brammel thought with a kind of weary admiration, extremely good. Better than good — it was practiced, though practice and guilt wear the same groove, and twenty-three years of being questioned by wardens would teach anyone the shape of an honest face.

"The footprints," he said. "Small. Bare feet. Running downhill."

"I heard a child crying in the night." She said it plainly, looking into her tea. "I meant to tell you. I thought it was the Osgoods' youngest having nightmares — sound carries strangely off the water. I listened a while and it stopped, and I went back to bed. Should I have come for you?"

Yes, Brammel thought. Yes, you should have come for me, and you know it, and you're telling me now only because I've told you I already know something happened, and you are building your story around the edges of what I know the way a mason builds around a crack.

"And did you see the child?"

"No." Too fast. Only a hair too fast, but he had spent nineteen years listening for hairs. Then, smoothly, covering: "I looked out the window. Moon was behind cloud. I saw nothing but the pond."

"Mm." He sipped his tea. "If a child came over the mountain in the night, half-frozen, alone — that child will turn up somewhere. Hungry. When it does, bring it to me. Don't feed it, don't question it, don't let it speak to anyone. Do you understand why?"

Something flickered behind her eyes then — not fear. Calculation. He'd have sworn it was calculation, quick and cold as a heron striking, gone before it finished. "Because strangers carry sickness," she said.

"Because strangers carry *things*," Brammel said. "Sickness among them." He set down the cup. "Mistress Lockridge, I'm going to say something plain, because I think you're a woman who prefers plain speech, and because I'm too old for games. Rumors reach me. They've reached me for years, little ones, foolish ones — that your geese obey you, that your horses come without calling, that sick animals mend around you faster than they should. I've never given them a moment's weight, because I've seen you shear your own sheep and burn your thumb on your own stove and curse like a fishwife when the fox takes a hen, and none of that is witchcraft. It's work. But there is dead grass below the stones that died of *nothing*, and a child's feet running toward your door, and a boy whose dog wouldn't sleep. So I'm going to ask you one question, and I want you to search yourself before you answer it, because I will know a lie, and worse than that — the valley will know it, eventually, the way it knows everything."

He leaned forward.

"Is there anything in your house that came from outside the ward?"

The fire ticked. The cat leapt down from the dresser and left the room, pointedly, the way cats leave rooms when the pressure changes. From the back room came a single low sound from the horse — not a whinny, something deeper, a note of unease — and Astrid's eyes went, for half a heartbeat, toward that door.

"No," she said.

And Brammel sat with the lie between them like a stone on the table.

He had known it was a lie before she spoke it. He had known it from the too-quick *no* and the glance at the back room and the perfect composure, which no innocent person wears when a warden asks about magic — innocents get indignant, or frightened, or confused, but never *composed*. And yet he found, sitting there in the warmth of her kitchen with the good bread and the honest smell of it, that he could not blame her. That was the trouble. That was the whole rotten trouble of wardenship in a valley like this: the law said report, and the heart said wait, and nineteen years of watching Astrid Lockridge bury her mother and mend her fences and pull drowning lambs out of the millpond at midnight with her own two arms had built up in him a great slow weight of evidence that whatever she was, she was not a harm to Hollowvale.

But the grass was dead. The grass was dead in a circle, and it had died of nothing, and nothing was the smell of the world outside the ward — the world of courts and hunters and souls sold by weight. If something had come over the mountain carrying that nothing, then the safety of every hearth in Hollowvale rested on what happened next, and Brammel had not spent nineteen years feeding the ward-stones to gamble it on the private virtues of one secretive woman, however many lambs she'd saved.

"Then you won't mind," he said, rising, "if I have a look at your outbuildings. Procedure. I look at every house within a mile of an occurrence. You can stand there and watch me do it, if you like."

"Of course," said Astrid Lockridge, and stepped aside from the door to the back room, and gestured him through with a hostess's grace, and Brammel understood, with a sinking certainty, that whatever was in that room had been moved, or hidden, or prepared for him, in the time it took to pour tea.

* * *

The back room held a mare, heavy with foal, dozing in fresh straw. The loft above held hay and a broken cartwheel. The shed held the horse's tack, jars of rendered fat, coils of rope. The coop held geese that hissed at Brammel personally, with what he could have sworn was coordination. The herb garden held rosemary gone woody with age. Under the floorboard he pried up in the pantry — and he pried up four, and she watched him do it without a word, arms folded, which was either innocence or contempt and he genuinely could not tell which — there were preserves, seed packets, and a box of her mother's old letters tied in ribbon, which he did not read, because he was not that kind of man, though his hands wanted to.

Nothing. He found nothing, and both of them knew he would find nothing, and the knowing sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

At the gate, he paused. Evening was coming down early, gray and smelling of rain. Behind him the cottage windows had begun to glow, and Astrid stood in her doorway with the lamplight behind her, watching him go with an expression he couldn't read and had never been able to read in twenty-three years.

"Mistress Lockridge," he said. "One more thing. Free advice, neighbor to neighbor, no law in it. If a child comes to your door — hungry, hurt, speaking strangely — send for me. Whatever you think you owe it, whatever you think it needs, the valley owes you more than you owe it, and I'd rather settle accounts myself than have the ward do it. The ward isn't gentle. Understand? The stones don't ask questions. They only starve what doesn't belong."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, quietly, in that low even voice that never rose and never broke:

"I understand perfectly, Warden. Nothing that doesn't belong should stay in Hollowvale."

It was the right thing to say. Every word of it was right, and she delivered it flawlessly, and Brammel walked away down the darkening lane with the coal still burning in his chest, because he had spent nineteen years learning the difference between a person telling the truth and a person telling you exactly what the truth would sound like, and Astrid Lockridge, God help them both, was the finest second kind he had ever met.

Behind him, faint across the water, he thought he heard the geese begin to call — not their ordinary quarreling, but something rhythmic, urgent, almost like alarm. Almost like warning.

And far to the north, past the standing stones, past the dead circle of grass, at the very lip of the ward where the world thinned to nothing — something patient had begun, very softly, to hum.
