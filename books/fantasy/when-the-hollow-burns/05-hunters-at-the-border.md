## 5. Hunters at the Border

The geese had not stopped calling since dusk.

Astrid stood in her dooryard with a bucket of wash-water going cold in her hands, listening to them argue across the darkening pond — not the ordinary bickering of birds settling for the night, but something with shape to it, something with rhythm. Three notes rising, two falling, then silence, then again. Over and over, like a bell rung by someone who could not stop.

*Come see,* they were saying. *Come see. Come see.*

She set down the bucket.

Wren was asleep inside, curled on the settle beneath Astrid's good blanket, the shard wrapped in three layers of wool and tucked into the bread box as far from the child's sleeping hands as Astrid could manage. Even through the walls she could feel it faintly, the way one feels a wasp trapped in a jar in another room — that thin, ceaseless vibration at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the skin. It had hummed all day. It hummed now, and the geese answered it, and somewhere beyond the standing stones, past the dead circle of grass where nothing grew and nothing ever would, something else had begun to answer too.

She walked north along the sheep path with her lantern unlit. There was no need of light; the sky had cleared after the rain, and the stars over Hollowvale were thick as spilled salt. The valley lay quiet around her — the black shapes of the byres, the silver thread of the millstream, Brammel's watchtower a single lit tooth on its hill. Behind her, her house looked small and ordinary and dear, and she found herself memorizing it without meaning to: the lean of the doorframe, the herb bundles drying under the eaves, the crooked fencepost Wren had laughed at that morning with a sound like water over stones.

Twenty-three years, and she had never once walked to the ward's edge at night. There had never been a reason. The ward was a fact of the valley the way the mountains were, the way the seasons were — invisible, absolute, older than anyone living. You did not visit it. You lived inside it the way a fish lives inside water, and you did not think about the surface of the world.

But the geese were still calling, and the shard was still humming, and so Astrid walked north until the sheep path gave out and the ground began to change.

She felt it before she saw it — a wrongness underfoot, a hollowness, as though she were walking on the skin stretched over a drum. The grass grew shorter here, then patchier, then not at all. The dead circle. She had been told as a girl never to graze stock near it, never to cut turf from it, and no one had ever said why, because no one knew why. It was simply a place where the valley declined to be itself: a scar of bare earth perhaps forty paces across, ringed by the old standing stones, gray and lichen-eaten, leaning together like conspirators.

And beyond the circle, at the very lip of the ward, the air itself changed.

It was not a wall. She had always imagined a wall, or a veil, or some shimmering curtain such as appeared in the few forbidden tales that reached the valley in peddlers' packs. But there was nothing to see. There was only a line her body understood without being told — a line where the night air of Hollowvale ended and the night air of everywhere-else began, and crossing it, she somehow knew, would be like stepping out of a warm house into a blizzard. Everything she was would change on the other side of it. Everything she had spent twenty-three years burying would wake.

She stopped at the edge of the dead circle and looked out.

At first she saw nothing. The land beyond the ward fell away into moorland, heather and rock running down toward the river country, silvered by starlight, empty as the bottom of the sea. Then her eyes adjusted, and she saw that it was not empty.

There were figures at the far side of the moor. Not close — half a mile, perhaps more, spread out in a wide arc facing the ward like an audience facing a stage. They stood utterly still. That was what made her blood slow and thicken in her veins: not their number, which she could not count in the dark, but their stillness. No firelight. No movement. No sound carried across the distance, though the night was clear enough that she could hear the river a mile south of her position. They simply stood, patient as fence posts, patient as winter, and even at this distance Astrid felt their attention like a hand laid flat against her sternum.

They were looking at the ward. And through it, she understood with a certainty that arrived whole and cold, they were looking at *her*.

The lantern she hadn't lit felt suddenly necessary, and she made herself turn slowly, walk back ten paces, twenty, before she let her breath out. Her hands were steady. Twenty-three years of practice had taught her hands steadiness if nothing else. But her heart was going like a hammer in a doorframe, and when she spoke aloud — to herself, because there was no one else — her voice came out low and rough.

"They can't come in."

That was true. That had to be true. The ward held; it had always held; magic died at the borders of Hollowvale, and whatever those things were — and she knew what they were, had known since the moment she'd touched the shard and seen the iron court and the girl being portioned out like bread — they could not cross. The valley had kept her safe for twenty-three years. It would keep her safe now.

Except.

Except that the ward was not a wall. It was a *holding of breath*. She had never thought of it that way before tonight, but standing at its lip she could feel it now, the way one feels one's own pulse when lying awake at night: the ward was effort. Somewhere, in some way she did not understand and had never questioned, the valley spent itself every hour of every day to keep that line drawn — and the thing that paid that cost, the fuel of it, the wood on the fire —

Her mother's face rose in her memory, sudden and sharp as a slap. Her mother, who had gone out one October morning to gather late honey and never come home, and whose body they had never found, and whose absence the valley had explained to itself with bears, with rivers, with a fall into some hidden crevasse. Astrid had been nine years old. She had grieved. And then, within the season, her gift — the gift that had gotten children's mothers to look at her sideways, that had gotten the priest to mutter about burning — had simply *quieted*. The beasts' voices had faded to whispers, then to almost nothing, and the whispering neighbors had forgotten, and the priest had died, and life had gone on.

She had thought, all these years, that she had simply grown up. That the gift had withered from disuse, the way a muscle withers.

Standing in the dead circle with soul-hunters arrayed against the horizon, Astrid felt the truth of it open under her like a trapdoor. The gift hadn't withered. It had been *muffled* — smothered under something vast and deliberate, the way a coal is banked under ash. And the timing — the timing of her mother's disappearance and the silencing of her daughter's dangerous tongue — was not coincidence. It was architecture. Someone had built her safety, stone by stone, and the quarry that stone came from was a question she could not yet bear to ask.

*Later,* she told herself firmly. *Grieve later. Think later. Right now—*

Right now there were hunters at the border, and a humming shard in her bread box, and a child asleep in her house who had crossed half the world alone to reach this valley, carrying a fragment of a stolen soul like a lit torch through dry grass.

Because that was what the shard was. She had known it since the visions, but she knew it fully now, watching the motionless arc of figures on the moor. The shard was not merely a piece of Wren's sister. It was a beacon — a lit window in a dark country, a voice crying out in a language only one kind of listener could hear. Every hour it sat in her house, wrapped in wool, it called to them. *Here. Here. Here.* And they had heard, and they had come, and they had arranged themselves at the edge of the ward with the patience of creatures who had hunted souls since before Hollowvale had a name.

They could not enter. But they could wait.

And the valley — the valley could not wait forever. She thought of the ward as she had felt it tonight, that holding of breath, that expenditure without end. Whatever fed it, it fed on something finite. Twenty-three years of safety might have been bought with a price already spent. And when the breath ran out — when the line wavered, even for a moment, even for a heartbeat — those patient figures would not need to break anything. They would only need to step through.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat by the hearth with the shard in her lap, unwrapped, watching it.

In the firelight it was beautiful, which seemed like a cruelty. A splinter of something larger, glass-dark shot through with veins of gold, it turned slowly in her cupped hands as though it weighed nothing at all — though she knew better now; it weighed exactly what it was, a portion of a living girl's soul, and no scale in the world could measure that. As she watched, the golden veins pulsed. Faintly. Rhythmically. Like a heartbeat. Like breathing.

Like a signal lamp.

"Stop," she whispered to it. "Be quiet. Please."

It pulsed on, indifferent, faithful to its nature. It wanted to go home. Of course it did. It had been torn out of a girl in an iron court and carried across the world in the arms of a child, and everything it was strained toward its own missing pieces, and it broadcast that longing every moment of every day, and the listeners on the moor had ears made for precisely that frequency.

The floorboard creaked behind her, and Wren stood in the doorway of the sleeping loft, hair wild, eyes huge and dark in the firelight. The child looked at the shard in Astrid's hands, and then at Astrid's face, and something in the small frame went taut as a bowstring.

"No," Wren said — one of the handful of valley-words the child had gathered in four days, deployed now with terrible precision. Small hands reached out. "*No.*"

"It's all right." Astrid patted the settle beside her. "Sit. Look."

Wren did not sit. Wren came and knelt at her feet instead, close enough that the firelit planes of the small face showed every flicker, and stared at the shard with an expression Astrid recognized because she wore it herself in mirrors: the look of someone guarding something that is killing them, and refusing to put it down.

Then Wren spoke — softly, in the Old Tongue, words flowing like water over unfamiliar stones — and touched the shard, and the vision took them both.

*Iron. A hall of iron, pillars grooved like teeth. Cold that lives in bone. A girl on a dais — older than Wren, fifteen perhaps, sixteen, with the same sharp chin, the same dark eyes — and around her robed figures working with instruments that made no sound, drawing out light from her chest in long luminous threads, winding each thread onto spools of black glass. The girl's mouth open in a scream that makes no sound either, because sound, like everything else in that place, has been sold. And on a throne above, a shape that is mostly shadow and mostly patience, leaning forward, interested. And threaded through it all, faint and far away, a hum — the hum of the shard, calling, calling, the one piece that got away—*

Astrid came back to her own hearth with her heart slamming and her cheeks wet and did not remember weeping. Wren was crying silently, great shaking sobs without sound, and Astrid pulled the child into her arms and held on, rocking, murmuring nonsense into the wild hair, and over the bowed head she looked into the fire and let herself understand.

They were coming for the shard. They would wait out the ward. They would wait out the winter, the decade, the century — things like that did not measure time the way people did. And when the ward failed — not *if*, her gut said, *when*, because a thing that breathed could stop breathing — they would take the shard, and they would take Wren, and they would take Astrid, whose gift had apparently been noticed by powers she had never dreamed existed, and they would carry all of it down into the mountain and wind it onto spools of black glass.

Unless the shard left the valley. Unless *they* left the valley, and soon, and took the beacon with them — drew the hunters away from Hollowvale, away from the miller's daughters and the shepherd boys and every other soul in the valley who had done nothing except live near a woman who'd once spoken to beasts.

The thought arrived complete, and once it arrived it would not leave. It sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"I have to take you out of here," she said aloud, and Wren lifted a tear-streaked face, and Astrid said it again, slower, pointing north, past the stones, past the dead circle, toward the moor and the waiting dark. "Out. Away. Both of us."

Wren frowned. Then the child pointed at the shard, and at the window, and made a low keening sound — and Astrid realized the child had understood perfectly, had perhaps understood before she had. Had crossed half the world knowing exactly what followed, and what waited, and what it cost.

Outside, the geese had finally gone quiet. In the silence that replaced them, thin and far and patient, the shard hummed on.

Three days, she thought. Give me three days to make ready.

And on the moor, in the starless dark beyond the ward, the nearest of the waiting figures turned its head — slowly, slowly, like a compass needle finding true north — toward the small stone house with its crooked fencepost and its drying herbs, and smiled with a mouth that had been sold long ago, and did not move.
