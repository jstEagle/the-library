## 6. Crossing the Ward

The last night in Hollowvale came down like a held breath.

Astrid had spent the day doing ordinary things, and the ordinariness of them nearly broke her. She milked the goats and did not tell them she was leaving. She split wood until her palms burned, because a full woodpile was the only apology she knew how to make to a house she would abandon. She fed the hens and let them squabble over the scratch grain, and when the old barred hen, Mother Copper, fixed her with one amber eye, Astrid said only, "Mind the others," and Mother Copper clucked as if she understood that the minding would have to go both ways.

Wren sat on the doorstep through all of it, knees drawn up, the shard wrapped in three layers of wool and bound against the child's chest with a strip of Astrid's best blanket. The child had not spoken since noon. That silence had a different texture than Wren's usual silence — not the silence of someone who did not know the words, but the silence of someone who had heard the adults talking low through the wall and understood, in the bone-deep way children understand, that the world was about to change shape.

Astrid had tried to explain it. She had stood in the middle of her kitchen with her hands full of flour and said, "We're going beyond the valley, little bird. Past the ward. There are things out there I need you to be brave about." Wren had looked at her with those dark, unblinking eyes and repeated, carefully, in a voice like a bell struck softly: *"Be brave."* And then, after a pause, in the Old Tongue, something longer — a whole sentence, patient and grave, as if correcting the record. Astrid had not asked for a translation. Some part of her suspected the child had said: *I have been brave in worse places than this.*

* * *

They left at the hour when even the dogs of Hollowvale had given up their barking, when the fog came off the river in long white fingers and the mountains to the north were a blacker black than the sky. Astrid carried a pack she had packed and repacked four times: bread, hard cheese, a skin of cider, a flint, a knife, a heavy shawl, and — wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom, because she could not make herself leave it behind — the little tin of her mother's sewing needles and the spool of gray thread that had outlived every garment it had ever mended.

Wren walked beside her without being carried, which Astrid had not expected. She had imagined carrying the child over the first miles, imagined the small weight going heavy with sleep against her shoulder. But Wren walked like a pilgrim, quick and silent on the frozen mud, head up, and once, when Astrid stumbled on a root, the child's hand shot out and steadied her elbow with a grip that was startlingly strong.

The village was behind them before they reached the first real decision. Astrid had not said goodbye to anyone. She had left coin on the miller's doorstep for the grain she'd taken, and a note — she was not a woman given to notes, and she had written and burned three drafts before settling on the truth, or a shape of it: *Gone to see to a matter beyond the valley. Tend my beasts. I'll come back if I can.* Brammel would read it and know it was a lie, or know it was not, and either way he would come to her cottage and find it swept, the fire banked, the animals' feed measured out in labeled sacks, and he would understand that whatever she had done, she had done it deliberately, and tidily, and with the particular care of a woman who expected not to return.

The ward was not a wall. That was the first thing every child in Hollowvale learned and the last thing they believed. There was no visible line, no standing stones with glowing runes, no gate. The ward was a *weather*, a pressure, a quality the air took on as you climbed the valley's throat — the way the light went thin and silver, the way sound carried strangely, the way your own thoughts seemed to arrive slightly delayed, as if crossing a room full of water. The old people said you could feel it in your teeth. Astrid had lived inside it for twenty-three years, and she had never once, in all that time, stood at its edge on the outside.

She knew where it ran, though. Everyone did, though no one said it plainly. It followed the high scree below the Widow's Teeth, crossed the river at the shallows where the water ran thin and musical over white stone, and climbed again through the black pines toward the saddle called the Shepherd's Notch. Beyond the Notch, the land fell away into the wide world — the river country, the mountain roads, the places on maps that the valley's maps declined to draw.

They climbed for two hours. The fog thickened, then thinned, then thickened again. Astrid's breath came in white plumes. Wren's small hand stayed in hers, dry and hot as a coal.

And the whole way, Astrid listened for the voices.

That was the thing no one in Hollowvale knew about her — no one living — and the thing she was about to stop hiding from at least one person: she heard the animals. Not as a shepherd hears them, reading mood in the set of an ear. She *heard* them, words and meanings, the way you hear a neighbor through a wall. Inside the ward, the voices came to her thin and muffled, like songs through deep water. She had spent twenty-three years learning to live with that muffle, the way a deaf woman learns to live with silence — not happily, but competently, and with a whole architecture of pretending built over the ache.

Beyond the ward, the muffle would be gone. She had no memory of it un-muffled. She had been carried across as an infant, her mother's hands tight around her, and her mother had never once spoken of what the crossing cost, or gave.

"Almost there," Astrid said, and was surprised by how rough her voice was. "Wren. Little bird. When we cross — when we go past the stones — things will be different. I'll be different. You mustn't be afraid of me."

Wren looked up. In the starlight the child's face was all shadow and shine, two eyes catching what little light there was. Wren said, in Astrid's own language, with the careful, over-precise diction of a student: "I am not afraid of you."

Astrid laughed — a short, startled sound that the fog swallowed. "You should be a little afraid. Everyone should be a little afraid of everything, past the ward."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then, in the Old Tongue, softly: a word Astrid did not know, but whose shape she was beginning to recognize. The shard, against the child's chest, hummed once — a low, waspish note — and went quiet, as if it too were gathering itself.

* * *

The ward's edge, at the Shepherd's Notch, was marked by two leaning stones that the valley folk called the Shepherd's Sisters. They were nothing, geologically speaking — two gray slabs tilted toward each other like women sharing a secret — and children dared each other to run between them and touch the far one and run back, and nothing ever happened to them, because nothing ever happened to anyone inside the ward, that was the whole point and the whole prison of it.

Astrid stopped three paces from the stones.

Her body stopped before her mind did. It was the strangest sensation — her boots planted, her breath held, every muscle locked as if she had walked into a wall of glass. And beneath the physical stillness, faint but unmistakable, came a sound she had not heard in twenty-three years: the ward itself, singing.

It was not a pleasant sound. It was the sound of a held note sustained so long the instrument had begun to suffer — a thin, high, strained tone, like a wet finger on a crystal rim, like wire under too much tension. And underneath the note, so faint she might have imagined it, something else. A voice. A woman's voice, worn down to almost nothing, singing the same phrase over and over, the way you sing to keep yourself company in the dark:

*Hold. Hold. Hold.*

Astrid's knees nearly went.

She knew that voice. She would have known it anywhere, in any register, in any ruin. It was her mother's voice — or the ghost of it, the last syllable of it, stretched across twenty-three years of holding. Her mother had not merely lived in Hollowvale. Her mother was in the ward. Of her. Part of her. And she was *tired*. Twenty-three years of singing one note, and she was so tired, and no one had ever heard her, no one had ever thanked her, and her daughter had spent her whole life resenting a silence that had been bought at a price she could not have imagined.

"Wren," Astrid said, and her voice cracked clean through. She crouched down to the child's level, gripping the small shoulders. "Listen to me. Whatever happens on the other side of these stones — whatever I do, whatever I say, whatever comes out of the dark to answer me — I need you to remember one thing. I am still me. Do you understand? Magic doesn't change what a person is. It only shows it."

Wren put one small hand flat on Astrid's cheek. The gesture was so adult, so gentle, so entirely without fear, that Astrid's eyes filled before she could stop them.

"I know what you are," Wren said. And then, in the Old Tongue, the word again — the one Astrid had heard twice now, the one whose shape she was learning. She would learn its meaning later, and when she did, she would have to sit down in the road. But that was later.

They stepped between the stones.

* * *

The crossing was not dramatic. That was the horror of it, at first — that it was nothing. One step, cold air. Another step, cold air. A third step, and the strained singing note faded behind them like a door closing on a distant room, and Astrid thought, with a wild, disappointed flash of anger: *Is that all? Twenty-three years, and it's nothing—*

Then the world came back.

It came back the way blood comes back into a limb that has slept too long — that first terrible rush of pins and needles, the flood of sensation so violent it is indistinguishable from pain. Astrid staggered. Her hand flew to her mouth. She went down on one knee on the frozen scree and stayed there, gasping, while the magic of the wide world poured into her like a river into a cracked bowl.

Every living thing within miles was suddenly, deafeningly *present*.

The pines below her were not silent. They murmured in their roots, slow green thoughts like the turning of enormous wheels. The river in the valley floor spoke in its sleep, a long liquid muttering about stones and distances. An owl somewhere north of them announced its hunger in precise, formal language. A fox — she could feel it, a bright red thread of attention moving along the treeline — paused mid-prowl and *turned toward her*, its mind a sharp star of curiosity and caution: *What is that? What is that, what is that, what—*

And beneath it all, deeper, older, a pressure she could feel in her back teeth: the mountain itself, dreaming. Not asleep. *Aware.* Aware of her, the way the sea is aware of a swimmer.

"Oh," Astrid said. It came out of her as a sob. "Oh, oh — it was *here* the whole time, it was always—"

She had known, of course. She had known the way a prisoner knows there is sky. But knowing and *feeling* were different countries, and she had just crossed the border, and the wealth of it was obscene. Twenty-three years she had lived on whispers, on muffled songs through water, on the thin gruel of a gift strangled at the root. And all around her, on the other side of two leaning stones, the world was shouting.

The beasts began to answer.

It started with the fox — the sharp star of its mind flaring into something like speech, a cascade of bright, yipping thought directed straight at her: *Small-one-loud-one-who-speaks-who-speaks-WHO-SPEAKS—* And the owl took it up, a round, cold voice dropping out of the dark like a stone into a well: *A speaker. On the mountain. A speaker, a speaker.* And then things she could not see, things in the deep pines and the high crags and the black water of the tarn below the Notch, waking one after another like lamps lit down a long road, all of them turning, all of them calling, all of them saying some version of the same astonished thing: *She hears. She hears us. The one who hears is here.*

The voices layered and tangled until they were less like voices than weather — a storm of attention breaking over her, and every drop of it hungry. That was the word that frightened her. *Hungry.* Not for her flesh. Hungry for her, for the channel she was, for the connection they had been starved of — because the ward's silence did not stop at the valley's edge, she realized; it radiated, a dead zone in the world's conversation, and every beast for miles had lived its whole life around that hole, and now she had stepped into it and she was *loud*, she was a bonfire in a country of the cold, and they were all coming to warm themselves.

Astrid knelt on the frozen ground with her arms around herself and shook.

It was too much. It was everything. It was the thing she had dreamed of every night of her childhood and dreaded every day of her adulthood, because a gift this loud was not a gift in the wide world — it was a beacon, a confession, a pyre waiting for its match. Somewhere out in that dark, soul-hunters were patient as winter, and she had just lit herself up like a festival.

And it was the most magnificent thing that had ever happened to her.

Both things were true. She held them together, one in each hand, kneeling in the scree with her breath tearing white, and understood that this was what her life would be now — terror and exhilaration in the same breath, forever, no more choosing between them.

Wren crouched beside her. The child was watching the dark treeline with wide, unafraid eyes, and Astrid realized with a jolt that Wren could hear it too — not the way she did, not the deep channel of it, but enough. Enough to know what was coming.

"Are they coming here?" Astrid managed. Her teeth chattered. It was not entirely the cold.

Wren tilted her head, listening to the night with the ease of long practice. Then the child said, in the tone of someone reporting the obvious: "Yes."

The fox arrived first.

It came out of the pines like a flame given legs, red-gold even in the starlight, and stopped ten paces away with its tail high and its black eyes fixed on Astrid with an intensity that had nothing animal about it — or everything animal about it, the animal-ness of the world before men taught it manners. Its mind pressed against hers, bright and bristling: *You. You are the hole-filler. You are the loud one. Say something. Say something, say—*

"I hear you," Astrid whispered.

The fox sat down. Just like that, in the frozen scree, like a dog at a hearth. And the whole night seemed to settle a degree, the way a room settles when a quarrel ends.

* * *

They did not go far that night. Astrid's legs would not carry her; the flood of the world's voices had wrung her out like a cloth, and every step brought new attention — a badger grumbling in its sett, a roe deer freezing on a slope above them to stare with huge dark wonder, moths spiraling down out of the air to orbit her head like tiny gray moons. By the time the sky went gray at the edges, she had found a hollow beneath a leaning crag, out of the wind, and there she built a small fire and sat with Wren against her side and watched the beasts watch her.

They came all night, in ones and twos. The fox stayed, curled at the hollow's mouth like a sentry. A pine marten ran down a trunk and hung upside down to study them. High above, on the crags, something large and horned stood silhouetted against the stars for a long, long time, and did not come down, and Astrid was both grateful and strangely bereft.

Wren slept at last, an hour before dawn, the shard's humming softened to almost nothing. Astrid sat with her back to the stone and let the night's voices wash over her and tried to learn, quickly, the first lesson of the wide world: how to be a lamp without burning.

It was the fox who taught her. As the gray light grew, the creature raised its head and spoke to her one last time — not the bright cascade of the night, but something slower, deliberate, almost kind: *They felt you from far, speaker. The cold ones. The ones who take the shining parts. They felt you the moment you crossed. You are loud as a burning house. Walk soft. Walk fast.*

Astrid's blood went to ice water.

She looked north, toward the high passes, and the mountain looked back — that vast, dreaming awareness beneath everything, patient as stone. And somewhere in it, or beyond it, or under it, she felt — faint, far, unmistakable — a coldness turn its attention toward the Notch. Not hurrying. Not needing to.

The fox stood, stretched, and trotted to the edge of the hollow. There it paused and looked back at her, and its last thought came clear as a bell across the morning air:

*They are coming. The mountain knows the way you sing. Move, speaker. Move before it learns your name.*

Astrid woke Wren with a hand gentle on her shoulder, and her own heart hammering a war-drum, and said, "Time to go, little bird. Time to go *now*."
