## 7. The Outlaw Road

The mountain road was older than the valley, and it did not care who walked it.

Astrid had known that in the abstract, the way one knows a thing from maps and her mother's warnings — *never take the high road, Astrid, never past the ward-stone* — but knowing it in the abstract was nothing like feeling it under her boots. The road climbed through pine shadow in long switchbacks, its stones worn smooth by feet that had not been pilgrims' or shepherds'. These were trader's stones, smuggler's stones, stones that remembered iron and fire. Here and there, old waymarkers leaned out of the moss like rotten teeth, their court-sigils chiseled away by hands that had hated what they meant.

Wren walked beside her, one small fist knotted in Astrid's cloak hem, and did not complain. The child had not complained once since they'd left the ward behind — not at the cold, not at the hunger gnawing at both of them since dawn, not at the way the forest went quiet whenever they passed beneath certain trees. Wren simply watched everything with those dark, unblinking eyes, cataloguing the world the way other children collected river stones.

The shard rode against Astrid's breastbone, wrapped in three layers of wool, and it hummed.

It had hummed all morning. Not the trapped-wasp whine of before — something lower now, deeper, almost conversational, like a woman murmuring through a wall. The mountain was listening to it. She could feel that too: a pressure at the edge of her awareness, vast and slow, the attention of stone itself turning toward them like a great eye opening.

*Move, speaker,* the fox had said. So she moved. But the road only went up, and up was where the mountain lived.

"Rest," she said aloud, and then again in the soft clicking cadence of the Old Tongue, which she was learning to shape badly and Wren understood perfectly. "*Eska.* Rest."

They sat beneath a lightning-split pine, and Astrid shared out the last of the hard cheese and the heel of black bread she'd wrapped in her hurry. Wren ate with the methodical ferocity of a creature who had known real hunger, and Astrid found herself doing arithmetic she didn't like: two days of food gone, four days maybe left if she stretched it, and no idea how far it was to anywhere. The map in her head — drawn from a peddler's gossip twenty years stale — said there was a river town beyond the mountains called Lowmark, and that Lowmark's ferryman paid in silence for passengers who asked no questions. Beyond that, nothing certain at all.

She was wiping crumbs from her knees when the jays stopped talking.

That was how it came, always, this new life of hers outside the ward — not warning but absence. Inside Hollowvale, magic died at the borders and the beasts spoke plainly, one voice at a time, honest as weather. Out here the whole forest was a choir, and when the choir went silent, it meant something had entered it that the singers feared.

The jays stopped. Then the squirrels. Then, far down the slope, a woodpecker abandoned its drumming mid-beat, and the silence rolled uphill toward them like cold water finding its level.

Astrid stood, pulling Wren behind her. "Don't run," she whispered, in both tongues. "Whatever comes, don't run."

They came out of the trees without sound, which was the first wrong thing. Six of them, maybe seven — figures in pieced leathers dyed the gray-brown of bark, faces smeared with ash and pine resin so that eyes floated in the gloom like coins at the bottom of a well. They carried crossbows, but held low, casual, the way a farmer holds a scythe he has used ten thousand times. The last figure carried a kettle on his back, an actual dented iron kettle big enough to boil a sheep in, and it clinked softly against his gear with each step, giving the band its name before anyone spoke it.

The Kettlebones. Astrid had heard the name twice in her life, both times in the low voice people used for things best not summoned.

"Well," said the one in front. He was not large, but he was made entirely of edges — cheekbones, elbows, a jaw like a plow-blade, and a scar that split one eyebrow into two pale worms. His voice was dry and pleasant, which was worse than a shout. "Look at this. A woman and a chick on the Kettle Road, alone, carrying bread." He sniffed theatrically. "Bread and fear. My favorite smells."

"We have nothing worth taking," Astrid said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Twenty-three years of practice at sounding calm while terrified — it finally had a use.

"Everyone says that." The man tilted his head. "And everyone is lying, and mostly they're right anyway, because what they carry isn't in their packs." He smiled. It was not a comforting arrangement of teeth. "I'm Sef. This is my road. You've been walking on it since dawn, which means you owe me toll, and you've been walking on it loudly, which means you owe me more than toll."

"Loudly?" Astrid kept her body between him and Wren. Behind her, the child had gone very still, the stillness of prey animals, of rabbits deciding whether the fox has seen them.

Sef's smile widened a fraction. "You've been singing, woman. All morning. Not with your mouth." He tapped his own chest, then his ear. "With here. Every bird on this slope has been losing its mind about it since first light. Jays don't mob a hawk for nothing. And last night—" he glanced at his companions, and some of the playfulness drained out of his face, leaving something older and warier, "—last night the wolves sang back at you. My wolves. Wolves I have spent six years making peace with, standing at the treeline and answering you like you were pack."

The shard pulsed against Astrid's sternum, hot as a coal.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"No," Sef agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. That's the trouble with gifts like yours — the ones who have them are always the last to understand what they're broadcasting." He raised two fingers, and the crossbows came up — not aimed, exactly, but no longer casual. "Here's what happens now. You walk with us to the camp. You eat our food, because you look like you need it, and I'm generous that way. And then we talk about what's under your cloak, because whatever it is, it's been humming like a struck bell since you crossed the ridge, and Doss here—" he jerked his thumb at the man with the kettle, "—spent eight years in the Undercourt's counting-houses before they took his thumbs' worth of dignity, and he says he knows that song."

The one called Doss stepped forward. Up close he was younger than his bulk suggested, with the gray, washed-out complexion of someone who had lived underground, and his eyes were fixed on Astrid's chest with an expression she recognized: the look of a starving man looking at bread.

"That's a fragment," Doss said quietly. "That's a soul-fragment, or I'm a tithe-collector's honest man. Wrapped in wool like it'll bite. Where did you get it?"

"It's mine," Astrid said.

Doss laughed, short and disbelieving. "Nobody owns a fragment. Fragments are collateral. That thing is a receipt, woman — somebody's soul, portioned out and pawned, and every soul-hunter between here and the deep courts can follow its song like gulls follow a fishing boat." He looked at Sef. "Do you know what a clean fragment sells for? Unclaimed? To the right buyer?"

"I know," Sef said, and his pleasantness had sharpened into something with a blade's geometry. "Which is why we're going to have such an interesting conversation at camp."

* * *

The Kettlebones' camp sat in a hollow beneath a shelf of granite, half cave and half timber, hidden so thoroughly that Astrid would have walked past it a hundred times without seeing it. There were more of them than the six who'd taken her — fifteen, perhaps eighteen, a mix of men and women with the same ash-smeared faces and the same watchful economy of movement. Children, too, two of them, small and quick as weasels, who stared at Wren with frank curiosity until Wren stared back, and then they scattered, delighted and unnerved.

They were given stew. Astrid hadn't expected that. It was mutton and turnip and something bitter-green, served in wooden bowls by a broad woman with a burn-scarred forearm who looked at Astrid's fine mended clothes and Wren's mud-caked rags and said nothing at all, which felt like a courtesy.

But they were also given a tent — a small one, pitched at the camp's edge, and Doss sat outside it on an overturned bucket with a crossbow across his knees, and that told Astrid precisely what kind of guests they were.

Night came down the mountain in blue layers. Around the central fire, the outlaws sang low songs with verses that changed every time, and Astrid caught fragments — names of hanged friends, names of stolen things, a running joke about a tax collector's hat. It should have been cozy. It was the opposite. It was the sound of people at home in a place where she was cargo.

Wren slept against her side, boneless with exhaustion, one hand curled around Astrid's finger even in sleep. The shard had quieted inside the camp — or been quieted; Astrid suspected the granite shelf above them swallowed its song the way the valley's ward swallowed hers. For the first time since crossing the ward-line, her own gift lay flat and silent too, and she felt deaf. Blind. She hadn't realized, in two days, how much she had come to rely on the chorus of the wild — the foxes reporting, the owls keeping count, the crows mapping every moving thing for miles. Silence, she was learning, could be a prison just as easily as a sanctuary.

Sef came to the tent after the fires burned low.

He crouched at the entrance, forearms on his knees, and for a while he just looked at her with those coin-bright eyes. Ash still streaked his face, but he'd wiped some away, and beneath it he was younger than she'd thought. Thirty, maybe. The scarred eyebrow gave him a permanent expression of skeptical amusement.

"Doss wants to take it to Lowmark," Sef said, without preamble. "There's a broker there — calls himself a relic-dealer, really a court fence — who'd pay enough for a clean fragment to feed this band through three winters. Doss lost family to the tithe-wagons. He sees selling it back to them as a kind of joke the gods would appreciate."

"And you?" Astrid kept her voice level. Wren stirred against her side, and she eased a hand over the child's ear as though she could keep the words out.

"I think," Sef said slowly, "that relics are heavy in ways that aren't measured in weight. I've handled court-tokens before — stolen seals, forged passes, a tithe-ledger page once that we sold to a man who hanged himself with it. They're not objects. They're hooks. And that thing under your cloak is the biggest hook I've ever heard of." He paused. "So before Doss makes a decision for all of us, I want to hear it from you. What is it? Whose is it? And why does the mountain sing to you like you're its long-lost daughter?"

Astrid weighed her lies and found, surprisingly, that she was tired of them. She had lied to Brammel the warden with a straight face and steady heart, and hated every word. Here, among thieves, some animal instinct told her the truth might be the safer currency.

"A child came to my door," she said. "Feral. Speaking the Old Tongue. Carrying that." She nodded toward her chest. "Her sister was taken. Sold. The fragment is — proof, I think. Or bait. Or a piece of the sister's soul, portioned out, and the hunters are following it. Two of them reached my valley's border already. They couldn't cross. They waited." She met Sef's eyes. "They were patient the way winter is patient. I ran because waiting longer wasn't living."

Sef was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere across the camp, a log collapsed in a shower of sparks, and a dog — she hadn't noticed they had a dog — lifted its head and looked directly at her, ears forward, and did not bark.

"The Old Tongue," Sef said at last. "The feral child speaks the Old Tongue."

"Yes."

"And you understood her."

"Some of it. Enough."

Something shifted in his face — not softening, exactly. Recalculation. "My grandmother spoke it," he said. "Before the purges. She used to say it wasn't a language, it was an agreement — that everything old agreed to be named by it, and everything named by it could be *asked*." He rubbed the scar through his eyebrow. "The court burned everyone who could ask. Or bought them. Mostly bought them, and burned the ones the buying didn't hold."

The shard pulsed once, warm against Astrid's heart, and she wondered — not for the first time — whether it could hear this conversation, and what it thought of being discussed like a sack of coin.

"So here's my offer," Sef said. "Doss gets his vote tomorrow, and the band votes with him more often than not, because winters are real and ideals are free. But I control the vote's timing, and I'll hold it until we reach the low country. In exchange — you do something for me first."

"What?"

"There's a wolf-pack on this stretch of the road. Big one. Led a bitch we call the Grey Widow — scarred, clever, hates men, and I mean *hates*, and I earned it fair, before you ask. Six years ago I bought passage from her with meat and patience, and it's held, but it's fraying, and lately she's been taking my traps, my sentries' nerve, last week a mule." His jaw tightened. "If your gift is what I think it is, you talk to her. You make the peace hold. You do that, and I'll argue your case with everything I have — and Doss's broker can go whistle."

Astrid looked at him. "And if I fail?"

"Then the band decides, and I won't lie to you about the odds." He rose, brushing pine needles from his knees. "Get some sleep, speaker. Tomorrow you meet the Widow."

He was three steps into the dark when Wren's voice stopped him — small, clear, sleep-thick, speaking words in the Old Tongue that Astrid only half knew, but whose shape she recognized the way one recognizes a melody heard through a wall.

Sef turned back slowly. "What did she say?"

Astrid translated, and the translation felt strange in her mouth, like handling someone else's knife. "She said: *the wolf remembers your smell, thief-man, and it is the smell of the cage.*"

For a moment Sef said nothing at all. Then he laughed — one short, genuine bark of a laugh, surprised out of him — and shook his head.

"Six years old, maybe seven, and already negotiating." He looked down at Wren, who had opened her eyes and was watching him with that bottomless dark gaze. "Keep her close, speaker. Children like that grow up to be dangerous." He paused at the edge of the firelight. "Or hanged. Depends entirely on who finds them first."

He vanished into the dark, and Astrid lay back against the tent's canvas with Wren's weight warm against her ribs, and listened to the strange music of an outlaw camp settling into sleep — songs dying, kettles clinking, the dog circling twice and lying down facing her tent, on guard or on watch, she couldn't tell which.

Out of one trap, she thought, into another. At least this trap fed them stew.

But sleep would not come, and near midnight it was replaced by something else: a pressure at the edge of her silenced senses, faint as a bruise. Down the mountain, far below, at the very limit of what the granite let her feel, something was climbing.

Two somethings. Patient as winter.

The shard warmed against her heart, and in its hum, for the first time, Astrid thought she could distinguish words — or almost words, a voice murmuring through a wall, saying the same thing over and over, gentle and terrible:

*Found. Found. Found.*
