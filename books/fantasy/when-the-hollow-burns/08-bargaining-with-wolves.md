## 8. Bargaining with Wolves

The Kettlebones did not keep cells. They kept a pen.

Astrid understood this within the first hour of her captivity, because the pen had been built for animals — a ring of sharpened stakes lashed with rawhide, set into the lee of the mountain where the wind could not strip the fire's warmth away. It was a wolf-pen. She knew it by the way the ground was packed hard in long, pacing arcs, by the pale grooves teeth had worn into the stakes at muzzle-height, by the smell that no amount of rain ever fully washed from such places: musk and blood and patience.

They had put her and Wren inside it at dusk, after Sef had turned the shard over in her scarred hands three times and said nothing at all. That silence had frightened Astrid more than shouting would have. Shouting meant a decision had been made. Silence meant one was still being weighed.

Now she sat with her back against a stake, Wren curled against her side like a small hot coal, and watched the outlaws' camp burn low across the clearing. There were perhaps thirty of them — men and women both, dressed in pieced leather and stolen court-livery gone gray with mending. They moved like people who had learned never to turn their backs on anything: to each other, to the trees, to the dark. A boy of maybe fifteen turned a spit. A woman with a burn-scarred jaw counted arrows by touch, not sight. Everywhere, the same watchfulness, worn smooth as river stone.

And everywhere, wolves.

They lay among the outlaws as if they were outlaws themselves — great gray-and-ash shapes sprawled against bedrolls, jaws resting on knees, ears turning lazily toward every sound. One dozed beside the cookfire close enough to be singed and did not care. Another stood sentry at the treeline, motionless as a carved thing, its breath rising in slow silver flags.

Astrid had grown up among beasts who could not speak to her. She had spent twenty-three years translating their silence into something like conversation — reading the flick of an ear, the set of a hip, the particular quality of a stare. It was a language built entirely of inference, and it had made her careful, and it had made her lonely in a way she had stopped naming years ago.

These wolves were not silent.

Even here, even with the ward of Hollowvale far behind them and her gift returned to her like blood flooding a numb limb, their voices reached her only faintly — not words, not yet, but pressure. Meaning without shape. The nearest wolf, a big male with a torn ear, was dreaming, and his dream leaked: *running, snow, the white throat of a deer, running.* It pressed against her mind like a hand laid flat on a door.

She wondered what would happen if she pushed back.

*Don't,* she told herself. *You don't know what they are to these people. You don't know what these people are to each other.*

Wren stirred against her side. The child had not spoken since the pen gate closed — not in the Old Tongue, not in anything — but the shard, wrapped now in three layers of cloth in Astrid's satchel, hummed its trapped-wasp hum, and Wren's eyes tracked the sound the way a compass needle tracks north.

"Hungry?" Astrid murmured.

Wren looked up. And then — deliberately, the way a child tests ice before stepping onto it — repeated, in a voice cracked from disuse:

*"Hun-gry."*

The word came out wrong, vowels flattened, but it was her word. Astrid's word. Given back to her in a small ruined voice, like a coin returned worn thin.

Something loosened in her chest that she had not known was knotted. "Yes," she said. "Hungry." She touched her own stomach, then Wren's. "Hungry."

Wren studied the gesture with the ferocious attention of someone memorizing a spell. Then touched her own stomach again, harder, and said, louder, *"Hun-gry!"* — loud enough that heads turned at the fire.

One of them rose. It was the woman with the burned jaw, the arrow-counter, and she crossed the clearing with a bowl in one hand and a knife in the other, and Astrid could not tell which was meant for them.

* * *

Her name was Maret, though she did not offer it; Astrid learned it later, from the way the others spoke around her, the way sparks fly toward the name of the person holding the flint. She crouched outside the stake-ring and passed the bowl through — barley and goat fat and something bitter, wild greens maybe — keeping the knife loose in her other hand the whole time, an afterthought or a first thought, impossible to say.

"Eat," she said. "Sef says you eat. Sef says you don't talk."

"Then Sef is wasting a good night," Astrid said.

Maret's eyes narrowed. Up close, the burn scar pulled her mouth into a permanent half-sneer that her actual expression had nothing to do with. "You know what that rock in your bag is?"

"I know what it isn't. It isn't yours."

"It's court-make." Maret spat into the dirt. "Everything court-make is ours. Everything they took, we take back. That's the whole law we've got left." She nodded at the satchel. "Soul-stuff in there. I can feel it from here, like a tooth going bad. So you'll understand when I say nobody's feeling generous."

"You fed us anyway."

"Sef said to." Maret stood, brushing off her knees. "Sef says a lot of things lately she didn't used to. Since the shard came up the road, she's been saying things." Her gaze flicked to Wren, who was eating with the desperate, unselfconscious hunger of the truly starved, and something in the burned face shifted — softened, or pretended to. "Kid talks funny."

"She's learning mine. I'm learning hers."

"What's hers?"

"The language under everything," Astrid said. "The one your court writes its contracts in."

Maret went very still. For a moment the only sound was Wren's spoon scraping the bowl and the wind combing the high pines. Then the woman laughed — one dry bark, entirely without humor. "Under everything. Aye. That's where my brother lives now. Under everything." She walked away before Astrid could answer, and Astrid sat with the bowl warming her hands and understood that she had just been shown a wound, and that being shown a wound in a camp like this was either a gift or a hook, and she would find out which.

* * *

They came for the wolves at moonrise.

Not all of them — two, the big torn-eared male and a lean female with frost-colored fur — dragged on chains by four outlaws apiece, out past the firelight to a flat stretch of trampled snow where the whole camp gathered in a wide ring. Astrid climbed to her feet at the stake-wall, Wren's hand locked in hers, and watched.

"They fight them," Maret said, appearing at her shoulder. She had come back. Astrid decided not to wonder why. "Each other. Winter's long up here and the passes are shut. Wolves fight, men bet, everybody eats till spring." She shrugged, a gesture that wanted very badly to seem casual and failed. "It's old. Older than the band."

In the ring, the handlers unwound the chains and fell back, and the two wolves circled each other in the torchlight, hackles up, and Astrid felt the pressure of their voices spike so sharply she flinched.

It wasn't rage. That was the terrible thing. She pressed her palm flat against her sternum and listened with the part of herself she had spent twenty-three years starving, and what she heard underneath the snarling was *fear*, vast and articulate — fear shaped like words almost, like a sentence being screamed through a wall: *I do not want this, I do not want this, why does the pack-who-feeds ask this of me, I do not want—*

"Oh," Astrid said aloud. "Oh, you *idiots.*"

Maret turned. "What?"

"They don't want to fight." Astrid's voice came out low and fast. "They're not fighting each other, they're begging each other to stop. Can't you hear it? Look at their feet. Neither one has committed. They keep breaking off and circling back because they're waiting for permission — they think if they refuse hard enough, the pack will call it off."

"That's—" Maret started, and stopped, because in the ring the torn-eared male had done exactly that: broken off, dropped his head, backed two steps with his tail low and his ears flat, offering the female his throat in surrender rather than fight.

And the handlers closed in with whips and chains and forced him back into the circle, and the crowd roared, and the female, cornered by the noise and the torches and the terrible arithmetic of her own survival, finally lunged.

Astrid did not decide to act. Later she would try to reconstruct the moment and find only a gap, the way there is always a gap between lightning and thunder. One instant she was gripping the stake-wall; the next she was through the gap in the stakes — when had there been a gap? — and crossing the snow, and the whole camp was turning toward her, and hands were reaching for her, and none of them mattered, because she had opened the door in her mind that she had kept barred since childhood and let her voice pour through it into the ring.

*Stop.*

It was not a shout. It barely made sound at all. But every beast in the camp heard it — she felt it go out from her like a stone dropped into still water, felt it strike the fighting pair, the sentry at the treeline, the drowsing wolves by the fire, all of them at once — and the ring went silent.

Not quiet. Silent. The female froze mid-lunge, jaws a hand's width from the male's throat. The male lifted his head. Thirty outlaws stood in a circle around two motionless wolves and a mud-spattered woman standing in the torchlight with her arms at her sides, and the silence had a texture to it, a pressure, like the moment after a bell is struck.

Astrid looked at the female wolf and spoke again, silently, in the wordless grammar of her gift: *No one will make you. Lie down. I have you. Lie down.*

The female lay down.

Then the male, slowly, folding himself onto the snow beside her, his torn ear twitching, his eyes fixed on Astrid with an expression she had never once seen in twenty-three years among beasts and recognized anyway, instantly, the way you recognize your own name in a stranger's mouth: *wonder.*

The camp erupted. Not in cheers — in noise, in movement, in the scrape of thirty weapons coming free at once. Someone shouted *witch* and someone else shouted *kill her* and Sef's voice cut through both, flat and cold as lake ice:

"*Quiet.*"

It worked the way only absolute authority works. The circle stilled. Sef came forward through the crowd with her hands empty and her eyes full of something Astrid couldn't read, and stopped three paces away, and looked at the wolves lying calm in the snow, and then at Astrid.

"Twenty years," Sef said. "Twenty years I've run this band, and those two have fought eleven times, and I have watched them break my people's hearts and their own doing it, because the winter doesn't care whose heart breaks." Her voice was level, but there was gravel under it. "You're telling me they never wanted to."

"They never wanted to," Astrid said. "They want to belong to you. That's the whole of it. That's all any of them want. You could have asked them to guard your children instead and they'd have died happy."

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind Sef, Maret was staring at Astrid with her burned jaw working, saying nothing.

Sef crouched, slowly, and laid her hand on the female wolf's neck — not a handler's grip, but flat, the way you'd touch a grief-stricken friend. The wolf leaned into it. Sef closed her eyes.

When she opened them, she looked older and younger at once. "Get her a fire," she said to the camp, jerking her chin at Astrid. "And the kid. And somebody get those chains off my wolves before I start remembering every winter we spent them." She turned back to Astrid, and her mouth twisted into something that was almost, almost a smile. "You sleep outside the pen tonight. Don't make me regret the upgrade."

* * *

The fire they gave her was small but it was hers, at the edge of camp, and Wren slept against her ribs with the boneless totality of the truly safe. Astrid sat awake longer than she should have, watching sparks climb.

The wolves had not left. The torn-eared male lay ten feet off, watching her, and after a while he rose and came closer and lay down again, nearer, and nearer still, until his warmth reached her like an offered hand. She put her fingers in his ruff. His dream leaked gently against her mind: *snow, pack, the strange voice like clean water, snow, warm.*

"You're welcome," she whispered.

From the shadows by the woodpile, Maret's voice: "You talk to them out loud too, then. When there's no one listening."

"There's always someone listening," Astrid said. "That's rather the problem."

Maret came and sat down across the fire, uninvited, and for a while neither of them spoke. Then, quietly, watching the flames: "My brother. Torv. The court took him eight years back. Debt-collection, they called it. He owed them nothing — our mother paid — but the ledger said otherwise, and the ledger is written by the people collecting." She turned her hands toward the heat. "If your rock in that bag knows anything about ledgers. About souls. About what they do down there." Her voice caught, just once, and steadied. "I'd stand in front of whatever's coming for it. That's all. Thought you should know you're not traveling alone tomorrow."

Astrid looked at her across the fire — this scarred, guarded woman who had brought a knife to feed prisoners and had come back anyway — and felt the first warmth of the journey settle into her bones like a coal carried in cupped hands.

"Tomorrow," Astrid said, "we cross the high pass. And at the top of it there's a river that isn't a river."

Maret frowned. "What else would it be?"

"A toll," Astrid said. "And I'm about to learn how much I owe."

Beside her, half-asleep, Wren stirred and murmured into Astrid's coat — soft, slurred, unmistakable:

*"To-mor-row."*

The torn-eared wolf sighed, and the fire popped, and far to the south, beneath the mountain, something patient began to move.
