## 9. The River-God's Toll

The river found them before they saw it.

Astrid heard it first as a change in the air — a wetness in the wind that had been dry mountain dust for two days — and then the trees began to lean, all of them tilting downhill like listeners bending toward a voice. Wren felt it too. The child stopped mid-stride on the deer trail, head cocked, and made a sound Astrid had not heard before: a low, rising note, half question and half warning, the Old Tongue folded into something almost like birdsong.

"I know," Astrid said. "I hear it."

The beasts had been telling her about the river since dawn. Crows passing overhead had dropped the word into her mind like a stone into a well — *wide, wide, old, old* — and a fox they'd startled at a stream crossing had sent back an image so vivid it had stopped her heart for a moment: brown water moving with intent, water that watched, water that remembered being worshipped.

Beyond Hollowvale's ward, where her gift ran through her like blood through a thawed limb, the animals did not merely answer her. They *spoke*. And what they spoke of, more often than anything else, was hunger, territory, fear — and gods.

*The river takes,* the fox had said, tail low, eyes bright. *The river takes from everyone.*

The trees broke open at midafternoon, and there it was.

Astrid had seen rivers before — the Greywater that fed Hollowvale's mills, tame as a milk-cow, channeled between stone banks her grandfather's generation had laid. This was nothing of the kind. The Vesswater came down out of the high snows a quarter mile wide, deep green-brown, moving with the slow muscular patience of something that had never once needed to hurry. Mist stood along its surface in columns, and the mist moved against the wind. On the far bank, barely visible through the haze, a ferry landing jutted out on black pilings, and beside it a single lantern burned even though the light was still good.

There was no bridge. There was no boat on their side. There was only the water, and the water was looking at them.

Astrid felt its attention the way she felt a hawk's — a pressure between the shoulder blades, a prickle across the scalp. Beside her, Wren pressed close against her hip, and the shard in its little hide pouch went suddenly, violently quiet, as if it too knew better than to make noise here.

"It knows what you carry," Astrid murmured. She crouched, put her hand flat on the child's back. "Everything here will know what you carry."

Wren looked up at her. In the weeks since the valley, since the ward, since the night flight down the mountain with hunters' lanterns blooming behind them like poisonous flowers, the child had learned perhaps forty words of Astrid's language. Now Wren reached up, touched Astrid's mouth with two small fingers, and said, carefully, in a voice still roughened by whatever wilderness had raised it:

"Pay."

Astrid's stomach dropped. "You know this place."

"Pay." Wren touched her mouth again. Then touched their own chest. Then pointed at the river, and made a shape with both hands — a cupping, an offering, a thing held out flat on open palms.

Somewhere behind them, far up the trail, a crow called once — and in the crow's voice Astrid heard the word again, layered under the bird-sound like a figure beneath a painting: *toll, toll, toll.*

"All right," she said softly. "All right. We pay."

* * *

The ferryman came when the light began to fail.

Astrid had built a fire small enough to smother with one boot, and they'd eaten cold oats and dried apples, and Wren had slept curled against her side with the shard tucked between them like a third heartbeat. Astrid had been drifting herself when the mist on the water gathered itself, drew inward, and became a boat.

It was a long, low craft of grey wood, wood gone silver with age, and it crossed the quarter mile of river without oars, without wake, without sound. At the stern stood a figure that might have been tall or might have been many figures standing very close together, wrapped in robes the color of the water, and where a face should have been there was a smoothness like the surface of a pool at dusk — and within that surface, if Astrid stared, things moved. Fish. Drowned leaves. Once, horribly, a hand.

She woke Wren with a touch. The child came awake the way wild things do — instantly, silently, already looking at the boat.

When the prow kissed the near bank, the figure spoke. Its voice was the river's voice: broad, unhurried, layered, the sound of current over stones and current over drowned things.

**"Two souls cross. Two tolls."**

"We have no coin," Astrid said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "We have nothing worth—"

**"I do not take coin."** The faceless head tilted, and the mist around the boat stirred. **"Coin is for men. I am older than coin. I take what cannot be weighed and cannot be returned."** A pause, and the water lapped the bank with terrible gentleness. **"Memories. One each. Chosen by me. Given freely, or the crossing fails and you remain on your side until you choose differently."**

Astrid's mouth went dry. "Chosen by you."

**"I choose well,"** the river-god said, and there was something in the layered voice that might have been amusement, might have been appetite. **"Ask any who have paid. If they remember asking."**

Wren tugged Astrid's sleeve. When she looked down, the child was holding out both hands, palms up, in that cupping gesture again — offering. Willing. As if paying a god were a chore like carrying firewood, as if memory were a berry to be handed over.

"No," Astrid whispered. "Wren, we don't know what it will take from you. You've lost enough. You've—" She stopped. The child had lost a sister, a home, a language, a name. What memory could the river want from someone so young? And yet the thought of letting it reach into that small history and pluck something out—

**"The small one pays first,"** Vess said. **"Or neither crosses."**

The mist thickened. Somewhere in it, Astrid heard the crows going silent, one by one, along the whole darkening bank.

Wren stepped forward before Astrid could move. Small bare feet on the mud. Hands still open. And then the child turned, looked up at Astrid, and said — slowly, tasting each word, arranging them like sticks for a fire:

"You. Keep. For me."

Astrid knelt in the mud. "What?"

"If I lose it," Wren said, and now the words came easier, the way they'd been coming easier every day, the child's tongue learning Astrid's language the way a vine learns a trellis, "you keep it. You hold it. So I don't lose whole."

And Astrid understood, with a lurch, what the child was asking. Not to be spared the toll. To be witnessed. *Remember for me*, those dark eyes said. *Whatever it takes, you carry the shape of it, so that I am not smaller after.*

"I will," Astrid said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "I swear it. Whatever it takes from you, I'll keep it safe. I'll tell it back to you every day if I have to."

Wren nodded once, satisfied, and turned to the god.

Vess bent — and it was like watching a hillside bend, slow and enormous — and the smooth pool of its face came level with the child's. The mist reached out. It did not touch Wren; it simply *surrounded*, a soft grey cocoon, and for three heartbeats the child stood inside it utterly still.

Then the mist withdrew, and Wren blinked, and frowned faintly, and looked around as if the world had been rearranged slightly while they weren't watching.

"What did it take?" Astrid asked. Her hands were shaking. "Wren. What did it take?"

Wren considered. Then, with the terrible practicality of children: "Don't know. That's how it works." A pause. "You keep it. Remember."

*I can't remember what I never saw.* But Astrid nodded, and pulled the child close, and felt the small body warm and alive and unbroken against hers, and told herself the ledger would balance someday.

**"Now you,"** said the river.

* * *

The god did not ask permission. It simply looked at her — that depthless pool-face turning toward her like a lens — and Astrid felt it begin to read her.

It was worse than anything the soul-hunters had done at the ward's edge. The hunters had probed like fingers at a locked door. Vess opened the door, walked through the house of her life, and lingered where it pleased. She felt it pause over the ewe Maren, dying in the spring mud, and the last words Astrid had spoken into her small grey ear. It paused over the ward, and the hum of it, and the twenty-three years of careful silence. It paused over the shard, and made a sound like water finding a new channel — interest, or greed.

And then it paused over her mother.

Astrid tried to flinch away from it and could not. The memories rose whether she willed them or not: her mother's hands, cracked and capable, kneading bread. Her mother's voice singing off-key on washing days. Her mother standing at the valley's edge the day Astrid was seven, staring at the shimmer of the ward with an expression Astrid had never understood and had never forgotten — grief and defiance braided together, a woman looking at a wall she had helped build and hated having built.

Her mother, who had died quietly nine years ago and been buried without marker because markers invited questions, and whose last coherent word had been Astrid's name, and whose second-to-last coherent word had been — Astrid had always assumed delirium —

*"Paid in full."*

The river took that memory.

Not the whole of her mother — Vess left her the bread and the songs and the grave with no stone. What it took was precise, surgical, deliberate: the moment at the deathbed, the fevered whisper, *paid in full*, and everything attached to it. Astrid felt the memory lift out of her like a splinter drawn from flesh, and felt the strange hollow it left behind, a socket shaped exactly like those four words.

And into the hollow, as the toll settled, poured understanding.

That was the cruelty or the mercy of Vess — Astrid would never know which. The god did not simply take. Taking a memory left a hole, and holes ached, and the ache explained itself. She knelt on the riverbank with the mist curling around her ankles and suddenly, catastrophically, she *knew*.

Her mother had known magic.

Not guessed at it. Not feared it in the abstract way the valley feared it. *Known* it — known what it cost, known where it was bought and sold, known the weight of a soul on a scale. *Paid in full.* Those were not the ramblings of a fevered woman. Those were the words of someone who had stood before a power — the ward, the court, something — and settled an account. Someone who had handed over the cupped palms and let the mist come down.

Someone who had paid a toll so that her daughter could live in silence and safety, and had carried the knowledge of that payment alone for twenty-three years, and had died with it still in her mouth.

"Oh," Astrid said aloud, and the word came out broken. "Oh, Mama."

The river said nothing. The river had taken what it came for. But the mist lay soft around her, almost gentle, and the great faceless head inclined — acknowledgment, or condolence, or simply the patience of water.

Grief opened in her like a door that had been painted shut for years. All the old grief for her mother's death came flooding through it, but behind the old grief came a new one, vaster: grief for the woman herself, for the size of what she had done and hidden, for the years she had spent kneading bread and singing off-key while carrying the knowledge that her own soul was gone — sold, spent, portioned out somewhere in the dark beneath a mountain — so that her daughter's gift would go unpunished and unheard. Every ordinary day of Astrid's childhood had been purchased. Every safe, small, suffocating year in Hollowvale had been bought with the only currency that couldn't be weighed.

And the ward. The ward that had kept the hunters out, that had smothered her gift, that had caged her as surely as it had shielded her — the ward was *fed*. Brammel said it was old law, valley law, laid down by the founders. But founders didn't build walls that breathed. Walls that breathed needed hearts.

*Mama. What did you give them? What is still giving?*

Wren's hand found hers. Small, mud-streaked, certain. The child looked up at Astrid's face, read whatever was written there, and did not ask questions. Instead Wren leaned against her side, the way the ewes used to lean against her legs in the lambing shed, and stayed.

**"The toll is paid,"** Vess said at last. **"Both tolls. Board."**

They boarded the silver-wood boat, and it turned against the current without effort, and the far bank came toward them through the mist. Astrid sat in the stern with her arm around the child and stared at the water sliding past and thought about her mother standing at the edge of the ward, hating the wall she had built, and understood at last that the hatred had not been for the wall at all. It had been for the price. And the love — the fierce, silent, unspoken love that Astrid had spent twenty-three years doubting, starved of it, hungry for it, telling herself her mother was a hard woman, a closed woman, a woman who kept even affection behind glass —

The love had been the payment. The love had been the coin.

Astrid pressed her knuckles to her mouth and wept without sound, the way her mother would have, the way the valley taught, and the river carried them on through the darkening mist.

* * *

The far landing was black pilings and a lantern burning with a flame that cast no heat. Beyond it, the outlaw road climbed into pine shadow, and somewhere up that road, according to the crows, waited men who traded in stolen court-tokens and trusted no one.

Astrid stood on the bank a moment longer than she needed to. Behind her the Vesswater moved, vast and patient, holding everything it had ever taken.

"Mama paid for my silence," she said quietly, to Wren, to the river, to herself. "Twenty-three years. And I never once thanked her, because I never knew."

Wren tugged her sleeve and pointed up the trail, where the pines stood black against the last light. Then the child pointed at the shard-pouch at Astrid's belt, and then — deliberately, gravely — at Astrid's chest, over her heart.

"Sister," Wren said. "Then mother. Then sister." The child's brow furrowed, working through the grammar of a language only weeks old, and found the words: "Same door. Same court. We open."

Astrid looked down at the child — this feral, fearless creature who had crossed a river-god's palm without flinching — and felt something settle in her that had been unsettled since the night the knock came at her door.

"Yes," she said. "We open it."

Above them, on the ridge where the outlaw road bent out of sight, a torch flared — and then another, and another, a line of fires kindling in the dusk like eyes opening.

"They know we're coming," Astrid breathed.

The crows, wheeling down from the pines, agreed.
