## 10. The Scribe Who Fell

The house at the edge of the fen had once belonged to a reed-cutter, and it still smelled of his trade: cut stalks, river mud, the sweet rot of things left to soften in standing water. Ilex had lived in it for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once called it home. Home was a word for people who had not sold their place in the world to buy their way out of it.

He was grinding ink when they came.

Not court-ink — he had sworn off court-ink, or told himself he had, though the mortar and pestle in his hands were the same ones he had carried out of the Undercourt beneath his coat, wrapped in oilcloth like a stolen infant. The pigment was cheap fen-soot, grey as old snow, and it made a thin, watery line on the page that no archivist of the Iron Court would have accepted. That was rather the point. He was writing a book that no one would ever read: a history of the Undercourt's soul-ledgers, copied from memory, entry by entry, name by name. Nine hundred and forty-one souls portioned and sold in the reign of the Soulkeeper alone. He knew them all. He had written them all down the first time, in violet ink on vellum, in a hand so beautiful the Soulkeeper had once held the page to the light and said, *Ilex, you write grief as if it were music.*

He had been proud then. He could still feel the shape of that pride, the way an amputee feels weather in a missing limb.

The knock came at dusk, three blows, uneven — a fist, then a smaller fist, then the first again. Ilex set down the pestle. His heart did something complicated and old, the way it always did when anyone knocked, because exiles learn early that visitors arrive in one of two costumes: need or consequence.

He opened the door on a woman and a child, both mud-colored, both wind-burned, both watching him with the particular wariness of people who had been hunted long enough to make caution into a posture. Behind them, at the fen's edge, two enormous wolves sat in the shallows like sentries, steam rising off their coats, their eyes catching the last light in flat yellow coins.

Wolves. At a scribe's door. Ilex's hand tightened on the frame.

"Forgive the hour," the woman said. Her voice was low and roughened, the voice of someone who had been talking to animals or to silence for a very long time. "We were told you keep records. Old ones."

"Who told you that?"

"The Kettlebones." A pause, and then, with the reluctance of someone paying a debt she resented: "Sef. She said if anyone between here and the mountain could read what we carry, it would be the scribe who fell."

*The scribe who fell.* So his disgrace had traveled ahead of him, worn smooth by retelling, turned into a kind of signpost. He supposed he should be grateful. In the Court, falling was the only promotion available to the honest.

"I don't keep records," he said. "I keep chickens. Badly."

The child stepped forward.

She was small — seven, perhaps eight — with hair cut raggedly, as if she had done it herself with a knife and no mirror, and eyes that were too still for her face. She wore a pouch of hide against her chest the way other children wore amulets, except that this one *hummed*. Ilex heard it before he saw it, a thin insectile note that seemed to pass through the doorframe and through his ribs and settle somewhere behind his sternum, where guilt kept its rooms.

He knew that sound. He had catalogued its source eleven hundred times.

"May we come in," the woman said, "or do we stand here until your wolves decide for us?"

"They're not my wolves."

"No," she agreed, and there was something in her voice — a dryness, a knowing — that made him suspect she spoke with wolves the way he wrote with ink, fluently and without thinking. "They aren't anyone's. Which is why they're waiting in your shallows instead of eating us."

---

He let them in because the alternative was letting them stand in the dark, and because — though he would not admit this until much later, turning it over at 3 a.m. like a stone with a warm underside — because the humming had reached into him and found the ledger-shaped hollow where his conscience lived, and it had begun, gently, unbearably, to read aloud.

The woman gave her name as Astrid. Astrid Lockridge.

The name went through Ilex like a nail through vellum.

He was fortunate that his back was to her, bent over the hearth, coaxing the fire he had let go small because small fires drew less attention. He was fortunate that eleven years of exile had taught his face to be furniture. But inside — inside, the archive opened. Entry four hundred and six. He had written it in his twenty-ninth year, when his hand was at its most beautiful and his soul at its least defended.

*Lockridge, Maren. One soul, entire, surrendered freely. Consideration rendered: silence-gift conferred upon issue; ward-sustenance in perpetuity; valley of Hollowvale sealed against the Court's reach. Witnessed and recorded under seal.*

He had asked about it afterward. That was the thing he had never forgiven himself for — not the recording, which was his office, his duty, the work of his hands whether he loved it or not — but the asking. He had gone to the Soulkeeper's second-in-command, a creature made mostly of folded grey robes and patience, and he had said, *the consideration seems slight. An entire soul, for a silence?*

And the grey thing had looked at him with eyes like the space between stars and said, *Slight? Scribe, we purchased a mother's love entire. There is no coin in any court that buys that. We got it for the price of one child's voice. The market would call us thieves.*

He had laughed. He remembered laughing. He remembered the sound of his own laughter in the archive, bouncing off the shelves of soul-vellum, and how it had seemed, for one bright moment, like wit rather than damnation.

Now the daughter of that transaction stood in his kitchen, dripping fen-water onto his floor, asking him to read the shard her companion carried — the shard that hummed, the fragment of a portioned soul, a beacon and a key and a wound all at once.

"You're a beast-speaker," he said, without meaning to say it aloud.

Astrid went very still. The stillness of prey, of a held breath, of a valley sealed against the Court's reach. "I'm a woman who keeps goats."

"In Hollowvale." He turned from the fire. "Where magic dies at the border. Where a gift like yours would starve quietly, year after year, until you almost believed you'd imagined it." He heard his own voice and hated its gentleness, its terrible accuracy. "I know the terms of your valley, Mistress Lockridge. I helped write them."

The child — Wren, Astrid called her, though the girl answered to nothing so much as the sound of her own breathing — had drifted to the table while they spoke. She was looking at his manuscript pages, the grey-inked history, and her finger moved along the lines of text without touching them, the way children trace writing before they can read it. Then she stopped. Tapped one line. Looked up at Ilex with those too-still eyes.

"That's my sister's name," Astrid said quietly. "Isn't it. On that page."

Ilex looked. Of course it was. He had written every name; the shard's fragment had come through his office like all the others, logged, weighed, portioned. *One soul, partial extraction, remainder retained for vault storage pending valuation.*

"It is," he said.

"And you're going to help us get her back."

It was not a question, but it deserved one, and the question underneath it — the one Astrid was too careful to ask — was *can you be trusted*, and the honest answer was *no one knows, least of all me*, and so Ilex did what guilty men have always done in the presence of hope. He put on his helpful face. It fit well. He had practiced it for eleven years in a cracked mirror, and if the seams showed, neither of them mentioned it.

"Yes," he said. "I'll help you."

---

Later, when Astrid slept in the chair by the fire with Wren curled against her like a comma against a sentence, Ilex sat at his table and did not write.

The shard lay on the cloth between them, wrapped now in three layers of wool, and even through the wool it hummed, patient as a wasp in a wall. He had examined it — professionally, briefly, the way a physician examines a wound he caused — and confirmed what Sef must have suspected: it was a fragment of a portioned soul, severed during extraction, carrying within it the memory of the vault, the throne room, the binding words. Carrying, possibly, more than memory. The Court's own records were ambiguous on the subject. Fragments were supposed to be inert. This one was not.

Which meant the hunters were already coming. Which meant every hour they spent in his fen-house was an hour borrowed against ruin.

He should tell them that. He would tell them that, tomorrow, in the morning, with tea and a straight face.

What he would not tell them — what he had already decided, in the cold administrative center of himself where the Court had trained its deepest habits — was that there was another way. That the shard alone might free one soul, but that a scribe who knew the ledgers, who knew the seals, who knew the *procedures*, might free all of them. That his knowledge was worth more than his loyalty, and that he intended to trade it carefully, keeping enough of himself in reserve to survive whichever way the bargain broke.

That was the helpful face's true architecture: kindness with an escape route built into the joists.

He looked at the sleeping child. Wren had taken one of his blank practice sheets and a stub of charcoal and was — even asleep, her hand had kept moving — covering the page with marks. Not drawing. Writing. The letters were wrong, backwards, half-formed, but they were *letters*, and Ilex leaned close and read them with a scholar's eye and felt the floor tilt beneath his certainty.

She was copying the words from the shard's visions. Binding words. Unbinding words. The Old Tongue, learned by rote from a fragment of stolen soul, syllable by syllable, the way a bird learns a song it does not understand.

*Unbinding,* he thought. *She is learning unbinding.*

He sat back. Outside, the wolves had begun to sing — low, sustained, a sound less like music than like testimony — and far to the north, past the fen, past the hills, the mountain stood black against the stars with its iron court beneath it, and somewhere in that court a Soulkeeper was doing sums, and Ilex understood, with the clarity that comes only to men who have already fallen once, that the next entry in his history would not be written in grey ink.

It would be written in whatever color the truth bled.

He reached for the wool-wrapped shard, and began, very softly, to hum along.
