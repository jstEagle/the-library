## 11. What Mother Bought

Ilex's house smelled of tallow and old paper, and Astrid thought she had never been anywhere so sad.

It was not the squalor — though there was squalor, dishes crusted green in a basin, a pallet bed that had never once been made — but the *incompleteness* of it. Every surface held the ghost of something removed. A shelf with one book's worth of dust-ring where ten volumes had stood. A desk whose drawers had been pulled out and left gaping like mouths after teeth. A wall hung with hooks from which everything hangable had been taken, except for a single grey quill, its barb chewed to a stub.

"They took my library when they took my rank," Ilex said, following her gaze. He said it lightly, the way men say things they have rehearsed until lightness was all that remained. "A scribe's books are court property. As is a scribe's memory of what he wrote, in theory. They burn the ledgers' twins when a man falls." He gestured at the cold hearth. "I saved them the trouble of mine by leaving before the burning could be arranged."

Wren sat on the floor near the door, exactly as far from it as Astrid had placed her, turning over a wooden token Sef had given her — a coin stamped with a closed eye, outlaw currency. The shard rode against Astrid's sternum, wrapped in wool, humming its low trapped-wasp note. All evening it had hummed louder, and she did not think it was the house. She thought it knew where it was going. She thought it was glad.

"You said you'd tell me," Astrid said. "You said if I came here, if I showed you the fragment, you'd tell me what was written about my family."

"I said I would consider telling you."

"You said—" Wren shifted, and Astrid made herself lower her voice. "We crossed the fen. We carried your seals out of a river-god's country. Sef's people died buying us the road to this door. You don't get to *consider* anything."

Ilex looked at her for a long moment. In the tallow light his face was older than it had seemed at the waystation, the softness gone out of it, and she saw the court in him then — saw the twenty years of sitting behind a recording-desk, watching souls be portioned into iron scales, writing it all down in grey ink because grey ink was what the court paid for.

"No," he agreed quietly. "I suppose I don't."

He went to the hearth, knelt, and worked loose two of the floor stones with his fingernails. From the cavity beneath he drew an oilcloth bundle no bigger than a loaf, unwrapped it, and set on the table a single ledger page, folded twice, browned at the creases.

Astrid did not touch it.

"Every entry in the Undercourt is written twice," Ilex said. "Once in the fair hand, for the throne's eyes, and once in the true hand, which is to say in the hand of whoever witnessed the transaction, sealed with their own blood-oath that they recorded honestly. When a soul is sold, both copies are kept. Forever. That is the whole architecture of the court — nothing is ever unrecorded, nothing is ever undone. It's why their bargains hold when gods' bargains fray." He tapped the fold. "This is a true-hand copy. Mine. My blood-oath on it, which is why I could steal it and why stealing it ruined me. A scribe who takes back his own testimony has broken the only law the court actually enforces."

"What does it say?"

"Read it yourself. You're a woman who reads, I think. You have the look."

She did have the look. Her mother had taught her letters at the kitchen table by rushlight, patient as water, saying: *A girl who can read can read anything, and a girl who can read anything can't be lied to.* Astrid had wondered, later, sometimes, why a woman so careful — so ferociously, exhaustingly careful about every other thing — had never once worried about that lesson.

She unfolded the page.

The court script was dense and angular, but the true-hand ran alongside it in plainer characters, and she read it standing, then read it again sitting down, because her legs stopped agreeing to hold her.

---

*Transaction recorded this ninth night of the Reaping Moon, in the 40th year of the Second Keeper's accounting.*

*Seller: Maren Lockridge, of Hollowvale-in-the-Ward, of sound mind and free will, witnessed.*

*Commodity offered: one (1) human soul, entire, unencumbered, freely given.*

*Commodity requested in exchange: perpetual silence upon the child Astrid Lockridge, beast-speaker, such that her gift shall sleep until woken by her own choice beyond the ward; and the maintenance of the Hollowvale ward against all seeking, scrying, and soul-hunting, for the term of the seller's natural life and the life of the child, whichever ends last.*

*Terms accepted. The soul of Maren Lockridge is entered into the vaults of the second throne. Upon the death of her body, the soul shall remain the property of the Undercourt in perpetuity, without redemption, ransom, or appeal.*

*Witnessed and recorded in the true hand by Ilex, under-scribe of the third desk.*

---

The tallow flame bent. Somewhere outside a wolf was singing, low and sustained, and Astrid heard none of it.

"She didn't die of the fever," Astrid said. Her voice came out flat, wrong, someone else's voice. "That winter. When I was nine."

"She died of whatever the court sent to collect her," Ilex said gently. "They're efficient. They don't like bodies lingering past the terms."

Astrid looked at him. Then at the page. Then at the words *beast-speaker* sitting there in her mother's bargain like a name scratched onto a cage.

"That isn't possible," she said. "My gift — I was born with it. I spoke to a goat before I could walk. My mother was *afraid* of it. She flinched every time I—"

"Yes," Ilex said. "And do you remember the first time you couldn't?"

She wanted to strike him. She almost did — her hand actually rose, and Wren looked up from her token with those feral amber eyes, tracking the motion like a hawk.

But underneath the wanting to hurt him, memory was moving, and she could not stop it. She was six years old, shrieking at a ewe to get out of the rain, and her mother coming white-faced out of the house, seizing her shoulders, saying *not so loud, Astrid, love, not so loud* — and then the next morning, the silence arriving like a lid set over a well. Not gone. Never gone; she had always felt the beasts at the edge of her hearing like voices through a wall. But muffled. Dimmed. She had thought, all these years, that she had learned to hide it. That the hiding had been hers, her own cleverness, her own discipline.

It had been purchased. Installed like a lock. Her whole small careful life — the valley that swallowed magic, the ward that turned aside every seeking eye, the twenty-three years of dying ewes and unsuspicious neighbors and a gift kept dim enough to survive — none of it was luck, or cunning, or her mother's fear working itself out in practical arrangements.

It was goods delivered. Terms fulfilled.

"She sold herself," Astrid said. The words tasted of iron filings. "For me. She went under the mountain and put her soul on their scale so I could live in a valley where I'd never have to be what I am."

"Yes."

"And she never told me. Twenty years. She watched me grieve her and she never—" Astrid stopped, because her voice had begun to shake, and shaking felt too close to weeping, and weeping felt like something she would not survive in front of this man.

"She couldn't tell you," Ilex said. "Read the terms again. *Perpetual silence upon the child.* Not just the gift, Mistress Lockridge. The court writes broadly. Your mother's silence was part of the price too — she bought your quiet with her own. She died unable to confess it even if she'd wanted to. Even on her deathbed, with you holding her hand." He hesitated. "There's a line in the fair-hand copy I never saw but heard described, by the under-scribe who kept the third desk before me. He said the seller asked for one amendment to the standard terms. She asked that the child be allowed to choose — that the silence should break *by her own choice beyond the ward*. Most purchases of that kind bind absolutely. She negotiated. With the second throne itself, they say. For a farm woman, they say she bargained like a river."

Astrid sat down. The chair scraped. The tallow guttered and steadied.

Beyond the ward. By her own choice.

All of it — the knock at dusk, the child on her doorstep, the shard humming against her heart — all of it had been waiting inside the terms like a seed inside wax. Her mother had not just bought her safety. She had bought her a door, and painted it shut, and hidden the hinges, and then gone into the dark under the mountain knowing that someday, somehow, her daughter would find the edge of the paint and pick at it.

Fury came up in Astrid like floodwater.

She stood, and the page was in her fist before she knew she'd grabbed it, and she was shouting — shouting at Ilex, at the dead air, at the grey-inked universe — "She could have *told* me! There were years, there were hundreds of mornings, she could have said *Astrid, listen, this is what I did, this is what it cost* — instead of letting me think I was a coward! Letting me build my whole life on being afraid! Do you know what it is to spend twenty years being ashamed of the thing your mother *bled for*? To hate your own gift because you think it's a curse, when it was — when she—"

The word broke in half.

Wren was beside her then. Astrid hadn't seen her cross the room; the child moved like weather. Small hands took the crushed page out of Astrid's fist with great care, smoothed it against the tabletop, pressed the folds flat, and set the wooden closed-eye token on top of it to weight it down, the way one marks a place in a book one intends to return to.

Then Wren looked up at her and said, in her careful, half-learned valley speech: "Mother. Good. Mother good."

And the fury went out of Astrid all at once, the way a wave goes out through shingle, leaving the beach rearranged.

She sank back into the chair. She put her face in her hands. And what came then was not weeping exactly, or not only — it was the terrible tenderness, arriving on the heels of the rage like dawn arriving on the heels of a long bad night, and it was worse than the rage. Because now she could see it. Now she could see her mother at forty years old, walking out of Hollowvale alone, down roads Astrid had never traveled, to a mountain that ate souls, to stand in front of a thing on an iron throne and offer the only thing she had — and she could see her coming home again, hollowed, carrying the silence like a stone in her belly, and planting the garden anyway. Milking the cows anyway. Teaching her daughter letters anyway, *so she could read anything and never be lied to* — oh, that had been a message, that had been the only confession the terms allowed, tucked inside a schoolroom lesson like contraband inside a hem.

Her mother had spent twenty years dying slowly into a scale, and she had never once let Astrid taste the salt of it.

*I bought you a valley,* she must have thought, every morning, kneading bread. *Let it be enough. Let it be enough. Let it be enough.*

"She's still there," Astrid said into her hands. "Her soul. In the vaults. Under the mountain."

Ilex said nothing, which was answer enough.

"The shard." Astrid lifted her head. Her eyes were wet and her voice was level now, and something in the levelness made Ilex take a half-step back. "The fragment Wren carries. It's a piece of a sold soul — Wren's sister's. And if sold souls are kept whole and separate, forever, without redemption, ransom, or appeal—"

"Then a fragment cut from one can find its whole," Ilex finished quietly. "Yes. That's why the hunters follow it. It isn't just stolen property to them, Mistress Lockridge. It's proof the vaults can be breached. If one soul can be carried out in pieces, any soul can be found. Their entire claim rests on permanence." He looked at the page under the wooden eye. "Your mother's soul is in those vaults. Entered under the name Lockridge. Fortieth year of the Second Keeper."

"Then I know where I'm going," Astrid said.

"Mistress Lockridge—" Ilex's voice cracked upward, and for a moment the exile fell away and he was only a clerk again, appalled at arithmetic. "Do you understand what you're saying? Hunters are already on the road. The Soulkeeper has *summed* you by now — your inheritance makes you valuable, that's why they want you alive, and value, to that throne, means *use*, and use means—" He stopped himself. Started softer. "Twenty years ago your mother stood where you're planning to stand, and she was clever, and she loved you more than her own existence, and the best bargain she could win cost her *everything*, forever. What do you imagine you'll win?"

"My mother's soul," Astrid said. "Mine to bargain with, since she spent it on me. And while I'm at it—" her hand found the wool-wrapped shard through her shirt, warm as a living thing, humming its glad, terrible hum — "every other soul they've got."

The tallow flame stood very straight, as if listening.

Wren picked up her closed-eye token, turned it over in her fingers, and then reached across the table and set it in Astrid's palm instead — closing Astrid's fingers around it with both her small hands, the way one closes a bargain, or passes a burden to stronger shoulders.

And outside, far off in the dark toward the mountain, the wolves stopped singing all at once, mid-note, the way animals stop when something vast has turned its attention toward them.

Ilex went to the shuttered window and stood listening, and his face in the tallow light had gone the color of old paper.

"They've stopped," he said. "They never stop." He turned, and Astrid saw that whatever he had been about to become — penitent, helpful, redeemed — was being weighed, right then, against whatever he had been before, and the scales were trembling. "Mistress Lockridge. Before first light, I have to go to the waystation. There are seals there — court seals, stolen, mine — that will open the root-doors of the mountain. If you mean to do this mad thing, you'll need them."

He swallowed.

"And the mountain will need to know you're coming. That's the price of the seals. They cry out when they're used. So understand me clearly: when we breach those doors, the second throne will look up from her sums, and she will see a beast-speaker with an inherited claim walking into her own hall, and she will smile." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "Because you are not breaking into her court, Mistress Lockridge. You are walking into the room where your family has been bought and sold twice already. And thrones always recognize returning customers."

Astrid closed her fist around the wooden eye until its edges bit.

Above the house, through the shutter slats, the first pale edge of moonrise showed the mountain's silhouette against the stars — black, patient, enormous, with all its lanterns of stolen light burning somewhere deep beneath the stone.

Somewhere under all that rock, in a vault with no redemption and no appeal, a woman named Maren Lockridge waited, and did not know her daughter was coming.

"Then let's not keep her waiting," Astrid said.
