## 12. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The Soulkeeper did not sleep. Sleep was a thing for creatures with only one life to tend, and the Soulkeeper had ten thousand.

They sat in the second throne of the Undercourt, in a hall carved so deep beneath the mountain that the stone had forgotten it was ever sky. The ceiling arched overhead like the ribs of something vast and patient, and set into every rib, every pillar, every flagstone of the floor were souls — portioned, portioned, portioned into slivers thin as winter light and laid into the rock like mortar. The walls murmured. They always murmured. It was the sound a library makes when all the books are dreaming, and the Soulkeeper had listened to it for longer than any kingdom above had stood.

Tonight, the murmur changed.

It was a small change. A single thread of sound going taut somewhere in the weave, the way a spider knows a fly has touched the outermost ring not by seeing it but by feeling the whole web lean. The Soulkeeper lifted their head from their hands — hands that looked like hands, that had been made to look like hands, though what moved beneath them was older and hungrier than anything with fingers — and turned their attention toward the mountain's western root.

There. Faint as a candle seen through three closed doors: a fragment of soul, walking. Carried in something small. Followed by something rarer.

The Soulkeeper smiled, and the nearest pillars dimmed, the souls set into them flinching away from the expression like moths from a cold flame.

* * *

"Vessik."

The word was not spoken so much as placed, the way one places a stone on a cairn. It traveled through the court's bones — through the sold souls mortared into its walls, each one shivering the syllable along to the next until it arrived at the ear it was meant for. The hunters did not have quarters in the Undercourt; they had perches. Vessik's was a niche above the Court of Ledgers, where he hung by his heels from a hook of black iron like a coat someone had forgotten to take down, and dreamed his long grey dreams.

He unfolded. It took him some time. He had been folded for eleven years.

"Soulkeeper." His voice was rust and riverbed. "You wake me for a whisper?"

"I wake you for a harvest." The Soulkeeper descended from the dais, and the court made way for them — not the courtiers, who scattered like blown leaves, but the architecture itself, the corridors lengthening, the arches lifting, the very stone leaning back to give its second throne room to pass. "A fragment has crossed the western lowlands. It moves toward us. And it is not alone."

Vessik flexed his fingers, one joint at a time, the way a man counts his blessings. "A thief, then. Some hedge-witch's trinket come home to roost."

"No." The Soulkeeper stopped before him, close enough that Vessik could see himself reflected in the dark of their eyes — a tall grey shape, gaunt as famine, with a hunter's hooked hands. "A fragment does not walk alone. Fragments are carried. Someone loved what we took, or feared what we kept, and they have been running with it clutched to their chest like a coal. And behind the carrier—" the Soulkeeper paused, savoring it, drawing the moment out the way a glutton draws marrow from bone, "—there is a beast-speaker."

Something moved in Vessik's face. It might have been hunger. With the hunters it was difficult to say; they had been remade so thoroughly that their faces were mostly habit.

"A beast-speaker," he repeated. "In this age. I thought the line burned out."

"The line was never burned out. The line was *bought*." The Soulkeeper turned, robes whispering across stone that whispered back, and began to walk, and Vessik fell into step behind them because there was no other direction in which a hunter could move when the second throne walked ahead. "Twenty-three years ago a woman came down the mountain road with her belly full of a gift she could not feed. She knew what we were. She knew what the child would be. And do you know what she did, Vessik? She did not run. Running is for people with nothing to trade. She *negotiated*."

They passed through the Court of Ledgers, where rows of scribes bent over desks of slate and iron, recording in their endless crabbed hands the terms of every sale the court had ever made. The scribes did not look up. Scribes who looked up were scribes who remembered there was an outside, and scribes who remembered there was an outside were scribes who eventually fled to it, and the court had learned to prefer scribes who did not look up at all.

"She sold herself," Vessik said. Not a question. Hunters knew the shape of sales the way dogs knew the shape of meat.

"She sold herself entire," the Soulkeeper said, and there was something almost warm in their voice, which was the most frightening thing about them, that they could make warmth out of ledger-entries. "Her memory of the bargain. Her name in the mouths of the living. Her voice, her face, her place in any story ever told. And in exchange — silence for the child. A valley warded so deep that magic drowned in it like a cat in a sack. Twenty-three years of a small safe life, purchased at the price of everything the mother was." They smiled again, and again the pillars flinched. "I have eaten kingdoms, Vessik. I have unmade bloodlines that lasted a thousand years. And I tell you honestly, I have rarely been paid so beautifully. That woman understood the market better than any queen I have devoured."

"And now the child comes to us."

"Now the child comes to us," the Soulkeeper agreed, "carrying a fragment that has no business being loose, followed by a scent of Old Tongue that my walls tasted three nights ago and have not stopped tasting since. Do you know what that means, Vessik? It means the ward is thinning. It means the payment is coming due. Contracts end, hunter. Even mine. Especially mine." They said the last lightly, but the light in it was the light of a lamp in a room where nothing else burns. "And when a contract ends, the collateral returns to the holder. All of it."

They had reached the antechamber of the thrones now, the long vaulted gallery where petitioners waited to be weighed. Along its walls, in niches cut at the height of a kneeling person, sat the souls too large to mortar into stone — the kings and prophets and stubborn saints the court had taken whole and could not bring itself to portion, because wholeness, the Soulkeeper had found, had a flavor that fragments lost. They knelt in their niches forever, eyes open, mouths moving without sound, praying to gods whose names the court had also purchased. As the Soulkeeper passed, the prayers stumbled. Every mouth in the gallery faltered at once, the way birdsong stops when a hawk's shadow crosses the field.

Vessik noticed. Vessik always noticed. It was why he was first among the hunters.

"They fear you," he said.

"They *remember* me," the Soulkeeper corrected. "Fear is for things that might happen. These know exactly what happens. There is a difference, and the difference is patience." They stopped at the gallery's far end, before a window of polished obsidian that showed not the mountain's heart but the mountain's skin — the world above, rendered in greys, the valleys and rivers and roads laid out like veins under flesh. The Soulkeeper pressed one hand flat against it, and the surface warmed, and deepened, and showed them the west.

There: a thread of light, faint, moving. A fragment of soul traveling in a small body. Beside it, a larger shape, wrapped in silence so dense it registered on the glass as a hole — a woman-shaped absence, a gap in the world's noise, and around that gap, faintly, the tracks of beasts turning to look at her as she passed, the way flowers turn toward a sun they cannot see.

"There," the Soulkeeper said softly. "Do you see her?"

"I see a hole."

"You see an inheritance. Look closer. Look at what walks beside the absence." The Soulkeeper traced the air above the glass, and the image swelled: a woman on a mountain road, mud to the knee, a child's hand in hers, and — yes, unmistakable, delicious — a wolf pacing the ridgeline above them, keeping pace, keeping watch. Not hunting. *Guarding.* "Beasts do not guard their prey, Vessik. Beasts guard their own. That woman speaks, and the wild things answer, and she has spent twenty-three years in a valley built to strangle exactly that. Do you understand what she is carrying besides the fragment?"

Vessik was quiet a moment. Hunters were not made for understanding; they were made for taking. But he had been first among them once, before his long folding, and the old shapes of thought still fit him.

"Her mother's contract," he said slowly. "She carries the proof of it. In her blood. In her gift. If the ward fails—"

"If the ward fails, the debt wakes, and the debt is *mine*, and the woman does not even know she is worth more than the shard she drags behind her." The Soulkeeper drew their hand back from the glass, and the image collapsed into ordinary darkness. "Which is why you will not touch her. Do you hear me, Vessik? You will not spill one drop of her. You will not frighten her into running somewhere my reach cannot follow. You will bring her to me *alive*, *unbroken*, and *willing* — or at least believing she is willing, which in my experience amounts to the same thing and keeps much better."

"And if she refuses?"

The Soulkeeper considered the question the way a jeweler considers a flaw — turning it, finding the setting that would show it best.

"Then she will refuse slowly," they said. "Everyone refuses slowly, given the right pressure. We will begin with the child."

* * *

The hunters gathered in the Molt Hall, where they shed their years. It was a chamber of hanging skins — their own, shed and stored, each one a decade or a century of service stretched on frames of iron — and in that company Vessik felt briefly young again, surrounded by all the shapes he had worn and outworn. Nine hunters. That was what remained of the court's teeth. The rest had gone out over the centuries and not come back, undone by wards, by weather, by the slow erosion of a world that had less and less use for them.

The Soulkeeper stood among the skins and did not need one. That was the difference between the hunters and the throne. The hunters changed shapes because they had nothing underneath. The Soulkeeper had never needed a shape at all.

"You have your quarry," the Soulkeeper said. Their voice filled the hall without effort, and every hanging skin stirred, rustling like a field of dead stalks. "A fragment of soul, carried east toward the mountain. A child, small, quick, speaking the Old Tongue — do not harm the child either; children are leverage, and leverage is not to be bent. And a woman. Astrid Lockridge." The name left the Soulkeeper's mouth like a coin dropped into a well, and the hall swallowed it. "Daughter of the woman who sold herself. Heir to a contract twenty-three years in the keeping. Her inheritance makes her useful to me, and I do not waste useful things."

One of the younger hunters — Maelle, folded only twice, still quick — raised a hand like a bird's foot. "And the ward, Soulkeeper? The valley will stop us as it has always stopped us."

"The valley will stop you," the Soulkeeper agreed pleasantly, "for exactly as long as it takes you to learn patience. You are hunters. Patience is the oldest tool you own. Sit at the edge of that ward like winter sits at the edge of spring. Watch. Wait. The woman will come out. They always come out — the warded ones, the hidden ones. A hiding place is just a trap you build yourself and pay rent on. Sooner or later the rent comes due, and she will crawl out of her valley on her own knees, and you will be there, courteous as undertakers, to receive her."

"And if the ward holds?" Maelle asked. "If she never comes?"

The Soulkeeper let the silence stretch until the youngest hunter began, very slightly, to tremble. Then:

"Then we will remind the world why it fears the dark under mountains. But it will not come to that." They turned toward the obsidian window, where the western thread of light crawled onward, patient, inevitable, a spark being carried through the night toward a pyre that did not yet know it would be lit. "Look at her, hunters. Look at how carefully she moves. How she hides the child, how she feeds the beasts, how she thinks silence is armor. She believes she is escaping." The Soulkeeper's smile was the oldest thing in the mountain, older than the thrones, older than the names of gods. "She is *arriving*. Everything she does from this moment forward, she does on my time, along my road, toward my door. Go. Bring me my inheritance."

The hunters went out through the mountain's roots like a breath drawn through teeth — nine grey shapes unfolding into the dark of the world, patient as winter, certain as debt.

And behind them, in the hall of murmuring walls, the Soulkeeper settled onto the second throne and listened to the sold souls pray, and waited, and was not disappointed, because the Soulkeeper had never once in all the long ages been disappointed. Hope was simply interest accruing. And interest, given time, always came home.

Far above, a woman with a stolen shard and a borrowed wolf crossed the tree line, and did not look back at the mountain she was walking toward.

The mountain, of course, was looking at her.
