## 13. Betrayal at the Waystation

The waystation had once been a shrine.

Astrid could see it in the bones of the place — the way the roofline curved like cupped hands, the way the lintel above the door still bore a carving worn nearly smooth: a river, a mountain, and between them a small figure walking. Pilgrims had built it, centuries ago, when roads were prayers. Now the pilgrims were gone and the Kettlebones used it to store salt pork and stolen candlewax, and the small walking figure had been defaced by some bored outlaw with a knife, its face scratched into anonymity.

Wren traced the carved figure anyway, every morning, with one grimy finger. *Sister*, the child's eyes said, though the mouth stayed silent. Wren had learned that much of Astrid's language in the weeks since the ward: silence could be a question too.

They had been at the waystation four days. Sef said five, no more — long enough for the horses to rest, for Vess's toll-memory to stop bleeding out of Astrid in cold sweats, for Ilex's feet to recover from the descent. The disgraced scribe sat by the fire most hours now, hood up, hands folded, radiating the particular misery of a man who had once owned seven cloaks and now owned one, and it smelled of mule.

Astrid did not trust him. She wanted to say so plainly, but her voice was gone — traded ahead of her, in a sense, promised to a throne beneath a mountain — and so she had learned to speak with her hands, with her eyes, with the shard's faint hum against her sternum where she wore it on a cord. Sef read her well enough. Ilex read her too. That was the trouble. A scribe who had recorded the sale of souls knew how to watch people decide things.

On the fourth evening, Ilex asked her to walk with him to the spring.

She went, because refusing would tell him how much she feared him, and fear was a currency he could spend.

* * *

The spring came up out of the rock in a thread of silver, pooling in a basin the pilgrims had cut, overflowing down the mountainside in a sound like someone whispering continuously and never finishing the sentence. Ilex knelt there and drank from his cupped hands like an animal, and when he looked up, water dripped from his chin and his face was naked in a way she hadn't seen before.

"You should know," he said, "that I have been composing letters in my head."

She tilted her head: *go on.*

"Letters to the court. Petitions. I am very good at petitions — it was half my office, before. *Ilex of the Third Desk, humbly reports.*" He smiled without any joy in it. "Do you know what I recorded, twenty years ago? Sales. Leases. Redemptions. The Undercourt keeps books, Astrid Lockridge, better books than any kingdom above ground, and every soul it holds is a line in a ledger in my handwriting." He wiped his mouth. "Your mother's line was the only one that ever made me sick."

Astrid's hand rose to her throat — the old gesture, the gesture for *voice*, though she had no voice to guard anymore. It had become, instead, the gesture for *mother.*

"They gave me a choice, back then," Ilex said. "Record it or join it. I chose the desk over the pit. I told myself the ink wasn't mine, only the hand holding the pen." He laughed, a dry terrible sound. "Twenty years I've been telling myself that. And then you walked into my exile with a stolen fragment humming on your neck and a child who speaks the Old Tongue, and I thought — here, at last, is the pen being handed back."

He stood. He looked at her with something that might, in a better man, have been love or grief, but in Ilex was only arithmetic wearing grief's coat.

"I'm sorry," he said. "That's not nothing. I want you to know it's not nothing. But they offered me my standing back. My desk. My seven cloaks." His voice thinned. "My *name*, struck back into the rolls. Do you understand what that is worth, to a man who has eaten dirt for two decades? You cannot. You have never been nothing."

Then he moved — faster than she'd credited his soft court body capable of — and caught her wrist, and pressed something into her palm: a wax seal, black, stamped with a spiral like a burrow seen from above.

"A root-seal. There are three on the mountain's lower doors; this opens one. If you get past them — if you get down there—" He swallowed. "Give them the shard. Give them whatever they ask. Don't give them the child. The child is worth more than you know, and I am not certain even the Soulkeeper knows why."

Behind his eyes, then, just for a moment, she saw it: not the traitor, not the scribe. A man drowning who had decided to climb over someone else's shoulders, and hated himself clearly enough to weep while doing it.

"I'm sorry," he said again, and turned, and walked up the trail toward the waystation, toward the hunters he must already have signaled, and Astrid stood at the spring with a stolen seal in her fist and the water whispering its unfinished sentence, and understood that she had perhaps an hour, maybe less.

She ran.

* * *

She reached the waystation as the light died.

Something was wrong before she saw anything wrong. The horses screamed first — not the whinny of alarm but the flat, tearing scream of animals smelling something their blood remembered from before horses were tame. Sef's gray mare broke her picket line and bolted into the dark. One of the Kettlebones' pack ponies followed, and then all of them, thundering away downslope like a river deciding to run backward.

And the wolves — Sef's wolves, the ones Astrid had spoken peace to, the ones who had slept curled around the band's fires like great grey children — the wolves were on the ridgeline, hackles up, facing *away* from the waystation. Facing north. Facing the pass.

Facing what was coming down it.

"Sef!" She couldn't shout it. She never could again. She hammered on the door instead, both fists, and the door swung open on chaos: outlaws grabbing blades and bundles, Sef in the middle of it roaring orders in his gravel voice, Wren pressed against the wall with the salt-pork barrel between the child and everything, clutching a strip of dried meat like a talisman.

Sef saw her and his face did something complicated. "You knew," he said. Not a question. He'd seen her run in. He'd seen the direction she'd come from. "Girl, what did you—"

She shook her head hard enough to hurt. *Not me.* She grabbed his arm and pointed north, at the ridge, at the place where the last grey light showed shapes that should not have been shapes — tall, patient, wrong, like shadows that had learned to stand upright and wait. The soul-hunters. Three of them. Maybe four. They did not hurry. Hurrying was for creatures that could be late.

Sef spat a curse that had teeth in it. "Kettlebones! Scatter pattern — the gully, the scree, the old mine — you know your holes. *Go.*"

The band scattered the way water scatters, the way they'd scattered from a hundred magistrates and bounty-men before this: instantly, professionally, each one a drop with a pre-chosen crack to run down. Astrid grabbed for Wren.

Her fingers closed on cloth — and then the cloth tore, because Wren twisted, because Wren had seen the hunters too and the child's whole body had gone rigid with a terror older than childhood, and in that moment of rigidity the child did the worst possible thing: froze.

Astrid got one arm around Wren, hauled — and the door burst inward off its hinges.

The hunter that came through it did not look like the ones on the ridge. This one had learned a trick: it wore a shape, a man-shape, grey-cloaked and mild, with a face like a page someone had written on and then scraped clean. Its eyes found Astrid first. They stayed on her the way a leech stays.

"The Lockridge inheritance," it said. Its voice was dust and ledger-pages. "Alive. The Keeper's word stands."

Then its gaze slid to Wren, and something in it *leaned*, the way a scale leans.

"No—" Sef lunged, knife out, brave and stupid and magnificent, and the hunter didn't even strike him. It simply *was more present* than he was; the space around it thickened like deep water, and Sef's lunge slowed, and slowed, and stopped, and the hunter stepped past him as if past furniture.

Astrid pulled Wren toward the back wall, toward the rotten shutter she'd noted days ago and meant to widen and never had. Her shoulder hit it. The wood held. She hit it again, and the shard against her sternum shrieked — a hum become a scream, resonating with the hunter's nearness, a beacon finally answering the thing it had been calling all along —

—and the world went white and full of the shard's remembering:

*Iron. A girl with Wren's same stubborn chin, older, fourteen maybe fifteen, standing in a hall of columns made of stacked vertebrae, while robed figures portioned her out — not her flesh, her* self *, ladled away in glowing measure like honey, like light, like the last warm thing in a cold room — and the girl's mouth forming a word, over and over, a word in the Old Tongue that the fragment had been teaching Wren syllable by syllable across all these miles without either of them knowing it—*

Astrid came back to herself on her knees. The shutter hung broken behind her. Cold air poured in.

Wren was gone.

The hunter stood in the middle of the ruined room, and one grey hand rested on the child's shoulder with a gentleness worse than any grip. Wren's eyes found Astrid's over the hunter's arm — wide, wet, furious — and the child did the thing that broke Astrid open: raised one small hand and signed, clumsily, in Astrid's own invented grammar, the sign they'd made together for *find me.*

Then the hunter stepped backward through its own shadow, and took Wren with it, and the room was only a room, and the wind through the broken shutter was only wind.

* * *

Afterward there was silence, and ash, and Sef.

The big outlaw picked himself up off the floor with the slow care of a man checking which parts still answered. Around him, three Kettlebones crept back from the dark, drawn by the absence of danger the way moths are drawn to its opposite. No one spoke for a long time. The fire had died. Nobody relit it.

"It was the scribe," one of them said finally. "Wasn't it. The quill-man."

"He's gone too," another said. "Horse, cloak, everything. Left the mule." A bitter laugh. "Left the mule. Even betrayal has manners."

Sef said nothing. He walked the room like a man reading a battlefield — the torn cloth in Astrid's fist, the broken shutter, the drag-marks that weren't marks at all but a *smoothness* in the dust where a shadow had passed through carrying a child. Then he crouched in front of Astrid, eye level, close enough that she could smell woodsmoke and wolf on him.

"You're going after it," he said. "Down the mountain. Into the court." It wasn't a question either. He'd learned her signs faster than anyone; he'd learned to read her silences like weather. "Alone. With that—" he nodded at the shard, still trembling faintly against her skin, "—and a dead man's seal, if he even left you a true one."

She opened her fist. The black root-seal lay in her palm, spiral-up, catching no light at all.

Sef stared at it for a long moment. When he looked up, something had shifted in his face — not warmth exactly, but the thing underneath warmth, the bedrock of it. "My brother was taken by the court," he said quietly. "Eleven years ago. Sold to them by a magistrate to settle a debt. I became an outlaw because the law called that *payment*." He stood, joints cracking, and raised his voice to the room, to the shadows beyond it, to the wolves on the ridge who had begun, silently, to gather. "Kettlebones! We're done running tonight. Make your choices. The girl goes down the mountain. Anyone coming?"

Astrid's chest clenched. She put her hand on Sef's arm — *no, don't, this is mine, this debt is mine* — but he covered her hand with his own huge one and shook his head slowly.

"You testified for us," he said. "By the beasts' mouths, on the road, every crime the hunters ever did — you spoke it true when no court would hear it. That makes you the only justice left in this valley of mountains, girl. And nobody," his voice dropped to gravel and iron, "nobody robs the Kettlebones' justice and walks away with the coin."

Two of the band stepped forward. Then a third. The rest drifted back into the dark with the particular dignity of people choosing survival, and no one blamed them, least of all Sef, who tipped his chin to each departing shadow like a host seeing guests to the gate.

Outside, the wolves came down off the ridge. Seven of them, then nine. They did not howl. They sat in a half-circle around Astrid in the starlight, yellow-eyed, waiting, and when she reached out with the wordless part of herself — the part that needed no voice, the part that had survived even the trade — the lead bitch rose and pressed her broad skull into Astrid's palm.

*Sister taken. Small-one. Ours-now-too,* the wolf said, in the way wolves say things: scent and pressure and image. *We run.*

Astrid looked north, where the hunters had gone. The pass yawned between two black shoulders of the mountain, and somewhere far below, behind root-doors and bone columns and ledgers written in a traitor's careful hand, Wren was waiting in the dark, signing *find me* into the memory of a woman who could no longer answer aloud.

But she could answer. Oh, she could answer.

She closed her hand around Ilex's seal until the edges bit, turned to the wolves and the outlaws and the indifferent stars, and began to walk.
