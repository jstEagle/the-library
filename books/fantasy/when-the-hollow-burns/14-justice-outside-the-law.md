## 14. Justice Outside the Law

She found them by the smell of blood.

Not fresh — three days old at least, soaked into the pine duff and gone sour with rain — but the crows knew where it pooled, and the crows had been talking to Astrid since dawn in their dry, gossiping way. *Dead men by the black rock. Dead horses. One man not dead but wishing.* They described the place as crows describe everything: as a shape, a shadow, a thing that was once warm. Astrid walked toward it through the thinning trees with the shard wrapped in wool against her breastbone and Wren's absence aching in her arms like a phantom limb.

Three days since the waystation. Three days since Ilex's face had gone smooth and sorry and he'd slipped out into the dark with his stolen seals and his thirty years of court grudges folded up small enough to sell. Three days since the hunters came over the ridge like a spill of ink, took Wren screaming her half-learned words, scattered the Kettlebones into the night.

Astrid had spent those three days learning what it was to grieve while walking. It was a specific kind of grief. It did not let you stop.

The black rock rose out of the pines like a knuckle of the mountain itself, streaked white where birds had done their business for a hundred years. Below it, in a hollow screened by deadfall, she found what remained of Sef's band.

They were worse than broken. Broken implied something whole before. What she saw was six people who had been ground down so long they'd forgotten they were supposed to have edges. A woman with a splinted arm cradling a crossbow she hadn't the strength to span. Two men sharing a single blanket against the cold, sitting upright because lying down meant admitting how hurt they were. A boy no older than sixteen picking apart a boot sole for the nails. And Sef himself, kneeling over a shallow grave, packing earth down with the flat of his axe as if he could apologize to the man beneath it through sheer pressure.

A gray wolf lay at the edge of the camp, ribs showing, one foreleg held stiff and wrong. When Astrid stepped into the open, every weapon in the hollow came up except hers.

"You," Sef said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. He looked at her the way a man looks at weather that has already ruined him once and might do it again. "The beast-talker. You're alive."

"Wren isn't." She said it plainly because there was no kind way to say it. "The hunters took her at the waystation. Ilex sold the route."

"Ilex." Sef spat into the dirt. "I told Dalla he smelled of the court. You can wash a scribe, but you can't wash the ink out." He straightened, and she saw how much it cost him — the knee, the hip, the long gray in his beard that hadn't been there a month ago. "We lost Tam on the ridge road. Lost four others somewhere in the dark, don't know if they ran or were run down. Lost the cache at Miller's Fork. Lost everything we had of worth, which wasn't much, because everything we had of worth was already stolen." He laughed, short and terrible. "So if you've come to beg our help, beast-talker, you've come to the wrong grave."

"I haven't come to beg."

"Then you've come to rob us. Also the wrong grave."

Astrid looked around the hollow. At the splinted arm, the ruined leg, the boy with his fistful of boot-nails. At the wolf, whose amber eyes had never left her face, and who was thinking thoughts too large for its starving body.

"No," she said. "I've come to pay you."

* * *

They didn't believe her at first. She understood why. She was one woman, mud to the knees, carrying nothing visible but a bundle of wool and grief, standing in the wreckage of their lives and promising payment. The world had taught these people that promises flowed downhill, from the strong to the weak, and always landed as weight.

So she didn't promise. She showed them.

"The court's hunters took your people," she said. "Tam. The others. And they've taken hundreds more, for years, all along this range — and no one has ever made them account for it. Not the king's magistrates; the court is above them. Not the temples; the temples are paid. There is nowhere in any law of any land where the Undercourt must answer for what it takes." She unwrapped the shard a hand's width, letting its hum rise into the cold air, and watched them flinch. "But laws only matter where someone keeps the record. And I am a record-keeper. Of a kind no court can burn."

Sef's eyes narrowed. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning I'm going to show you what happened to your people. All of it. Everything the hunters did between the waystation and now — and everything they've done for the years before, if the land remembers it. Beast-spoken. Witnessed." She turned her palm up. "You want justice, Sef. No magistrate will give it to you. So I'm offering you the only version of it left outside the law: the truth, told loud enough that it can't be unheard. And then you can decide what a just man does with it."

The silence in the hollow was total. Even the wind seemed to hold off.

It was the wolf who broke it — limping forward on three legs to press its scarred muzzle against Astrid's hand, whining low, and then turning to look at Sef with an expression no outlaw in that hollow could mistake.

"She means it," Sef translated quietly. He'd grown up around wolves before the Kettlebones, back when he was somebody else entirely. "Gods help me. She means it."

* * *

She gave them Tam first, because he was theirs.

The ravens had followed the hunt from the waystation — ravens follow everything that bleeds — and though they had not seen the taking itself, they had heard it, carried on the wind in the way ravens hear everything. Astrid sat on the black rock with the camp gathered below her, closed her eyes, opened the door she had kept shut for twenty-three years, and let the voices come through her.

She did not speak their words herself. That was the thing none of them understood until it happened: she became the instrument, not the player. Her mouth shaped sounds in a dozen throats at once — raven-croak and fox-laugh and the deep subterranean groan of a bear woken angry — and the words assembled themselves out of borrowed memory into something the listeners understood in their bones even when the language was not quite their own.

*Tam ran east,* said the ravens through her. *Tam ran well. The tall hunter with the iron rings caught him at the stream fork and asked him where the shard went, and Tam spat blood and said nothing, and the hunter smiled because he liked that. They took his boots first. They always take the boots, so no one runs twice.*

Below her, the woman with the splinted arm made a sound like something tearing.

*He sang on the road,* said a fox, quick and bright and merciless. *A marching song, badly. The young hunter told him to stop. He sang louder. They put him in iron at the next waystation and fed him half-rations and asked him questions every day, and every day he answered with the song, until they stopped asking and simply left him in the dark, because the Undercourt does not waste souls it cannot crack. It stores them.*

Astrid opened her eyes. Tears had cut clean lines through the dust on her cheeks without her noticing.

"That's how they work," she said, her own voice again, rough and human. "That's what they are. Not soldiers. Collectors. Every person they take goes into the vault under the mountain, alive, portioned out piece by piece — courage, memory, love — sold to whatever thing below has coin or favor. Your friend is not dead. He is inventory." She let the shard hum against her chest. "And so is the child they took from me. And so, for thirty-one years, was a woman named Maren Lockridge, whose crime was loving a daughter too loudly."

She told them about the mother then. Not all of it — some of it she still couldn't carry — but enough. Enough that the outlaws in the hollow understood what kind of enemy they were looking at, and what kind of woman was asking them to look at it with her.

When she finished, the boy with the boot-nails was crying without shame, and the two men under the blanket had stood up, and the woman with the splinted arm had loaded her crossbow one-handed through sheer fury.

Sef came and stood in front of Astrid. Close. Searching her face the way he'd once searched the horizon for riders.

"When I was nineteen," he said, "the court's tithe-men took my brother for a debt my father owed. Not to the crown — to the Undercourt, private debt, soul-collateral. My father signed papers he couldn't read because the scribe read them aloud wrong on purpose. My brother went under the mountain and my father went into the ground within the year and nobody — *nobody*, beast-talker — ever wrote down that anything wrong had been done. Because the court writes the records, and the court says it did nothing." His jaw worked. "You're telling me you can make the record say otherwise."

"I'm telling you I can make the mountains themselves swear witness," Astrid said. "Every hunter, every theft, every name. Spoken in voices that cannot lie, before witnesses who cannot be bought. The court owns the law. It doesn't own the truth. Nothing owns the truth — it just needs someone willing to carry it."

"And after?" Sef asked. "After the truth is spoken. Then what?"

Astrid looked north, toward the massif that held the Undercourt in its roots, its peak white against a bruising sky. The shard pulsed against her sternum like a second heart, and beneath the pulse she felt something else now — fainter, farther — the tug of a child's terror, threaded through the fragment the way thread runs through cloth. Wren was alive. Wren was afraid. Wren was waiting.

"After," Astrid said, "we go down through the root-doors and take them back."

* * *

They worked through the night, and by morning it no longer looked like a camp of survivors. It looked like a war band, which is what the Kettlebones had always secretly wanted to be and had never had a reason for that would survive daylight.

Sef sent the boy — Pip, his name was, and he could move through forest like smoke — running the ridge lines to find the four scattered others, carrying tokens and a message: *The beast-talker pays in truth. Come see.* He set the woman, Dalla, to restringing every bow in the hollow while her arm healed, because fury was a fine substitute for strength. He dug up the emergency cache he'd sworn he'd never reveal, and out of it came blades, rope, lamp-oil, and a rolled oilskin map of the mountain's southern flank marked in faded red with things that were not roads.

"What are those?" Astrid asked, touching the red marks.

"Root-doors." Sef's grin had teeth in it. "The old ways in. The court sealed them a century ago, but seals are just locks, and locks are just opinions. Ilex's seals are gone with Ilex — traitorous little inkpot — but the doors themselves remember other keys. There's a stonemason in the freeholds owes me a life. Stone speaks to stone. And you—" he tapped the wool-wrapped shard, gently, the way one taps a sleeping animal, "—you talk to everything."

"Not stone," Astrid said. "Not yet. But the mountain is full of things that burrow, and burrowing things know every crack in the world."

The wolf — Sef called her Greyla, and she had decided, with the absolute authority of wolves, that Astrid belonged to her now — slept across Astrid's feet that night, her ruined leg bound and splinted, dreaming hard dreams that leaked into Astrid's sleep like embers. In the dreams there was a mountain with its roots torn open and light pouring out of the wound, and a great many small frightened souls rising out of it like sparks off a bonfire, and somewhere among them two small hands pressed against iron bars, and a voice — a new voice, a child's voice, speaking words in the Old Tongue that Astrid almost, *almost* understood —

She woke before dawn with the shard singing against her breastbone, louder than it had ever sung, and Greyla standing rigid at the edge of the hollow with her hackles up, staring north.

Through the gray light, Astrid felt it too: the hunters, far away, moving fast. Moving *down*. Not retreating.

Coming to meet them.

"They know," she said to Sef, who had risen silently beside her, axe already in hand. "The Soulkeeper knows exactly where we are and what we mean to do. We're not sneaking anywhere anymore."

Sef considered this. Rolled his shoulders. Around the hollow, one by one, the Kettlebones woke and reached for their weapons, and not one of them asked what was happening, because they had already chosen, in the night, in the space between Tam's story and the sunrise, and choices like that don't get unmade.

"Good," Sef said. "Then they'll see us coming."
