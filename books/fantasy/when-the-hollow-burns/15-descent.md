## 15. Descent

The mountain did not have a door. That was the first thing the outlaws taught me, crouched in the black pine shadow at its northern root while the wind came down off the snowfields smelling of iron and old frost.

"Everything above ground is court," Sef said, tapping the rock with the toe of her boot as if knocking on a neighbor's wall. "Cliffs, scree, the caves the shepherds use — all of it surface, all of it watched. The Undercourt doesn't want to be found by anything that walks in daylight. So it built downward." She turned her face to me. In the starlight her scars looked like riverbeds. "You go in through the roots."

I put my palm against the stone. Even through the leather of my glove I could feel it — not cold exactly, but *hungry*, the way a well-shaft is hungry, the way a silence between two spoken sentences is hungry. The beasts that clung to the mountainside — the marmots in their warrens, the ravens hunched in the pines, a pale fox watching us from the treeline with eyes like struck flint — none of them would come near this place. When I reached for them with the small quiet part of me that had always been able to listen, they flinched away like hands pulled from a fire.

*Dead ground,* the fox had said to me that morning, the only creature willing to speak of it at all. *Below there, everything is taken. Nothing wild lives under the mountain. Nothing wild would want to.*

Wren stood pressed against my side, one small fist knotted in my coat. The shard hung at their throat now, wrapped in three layers of wool and one of Sef's own charm-braided rope, and still I could feel it through all of it — a hum, a wasp-in-a-jar thrumming, growing louder the closer we came to the thing it had been stolen from. Wren had not slept properly in two days. Neither had I. But the child's eyes were clear and fixed on the rock face with an expression I had learned to fear and admire in equal measure: recognition.

"They know we're here," Wren said. Their Common Tongue was better now, though the words still came out shaped strangely, rounded at the edges like river stones. "The walls are listening. They've been listening since the river."

"I know," I said.

"Do the walls talk to you too?"

I looked down at them. In the dark, in the cold, with soot smeared on their cheeks from the fireless camp, they looked impossibly young and impossibly old at once, the way children do when the world has asked too much of them too soon. "No," I said. "But I think they're about to."

Sef's people moved along the root of the mountain like water finding cracks. There were nine of them left after the waystation — nine, plus Sef, plus a scarred grey wolf called Brackle who had followed Astrid Lockridge into hell without being asked, because I had once spoken peace into his heart and wolves keep their accounts longer than men do. They carried no torches. They carried crowbars, drills, sledgehammers wrapped in sheepskin to muffle their strike, and — most precious of all — four iron seals lifted from Ilex's satchel the night before he sold us.

I had not wanted to take them. Stealing from a traitor felt like picking his pockets on the gallows; it made us no cleaner than him. But Sef had only shrugged, turning the seals over in her scarred fingers.

"He bought his way back into the court with our names," she said. "We'll buy our way into the court with his. Seems fair trade to me. The Kettlebones believe in fair trade." And then, quieter, almost gentle: "He was always going to run, Astrid. Men like him don't betray on purpose. They betray the way rivers flow downhill. It's just what they're for."

She was right, and knowing she was right did not make the hurt smaller. It only made it older, somehow, as if I had been carrying it since before Ilex ever joined us on the road.

* * *

The root-doors were not doors until you knew where to look. Then they were everywhere.

Sef found the first seam an hour past midnight, running her bare hands over the frost-glazed stone inch by inch, reading it the way I read the moods of animals — by touch, by patience, by long acquaintance with things that cannot speak plainly. Here the rock was too smooth. There, a line as fine as a hair, running vertical for six feet and vanishing into the earth. A third seam crossed it. Together they made the shape of a doorway that had spent centuries pretending to be a cliff.

"The seals," Sef said, and her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman standing at the edge of something very deep. "Everyone back. If this goes wrong, it goes wrong fast."

We pressed ourselves against the opposite scree. She set the first seal into the seam and it sank into the stone like a coin into soft bread, and the mountain *noticed*. I felt it the way you feel someone enter the room you sleep in — a change in the air, a held breath. The shard against Wren's chest surged up from a hum to a whine, and Wren clamped both hands over it and made a sound low in their throat, an Old Tongue syllable that meant *quiet, be quiet*, and to my astonishment the shard obeyed, dropping back to a tremble.

The second seal went in lower. The third. With each one the seams began to glow, a thin sickly light the color of pus or pale honey, tracing the outline of a door tall enough for a giant and narrow as a coffin lid. Frost crackled outward from it in fern-patterns. Behind us, on the slope, every raven on the mountain took wing at once, a black rag torn across the stars.

"Fourth seal," Sef breathed. "Last chance to walk away."

Nobody walked away. Brackle sat down at my left side, deliberate as a judge, and leaned his warm weight against my leg.

Sef slammed the fourth seal home with the heel of her hand.

The door opened the way a wound opens. No grinding of stone, no roar — just a soft wet sigh of pressure equalizing, and the seam parted, and beyond it was darkness with a texture to it, a darkness that had weight and temperature and, I realized as the first breath of it rolled over us, a *smell*: cold candle-tallow, dried lavender gone musty, and underneath both, faint as a heartbeat, the smell of the inside of a locked room where someone has lived alone a very long time.

"Well," Sef said. "After you, beast-speaker. You're the one it wants."

"That's what worries me," I said, and stepped through.

* * *

The antechambers began thirty paces in, and I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe them.

The passage widened into halls, and the halls were wrong in a way my mind kept sliding off of, like a word repeated until it loses meaning. The proportions were almost human — doors at shoulder height, steps cut for walking feet — but everything was a half-measure off, a finger's width too narrow, a tread a hair too shallow, so that walking through them felt like wearing someone else's clothes. The walls were dark stone veined with something paler, and the pale veins were not mineral. I touched one, early on, before I understood. It was warm. It moved, very slightly, under my fingertip, the way a sleeper moves when you brush a strand of hair from their face.

And they murmured.

That was the thing. That was the thing I could not stop hearing once I heard it. The walls were full of voices — hundreds of them, thousands, layered and overlapping and worn down to a whisper, like wind through a reed bed, like a congregation praying far away through thick stone. Not words, mostly. Occasionally a word would surface whole: a name, once. *Maribel.* Once, a number, counted in a child's voice, reaching forty-seven and starting over. Once, close to my ear as I passed, unmistakable, a woman saying *it's cold, why is it always so cold in here*, in the flat resigned tone of someone who has said it every day for a hundred years.

Sold souls. Ilex had told us, in those last days when I still believed him: *the court doesn't destroy what it buys, it stores it. Souls are currency, and currency sits in vaults, and the vault walls are the cheapest mortar they have.* He had laughed when he said it, that brittle laugh of a man who has made his peace with horrors the way other men make peace with bad weather.

Wren pressed so close to me that we were nearly one body moving through the dark. Their hand found mine and gripped it, small and fierce.

"They're in the walls," Wren whispered. "All of them?"

"All of them," I said. "And more below."

"Including—" They didn't finish. They didn't have to. Somewhere beneath our feet, behind some door we had not yet reached, was a girl whose soul had been portioned out piece by piece until what remained of her was barely enough to hold a shape, and the fragment burning against Wren's chest was hers, carried a hundred miles by a child who could not yet say goodbye but had already learned to say *find her.*

"We'll get her back," I said. I had said it before, on the road, by the river, in the ashes of the waystation. Each time it cost more to say. This time it came out sounding like something I might actually owe.

The outlaws fanned out behind us with the practiced economy of people who had raided toll-houses and tax-wagons and knew how to move quiet in hostile country. But I watched Sef's face as the murmur of the walls rose around her, watched the color leave her knuckles where she gripped her axe, and I understood that even outlaws — especially outlaws, who lived by taking — had a horror of this particular theft. To steal a purse is one sin. To build your house out of stolen souls is another order of abomination entirely, and everyone who has ever traded with the Undercourt knows, in the marrow-deep place where they keep such knowledge, that someday the bill comes due.

"Which way?" Sef asked me.

I closed my eyes. The shard answered for me — it strained forward like a hound at the end of its rope, humming harder, tugging at Wren's neck, and Wren simply began to walk, sure-footed, unerring, a compass needle in child's form. We followed the child. What else had I done, since the night they knocked at my door, but follow?

Down. Always down. The halls gave onto galleries lined with alcoves, and in each alcove stood a jar, and I did not let myself look closely at the jars. Stairways spiraled around shafts where pale light rose from below like heat from a forge, except it wasn't heat, it was the opposite of heat, it was light with all the warmth boiled out of it. Twice we passed doors banded in iron, and from behind them the murmur swelled briefly, hopefully, into something almost like speech, almost like *help*, and then subsided as we went by, and each time it subsided I felt it like a hook dragged through my chest.

Once, Wren stopped at such a door and put their palm flat against it and said something in the Old Tongue, softly, and the murmuring behind the iron went silent — not frightened, I thought. Listening. Memorizing. Whatever the child had said, the souls in that room would carry it with them, wherever souls carry things.

"What did you tell them?" I asked.

Wren looked up at me. In the pale light their eyes seemed lit from inside. "That we came for everyone," they said. "Not just Lark." Their sister's name, said plainly, at last. "The shard says the words work on all the locks the same. One lock is all locks, if the lock is right." They frowned, wrestling the concept through a language still new to them. "Ilex taught me that. Before."

Before he sold us. Even betrayal, apparently, leaves gifts behind, like a fever that burns out and leaves you somehow stronger. I filed the thought away. It would keep.

* * *

An hour down, or three — time moved strangely in the antechambers, stretched thin like tallow — we came to the last gallery, and the gallery ended at the antechamber proper, and there I finally stopped walking, because my legs would not carry me further without my permission, and I could not give it.

The chamber ahead was vast and round and pale-lit, its ceiling lost in gloom, and it was *occupied*. Figures stood in ranks along its walls — tall, slender, gray as ash-paper, robed in colors that hurt to look at directly because they were not colors so much as the memory of colors, faded from some spectrum the surface world had never been given. Courtiers. They did not move. They did not need to; their attention moved for them, and all of it, the attention of every figure in that room, was already turned toward us, toward the corridor mouth, toward the shard singing at a child's throat. They had known we were coming since the river. Perhaps since the valley. Perhaps since before I was born.

Beyond them, at the chamber's far end, rose the thrones.

I could not see them clearly. I want to say that plainly, because everything else in this account I can describe, and the thrones I cannot — only that there were two, and that looking at them was like looking at the place where a person should be and finding instead the shape they leave in a crowd, the negative space, and that the space was aware of me.

"Astrid." Sef's hand on my shoulder. Her voice very low. "Say the word and we take this room or die loud. Nine blades, a wolf, and whatever you can call. That's the offer."

I looked at her — at this woman who had met me as prey on a mountain road and now offered to charge a throne-room on my say-so — and I felt something in my chest crack open a little wider, grief and gratitude fused into one hot seam.

"No," I said. "If you fight them here, your people die in the walls with the rest of the souls, and Wren's sister stays sold, and the court adds nine more currencies to its ledger. This isn't a battle, Sef. It's a bargain. It was always going to be a bargain." I swallowed. My throat ached, though I could not have said what for. "Hold the stairs. Hold the way open. However it ends in there — however it *ends* — I need a road out."

"And if there isn't one?"

"There will be," said Wren, before I could answer. They stepped forward, chin up, the shard blazing through its wrappings now, light leaking through wool like sun through cloud. "The mountain doesn't like them either. Ask it later."

Sef stared at the child for a long moment. Then she laughed, short and rough, the laugh of a woman conceding a point to fate. "Gods preserve me from prophets in miniature," she muttered, and turned, and signed to her people, and they melted back along the gallery to hold the stairs, all but Brackle, who lay down across the threshold of the great chamber like a wolf guarding a den, and would not be moved.

I took Wren's hand. Or Wren took mine. Afterward neither of us could say which.

Together we walked out of the antechamber's shadows, into the pale light, toward the thrones — and as we crossed that vast floor, every murmuring soul in every wall of the Undercourt fell silent at once, thousands of voices hushing like a held breath, waiting to hear what the daughter of Elsbeth Lockridge had come to say.

And the nearer throne began, very slowly, to empty its darkness into a shape.
