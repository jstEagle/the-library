## 16. The Iron Court

The antechambers ended where the walls began to breathe.

Astrid felt it before she saw it — a change in the air, the way a flock feels the hawk's shadow cross the field. The murmuring in the stone, which had followed her down through seven descending halls like water finding its level, went quiet all at once. Not silent. *Attentive.* A thousand sold souls pressing close against the inside of the mountain's skin, listening to what she would say.

Wren was somewhere above her, or below her — the vaults, Ilex had said, past the throne room, behind doors that only answered to the Soulkeeper's voice. Astrid carried that knowledge the way she carried the shard: close to the bone, where it could not be taken without taking her apart.

The last door was iron, and it opened for her without being touched.

Beyond it, the court unfolded like something grown rather than built. Pillars of black basalt rose into a dark so complete it seemed less an absence of light than a substance, a velvet weight pressing down. The floor was polished obsidian, and in it Astrid saw reflections that did not match the room — faces looking up at her from beneath the stone, mouths moving without sound. Souls set into the floor like cobblestones. She made herself walk on them. She felt each one flinch.

*They know why you're here,* she told herself. *Walk gently anyway.*

Two thrones stood at the far end of the hall, and they were not alike.

The first was empty — a chair of woven shadow and old gold, draped in chains that hung slack as sleeping serpents. The Underking's seat, Ilex had whispered. Vacant these hundred years, and no one dared say why. Power pooled around it anyway, patient as sediment.

The second throne was occupied.

The Soulkeeper did not look up when Astrid entered. It — she? the shape suggested a woman the way a suggestion of smoke suggests fire — sat with one hand resting on the arm of her throne, fingers long and grey as candle-wax, and in those fingers she turned a small object over and over. A ring. A plain iron band, worn smooth.

Astrid knew it. She had seen it once, in a vision the shard had shown her on the outlaw road: a woman's hand, a wedding band pried loose, a ledger line written in ink that gleamed like oil.

Her mother's ring.

Something in Astrid's chest went very still, the way a rabbit goes still under the hawk's shadow. Not dead. *Waiting.* Every beast she had ever spoken to had taught her this: stillness is not surrender. Stillness is the moment before the run.

"You are the Lockridge daughter." The Soulkeeper's voice was soft, unhurried, the voice of someone who had never once needed to raise it because everything in this place already belonged to her. "Twenty-three years in your valley. Twenty-three years of silence bought and paid for." She looked up then, and her eyes were the grey of ash after the fire has passed through. "You have your mother's chin. She bargained with that same stubborn jaw."

"I've come to settle accounts."

"Have you." The Soulkeeper rose. Her gown moved like poured ink, and where it dragged across the souls in the floor, they whimpered — Astrid heard it, faint as mice behind a wall, and her hands curled into fists. "Accounts. Yes. Your mother understood accounts. Do you know what she purchased with hers, little beast-speaker? Not merely your silence. The ward itself. Every year of your hidden life was drawn from her, coin by coin by coin. She paid until there was nothing left to pay, and then—" the grey eyes glittered, "—she paid with what remained. You are standing in the interest."

Astrid's throat tightened. She had known this — Ilex had told her, had laid out the ledger lines in his careful scribe's hand — but knowing and hearing were different animals. Knowing was a map. Hearing was the country itself, vast and cold.

She made herself uncurl her fists. She made herself speak evenly, the way she spoke to a wounded animal that might bolt or bite.

"Then you'll want what I've brought."

She drew out the shard.

It came away from her breast pocket humming, that trapped-wasp whine she had lived with since the night Wren appeared mud-caked at her door. In the court's darkness it glowed faintly, a sliver of light with a soul folded inside it — Wren's sister, portioned and parcelled and held. Around them, the walls stirred. The murmuring rose again, and this time Astrid could hear words in it, or almost: *light, light, light,* repeated like a prayer worn smooth by centuries.

The Soulkeeper's fingers stopped turning the ring.

For the first time, something moved behind the ash-grey eyes. Hunger, perhaps. Or recognition. To a soul-hunter, a fragment outside the vaults was not merchandise. It was a wound in the world. It was proof that what was taken could be carried away.

"That does not belong to you," the Soulkeeper said.

"No," Astrid agreed. "It belongs to a girl who was sold. And I'm returning it — on terms."

"Terms." The word left the Soulkeeper's mouth like something distasteful spat onto a clean floor. She descended the three steps from her throne, and the temperature of the hall seemed to drop with her, degree by degree, frost creeping through the air the way dusk creeps across a valley. "Nothing here is bought on terms, Lockridge. Everything here is *owed*. Your mother learned that. Would you like to hear her final words? I keep them. I keep everything." She smiled, and it was the smile of a ledger closing. "*Protect her. However you must. Take whatever of me is required.*" The Soulkeeper tilted her head. "She wept, at the end. They usually do. But she never once asked me to stop."

Astrid breathed through the pain. In, and out, slow, the rhythm she used on foaling mares. *Grief later. Bargain now.*

"Then you know she'd have hated this," Astrid said quietly. "Every soul in these walls. She paid everything she had so that I would never belong to you. And you've filled a mountain with people who do." She lifted the shard higher, and its glow caught the Soulkeeper's face, carving the hollows into sharp relief. "So here are my terms. The fragment — returned whole, the girl's soul restored to her body, which I understand you also hold. My mother's debt — marked paid, closed, every coin of it, so that nothing of mine is ever again drawn against her account. And in exchange—"

She stopped.

Here it was. The thing she had rehearsed on the road, in Sef's camp, in the dark hours before dawn when Wren slept against her side. She had said the words aloud to herself until they wore smooth. But saying them here, in this breathing dark, in front of the thing that had consumed her mother — saying them made them real in a way rehearsal never was.

"In exchange," Astrid said, "you may take my voice."

The hall went utterly silent. Even the souls in the walls stopped murmuring.

"The gift dies with the voice," Astrid went on, and her own words sounded far away to her, as if someone else were speaking them. "No voice, no speech to beasts. No speech to beasts, no inheritance worth hunting. You want the Lockridge line quiet? Here it is, quiet forever. One payment instead of a lifetime of hunting me across the world. You're a creature of ledgers. Weigh it."

The Soulkeeper regarded her for a long moment. Somewhere high above, water dripped in the dark, each drop landing with the patience of geology.

"You would give up the beasts," the Soulkeeper said at last. There was something almost gentle in it, which was worse than cruelty. "The ewes and the ravens and the river-foxes. The only conversation you have ever truly loved. You would sit in your valley deaf to all of it, for two children you have known a season."

"Yes."

"Why?"

And Astrid, who had lied well to Brammel and bargained hard with Sef and kept her truth locked behind her teeth for twenty-three years, found that the answer came easily, because it was the one true thing she had never had to hide.

"Because someone should have done it for me."

The Soulkeeper studied her. Then, slowly, she extended one grey hand — not for the shard, but palm-up, open, an invitation.

"Your voice, then. Freely given, freely taken. That is the oldest law, older than my court, older than the mountain. Speak the offering, and it is binding. Speak it, and the child and her sister walk out of my vaults, and the Lockridge account closes forever."

Astrid stepped forward. The shard hummed against her palm. She could feel Wren's sister inside it, feel the small bright pressure of her, and beneath that, faintly, the shard's other whisper — the Old Tongue words it had been teaching Wren all along, the ones Astrid couldn't read but the child could. *Unbinding words,* Ilex had called them, pale with fear. *Why would a soul-shard teach anyone to unbind—*

She had decided not to think about that. She thought about it now, one heartbeat's worth, and let it go. Whatever Wren might do, Wren was behind iron doors. This was the bargain. This was the price. Her mother had taught her how to pay it without flinching, though neither of them had ever spoken of it.

Astrid opened her mouth.

And the Soulkeeper's hand closed around her wrist.

The touch was cold as well-water, cold as the underside of stones, and it did not grip so much as *claim*, the way a brand claims hide. The grey eyes were suddenly very close, and very bright, and all the gentleness had drained out of them like water out of a cracked jar.

"Freely given," the Soulkeeper murmured. "Yes. But I find, Lockridge daughter, that I am owed more than a voice."

The cold spread up Astrid's arm. Not pain — something older than pain. Inventory. She could feel herself being *appraised*, itemized, entered into columns: bone, blood, breath, the beast-gift coiled in her chest like a second heart.

"Your mother's debt," the Soulkeeper said softly, "was not paid in full. She gave her soul, yes — but interest accrued. Twenty-three years of ward-light, drawn daily. You stand here in arrears, child, whether you knew it or not. A voice settles the principal." The grip tightened. "The inheritance settles the rest."

"What inheritance?" Astrid's voice came out steady. She was proud of that, later. "I have nothing. I'm a shepherd."

"You have *everything*." For the first time, the Soulkeeper raised her voice, and the whole mountain heard it; the walls groaned, the souls in the floor cried out, the empty throne's chains rattled like teeth. "Do you think the gift is a trick? A knack? It is a *line*, Lockridge. An unbroken current running back further than your valley, further than your warden's precious law. Your mother had it. Her mother had it. Back and back to the first woman who spoke to the first wolf beside the first fire. And currents do not die — they *pool*. They pool in the last one standing." The ash-grey eyes burned. "You are the last one standing. Your voice is a cupful. Your inheritance is the *river*."

Astrid pulled against the grip and could not move. The shard screamed in her hand — a thin, furious note, and all around them the walls began to answer it, the murmuring rising toward something that was almost a roar.

"And here is the beauty of it," the Soulkeeper continued, conversational again, terrible again, "the elegance the old law provides. You offered freely. I accepted. But the law says the *offering* binds — and an offering made by an heir binds the heir's whole estate. You stood in my hall and pledged yourself to settle accounts. Very well." She smiled, and it was her mother's ring glinting, it was the ledger opening, it was twenty-three years of safety revealed as a leash. "All accounts. Everything. The voice, the gift, the line entire — surrendered to the Undercourt, now and forever, heirless and ending with you."

The cold reached Astrid's shoulder. Her chest. The place where the gift lived, that second heart that had been her curse and her secret and, she understood now, her mother's final gift — began to dim like a lamp running out of oil.

Through the roaring in her ears, through the frost climbing her throat, Astrid heard it: far away, beyond iron doors, a small voice crying out in the Old Tongue — Wren's voice, high and clear and *angry*, and the shard in her hand blazing now like a splinter of sunrise, and the words pouring out of the child were words Astrid did not know but the mountain did, the walls did, every sold soul pressed against the stone did—

The Soulkeeper's head snapped toward the sound.

For one instant — one heartbeat, one drip of water in the dark — her grip loosened.

Astrid ran.
