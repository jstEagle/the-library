## 17. The Voice in the Walls

The vault did not have a door that Wren could see.

That was the worst part, or one of the worst parts. In the stories the shard had shown — and the shard had shown so many, spilling them into Wren's sleep like water into a cup — doors had hinges, doors had locks, doors could be kicked. This place had only walls, and the walls were made of murmuring.

Wren sat with their back against the coldest wall and their knees pulled up and counted the voices.

Not out loud. Out loud was dangerous here. The vault drank sound the way dry earth drank rain; every word a person spoke sank in and did not come back, and Wren had learned on the first day — hour? year? time moved strangely when there was no window — that the walls *kept* what they drank. Somewhere in this stone was every plea ever spoken by every soul the Undercourt had bought. Somewhere in this stone, maybe, was a piece of Mama's voice, from before the iron men took her away to be portioned. Wren listened for it sometimes. They had not found it yet. But they kept listening, because stopping would mean admitting it might not be there at all.

*One,* Wren counted. A man's voice, deep and cracked, saying a name over and over — *Maret, Maret, Maret* — like a prayer worn smooth as a river stone. *Two.* A woman singing half a lullaby, the other half sold off somewhere, so the song just stopped in the middle and started again, stopped and started, forever. *Three.* A child, younger than Wren, who only said *please*, over and over, in six different languages, and then in no language at all.

Wren knew all of them now. That was the trap inside the trap: the vault was lonely enough that even its prisoners' ghosts became company.

And then there was the shard.

Or what was left of it. The iron men had taken the big piece when they took Wren — pried it out of their fists finger by finger, and Wren had bitten one of them hard enough to leave a mark, which had earned Wren a shake and a hiss and nothing else, because beasts like these did not bruise where you could see. But the shard had been generous the way rivers are generous: it had scattered itself. And one small splinter, no bigger than a fingernail, had stayed behind — tucked in the seam of Wren's boot, against the ankle bone, warm as a sleeping bird.

It hummed. It had always hummed, since the night it came to Wren's village wrapped in its stolen light, since the night before the iron men came for Senna. But down here, in the dark, in the drinking-quiet, Wren could finally hear what the hum was *saying.*

Words. Old ones. Older than the valley, older than the ward Astrid carried around her like a coat, older maybe than the mountain itself. The shard had been teaching them all along — Wren understood that now, understood it the way you understand a dream only after waking. Every night by the fire while Astrid slept with one hand near her knife, every morning on the outlaw road while Sef argued about tolls, the shard had been humming syllables into Wren's skin, patient as rain filling a jar. *This word. Then this one. Then this.*

Wren hadn't known why. Words were just words. But now, sitting in a vault full of sold souls, with the cold seeping through their boots and the lullaby-woman starting her song again three stones to the left, Wren pressed their thumb against the splinter through the leather and let the words arrange themselves, one by one, like beads on a string.

They were unbinding words. Wren was almost sure. They had the shape of unbinding — the way they ended open instead of closed, the way they asked rather than commanded. Binding words slammed. These words *opened*, the way Astrid opened her hand to show a frightened animal there was nothing in it.

*Astrid.*

Wren's chest did the thing it did whenever they thought the name, a hot ache right under the ribs, like swallowing a coal that didn't burn. Astrid, who talked to animals with her whole face. Astrid, whose hands smelled of lanolin and woodsmoke. Astrid, who had crossed the whole world's edge for a mud-caked child she'd known for four days, because that was the kind of person she was underneath all the careful quiet.

The iron men had taken Wren at the waystation. They had not taken Astrid. Wren had screamed her name until the hunters clamped a cold hand over their mouth, and the last thing Wren saw was Astrid running, *running*, too slow, always too slow, the shard's light streaming between her fingers like water she couldn't hold —

She would come. Wren knew that the way they knew the sun would come up, the way they knew hunger. But knowing didn't stop the dark from being dark.

Wren put their forehead against the murmuring wall and began, silently, to practice.

* * *

Time in the vault was measured in meals, and meals came when a slot in the floor — a slot, everything here was wrong, even the doors were in the floor — slid open and a bowl slid through, gray porridge, gray spoon, gray light from nowhere. Wren ate because Senna would have told them to eat. Senna, who had gone hungry herself for a whole winter once so Wren wouldn't have to. Senna, whose soul was portioned out somewhere above or below or inside these walls, sold by weight like flour.

On the fourth meal — or the fifth; Wren lost count on purpose, out of spite — something changed.

The voices in the walls went quiet.

All of them. At once. The man saying *Maret*. The woman's broken lullaby. The little one saying *please*. Every murmur in every stone stopped together, the way birds go silent before a storm, and the silence that replaced them was worse than any noise, because it was a *listening* silence, a held-breath silence, and Wren felt the hair rise on their arms the way it did when wolves circled the outlaw camp at night.

Then, far away — above, Wren thought, or deeper, it was impossible to tell direction in a place built by people who put doors in floors — a voice began to speak.

It was not a loud voice. It was not a grand voice. It was a tired, steady, careful voice, the voice of someone who has spent twenty-three years saying very little and means every word, and it was speaking the language of the valley, the plain warm language of sheep-pens and kitchen fires, and it was saying:

"—I know what I'm offering. Take it or don't. But take the child out of your walls first."

Wren was on their feet before they knew they'd stood up, palms flat against the stone, heart slamming.

Astrid. *Astrid.* Here, in the Undercourt, in the iron dark, alive and bargaining — bargaining for *them*, for Wren, offering who-knew-what to who-knew-whom, because of course she had come, of course she had, and some small starved thing inside Wren that had been crouched in a corner since the waystation finally stood up and unfurled.

But the walls were still listening. And the walls, Wren realized with a chill that had nothing to do with cold, were *drinking* again. Drinking Astrid's voice. Keeping it. Adding it to the collection of *Maret* and half-lullabies and *please*.

No. No, that wasn't — they couldn't have her. She wasn't sold. She was giving, there was a difference, there had to be a difference, except the vault didn't care about differences, the vault just drank —

And then Astrid spoke again, and this time it was not the valley tongue.

It was the Old Tongue.

Wren's whole body went still. Because the words were wrong for the moment — they weren't bargain words, weren't pleading words — and they were wrong for Astrid, who didn't know the Old Tongue, couldn't know it, and yet there they were, falling out of her mouth one after another, accented strangely, shaped carefully, like someone reciting a lesson learned by heart without understanding it.

And Wren *knew* them.

Every single one.

*This word. Then this one. Then this.*

The shard's lesson. The bead-string of syllables the splinter had been humming into Wren's ankle for weeks, patient as rain. Astrid was speaking the unbinding — she must have touched the great fragment, must have let it pour itself into her the way it poured into Wren, and now it was using her voice, her tired brave careful voice, to say the opening words into the drinking dark of the soul-vault itself.

The walls shivered.

Wren felt it through their palms — a tremor like the first thaw, like ice groaning on the river before it breaks. All around, in every stone, the drunk voices stirred. The man saying *Maret* faltered mid-name. The lullaby-woman's song caught, hung, and — for the first time since Wren had arrived — continued past the place where it always broke, two more notes, three, trembling, astonished at themselves.

The words were working. The words were *opening*. Not the vault — not yet, not all at once, the court's iron was thick and old — but the souls inside it. Something in each of them was turning toward the sound like flowers toward a window.

Wren pulled the splinter from their boot.

It glowed faintly in the gray light, a sliver of the same stolen light the iron men had carried away, and it was warm, warmer than it had ever been, warm as if it knew. As if it had been waiting down here all along, buried in a child's boot, doing exactly what shards do: remembering. Waiting for the moment the last word of the lesson would arrive from outside.

Wren looked at it. Looked at the wall. Thought of Senna, portioned and weighed. Thought of Mama, taken before that. Thought of the little voice saying *please* in six languages, and how nobody had answered, and how somebody was going to answer *now*.

Astrid's voice was still going, further along the string of words, and the walls were drinking it, and Wren understood suddenly — with the terrible bright clarity children have, the clarity adults pay for with their caution — that this was the plan. Or part of it. Or maybe there was no plan and the shard was simply doing what it had been trying to do since the beginning, and everyone — Astrid, the Soulkeeper, the whole rotten iron court — was just the weather around it.

The binding needed to be spoken *inside*. That was the shape of it. The words ended open, and an ending needs to land somewhere. Astrid's voice was landing in the walls, soaking in with all the rest — but a voice given freely is not the same as a voice spoken freely, and the court was taking hers, *drinking* hers, and a drunk word is a caged word, and caged words can't open cages.

Someone had to say it here. Inside. Free.

Someone small enough to be overlooked. Someone the iron men had shaken and hissed at and dismissed, because what threat was a mud-caked child?

Wren pressed the splinter against the wall, right beside their palm, and felt it pulse like a second heartbeat.

"Okay," Wren whispered, in the valley tongue, the plain warm tongue of sheep-pens and kitchen fires. "I'm listening. Teach me the end."

The shard flared.

And the last words came — not hummed this time but *given*, poured straight into Wren's mind whole and shining, and they were beautiful the way a key is beautiful, the way an open door is beautiful, and Wren's lips began to move around them, shaping sounds older than the mountain, while far above or below, Astrid's voice spoke on into the drinking dark, word after word after word, and the walls of the Undercourt — for the first time in an age of ages — began, very softly, to sing back.

* * *

They came for Wren before the lesson was finished.

The floor-slot opened — not for porridge — and cold hands closed around Wren's arms, lifting, and Wren did what Astrid had taught them without ever meaning to: went loose, went quiet, went *small*, the way a rabbit goes when the hawk's shadow passes. Let them carry me, Wren thought, teeth clenched around the unfinished words hidden behind them. Let them think I'm nothing. Nothing is the best thing to be, right up until the moment it isn't.

They carried Wren up through corridors of murmuring stone, past antechambers where sold souls stood in niches like lamps that had forgotten how to burn, and Wren heard it all through the splinter pressed tight in their fist: the unbinding spreading. Slowly. Secretly. Word passing to word the way warmth passes hand to hand in a winter room. Somewhere below, the man was saying *Maret* again — but differently now. Louder. Like a name being called home instead of mourned.

They brought Wren into a hall of iron and set them down before thrones, and there — kneeling on black stone, wrists bound in light that drank instead of shone — was Astrid.

Her eyes found Wren instantly. Fear first, fierce and naked. Then something else, quick as a swallow: she saw Wren's fist. Closed. Glowing faintly between the fingers.

She couldn't speak — oh, Wren understood that with a lurch, her voice was already in the walls, they had *taken* it, the ravenous throne had accepted the bargain and swallowed her voice whole — but her eyes said everything anyway. *Don't. Whatever it costs. Don't.*

And behind her, on the second throne, the Soulkeeper leaned forward with a smile like a crack in ice, and said, in a voice that made the sold souls in the walls go silent with old terror:

"You brought it back to us, little thief. How *convenient.*"

Wren looked at the splinter. At Astrid. At the walls, where a lullaby was waiting, two notes from finishing.

And opened their hand.
