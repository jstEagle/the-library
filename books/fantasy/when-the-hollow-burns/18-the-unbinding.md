## 18. The Unbinding

Silence has a shape. Astrid had never known that until now.

It is not the silence of Hollowvale, which was a held breath, a hand pressed over her mouth for twenty-three years. This is a carved thing, hollowed out of her like the pith from a reed, and it rings. When she tries to speak — and she does try, some animal part of her still reaching for words the way a tongue reaches for a broken tooth — what comes out is not nothing but a kind of pressure, a bell struck with no clapper, felt in the bones of the court rather than heard.

The Soulkeeper feels it. She smiles.

*There she is,* the throne seems to say, though its mouth does not move, and Astrid understands with a lurch that this is how it will be now: everything said to her will arrive without sound, pressed directly into the meat of her mind, because she has no ears left for anything else. *There is the inheritance. Do you feel it settling? Like a house settling. Like snow on a roof.*

She does feel it. That is the horror of it. The court's claim goes into her the way roots go into soil — patiently, finding every channel her mother's bargain left open, and it is *warm*, which is worse than pain would be. Pain would let her fight. Warmth only lets her sink.

Around the iron dais, the antechamber breathes. The walls are full of murmuring — they have been murmuring since she descended, a tide-sound of sold souls layered into the stone like silt, and Astrid had thought, walking in, that she could bear it. She cannot. She bears it anyway. The murmur rises now, thickening, as if the walls themselves lean closer to watch a soul being portioned for the second time in one family.

*Your mother fought,* the Soulkeeper says, almost fondly. *Did Ilex tell you that? She stood where you stand and she wept, and she bargained, and in the end she gave more than we asked, because love is the only currency that overspends itself. We took her whole. Every year of her. And she asked only one thing in return.* The throne's attention sharpens, a blade laid flat against Astrid's cheek. *That you would never know. That you would live small and safe and stupid with peace. And look at you. You came down the mountain anyway. Children always do.*

Astrid lifts her chin. Her hands are empty. The shard — no. Not empty. The shard is still hers; the Soulkeeper accepted her voice, accepted the debt, but demanded the inheritance entire, and the inheritance, it turns out, is bound up in the fragment Wren carried across half the world. It sits in the crook of Astrid's arm where she holds it against her ribs, humming its trapped-wasp hum, hotter now than it has ever been, as if it knows where it has come home to.

*Give it here,* the Soulkeeper says, *and we will finish. Your mother's debt dies with the taking. You will walk out of this mountain lighter than you came in — a little less than human, a great deal less than grieving. Everyone agrees to the trade eventually. It is only a question of how much of themselves they carry back up the stairs.*

And Astrid — voiceless, root-bound, sinking warm — looks past the throne.

Past the Soulkeeper's patient iron face, past the second throne where something older and quieter watches with the stillness of deep water, past the braziers burning green, to the far wall of the antechamber, where the court keeps its vault behind a grate of black filigree.

Wren is there.

They took the child an hour ago — or a day ago; time runs strange under the mountain — and put them behind the grate with the other small holdings, the souls too young or too broken to be worth portioning out. Astrid can see her through the filigree: a girl of perhaps fourteen, Wren's sister, sitting very straight with her hands folded, the way children sit when they have decided dignity is the last thing anyone can't take from them. Her name comes to Astrid through the shard's heat, unbidden, the way names come through a dying ewe's last breath: *Maren.* Maren, who was sold at nine years old. Maren, who has been a coin in this court's purse for five years while her little brother walked rivers and roads and outlaw camps carrying a piece of her soul in his two fists.

Wren stands beside her, small hands wrapped around the black bars, watching Astrid with eyes that do not waver.

All along the road — the Kettlebones' camp, Vess's grey water, the waystation burning — the child had been learning. Astrid had thought it was simple mimicry, the way any child repeats the sounds around them: *sheep, river, run, hide.* But the shard had been teaching too. Astrid sees that now, understands it the way you understand a trap only after your foot is in it — the fragment was never just a beacon. It was a lesson. A soul remembers its binding, and Maren's soul remembered the Old Tongue in which all the Undercourt's contracts are written, and it had been whispering those words into a child's ear for a hundred miles of dark road.

The Soulkeeper follows her gaze and laughs — a soundless laugh, felt rather than heard, like a landslide far away.

*The little vessel? What can a vessel do? It holds. That is all it was ever for. Bring me the shard, Lockridge, and I will even let the child go. Consider it mercy. I am told the living appreciate being given things.*

Astrid's legs move. She does not choose to move them; the claim moves them, warm and root-deep, walking her toward the dais step by step, and the murmur in the walls rises to meet her, and somewhere in the ringing silence where her voice used to be she thinks: *Mother, you bought me twenty-three years and I spent them all getting here, and I'm sorry, and thank you, and I'm sorry.*

Her foot touches the first iron step.

Behind the grate, Wren opens their mouth.

* * *

Later — in the collapsing dark, in the years after, in every retelling — no one will agree on what the child actually said. Sef, holding the root-doors with eleven outlaws and a wolf at each hip, will swear the mountain itself flinched. Ilex, who stayed, who chose the penance of staying, wrote in his final record that the words were perfect, flawless Old Tongue, better than his own, and that he wept to hear them spoken by someone the court had never thought worth teaching. Astrid herself will never be able to describe it at all, because she has no voice to describe anything with, and because some things happen too large for language.

But in the moment, standing on the first iron step with her inheritance being drawn out of her like thread from a spindle, Astrid hears it plainly:

Wren speaks the binding words first.

Not the words of sale. Not the words of claim. The *unbinding* — the counter-phrase, the court's own lock turned with the court's own key, the syllables every contract in the Undercourt is built to obey and none is built to survive. The shard has been teaching them since the valley. Maybe since before the valley. Maybe a stolen fragment of soul carries its own rescue folded inside it, waiting all this time for a mouth small enough and stubborn enough to say it aloud.

The child's voice is high and clear and absolutely unafraid, and it fills the antechamber the way Astrid's voice never filled anything, and the words land on the iron floor like sparks on oil.

The shard screams.

Astrid feels it scream against her ribs — a note that is not sound, that goes through bone and claim and warmth and root, and then the fragment of Maren's soul *shatters*, and it does not shatter like glass. It shatters like dawn. Light comes off it in sheets, off her arms, off the steps, up the walls, and everywhere the light touches the murmur in the stones stops murmuring and begins to *wake*.

That is the only word for it. The walls wake.

Five years of Maren poured back into Maren — Astrid sees it strike the girl behind the grate like a wave striking a seawall, sees her head snap up, sees five lost years return to her face all at once, the growing she never did, the grief she never got to have, and the girl opens her mouth and says her brother's name in a voice cracked with disuse and it is the most human sound the mountain has ever held.

And then the rest.

Every sold soul in the court wakes at once. The antechamber walls blaze with them — hundreds, thousands, decades of bargains unraveling in a single cascade, each unbound soul tearing free of the stone and rising like embers off a bonfire, and the sound they make is not a murmur anymore. It is a chorus. It is a *roar*. Farmers and queens and children and thieves, everyone who ever traded a piece of themselves to the thrones beneath the mountain, all of them coming back to themselves in the same heartbeat, and their returning is a flood, and the flood has nowhere to go but *out*.

The Soulkeeper rises from the throne for the first time in a thousand years.

Iron groans. The green braziers gutter. The second throne — the older, quieter thing — does not rise at all; it simply comes apart, quietly, like frost giving up on a window, because whatever it was, it was made mostly of the contracts, and the contracts are dying.

*Stop,* the Soulkeeper says, and for the first time the voice has edges, has panic in it, has *fear*. *Child. Vessel. You do not know what you have—*

But Wren does know. Wren learned it word by word on the road, in a stolen coat, eating outlaw bread, repeating after a woman who could talk to beasts: that small things are not powerless things, that the ones the powerful never bother to teach are the ones who learn anyway, that a court which buys souls has never once bothered to buy a child's silence — and so a child's silence was the one thing it never owned.

The last syllable falls. The last chain breaks.

The claim inside Astrid — the warm, rooting, sinking weight of it — *lets go*. All at once, like a fist opening. She staggers on the iron step, catches herself, and stands in the blazing antechamber feeling nothing in her mind but her own mind, nothing in her blood but her own blood, her mother's debt burned to ash by a phrase a six-year-old learned on a mountainside.

Her voice does not come back. That door has closed, and she knows it, and she finds — strangely, terribly, truly — that she does not reach for it. Some prices you pay gladly twice.

The Soulkeeper comes down the dais.

It should be terrifying. It is terrifying. The iron thing crosses the floor in three strides with its hand outstretched toward the children, toward the last loose threads of a thousand-year loom, and Astrid moves without deciding to — puts herself between the throne and the grate, arms spread, voiceless and unarmed and utterly in the way — and the Soulkeeper stops.

Not because of her. Because of the walls.

The waking souls are pouring out of the stone now in their hundreds, and they are not fleeing. They are turning. They are looking at the throne that bought them with the faces of people remembering what was taken, and the oldest of them — a woman in robes three centuries out of fashion, a soldier with a rusted crown, a boy who cannot be more than ten — begin, slowly, to close ranks between the Soulkeeper and the grate.

*You cannot,* the Soulkeeper says, and its voice cracks like cooling metal. *I am the court. I am the—*

"You," says the woman in the old robes, in a voice like a door opening after three hundred years, "are *overdrawn*."

The antechamber lights white.

* * *

Afterward there is running, but that belongs to the next hour, and Astrid's memory of it will always come to her in pieces: Sef's war-howl echoing down the root-stairs; wolves streaming into the iron halls with their hackles silvered by soul-light; Wren's hand in hers, small and gripping hard; Maren stumbling free of the melted grate, five years older in a single breath, refusing to let go of her brother's other hand. The mountain shaking itself awake around them like a dog shedding water.

But the last thing she sees, before the stairwell swallows them, is the throne room filling from wall to wall with the freed — thousands upon thousands, rising like reversed snowfall, streaming up through the mountain's veins toward the open sky — and at the center of it, alone on its dais, the Soulkeeper, shrinking, dimming, feeding on nothing, owed by no one, its thousand-year ledger closing page by page in front of its eyes.

Its gaze finds Astrid across the blaze.

*This is not justice,* it says, thin as smoke. *This is only arithmetic.*

And Astrid — voiceless, free, her mother's daughter and no one's purchase — smiles at the second throne of the Undercourt, and does not answer, and does not need to.

Then the mountain roars, the stairs crumble, and they run.
