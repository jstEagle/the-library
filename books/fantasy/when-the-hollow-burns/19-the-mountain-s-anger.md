## 19. The Mountain's Anger

The mountain began to scream before they reached the first antechamber.

It was not a sound the ear caught so much as a sound the bones did — a deep groaning in the stone, as if some vast animal had been sleeping beneath them for a thousand years and had just been kicked awake. Dust sifted from the ceiling in pale threads. The murmur of the sold souls in the walls rose to a chorus, then fractured into a thousand separate voices, all crying out at once, and Astrid felt every one of them pass through her like wind through an open window.

*Wake,* they were saying. *We are awake. We are awake and we do not know where we are.*

She could not answer them. She had nothing left to answer with.

Her voice was gone. It had gone the way she had given it — poured out onto the iron floor of the court like water from a broken jug — and what remained in its place was a silence so complete it felt less like absence than like a second body wrapped around her own. She had thought, in the first terrible moments after the Soulkeeper took it, that she would suffocate on it. Now she understood: silence was not empty. Silence was full. It was full of everything she had ever said to beasts, everything the beasts had ever said back, and all of it was still there, waiting, pressing against the inside of her like a river against a dam.

And the mountain was shaking that dam loose.

Wren's hand found hers in the dark. The child's palm was slick with sweat and soot, and their grip was fierce enough to hurt. Above them, the vault doors hung open — blown open, in truth, when the shard had shattered and every binding in the Undercourt had come apart at once — and through them poured souls. Not ghosts, not quite; more like heat-shimmer given shape, like the air over a summer road made weeping and human. They streamed past Astrid without seeing her, up the stairwells, toward whatever thin light the mountain still allowed. Somewhere among them, Astrid knew, was Wren's sister. She had seen her go — a tall girl with Wren's same dark eyes, stumbling, weeping, touching her own hands as if she could not believe they were whole.

But the court was not finished dying, and a dying thing is at its most dangerous.

The thrones were empty. The Soulkeeper had not been destroyed — Astrid had felt it *leave*, felt its attention withdraw from the hall like a hand pulled from a wound — but the lesser court, the hunters and tally-keepers and soul-clerks with their ink-stained fingers and their faces like closed ledgers, had scattered into the corridors, and now they came back armed. Astrid heard them before she saw them: the ring of iron on stone, the wet hiss of soul-hooks being drawn, those long barbed instruments designed to drag something out of a body without killing the body, because a body was useful, a body could be sold twice.

Wren tugged her sleeve and pointed. A side passage — one of Ilex's marked routes, chalked in the scribe's careful hand on the wall where only a trained eye would find it. Astrid nodded and ran.

They ran. The passage was low and sloped upward, and the mountain screamed around them, and behind them the iron boots rang closer. Astrid's lungs burned. Her legs ached from days of descent, from the root-doors, from the long dark climb down into the place where souls were kept like coins in a miser's strongbox. But fear is a fuel older than exhaustion, and she burned it freely now.

A soul-hook struck the wall beside her head and buried itself two inches deep in solid rock.

Astrid spun. Three hunters filled the corridor behind them — tall figures in grey lacquered armor, their faces hidden behind masks shaped like blank ledger pages. The lead hunter wrenched his hook free and leveled it at Wren, and Astrid saw the calculation in the set of his shoulders: *the fragment is destroyed, but the child spoke the words, the child is the instrument, the child can be studied.*

No.

The word rose in her — *no* — and having no voice to carry it, she gave it somewhere else instead. She reached outward, past the walls, past the stone, into the mountain's living dark, and she *shouted*.

There were things in the Undercourt's deep places that the court had never bothered to name, because the court named only what it could sell. Blind fish in flooded galleries. Pale moths the size of dinner plates. And below everything, coiled in the oldest dark, the mountain's true children — the burrowers, the gnawers, the vast slow worms whose passing hollowed out whole caverns, the things the scribes had written off as vermin centuries ago. They had never heard a human voice. They had certainly never heard a human voice that spoke to them as an equal, in the tongue that needed no throat.

*Stop them,* Astrid said. *Please.*

The corridor behind them erupted.

What came out of the side-galleries was not beautiful. It was a flood of grey flesh and hooked claws and blind, questing snouts, and the hunters' disciplined formation lasted exactly as long as it took the first burrower to reach them. Astrid did not stay to watch. She caught Wren's hand again and ran, and behind them the screaming changed key — no longer the mountain's groan but the hunters' own voices, high and human under the masks, which was its own kind of justice, though it turned her stomach even now.

* * *

They broke into the great root-gallery at a dead run and nearly ran into Sef.

The outlaw leader stood in the mouth of the root-doors with her back to the coming light, and around her, in a ragged crescent, stood what remained of the Kettlebones. Astrid counted them and hated herself for counting: eleven. There had been thirty-one at the waystation before Ilex's betrayal. Eleven now, bloodied, singed, half of them holding weapons they'd scavenged from fallen hunters, all of them planted in front of that doorway like fence posts in a flood.

"You're alive," Sef said. Her voice was raw. There was a burn scar down the left side of her face that hadn't been there three days ago. "Both of you. Good. Because I've got news, and you're not going to like it."

Astrid touched her own throat and shook her head.

Sef's eyes narrowed, then widened with understanding. "Ah," she said softly. "You paid." She didn't ask what. Outlaws don't. "Well. Then listen. The mountain's coming down — not all of it, the court's wards are failing and taking the load-bearing chambers with them, don't ask me how I know, ask Doss, he used to be a miner before he was a thief. We've got maybe a quarter hour of roof over our heads. And the hunters—" she jerked her chin back down the gallery, toward the sound of distant fighting, "—the ones who got out of the throne hall are falling back here. All of them. This is the only door."

Behind Sef, the root-doors stood wedged open with iron pry-bars, and beyond them Astrid could see it: daylight. Thin, grey, snow-dusted daylight, filtering down through the shattered seal Ilex's stolen keys had opened. It looked impossibly far away and impossibly bright, like the memory of a different life.

"How many?" Astrid wrote in the dust on the floor, kneeling, her finger moving fast.

"Forty," Sef said. "Maybe fifty. And they're not fighting to kill anymore. They're fighting to *keep us in*. The Soulkeeper wants this door shut with everyone inside it." Her jaw tightened. "My people can hold for a while. We're good at holding doorways; it's most of what we do. But a while isn't a quarter hour, Lockridge, and there's another problem."

Astrid raised her eyebrows.

"The tunnel's blocked. Half a mile in, the collapse has already started — Doss says there's a chamber down that came down across the main passage, and we can't dig through it fast enough, and even if we could, we'd be digging *up*, into who knows what." Sef crouched, bringing her scarred face level with Astrid's, and for the first time since the Kettlebones had robbed them on the mountain road, Astrid saw something in the woman's expression that was not calculation. It was trust. Offered plainly, like a coin set on a table. "So. You're the beast-speaker. The whole mountain's waking up — my wolves felt it hours ago, they've been howling since before dawn. Tell me you have a way out of here that doesn't involve that tunnel."

Astrid looked at Wren. Wren looked back at her, and in the child's dark eyes there was no fear — or rather, there was fear, plenty of it, held the way a child holds a heavy thing: with both arms, refusing to put it down. Wren pointed upward. Then outward. Then made a shape in the air with both hands — wings? No. Climbing. Claws on stone.

*Yes,* Astrid thought. *Exactly that.*

She knelt again in the dust and wrote: *I need the gallery clear. Everyone inside the doors. And someone to hold them open as long as they can.*

Sef read it, and Astrid watched her understand what she was asking — watched her understand that *as long as they can* meant until the mountain came down, and that whoever held the doors might not walk away from it. The outlaw leader was quiet for a moment. Then she stood, turned to her eleven, and grinned with all the warmth of a drawn knife.

"You heard the lady," she said. "Inside. All of you except Brannick, Doss, and me. Somebody's got to hold the door, and I'm not asking anyone to do what I won't."

One of the outlaws — a big man with a butcher's forearms — spat and stepped forward anyway. "Then I'm staying too."

"Sef," Astrid wrote, urgently. *Don't. Please. I may be able to—*

"Don't waste dust on it," Sef said. "This is what we're for, Lockridge. You think we take stolen court-tokens and rob toll-keepers because we like the money? Half my band lost someone to the tithe-carts. Whole villages, sometimes, when the census-takers found something they didn't like." She rolled her shoulders. "The court's been eating people since before my grandmother's grandmother. Tonight it chokes on us. Go. Call your beasts. Make it loud."

* * *

Astrid stood in the center of the root-gallery, in the dark, in the shaking heart of the mountain, and opened herself completely for the first time in her life.

It was not like speaking. It was not even like the calls she had sent down into the deep galleries to save Wren. Those had been words, shaped and aimed. This was different. This was her standing in the middle of the mountain's dreaming body with her silence spread wide like a net, like a bell rung without sound, and everything that lived in the stone turning toward her at once.

She felt them come.

The burrowers first, the great pale worms, rising through their own tunnels from depths where no light had ever been. Then the bat-colonies pouring out of the high chimneys in rivers of leather and echo. Then the wildcats, the cliff-goats, the foxes, the hares, the ravens roosting in the mountain's throat — and beyond the mountain, beyond the root-doors, the whole night country answering: wolves streaming down from the high passes, bears lumbering out of winter dens half-asleep and furious about it, elk, deer, foxes, weasels, a hundred thousand small hearts all beating toward the same summons.

They had never been called like this. No one had ever called like this. Every beast-speaker before her had spoken to one creature at a time, quietly, in corners, hiding. Astrid stood in the ruin of the Undercourt and spoke to a mountain, and the mountain listened.

*Carry us out,* she said. *Up. Not through the stone — over it. To the surface. My friends are coming with me. Do not let them fall.*

The answering roar — silent, total, felt in the marrow rather than heard — was so vast that Astrid staggered. Wren grabbed her arm and held on.

And then the hunters came.

They came down the last corridor in a wedge, fifty strong, hooks leveled, and the Kettlebones met them at the root-doors, and the gallery became a single shrieking knot of iron and fire and fury. Sef fought the way water fights — flowing around blades, finding every gap, her twin knives flickering. Brannick swung a pry-bar like a scythe. Doss had gotten hold of a hunter's own hook and used it with a miner's knowledge of leverage, ugly and effective. They held. Astrid had told them to hold, and they held, and every second of it cost her something she couldn't name, watching them bleed for a door while she stood useless in the middle of the floor with her empty throat.

*Ilex,* she thought suddenly. *Where is Ilex?*

She found him at the edge of the fight, pressed against the wall, and for one absurd moment she thought he was trying to reach the doors — trying to flee with them, to buy his way out a second time. Then she saw what he carried. The seals. All of them, the stolen court-seals, the keys that had opened the root-doors and the vaults and every locked thing between. He was gathering them up, all of them, into his scribal satchel.

Their eyes met across the chaos.

He didn't smile. He didn't wave. He looked at her the way a man looks at a debt he has finally decided to pay, and he turned his back on the daylight, and he walked *down* — down the corridor, toward the collapsing heart of the court, toward the Soulkeeper's empty throne and the burning archives where every record of every sale was kept. Toward the records of his own hand. Astrid understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, what he meant to do. Seals could lock doors. Seals could also lock *records*, could seal the archives against rescue, could make sure that when the mountain fell, nothing in it remained for the next court to rebuild from.

He was going to bury the ledger with himself inside it.

She tried to call after him. Of course she did. She opened her mouth and nothing came, and the nothing was so complete, so perfectly the thing she had traded away, that for a moment the whole roaring gallery went quiet inside her. Then Wren's small hand pressed against her back — once, firmly, the way you steady someone — and Astrid let him go.

Some penances you cannot do for another person. Some you should not try to.

* * *

The beasts arrived as the doors began to fail.

They came up through the mountain's own passages — the worm-burrows widening, the old chimneys clearing themselves of five centuries of rubble as if the stone itself had decided to cooperate — and they poured into the root-gallery in a tide that stopped even the hunters' advance. Wolves first, Sef's own pack leading dozens of others, hitting the hunters' flank like a landslide with teeth. Then the bears, and behind them things Astrid only half recognized, deep-mountain shapes that had never seen a human before and had no reason to be gentle.

"To the animals!" Sef bellowed, bloodied, laughing, insane. "Everyone on something with fur! *Now!*"

It was not dignified. It was not orderly. It was a dead run and a desperate scramble — outlaws flung onto the backs of elk and bears and one enormous shaggy cave-ox that had apparently been sleeping under the mountain since before the court was built; Wren swept up onto the shoulders of a wolf the size of a pony, who had taken one look at the child and appointed itself; Astrid hauled up onto the neck of a red stag with antlers like winter branches, who knelt for her with a courtesy that undid her entirely.

The hunters regrouped. Of course they did. Hooks flashed, and a wolf screamed, and Brannick went down under three of them and did not get up, and Astrid screamed too — silently, hugely, with everything she had — and the mountain answered. The ceiling of the root-gallery came down across the corridor the hunters had come by, a deliberate, surgical collapse, the burrowers biting through the last supporting stone as if they had been waiting ten thousand years for permission.

That left the other way out: up.

The stag took the slope at a gallop. Behind and around her, the exodus thundered — wolves and riders swarming up the mountain's inner chimneys, bats streaming overhead like smoke, the whole living weight of the Undercourt's prisoners fleeing toward the thin grey light. The mountain groaned and shrugged and shed its skin. Chunks of the court's spires, Astrid would learn later, burst from the peak above them like teeth from a rotting jaw, and the snowfields slid, and the night sky filled with dust that glittered like dying stars.

Astrid did not look back. Once — only once, at the top of the chimney, where the stone opened onto open air and moonlight — she looked back, down into the smoking dark where the Iron Court had ruled for a thousand years. She saw no throne. She saw no Soulkeeper. She saw only the small, stubborn glow of a fire in the archive levels, spreading, deliberate, tended by a hand that had once recorded every crime and was now erasing them at the cost of everything.

Goodbye, Ilex, she thought. *Rest.*

Then the stag crested the ridge, and the wind hit her face, and the mountain's anger became, all at once, only a sound behind her — enormous, receding, almost mournful — and the night country opened ahead, silver and cold and free.

Wren whooped from the wolf's back, a wild, unbroken sound, and the wolf howled, and the pack answered, and somewhere in that chorus Astrid felt her own silence ring like a struck bell.

They rode east through the snow until dawn. And on the horizon, faint as a memory, the valley of Hollowvale waited — ward flickering, gates unknown, and Brammel the warden standing at its border with a question Astrid had no voice left to answer.
