## 20. Homecoming Without Words

The mountain let them go grudgingly, the way a fist opens finger by finger.

Astrid felt it in her bones for three days after the last tremor died — the deep grinding of stone settling over the ruined court, the mountain swallowing its own dead. She could not hear it as she once would have. Her silence was a country she was still learning to walk through, its borders unfamiliar, its weather strange. But Wren heard it. The child walked with one hand pressed flat against boulders and cliff-faces, listening to the rock remember, and sometimes stopped to tell Astrid what the mountain said.

*It is ashamed,* Wren signed, in the rough hand-language they had built between them on the road — half Astrid's invention, half the child's own fierce grammar. *It says the court was a splinter. It says it is glad.*

*Tell it I'm glad too,* Astrid answered, and Wren laughed — a sound that had grown less feral with every mile, more like a bell than a snarl — and pressed both palms to the stone and delivered the message with great solemnity.

They came down out of the high passes on the seventh morning, when the frost still silvered the gorse but the sun had enough weight in it to promise spring. And there, spread below them in its cup of hills, lay Hollowvale.

Astrid had left it in autumn. She returned to find it wearing winter's last grey like a borrowed coat — smoke rising thin from the chimneys of Thistle Row, the mill-wheel turning slow and brown in the river, sheep scattered across the lower pasture like spilled rice. Ordinary. Small. The most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

She stood at the ridge until her legs shook, and Wren stood beside her, and neither of them spoke, because only one of them could.

* * *

She had rehearsed this moment on the road, in the wordless way of the voiceless: how it would feel to see the valley again, how she would explain — somehow — everything that had happened. She had imagined Brammel meeting them at the border with his ledger and his questions, and herself answering with chalk and slate and Wren's quick tongue. She had imagined the ward rising before her as it always had, that invisible wall of hush where magic went to die, and herself stepping through it into the old numbness, the old safety, the old small life.

What she had not imagined was the ward flickering.

They saw it from the ridge, actually — or rather, Wren did. The child tugged Astrid's sleeve and pointed down at the valley's edge, at the place where the road crossed the boundary near the old boundary-stone, and Astrid followed the pointing finger and saw nothing at first. Then, as the light shifted, she saw it: a shimmer, faint as heat-haze over summer gravel, running along the line of the ward like a crack running through glaze.

It pulsed. Dimmed. Pulsed again. Like a heartbeat with too few beats left in it.

*It is dying,* Wren signed. Then, correcting itself with the scrupulous honesty of children: *Or waking. I cannot tell which. The stones say both.*

Astrid knew which. She had known since the river-god Vess had taken her mother's memory and shown her, in the moment of its leaving, exactly what her mother had done — had seen the bargain struck in an iron antechamber twenty-three years ago, a young woman kneeling before a cold throne, offering the whole of her soul, every year of it, every hour, to buy two things: her daughter's silence-gift sealed safe beneath notice, and a valley warded so thoroughly that no hunter could follow. The ward of Hollowvale had never been the warden's work, or the elders', or any prayer of theirs. It had been fed, all these years, by Maren Lockridge, burning slow at the bottom of the Undercourt like a lamp kept lit in a window.

And now the court was rubble, and the lamp was out, and the ward was guttering because there was nothing left to feed it.

*Come,* Astrid signed, and they went down together into the valley that had swallowed magic for twenty-three years, and found that it could barely swallow anymore.

* * *

The crossing itself was nothing. That was the strangest part. Astrid braced at the boundary-stone the way she'd braced her whole life — shoulders up, breath held, ready for the familiar deadening, the muffling of the world's loud secret life — and felt only a cool shiver pass over her skin, like walking through a curtain of water. No wall. No hush. Just air, and beyond it the smell of woodsmoke and wet earth and, distantly, sheep.

Wren skipped across without noticing at all, chattering aloud now in a mixture of Old Tongue and broken Common, narrating everything — *stone, bird, bird, big bird, Astrid-face* — because the child had discovered speech the way other children discover rivers, plunging in everywhere, getting gloriously soaked.

The beasts noticed, though. That was the thing that made Astrid stop in the lane above the pastures and press her hand to her mouth. A ewe lifted her head from the winter grass and looked straight at Astrid across forty yards of open ground, and Astrid heard her — faintly, thinly, through whatever thread of the gift still ran in her blood — a low woolly murmur of recognition. *Small-mother. Small-mother comes back thin.* And around her, others: the drowsy complaint of cattle in the byre, a dog's sharp joy three farms away, crows arguing over the midden. Not the flood she'd known beyond the ward. But present. Alive. Leaking back into the valley through the ward's cracks like light under a door.

Twenty-three years of silence, ending not with a thunderclap but with a ewe's mild greeting.

Astrid sat down right there in the lane, in the mud, and wept without sound, and Wren sat down beside her and patted her arm with tremendous authority until she was done.

* * *

Brammel found them before they reached the village.

Of course he did. He had been warden of Hollowvale for thirty years, and a warden who couldn't find two travelers on the one road into his valley didn't deserve the office. He came up the lane on foot, staff in hand, grey-bearded and square as a fencepost, and Astrid watched him take them in — the mud, the burns, the child, the way Astrid moved like someone carrying something breakable inside her chest — and watched him arrive, within the space of three heartbeats, at approximately the truth.

"Astrid Lockridge." His voice was careful. "You were seen leaving the valley in the night, four months past, with a child no one could account for. There are those who said you'd been taken. There are those who said worse." His eyes went to Wren, who stared back with the unblinking frankness of a hawk. "There are those who said you'd been dealing in magic."

Astrid opened her mouth.

Nothing came.

She had known it would be so. She had lived four months with the silence; she had made her peace with it on the mountain, in the dark, holding Wren while the court fell. But knowing a thing and standing in the lane of your own valley unable to answer your warden's question were different animals entirely, and the old shame rose in her throat like bile — the shame of a lifetime spent hiding, now made flesh, made permanent, written on her body where everyone could read it.

She took out the slate instead. She had carried it since the mountain, along with a stub of chalk worn to a pebble.

*I went to bring back this child's sister,* she wrote. *I went because the ones who took her would have come here next. I went so Hollowvale would be safe.*

Brammel read it twice. His jaw worked. "That's not an answer," he said. "Where did you go? What did you do? The ward has been failing since midwinter — flickering, dying at the edges, letting things *through*. I've had foxes speaking in my dreams, Astrid. Foxes. Old Marta's goat recited poetry. The elders want answers and I don't have them, and you come walking out of the mountains looking like a survivor of a war nobody's heard of, mute as a stone, with a child who talks to rocks." He lowered his voice. "The law says magic in the valley is death by burning. You know that law. Your mother knew that law. So you'll tell me plainly: did you work magic?"

And there it was. The question. The whole question, the only question, the one that had shaped every day of her life in this valley — asked now not of a girl hiding a gift but of a woman who had walked into the Undercourt and broken it.

Astrid looked at him. At his weathered face and his honest, frightened eyes. Brammel had been kind to her in his flinty way — had hired her for beast-work when no one else would, had never asked why she lived alone at the valley's edge, had turned the warden's eye conveniently blind to small oddities for twenty-three years. He deserved better than a lie. He had also spent thirty years enforcing a law that burned people like her, and he deserved the truth more.

She bent to the slate.

Then Wren stepped forward.

"No," the child said — clear, ringing, in accented but unmistakable Common, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "I will tell. Astrid cannot speak. The iron court took her voice. It wanted more but the words were spoken first." Wren drew itself up to its full unimpressive height, mud-caked and scarred and shining. "I will tell you what happened. All of it. You will wish to sit down, warden-man. The stones told me you are stubborn, so I will speak slowly."

"Wren—" Astrid began, uselessly, silently.

But the child was already talking.

* * *

Wren told it all, standing in the lane while the light lengthened and the first villagers crept close to listen — old Marta with her shawl, the cooper's boys, Tamsin Ree with flour still on her hands, drawn out of their houses by the rumor of Astrid's return and held there by the impossible story spilling out of the feral child. Wren told of the iron court beneath the mountain, of thrones and soul-hunters and walls that murmured with the sold dead. Told of the shard, the sister, the hunters patient as winter at the ward's edge. Told of outlaws and a river-god and a scribe who betrayed them and an army of thieves who became something else. Told of a woman who walked into the dark with no weapon but her voice, and gave her voice away, and lost the bargain anyway — and won it anyway, because a child had been learning the binding words all along, hidden in songs and games, stitched into lullabies by a shard of stolen soul that had chosen, in the end, to choose.

"And the souls woke up," Wren finished, "all of them, hundreds, thousands, and the mountain shook off the court like water off a dog's back, and the sister flew free, and Astrid carried me out on the back of a bear the size of a barn, and the bear sang. Badly. Bears do not sing well. That is the end except for the part about Ilex staying behind, which is sad, and the part about Sef's band holding the doors, which is brave, and the part where Astrid's mother—"

"*Enough.*" Brammel's voice cracked like ice on a pond.

He had gone very pale. Around him the villagers stood in a silence so complete Astrid could hear the mill-wheel turning half a mile off. Old Marta had both hands pressed to her mouth. Tamsin Ree was crying without appearing to know it.

"The ward," Brammel said at last, slowly, as if each word cost him. "You're saying the ward — our ward, the valley's ward — was bought with a soul. With a *Lockridge* soul. Fed by—" He looked at Astrid, and Astrid met his eyes and nodded, once, and saw him understand: *my mother. Twenty-three years. Every day of my life.*

"Your mother," Brammel said hoarsely, "who the elders remember as a quiet woman who kept to herself. Who they whispered about. Who they—" He stopped. Started again. "Who this valley let believe she was nothing."

Astrid took out the slate.

*She was everything,* she wrote. *She was the whole reason I survived. Don't let them remember her as nothing.*

Brammel read it. Read it again. And then the warden of Hollowvale, keeper of the law, did something Astrid had never seen him do: he bowed. Not deeply, not grandly — a short, stiff, painful inclination of the head, the bow of a man acknowledging a debt he could never repay and knew it.

"The law says magic is death," he said quietly. "That law was written in fear, by people who thought the ward would keep them safe forever. The ward is failing. The magic is already coming back — you can hear it in the fields if you've ears for it, and apparently some of us do now." A ragged, uneasy stir ran through the gathered villagers. "So the law has a choice to make. Burn the Lockridges, or burn the law." He straightened, and his voice hardened into the tone he used at council meetings, the one that permitted no argument. "I know which I'll be proposing at the spring assembly. Anyone who disagrees can come argue with me after they've heard the rest of what this child knows. Which, I suspect, is considerably more than any of us will enjoy hearing."

He turned to go, then paused. "Astrid. Your house — it stands. I kept the roof mended. I didn't know why. Now I suppose I did." And he walked down the lane toward the village, an old man carrying a new world on his shoulders, and the villagers drifted after him in knots of twos and threes, whispering, glancing back.

* * *

Dusk found Astrid and Wren at the boundary-stone, because Astrid needed to see it once more before she could sleep.

The ward shimmered fitfully in the last light, thinner than breath, a ghost of a ghost. Through it she could feel the outer dark pressing close — the great breathing world, full of voices, leaning against the valley's failing skin. Soon it would come through altogether. The beasts. The wild magic. Everything her mother had paid an eternity to keep out.

Everything her mother had paid an eternity to keep out — and yet, Astrid thought, sitting in the cold grass with Wren warm against her side, perhaps that wasn't quite the truth of it. Perhaps her mother hadn't been building a wall at all. Perhaps she had simply been buying time. Twenty-three years for her daughter to grow strong enough, kind enough, stubborn enough to walk into the dark and end the thing that ate souls. A lamp lit in a window, not to keep the night away, but to show the child the way home.

The ward pulsed once, faintly, and dimmed.

And then, from the darkness beyond it — from the hills, the forests, the high white passes — came a sound that raised every hair on Astrid's arms. Not one voice. Hundreds. Wolves and bears and elk and eagles and things with older names, all of them moving, all of them converging on the valley's edge, all of them singing one low sustained note like a held breath.

Wren sat bolt upright. "Astrid," the child whispered, gripping her sleeve. "They're coming. They're all coming. They say—" The child listened, eyes wide, and then turned to her with an expression Astrid would remember for the rest of her life. "They say they have come to speak for you. They say the valley must hear. They say—" Wren faltered, then steadied. "They say it is time for your mother's name to be spoken aloud."

Beyond the flickering ward, the night filled with waiting eyes.
