## 21. What the Beasts Said

Astrid had spent her whole life learning how to be quiet in a way that looked like silence and was actually speech. She had spoken to ewes in lambing sheds and to cart-horses with colic, to geese who hated the fog and to a half-wild tomcat who permitted her existence the way a landlord permits a tenant. She had never once spoken *for* herself, out loud, to the people of Hollowvale — because there had never been anything safe to say.

Now she could not have said it if she wanted to.

She stood in the dooryard of the meeting-house in the grey middle of the morning, three days home from the mountain, and the whole valley had come to look at what the mountain had sent back. Brammel stood at the front of the crowd with his warden's iron ring on his thumb and his face arranged into the expression of a man doing his duty by a thing he did not understand. Behind him: the miller, the smith, the midwife Old Sarra who had pulled Astrid into the world and had not once held her since, farmers and their hired hands, children hoisted onto fences. Wren pressed against Astrid's side like a warm coal. And a little apart from everyone, wrapped in Astrid's own spare cloak, stood the girl.

The sister. Lark, Wren said her name was — had said, in the broken sweet pidgin of the valley tongue they'd built together between them on the road home. Lark, who had gone under the mountain a child of nine and come out of the soul-vault with her soul newly sewn back into her body and no memory of the years between. She looked at the crowd the way a deer looks at a frozen pond, testing where the ice would hold.

Brammel raised his hand for quiet that had already come.

"Astrid Lockridge," he said. "You went over the ward with this child without leave. You went under the mountain, which is a thing I don't have law for because no one ever thought it needed naming. You've come back voiceless, and there's a girl at your shoulder the Undercourt would tear the valley apart to reclaim." He paused. His thumb turned the iron ring around and around. "I'm obliged to ask what you did. And you're not able to tell me. So before I bind you over to the council's judgment — is there anyone, or *anything*, that will speak for you?"

It was a warden's joke, or half of one. Beasts did not speak. That was the whole architecture of Astrid's hiding: everyone knew the gift existed only in old wives' tales and burnings two generations past, and so no one had ever looked at the woman who was always, always among animals.

The crowd murmured. And then the murmur stopped, because the dogs began to arrive.

They came up the lane from the farms in twos and threes — the miller's brindled hound, Sarra's little sharp-faced terrier, the shepherd dogs off the high pasture with their work still in their spines. They did not run. They walked, deliberate as elders processing, and sat themselves down in the frost-wet grass between Astrid and the crowd. Then came the horses, four of them, breaking their paddock gates without hurry, standing along the fence line with their breath steaming. The oxen came slowest, and the geese came loud, and the barn cats simply appeared along the top of the stone wall like they had always been there and were choosing now to be visible.

Wren made a sound low in her throat — not fear. Recognition.

And the miller's old hound, whose name was Bess and who had slept in Astrid's hayloft through eleven winters because her hips could not manage the mill stairs, stood up, faced the crowd, and spoke.

Not barking. Not the borrowed half-words Astrid's gift had always been — the sense pushed through an animal's mouth like water through a sieve. This was different. This was the ward itself, Astrid realized dimly; the ward that had drunk her mother's soul for twenty-three years was thinning now, opening now, and magic moved through the valley like thaw through frozen ground, and every beast within it was suddenly a bell that could ring true.

"She tended us," Bess said, in a voice like gravel rolled in honey. "When your lambs were dying of the red scour and you prayed to saints you weren't sure were listening, she sat in the straw with them all night and told them where the light was. When your horses foundered she asked them what hurt and believed the answer. Twenty-three years, she has carried your animals' pain in her hands and told no one, because telling would have burned her."

The crowd had gone very still. Somewhere a child began to cry and was hushed.

Old Sarra stepped forward, one step, her knuckles white on her stick. "That isn't possible," she said, but her voice shook, and the terrier at her feet looked up at her with an expression that dared her to call its life impossible.

"There's more," said the black cart-horse named Dunny, and his voice was deeper, slower, like a river under ice. "Ask her why she has no voice to answer you. Ask her what her mother paid."

* * *

Astrid had pieced it together on the road home, in the fragments the shard had shown her and the things Ilex had confessed with his hands shaking around his stolen seals, and she had grieved most of it already, alone, in the wordless way that was all she had left. But she had never told it. There had been no one to tell it to.

The beasts told it for her.

The old ram from the high pasture took up the thread first, and then the mare, and Bess again, each one handing the story along like a bucket line at a fire. They told it plainly, without ornament, which was somehow worse than ornament.

"Her mother was Maren Lockridge, and she had the same gift, and when Astrid was born squalling truth into a valley that burns beast-speakers, Maren knew the child would not live to walk."

"So she went looking for the one power that keeps its bargains absolutely, and found it under the mountain, in the court that buys souls and sells silence."

"She sold her own soul entire. Not her death — her death was nothing, deaths are cheap. Her *soul*. Her warmth, her memory, her selfhood, portioned out across the court's vaults to feed its lamps."

"And what she bought with it was this: that her daughter's gift would go unheard by any ear that meant harm. That the valley's ward would close around the child like a hand and starve all magic at its borders, so that no hunter could smell what Astrid was. That Astrid would live small and hidden and safe until the debt came due or didn't."

"It came due when the shard crossed the ward. The beacon woke. The ward thinned. The bargain began to fray — because a bargain bought with one soul cannot cover another soul's choices, and Astrid chose."

The geese took up the last part, honking it in ragged chorus while the crowd's faces went from disbelief to something rawer. They told of the descent, the root-doors, the antechambers where sold souls murmured in the walls — and how one of those murmurs, faint as a candle seen through ten miles of dark, had been Maren Lockridge's.

"She heard her mother in the walls," the lead goose said, "and she could not free her alone. So she offered the throne the trade: the fragment, the mother's debt, and her own voice. Her voice — the instrument of the gift itself, the one thing a speaker has. And when the Soulkeeper reached to take it—"

"—the child," said Bess, and turned her greying muzzle toward Wren, "spoke first."

Every eye in the crowd went to Wren. Wren, who had learned the valley tongue word by word on the road and the Old Tongue the way rivers learn downhill, straightened her small spine and looked out at the assembled valley with eyes that had seen an iron court from inside a cage.

"The words were in the shard all along," Wren said. Her accent was strange and her grammar was a house still being built, but her voice carried. "Unbinding words. Teaching me. Every night. I think — I think maybe the fragment *wanted*. It was a piece of somebody, and pieces of people want to be people again." She swallowed. "So I said them. And it broke, and everything it touched woke up."

Silence, then, of a quality Astrid had never heard in Hollowvale. The kind of silence that is not empty but listening.

* * *

It was Brammel who broke it, because it was his job to break silences, and Astrid watched him do the hardest work of his life right there in the dooryard with the frost melting around his boots.

He looked at the beasts. He looked at Astrid. She saw him weigh the law — his whole life the law, the neat stone wall of it built around the valley against everything wild — and she saw him find, at the bottom of himself, the thing the law was supposed to protect.

"The court's crimes," he said slowly, to the crowd more than to her. "The beasts mentioned crimes. Sold souls. A girl taken from her family." His jaw worked. "We have all known, haven't we. In the way you know the water's bad but drink it anyway. We knew what the mountain did, and we let the ward keep it out and called that virtue." He turned to face Astrid fully, and took off his warden's cap, which Astrid had never seen him do. "There's no law for what you did, because nobody imagined a person would do it. I'm not binding you over. I'm asking your pardon. For twenty-three years of a valley that would have burned you, and called it safety."

One by one, the valley followed him.

Not all at once. The miller first, gruffly, because Bess was his dog and had been telling him truths about his dead wife all morning through the bond of her loyalty. Then Sarra, weeping without shame, coming to take Astrid's hands in her cracked ones — *I pulled you into the world and then I was afraid of you, child, God forgive me.* Then the others, in ones and twos, some with words and some only with a touch, a bow of the head, an offered loaf or jar as though food were the only language equal to the moment. Some held back — the Hendersons at the fence's far end, arms folded, unconvinced or unwilling — and Astrid found she could hold even their doubt gently, because it was only human, and she had been human among them her whole life.

The beasts watched the whole reconciliation with the patient satisfaction of witnesses whose testimony has finally been entered into the record.

Lark had stood through all of it very still beside Astrid. Now she moved forward, into the space the crowd had opened, and looked out at the faces of the valley — these strangers, these soft-spoken farmers with their frost-red hands — and Astrid felt Wren's small fingers slip into hers, gripping hard, waiting for the verdict that mattered most.

"You're Wren's sister," said Old Sarra, kneeling down to Lark's height with the terrible gentleness of a woman who had delivered half the valley. "You don't owe anyone here anything. Not a story, not a smile. But there's a bed going spare at my place, and porridge, and nobody in Hollowvale will ever let the mountain have you back. That's a promise with the whole valley standing surety on it."

Lark looked at Wren. Something passed between them in the Old Tongue, quick and private, a conversation of glances and half-words that needed no voice at all. Then Lark nodded once, and Wren burst into tears and laughter at once, and the geese set up such a racket that the whole gathering dissolved into something that wasn't quite a trial anymore and hadn't quite become a festival but was unmistakably headed that way.

* * *

Evening came down gold and cold. The crowd had thinned to stragglers carrying leftovers; Sarra had marched both girls off to feed them and was refusing all assistance; and Astrid stood alone at the pasture gate watching the light die on the western ridge, beyond which the mountain still crouched, wounded, remembering.

Dunny came and stood beside her, big and warm, his breath stirring her hair.

"You're wondering if it's real," the horse said. "The forgiveness. Whether it'll hold through a hard winter and a bad harvest, when people need someone small to blame."

Astrid shrugged. It was exactly what she wondered.

"Bess says it holds," Dunny said. "She says people believe what they hear spoken plainly, and today they heard it plainly. And besides—" the great head swung round, one dark eye finding her, "—they aren't forgiving you, mostly. They're forgiving themselves. You just gave them somewhere to put it."

Down the slope, Wren came running with a lantern, Lark following more slowly, and behind them the dogs escorting, and the cats supervising from the wall, and the whole living chorus of the valley settling into the dusk around her like a coat being put over her shoulders.

Wren skidded to a stop at the gate, breathless, lantern swinging. "Sarra says there's apple cake," she reported gravely, "and also that you have to come now or Lark eats yours."

Then the child's face changed. She looked past Astrid, east, toward the ward-line, and went still.

Astrid turned.

At the valley's edge, where the ward had flickered and frayed since the day the shard crossed it, the air above the boundary stones was doing something new. It was not closing. It was not opening. It was *shimmering* — the faint silver lattice of her mother's great sacrifice showing itself for the first time in twenty-three years, visible to every eye in the valley, and along its length, one by one, the stones were beginning to sing.

Low. Sweet. Like something enormous waking gently after a long sleep, and turning its attention, for the first time, toward the open world.

Wren's fingers found Astrid's hand.

"Astrid," she whispered. "What's the ward *doing?*"
