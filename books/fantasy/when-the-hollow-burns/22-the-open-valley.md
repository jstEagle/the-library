## 22. The Open Valley

Spring came late to Hollowvale that year, the way it always did — creeping down from the high passes in slow green fingers, finding the frost still hiding in the shadowed folds of the hills. Astrid felt it arrive before she saw it: a stirring in the roots beneath her feet, a low murmur rising through the soil like a conversation resuming after a long sleep. The worms turned. The bees woke grumbling in their hollow trees. And somewhere far below, deeper than roots, deeper than water, something vast and patient shifted its weight and settled into stillness.

The mountain had gone quiet since the court fell. Not dead-quiet — she had learned the difference — but quiet the way a house is quiet after grief has passed through it and left. The sold souls were free. The walls of the Undercourt stood empty, and the root-doors had been sealed again with stone and Sef's oaths and the mountain's own slow anger, which did not need anyone's permission to hold a grudge.

Astrid stood at the edge of the south field with a spade in her hands and the morning fog unraveling around her knees, and she looked at the patch of ground where nothing had ever been planted, because there had never been anything to plant over.

Her mother's grave was unmarked. It had been unmarked for twenty-three years, because you cannot mark a grave when the body is not in it — when what remains is a debt, a silence, a daughter who could speak to beasts but not say why. Astrid had spent most of her life walking past this spot without knowing she was walking on it. Now she knew. She had always known, some animal part of her had known, the way a dog knows where the old bones are buried.

She drove the spade into the earth.

* * *

Wren came out to help, or to hinder; at seven years old the two were difficult to distinguish. The child had grown through the winter like a weed given sudden sun — taller, broader in the shoulder, the feral watchfulness softening into something more ordinary and no less fierce. Wren's hair had been cut at last, Brammel's wife doing the honors with sheep-shears and no ceremony, and without the matted curtain of it the child's face looked startlingly open, the dark eyes quick as a wren's, always moving, always cataloguing.

"Turn," Wren said, holding up a clod of earth for inspection. "Turn means dirt?"

"Soil," Astrid said. Her voice was a shape she made with her hands and her face and the small movements of her throat that still worked, even if they would never again make words. "Dirt is under your nails. Soil is what things grow in."

Wren considered this distinction with the gravity of a scholar. "Soil," the child repeated, and pressed both hands into the turned earth as if to confirm it personally.

They planted in rows because Astrid's mother had planted in rows — she remembered that much, remembered small hands and a woman's larger ones guiding them, remembered the smell of seed-potatoes and the particular way her mother had hummed when she worked, tuneless and content. That memory had come back to her whole from the river-god Vess, returned unasked among the flood of memories the river had loosed when the court's claim broke — Vess had spat them all out like stones from a shoe, every toll ever paid, and Astrid had knelt on the ferrybank and wept over a spring morning thirty years old.

Her mother had sung while she gardened. Off-key. Astrid planted the beans where the singing memory said the beans should go.

The ewes watched from the fence line, as they always watched, and one of them — the gray-faced elder named Sorrel, who had been a lamb the year Astrid first learned her gift — spoke up in the woolly, unhurried voice of sheep everywhere.

*You dig like her,* Sorrel observed. *The quiet one. Same shoulders.*

Astrid paused, spade half-buried. *You remember her?*

*We remember everything,* Sorrel said, mildly offended. *You're the ones who forget.*

That was true, and it was the thing Astrid was still learning to live inside. For twenty-three years the valley's beasts had held their tongues about her — not because they could not speak, but because her mother's bargain had bought their silence too, or rather had woven it into the ward itself, so that any beast who might have betrayed her found the words dying in its throat at the valley's edge. Every crow, every fox, every horse in Hollowvale had carried her secret like a stone in its beak for two decades, and none of them had ever once complained.

Now they talked constantly. It was, Wren declared, much better.

*Deeper,* Sorrel advised, nodding at the bean row. *And mind the wet spot by the fence. The moles say the drainage's gone sideways since the thaw.*

Astrid dug deeper. She minded the wet spot. Somewhere under the fence line, a mole she'd never met took personal satisfaction in being believed.

* * *

By midmorning the fog had burned off and the light lay long and gold across the field, and Wren had abandoned gardening in favor of an education. This was how it went now: the child would point at a thing and wait, and Astrid would give it its name, and Wren would turn the name over like a coin bitten for gold.

"Bird," Wren said, pointing at a robin.

"Robin."

"Robin." A pause. "Not bird?"

"It's a bird. Robin is its name. Like Wren is yours."

Wren stared at the robin with new intensity, clearly deciding whether this constituted kinship. The robin, digging for worms three feet away, regarded the child with equal suspicion. Then, because the world had changed and the robin seemed to know it:

*Good morning,* the robin said. *Your soil smells turned. Are there grubs?*

"There might be grubs," Astrid signed, and Wren shrieked with delight and began excavating the bean row with both hands, which put an end to the beans but produced, within minutes, four fat grubs and one extremely satisfied robin perched on Wren's head.

This was the lesson plan now. Names for things, and then the things themselves speaking up to correct the pronunciation. Wren was learning the valley's language twice over — the human words and the older ones underneath — and moved between them with the ease of someone who had never been taught that languages were supposed to have borders. The Old Tongue sat in the child like a second heartbeat. Some nights Astrid woke to hear Wren talking in sleep, and the words were neither the valley tongue nor the court's iron speech but something flowing and green and very old, and the beasts outside the window would go still to listen.

The shard was gone. Shattered against the Soulkeeper's throne, its fragment released, its long terrible work finished. But Astrid sometimes thought the shard had been teaching Wren all along — that every vision it showed, every word it hummed, had been a lesson disguised as a wound. The child had spoken the unbinding before the throne could finish its hunger. Whatever else the Undercourt had made, it had also, without meaning to, made the instrument of its own ending.

Astrid turned another row and let herself feel, for the thousandth time, the shape of that morning: the throne room shaking, souls streaming upward through cracks in the iron like light through a broken roof, and Wren standing small and blazing in the wreckage, saying words no seven-year-old should know in a voice that was not afraid.

Then she put the feeling down, the way she'd learned to, and kept digging.

* * *

In the afternoon she walked the boundary.

It was a habit now, three weeks old. Every day she walked the ward's edge — the invisible line where her mother's soul had held the world shut for twenty-three years — and every day she tested it, gently, the way you test a healing bone.

The ward was thin. Everyone could see it now, or feel it: a shimmer like heat-haze over cold ground, a faint taste of copper on the air. Brammel's stones, the great warding cairns set at the valley's mouths generations ago, had gone dim as banked coals. Magic no longer died at the border. It slowed there, hesitated, drifted through like smoke through a cracked window. The valley was becoming an ordinary place, which was to say: a place where extraordinary things could happen and be survived.

Brammel hated it. Astrid had watched him walk his rounds with his ledger and his grim mouth, cataloguing each flicker, each softening, each wildflower blooming out of season near the cairns, and she had watched him write less and less as the weeks went on. His book of infractions was nearly empty. There was nothing left to enforce against. The law he served had been built to contain a threat that no longer existed, and a man can stand guard over an empty room only so long before he has to ask himself what he is.

He came to her door sometimes. He would sit across the table, drink tea he didn't want, look at her throat — at the scar the Soulkeeper's claiming had left, a thin pale line like a necklace of wire — and struggle visibly with the fact that she could not answer his questions and no longer needed to.

Yesterday he had asked, in a voice rougher than usual: "If I'd found out, twenty years ago. If I'd caught you. What would you have done?"

She had answered honestly, in signs, in the direct plain grammar her hands had developed: *Burned. Or run. One of those.*

"And now?"

*Now you'd have to catch me first,* she'd signed, and after a long moment Brammel had laughed — actually laughed, a startled bark of it, like a man discovering a room in his own house — and finished his tea, and left.

Today, at the western cairn, she stopped and stepped across.

There was no resistance. No wall of dead air, no sickening drain. She simply walked from inside the valley to outside it, and the magic of the wide world rose to meet her like warm water — the beasts' voices swelling from a murmur to a chorus, hawks and voles and a distant red deer and something huge and slow-thinking turning over in its winter den beneath the ridge. They all knew her name. They had all known her name for years, carried along the wind from beyond the ward by cousins and rivals and passing birds, and now at last they could say it to her face.

*Astrid Lockridge,* said the ridge itself, in the voice of stone and lichen, deep enough to feel in her teeth. *The silent one. The door-opener. Welcome outside.*

She stood with one foot in either world and breathed.

This was the thing she could not explain to Brammel, and did not need to: that she had expected to feel naked without the ward, exposed, and instead she felt *finished*. Her mother had built a box around her life and called it safety, and it had been safety, of a kind — the safety of a jewel in a locked drawer, of a letter never sent. But a door was not a wall. A door could open. And what her mother had actually paid for, Astrid understood now, standing in the thinning shimmer of the last ward she would ever hide behind, was not a cage. It had only become one because everyone involved had forgotten what doors were for.

*I'm here,* she told the ridge, and meant all of it: I'm here, I'm outside, I stayed anyway, I came back, I am not hiding anymore.

*We know,* said the ridge, and the hill foxes yipped somewhere above, and the wind came down off the mountain smelling of pine and thaw and the far green sea of grass beyond the valley, and it smelled like beginning.

* * *

She planted the last of the garden at dusk.

Over the grave itself — the true center of it, the place her hands had always known — she planted rue and rosemary, for remembrance, and a single rowan sapling whose roots she had carried down from the high slopes in a sack of its native soil. Rowan kept away ill-wishing, her mother had used to say. Astrid suspected her mother had said it the way people say such things: as a spell dressed as a proverb, a small working hidden in plain sight, the way she must have hidden so much else. The woman had lived a whole secret life inside an ordinary one, and had never once let it show, and had paid for her daughter with everything she was, and had never once said so.

Astrid pressed the soil down around the rowan's roots with her palms flat, the way you press a hand to a sleeping child's back, and she did not weep. She had wept enough for the buying. This was for the living.

*She'd have liked the beans in that corner,* Sorrel offered from the fence, chewing. *More sun.*

*Next year,* Astrid agreed.

*There will be a next year?*

Astrid looked up. The ewe's eyes were mild and ancient and genuinely asking, because sheep did not assume tomorrow the way people did; they knew better.

*There will be many next years,* Astrid signed, and then, because Sorrel's eyesight was poor and her sign-language worse, she reached out along the old bright thread and said it in the way that needed no hands: *Many. As many as the valley wants to give us.*

The valley, listening — and the valley was always listening now — gave a long slow sigh through its grasses that might have been laughter.

Behind her, the cottage door banged open and Wren came running down the path with a lantern and a piece of bread and butter held carefully in both fists, calling words that were almost right: "Astrid! Astrid, Brammel comes! Brammel comes up the road!" A pause, self-correcting. "*Is coming.* Brammel is coming."

Astrid straightened, brushing soil from her knees.

Down the lane, past the darkening fields, a single figure climbed toward the cottage with a lantern of his own and his ledger under his arm, and even at this distance she could read him the way she read weather: the set of the shoulders, the deliberate pace of a man coming to say something he had rehearsed and was terrified of. Behind him, at the valley's southern mouth, the old ward-stones flickered — once, twice — like a lamp guttering, or like a wink.

The beasts along the fence line lifted their heads, all together, and waited with her.

Brammel stopped at the garden gate. He looked at the fresh-turned rows, the rowan sapling, the child on the path, the mute woman with dirt to her elbows, and he opened his ledger — and then closed it again, and set it down on the gatepost, and left it there.

"Astrid," he said, and his voice was strange, careful, like a man stepping onto ice. "I need to tell you what I've decided to do about the ward."

Far off, at the edge of hearing, something in the high passes answered the flickering stones with a sound that was not quite thunder, and every beast in Hollowvale turned its head toward the mountains at once.
