## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The trouble with living in a valley where magic goes to die is that it makes an excellent hiding place, provided you don't mind the company of people who have also come here to hide from things.

I mind. I've minded for seven years. But I mind quietly, which is the whole trick of it.

My name is Felix Ravensworth, and tomorrow I turn thirty, and I have spent the better part of a decade pretending that this fact — that I have *survived* to thirty — is not itself a small miracle requiring explanation. In Hollow Vale, nobody asks for explanations. The Vale asks nothing of anyone. It sits at the bottom of the world like a cupped hand, ringed by hills that drink the weather before it can arrive, and inside its borders nothing unnatural has happened in living memory. Crops grow because farmers grow them. Rain falls when the sky feels like it. And men like me can stand in a field with a storm howling under their skin and feel, for once, only silence.

Silence. You get so you treasure it the way misers treasure coin, counting it every night, terrified of finding one piece missing.

I was counting mine on the morning of the peddler's arrival, standing in my garden with dirt to the elbows, deciding whether the beans had another week in them or whether the frost — such frost as the Vale permits — would take them first. Ordinary work. Ordinary morning. I had built my life out of ordinary mornings the way other men build cathedrals out of stone: patiently, obsessively, one at a time, until the structure could hold up something enormous.

Something enormous being, specifically, me.

You should understand what I mean when I say storm-calling, because I will be using the word sparingly and lying about it often. I do not mean I can make it rain. I mean that there is weather in me — has been since birth, though it woke properly the night my family died — a pressure behind the ribs, a taste of ozone on the back of the tongue, a hum in the teeth that never entirely stops. When I was young and stupid I thought of it as a gift. Then one night in Ravensworth Hall it came out of me all at once, unasked, and took the roof off, and the lightning found my mother's room, and my father's, and my sister Liss's, and after that I stopped thinking of it as anything but what it was: a debt collector wearing my skin.

So I ran. Everyone said no one survived that fire. Convenient. I let them believe it and walked south until the hills closed over me, and when I crossed into Hollow Vale I felt the hum gutter out like a lamp pinched between wet fingers, and I wept in the road like a fool, and then I got up and found a cottage with a leaky roof and began the long business of forgetting.

That was the plan. Forget, tend beans, turn thirty, die eventually of something boring.

The peddler came at midday, which was the first strange thing. Peddlers came to the Vale perhaps twice a year, and they always came at dusk, trundling down the pass road while the light still held. This one arrived under a high white sun that made his cart look bleached as bone. He was old the way driftwood is old — grey, wind-cured, hard to guess at — and he had a coat hung with ribbons and charms that, I noticed with a lurch, did absolutely nothing. Dead charms. The Vale ate their magic at the border same as it ate everything else, and he wore them anyway, rattling, like a man carrying keys to a house that burned down.

"Good day," he called, in a voice like gravel poured slow. "Buying or selling?"

"Selling," I said, because I had eggs and he looked like a man who'd pay for eggs.

He bought the eggs. He haggled, too, which comforted me — magic doesn't haggle, it just takes — and while we argued over two coppers he kept looking at me. Not rudely. The way a man looks at a door he half-remembers closing.

"You're the one as keeps to himself," he said. "Up past the mill."

"I'm the one selling eggs."

"Mm." He dug in his cart and produced a book. It was thick and swollen with damp, bound in leather gone the colour of old tea, and its pages were edged in gilt that had mostly flaked away. "Prophecies," he said. "A century old, near enough. Written by some mad monk in the mountains who saw things, they say. Won't take much of your coin."

"I don't want it."

"Didn't say you did." He set it on the fence post between us, casual as a man setting down a jug. "Said it wouldn't cost much. Different thing entirely."

I should have walked away. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone: Felix Ravensworth knew, in the marrow of him, that he should walk away. There are moments when dread arrives ahead of its reason, the way thunder comes before you see the lightning's branch, and every hair on my arms stood up and told me *leave*.

But the book lay there with its ruined gilt, and I was thirty years old tomorrow, and thirty years of curiosity is a heavy thing to carry uphill.

I opened it.

* * *

The script was cramped and brown, the ink of a man writing fast by bad light. Most of it was gibberish to me — dates and names of dead kings, verses about crowns and rivers and a hundred years of ash. I turned the pages quickly, meaning to put it down, meaning to say *no thank you* and go back to my beans.

Then I turned a page and the ink moved.

Not flowed. Not smeared. *Moved*, the way a sleeper turns over — a settling, a rearrangement, letters sliding into new places with a soft sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard. I stood very still in my own garden, on my own side of the border where magic is supposed to be dead, and watched fresh words surface through the paper like bodies rising in a pond.

*On the eve of his thirtieth year,* the page read, *the storm returns to the kingdom that bound it.*

Below that, smaller:

*Felix Ravensworth walks out of the Vale tonight, or is thrown.*

I dropped the book. It landed face-up in the bean rows, insolent as a cat, and the words stayed exactly where they were. My name. My name in ink a century old, in a book written by a madman dead before my grandfather drew breath, sitting in my garden among my beans.

"That's not possible," I told it conversationally, because talking to books is more sane than screaming at them.

"Isn't it?" said the peddler. He hadn't moved. The sun seemed dimmer than it had been, or maybe that was just the blood leaving my head. "Vale eats magic, friend. Always has. Keeps everything inside nice and quiet and dead." He tapped his temple. "But quiet isn't the same as empty. A valley that forgets — well. Things forgotten have a way of coming round the back way when they want finding."

"What are you?" I asked. "What *is* this?"

"A seller of prophecies." He gathered his reins. His charms clacked together, mute and hollow. "And a reminder, I suppose. We all serve somebody's purpose, friend. Even the ones who ran."

He rolled out past the mill without looking back. I let him go. I stood in the road until the cart-sound faded, and then I went and picked the book out of the beans, because apparently I was the sort of idiot who picked prophecy books out of bean rows, and carried it inside, and sat with it at my table while the light went long and gold across the floor.

* * *

Here is what I know about the border of Hollow Vale, from seven years of testing it the way a prisoner tests bars: it is a line you cannot feel until you cross it. Step over, and the world changes texture. Outside, colors are louder, sounds carry wrong, and if you have weather in you, the hum comes roaring back like a dog hearing its name. Inside, nothing. I crossed twice in the early years, stood one boot-length over the line just to feel the storm wake in me, hungry and enormous, and stepped back and let the Vale swallow it again. I told myself that proved I was master of it.

It proved I was afraid of it. Those aren't the same thing, whatever you tell yourself at night.

By dusk the book had done nothing further, which I had almost managed to convince myself meant it was finished, spent, a trick played out. I lit my lamp. I made tea. I told myself tomorrow I would be thirty and it would be an ordinary day, because the Vale forgets, the Vale is kind, the Vale lets a man bury things so deep even he can't find them.

Then the words started rewriting themselves again.

I watched it happen this time instead of dropping the book. Letters unstitched themselves and re-formed, patient as spiders, and new lines surfaced between the old ones, and the page filled the way a wound fills with blood:

*Tonight. Tonight. Tonight.*

And beneath that, in ink that glistened wet:

*The border remembers what the valley forgot. It is waking. It knows what is owed, and who must pay, and where he sleeps.*

The lamp guttered though there was no draft. Under my skin, in the sealed cellar where I keep the storm, something knocked. Once. Twice. Like a fist on a door from the inside. Seven years of perfect silence, and the hum rose in my teeth like water coming to boil, and outside my window the evening sky flickered — flickered, in the Vale, where the sky does *nothing* — with a green-white light that had no source and no thunder following it.

I got up. I don't remember deciding to; my body simply went, walking me out the door and down the path toward the border as though pulled on a line. The book came with me, tucked under my arm, warm as a living thing. Behind me the cottage sat dark and ordinary and already beginning to look like something I was remembering rather than something I owned.

The border ran along the old sheep track above the mill. I reached it as full night fell, and stood with one boot on the Vale side and one hovering over the line, and the storm in my chest pressed against its cage and *sang*.

"No," I said aloud, to the book, to the sky, to whatever century-old madman was listening. "I'm not going anywhere. This is my valley. I paid for this valley."

The border shuddered.

There's no honest way to describe it except that the air itself shivered like a struck drumhead, and the hills around me leaned — I swear it, they *leaned*, a great slow tilt like the walls of a room narrowing — and the book under my arm grew hot, and the last line surfaced on its open page in letters of burning gold:

*Thrown,* it said. *I did warn him.*

The valley expelled me the way a body rejects poison — violently, completely, and with a sound like the world clearing its throat — and the last thing I saw of Hollow Vale was my own lamplit window going out.

Then the ground left, the sky turned the wrong colour, and Felix Ravensworth's seven quiet years ended all at once, somewhere far below me, in the dark.
