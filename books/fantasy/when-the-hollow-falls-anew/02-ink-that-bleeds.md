## 2. Ink That Bleeds

I want it on the record that I did not touch the book. Not at first. I set it on my kitchen table the way you'd set down a sleeping adder, and I backed away from it, and I put the kettle on, because in Hollow Vale the answer to every crisis is tea. It had been the answer to every crisis for seven years. Crisis, apparently, had finally learned to read a calendar.

The peddler was long gone. Of course he was. Men who sell books with your death written in them do not linger for pleasantries. I'd watched him walk his cart up the eastern road until the mist took him, and I'd stood there like an idiot holding two coppers he hadn't asked for, because something in me had needed to *pay*, to make the transaction ordinary, to make it commerce instead of omen.

Now the book sat on my table and breathed.

That's not a figure of speech. Well. It's not only a figure of speech. The cover rose and fell, faintly, the way a chest rises when someone is sleeping off a fever and deciding whether to wake. The leather had gone from road-dust brown to a deep arterial red since I'd carried it inside, and the gold lettering on the spine — *THE HOLLOW FALLS ANEW* — had begun, very quietly, to drip.

Ink. Gold ink. Down onto my shelf of preserves, where it pooled around a jar of blackcurrant like a halo.

"Absolutely not," I told it.

Books, in my experience, respond poorly to firm boundaries. This one responded by opening itself.

The pages turned without wind, each one landing flat with a sound like a tongue clicking against a palate, disapproving, and stopped at the page I had seen in the lane. My name. *Felix Ravensworth.* Tonight's date. And beneath them, the line that had sent the kettle-whistle shrieking unattended while my ears filled with the sea:

*On this night the storm returns to the kingdom that made it, and the Hollow King falls anew.*

Except — and here is where I would like to lodge a formal complaint with whatever gods handle prophecy — the line was moving. The words crawled across the page like ants disturbed under a stone, rearranging, unmaking themselves, and I watched letters peel up out of the parchment and reassemble into new sentences while my stomach performed maneuvers I hadn't attempted since my last attempt at magic.

*the storm returns* became *the storm is summoned*. Then: *the storm is owed*. The word *falls* flickered, hesitated between *falls* and *rises* and *is unbound*, cycling like a coin that couldn't decide which face to land on. And underneath it all, in smaller and smaller script, as though the book were muttering to itself, lines were accreting that I had not read in the lane, that the peddler had certainly not shown me:

*bargain of blood, bargain kept*
*seven years the debt has slept*
*ravensworth, ravensworth, come home, come home—*

My family's name. Sung, almost, in meter, in the rhythm of a skipping rhyme children chant over cobblestones. A century old. Written before my grandfather's grandfather had learned to walk, and it knew me. It knew what I was. It knew what I'd done, or what had been done through me, the night the sky came down on Ravenmoor Hall and took my mother's voice mid-word and my father's hands mid-reach and Tammy —

It knew about Tammy.

"No," I said again, and this time my voice cracked in the middle like young ice, and I crossed the room and put my hand flat on the page to hold it still, to pin it shut, to do *something*, because seven years of stillness were unraveling in front of me and I have never in my life been able to leave a broken thing alone.

The moment skin met parchment, every candle in the house went out.

Not guttered. *Out.* Snuffed, all six, simultaneously, and the fire in the hearth dropped to embers the color of old bruises, and the light through the windows turned strange and thick, like looking up from underwater. The valley's border. I knew it the way you know a change in pressure before a storm — in my teeth, in the scar tissue of my magic, in the hum beneath my breastbone that I had spent seven years teaching to be quiet. That border was the whole reason I'd chosen Hollow Vale. Magic died at its edges. Walked in carrying a gift and walked out ordinary, or stayed in carrying a gift and let it starve slowly, politely, in the dark. Seven years of slow starvation. Seven years of quiet.

And the quiet had noticed me touching the book.

The page warmed under my palm. Then it *heated*, fast, past comfortable, past painful, and the ink beneath my fingers wasn't ink anymore but something moving, something swimming, and the words rose up against my skin like fish rising to feed:

*come home, come home*

"I don't *have* a home," I snarled at it, which was a lie so large the whole house should have collapsed from the weight of it, because I had built one, plank by plank and lie by lie, a cottage and a garden and a routine worn smooth as river stone, and I loved it, God help me, I loved the small gray safety of it more than I had ever admitted to anyone, including myself, especially myself.

The border decided the argument.

I felt it happen rather than saw it — a shudder running through the floor, the walls, the ground itself, like the valley was a dog shaking off rain. Hollow Vale had never expelled anything in living memory; things came *in*, broken magics seeking sanctuary, and settled. But the book was magic, and I was touching the book, and the valley's law was absolute: *magic does not pass our borders.*

It cannot follow me out.

But it can throw me.

I have been struck by lightning once in my life, deliberately, as a test, at fifteen, standing on my father's highest tower with my arms out like a fool prince in a storybook. I remember it as bright, and brief, and total. Being thrown through the border of Hollow Vale was none of those things. It was long. It lasted years, or seconds, or both, and in it I felt every thread of the quiet life I'd spun get ripped off me one at a time — the garden, the kettle, the neighbor Marta who left eggs on my step and asked no questions, the shelf of preserves with its halo of golden ink — torn away and left behind on the far side of a line I could feel closing behind me like water over a drowned man.

The last thing I heard from the valley was the book, still open on my table, still singing.

The next thing I knew was grass. Actual grass, wet, against my cheek, and the smell of turned earth and horse, and somewhere distant a bell tolling with the smug confidence of a bell that believes it is announcing good news.

*

I lay there a while. There is no shame in this. I have read accounts of shipwreck survivors who cling to spars for days, and no one writes essays about how they should have shown more initiative.

When I finally rolled over, the sky was wrong.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was the one thing my father taught me that survived everything else: the sky was not merely unfamiliar, it was *incorrect*. Hollow Vale's sky was gray, reliably, eternally gray, the gray of wool and forgetting. This sky was the color of tarnished silver, shot through with veins of dull copper, and it moved. Not the clouds — the light itself moved, crawling eastward in slow ripples like heat-shimmer over a forge, though the air was cold enough to bite. Above me, where the sun should have been hanging low and fat in late afternoon, there hung instead a pale ring, a hole punched in the tarnish, and through the hole I could see — I am going to write this and then never say it aloud — stars. At four in the afternoon. Stars burning coldly in a patch of proper night, as if the day were a curtain someone had left slightly askew.

"Well," I said, to the grass, to the horses, to nobody. "That's not ideal."

The horses belonged to a plowman three fields over who had, credit where due, not fled screaming at the sight of a mud-caked stranger falling out of nowhere. He had simply stopped his plow, folded his arms, and waited with the weary patience of a man who lived near a capital city and had therefore seen everything twice. When I staggered upright and brushed myself off, he jerked his chin at my person and said, in an accent rounder than any I'd heard in the vale:

"Fell through the Veil, did you?"

"The — possibly. Where am I?"

"Veymar." He said it the way you'd say *you're standing in it, use your eyes*. "Kingdom of. You're on the king's road not two miles from Ashfall, so mind yourself tonight — whole city's gone mad for tomorrow. Centennial, see."

"Centennial of what?"

He looked at me the way countrymen everywhere look at city fools and their cousins, strangers who fall out of the sky.

"Coronation," he said. "Hundred years to the day since the Hollow King took the throne. Greatest celebration in Veymar's history, they're saying. Criers have been at it since dawn."

As if summoned — and given the week I was having, it probably was — the sound reached us across the fields. A voice, thin and reedy with distance, rising from the direction of the road, pitched in the practiced cadence of official announcement:

*"…one hundred years of grace! One hundred years of the Hollow King's mercy! Come all ye faithful to Ashfall tomorrow, when His Majesty takes anew the crown that never ages, the crown that feeds us all—"*

*The crown that feeds us all.*

Something under my breastbone — the thing I had starved for seven years, the thing I had buried so deep I'd convinced myself it was dead — shifted in its sleep. Turned over. Opened one eye.

And far to the east, past the plowman, past the road, past hills I didn't know, I felt it answer. A pull, vast and patient and hungry, like the tide noticing a single grain of sand has moved.

The crier's voice faded. The plowman clicked to his horses. And I stood on the king's road in the wrong-colored sky with nothing — no book, no coin, no coat worth the name, no valley, no home — while somewhere behind me, a hundred miles of magic-dead mist sealed itself against my kind forever.

Seven years I had spent building a cage and calling it shelter. It had taken the cage less than a minute to throw me out of it.

Ahead, Ashfall's towers broke the horizon, crowned in banners, and every banner bore the same device: a circlet of black iron, empty.

Waiting, some treacherous part of me whispered, to be filled.

I started walking. What else does a storm do, when it finally finds the sea?
