## 4. A Voice Under the Stones

Wren Ashvale had a talent for making terrible ideas sound like administrative procedure.

"The undercroft access is a matter of routine maintenance," she said, pressing a rusted key into my palm as we stood in the shadow of the palace's east stair. "Every archivist inspects the lower collections quarterly."

"Quarterly," I repeated. "And when was the last inspection?"

"Forty-one years ago." She adjusted the satchel of confiscated books on her hip and started down the steps without waiting to see if I followed. Which was, I was learning, her way of expressing confidence in me.

I looked at the key. I looked at the dark mouth of the stairwell breathing cold air up at me like something patient. Seven years ago I'd have said no. Six years and eleven months ago, too — grief keeps a strict calendar. But tonight's date had been inked in a book a century before I was born, and my name sat on that page like a debt collector's signature, so apparently my opinion was no longer a required element of any decision involving me.

The stairs went down farther than stairs have any right to go. The palace above was all gilt and trumpets tonight — centennial coronation preparations, criers shouting the Hollow King's hundred years of glory from every corner until the words wore smooth as river stones. Down here there was only stone, and cold, and a silence so complete I could hear my own pulse complaining about the exercise.

"You're quiet," Wren said, somewhere ahead of me with a hand-lamp.

"I'm rationing words. They've been getting me into trouble lately."

"Mm. My condolences." Her light swung left. "The bound king is supposed to be beneath the foundation vault. If Aldric lives — if he *is* — this is where a hundred years of him would be."

"And you believe that. Truly."

She stopped. In the lamplight her face did something complicated. "I lost my rank, my rooms, my pension, and the friendship of every person I respected over this belief. Belief is the wrong word. Belief is what you do with things you can afford to doubt." She turned back to the dark. "I know it the way you know a load-bearing wall. You don't get to stop knowing it just because it's inconvenient."

I decided not to mention that I'd once known, with equal certainty, that exile would keep me safe. We were both carrying our own collapsed architecture.

* * *

We had been walking perhaps an hour when I heard the singing.

It came through the stones themselves — no corridor led toward it, no door stood near it. It simply arrived, thin and high, threading through the rock like water through chalk. A child's voice, singing a tune I didn't know and then, horribly, one I did. A lullaby my mother used to hum while she mended nets back home, before home became a word for a place where everyone I loved had burned.

My feet stopped working. That happens sometimes. The body has its own archives.

"Felix?" Wren's lamp swung back toward me.

"That song." My voice came out strange. Flat. Like someone else borrowing my throat. "That song is from Hollow Vale. It isn't sung anywhere else. I'd stake the seven years I wasted on it."

"Then either your valley travels," Wren said slowly, "or whatever's singing learned it from someone who did."

The singing stopped. In the ringing quiet after it, another voice spoke — and I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the one luxury exile never afforded me: the voice did not come through the stones. It came *from* them. From everywhere. It was deep and worn and gentle the way old rope is gentle, frayed soft by long use, and it said:

"*Storm-child.*"

Every hair on my arms stood up and paid attention.

"Do you hear that?" I whispered.

"Hear what?" Wren was already moving, lamp raised, archivist instincts outrunning her caution. "Felix, if you're hearing voices in a sealed undercroft, I need you to describe them *in detail*, because—"

"*Not her,*" the voice said, amused, tired. "*You. The storm-child who ran. Come closer. The stones are thick here, but for you they will listen.*"

I should have run. Let the record show I considered running. I considered it with real enthusiasm. But the voice had said *ran*, and there was a whole lifetime folded into that small word, and my legs, traitorous things, carried me toward it instead.

* * *

The passage opened into a chamber that no architect would claim.

It wasn't grand. That was the first shock. I'd expected — I don't know what. A throne of chains. Sorcery written in fire. Instead: a low vaulted room, older than the palace around it, its walls crowded with copper rods driven into the stone like enormous nails, each one etched with script that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts if you're foolish enough to try. The rods hummed. Not sound exactly — more the memory of sound, a vibration that lived in my teeth.

At the chamber's far side, sitting cross-legged on the floor beside a crack in the wall, was a child.

Small, maybe eight or nine. Scullery livery three sizes too big, sleeves rolled into cuffs at the elbows, a smudge of soot across one cheek like a thumbprint left by some careless god. The kid looked up at me with entirely unimpressed brown eyes and said, "Took you long enough. He's been asking about you since Tuesday."

"...Tuesday," I managed.

"King's voice says time goes funny down here." The child shrugged with the boneless indifference of the professionally unbothered. "I'm Pip. You're the storm man. He told me you'd come smelling like rain even though it hasn't rained in weeks, and he was right, which is annoying, because now he'll be smug about it for a century."

"Pip," Wren breathed behind me. She'd gone very still. "The scullery records list a Pip among the foundlings. Filed under — under behavioral irregularities. They keep trying to move the post assignments away from the east wing and the child keeps ending up back here."

"It's not my fault the corridors are confusing," Pip said with dignity.

The humming of the copper rods shifted. Deepened. And the voice came again, and this time it filled the room the way a tide fills a cave, and I felt it against my sternum like a second heartbeat:

"*Seven years you hid in the dead valley. Did you truly think that was chance?*"

The question landed in me like a hook finding meat.

"What do you mean?"

"*Hollow Vale does not forget, storm-child. Hollow Vale was made.* Your valley, your borders where magic dies — do you imagine nature drew that line? It was drawn by men. By Ravensworths. Generations of them, feeding their gift into the binding that holds me, until the last of them had nothing left but the residue — the echo of the bargain — and the sense, passed down like a cursed heirloom, that they were being *kept*.*

"My mother—" My mouth went dry. "My mother said our family came to the Vale because of the storms. Because of *me*. She said—"

"*She believed it. They all believed it. That is the cruelest engineering — the kind that builds the cage and then teaches the bird it chose the bars.*" The voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "*Your great-grandfather made a bargain with the thing that wears my face. His line would carry the binding's anchor in their blood. And when the anchor grew too heavy to hide — when the storm-calling bloomed too bright — the valley would spit them out. Or swallow them. Whichever the impostor required.*

"*The night your family died, it was not your gift that failed. It was the binding, feeding. It drank your storm and called it accident, and it let you live because a grieving survivor runs farther than a dead martyr — and a survivor in exile is a hook it can pull whenever it needs one.*

"*Tonight it needs one.*"

I sat down. There was no dignity available; I simply stopped being upright. The stone was cold through my clothes and I was grateful for it, grateful for anything solid, because everything else had just been pulled out from under me like a tablecloth at a magician's dinner.

Seven years. Seven years of rationed candles and counted coins and flinching at thunder, of building a small life so carefully, brick by brick, believing I had chosen it — chosen safety, chosen penance, chosen the slow gray mercy of a place where magic went to die. And none of it had been choice. It had been *design*. I was not a man who had fled. I was a tool that had been *stored*.

"Felix." Wren crouched beside me. For once she didn't reach for a book or an argument. She just said my name, twice, like knocking on a door.

"He knew," I heard myself say. "The impostor. It's known where I was the whole time. It *put* me there."

"Yes." No comfort in it, no cushioning. Wren Ashvale delivered truth the way executioners deliver sentences: promptly, and looking you in the eye. I was starting to find that less horrifying than kindness, actually. Kindness would have broken me right then.

"*Now you understand why you are named in the book,*" the king said. "*The prophecy is not a prediction, storm-child. Prophecies are receipts. The bargain wrote itself down a century ago, and the ink waits for its date.*"

Pip had crawled closer during all this and was now studying me with frank, unnerving attention, the way children study beetles. "He sounds sad," Pip reported, jerking a chin upward — at the walls, at the rods, at the presence soaked into all of it. "The king, I mean. He's been sad for a hundred years, but this is a different sad. This is the kind where somebody finally showed up and he doesn't know whether to hope or not."

"Pip," I said. My voice cracked on the single syllable. I tried again. "Why can you hear him?"

"Dunno." Pip shrugged. "Foundlings hear lots of things. Walls talk if you're small enough and nobody important enough to matter. Nobody ever tells a scullery brat secrets worth keeping, so the walls figure it's safe." A pause. Then, quieter, with an oldness that didn't belong to that young face: "He gets lonely. So I sing."

The lullaby. From the Vale. My mother's song.

"How do you know that song?" I asked.

Pip picked at a thread on the oversized sleeve. "He taught me. Says his mother sang it to him, before." A frown. "But he also said a lady in a far valley sang it, and that she'd be your mother, and that you'd come on a night with no rain and smell like a storm anyway. I thought he was being silly." Pip looked up at me. "He's never silly."

Above us — far above, filtered through a hundred feet of palace and ceremony — a bell began to ring. Then another. The coronation feast bells, calling the court to celebrate a hundred years of a lie.

The copper rods shuddered. The king's voice, when it came again, had gone taut as wire:

"*They are renewing him tonight, storm-child. A hundred years is a hunger, and hunger is patient only until it isn't. When the impostor feeds tonight, the binding will strain — and the binding will call its anchor home. It will reach for you through every mile between here and your dead valley, and it will not ask politely.*

"*So choose now, Felix Ravensworth. Run, and be dragged. Or walk, and be weapon.*"

Pip reached out and patted my knee, once, briskly, the way one comforts a large frightened animal.

"For what it's worth," the child said, "he thinks you're going to stay. He said you've got your father's stubborn and your mother's temper and neither one of those knows how to run when it counts." A beat. "Also he says the archivist should stop eavesdropping and write this part down, because nobody's taken minutes down here in a century and honestly it's becoming a problem."

Behind me, there was the unmistakable sound of Wren Ashvale uncapping an inkpot with shaking hands.

I looked up at the humming rods, at the dark that held a king, at a hundred years of patience balanced on the edge of a bell-rung night — and I felt, beneath my skin, the storm answer the binding's distant pull like a dog hearing its name.

It was going to hurt. Some part of me, the part that had survived the burning, already knew exactly how much.

"All right," I said to the walls. "Tell me what I have to do."
