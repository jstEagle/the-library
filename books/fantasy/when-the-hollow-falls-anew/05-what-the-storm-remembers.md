## 5. What the Storm Remembers

The first thing you should know about calling a storm is that it never comes alone. It brings its cousins. Its grudges. Every storm you've ever swallowed, every one your mother swallowed before you, all of them queued up behind the one you asked for, patient as creditors.

I didn't know that yet. What I knew was that Wren Ashvale had dragged me to the top of a hill outside Veymar's walls at dawn, planted my feet in the wet grass, and said, "Now."

"Now what?"

"Now you do the thing you've spent seven years pretending you can't do."

"That's not how pretending works," I said. "You can't just unpick it like a knot. Seven years is—"

"Seven years is exactly long enough," she said, "for the debt to get interesting."

She had this way of talking about magic the way moneylenders talk about interest, which should have been funny and instead made my stomach drop through the hill into whatever waited beneath it. She'd brought me here because the palace undercrofts had too many ears, and because Pip was still asleep in the laundry loft where we'd stowed him, and because — though she'd never say it plainly — because she was afraid of what would happen if I *didn't* try. A storm-caller who never calls, she'd told me the night before, is a dam with no spillway. The water doesn't stop rising. It just finds the weakest wall.

I knew about weak walls. I'd grown up inside one.

"Close your eyes," Wren said. "Don't reach for the sky. That's the mistake everyone makes — they think calling a storm is pulling. It isn't. You're not hauling anything down. You're opening a door that's already there and letting something through that's already coming."

"And if I don't want it to come?"

"Then don't open the door," she said. "But Felix." Her voice did something then, softened by maybe a degree, which for Wren was the equivalent of weeping openly. "It's going to come anyway. Sooner or later, on its own terms or yours. Better yours."

I closed my eyes.

Here is what seven years of suppression actually feels like, for those keeping score at home: it feels like holding your breath underwater while someone above you keeps dropping stones. The valley had helped — Hollow Vale eats magic, that was the whole point of it, the whole reason I'd chosen it out of a peddler's atlas without knowing why the name pulled at me like a hook behind the sternum. But the storm hadn't died in there. Nothing dies in Hollow Vale. It had just gotten very, very quiet, the way a thing gets quiet when it's waiting.

So I opened the door.

Not far. A crack. The width of a thought.

The sky answered before the wind did. There was a pressure change, a taste of iron and green rain at the back of my throat, and then the hum beneath my skin — the hum I'd spent seven years smothering under routine and turnips and the particular silence of a valley that forgets — rose up and *recognized* the opening, and went through it like water finding a breach.

I got maybe four seconds of the good kind of power. Four seconds of feeling enormous, of feeling like the weather and the man were finally the same shape. And then everything I had ever suppressed came through the door at once, because doors don't check credentials, and the storm remembered every year I'd starved it.

* * *

I have been told what happened next. I remember almost none of it. Memory is a courtesy the mind extends when it thinks you'll survive; mine looked at the situation and declined.

Wren says the clouds came down off the hills like something hunting. She says lightning struck the same oak three times, and the third time the tree didn't burn so much as *come apart*, politely, along lines no axe had ever drawn. She says she grabbed my collar and shouted my name and I turned around with eyes full of white light and no one home behind them, and that when she touched my arm the current went through her and threw her ten feet down the slope, and that she got up anyway, because apparently that's who Wren Ashvale is.

What I remember — the only piece, one frame, like a single surviving page from a burned book — is my mother's kitchen.

I am fourteen again. It is raining the way it only rains the night something terrible is being decided somewhere. My father has his hand flat on the table between us, and my mother is saying my name in the voice you use for a person standing on a ledge, and I am trying to tell them I'm sorry, I'm trying to tell them I didn't mean to, and the lightning is coming *through the windows inward*, which is wrong, which is backwards, which is the storm eating the house instead of striking it, and I understand even then, even at fourteen, that the storm isn't angry at me. It's *hungry*. Something taught it hunger. Something taught it that Ravensworth storms are meals.

Then the frame ends. Then there is grass against my cheek and Wren's voice saying my name like she's said it forty times already and will say it forty more if that's what it takes, and rain falling on us both, ordinary rain now, exhausted rain, the storm having eaten its fill and wandered off over the hills like a dog that got into the pantry.

I lay there and shook.

"Felix." Wren knelt beside me, soaked through, a bruise already purpling along her jaw where a branch had caught her. "Look at me. Are you back? All of you?"

"I think so." My voice came out sanded down to gravel. "Wren. I saw — I was in the kitchen. The night they—" I couldn't finish it. Seven years, and I still couldn't finish the sentence.

"You weren't remembering," she said quietly. "You were *retrieving*. The storm keeps accounts, Felix. Everything it ever took from you, it kept. Including that night. Including the truth of that night."

"What truth?" I was sitting up now, or something adjacent to sitting up, some posture that technically qualified. "I know the truth. I lost control. I killed them. I've carried it for sixteen years, I've built a whole life on top of it like a cairn—"

"No," Wren said, and her voice had gone archivist-flat, the tone she used when the evidence contradicted someone's grief, which was, I was learning, most of the time. "You've built a life on top of a *story*. The binding fed on your gift that night. Do you understand what fed means? Something was drinking, Felix. Storms don't drink. Bindings drink. Whatever sits under that palace wearing Aldric's face has been drawing on every Ravensworth gift for a hundred years, and the night your family died wasn't an accident. It was a *collection*."

I stared at her. Rain ran off my chin.

"The king heard you," she said. "In the undercroft. He named the bargain. Your family didn't stumble into Veymar's debts. They were born into them. Signed for, generations back, by someone whose name is probably inked in a ledger I confiscated myself and filed under agricultural surveys, because the people who hide things count on archivists being tired."

"So exile," I said slowly, "the valley, the seven years—"

"Wasn't hiding," Wren said. "It was *storage*. You put yourself somewhere the collector couldn't reach you, and the collector waited, because collectors always wait, and the prophecy book called you out by name and date because the account came due tonight." She wiped rain from her face with the heel of her hand. "I'm sorry. I know how that sounds. I know what it does to a man to learn his guilt was engineered."

"It doesn't do nothing," I said. "That's the problem. It should be a relief and it's worse. Because if I didn't kill them, then I spent seven years burying myself for a murder somebody else committed with my hands."

* * *

We sat on the hill until the sun properly cleared the ridgeline and the rain burned off into steam. Wren produced bread and hard cheese from somewhere in her coat — archivists, I've since learned, are basically pack animals with opinions — and laid out the lesson she'd apparently been waiting her whole disgraced career to teach.

"Magic in Veymar is not power," she said. "Say it back."

"Magic in Veymar is not power."

"It's debt. Every spell is a promise, and every promise is collected. The storm-caller who hurls lightning pays in years. The hedge-witch who mends a fever pays in sleep. The Hollow King pays in—" she gestured vaguely at the horizon, at the kingdom entire, "—everything, continuously, forever, which is why he needs to eat courtiers. The trick isn't avoiding the debt. Nobody avoids it. The trick is knowing the terms before you sign."

"My family didn't know the terms."

"Your family's *signature* was known. That's different, and it's crueler." She bit her cheese with the air of a woman chewing through history itself. "So. New rules. One: you never call alone. Two: you call small. A gust. A shiver of static. You're not summoning the ocean, you're asking it for a cup. Three: when the old hunger comes — and it will come, it lives in your gift like rot in a beam — you stop. Immediately. You say my name. I anchor you. That's the arrangement."

"And if the anchoring doesn't hold?"

"Then we revise the arrangement," she said, with the serene confidence of a woman who had been thrown ten feet down a hill two hours ago and had gotten up anyway. "Felix. Look at me. You nearly killed us both this morning, and here's what matters: you *stopped*. When I said your name enough times, you came back. Sixteen years ago there was no one saying your name. There was no one who knew the terms. That's the difference. Not your control. Your company."

I wanted to argue. Some stubborn cellar-door part of me wanted to keep believing I was a weapon that had misfired once and must therefore be locked away forever, because the alternative — that I could practice, that I could be careful, that I could have help — required trusting people with the thing that had destroyed everything I loved.

Instead I held out my hand, palm up, over the wet grass.

"Small," Wren warned.

"Small," I agreed.

I opened the door a crack — the width of a cup, not an ocean — and asked. Static crawled across my palm, blue-white, delicate as frost ferns, and sat there trembling, and did not grow, and did not hunger, and after a moment I closed my fingers around it and let it go, and it went out into the morning like a firefly released.

Wren exhaled. "Well," she said. "Terms understood."

"Terms understood," I said. And for the first time in seven years, the words felt less like a sentence and more like a contract I'd actually read.

We started down the hill toward the city, toward Pip, toward the feast that evening where a hollow thing in a crown would renew itself a century to the day. Halfway down, Wren stopped walking.

"Felix," she said. "There's something I didn't tell you. About the collection schedule."

"Don't," I said.

"It's tonight. At the coronation feast. The binding draws heaviest when it renews, and it knows where you are now — it tasted you this morning, the whole city felt that storm, and things that taste you remember you." She met my eyes. "It's going to send hunters. Tonight. Before the feast, if it's smart. And Felix — the thing in the crown is going to look at you across that hall and *know your name*."

I stood very still on the wet hillside, static still ghosting faintly off my knuckles, and understood that the quiet life was not just lost. It had never existed. Somewhere below us, bells were rehearsing for a coronation, and every bell in Veymar sounded, suddenly, like a dinner bell.
