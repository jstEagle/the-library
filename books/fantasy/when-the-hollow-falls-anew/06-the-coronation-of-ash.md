## 6. The Coronation of Ash

I want it noted, for the record, that nobody ever listens to scullery children. Not when we say the soup's burnt. Not when we say Cook's been drinking the cooking sherry again. And especially not when we say there is a dead king under the palace who sings to us through the floor.

My name is Pip. I am eleven years old, I have washed more dishes than I can count and fewer than I deserve, and this is the story of how I tried to save a courtier's life at a feast where I wasn't even supposed to be, and failed, and what happened after.

* * *

The thing about centennial coronations is that they make everyone stupid.

Normally a scullery girl like me stays below stairs on feast days — below stairs and below that, down in the wash-rooms where the steam hides you and nobody asks questions. But the palace was so full of extra hands brought in from the city that nobody knew whose job was whose, which meant for one glittering evening I could carry a tray of wine up three staircases without anyone shouting *Oi, you, back down* — because everyone assumed I belonged to somebody else.

That's how I got into the gallery above the feast hall. That's how I saw everything.

And that's how I heard him singing.

He always sings louder in the deep places, the bound king. Under the pantry he hums like a kettle deciding. In the wine cellar he does little marching songs, like he's keeping soldiers company who died a hundred years ago. But tonight, with the whole palace packed with music and candlelight and the smell of roast swan, his song came up through the stone like water through a cracked jug, thin and steady and terribly sad.

*Hold fast, hold fast, the crown will pass.*

I'd learned to sing it back to him sometimes, quiet, so the other kitchen girls wouldn't think me mad. Mad gets you turned out. Turned out in Veymar in autumn gets you dead by winter. So mostly I just listened, and I listened tonight too, standing behind a pillar in the gallery with a stolen honey-cake in my pocket, watching the Hollow King hold his hundredth coronation.

He looked fine. That's the horrible part. From up there he looked like any king from a tapestry: tall, silver-haired, wearing a crown of pale glass that caught the light like frost catching sun. He smiled at the right moments. He lifted his cup at the right moments. He said all the words the herald fed him, and the hall cheered, and the candles burned straight as soldiers.

But I could hear what the cheering drowned out. The king beneath the stones had stopped singing.

He'd started counting.

*One, two, three* — soft, urgent, like a man counting heartbeats. Like something was coming that needed counting toward.

* * *

The courtier's name was Lord Merrivale. I know because the woman next to me in the gallery kept saying it — *Lord Merrivale has outdone himself, Lord Merrivale's daughter is promised to the eastern duke, isn't Lord Merrivale splendid in that grey?* — and I remember thinking grey was the wrong word, because when the crown took him he went through every color there is.

It happened between the toasts. The Hollow King rose to give the blessing, and the hall hushed, and that's when I felt it — a pulling, like the moment before thunder when your teeth ache and the air goes thin. The candles all leaned sideways at once. Every flame in that enormous hall bent toward the dais like flowers toward a window.

And Lord Merrivale stood up.

Nobody told him to. Nobody pushed him. He just stood, smooth and slow, the way people move in dreams, and walked down the center aisle with his grey silk whispering, and everybody thought — I could see them think it — that this was part of the ceremony. Some old rite. Some honor.

The Hollow King looked at him and smiled wider than a face should go.

"Come," said the King, in a voice like a door opening onto nothing. "A hundred years, and the crown grows hungry. Let us feed it what loves us best."

And I want to be fair to Lord Merrivale, because in the last second — just before — his eyes came back. Whatever had been walking him let go for one heartbeat, maybe because the crown wanted him awake for it, maybe because even monsters get bored. His eyes found the hall full of feasting, oblivious faces, and they were the most frightened eyes I have ever seen, and his mouth opened and he said —

Nothing. There wasn't time. The crown flared white, the way ice looks when it's about to crack off a roof and kill somebody, and the light went *into* him instead of out. He didn't scream. That was worst of all. He folded inward like paper in a fire, grey silk and grey skin and grey hair all drawn up into a point, into a thread, into a single falling fleck of ash that drifted down and settled on the dais steps, and then there was no Lord Merrivale at all, only an empty chair and a half-finished cup of wine going warm.

The crown drank. I could hear it drink. A sound like a long swallow in a very quiet room.

Then the hall burst into applause, because the light had been beautiful, because people see what they're told to see, and the woman next to me sighed happily and said, "Oh, *magnificent*, whatever did it mean?" and ate another candied plum.

I was the only person in the palace screaming.

* * *

They say I screamed for a long time. I don't remember much of it. I remember hanging over the gallery rail with my voice tearing, shouting *he ate him, the king ate him, look at the chair, LOOK AT THE CHAIR,* and I remember guards coming up the stairs two at a time, and I remember someone saying the word *mad*, and someone else saying *scullery brat, probably ate bad fish*, and I remember being carried out under a guard's arm like a sack of turnips while the music started up again below, because the feast must go on, and kings are hungry, and nobody looks in a sack of turnips.

They dumped me in the scullery yard among the slop pails. The head of household staff, Mistress Corvel, stood over me with her arms crossed and her mouth a flat line.

"Pip. Girl. What in the name of every saint possessed you?"

"He *ate* him." My voice was wrecked, all gravel and whine. "Mistress Corvel, I *saw* it, the crown took Lord Merrivale and there's nothing left, there's just ash —"

"You fell asleep in the gallery and dreamed."

"I don't sleep in galleries! I stole a honey-cake and I watched the whole —"

"There it is." She pointed at my pocket like she'd solved a murder. "Stolen cake, fevered dreams. You'll take bread and water for a week and thank me for the mercy."

"Mistress." I grabbed her sleeve, and she frowned, but she didn't shake me off, which from her was practically an embrace. "You've worked here thirty years. Have you ever once seen the King eat? Drink? Bleed? Have you ever seen him sweat?"

Her frown deepened. Somewhere behind her eyes a small door creaked open onto a room she'd kept locked a long time.

"The King keeps his own counsel," she said carefully.

"The King doesn't *counsel*. The King doesn't anything. He's a coat on a hook, mistress, and something wears him, and tonight I watched it eat a man in front of four hundred guests who clapped." My hands were shaking. I made fists to stop them, which never works. "And there's more. The king under the palace — the real one, the one in the stones — he's counting. He's been counting all night. Something's coming. Something the crown is afraid of."

Corvel looked at me for a long moment. Then she did the kindest and cruelest thing anyone did to me that night: she crouched down to my level, took my face in her flour-dusted hands, and said gently,

"Pip. You're a clever girl and a strange girl, and strange girls in palaces need to learn to be quiet ones. Go to your cot. Sleep. Tomorrow this will be a story."

She stood, shook out her skirts, and walked back inside to a feast where a chair sat empty and no one would ever ask why.

I sat down among the slop pails and put my ear against the yard's stone wall, because I didn't want tomorrow to be a story. I wanted tonight to be a warning.

And through the stone, faint and ragged, the bound king had stopped counting.

He'd started saying a name.

*Felix. Felix Ravensworth. Boy of storms — if you can hear me — run, or don't, but come either way. It knows you now. It tasted the sky where you broke it, and crowns keep their appetites, and mine — mine is nearly empty enough to reach for you.*

*Hold fast, little singer. Help is coming or doom is coming, and in Veymar those have always worn the same coat.*

* * *

Here is what I decided, sitting in the dark among the slops with a dead lord's ash still drifting somewhere through the palace air: I was done being a girl nobody listens to.

If the guards wouldn't carry my warning, I'd carry it myself. If the grown-ups wouldn't leave their posts, I'd leave mine. I knew the undercrofts better than anyone alive except the rats — I'd followed the singing down there half my life, mapping every crack the king's voice came through. And I knew something else, something I'd never told a soul: three days ago, a stranger had come through the palace gates with storm-light still clinging to him like burrs, and the stones had sung so loud when he passed that the wine bottles rattled on their racks.

Felix Ravensworth. I knew his name before I ever heard it spoken, because the king sang it in his sleep, the way you or I might dream of home.

So I climbed out of the yard over the wall where the ivy grows thick, dropped into the delivery lane, and ran — through service doors and cellar stairs and passages that smelled of tallow and old rain — down, always down, following the pull in my chest like a fishhook of sound, until I reached the deepest vault door, the one shaped like a keyhole, where the singing pooled thickest.

And there, pressed against the far side of the door, listening just as hard as I was, stood the storm-caller himself — pale as whey, hair full of static, with a tall archivist woman behind him holding a lantern and looking at me like I was either a miracle or a problem.

Probably both. I usually am.

"It ate a man tonight," I told them, breathless, before either could speak. "In front of everyone. And now it knows your name."

The storm-caller went very still. Outside, far above us, thunder rolled across a sky that hadn't been forecast to hold any — and every bottle in the vault rang once, softly, like struck glass.

The king began, very quietly, to laugh.
