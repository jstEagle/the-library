## 7. Bargains Collected

I have spent my entire career among documents that could get a person killed. I catalogued them. I shelved them. I once indexed a heresy so dangerous that reading its spine label aloud was grounds for exile — I know because I read the spine label aloud, to a colleague, as a joke, and that colleague is now tending goats in the Greymarch and sends me cheese at midwinter.

So believe me when I say I understood the risks of what we were doing.

I simply did not expect the hunters to be quite so punctual.

We were three days out of Veymar City, holed up in the ruins of the Chapel of Saint Verity — an irony Wren Ashvale, former First Archivist of the Royal Collections, appreciated deeply — when the dogs began barking in the valley below. Not farm dogs. Palace hounds have a particular voice, low and disciplined, like choirboys who've been taught to hate.

"They found the cart," Felix said. He was at the broken window, one hand pressed flat against the stone as though he could feel through it. Perhaps he could. The air around his fingers had been doing something wrong all morning, a faint pressure like the moment before thunder. "Three riders. Maybe four."

"Then we leave through the crypt passage and—"

"There is no crypt passage, Wren. You checked yesterday. You were very disappointed about it."

He was right, damn him. Saint Verity's chapel had been stripped for stone two generations ago; whatever catacombs it might once have boasted were now a farmer's root cellar full of turnips. Our only exits were the front door, which the dogs had already claimed, or the collapsed eastern wall, which offered a scenic drop onto rocks.

Felix turned from the window. In the seven weeks since I'd dragged him out of Hollow Vale — since the valley itself spat him across the border like a pit from a plum — he had changed. Not softened, exactly. Settled, the way sediment settles: everything loose sinking down until what remained was dense and heavy and hard to move. His storm-calling had grown from a stuttering candle-flame into something I could feel standing near him, a static hum against my teeth.

And he had been quiet lately. Quiet in the way archives are quiet before someone requests a restricted folio.

"Felix," I said carefully, "whatever you're planning—"

The front doors came off their hinges.

* * *

There were four hunters, as it happened, and they were not the palace guard. The palace guard wears crimson and looks bored. These four wore ash-grey cloaks and moved like men who had rehearsed this room a hundred times in their sleep. Their leader carried no weapon I could see, which frightened me more than any sword, because the impostor's household guards did not need weapons. They needed only proximity, and time, and the thing in the crown reaching through them like fingers through a glove.

"Wren Ashvale." The leader's voice was pleasant. That was worst of all. "Her Majesty's collection misses you."

"The Crown's collection," I corrected, because if I was going to die in a ruin I would die accurately. "His Majesty has never touched a book in his life. I checked the ledgers. A hundred years of ledgers. He doesn't even sign his own decrees — the ink moves wrong when he tries."

Something flickered behind the hunter's eyes. Interest. And beneath the interest, briefly, something exhausted.

"You always did read too much," he said. "That's why you're here and not dead. She wants the archivist alive. The storm-caller—" he glanced at Felix, and the pleasantness dropped away entirely, replaced by something like pity, which from these people was infinitely worse, "—she wants most of all."

Felix didn't run. I want that on the record. Whatever he decided in those next moments, he did not run, and he did not let them take me, and when the nearest hunter raised a grey-gloved hand toward us both, Felix stepped forward instead of back and the pressure in the air went tight enough to ache.

"Don't," I said, low. "Not here, not untrained, not—"

"I'm not going to do anything." But his voice had gone strange. Distant. Like a man reciting something he'd memorized in the dark.

And then I did the stupidest, bravest thing available to me, which is the only kind of brave thing I have ever managed: I opened my mouth and offered the hunters a trade.

"The Third Concordance," I said.

Every head in the room turned. Even the hounds outside went quiet.

"You don't know what that is," the leader said slowly, "because it doesn't exist. It was burned."

"A copy exists. There is always a copy; that is the first law of archives and the reason tyrants lose eventually." My heart was slamming against my ribs like a prisoner at a door. "The original Concordance of Aldric's coronation year. The binding rite. Every clause, every condition, every loophole your mistress prayed no one would ever find. I hid it eleven years ago, when I still believed the king was merely ill. I can tell you where."

"Liar," said one of the younger hunters.

"Probably," I agreed. "But consider the arithmetic. If I'm lying, you've lost an afternoon. If I'm telling the truth and you kill me, you've lost the only copy of the document that explains why your mistress cannot simply make another storm-caller when this one fails. She needs Ravensworth blood, gentlemen. There isn't an abundance."

The leader studied me for a long moment. Then he made a small gesture, and the grey gloves lowered — not far, but lowered.

"Where."

"Under the floor of the Scriptorium of Saint Ossian, third tile from the heretic's alcove. But here is my price, and I advise you to hear all of it before deciding I'm bluffing, because I am about to say something that will sound like madness." I drew a breath. "In exchange, you will tell me the exact terms of the transfer clause. All of them. Because I have read every fragment of that rite that survived the burning, and there is a gap in it — a page missing, a clause redacted — and I know your mistress has the missing piece, because she wrote it. A century ago. Under a different name."

Silence. Rain had started somewhere above us, drumming on the ruined roof, and Felix had gone very still beside me — the particular stillness of a man listening to his own funeral arrangements being negotiated.

"You think you can bargain your way into the truth and walk out with it," the leader said.

"I think," I said, "that you are tired. I saw it when you looked at me. I've spent my life among old documents, and old documents are just old lies written down, and I know the look of a man carrying one he no longer believes. So here is the trade: the Concordance's location for the transfer clause. Spoken plainly, witnessed by the storm-caller himself, whose gift — I'm told — makes lying to him remarkably difficult."

That last part was improvisation. Felix shot me a look that said *is that true?* and I returned a look that said *it is now.*

The leader looked at Felix. Felix looked back. And whatever passed between them — whatever the storm in Felix's skin showed him, or seemed to show him — made the hunter's shoulders drop half an inch.

"Willing," the man said quietly. "The vessel must be willing. That was the amendment. The original rite bound the king by force, and force frays — a century of fraying is why she must feed it courtiers now, why it starves. Her amendment, written in her own hand ninety-nine years ago: the binding may pass whole to a new vessel only if that vessel takes it freely. Willingly. Knowing what it is." He paused. "Of the line that made the original bargain. Ravensworth blood. Your family signed the first contract, storm-caller. Your great-great-grandmother's signature is on it. The exile valley, the suppressed gift, the fire that took your family — none of it was chance. It was husbandry. They kept the bloodline alive and desperate and alone, because a willing sacrifice must be grown, not taken."

The rain got louder. Or perhaps everything else got quieter.

"And if no one volunteers?" I asked, though I already knew, because archivists always know, we just need to hear it confirmed in a living voice.

"Then the binding frays through, the thing in the crown comes apart, and everything it has held down for a century comes up with it." The hunter smiled without any pleasure at all. "She wins either way, archivist. That was rather the elegance of it."

"Let them go," he added, before his companions could object. "Orders were the storm-caller alive. Nothing says unbruised. Walk south, don't stop before dark." And then, to me, almost gently: "Burn after reading, Wren Ashvale. Some pages deserve it."

They left the way they'd come, through the ruined doors, and the hounds' voices faded down the valley, and then it was only rain and turnip-smell and the two of us standing in a dead saint's house with the shape of a trap laid out between us like a corpse on a table.

* * *

Felix didn't speak for an hour.

I used the time the way I always use unbearable time: I inventoried. I counted our supplies twice, rewrapped the prophecy book in oilcloth, sharpened a letter-opener I had been pretending was a knife since Veymar City. Through all of it, Felix sat in the corner with his palms upturned on his knees, watching the rain come through the broken roof, and the air around him did that tightening thing, over and over, like a man practicing a grip.

When he finally spoke, his voice was calm, and that was how I knew.

"The tile in Saint Ossian's," he said. "Is there actually a concordance under it?"

"No. The scriptorium flooded forty years ago. Anything under that floor is pulp." I set down the letter-opener. "You knew that."

"I suspected. You lie better than you think you do, Wren. It's your best quality." He looked up at me, and his eyes had the flat bright look of weather coming. "A willing vessel. Grown, not taken. Seven years in a valley where magic dies — that wasn't hiding, it was curing. Keeping me tender. And my family—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "My family died to keep me desperate enough to say yes."

"Felix."

"If I go to her willingly, the binding passes clean. The king comes free. No fraying, no catastrophe, no century of eaten courtiers. One life against a kingdom." He spread his hands, reasonable, arithmetic. "It's not even a difficult sum."

"It's a monstrous sum, and you're doing it wrong, because you keep forgetting the carry-over." I crossed the room and stood over him, and I confess my hands were shaking, but my voice held, which is the Ashvale inheritance — composure as a family curse. "You forget Pip, who sings to a king under the stones and believes you'll both walk out. You forget me. You forget that I did not spend eleven years being right about a dead king and lose my rank and my name and my entire life in order to trade one friend for a ledger entry. Bindings pass willingly, do they? Fine. Then hear this: I am willing to stand in that vault and refuse to let you do it alone, and I am apparently immune to clauses."

"That's not how—"

"I know it's not how it works. Neither is growing a man in a valley for thirty years and calling it fate." I crouched to his level, rain running down the back of my collar. "Your great-great-grandmother signed a bargain in desperation. You are not obligated to countersign it in resignation."

For a moment something cracked across his face — grief, fury, and underneath both, so faint I nearly missed it, relief. The relief of a man who has been told he must drown, hearing someone argue with the water.

Then he closed his hands, and the pressure in the air eased, and he said, in a voice I chose to believe:

"All right. We do it together or not at all."

Later — much later, when it mattered — I would remember that he agreed a little too easily, and that while I slept he sat by the window with the prophecy book open on his knees, reading the page that bore his name, and that the ink was moving again, rewriting itself, and that he let it.

Because the words had changed. Where a century of editions had promised *the storm shall take the crown*, tonight the page said something new, fresh-inked, wet enough to smudge:

*Unless someone else holds the storm when it falls.*
