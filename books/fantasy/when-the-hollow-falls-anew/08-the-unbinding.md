## 8. The Unbinding

The vault beneath the palace had no door so much as a suggestion of one — a slab of black stone that Wren said hadn't opened in a hundred years and which, in fairness to her archives, had opened for us on the third try. I chose not to think about what that meant. I was doing my best not to think about most things, because thinking led to the ledger, and the ledger read: *Ravensworth blood, willingly given, binds what the crown cannot hold.*

That was me. I'd checked twice.

The stair down was cut from rock that sweated cold, and each step took something from me — not strength, exactly. Certainty. By the time we reached the bottom, I was remembering Hollow Vale with a nostalgia that felt obscene, the way you miss a prison only once you're standing at the gallows. My little cottage. My leaky roof. The seven years I'd spent teaching myself to be small.

Small had been the whole trick, it turned out. Small was the trap.

The vault itself was round, or roundish, or possibly it simply refused to commit to a shape; the walls breathed when you weren't looking directly at them. In the center stood the binding — not chains, not a throne, nothing so honest. A circle of standing stones no taller than my knee, arranged around a hollow in the floor, and rising from that hollow a light the color of an old bruise. The Hollow King's essence didn't roar or shriek the way stories promise. It *waited*. It had waited a hundred years. It was very good at it.

"You don't have to do this," Wren said, for the eleventh time. She'd counted. I'd watched her lips.

"Yes, I do."

"That's not what willing means, Felix. Willing isn't *have to*. Willing is—"

"I know what willing means." I knelt at the circle's edge and pressed my palm flat against the first stone. It was warm. That was the worst part, somehow. Everything down here was cold except the thing waiting to eat me. "It means nobody's making me. Nobody is. I'm making me. That's the entire problem with my life."

Wren made a sound I'd learned to translate as *archival frustration* — a sort of clipped exhale that meant she had seventeen citations prepared and no jurisdiction to use them.

"The ritual," she said instead, unrolling the page she'd stolen back from her own confiscated collection at considerable personal cost. "You begin the call. You open the storm. The binding transfers along the path of least resistance, which is you, because your family built the original channel. Once it's in you, you speak the closing words. Then—" she hesitated over the next line, and I watched her decide whether to lie to me. She decided against it. Wren lied about feelings, never about sources. "Then you hold it. Forever. Or until it unmakes you. The text is ambiguous on timeline."

"Ambiguous. Lovely. A century-old prophecy with commitment issues."

"Felix."

"Wren." I looked up at her — this fierce, disgraced woman who had lost her rank, her library, and nearly her freedom for telling a truth everyone in Veymar could feel in their bones and no one would say aloud. "If I don't come back from this, catalog the Ravensworth material under *Tragic Irony, Subsection: Self-Inflicted.*"

"If you don't come back from this," she said, "I will find whatever afterlife you've gone to and reorganize *you* by an arbitrary system I invent purely to spite you."

I believed her. God help me, I believed her, and it made the next part harder, because dying is easy when no one is waiting up.

I closed my eyes and reached for the storm.

Seven years I'd kept it leashed. Seven years of clamping down on the thing under my skin every hour of every day, a muscle clenched so long I'd forgotten what relaxed felt like. Opening it now was like unstoppering a wound I'd bandaged with willpower alone. The pressure came first — that familiar hum climbing toward a howl — and then the wind rose in a room with no sky, and lightning crawled the breathing walls like veins waking.

"Begin," Wren said, voice steady as a footnote.

I spoke the opening words.

* * *

The world came apart politely, the way bad news does.

One moment I was kneeling on cold stone; the next I was nowhere, in the dark between heartbeats, and the Hollow King's essence turned toward me the way a starving man turns toward bread. It didn't lunge. It simply *arrived*, all around me, patient as arithmetic, and I understood with terrible clarity that I had spent thirty years being afraid of the wrong hunger.

*"Ravensworth,"* it said, without sound, without mouth, straight into the marrow of me. *"Little storm. Little debt. Come to pay your grandfather's grandfather's bill."*

"I come to close the account," I said. My voice sounded young down here. Thin.

*"Accounts don't close. Accounts transfer."* And then it began to pour.

I had imagined pain. Fire, tearing, the dramatic agonies of ballads. This was worse. This was *occupancy*. It slid into the spaces the storm had opened, filling me like water fills a cast-off glove, and it wore my hands — I felt my fingers flex with someone else's patience — and it looked out through my eyes and found them adequate. Sufficient. *Available.*

The binding settled into my chest like a stone dropped into a well, and I understood, distantly, that I was already losing the argument. The storm raged, yes, my own gift screaming against the intrusion — but rage wasn't strategy. Rage was just fuel for the fire consuming me, and the closing words sat in my throat like a swallowed key I could no longer reach.

*Hold it forever,* the prophecy said. *Or until it unmakes you.*

I felt the unmaking begin at my edges. My memories went first, quietly, the way frost leaves a window — Hollow Vale blurring, my mother's laugh thinning to static, the smell of rain going generic. I grabbed for them and my hands passed through.

*This is fine,* I thought, with the last coherent part of myself. *This is the price. This was always the price. Nobody told me to—*

"FELIX!"

The vault doors slammed open hard enough to crack the breathing walls mid-breath.

* * *

Later, I would learn how they got down there — Wren's stolen key, Pip's impossible knack for hearing locks, and a guard named Doran who owed Wren a favor dating back to some scandal involving the royal wine cellar, which she refuses to this day to catalogue. At the time, all I knew was that two voices broke into my dying like light breaks into a cellar.

Pip hit me first. Small body, enormous velocity, arms locked around my neck, and through the contact came — noise. Ordinary, mortal, gloriously irrelevant noise.

"*You said you were getting a book!* You said *ritual supplies!* That's lying, Felix, that's *lying*, Wren says liars go in Subsection Self-Inflicted and I'm not letting you—"

"Pip," I tried. My mouth barely worked. "Pip, let go, it'll take you too—"

"IT CAN'T TAKE WHAT IT CAN'T CARRY." She said it with the absolute conviction of a nine-year-old who has decided physics is optional. "Wren! Do the words!"

Wren was already there, on my other side, her hands clamped over mine where they gripped the stone, and her voice came down like a verdict: "*Hear me, binding. Witness me, storm. This man is not yours to hold alone, for he does not hold alone. Amendment entered into the record by Wren Ashvale, archivist-chancellor designate, and let the record show—*" her voice cracked, one hairline fracture in all that granite, "*—let the record show we refuse.*"

And here is the thing I still cannot fully explain.

Magic in Veymar is debt. Every spell a promise collected later. Wren taught me that, and I thought I understood it, but I understood it the way you understand drowning until someone pulls you out. When they touched me — Pip's arms, Wren's hands — the binding *recounted*. I felt it happen, felt the ancient arithmetic stumble, recalculate, stagger. One bearer. Two anchors. Three names on the deed.

The Hollow King's essence recoiled from Pip like a hand from a stove. Not because she was powerful. Because she was *loud* — loud with scullery songs and stolen apples and the bound king's hundred years of whispered company, loud with everything the hollow thing had never been and could not digest.

*"Impossible,"* it said, in my marrow, and for the first time in a century, it sounded uncertain. *"The terms specify one—"*

"The terms," Wren said, reading now from the stolen page, her voice gathering speed like a landslide, "specify a willing vessel. The vessel is willing. The vessels are willing. All three of us, entered jointly, severally, and with extreme prejudice. Take us or take none. Those are the amended terms. I have *citations.*"

The storm inside me stopped raging toward self-destruction and started raging toward something else entirely. Purpose. Direction. All those years I'd thought my gift was a weapon pointed inward, and it had only ever needed somewhere to stand. The lightning in my veins found their hands — Pip's small warm grip, Wren's white-knuckled resolve — and used them as ground, as anchor, as *home*, and the Hollow King's essence, squeezed between three stubborn hearts and one furious storm, did the only thing left to it.

It screamed. Briefly. Almost politely.

And then the binding tore loose — not into me, not into any of us, but *between* us, scattered like seed, thinned past dominance, a residue rather than a ruler. I felt it settle into the three of us in pieces small enough to survive: a cold thread behind my ribs, a flicker in Wren's eyes, a faint gray streak appearing overnight in Pip's hair that she would later declare *distinguished.*

The bruise-light guttered. The stones went dark. The hollow in the floor was just a hollow.

I came back to myself on the floor of the vault, folded between a child and an archivist, soaked in sweat, alive, my mother's laugh intact in my memory where I'd grabbed it and never let go.

"You're heavy," Pip announced from somewhere near my collarbone.

"You're crushing my windpipe."

"Good. Means you're breathing."

Wren sat back on her heels, looking ten years older and thirty years lighter, and stared at the dead circle of stones. "For the record," she said hoarsely, "the amendment shouldn't have worked. There's no precedent. There's no textual basis. It should have failed."

"But it didn't," I said.

"No. It didn't." She looked at me, and then at Pip, and something moved across her face that she'd deny under oath. "Apparently the archive was incomplete."

Above us, muffled through a hundred feet of sweating stone, a sound began — distant at first, then swelling, a great shifting and groaning throughout the palace, as though the building itself had been holding its breath for a century and had finally, at long last, remembered how to inhale.

From somewhere far below the vault — deeper than our vault, deeper than anything with a right to be deep — came a second sound.

Footsteps.

And a voice, rusty from a hundred years of disuse, worn but unmistakably alive, calling up through the stones:

"Whoever is down there making all that racket — *someone fetch my crown. The real one this time.*"

Pip burst out laughing. Wren swore in three dead languages.

And I lay on the cold floor of the place I'd come to die, and laughed until it hurt, because the true King of Veymar sounded exactly like a man who had been napping through his own funeral and intended to complain about the arrangements.
