## 10. Where the Hollow Ends

One year, and the sky over Veymar still surprises me.

I wake some mornings expecting the flat grey lid of Hollow Vale, that colorless nothing I lived under for seven years, and instead there's this — light coming through the east windows of the storm-warden's tower in long gold bars, catching dust motes like a slow-motion snowfall. The first year in exile I forgot what dawn looked like. Now I keep forgetting to stop looking at it.

The tower was Aldric's idea. "A warden should watch the weather," he said, "not be watched by it," and gave me the highest room in the palace with a view of the whole city and, beyond it, the hills where the Vale sits like a closed fist. Some days I look that way. Most days I don't. It's getting easier.

Pip is already in my workroom when I come down, which means she climbed the tower stairs before I woke, which means she's been reading my weather journals again without permission. She's eleven now — she informed me of this with great ceremony on her birthday and has used it as license for everything since — and she has developed the unshakable confidence of a child who once heard a king's voice through stone and turned out to be right about everything.

"You wrote that the pressure's dropping from the southwest," she says, not looking up from the journal, "but you drew the wind arrows wrong. They should curl. Storms curl."

"They do not always curl."

"These ones do." She taps the page. "You said so yourself, three entries ago. You just didn't want to admit you were wrong in front of me."

"I am your teacher."

"You're my teacher who's wrong sometimes." She finally looks up, and there's the grin, gap-toothed still, one new tooth coming in crooked. Wren swears it's charming. Wren also catalogues things by color, so her judgment is suspect.

"Fine," I say. "Show me how they curl."

And she does — drags my own hand across the page in a spiral, explaining barometric pressure with the fierce authority of someone who learned the word last week — and I stand there in my own workroom being lectured by an orphan scullery girl about meteorology, and I think: seven years ago I would have given anything for this. For someone to be wrong at. For someone to correct me. For a morning with noise in it.

The storm-calling lessons come after breakfast. We go up to the roof because Pip insists roofs are where weather lives, and because after the vault, after everything, neither of us likes small rooms much.

"Reach out," I tell her, the way Wren once told me. "Don't grab. Ask."

Pip closes her eyes and holds out her hands like she's offering bread to a bird. And the wind — a small wind, a testing wind — comes and circles her wrists and tugs at her sleeves, and her whole face goes solemn with wonder, every single time, as if it's the first time.

"It tickles," she reports.

"It's saying hello."

"What do I say back?"

"That's between you and it."

She frowns at me. "That's a non-answer."

"It's the only answer. Magic here isn't power, Pip. It's debt. Every spell is a promise collected later. So when the wind asks something of you, you decide what you're willing to owe." I hold up my hands, scarred at the knuckles where the binding's residue still sits like old frostbite, aching in cold weather. "I owed seven years of silence and almost everything else. You get to choose better than I did."

She considers this with the gravity of a judge. Then she whispers something to the wind — I don't catch what, and I don't ask; some bargains aren't mine to hear — and the wind lifts a strand of her hair and lets it fall, gentle as a hand on the head.

"There," she says, satisfied. "We're friends now. No debt."

"No debt," I agree, and I don't tell her that no-debt friendships with the weather are rarer than crowns, or that I spent thirty years learning what she just did in a morning. Children learn fast what adults have taught themselves to fear.

* * *

Wren finds me in the archive at midday, which is where Wren finds everyone, because Wren believes all problems are ultimately filing problems.

She is Archivist-Chancellor now, which means she has a chain of office she wears like it's a punishment and a staff of twelve assistants she terrifies daily. She has reorganized the royal archive four times since her appointment. Each reorganization has been declared final. Each one has been followed by another.

"The catalogue is wrong," she announces, dropping a ledger on my table hard enough to scatter my notes.

"Good afternoon to you too."

"The catalogue, Ravensworth. Section Nine. Prophecies, contested. Someone filed the Veymar succession documents under 'weather omens' because the binding had atmospheric side effects, and now the cross-references are a disaster, and I know it was Tamsin because she alphabetizes by *vibes*—"

"You could just fix it."

"I *have* fixed it. Four times." She drops into the chair across from me, and beneath the outrage there's the tiredness she never shows her staff. A year of chancellorship has put grey in her hair that wasn't there before. Or maybe I just notice it more now, because noticing things about people is another thing I'm relearning how to do. "Sometimes I miss being disgraced. Disgrace was simple. You knew exactly who hated you."

"And now?"

"Now half the court loves me and I trust them less than the ones who hated me." She rubs her eyes. Then, quieter: "How is he? The king. He canceled council again."

"Weak. Getting less weak. He walked the eastern wall yesterday without stopping." I say it lightly, but we both hear what's underneath it: the binding's residue scattered between three bearers means Aldric will never be what he was, and neither will I, and neither will Wren, whose share of it sits behind her eyes and gives her headaches when she reads too long. We split a century of prison three ways. None of us got off cheap. All of us got off.

"He'll hate being weak," Wren says.

"He hates being *grateful* more. He thanked me again yesterday. Fourth time. I told him if he does it a fifth time I'm calling a storm inside the throne room."

"What did he say?"

"He laughed. Actually laughed. I think it startled him." I turn the ledger toward me without really seeing it. "Wren. The book."

Her tiredness sharpens into attention. She knows what I mean. The prophecy book — the one the peddler sold me, the one that named tonight's date a century in advance, the one that rewrote itself in my hands and threw me out of the only safe place I had — sits in this archive now, in a case Wren built herself, warded six ways. Since the unbinding, its pages have been changing. The prophecies that came true have gone blank, one by one, like debts paid and struck from a ledger.

Only one page left with writing on it.

"Come see," Wren says.

* * *

The case stands in the deepest alcove of Section Nine, and the book lies open to its final page, and I read it the way I've read it every week for a month, hoping for change, braced against change.

The page is nearly empty now. At the top, in ink that no longer bleeds but simply rests, black and quiet:

*When the hollow falls anew, the storm shall learn its name.*

Below that, the rest of the page waits. Blank. But not empty-blank — I've learned the difference this year. It's the blank of unwritten paper, of tomorrow, of a page that hasn't decided yet. Once, every line of this book was a cage with my name on the door. Now it's just... paper. Waiting.

"It stopped rewriting," Wren says beside me. "Three days ago. Whatever was writing it — the bargain, the binding, the thing that set all this in motion — it's finished. Paid in full." She looks at me sideways. "Do you understand what that means?"

"That nobody's holding my pen anymore."

"That nobody's holding anyone's pen. Not yours, not Aldric's, not the Ravensworth line's. A hundred years of engineered fate, ended." Her voice does something complicated on the word *ended*. Wren spent her whole career believing history was a text you could read correctly if you were rigorous enough. Finding out it was being written *at* people — that shook her faith worse than the impostor did. "The future's unwritten, Felix. Genuinely. First time in a century."

I touch the glass over the blank page. My reflection looks back, faint, doubled with the empty lines.

Seven years in the Vale, I thought safety was a wall that kept magic out. Then I thought it would be killing the thing in the crown. Then I thought it would be dying in that vault, paying the family debt with my body, and there was a terrible clean relief in planning it — the arithmetic of love versus duty, duty winning by forfeit. And then two impossible people broke down a warded door because they refused to let me carry the binding alone, and the residue scattered, and I woke up alive in a world where my survival had been *chosen for me* by people who loved me, which was somehow harder to bear than any prophecy.

Belonging, it turns out, isn't a wall. It's the opposite of a wall.

"When's the last time you went home?" Wren asks suddenly. "To the Vale."

"I live here."

"Felix."

"I know what you're asking." I take my hand off the glass. "No. I haven't been back. The border threw me out once. I'm not eager to give it a second chance."

"The border's dead. Has been since the binding broke — the Vale's magic-dead edge was part of the bargain, a cage built around your whole family line, and cages don't survive their purpose." She says it gently, but Wren's gentleness has always arrived dressed as fact. "There are people there, Felix. People who knew you. Who think you vanished."

"People who watched me bury my family and then hide for seven years."

"People who buried your family *with* you." She lets that sit. "I'm not saying go today. I'm saying the road exists now. Both directions."

Both directions. I look at the blank page a moment longer. Tomorrow, unwritten. Maybe that's all home ever was — a direction you're allowed to travel both ways.

* * *

The first storm of autumn comes in on a Tuesday, rolling up from the southwest exactly as Pip predicted, curling exactly as she insisted, and she makes sure I know it.

We stand on the roof together as it comes — a big one, the season's opening argument, lightning walking the horizon like something pacing behind a fence. A year ago a storm this size would have had me bolted indoors with wax in my ears and iron filings in my pockets, suppressing, suppressing, white-knuckled against the hum under my skin. Five years before that, a storm like this killed everyone I loved.

Now I just stand in the wind and listen.

It's different, hearing it now. The binding's residue in my body changed the frequency somehow — Wren theorizes endlessly about why, filling notebooks — but the practical truth is simpler: the storm sounds like a voice I know. Like something calling across a field. Not demanding. Asking.

"Pip," I say. "Watch."

I raise my hands — not grabbing, asking — and call.

Not the way I called in the vault, all desperation and self-immolation. Gently. The way you'd call a dog that's run ahead on a trail, the way you'd hail a friend across a market square. *Here. I'm here. Come home when you're ready.*

The storm hears me.

I feel it hear me — a shiver through the pressure, a leaning-in of the whole grey mass of it, and then it breaks over the city not as violence but as rain, warm and enormous, drumming on the roof tiles, sheeting off the eaves, and Pip shrieks with delight and runs out into it with her arms spread while I stand under the shelter of the parapet and let it wash the dust of a year off the stones.

Lightning cracks the sky, close, bright, and instead of flinching I laugh. The thunder rolls back over the hills toward the Vale, toward the dead border, toward the graves I tended alone for seven years, and it sounds — I know how it sounds, I've checked myself for madness twice — it sounds like an answer. Like family. Like all those Ravensworths who came before me, bound and burdened and bargaining, finally telling me the debt is done, kid, the debt is done, come home whenever you like.

Pip runs back soaked to the skin, grinning, hair plastered flat. "It answered you!"

"It answered us," I say, because that's the lesson, the only lesson, the one thing I actually have to teach. "Weather doesn't belong to anyone. That's the whole point. You don't own a storm. You just get to be on speaking terms with it."

She sticks out her hand to shake the rain off, then changes her mind and shakes mine instead, solemn as a treaty. "Speaking terms," she says.

"Speaking terms."

And that night, after she's gone to bed and the storm has moved on eastward, I climb back up to the roof alone and look southwest, where the hills sit dark under the clearing sky, and somewhere past them the valley that forgot me is forgetting a little less each day.

Tomorrow, I decide. I'll write to Wren in the morning — she'll insist on organizing the expedition, obviously, probably with a catalogue — and then I'll ride out to the Vale, both directions, and see whether a man can go home not to hide but to visit.

The sky above Veymar is washing itself clean, star by star, and every star looks like a blank line on a page.

Unwritten. All of it, unwritten.

I find I can't wait to read it.
