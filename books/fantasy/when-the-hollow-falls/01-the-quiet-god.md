## 1. The Quiet God

The luck-charms were lies, and Freya Hartwell had gotten very good at lying with her whole face.

"River-stone," she told the mother, turning the grey pebble in her fingers so it caught the lamplight. "Pulled from the shallows below the Falls myself. Carried it three days in my boot for luck before I ever set it in the setting. You can't fake that sort of thing."

This was true only in the sense that all her best lies were. She'd bought the stone from a quarryman's cart that morning for two coppers. The three days in her boot were pure invention. But the mother — thin as a whip handle, with the particular grey look of a woman who hadn't slept properly since winter — didn't want truth. She wanted something she could hold in her fist while the world did its worst.

"How much?" the woman asked.

Freya glanced at the child on her hip. A girl, maybe four, with the kind of fever-bright eyes that said the cough was in the chest now and not the throat. Corvale had three physicians worth the name, and all three charged more for a single visit than this woman would earn between then and spring.

"For you?" Freya said. "Six bits."

It was robbery by any honest measure. It was also half what she'd have asked anyone else, and they both knew it, and neither of them was going to say so, because there is a dignity in pretending a bargain is a bargain.

The mother counted the coins out one at a time, like each one hurt going, and Freya took them and pressed the charm into her palm and folded her fingers over it.

"Now listen," she said, dropping her voice the way she did when she wanted people to believe she was letting them in on something. "The stone does nothing on its own. It's not a sword, it's not a wall. What it does is remind. You hold it when you're frightened, and you remember that your luck has held this long, hasn't it? Through worse than this?"

The mother's jaw worked. "Hasn't it," she agreed, in a voice that suggested her luck had held precisely as well as everyone else's in the Tanners' Ward, which was to say barely, and by main force.

"That's the whole trick of it," Freya said warmly. "The god of luck doesn't hand you anything. He just refuses to leave."

She watched the woman walk off into the crowd with her fist closed tight around six bits' worth of riverbed, and felt the familiar sour taste settle under her tongue. Not guilt, exactly. She'd made her peace with guilt years ago; guilt was a lodger who paid no rent but kept mostly to his room. It was something closer to grief's poor cousin. The charm would work, because the woman believed it would work, because belief was the only magic left in Corvale that the Magistrate hadn't managed to tax or burn. And Freya would take her supper money from that belief, and sleep soundly, more or less.

Fifteen years ago she had not needed to sell luck. Fifteen years ago luck had known her name.

* * *

The night market ran along Cutter's Canal from the Salt Gate to the old tannery steps, forty stalls under oilcloth and rope, selling everything the daylight city pretended not to want: secondhand boots, poached eels, love philtres, news from the coast, and charms. Always charms. Corvale had burned its open temples thirty years ago, when the first Magistrate declared that the age of gods was ended and the age of law begun, and the gods — quiet already, quiet for years by then — had not objected. But people are people. They still got sick, still lost coins, still stood at harbors watching ships take their children away. So the market sold them little pieces of the divine, wrapped in copper wire and priced to move, and the Wardens let it stand because a charm-seller was a peddler and a priest was a criminal, and everyone understood which side of that line the coin changed hands on.

Mostly.

Freya packed up early. This was habit, not fear — or rather, it was fear so old it had worn down into habit, the way a stairway wears a groove. She banked her brazier, folded her cloth, counted her takings twice, and slipped the small strongbox with the false bottom into her basket beneath a layer of dried lavender. On top of everything, where a searching hand would find it first, she kept a second purse with four coppers in it. A tithe for trouble. If you were going to be searched, be searched finding something worth finding.

"You're leaving early, Hartwell." Old Maren from the pie stall, hands dusted white, nodding at the sky as if it had personally offended her. "Rain coming."

"Then I'll be dry and rich," Freya said, "and you'll be wet and beloved. Every fool in the Ward will stop to buy a pie if it rains."

"They'd better." Maren squinted at her. "That new girl across the canal — the one doing candle-work? She been by tonight?"

And there it was. Freya kept her face pleasant and her heart very still. "Haven't seen her."

"Hm." Maren wiped her hands. "Well. Mind how you go, then."

Everyone knew. That was the thing nobody warned you about living carefully: the carefulness itself became visible, after enough years, like a scar's pale seam. Freya Hartwell, charm-seller, never drunk, never loud, never seen after the tenth bell, always with a spare coin for Maren's pies and a kind word for everyone and no history anyone could name. She had built herself out of unremarkable parts, and the building showed, if you knew what to look for.

Maren knew what to look for. That was why she asked about the girl across the canal, whose candle-work was hedge-craft, and whose supplier — though only two people alive knew it — was Freya, and one of those two people was currently lying through her teeth about it.

"She'll be fine," Freya said. "She's careful."

"Careful." Maren snorted. "So are rabbits."

* * *

Freya's rooms sat above a chandler's shop on Bellows Lane, one room and a lean-to privy, chosen for exactly one virtue: the roof. Corvale's rooftops were a second city, an unofficial country of gutters and clotheslines that belonged to cats, pigeons, and anyone else with balance and no interest in being governed. From her window to the chandler's roof to the cooper's ridge-beam to the wide warm slope over the dye-works, a person could travel half the Tanners' Ward without once touching ground the Magistrate's law considered public.

Not tonight, though. Tonight she stayed home, barred the door, lit one candle, and pulled the floorboard up beside the hearth.

The ritual kit lived in a sailcloth roll that had once — fifteen years and a lifetime ago — belonged to the company's field-chaplain. Inside: a clay cup, blackened with old smoke. A knife with a horn handle, edge gone soft from honing around nicks. A twist of dried rowan bark. And a smooth oval of grey stone, no bigger than a goose egg, worn glossy on one side where a thumb had rubbed it ten thousand times.

Her thumb, mostly.

Vaskar's rites had never been complicated. That was the joke the other companies used to make — the Iron Kestrels, the Salt Company, all the polished outfits with their robed hierophants and their silver censers — that Vaskar's faithful were too simple-minded for proper ceremony. Freya had broken a man's nose over that joke once, and been fined a week's pay for it, and considered it money well spent. Because the joke had it backwards. Vaskar's rites weren't simple because his people were stupid. They were simple because he had asked for almost nothing, and what he'd asked for he'd meant.

Blood, freely offered, not taken. A name, spoken aloud, in a place the speaker chose. And honesty — that was all. Just the truth, said to the dark, once a day, like reporting for watch. Vaskar was a soldier's god. He didn't want groveling. He wanted muster.

Freya filled the cup with water from her jug and set it on the hearthstones. She pricked her thumb on the soft old knife — the wound barely wept anymore; fifteen years of the same small cut had taught her skin to be economical about bleeding — and let one drop fall in the water. She crumbled a pinch of rowan bark after it.

"Freya Hartwell," she said to the candle. "Daughter of nobody in particular. Sworn sword of the Grey Company, may it eat well in whatever fields the dead go to. Still here. Still yours."

She waited.

This was the part the books — the few forbidden, hand-copied books that still described such things — never managed to convey. The waiting wasn't empty. For the first years it had been full of listening, of leaning inward with her whole soul the way you lean against a locked door, hoping to feel it give. Then it had been full of anger, which burns hot and fast and leaves nothing to cook. Now it was just... waiting. A shape her evenings made. The candle fluttered. The water sat in the cup, being water. Somewhere down the lane a dog was arguing philosophy with another dog, and losing.

Nothing answered. Nothing had answered for fifteen years.

"Fifteen years," Freya said, because honesty was the rite, and she had never yet skipped it. "You know that? I've done the sums. Five thousand four hundred and some mornings I've given you your muster, and five thousand four hundred and some nights I've told you the truth when I couldn't tell it to anyone breathing. And I don't even know if you're—" Her voice did something she didn't authorize, a small crack, and she stopped, breathed, started again. "I don't know if you're deaf, or dead, or if you were ever anything but the company's collective imagination wearing a war-god's face. That's the honest report, sir. That's the state of the line. Freya Hartwell, sworn of the Grey Company, is tired, and starting to think she's been talking to a stone for half her life."

The stone sat on the hearthstones next to the cup, grey and smooth and silent, being a stone.

"Right," she said, when the silence had gone on long enough to be officially rude. "Same time tomorrow, then."

She tipped the water out the window, washed the cup, put the kit away under the floorboard, and sat back on her heels with her arms wrapped around her knees.

Here was the thing she admitted to no one, not even to the dark, not even during the rite, because admitting it aloud might make it real in the way words make things real: she didn't know which was worse. That Vaskar had died — truly died, gone out like a lamp, and all her faith had been spent lighting a wick that wasn't there. Or that he had never existed at all, and she'd spent her whole life in service to a story. Either way, the ache was the same ache, only the arithmetic differed. She had given a god her loyalty the way other women gave men their hearts, and she had received, in fifteen years, exactly nothing back. No voice. No sign. Not so much as a dream worth the name.

And she kept the rite anyway.

Because — she told herself — discipline was its own reward, and a promise kept past hope was still kept, and besides, what else was she going to do with her evenings?

The candle guttered. Down in the street below, boots on cobbles — several pairs, moving quick and together, the particular rhythm of men who had somewhere to be and authority to be there. Freya went still, the way she'd learned to go still in another life, breath shallow, ears doing the work her eyes couldn't.

The boots passed Bellows Lane without stopping. Faded toward the canal. Toward the market.

Toward the candle-girl.

Freya closed her eyes and performed the oldest exercise in the soldier's book: the assessment of things not in evidence. How many Wardens in a night raid? Six, usually, or eight. Who had informed? Not Maren — Maren wouldn't. Some neighbor, then, or someone who'd bought candles and talked. Was the girl careful? She was nineteen and in love with her own power and called it careful, the way nineteen-year-olds call everything careful.

Freya looked at the floorboard where her kit lay hidden. Looked at the window, where the night was doing nothing illegal at all.

"Rabbits," she muttered.

She should stay put. She had stayed put for fifteen years; it was practically her craft, her masterwork, the great work of her hidden life — staying put while other people paid for being visible. Staying put had kept her alive, fed, whole. Staying put was the entire architecture of Freya Hartwell, charm-seller, and she could feel its every joint groaning now, holding, holding, the way good timber holds.

She got her coat anyway.

At the window she paused, one last time, and told herself a lie so old it had nearly worn through to truth: *just to watch, just to see, just to know*. She climbed out onto the chandler's roof and moved along the ridgeline toward the canal, where the night sky over the market had begun, very faintly, to turn orange.
