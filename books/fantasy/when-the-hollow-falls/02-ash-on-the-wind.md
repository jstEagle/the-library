## 2. Ash on the Wind

The trouble with selling luck, Freya had learned over fifteen years in Corvale's night market, was that people kept coming back when it failed.

"You said this one was for safe births," the cooper's wife said, jabbing a finger at the little knotted cord on Freya's counter. Her belly was enormous under a coat that had given up pretending to button. "My sister-in-law bought one. Twins came early and the midwife slipped on the stairs."

"And yet," Freya said, "here you both are, complaining at me instead of mourning at a graveside. Sounds like it worked to me."

The woman's mouth opened, closed, opened again. Behind her, her husband — a barrel of a man with sawdust permanently lodged in his eyebrows — made a sound that might have been a laugh swallowed whole.

"That's not — charm-sellers shouldn't be allowed to talk like that."

"Charm-sellers aren't allowed to do much of anything, mistress. Talking is the one thing left. I try to do it well." Freya slid a second cord across the counter, twin to the first, and pressed it into the woman's palm with just enough pressure to make it feel like a secret. "For your sister-in-law. No charge. Tell her to hang it by the window where the morning light touches it, and to stop buying charms from anyone who promises too much."

The woman looked down at the knot, then up at Freya, and something in her face softened from outrage to the wary gratitude of a customer who has been beaten at haggling and knows it. She paid for the original cord after all, overpaid, and left with her husband trailing behind like a barge behind a tugboat.

Freya watched them go and felt the familiar small ache in her chest, the one that lived somewhere between pride and fraud. The cords were pretty work. Good knots, good wool, dyed with madder she boiled herself. Whether they did anything beyond giving a frightened person something to hold — well. That depended entirely on whether there was anything left in the world to put power into things, and Freya had spent fifteen years assuming the answer was no, and building her life accordingly, and trying very hard not to think about it more than once or twice a day.

Her stall sat at the crooked elbow of Tanner's Row where the night market bent around the old tannery wall, which meant she got the overflow of customers nobody else wanted: the desperate, the superstitious, the poor, and the ones who were all three. Directly beside her, close enough that their awnings overlapped in the rain, was the stall of Maren Ostwyk, hedge-witch, herb-seller, and — though the word existed nowhere outside Freya's own careful skull — the closest thing to a friend Freya had allowed herself since the war ended.

"Charming as ever," Maren said now, not looking up from her bundles of dried feverfew. She was sixty if she was a day, thin as a broom handle, with iron-grey hair pinned up by what appeared to be two pencils and genuine malice. "I heard the whole performance. 'Sounds like it worked.' You're a menace."

"I'm a businesswoman."

"You're a coward with good knots."

Freya snorted. "And what does that make you? You sell love potions to teenagers."

"Those work," Maren said primly. "Feverfew and rose oil, smells like devotion, costs nothing. If a boy thinks he's fallen in love because his girl suddenly smells sweet, who am I to argue with the results?" She tied off a bundle and finally looked up, and her eyes — quick, dark, far too knowing for comfort — flicked to the little drawer under Freya's counter. The locked one. "You have any more of that willow-bark tincture? The good kind."

"The kind that isn't willow-bark, you mean."

"The very same."

Freya glanced down the row. Night market nights were usually safe — the Wardens kept banker's hours, mostly, and the Magistrate liked the market because it gave the poor somewhere to spend their poverty — but there was a feeling in the air tonight she didn't like. A stillness. The lamplighters had passed early, and the crowd was thinner than it should have been for a warm evening, and twice she'd seen a man in a grey coat drift past without buying anything, which was the single most suspicious thing a person could do in a market.

"It's not for you," Freya said quietly. "Not tonight. Take the stock you have and go home early, Mare."

Maren's eyebrows climbed. "Because of the grey coats?"

"Because of the grey coats."

"Child, I've been selling herbs on this row since before you were weaned. I know every Warden captain by his limp. It's the third Tuesday. Third Tuesdays they're always short-staffed because half of them drink at the Gilded Cask and the other half lie about it." Maren began bundling her herbs again, brisk and unbothered. "Go home early yourself, if you're spooked. Sell your strings to somebody who believes in them."

Freya wanted to argue. There was a version of herself — an older version, a version with a sword on her hip and a god in her ear — who would have grabbed the old woman by the shoulder and marched her out of the market bodily. But that version had been dead fifteen years, and the woman who'd replaced her sold knots and kept her head down and told herself that warnings were the same thing as help.

She gave Maren the tincture anyway. Slid the bottle across beneath a fold of cloth, quick, done before either of them acknowledged it. Maren took it with the smoothness of long practice and hid it among the feverfew, and for a moment their eyes met over the bundles, and Freya thought: *if it goes wrong someday, this is the moment I'll remember. This small conspiracy. This warmth.*

"Get home safe, Mare."

"I always do." Maren smiled, gap-toothed and wicked. "Third Tuesday, girl. Go count your coins."

* * *

It was not the third Tuesday.

Freya was halfway through packing up — stacking her cords into their cedar box, banking the coals of her little brazier — when the screaming started at the top of the row.

She knew the sound. Everyone in Corvale knew the sound; children learned it in their cradles the way they learned lullabies. It wasn't the screaming of a fight or a theft, which had a shape to it, a rise and fall. This was the flat, continuous shriek of a crowd parting, and underneath it, cutting through everything, the whistle the Wardens used: three rising notes, three falling notes, repeated. *Clear the way. Clear the way. Law comes through.*

Freya's hands went still on the cedar box.

Then she did what she had trained herself to do for fifteen years, what she could do now faster than thought, with the smooth efficiency of a soldier falling back into old formation: she became nothing. She closed the box. She banked the coals fully. She pulled her plain grey hood up over her hair and stepped back into the shadow between her awning post and the tannery wall, where the lamplight didn't reach, and she watched.

They came down the row in a wedge of six, torches out despite the hour, and the crowd peeled away from them like skin from a scalded hand. Grey cloaks, black boots, the sunburst-and-bars of the Magistrate's law stitched silver on every shoulder. And in the center of the wedge, held by both arms, struggling with a ferocity that would have been funny in any other context —

Maren.

They'd already tipped her stall. Freya watched the bundles of feverfew and chamomile scatter under boots. Watched someone — a young Warden, barely shaving, face white and set — hurl a lantern into the spilled herbs, and watched the dried plants catch with the soft, horrible eagleness of dry things burning. Flames ran up the tannery wall. Maren's awning went up like a struck match.

"— charged under the Edifice Statute, article seven," a Warden was reciting, in the bored voice of a man reading a shopping list, "trafficking in unlicensed workings, corruption of civic order—" He was middle-aged, heavyset, with the weary competence of a career officer. Freya didn't know him. That was almost worse. At least if she'd known him she'd have known how much he could be bought with.

"I sell *herbs*," Maren was shouting, and her voice cracked on it, and Freya heard — everyone on the row must have heard — the precise moment the old woman realized that the distinction she had leaned on for forty years, *herbs not hexes, teas not spells*, was worth exactly nothing here. "I sell herbs, you idiot boys, I sell *herbs*—"

A Warden struck her across the mouth to shut her up. Not hard, by their standards. Enough.

Freya stood in her shadow with her nails biting through the wool of her hood into her palms, and she did the arithmetic.

Six Wardens. One sword among them visible, the rest with truncheons and one crossbow. Freya had killed men carrying better than that. Fifteen years ago she had cleared a bridge at Vasser's Ford with eleven others against four times their number, and Vaskar had been singing in her blood like a struck bell, and she had come off that bridge alive while the enemy's banners burned behind her.

Fifteen years ago.

Now she had forty cords, a tin of buttons, a lockbox with three bottles of tinctures that worked and nine that didn't, and a body that ached in the mornings. And if she stepped out of this shadow, she died on this row, and it wouldn't save Maren — six Wardens would become sixteen by moonrise, and they'd sweep the whole market, and every seller of knots and teas and love-potions from here to the Gate would burn with her.

That was the arithmetic. She ran it twice. It came out the same both times, cold and clean and unforgivable.

The Wardens dragged Maren past Freya's stall — so close that the old woman's flailing sleeve brushed the counter where Freya sold her luck. For one instant their eyes met.

Maren Ostwyk did not call out to her. Did not say her name, did not betray her with a glance or a plea. What she did was worse. She looked at Freya — directly, deliberately, through the shadow and the hood as if the shadow weren't there — and she shook her head. Once. A small, tired motion. *Don't. Stay where you are, girl. Don't be a fool.*

Then they dragged her around the elbow of the row, toward the processional stair, toward the white noise of the Falls two districts away where the water fell forever and the Magistrate's law sent the ones it called corrupted, and the crowd closed up behind the Wardens like water behind a keel, and the fire crackled on the tannery wall, and Freya Hartwell stood absolutely still in the dark and let it all happen.

* * *

She stayed until the fire brigade came, because leaving immediately would look like something, and she stayed another hour after that, because her legs had decided independently of her that they weren't going anywhere. She helped douse the last of it. A bucket line formed out of habit; nobody spoke to her, and she spoke to nobody. The tannery wall was scorched but standing. Maren's stall was a black square of wet ash and trampled herbs, and the smell — burnt feverfew, burnt chamomile, the sweetish reek of the tinctures boiling away in their bottles — clung to everything.

The three bottles. Freya found one shard of green glass near the awning post and understood that whatever the Wardens had inventoried, they hadn't inventoried well, or they'd taken it all, and either way Maren's secret stock was gone into Warden evidence sacks along with forty years of quiet practice and probably the names of everyone Maren had ever traded with.

Including, eventually, a charm-seller two stalls down who bought unlicensed tinctures and had been seen doing it, if anyone cared to ask the neighbors. Someone always cared to ask the neighbors. That was how the Wardens worked: slow, patient, thorough as water finding cracks.

Freya walked home the long way, through the tanners' district, past the shrine.

There were shrines all over Corvale — little stone niches in walls, most of them, worn smooth-faced gods whose names nobody used anymore, because using them was article seven too. This one was a niche in a tannery wall with a squat stone figure whose features time had erased, and a shallow basin before it where rain collected, and someone had left a stub of candle burned down to a coin of wax.

Freya stopped. She didn't know why. She never stopped at shrines; stopping at shrines was the act of a woman who believed prayers went somewhere, and Freya had retired that woman with full military honors fifteen years ago.

But she stood there in the dark with ash in her hair and Maren's head-shake printed on the inside of her eyelids, and she heard herself say, out loud, in a voice like gravel:

"She was kind. She was kind for forty years and you people — wherever you went — you let them drag her to the Falls for it."

The stone face said nothing. Stone faces were excellent at that. Fifteen years of practice.

Freya laughed, once, ugly. "Right. Yes. Thank you for your counsel, as ever."

She turned to go, and her hand — reaching out blindly to steady herself against the wall, just an old mercenary's tired hand finding a wall — landed in the basin.

The water was warm.

Warm, on a night cool enough to see your breath. Warm like a body. And through her palm, faint as a pulse through a wrist, Freya felt a rhythm she knew the way you know your own heartbeat: *da-DA, da-DA, da-da-DA.* Marching cadence. Battle-time. The beat every soldier of Vaskar had drilled to, the rhythm of the god's own heart, which she had felt once a day for twelve years of service and had not felt in fifteen years of silence.

It lasted three beats. Then it was gone, and the basin was cold rainwater, and the squat stone face was only stone, and Freya Hartwell stood in a tannery alley with her hand in a puddle, shaking.

Somewhere across the city, faint under the wind, the Hollow Falls roared on like something breathing in its sleep.
