## 3. An Answered Prayer

Freya Hartwell had a rule about mornings: she did not trust them.

Mornings in Corvale were when the Wardens changed shift, which meant they were when the new shift tried to prove itself by being twice as thorough as the old one. Mornings were when the fishmongers on Tanner's Row set out their catch and their opinions with equal volume. And mornings were when the city's beggars — the ones who survived the night, at least — made their slow pilgrimage to the wayside shrines that still stood in the cracks of the Magistrate's tidy city like weeds through flagstones.

Freya was not a beggar. She had a stall, a roof, and a kettle that only whistled when it felt like it. But she walked that same pilgrim road most mornings anyway, because her route to the market took her past the shrine at Cooper's Cut, and because fifteen years of habit is not a thing you can simply decide to stop being.

The shrine at Cooper's Cut was nothing much to look at. A squat grey stone, knee-high, worn smooth on top where generations of hands had rested their palms. Someone had once carved a sunburst into its face, but the Warden chisels had gotten to it decades ago, leaving only a shallow scar that might have been a flower or a wheel or nothing at all. There were no offerings left there anymore. No candles, no coins, no ribbons. The Magistrate's law said the shrines could stand but not be honored, which everyone understood to mean: we will let the stones rot rather than spend the money to pull them up.

This morning, someone was kneeling at it.

A child. A girl, Freya guessed, seven or eight, though hunger and grime made such guesses unreliable. She wore a coat three sizes too big with the sleeves rolled into thick doughnuts at her wrists, and she knelt in the mud with her small palms flat on the shrine-stone as if it were the only solid thing left in the world.

Freya slowed. She told herself it was because stopping would look suspicious, and moving along briskly was what an honest charm-seller did. But her feet had already decided otherwise.

The girl was praying. Not the way people prayed in Corvale anymore — the quick, guilty mumble of a hand over the mouth, eyes darting for Wardens — but properly, aloud, the way prayers had been said back when saying them didn't cost anything worse than your pride.

"Please," the girl was saying, to the stone, to the air, to whatever might be listening down in the dark places under the world. "Please. Tammy's got the fever and Mam's got nothing and I prayed every night this week and nobody's come and please. Please. I'll give anything. I'll be good forever. Please."

Freya's chest did something complicated and unwelcome. She knew that prayer. She'd said versions of it herself, once, in another life, in another voice, kneeling in the mud of battlefields instead of city streets. *I'll give anything.* The gods' favorite currency, and the one they so rarely spent.

"Girl." She hadn't meant to speak. Her mouth went ahead without her, the way it did. "You shouldn't pray out loud here."

The child looked up. She had a chipped tooth and enormous brown eyes and the particular stubbornness of the very young and very desperate, which no amount of hardship ever quite sands away. "He listens better when it's loud."

"*He*?"

"Whoever's there." She shrugged, sleeves sliding down over her fists. "Tammy says nobody's there. But Tammy also says the fever's nothing, and Tammy's got it, so."

Freya crouched down beside her, which put her knees in the mud and her face level with the girl's, and glanced up and down Cooper's Cut. Empty, except for a cat with strong opinions regarding them both, watching from a wall.

"Listen," Freya said, keeping her voice low and practical, the voice she used for selling charms and talking people out of foolish hope. "There's nothing listening. That's not cruelty, that's just the weather. If your sister's sick, what you need is feverfew and willow bark, and I know a woman who trades in both, and I'll knock the price down for you because you've got honest knees. Prayers are free, but free doesn't break a fever."

The girl looked at her with the flat pity children reserve for adults who have said something profoundly stupid. "Then why're you crying?"

"I'm not crying. It's the wind off the river."

"There isn't any wind."

"It's a very local wind."

But her hand had gone to her cheek and come away wet, and she hadn't noticed it happen, and that frightened her more than any Warden patrol. Fifteen years, she had kept her grief folded up small and tucked somewhere behind her ribs, and this grubby child had gone and shaken it loose in four sentences on a public street.

The girl turned back to the stone. She patted it, twice, the way you'd pat a tired horse. "Please," she said again, quieter now, just between her and the rock. "Please, please, please. Tammy's burning up. Please."

And the stone answered.

Not with thunder. Not with light. Nothing so obliging as that. What happened was this: the shrine-stone at Cooper's Cut, which had stood deaf and dumb through two decades of Warden law, grew warm beneath the girl's small hands — warm as a living thing, warm as bread, warm as a body — and a sound came up out of it, low and rhythmic, like a drum heard through a hillside.

Freya knew that rhythm.

She knew it the way you know your own heartbeat. She knew it from a hundred dawns on a hundred ridgelines, when the company stood in their ranks and Vaskar's priests — real priests, with real voices — beat the war-cadence on their shields. *Dum. Dum-da-dum. Dum.* The cadence that meant *stand*. The cadence that meant *we hold, and because we hold, you may sleep safe*. The cadence that had been sung into her bones at nineteen years old by a god whose voice she had heard exactly once and never forgotten and never heard again.

The stone was humming Vaskar's battle-rhythm.

Freya sat down fully in the mud. It seemed the polite thing to do, given the circumstances.

"Did you hear that?" the girl whispered. Her eyes had gone wide and shining, and she was looking at Freya now with something terrible in her face. Hope, Freya thought. That's what hope looks like up close. Nobody tells you how frightening it is.

"Hear what?" said Freya, who had heard it, who was hearing it still, who could feel it climbing up through her palm where she had braced her hand against the stone without meaning to. Warm. Gods, it was warm. Fifteen years of cold silence and the stone was *warm*, and under the warmth ran that patient, unmistakable pulse. *Dum. Dum-da-dum. Dum.*

"You heard it." The girl grabbed her sleeve with both hands, doughnut cuffs and all. "You went all funny. You heard Him!"

"I don't know what you think I—"

"He answered!" The child was on her feet now, spinning, laughing, and Freya saw with a lurch that she was crying too, great gulping sobs of joy, the way children cry when the world does something kind. "He heard me! He heard me, he heard me, he *heard* me!" She flung her arms around the shrine-stone and hugged it like a grandmother, and the stone hummed on beneath her cheek, steady as a sleeping heart.

Freya stayed in the mud and did not move.

Because here was the thing — here was the thing that her hands had known before her head, that had sent her sprawling into the dirt while the child celebrated. The cadence wasn't just familiar. It was *specific*. Every god had a rhythm, the way every smith had a hammer-mark. The Mother of Rivers moved in long rolling swells. The Lantern Twins ticked, quick and bright. And Vaskar, the Shield-Bearer, the Quiet God, the god of standing fast when standing fast cost everything —

*Dum. Dum-da-dum. Dum.*

Her god. Her silent, absent, possibly-imaginary god, humming up out of a roadside rock after fifteen years of nothing, answering a fever-prayer from a beggar child in the worst district of Corvale.

It should have been joy. Some distant part of Freya understood that it was supposed to be joy, the way you understand that a feast is supposed to make you happy even when your stomach's turned. But what she actually felt, sitting in the mud with her heart slamming against her ribs, was fear. Cold, crawling, full-bodied fear, because she had built a whole life on the silence. She had taken the silence and made it into a floor and built a shop on top of it and learned to live there. *The gods are dead or gone or never were; sell your charms honestly, keep your head down, survive.* That was the architecture of everything she was. And this morning, quietly, warmly, patiently, the ground had started to hum.

"Miss?" The girl had stopped hugging the stone and was peering at her with that unnerving directness. "You look scared."

"I'm not scared. It's the wind."

"There still isn't any wind."

"You're a very literal child, do you know that?"

"What's your name?"

"Freya." It slipped out before she could stop it. Rule one of the invisible life: no names in the open air. She'd broken it for a child in the mud, because apparently fifteen years of discipline lasted exactly until someone hugged a rock.

"Freya." The girl tried it out, tasting it. "I'm Pip. Well, Pippa, but Pip's faster." She looked down at the shrine-stone, and her joy dimmed a little, folding itself around the worry underneath. "Tammy's still sick, though. Being heard isn't fixing her."

"No," Freya said. "It wouldn't. Gods are — " *Dead,* she almost said. *Gone. A story soldiers tell.* But the stone was warm under her hand and humming her god's own heartbeat, and the word wouldn't come. "Gods are slow," she finished. "They hear you today and answer next month, sometimes. Or they don't answer at all and expect you to carry on regardless." She heard her own bitterness arrive in the sentence too late to stop it.

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "That sounds rude of them."

"It is, frankly, extremely rude of them."

"So will you help? Since He's slow?" Pip dug in the enormous coat and produced, triumphantly, a button. Brass, military issue, stamped with the Magistrate's sunburst — probably prised off a Warden's discarded uniform, which took either courage or a complete absence of sense. "I'll give anything. This is anything."

Freya looked at the button. Then at the child. Then at the stone, which pulsed on, indifferent and alive, *dum, dum-da-dum, dum*, like a drummer boy marching toward something she couldn't see yet.

"Keep your button," Freya said. "Come on. Feverfew first, miracles second. And Pip — "

"Yeah?"

"When you pray, from now on? Pray quietly. Loud prayers get people burned in this city." She said it gently, but she said it, because someone had to, and because the alternative was letting this child learn it from a Warden's pyre.

Pip nodded solemnly, then ruined the effect entirely. "Can I still hug the rock?"

Freya opened her mouth to say no, to say *never touch it, never go near it, forget all of this*, and found that she couldn't. Because the truth — the truth sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal — was that she wanted to hug the rock herself. Wanted it so badly her teeth ached. Fifteen years of silence, and one muddy morning had undone all of it with four beats of an old drum.

"Hug the rock," she said. "Quietly."

* * *

She bought the feverfew herself. Told herself it was an investment in customer loyalty, which was a lie so transparent that even Bram would have winced, and Bram had once accepted payment in chickens. She walked Pip home — a lean-to in the shadow of the tannery walls, where a woman with hollow cheeks pressed Freya's hand too hard and called her "saint" until Freya corrected it to "saleswoman" — and by the time she reached the night market square, the morning was properly awake and her stall needed setting up.

She set it up. She always set it up. That was the point of a life built on routine: it held together even when the person inside it did not.

But her hands shook arranging the charms, and she caught herself laying them out in rows of five, spaced like shield-wall intervals, the way Vaskar's quartermasters had laid out rations on campaign. Old habits surfacing like corpses in a flooded river. She flipped the row over. Laid them out in threes instead. It looked wrong. She flipped them back.

At midday, unable to stand it, she closed the stall early — unheard of, scandalous, the other vendors would talk — and walked back to Cooper's Cut alone.

The shrine-stone was cool.

Cool as any rock, smooth as ever, dumb as ever, the shallow scar of the chiseled sunburst holding nothing but afternoon light. Freya stood over it for a long moment, feeling like a fool, then knelt, then pressed both palms flat against it the way she had seen a thousand soldiers do at a thousand altars, and waited.

Nothing. Stone was stone. The wind off the river — an actual wind, this time, she checked — moved cold across the back of her neck.

"Answer her, then, but not me," she said quietly. "Is that how it is? Fifteen years I carried you. Fifteen years I kept the rites when keeping them was worth a pyre. I sang the dawn-cadence alone in my room like a madwoman every single — " Her voice cracked and she hated it and pushed on. "Every single week. For fifteen years. And you give your first word in two decades to a child who doesn't know the words?"

The stone said nothing. Of course it did. Stones were famous for it.

But as she knelt there, furious and foolish and grieving all over again, something happened that she would turn over in her mind for many nights afterward. Beneath her palms — faint, so faint she might have invented it, might have dreamed it, might have been going quietly mad at last as Bram had always predicted — she felt it.

One pulse. *Dum.*

Then nothing.

Just stone. Just the wind. Just Corvale going about its lawful business above whatever lay beneath it.

Freya rose slowly, wiping her hands on her apron, and looked down at the shrine as one looks at a door that has just been knocked upon from the other side.

The gods were waking. Something down in the roots of the world had stirred, and it had stirred *here*, in her city, under the Wardens' noses, and it was answering prayers with her god's own rhythm — or something wearing it.

She thought of the hedge-witch Marta two stalls down, who'd joked last week that the charms had been *singing* lately, and they'd laughed about it over cheap wine. She thought of the fever-bright eyes of the girl called Tammy, who would wake tomorrow either healed or not, and whose sister believed with her whole small body that somebody up — or down — there had said yes.

She thought, unwillingly, of Sera. Of a drawn blade and a younger face and a promise made in blood and mead that neither of them had managed to keep. If the gods were waking in Corvale, then the Wardens would know soon enough. And Sera Voss, Captain of the Wardens, would come to Cooper's Cut the way winter comes — inevitable, thorough, and without mercy for anything growing in the cracks.

Freya pressed her hand to the stone one last time. Cool. Silent. Patient.

"All right," she said to it, to him, to whatever was listening down in the dark. "You get one more chance. One. And if you spend it on anybody's fever but mine, so help me —"

The stone stayed quiet. But walking away, three streets on, heart still hammering, Freya realized she was smiling, wild and unwelcome as a spring thaw, and that terrified her more than the Wardens ever had.

Because hope, she remembered now, hope was the thing that got people killed. Hope made you visible. Hope made you kneel at shrines and sing loud prayers and stand in shield-walls when the smart thing was to run.

Hope, she thought, watching the smoke of the evening fires rise over the Falls in the distance — and feeling, beneath her feet, through boot-leather and cobblestone and fifty feet of buried earth, the faintest echo of a drum —

Hope was exactly what the gods counted on.
