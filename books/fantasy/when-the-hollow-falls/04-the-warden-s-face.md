## 4. The Warden's Face

Captain Sera Voss had a theory about interrogations, and it was this: everyone talked eventually, but the ones who mattered talked to themselves first.

So she let Maren Cotter sit in the holding room for two hours before she went down. Let the woman have her water, her blanket, her long argument with the ceiling. Sera spent those hours upstairs with the confiscated stock from the night market raid spread across three tables — charms, sachets, knotted cords, little clay tokens stamped with symbols that meant nothing to anyone who hadn't been paying attention for fifteen years.

Sera had been paying attention for fifteen years. It was practically all she did.

"Report," she said, and Corporal Ellis, who had the misfortune of standing closest, snapped upright like a startled cat.

"Forty-one items catalogued, Captain. All low-grade. Warming charms, milk-preserving knots, that sort of thing. Nothing battle-grade." He hesitated, which was unlike him. "Except."

"Except."

"The stall next to the hedge-witch's. Charm-seller named Hartwell. Her goods cleared inspection."

Everything in Sera went very still and very polite, which her officers knew was the dangerous version of her. "Hartwell's goods always clear inspection," she said. "She has a vendor's license older than half this roster and a gift for looking boring. Move along, Corporal."

Ellis moved along. Sera stood alone among the dead charms of a woman she'd never met, and thought about a name she'd been trying not to think about since dawn.

*Freya Hartwell.* Some names you could go years without saying aloud, and then one day a corporal said them for you, casual as weather, and the whole scar split open like it was fresh.

Sera straightened a warming charm on the table. It wasn't Freya's fault that her name existed. It wasn't even, strictly speaking, against the law.

It just felt like an ambush every time.

* * *

Maren Cotter turned out to be smaller than Sera remembered. That happened to people once the Wardens got hold of them — they shrank in the cells, folded themselves up like letters no one wanted to read. She sat on the bench with her hands around a cup of water, and when Sera came in, the hedge-witch looked at her with something worse than fear.

Recognition. And under it, pity.

"Don't," Sera said, sitting down across the table. "Whatever you're about to feel sorry at me for, save it. I'm not the one in chains."

"No," Maren agreed mildly. "Though I expect it chafes all the same." She was perhaps fifty, with burn-scarred forearms — real burns, workmanlike, the kind you got from a life over a cauldron rather than a life under suspicion — and eyes the color of pond water. She drank her water like it was tea she'd paid good money for. Sera found herself disliking her enormously, mostly because she couldn't rattle her.

"You know why you're here."

"I set fire to nothing, hurt no one, and sold a farmer's wife a charm that kept her milk sweet through a hot summer," Maren said. "But I expect I'm here because magic is outlawed, and I have some."

"That's the whole of the law, yes."

"Mm. Simple laws are how you know someone was in a hurry."

Sera laid the evidence folder on the table between them. Procedure said she should open with the charges, establish the timeline, give the prisoner every chance to hang herself honestly. Sera had written half the procedure manual herself. She knew exactly what came next, and she did it, because duty was a muscle and muscles only worked if you used them even when your hands shook.

"Walk me through last night."

So Maren walked her through it. The market, the customers, the boy who bought a fever-charm for his sister. The Wardens arriving with torches. It was all ordinary, all damning, all beside the point, until Sera opened the folder and slid the inventory sketch across the table.

"Your supplier," Sera said. "The one who's been keeping you stocked. We found these sigils worked into the charm-bases. Not your hand — your knots are sloppy and hers aren't. Whose is it?"

And there it was. The moment. Sera had known it was coming since the raid, had felt it circling her like a gull over a fish cart, but knowing didn't blunt it. Because there on the paper was the mark: a broken circle crossed by a single vertical stroke, with three small nicks on the left arc.

She'd drawn that mark herself once, badly, on the back of another woman's hand with charcoal, laughing, the night their company won the Vellin contract. *"There. Now you're marked property of the worst quartermaster in the Marches."* And Freya had laughed and said, *it's not a brand, Sera, it's a promise — the circle's broken so the luck can get in,* and Bram had grumbled that the luck got out just as often, thank you very much, and the whole smoky tent had smelled of stew and wet wool and the particular happiness of people who did not yet know how things would end.

Fifteen years, and her body still recognized the shape before her mind caught up. Three nicks. Broken circle. The Gray Company's mark, drawn into the base-glaze of forty-one harmless charms sold by a woman who'd been careful for fifteen years and had finally, quietly, stopped being careful enough.

"I don't know whose mark that is," Maren Cotter said.

Sera looked up. The hedge-witch was watching her with those pond-water eyes, and her face had gone carefully blank in a way that told Sera she knew *exactly* whose mark it was, and knew, further, who was sitting across the table, and had decided — the stubborn, foolish, decent old woman — to lie to a Warden captain on behalf of somebody else's ghost.

"You don't know," Sera repeated.

"I'm old," Maren said. "Marks blur. Could be anyone's."

"It's a company sigil. Pre-Interdiction mercenary work. There were maybe thirty companies using marks like this, and I can name twenty-nine of them." Sera kept her voice level, bored, procedural. "This one belonged to the Gray Company. Disbanded fifteen years ago. Its members scattered or died. One of them sells charms two stalls down from you and has an unimpeachable license and a habit of donating to the wardens' benevolent fund."

"Funny," said Maren. "Sounds like a pillar of the community."

"She is a civilian," Sera said, and heard the crack in her own voice, hairline-thin, and hated it. "She has no gift. She never did. Whatever she supplied you, it's trinkets and folk-medicine and nothing the Magistrate's law touches. So consider your options carefully, Mistress Cotter, because right now the only person in this city with any reason to burn is you."

Silence. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the yard. Maren Cotter turned her cup slowly on the table, around and around, and then looked up with something almost gentle.

"When you say she has no gift," the witch said, "you say it like a prayer. Like you've checked recently."

"That's not relevant—"

"You've checked," Maren said, wonderingly. "You keep checking. Fifteen years, I'd wager, and her name stuck in you like a splinter. You know what we called that, back when there were more of us? Watching over someone. Tending. It's nearer to prayer than anything the Magistrate allows, and you do it every day, and you'll stand there and tell me the gods are dead and the faithful deserve the Falls."

"The gods are dead," Sera said flatly. "Or silent. Or they were never there. It changes nothing. What matters is what's true this morning, and this morning you're a convicted hedge-witch headed to the Falls unless you give me something useful."

"And if I give you her?" Maren asked softly. "Is that what you want? Someone to say the name out loud so you can stop carrying it?"

Sera stood up so fast the bench scraped. For one unprofessional heartbeat she wanted to put her fist through the wall, or through the window, or through the infuriating calm of a woman who had read her in ten minutes flat when her own captain hadn't managed it in fifteen years.

"The transport to the Falls leaves at dusk," she said. "Confess fully and cooperate, and I can request censure instead of burning. First offense, nonviolent, cooperative prisoner. It's within my authority."

"It's within your authority," Maren agreed. "Will you use it?"

Sera didn't answer. They both knew the answer. Censure requests went to the Magistrate's clerks, and the clerks had quotas, and quotas needed fires. Sera could write the request; she could not write the outcome. They both knew that too. It hung in the room between them, ugly and obvious.

"That's what I thought," said Maren Cotter, and drank her water.

* * *

She requested the censure anyway. She wrote it out in her precise captain's hand, cited precedent, attached recommendations, and sealed it, knowing — she estimated — four chances in five that the clerks would convert it to a full sentence anyway, because the Falls had been hungry lately. Everyone said so. The mist off the falls had been thicker these past weeks, and the ash came down blacker, and twice now Sera had stood at the rail above the plunge pool and felt, distinctly and absurdly, like something down there had turned over in its sleep.

Superstition. She was the captain of the Wardens. Superstition was what they burned.

In her office, she took out the company roster.

It wasn't official. It would never appear in any archive. It was a page in her own handwriting, fifteen years old and rewritten whenever the guilt got loud, listing every soul who'd ever worn the Gray Company mark — because when the Interdiction came down and the companies disbanded, Sera Voss had made a cold calculation: someday, someone would come asking about the old soldiers, and she intended to be the one who answered the door.

She'd used it twice. Once to warn a former scout that a Warden investigator was sniffing at his parish. Once to attend a funeral in plain clothes, standing at the back while they buried the company's drummer in unconsecrated ground, because the man had been faith-touched and the law said even his grave was suspect.

Thirty-one names. Tonight she read them all, the way she always did, a rosary without a god. Then she dipped her pen.

Three of the names had to go. The hedge-witch's testimony, thin as it was, pointed toward active supply lines in the undercity, and if the investigation widened, anyone connected to the Gray Company mark became evidence. Better that her record showed those three as unknowns, untraceable, faded ink. Duty. Cold, necessary, the whole weight of the badge pressing down on the pen.

She struck the first name. Struck the second. Her hand was steady. She was very good at steady.

The third line read: *Hartwell, Freya. Charmwright (unlicensed, formerly). Corvale night market.*

The pen hovered.

It was ridiculous. Nobody knew Freya had ever carried the mark except Sera, Bram, and the dead. The inventory sketch proved nothing — sigils got copied, traded, forged; half the charm-sellers in the Marches stole each other's flourishes. There was no chain leading from that broken circle to Freya Hartwell unless someone already knew where to look, and the only person alive who'd look was sitting at this desk refusing to strike her own name off her own private list, which was either loyalty or madness and had been both for fifteen years.

*Duty hardens,* her old commander used to say. *That's what it's for. Soft hearts make soft laws, and soft laws get people killed.*

Her old commander had also, Sera now remembered with a strange lurch, drunk himself to death within a year of the Interdiction, whispering apologies to a god he claimed not to believe in. Perhaps duty hardened, and perhaps it only buried you alive in your own uniform.

"He has no gift," Sera said aloud to her empty office, testing the words. "She has no gift. Never did."

The lamp guttered. Outside, far off toward the river, thunder rolled though the sky was clear, and beneath it — softer, rhythmic, unmistakable to anyone who'd ever marched behind drums — something that sounded like footsteps. Like a cadence. Like a war-drum keeping time under the world.

Sera sat very still for a long moment. Then she drew a single line through the third name — light, deliberate, deniable — set down the pen, and gave the order that would send her best riders to the night market before dawn.

Not for Hartwell. Officially, not for Hartwell. Officially, a routine sweep of unlicensed vendors, standard procedure, nothing personal.

But she chose the riders herself, picked the three least observant men on her roster, and wrote the orders in language vague enough that a clever charm-seller with old eyes in dark windows might just read it coming.

Duty, she told herself, hardening over the soft spot like ice over deep water.

The water underneath, she did not look at.
