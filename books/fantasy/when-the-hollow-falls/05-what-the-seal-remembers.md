## 5. What the Seal Remembers

The hum did not let go of her.

Freya had spent the walk home from the shrine-stone telling herself it would fade. That was how charms worked, after all — you held them too long and they got into your blood like cheap wine, singing their little songs until you mistook the song for your own thoughts. She'd sold a thousand luck-knots that had whispered *fortune, fortune* to buyers who never noticed the whisper at all.

This was not that.

She sat on her cot above the dye-shop with the shrine-fragment wrapped in three layers of wool inside an iron pot, because iron dampened resonance — first lesson of charm-work, second lesson of staying alive in Corvale — and she could still feel it. Not hear it. *Feel* it. A low pulse through the floorboards, up through her knees, settling somewhere behind her sternum like a fist deciding whether to knock.

It was keeping time.

That was the part she couldn't shake. The pulse wasn't random. It marched. Four beats, rest. Four beats, rest. She knew that cadence the way she knew her own heartbeat, because for eleven years she had drilled to it — shield-line advancing on four counts, javelins loosed on the rest. Vaskar's battle-cadence. The rhythm his priests beat on hide-drums until it lived in the marrow of every soldier who served him.

A dead god's drum shouldn't be keeping time in a scrap of wayside shrine.

"Stop it," she told the pot.

The pot, being a pot, said nothing. Fifteen years of practice at that particular silence. It was almost comforting.

Freya lay back and stared at the ceiling beams and tried very hard not to do arithmetic. But she was a charm-seller, and charm-sellers were arithmeticians of the soul — this much grief plus this much longing equals one purchase, one knot, one lie about a grandmother's blessing. Her mind did the sums whether she wanted them or not.

If the old gods were waking — if prayers were being answered all over Corvale like mold blooming after a wet spring — then the silence might have been a door closed rather than a room empty.

And if it was a door—

Then someone had locked it.

She didn't sleep. Around the third bell she gave up pretending, dressed in her plainest gray, wrapped the shrine-fragment against her skin under her shirt where its warmth pressed against her ribs like a small stubborn animal, and went down into the city before dawn could catch her at it.

* * *

Corvale was honestest at that hour, or as close as it came. The night market stalls shuttered, the watchmen drowsy at their posts, the great aqueduct throwing its long stone shadow across the Tanner Quarter. And beneath everything, so constant that no one born here heard it anymore: the Falls.

The Hollow Falls weren't waterfalls in any way that made sense to outlanders. The river Vell split at the city's heart and plunged into a chasm the founders had never explained, falling into darkness without sound — that was the horror of it, the thing children learned young. A hundred tons of water a minute, swallowed whole, and not a whisper back. Pilgrims used to say the river fell into the gods' cupped hands. The Magistrate's histories said it fell into a natural cavern system. Neither version accounted for the way the air above the rim tasted faintly of struck flint, like the moment before lightning.

Freya hadn't been to the rim in fifteen years. Soldiers of Vaskar had made pilgrimage there once, before the Purges — she remembered standing at the rail with Sera, both of them nineteen and invincible, watching the water vanish, and Sera had said, "Where does it *go*, though?" and Freya had said, "Don't be a hedge-priest," and they'd laughed, and that had been the whole of her theology: laughter at the rim of the abyss, shoulder to shoulder with her sworn sister.

Now she walked toward it alone through streets gone gray with pre-dawn, following a pulse only she could feel, and she thought: *this is either the most important thing I've ever done or the most stupid.*

Given her history, the odds were even.

The rim-walk was barred. Of course it was. Wardens' law: the Falls were a place of execution, not contemplation, and the faithful were forbidden within fifty paces of the rail lest they contaminate the justice. Two ward-posts flanked the stair down, lanterns burning low, and Freya assessed them with the professional eye of a woman who'd scouted river crossings for a living. One guard awake and bored. One asleep and honest about it. The bored one she could manage.

But she didn't need the stair.

Because the hum wasn't coming from the rim. It was coming from below — from the old pilgrim tunnels, the ones that had been walled shut a century ago and then walled again by the Wardens, badly, because masons who built walls for fear worked quickly and cut corners. Every wall in Corvale had a scar somewhere. You just had to know which scars to press.

She found hers behind a tannery's lime-heaps, a seam of newer mortar in old stone, and beyond it a crawlspace that smelled of two centuries of pigeon. She went through with her cheek in guano and her elbows screaming, and emerged in the Pilgrim Dark — the under-gallery where the faithful had once walked to meet their god, walls covered in votive carvings worn smooth by ten thousand fingertips.

Her light-charm — the one honest piece of work she owned, a pebble her grandmother had actually blessed, worth more than everything else Freya carried combined — woke with a glow the color of dawn on water.

The carvings caught the light.

Freya stopped breathing.

They were war-carvings. Shields, spears, a host marching in ranks — and above them all, repeated across forty feet of gallery wall, the figure she knew better than her own face: the Bull-Crowned, broad-backed, hammer shouldered, leading the line. Vaskar. Here, under Warden Corvale, in numbers that must have taken generations of hidden hands to carve. Someone had scratched *the god walks among us* in Old Tongue beside the largest figure, and beneath it, smaller, more recent, desperate: *he has not answered. he has not answered. he has not—*

The last carving trailed off into a gouge, as if the hand had been interrupted.

Or arrested.

"Right," Freya said aloud, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You're going to explain yourself."

The fragment against her ribs pulsed. Four beats, rest.

And for the first time in fifteen years, something pulsed *back*.

* * *

The tunnel descended the way grief descends — gradually, then all at once. The pilgrim steps gave way to raw-cut rock, the carvings thinned and then vanished, and the air changed from pigeon-dust to something older: cold stone and river-mist and that flint-taste, stronger now, sharp enough to sting the back of the throat. The hum was no longer behind her sternum. It was everywhere, in the walls, in her teeth, in the wet black air, and it was louder, and it was not alone.

There were two rhythms now.

One was the cadence she knew — four beats, rest, steady as a shield-line, though thinner than memory, like a drum played with a cracked stick. Faint. Labored. But *hers*. Eleven years of marches lived in that rhythm.

The other was underneath it.

Not a rhythm at all, really. More like a hunger given tempo — a slow dragging pull, irregular, arrhythmic, the way a drowning man kicks. It didn't keep time. It *consumed* it. Where the drumbeat passed over it, the drag pulled the beats slightly apart, stretched them, and Freya felt each stretch physically, a hairline flexing somewhere behind her eyes.

Two voices in the water.

She came out onto a ledge above the Falls themselves and understood, finally, why the water made no sound.

The chasm opened beneath her like the throat of the world, and the river poured into it, and it did not fall so much as *arrive* — drawn down and down into a darkness that drank it without effort, without ripple, without end. Her light-charm threw its little glow perhaps thirty feet before the dark ate it. Below the reach of the light, impossibly far down, two lights moved. Or two shapes. One burned low and steady, banked-coal red, guttering. The other did not burn at all; it was an absence of light, a moving hole, vast and patient, circling the red one the way a shark circles a wounded whale.

The red one pulsed. Four beats, rest.

"Oh," Freya said. It came out very small. "Oh, you *idiot*. You magnificent, stubborn, *absent* idiot. You're *down there*."

She gripped the ledge-edge and the truth arrived in her the way floodwater arrives in a cellar — not all at once, but rising fast, finding every crack.

Fifteen years ago the prayers had stopped being answered. Not faded — stopped, between one dusk and the next, company-wide, empire-wide, every priest and soldier of Vaskar suddenly speaking into a well. The other gods had fallen silent in the years after, one by one, as the Wardens rose, and everyone said the new law had driven the old gods off, and Freya had believed she was simply the first abandoned instead of merely the earliest.

But the other gods hadn't gone silent. They'd been *muffled*. Slowly. And Vaskar — Vaskar had been silenced first, hardest, and most completely, because he was the strongest, because a war-god was the one thing the new order couldn't permit —

Because he was down here.

Because he was down here, wounded, banked to embers, holding a rhythm with the last of himself — and the thing circling him, the hungry absence, the hollow —

"You're not guarding him," Freya whispered, and the chasm took her words and gave nothing back. "You're *caging* him together with *that*."

The founders. The Wardens' founders, the nine saints in their stained glass, the men whose names were on the law-courts and whose statues frowned from the Magistrate's hall. Everyone knew they had ended the Godswars by force of faith and fire. Nobody asked what force, exactly, was sufficient to stop a war-god mid-stride.

Now Freya stood at the throat of the world doing the arithmetic she couldn't refuse.

You cannot kill a god. Every hedge-wife knows it, every drunk philosopher agrees. So what do you do with one? You don't destroy it. You *bind* it. And a binding needs material, needs weight, needs a lock strong enough to hold a storm — and what is stronger than a god?

Another god.

They had wounded Vaskar and used him. Used his strength as chain and his body as vault, and sealed the twin — the hungry hollow circling below her, the shadow-self every war carries, all the slaughter and rage the Bull-Crowned had ever refused to become, cut loose and buried here since before the city had laws. And the seal needed feeding, or tending, or *silence* — and so the founders had outlawed prayer, burned the faithful, starved the old gods of voice and worship, because every answered prayer anywhere shook the lock.

Every miracle was a crack in the seal. Every warming stone. Every child's wish granted.

And fifteen years of her prayers — her loyal, aching, nightly, humiliatingly sincere prayers — hadn't been ignored. They'd been *received* by a god lying wounded in the dark with a muzzle on his soul, listening to every word, unable to answer, answering anyway the only way left to him:

By holding the beat. Four counts, rest. Keeping his rhythm alive so that when she finally followed it down, she would know the way.

Freya Hartwell, celebrated nowhere, mercenary retired, seller of lucky knots, sat down hard on the cold ledge above the Hollow Falls and wept the way soldiers weep — silently, furiously, hating every second of it — for the god who had never forsaken her, and for the fifteen years she had spent cursing him for it.

The red light far below flickered. Steadied. Pulsed.

Four beats. Rest.

"I'm here," she said, when she could speak. Her voice cracked and she didn't care; there was no one to see and one ancient god to hear. "I heard you. Late. But I heard you. What do you need?"

The answer didn't come in words. It came in the fragment against her ribs, which flared hot enough to brand, and in the chasm itself, where the red light guttered lower, dimmer, and the hungry dark circled closer, closer, and the flint-smell sharpened to the taste of a strike about to fall.

And up through the stone, through the ledge, through the soles of her boots, Freya felt the seal flex — felt the whole vast wound in the world strain around its two prisoners — and understood with perfect clarity that it was failing, had been failing for fifteen years, was failing faster now that the prayers had started being answered again, and that whatever she had come down here to learn was the smallest part of what she had come down here to do.

Behind her, back up the tunnel, faint through two centuries of rock, she heard a sound she knew even better than the battle-cadence.

Boots. Many. Descending the pilgrim stair from the rim — the Wardens' stair, the lawful stair, coming down toward the dark with lanterns and law and, knowing her luck, a warrant.

Freya wiped her face, put her hand flat against the warm stone, and mouthed the oldest promise her tongue knew: *hold the line.*

Then she put out her light, and began to climb.
