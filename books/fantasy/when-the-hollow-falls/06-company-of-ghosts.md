## 6. Company of Ghosts

The road to Saltmere was three days' walk, two if you lied to your knees, and Freya had been lying to hers since dawn.

She told herself, for the first mile out of Corvale's southern gate, that this wasn't a choice. That the hum in her palm — that low, patient thrumming like a war-drum heard through a wall — had simply carried her feet along. That she was going to see Bram because Bram owed her money, which he did, four silver and a debt of insults dating back to the siege of Marrow Ford, and any sensible woman would collect on it.

It was a comfortable lie. Freya had learned to live inside comfortable lies the way other people lived inside houses: furnish them well, keep the drafts out, never look too hard at the walls.

By the second day she'd stopped pretending even to herself.

Saltmere wasn't a town so much as an apology for one — a scatter of roofs huddled against a gray sea, a harbor full of boats too old to sail far and fishermen too stubborn to care. It smelled of salt and tar and the particular despair of places the war had passed by. Which was, Freya supposed, exactly why a one-eyed quartermaster who wanted to be forgotten would settle here. Nobody went looking for anything in Saltmere. There was nothing here worth finding.

Except, apparently, her.

She found him at the end of the harbor road, mending nets outside a cottage whose chimney smoked with the cheerful industry of a man who had nothing better to do than keep warm. He was broader than she remembered, or perhaps the world had narrowed around him; his remaining eye — green as bottle glass, sharp as a fishhook — found her before she'd cleared the last fence, because of course it did. Bram had always claimed his lost eye saw better than the one he kept.

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he went back to his net.

"Four silver," Freya said.

"Hello to you too."

"You owe me four silver from Marrow Ford. You bet against me arm-wrestling Dossen and then paid out in promises."

"I paid you in *character*, girl. Character appreciates." His hands didn't stop working. The knots went through them like thoughts. "What are you doing here?"

Freya leaned against his fence. The heartstone-hum in her palm had gone quiet since morning, but she could feel it the way you feel an old scar in cold weather — present, waiting. "You heard about the shrines?"

"Saltmere has one shrine and no god in it. We burned our god's statue for firewood during the famine winter. Very practical town." He tied off a knot, bit the thread, finally looked up at her properly. "You've been crying."

"I have not."

"Your left eye does it when you lie. Goes all shiny, like a cat caught at the butter." He set down the net at last, and something in his face shifted — the lazy humor draining out of it like water from a cracked pot, leaving behind the man who had kept forty mercenaries fed, armed, and alive through nine years of bad contracts. "Sit down, Freya."

"I'll stand."

"Sit down," he said again, gently, "because I've been waiting fifteen years for this conversation, and my legs aren't what they were, and I don't want to have it shouting across a fence."

* * *

His cottage was small and smelled of pipe smoke and dried fish and the particular mustiness of a man living alone with his memories. He poured her tea without asking, which was Bram's way — he'd been deciding what people needed since before she could hold a sword, and he hadn't stopped just because there was no more company to quartermaster.

"The hum," Freya said, before the tea could make her soft. She set her hand flat on his table. "You know about it."

Bram sat down across from her. For a while he just studied her with his one eye, and she let him, because some examinations deserved patience.

"Vaskar answered prayers," he said at last. "Three days ago, in Corvale. At a wayside shrine. A child prayed and the stone sang." He said it flatly, factually, the way he'd once reported supply numbers. "Word travels. And I thought — well. I thought either the world's ending, or you'll be walking up my road within the week."

"So you knew." Her voice came out harder than she intended. "You knew the gods were waking, and you knew why they'd stopped, and you sat here mending nets."

"I knew pieces." He held up a scarred hand before she could speak. "Pieces, Freya. Not the whole. And what I knew, I swore an oath to bury deeper than a corpse. You want to be angry at me, get in line. There's a queue. It starts with me and ends somewhere past the horizon."

"Bram—"

"He gave us the stone." The words came out of him like a tooth being pulled. "Vaskar did. Before the silence. Do you understand what that means? A god doesn't hand over his own heart unless he expects to lose everything else."

The fire popped. Outside, a gull screamed its opinion of the weather.

"Tell me," Freya said. "All of it. Don't arrange it first. Don't make it kind."

Bram laughed, short and joyless. "Kindness is the one supply I never managed to stock." He leaned back, and his one eye went distant, looking at something fifteen years away.

* * *

"There were eleven of us at the founding. Not the company — the company came later, grew fat on contracts. Eleven original sworn swords, before Hartwell Company, before banners, back when we were just people who'd fought together and couldn't stomach going home. Vaskar walked among us as a man those days. Big fellow. Laughed like rockfall. Used to lose at dice on purpose so the young ones would eat better." Bram's mouth twisted. "I only figured out the losing-on-purpose part years later. A god who rigs games *in your favor*. There's humility for you."

"And the Wardens?"

"Came after. Magistrate Aldric Vane and his circle — clever men, lawful men, men who looked at a city full of squabbling cults and saw disorder. They wanted one law, one faith-shaped hole where faith used to be. Clean streets, silent temples." He tapped the table. "But you can't kill gods, girl. You can only move them. So they went to Vaskar with a bargain, and when he refused — because he refused, loudly, in front of witnesses — they took anyway."

"Took what?"

"His voice, first. There's a rite — old, older than the Wardens admit — that unmakes the bond between a god and his faithful. Cuts the thread. They performed it on him and thought that was the end of it." Bram shook his head slowly. "But a god isn't a lamp you can snuff. Vaskar had a twin. Born of the same war, same worship, same blood-soaked century. Where Vaskar took the prayers of soldiers and turned them into courage, his brother took the same prayers and turned them into *hunger*. Hollow-Vaskar, the old texts call him. The part of war that fights past the killing. The part that eats its own side."

Freya's hands had gone still around her cup. The tea was cold. She didn't remember it going cold.

"They bound the twin beneath the Falls," Bram continued. "And here's the part they don't write in any archive, the part I buried under oath: a binding needs an anchor. A sworn heart. Something freely given, holding it shut from the mortal side. The founders used—" he hesitated, and Freya watched him choose, deliberately, to break fifteen years of silence, "—they used *him*. They sealed Vaskar in alongside his twin. His presence holds the lock. His silence IS the lock. Every year he stays quiet, the seal holds. Every prayer he answers, every thread of connection to the faithful he lets himself feel, the seal strains."

The room tilted slightly. Freya gripped the table.

"Fifteen years," she said. "Fifteen years I prayed to him and got nothing. I thought—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "I lit candles. I kept the rites. I sold charms with his sigil and told desperate people luck would come, and half the time I believed it myself, and the whole time he was *right there*, listening, unable to say a single—"

"He heard every word." Bram's voice was gentle now, unbearably gentle. "That's the cruelty of it. The founders made sure he'd hear. A binding anchored in a god works best if the god is *suffering*, they wrote. Suffering anchors hold tighter. Cold scholarly language, but that's what it says." He reached into his shirt and pulled out something on a leather cord. "Which brings me to this."

The stone was small, dark red, unremarkable until the light caught it — and then it moved, the way embers move, the way coals breathe. The humming Freya had felt in the shrine-stone lived in it, faint and constant, a heartbeat wrapped in cloth.

"He gave it to us the night before they came for him. Told us to carry it far and forget it. Said if it ever began to sing, the seal was failing, and the last sworn of his company would have a choice to make." Bram set it on the table between them, but his hand stayed on it a moment longer. "There are four of the original eleven left, Freya. Two mad, one dead last spring. That leaves me, and my legs are done, and my eye sees mostly regret these days. It was always meant to come to you."

"Why me? I joined late. I'm not founding anything."

"You're the only one still praying." He pushed the cord across the wood. "The others stopped years ago. Stopped hurting, stopped hoping. But you — fifteen years of unanswered prayers, and you *kept going*. That's not foolishness, girl. That's what an anchor looks like from the mortal side."

Freya stared at the stone. It pulsed against the grain of the table, slow and patient, and she felt something in her chest crack open — not grief exactly, and not relief, but the terrible space between them where fifteen years of abandonment should have been and instead was *explanation*. Explanation was worse. Explanation meant there was no one to hate except everyone.

"If I take this," she said quietly, "I'm not a charm-seller anymore. I know how this goes. I did the mercenary thing. I know what happens to women who become important."

"Aye."

"People burn, Bram. Warden fires."

"Aye." He slid the stone the rest of the way. "So don't get caught."

She almost laughed. Almost. Instead she picked up the heartstone, and it was warm, warmer than her skin, and it settled against her sternum like it had been measured for the space.

"The seal's straining," Bram said. "The singing shrines mean Vaskar's reaching, or the twin's pulling, or both. And there's a Captain Voss running the Wardens now, from what the fishing boats gossip. Efficient, they say. Devout, they say." His one eye fixed on her, fishhook-sharp. "Anyone we knew?"

Freya stood. Her legs held, barely.

"Someone we knew," she said.

Bram nodded slowly and reached for his pipe. "Then you'd best decide quick, girl, whether she's the wall or the door. Because the stone's going to sing louder, and when it does, the Magistrate will hear it too — and old men who built their law on a stolen god don't surrender their thefts peacefully. They feed things to locks." He struck a match, and the flame lit his face from below, hollowing it. "Go home. Pray loud. And Freya —"

She paused at the door.

"When you see him — when he can finally speak — don't waste the first words on anger. Fifteen years is long enough to spend on that."

Outside, the sea wind hit her face, and against her chest, faint as a dying coal refusing to die, the heartstone hummed the old battle-cadence — the one the company used to drum on shield-rims before a charge.

Freya turned south toward Corvale, toward the Falls, toward the sister hunting her, and walked into a war she had promised herself was over.
