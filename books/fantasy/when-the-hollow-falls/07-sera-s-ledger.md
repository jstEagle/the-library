## 7. Sera's Ledger

The Warden barracks at Corvale Keep kept its fires lit all night, and Sera Voss had learned, over nine years of service, to hate that most of all.

Not the cold—she'd soldiered through colder in the border campaigns, back when she'd slept in mud and called it a bed. Not the work, either; paperwork was just another kind of patrol, and she was good at patrols. It was the fire itself. Every hearth in the Keep burned peat and oak and whatever else the quartermasters could buy cheap, but every Warden knew what the flames were *for*. Somewhere above the courtyard, a brazier of iron stood taller than a man, and once a week it took what the Falls did not.

Tonight there was no execution scheduled. Tonight the fire in her office grate was only fire, and Sera sat before it with a ledger on her knees and a decision she'd been avoiding for three days.

The ledger was old. Older than her captaincy, older than her commission, older than the neat black uniform hanging on its peg by the door—the uniform she still sometimes caught in the corner of her eye and mistook, for half a heartbeat, for someone else's armor. The ledger's cover was cracked leather gone soft as bread, and stamped into it in faded ink was a boar's head over crossed spears.

The mark of the Grey Boars. Free company. Disbanded fifteen years, scattered to the winds, mostly dead.

*Mostly.*

She opened it to the roster page and read the names again, though she knew them the way she knew the scar on her left forearm: without looking, without wanting to, forever.

There were forty-one names. She counted them out of habit, and then hated herself for counting, because counting was how you started an inventory, and inventories were how you decided what got written down and what got burned, and she had not yet decided anything. She was only sitting by a fire holding a book. That was all. A woman was allowed to sit by a fire.

"Captain?"

Her clerk, Pellam, stood in the doorway with a lamp and the particular apologetic posture of a man who had knocked twice already and been ignored twice already. He was twenty-four, round-faced, and possessed the rare gift of being afraid of Sera in exactly the right amount—not so much that he lied to her, not so little that he interrupted her at inconvenient moments without good cause.

"Pellam."

"The Magistrate's office sent a runner. Third one tonight." He held out a folded paper sealed in grey wax. "I told him you were at prayer."

"I wasn't at prayer."

"No, Captain. I know." He set the letter on her desk anyway and lingered, which he never did. "It's about the hedge-witch, isn't it. The one from the night market. The whole barracks is talking. They found a sigil in her things, they're saying. An old company mark. They're saying it matches—"

"Thank you, Pellam. That will be all."

He went. He was learning.

Sera broke the seal and read the letter standing up, because some letters you had to receive on your feet like a blow. It was short. The Magistrate's secretaries wrote short letters the way surgeons made short cuts.

*In light of evidence recovered from the witch Merrin Dole, and in light of recent irregularities at the wayside shrines, the Wardens are instructed to locate and detain one HARTWELL, FREYA, formerly of the mercenary designation GREY BOARS, presently residing within Corvale or its liberties. Description attached. The Hartwell woman is to be treated as a material witness in matters touching the founding covenant. She is not to be harmed. She is not to be spoken to beyond formal questioning. She is to be brought to the Keep alive and intact.*

*Alive and intact.* Someone upstairs had thought those words necessary, which meant someone upstairs knew exactly who Freya Hartwell was to her, which meant someone upstairs was watching to see what Sera Voss did next.

Sera read the description attached. *Female, mid-thirties. Dark hair, usually bound. Brown eyes. Scar, right brow. Carries herself like a soldier trying very hard not to.* She almost laughed at that last part. Whoever had drafted it had never seen Freya walk through a crowd. Freya didn't carry herself like a soldier avoiding notice; she carried herself like smoke avoiding a bottle. You could stand three feet from her in the night market and your eyes would slide off her the way rain slid off oiled canvas. It had always infuriated Sera, that talent. It had also, more than once, saved both their lives.

She put the letter face-down on the desk.

Then she looked at the fire, and at the ledger, and did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do for three days.

Forty-one names. If the Wardens pulled the thread of the sigil—if they traced the boar's-head mark from Merrin Dole's stall backward through fifteen years of quiet lives—they would find the ones who hadn't died. Oskar Nell, who ran a ferry now and named his boat after their dead sergeant. Tam Wicke, who'd lost his sword arm at Redford Bridge and taught letters to mill children with the other one. Old Marta, who'd buried two husbands and kept the company's pay-book hidden under her floorboards out of pure stubbornness, because somebody had to remember what they'd been owed.

And Freya. Freya selling charms in the dark like a woman apologizing for existing.

Sera had seen the sigil report that morning. Merrin Dole had scratched the boar's head into the underside of her market table—a warding mark, the analysts said, old-fashioned, protective, the kind of thing a supplier might teach a customer. Which meant someone had been supplying Merrin Dole with charm-work marked with the Grey Boar crest, and there was exactly one person alive who still used that crest, and Sera had spent the last three days pretending not to have done that arithmetic either.

She sat back down. She opened the ledger to the roster page one more time.

Here was the thing about the Grey Boars that no Warden archive recorded: they had not been heroes. Sera bristled even thinking the word. They'd been mercenaries—hungry, underpaid, occasionally drunk, frequently lost. They had marched for whoever paid and grumbled about it the entire way. They had cheated at cards and stolen each other's boots as a running joke and once, memorably, gotten lost for four days in fog and eaten a sergeant's prize ham rather than admit they were lost. Bram had charged them all a fine for it. In writing. With interest.

But they had shared water in the Redford summer when water meant fever. They had carried their wounded thirty miles rather than leave them. And when Vaskar's silence came—when every god went quiet and the world decided, in its tidy new way, that the gods had never existed and anyone who said otherwise would burn—forty-one people had looked at each other across a cold campfire and silently agreed to forget each other, for safety, forever.

That had been the oath beneath the oath. *You don't know me. I was never yours. Live.*

Fifteen years, and Sera had kept it. She'd risen in the Wardens by being exactly what they wanted: thorough, incorruptible, immune to sentiment. Her superiors praised her for having no past. She let them believe it. It was easier than explaining that she had a past, and it had a name, and she'd reported to it every morning for six years in the mud at Redford while the girl complained about the food and mended everyone's cloaks and prayed to a god who never answered, and Sera had listened to those prayers and said nothing, because saying something would have been kindness, and kindness between soldiers was a debt you couldn't repay.

She picked up the tongs and turned a log. Sparks climbed. Somewhere below, in the cells, Merrin Dole waited for the Falls, and Sera had signed the transfer order with a steady hand because that was the job, and the law was the law, and if the law was wrong then—

Then what? Then what, Captain? Finish the sentence.

She couldn't. She'd been failing to finish it for three days.

So instead she did the smaller thing, the coward's compromise, the first dishonest act of her otherwise spotless career: she fed the ledger to the fire.

Page by page. The roster first—forty-one names curling at the edges, the boar's-head stamp going brown then black then nothing. Then the campaign notes, the pay records, the joke inventory Bram had kept of stolen boots (eleven pairs, all accounted for except Sergeant Hale's, which Oskar had worn until it fell apart and then wept about). The fire took it all greedily, hungrily, the way the Falls' great brazier took everything, and Sera watched fifteen years of brotherhood go up the chimney of Corvale Keep and felt—nothing noble. No relief. Just a small sick sense that she'd done the right thing in the worst possible way, at the last possible moment, for reasons she refused to examine while anyone might see her face.

Except.

One name remained. She had torn the roster's final leaf free before it burned, and it lay on her knee now, and on it, in her own younger handwriting, in ink gone the color of weak tea:

*Hartwell, F. — quartermaster's assistant, medic. Sworn at Redford. Owes the company: nothing. The company owes her: everything, and has paid none of it.*

She'd written that last line herself, years ago, in a fit of honesty during a fever. She'd never crossed it out.

The tongs were in her hand. All she had to do was drop the leaf in the fire and the arithmetic would be complete. Forty-one names to ash, one signature on a transfer order, one clean conscience built on top of a dirty foundation, same as everyone else's. The Hartwell woman would be found eventually by someone else—some junior officer with sharp eyes and no history—and questioned, and held, and perhaps, in some future season when the Magistrate's patience wore thin, delivered gently to the Falls after all. Alive and intact right up until she wasn't.

And Sera would be able to say, honestly, that she had burned everything. That she'd protected whom she could. That her hands were clean.

She looked at the fire for a long time. The fire looked back, doing what fire does, which is waiting.

"You're stalling," she said aloud to the empty room. "You've been stalling for three days and you're calling it diligence."

The empty room declined to argue.

Because here was the other thing, the thing under the thing: the shrines were waking up. Sera had seen the reports herself, filed by embarrassed officers who clearly hoped their superiors would laugh them out of the folder—a beggar child's prayer answered in daylight outside the Tanner's Row shrine, a stone warm to the touch, a sound in it like drums. Like battle-drums. Sera had read that word—*drums*—and felt her spine go cold in a way nine years of Warden discipline couldn't fix, because she knew those drums. Every Grey Boar knew those drums. You heard them in your chest before a charge, in the space where fear should have been, and you went forward anyway, singing.

Vaskar's cadence. Fifteen years silent, humming in a shrine-stone like a man clearing his throat after a long illness.

If the old god was waking—if the founding covenant, whatever it truly was, was straining—then the Magistrate's answer was fire, because fire was always the answer, and Sera had signed her name under that answer so many times the ink had worn a groove in her conscience. And Freya—stubborn, careful, invisible Freya—was going to walk straight into the middle of it, because Freya had never once in her life walked around the middle of anything that hurt.

Sera folded the leaf along its old creases and put it inside her breast pocket, against her heart, where it sat like a coal that wouldn't cool.

"There," she told the fire. "Happy? I've kept exactly one crime. It's a start."

The fire crackled, unimpressed.

She was still sitting there, feeling foolish and awake in a way she hadn't felt in years, when the knock came again—sharper this time, wrong for the hour. Pellam again, but Pellam wouldn't knock like that. Sera stood, buttoned her collar, became Captain Voss in the space of a breath, and opened the door on Lieutenant Harrow, night-shift, rain on his shoulders and something carefully blank in his face. Carefully blank faces were how bad news introduced itself in the Keep.

"Captain. Sorry for the hour." He glanced past her at the grate, at the drifts of ash, and she saw him note them and decide, professionally, not to have seen them. "Two things. First: the analysts finished with the witch's effects. There's a delivery record. Charms supplied monthly, eighteen months, cash on collection, no name—but the hand that wrapped them ties a slipknot off the left thumb. Odd habit. We thought you'd want to know."

Sera's mouth went dry. She knew that knot. She'd taught it to Freya herself, eleven years old in company years, laughing, *no, from the left, you absolute disaster, how have you survived this long.*

"And second?" she said, when she trusted her voice.

Harrow hesitated. Rain ticked against the corridor window. "Second—the night market watch reports someone asking questions at the Tanner's Row shrine tonight. A woman. Asking about the hum in the stone." He met her eyes. "Description matches the Hartwell woman, Captain. Watch says she headed toward the river quarter. Toward the Falls."

Toward the Falls. Alone, at midnight, toward the place where the city's secrets drowned.

Sera reached for her cloak. Later—later she would sit with the crack in her loyalty and study it like a fault line, wondering when it had appeared and whether it had been there all along, waiting for weight. For now there was only motion, only the old drill rising up through the new uniform: *move fast, move quiet, get there first.*

"Ready my horse," she said. "And Lieutenant—no one follows us. That's an order."

Harrow blinked. "Us, Captain?"

Sera paused in the doorway, one hand flat over her breast pocket, over the folded leaf, over the name she could not burn.

"Mine," she said. "I'm going alone."

* * *

The rain had thinned to a drifting mist by the time she reached the river quarter, and the Hollow Falls announced themselves the way they always did—not by sight but by sound, a low ceaseless swallowing beneath the streets, louder here, much louder than it had any business being. Louder than she'd ever heard it. And threaded through the water-noise, faint as a pulse under skin, came something else.

Drums.

Sera reined in at the head of the stairs that wound down to the Falls' viewing gallery, and pressed her hand to her breast pocket, and told herself she was riding ahead of her own officers purely to control the arrest.

Below, in the mist, a figure stood at the rail with her hood back and her dark hair plastered to her face, leaning toward the water as if listening to a voice.

Sera's heart did something complicated and old.

"Freya Hartwell," she said, and drew her sword—not to threaten, she told herself, just because procedure demanded it, just because her hand needed something familiar to hold. "Step away from the rail."

The figure turned. Even across the wet dark, even after fifteen years, Freya's smile was exactly the same: tired, wry, and utterly unafraid of her.

"Hullo, Sera," she said. "You're late. It's been talking about you."
