## 8. The Charm-Seller's War

The first charm Freya sold after the shrine-stone warmed in her hand was, by every measure she could think of, a fraud.

She hadn't meant it that way. She had meant it very much the opposite, which was the problem. For fifteen years her charms had been honest in the only way that mattered: they were wood and thread and salt and a great deal of confident patter, and they worked exactly as well as believing they would work. A mother with a feverish child felt better holding something warm in her fist. A fisherman with a knotted wind-charm walked to the docks with his shoulders straighter. That was not nothing. Freya had built a quiet philosophy on the back of it — *hope, bottled, at three coppers* — and she had never once felt like a liar, because the hope was real even when the magic wasn't.

But now the magic *was*, and she had sold a fraud anyway, because the magic was also terrifying, and terror made her hands stupid.

The buyer was a lamplighter with a bad knee and a worse landlord, and he'd asked for something to keep the Warden patrols off his street. Old Freya — the Freya of six days ago — would have sold him a knotted cord with a river-stone in it and told him to hang it over his door, and he'd have felt watched-over, and that would have been the end of it. New Freya, the Freya with a god's battle-cadence still humming in her palm like a wasp in a jar, had sat up half the night with that same cord and that same stone and *poured* — because that was the only word for it, pouring, like ladling soup — a measure of something into it that she did not understand and could not name. And then, in the morning, with the thing warm and faintly singing in her pocket, she had looked at the lamplighter's hopeful face and lost her nerve entirely.

"Keep it dry," she'd said. "It's — mostly decorative."

He'd paid his three coppers and gone off whistling.

She stood in the doorway of her stall watching him go and felt like the world's greatest coward. Somewhere under the city, a god she had mourned for fifteen years was faintly, painfully alive. Somewhere in the undercity, people were being dragged to the Hollow Falls for the crime of praying to gods exactly like him. And she had just told a man his charm was *mostly decorative*.

"Well," she said to her unsold stock of knotted cords, "we're all very proud of ourselves."

* * *

The trouble — one of the many troubles, Freya was learning, that came with answered prayers — was that the faithful of Corvale's undercity had spent fifteen years getting very good at finding each other.

She noticed it the way you notice weather. A woman buying a luck-charm lingered a moment too long, and her thumb brushed the cord in a particular way — twice, then once — and Freya realized with a cold slide in her stomach that she was being asked a question in a language she'd only just learned existed. The old sign, Bram had told her once, over cheap wine and cheaper memories: *twice for the question, once for the trust.* The faithful had kept it alive through the burning years, a little code passed hand to hand, and now that the gods were waking it was everywhere, like frost after a warm spell. Twice for the question. Once for the trust.

By the fourth day, Freya had counted eleven of them. By the seventh, they had found her stall.

They came quietly, one at a time, in the guise of customers. A cooper with ink-stained fingers who asked, in a bored voice, whether she had anything "for the cough." A girl of maybe fifteen who stood in front of Freya's table for a long moment and then said, barely above a whisper, "They say there was a shrine on Tanner's Row that got warm. They say a charm-seller's hand was on it."

"People say a lot of things," Freya said.

The girl looked at her. She had the flat, patient stare of someone who had grown up learning which questions got people burned, and she was not going to ask any of them aloud. She simply set two coppers on the table, took the first charm her fingers found, and left.

It was a charm to find lost keys. It had never worked in its life.

Freya stared after her for a long moment, then at the two coppers, and felt the shape of something settling onto her shoulders that she recognized, because she'd worn it before. It was the old company weight. The one that said: *you know where the line is, and you know you've already crossed it, and the only question left is how fast you run.*

* * *

She went to see Maren that evening, which was either wisdom or suicide, and she genuinely could not have told you which.

Maren kept a wash-house two levels under the street, where the steam never quite cleared and the walls wept mineral stains in patterns that Freya privately thought looked like old maps. Maren herself was sixty, built like a rain barrel, and had been quietly keeping the undercity's faithful alive for longer than the Wardens had been burning anyone. She was also, Freya was fairly sure, the reason the hedge-witch's stall had been the one torched and not Freya's — Maren had a talent for moving trouble along, like a woman shooing cats off a table.

"You're the charm-seller," Maren said, by way of hello. She didn't look up from her mending.

"I'm a charm-seller. There are four of us on Gull Street alone."

"Mm. The other three sell knots and lies." Maren bit her thread and finally looked up, and her eyes were the pale, unhurried gray of a woman who had outlived three Magistrates and intended to outlive a fourth. "Yours got warm."

"That's not —"

"Don't." Maren set down the mending. "I've been waiting thirty years for one of the old blood to walk into my wash-house with a warm charm in her pocket and a lie on her lips, and I'll tell you now, I don't have the patience left to be polite about it. Sit down, girl. You look like a woman who hasn't eaten."

It was, annoyingly, true. Freya sat. Maren put a bowl of something in front of her that was mostly barley and entirely welcome, and then sat across from her with her arms folded, and waited, the way stone waits.

"I don't know what I am," Freya said at last, which was more honesty than she'd planned on. "I sold charms. Knots and lies, like you said. Good ones. The hope was real even if the —" She stopped. Started again. "Fifteen years ago I served a god. Vaskar. The war-god. I was sworn, properly sworn, the old way. And then he went silent, and the company broke, and I came here and learned to be small." She turned the spoon over in the barley. "Six days ago a shrine-stone hummed in my hand. His battle-cadence. I'd know it anywhere. It's the first thing I've heard from him in fifteen years, and it came through a beggar child's prayer at a wayside shrine, and I have been lying awake every night since trying to decide whether it's a miracle or a trap."

"And what did you decide?"

"That it's both, probably."

Maren snorted. It was, Freya suspected, the closest thing to laughter the wash-house permitted. "You'll do," she said. "The others — the ones who've come asking — they don't need a miracle, charm-seller. They need charms that work. Fever-charms. Warding-charms. Something for the cough that isn't boiled willow bark and a prayer the Wardens can hear you making." She leaned forward. "You've been pouring into your knots. I can smell it on you from here, like a hearth that's been banked too long. You're afraid to give it away."

"Of course I'm afraid. Every person I hand a working charm to is a person the Wardens can hang me with."

"Every person you *don't* hand one to is a person the Wardens can burn anyway." Maren's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about her; nothing about her ever rose, she simply became more there. "I've buried forty-one of mine in fifteen years, girl. I stopped counting the ones I couldn't bury. You've been selling hope at three coppers and telling yourself that was enough. It was enough, when it was all there was. It isn't all there is anymore."

The wash-house hissed and dripped. Somewhere above, muffled by stone, a Warden drum beat the hour, and Freya felt the sound go through her like a hook.

"I don't know what I'm doing," she said. "The old rites, the pouring — I did it once, half by instinct, and I've been afraid to do it again because I don't know where it *comes from*. Whether it's me or him or something leaking up from under the Falls, and if it's the last one —"

"Then you'd best find out," Maren said, "before you sell it to somebody."

* * *

She practiced on herself, because that seemed the honorable way to be an idiot.

The wash-house had a back room with a drain in the floor and a door that locked, and Maren stood outside it for two hours on the stated grounds that "if you blow yourself up I'll want to be able to say I heard it coming." Freya sat on an upturned tub with a fever-charm in her lap — a knotted cord, a river-stone, a thread of red wool, the same as she'd sold a hundred times — and tried to remember what she had done with the lamplighter's charm, that first night, before the fear closed her hands.

She had prayed. That was it. That was the whole of it. She had sat at her table with the cord in her palm and done, without thinking, the thing she had done every night for fifteen years to a god who never answered: she had spoken to Vaskar. *Keep this one warm. Keep this one found. You know how it is, being small and hunted — you of all gods know.* And the cord had filled, the way a cup fills, and she had felt it go warm and had told herself it was the candle.

So she did it again. Deliberately, this time. Eyes open.

"Vaskar," she said, to the drain and the damp and the dark. "I don't know if you can hear me, or where you are, or why it took you fifteen years and a beggar child to find your voice. I have a lot of questions and a list of grievances as long as my arm, and we are going to have words, you and I, the moment either of us has the time. But right now there's a cooper on Tanner's Row with a cough that's going to kill him before the Wardens get the chance, and a girl who bought a key-finding charm because she didn't dare ask for what she needed. So." She closed her hand over the cord. "Lend me your shoulder. Just the shoulder. I'll do the lifting."

Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered.

But the cord warmed. Slowly, the way a stone warms in a hand that's held it a long while — and then not slowly at all, a sudden bloom of heat and a hum she felt in her teeth, that old cadence, *dum-da-dum, dum-da-dum*, the marching rhythm of an army that never lost, and Freya laughed, actually laughed, a startled bark of a sound that echoed off the wet stone, because it was *him*, it was unmistakably him, faint as a voice through three walls but there, *there*, and he had heard her, or something of him had, and fifteen years of silence cracked down the middle like river ice in spring.

"Right," she said, wiping her face with her sleeve, because apparently she was crying, which was undignified and could be dealt with later. "Right. The cooper first."

* * *

Word travels in the undercity the way water travels through chalk: invisibly, patiently, and arriving everywhere eventually.

By the end of the second week, they came to her at all hours. Not to the stall — Maren had put a stop to that within a day ("You warm-handed fool, do you want a pyre?") — but to the wash-house, by the back stairs, two at a time, hooded and quiet. A grandmother with a grandson who saw things in the dark that weren't there. A dock-hand whose hands shook so badly he couldn't hold a rope, and who wept with relief when a charm steadied them. A boy of twelve who had been praying to the river-god under his breath for a year and whose mother had found out and needed somewhere to run.

Freya worked. That was the strange, simple mercy of it. She sat at Maren's mending table with her sleeves rolled up and her hands full of cord and stone and salt, and she prayed the way she used to pray on the eve of battles — plainly, without ceremony, a soldier talking to her god — and the charms filled, one after another, each with that faint warm hum, that distant drumbeat. She got better at it. She learned that the pouring came easier when she meant it, and harder when she was afraid, which was its own kind of lesson. She learned that some charms took more than others, and that afterward she was hungry — ravenously, specifically hungry, the way she'd been after long marches — and Maren started leaving bread on the table without being asked, and never once mentioned it.

"You're becoming the answer to prayers," Maren observed one night, watching her knot the hundredth cord.

"I'm becoming a *middleman*," Freya said. "The god answers. I just carry the parcel."

"Mm. And when the Wardens come asking who's been carrying parcels?"

"Then I'll be a very surprised charm-seller who has no idea what you're all on about."

Maren looked at her over the steam. "Girl," she said, "you've started standing differently."

Freya looked down at herself, at the table, at the neat rows of working charms, and realized it was true. Somewhere in the last two weeks her shoulders had come back down from around her ears. She'd stopped flinching at the Warden drums. She'd caught herself, that very morning, humming the old company marching song while she worked, and had only just stopped herself before the whole wash-house learned the words.

Fifteen years of being small, and it had taken two weeks of being useful to forget how.

That was the danger. She knew it the way she knew weather coming. The hedge-witch's stall was still a black scar on Gull Street. The Wardens had not found the hedge-witch's supplier yet, but they were looking — the patrols had changed their routes, started lingering near the market, and twice now a Warden had paused at her stall and turned her charms over with a gloved finger, unhurried, reading them the way a man reads a suspect's face. Her charms were honest knots and lies. They'd find nothing. But the *warm* ones, the ones she'd started passing through Maren's hands — if a Warden ever got one of those in a gloved fist, if one of them ever felt that hum —

She was becoming visible. Purpose had done what fear never could: it had made her worth finding.

* * *

It was the girl with the key-finding charm who brought the warning, eleven days in.

She arrived at the wash-house after midnight, out of breath, hood soaked with river fog, and she didn't do the hand-sign. She just grabbed Freya's wrist and said, fast and low: "There's a Warden captain asking after you. By name."

The wash-house went very quiet. Even the steam seemed to hold itself still.

"By name," Freya repeated. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because the rest of her had gone cold and ringing, like a struck bell.

"Sera Voss. She had the roster of your old company. She showed it at the toll-house on the third level — showed it to the toll-man, like she had a right to. And she had a drawing." The girl's fingers tightened on Freya's wrist. "A drawing of the company mark. The one from before the burning years. She's asking who in the undercity carries it."

Freya sat down slowly on the upturned tub, because her knees had opinions of their own.

*Voss.* Sera Voss. The name arrived in her chest like a knife left out in the snow — cold, and familiar, and hers once. Her sworn sister. The girl she'd taught to hold a shield wall, the girl who'd held *her* up when Vaskar went silent and the company broke and Freya had cried into her shoulder like the world was ending. Fifteen years, and the last she'd heard, Voss had taken the Warden gray and climbed, and climbed, and climbed.

She had known, in some cellar of herself, that it would come to this. Gods waking, seals straining, a charm-seller pouring miracles into knotted cord in a city that burned the faithful — of course the law would come looking. But she had imagined the law as a thing, a gray wall, a drumbeat. She had not let herself imagine a face.

"She'll come here," Maren said. Not a question.

"She'll come everywhere," Freya said. "That's what she's *for*." She looked at her hands, at the faint warmth still lingering in her palms from the night's work, a hundred small prayers carried and delivered. Somewhere out in the fog, a woman was sleeping fever-free because of her. Somewhere a boy was holding a rope. And somewhere in the gray levels above, her sworn sister was walking the streets with a drawing of a dead company's mark and a list of names, and one of those names was hers.

The girl was still watching her. "What do we do?"

Freya took a breath. And found, to her considerable surprise, that the fear had settled into something else — something older and steadier, something with a drumbeat under it.

"We keep working," she said. "And somebody fetch me a map of the third level. If Sera Voss is hunting me—" she reached for the bread Maren had left, because there was a war on, and wars were won by the side that remembered to eat, "—then it's long past time I stopped being the one who's found."
