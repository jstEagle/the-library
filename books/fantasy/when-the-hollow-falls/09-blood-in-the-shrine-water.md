## 9. Blood in the Shrine-Water

The chapel smelled of tallow and wet stone, which was how Sera Voss knew it would be trouble before they ever went down the stairs.

Real chapels — the sanctioned kind, the kind that paid their tithe to the Magistrate's office and hung the twin-hammer seal of Warden law above their altars — smelled of incense and beeswax and the particular institutional loneliness of a building that had been cleaned by someone who hated cleaning. This place under Tanner's Row smelled like people. Like soup that had been cooked in the same pot as a prayer candle for twenty years. Like a hundred bodies sleeping close together against the cold because the undercity didn't heat itself.

"Captain." Corporal Denny Ashford crouched at the top of the stairs, lantern held low. He was nineteen, freckled, and had the unshakable conviction of the young that everything could be handled by procedure if you followed it hard enough. "Warrant says unauthorized assembly and unsanctioned devotion. Twelve souls, maybe more."

"Twelve souls," Sera repeated quietly. "You say that like it's a number of rats."

Denny blinked at her. "It's — it's what the warrant says, Captain."

"Yes." She settled her sword belt and did not sigh, because captains didn't sigh, they merely breathed with weight. "Stay behind me. Nobody draws unless I draw first. And if any of them run toward the water side, let them. We're not fishing children out of the cistern tonight."

"You think they'll run?"

"I think," Sera said, "that I was twelve once, hiding from the Wardens, and I ran everywhere." She left him with his mouth open — Denny was constitutionally incapable of imagining her young — and started down the stairs.

She'd been a Warden for nine years now. Long enough that she'd stopped counting the raids, or rather long enough that she'd learned to count them differently: not by arrests made but by how few things broke. A good raid, in her private ledger, was one where everyone walked home, including the guilty. The Magistrate's clerks would have found this accounting sentimental. Sera kept it anyway, in the same part of her mind where she kept Freya Hartwell's face, which was to say somewhere she never looked at directly but which colored everything.

The stairs turned twice. At the bottom, lamplight leaked gold under a door of mismatched planks, and through it came singing.

That was the thing she hadn't told Denny. That was the thing she couldn't put in a report without ending up in front of the Magistrate's own tribunal explaining herself. The singing had come back. Fifteen years of silence — fifteen years during which every god in Corvale had been as responsive as a brick, and the Wardens' whole grim economy of burning had been built on the premise that magic was a crime and gods were dead and anyone who claimed otherwise was either mad or lying — and now, in the last month, people were singing again. Not the sanctioned hymns. Old ones. Battle-cadences. Harvest rounds. A lullaby Sera half-remembered from a mercenary camp when she was small enough to sleep under a wagon.

*Vaskar's cadence,* said the treacherous little voice at the back of her head. *You know it. You marched to it.*

She put her hand on the door instead of answering herself.

* * *

The chapel was a cellar that had been given ambitions. Rough benches, an altar made of a door laid across two barrels, and above it — this was the detail Sera would carry longest — a shrine-stone no bigger than a loaf of bread, worn smooth by hands. Not stolen, not smuggled. Just a stone, the kind that sat at crossroads all over the old country, the kind the Wardens had spent fifteen years telling everyone was nothing.

There were thirty-one people. Denny's count had been off by nineteen, which was what happened when your informant was a frightened child who counted heads through a knothole.

They didn't run. That was the worst of it. They stood up from their benches with the terrible dignity of people who had already decided what this night was going to cost, and a woman at the front — gray-haired, flour on her sleeves, a baker by the look of her — stepped between the Wardens and the altar and said, in a voice like she was announcing bread out of the oven:

"You're late. We've already prayed."

Sera had a speech prepared. She always had a speech prepared; it was part of the armor. *By order of the Magistrate, this assembly is dissolved. You will be processed fairly. No one need be hurt.* It was even true, mostly, in the way that a cage is fair because it doesn't rattle.

She got four words into it.

Then the shrine-stone woke up.

Later, the survivors would describe it a dozen different ways — a bell, a drum, a shout, a heartbeat the size of the room — and all of them would be wrong and all of them would be right. What Sera felt was warmth, sudden and enormous, rolling off that little loaf-sized stone like heat off a forge door thrown open, and underneath the heat, unmistakable, the cadence. The marching rhythm. *Left, left, left-right-left,* the drumbeat her company had moved to across three borders and too many battlefields, the rhythm Vaskar's faithful had timed their hearts to since before Corvale had walls.

The lamps flared white. The water in the cistern at the cellar's edge rose without wind, standing up in ropes and ribbons, and the stone sang — there was no other word — and Sera felt it in her teeth, in the scar on her shoulder, in a place behind her ribs she'd thought sealed shut years ago.

And then it went wrong.

Not the power. The power, whatever it was, seemed almost gentle, in the way a flood tide is gentle to things that float. But Corporal Ashford — nineteen, procedural, terrified — saw the water move and the light flare and did exactly what a boy does when the world stops obeying rules.

He drew.

"No!" Sera's shout came a half-second too late, the way it always does.

What happened next took perhaps six seconds and would take Sera the rest of her life to stop re-living. One of the men on the near bench — a dockhand, big, gentle-faced — lunged not at Ashford but at the shrine-stone, as if to shield it with his body. Another Warden, Sergeant Ille by name, fourteen years of service, a woman who played cards with Sera every Restday and cheated badly and laughed about it — Ille saw the dockhand moving and read it as an attack and drove forward with her baton. The baker screamed. The dockhand turned, off-balance, and Ille's strike caught him wrong, and he staggered back against the altar, and the barrels shifted, and the stone slid —

— and the moment the stone tipped, the power *twisted.*

Sera had seen magic flare once before, years ago, at a burning: a hedge-witch whose protections snapped at the stake like a wire under load. It had been ugly. This was uglier, because this wasn't a defense breaking. This was something vast being jostled in its sleep, and the cellar filled with pressure and light and a sound that was not sound, a note below hearing that Sera felt crack along the mortar lines of the walls, and the cistern water leapt straight up and hung there, a chandelier of black glass —

Three Wardens died.

Not burned. Not cut. Nothing so clean. The pressure simply went through them, through Denny Ashford and his partner Quill and poor Ille, still mid-swing, still trying to do her duty, and it stopped their hearts the way a hammer stops a clock. They fell among the faithful like sacks of grain, and the light guttered, and the water fell back into its cistern with a crash, and the stone rolled off the altar onto the floorboards, warm as a fresh loaf, glowing faintly, dimming.

Silence. The specific silence of a room full of people checking whether they are still alive.

The baker was kneeling over the dockhand, who was breathing, who would live. The faithful stood frozen. And Sera Voss stood in the middle of her own raid with three of her people dead on the floor and the taste of a god's attention still humming in her bones, and she understood, with the cold clarity that comes after shock has done its work, that everything she had believed about the world had just been demonstrated false in front of thirty-one witnesses.

Because the stone had answered. Gods were not dead. Magic was not a lie. And the Wardens — the institution she had given nine years, her oath-sisterhood, her whole moral architecture — had been burning people for a crime that, as of tonight, demonstrably existed, committed by powers that, as of tonight, demonstrably did not care about warrants.

"Captain?" The baker's voice, careful. "Captain, we'll come quiet. Just — let us bury our dead proper, theirs and ours both. They're only boys, two of them. Ille deserved better than a ditch."

Sera looked down at Ille. There was a card in her dead hand, folded once, the ace of cups she'd been planning to spring on Sera at the next Restday game. She could see the pencil mark on the corner where Ille marked her cheats.

"Everyone sits down," Sera heard herself say. "Nobody moves. Nobody runs." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; inside, something was coming apart in large pieces. "We bury them together. All five — " she corrected herself, "all four. All the dead. Together. That's my order, and if the Magistrate doesn't like it, the Magistrate can send somebody who wasn't here."

* * *

They buried the dead at dawn, in the scrubby ground beyond the cistern outflow, Warden blue and laborer's rough cloth laid side by side, and if that image got back to the Magistrate's office, Sera decided she would defend it with the flat of her sword if necessary. The faithful dug. The surviving Wardens helped. Denny Ashford wept openly over his partner and was not mocked for it, and Sera stood apart because someone had to stand apart, and that was the job, and the job had just gotten much heavier.

It was while the graves were being filled that she found it.

A child — one of the faithful, maybe seven, with the enormous solemn eyes of undercity children — tugged her sleeve and pointed wordlessly at the chapel doorway. On the lintel, tucked into a gap in the planks where a nail had been, hung a charm.

Sera had seen ten thousand charms. The city sold them by the fistful: knotted string, stamped tin, dried flowers in wax, all of them frauds, all of them harmless, which was why selling them was tolerated and why the tolerance had always struck her as the state's small mercy. This was not that.

This was a disc of river-clay, fired hard, worked with a sigil in a spiral pattern that seemed to shift if you didn't look at it straight. It hummed. Faintly, warmly, in time with the cadence she'd felt in the cellar — the same rhythm, gentler, like a drum heard through a wall. And around the spiral's rim, pressed into the clay by a thumb, was a maker's mark.

She knew the mark. Of course she knew the mark. You didn't forget the brand on the company shield you'd sworn under at sixteen, the one they'd painted on wagons and stitched on collars and scratched into the doorframe of every safehouse from the coast to the mountains: a hart's antler crossed with a smith's hammer.

Hartwell. Freya Hartwell. *Sworn sister.* The woman who had taught her to throw a knife properly, who had carried her four miles through a fever swamp with jokes the entire way, who had vanished fifteen years ago into Corvale's crowds and left Sera holding an empty oath like a coat that no longer fit.

Freya was making charms. Real ones. Working ones. Charms tuned to Vaskar's cadence, distributed through the undercity, hanging over chapel doors — and three of Sera's Wardens were dead in the fresh-turned earth because the gods had woken and Freya Hartwell was feeding the fire.

The betrayal landed strangely, and she took a moment to be honest with herself about its shape, because honesty was the discipline that held her together. It wasn't that Freya had broken the law. Some traitorous cellar of Sera's heart had been expecting that, maybe even hoping for it, ever since the hedge-witch's interrogation had turned up that same antler-and-hammer sigil scratched into a spellbook. It was that Freya hadn't *told her.* Fifteen years of silence, and then the world cracked open like an egg, and Freya Hartwell was out here answering prayers and lighting shrines and leaving her mark like a signature on a declaration of war — and Sera had learned about it from a corpse and a piece of pottery.

*You could have come to me,* she thought, and then, immediately, furiously: *and what would you have said, Sera? Come desert with me? Come burn with me? You'd have had to arrest her on the spot, and you know it, and she knows it, that's why she didn't —*

Except. Except.

She turned the charm over. On the back, small, deliberate, pressed deep enough that it had to have been meant for whoever finally found it: a second mark, beside the first. Not a maker's mark. A message, in the old field-code every member of their company had learned before they were trusted past the picket line. Three strokes and a dot.

*Sera. Come alone. Before it's too late.*

Sera stood in the dawn light with the graves at her back and the charm in her hand and her heart doing something complicated and painful that she refused to dignify by naming.

"Captain?" Denny Ashford, red-eyed, saluting. "Orders? The report on the deaths — the Magistrate's office will want cause of death, and I don't rightly know how to write down what killed them."

"Write *structural collapse of premises,*" Sera said. "The cellar floor gave way. Tragic accident. I'll sign it."

"But it didn't —"

"Denny." She looked at him, and he shut his mouth, because he was young but not stupid. "The truth gets those thirty-one people burned. All of them. Every soul who dug those graves this morning. Is that justice?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. She watched the question go into him and start doing its slow corrosive work, the same work it was doing in her, and she felt a flicker of something awful and necessary: pity for the boy, and gratitude that she wouldn't be the one to finish teaching him.

She pocketed the charm. Then, because she was still a captain and the morning required it, she gave the orders: escort the faithful back to their doors, file the falsified report, deliver Ille's effects to her sister with the card included, and tell absolutely no one about the stone.

And then, alone, in the grey hour before the city fully woke, she took out the charm again and read the field-code a second time, as if it might have changed.

*Come alone. Before it's too late.*

"It's already too late, Freya," she said aloud, to nobody, to the river, to a woman fifteen years gone who had apparently been closer than a ghost the whole time. "It's been too late since the night you walked out."

But her boots, traitorous things that had carried her away from every fight she'd ever regretted, were already carrying her toward Tanner's Row — and she noticed, distantly, that they were walking softly, the way you walk when you don't want your own side to hear you coming.

Behind her, in the chapel cellar, in the dark beneath the new-laid boards, the shrine-stone lay cooling on the floor. And far below the city, beneath the white noise of the Hollow Falls, something that had been listening through it began — patiently, hungrily — to speak.
