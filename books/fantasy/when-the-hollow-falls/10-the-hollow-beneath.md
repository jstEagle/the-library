## 10. The Hollow Beneath

The heartstone had not left her skin in three days.

It hung against her sternum on a leather cord Bram had cut from his own pack-strap, and it was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm — faintly, stubbornly, as if it resented being carried and intended to make that known. Freya had tried sleeping with it under her pillow the first night. It had hummed until two bells past midnight, at a pitch that lived somewhere between a war-drum and a wasp in a jar, and she had finally hung it on the bedpost, where it sulked quietly until dawn.

"You could just tell me what you want," she'd said to it.

It had hummed.

Gods, she thought, were the same dead or alive. They never answered a straight question.

Now she stood at the top of the Warden's Stair, the long switchback cut into the cliff above the Hollow Falls, and told herself for the fourth time that this was a bad idea. The fourth time, because the first three hadn't taken.

The Falls were beautiful in the way of most things in Corvale that got people killed. The river came down off the moors in a single broad sheet and dropped through a gap in the rock into a chasm so deep the bottom was a rumor. Mist climbed out of it all day and most of the night, and where the mist touched stone it grew a soft grey moss that the faithful called god's-beard and the Wardens called a fire hazard. The sound was enormous. It wasn't loud, exactly — it was *enormous*, a sound you felt in your back teeth, in the soles of your boots, in the old scar tissue along your ribs where a Corvish spearman had nearly ended your career before it started.

Fifteen years ago, soldiers of the new Magistrate's court had thrown the old shrine-stones from the temple district into that chasm. Freya knew because she'd been there. Not fighting — the company had already disbanded by then, scattered like spilled coins — but standing in the crowd, watching, doing nothing, which was becoming an unfortunate theme of her life.

She touched the heartstone through her shirt. It pulsed once, slow, like something turning over in its sleep.

"All right," she muttered. "But if I die, I'm haunting you specifically."

She started down the stair.

* * *

The Warden's Stair had ninety-one steps. She counted them, because counting kept the fear in a box with holes poked in it, and because Bram had told her to count them, and Bram was the sort of man whose advice you took even when it sounded mad, because his advice had kept forty mercenaries alive through nine years of other people's stupid wars.

"Past the eightieth step," he'd said, one eye glittering in the lamplight of his cluttered storeroom, "you'll feel it start pulling. Don't fight it and don't rush it. It's not dragging you, girl. It's *inviting* you. There's a difference, and the difference matters."

"What's at the bottom?"

"Steps." He'd turned back to his ledgers. "And then whatever's been waiting fifteen years to be asked a question."

She passed the eightieth step and felt it: a gentle lean in the air, as though the whole chasm were a cupped hand and she a coin slipping toward its center. Her feet wanted to hurry. She made them walk. Somewhere below, the mist glowed — actually glowed, a pale gold light with no source she could find, rising and falling with the pulse of the heartstone against her chest.

At the ninetieth step the stair ended at a ledge, wet-slick with spray, and from the ledge more steps continued downward into the rock itself, cut into the living stone, older than anything the Magistrate had built. Older than the Wardens. Older, she suspected, than Corvale.

These steps were not on any map. These steps had never been built by human hands that anyone would admit to.

She went down anyway. Of course she did. Fifteen years of selling luck-charms to people who needed miracles, and when a miracle finally opened a door in front of her, she was going to walk through it or die of curiosity on the threshold.

The passage descended in a slow spiral. The glow strengthened. And the voices began.

* * *

She heard Vaskar first.

There was no mistaking him; she'd have known that voice in a crowd of ten thousand gods. It was the voice that had spoken the words of binding over her at nineteen, rough as a whetstone, warm as a forge: *Freya Hartwell, who does not flinch.* It came up through the stone now thin and frayed, a thread of itself, a man shouting through a wall from a very great distance — and yet it was *him*, unmistakably, and every hair on her arms stood up and every year of silence crashed down on her at once.

*—hear me—* the voice said, or seemed to say, worn almost to nothing by the water's roar. *—little one—do not listen—*

"Do not listen to what?" she asked aloud, and her voice sounded absurdly small in the dark. "You're going to have to be more specific. There's rather a lot going on down here."

*—below me—*

And then the other voice rose.

She had no word for it at first. Later, walking home, she would try *beautiful* and reject it, try *terrible* and reject that too, and settle finally on *hungry*, because hungry was the only honest word. It did not shout. It did not need to. It simply arrived inside her skull the way cold arrives, everywhere at once, patient, and it said:

**Freya Hartwell.**

Her own name, in a voice like deep water over deep stone. Her knees went loose. She put a hand on the wall and the wall was warm.

"That's me," she managed. "You have me at a disadvantage."

**I have watched you for fifteen years,** the voice said, and there was something in it that was almost tender, which was infinitely worse than menace. **Through his eyes. Through the seal he is. You prayed every night for six years after the silence began. Did you know that? Six years, faithfully, before you let yourself stop.**

"I don't — how could you possibly know that?"

**I was there. I am here. I am beneath everything, little soldier. Every prayer said in this city passes down through the rock on its way to heaven, and heaven has been sealed shut, and so the prayers pool.** A pause, and then, with something horribly like a smile in it: **I drank them. Yours especially. You have such an angry way of praying. I always looked forward to yours.**

Freya discovered she had walked further down without deciding to. The passage opened now into a hollow in the rock, a natural chamber the size of a chapel, and across its far side — behind nothing, behind no door, behind only air that shimmered like heat off a summer road — she saw it.

A shape. Vast beyond sense, folded impossibly small, the way a storm is folded into a black cloud. It had shoulders. That was the detail her mind seized on and refused to release: it had *shoulders*, broad as bridge-piers, and they were shaped like Vaskar's shoulders, like the shoulders carved on the old shrine-stones, except wrong, except stretched, except *starved*. Chains of light crossed it — no, not chains. Oaths. She could see them now, woven bands of golden script circling the shape again and again, hundreds of them, thousands, each one an oath sworn by some mortal mouth in some vanished year, and the script was cracked. Frayed. Here and there whole letters had gone dark.

And beneath the shape, beneath the chamber floor, beneath everything, the Falls thundered down into the true dark, and she understood suddenly that the waterfall was not a waterfall. It was a *lid*. The city had built itself on top of a prison and called the view scenic.

"You're him," she said. "The twin. Hollow-Vaskar. The thing the founders bound."

**I am the part of the god they didn't want,** the voice agreed, unbothered. **He is the mercy and I am the meaning. He is the shield-arm and I am the sword. Your founders — your pious, frightened founders — they caught us both, fifteen years ago, and they could not kill a god, so they did the next best thing. They starved half of one and muzzled the rest.**

"They wounded him," Freya whispered. The heartstone burned against her chest. "They made his silence."

**They made his silence,** the voice said, **and called it peace, and called it law, and burned everyone who remembered otherwise. Yes.**

Something hot and furious rose in her throat, fifteen years of it, every night she'd knelt on a cold floor and gotten nothing, every morning she'd told herself *he has his reasons, he is a war-god, wars end, gods go quiet between them* — all of it curdling at once into rage, and the rage felt so good, so clean, that she distrusted it immediately.

Because the voice had *given* it to her. She could feel that now, feel the anger arriving in her chest like warmth from a fire she hadn't lit.

"No," she said aloud. "No. Stop that. Whatever you're doing — the anger, the — stop it."

**I am doing nothing.** Gently. So gently. **That fury is yours. I have merely kept it safe for you, all these years, since you had nowhere else to put it. Would you like the rest?**

The shimmering air rippled, and images moved in it like fish under ice.

She saw the founding of the Wardens — saw the men and women in grey robes, saw them take a captured god's blood and write laws with it, saw the first seal-oath sworn by a founder named Aldous Crane, whose face she recognized from the statue in Warden Square, the noble profile, the kindly stone eyes. She saw what the statue did not show: Crane weeping as he swore, Crane knowing exactly what he was doing, Crane going home to a warm house while a god bled beneath the Falls.

She saw the hedge-witch from the night market — Marta, her name was Marta, Freya had supplied her comfrey tinctures for three years — dragged to the lip of the chasm, and she saw, with a lurch, that the Falls had *drunk* her. That the executions were not executions. They were feedings. Every witch burned, every prayer-crime punished, every life given to the water had gone down into the dark, and the dark had grown a little stronger on each one, and the seal had grown a little weaker, because the seal was made of oaths and oaths are made of faith and the Wardens had spent fifteen years murdering the faithful, draining the very substance that held their prison shut.

**They are starving their own lock,** the voice murmured, **and feeding the prisoner. Your Magistrate is a fool, but he is a useful one. In a year, perhaps two, I will need no help at all.**

"Then why talk to me?"

**Because I would rather not wait.** And the tenderness in the voice turned, for just a moment, into something vast and cold and ancient, and the chamber lights guttered, and Freya's breath frosted in the warm air. **And because you, Freya Hartwell, are the only person in this city who can open this door tonight.**

*Little one,* Vaskar's ruined voice pleaded from somewhere far above her, or far within her, *little one, do not—*

"Be quiet," she snapped at both of them, and to her astonishment, they both did.

* * *

She made herself sit down on the wet stone. It seemed important, suddenly, not to have this conversation standing, with her weight already leaning toward the shimmer. She sat cross-legged like a soldier at rest, hands open on her knees, the way she used to sit at watch-fires twenty years ago, and she looked at the folded storm behind the veil of oaths, and she said:

"Talk, then. What are you offering?"

**Everything he never gave you.** No hesitation. The answer had been waiting, polished, ready, the way a merchant keeps his best goods under the counter. **Answers. You have spent fifteen years not knowing why. I will tell you why, all of it, every hour of it, beginning to end. Power. You were a battle-charmer of the Ninth Company; you could once put courage into a hundred men with a word. That word is still in you. I can wake it. I can give you back everything the Wardens' world took, and more — I can make you the hand that breaks a god's chains, Freya Hartwell. Your name would be sung.**

"I don't want my name sung."

**No?** Amusement, real amusement, rolling through the stone. **Liar. You want it *known*. There is a difference, and you know it as well as I do. You do not need statues. You need someone — something — to look at the fifteen years you gave and say: *that was seen. That was worth something. You were faithful and you were forsaken and it was not your fault.***

The words went into her like a knife going into a joint, finding the exact place.

Because that was it. That was the whole wound, and he had found it in under a minute, the way water finds a crack. Fifteen years of kneeling to silence. Fifteen years of telling herself loyalty was its own reward while knowing, in the small hours, that loyalty unpaid curdles into something shameful — that the other merchants were right, that she was a woman talking to a wall, that she had given her best years to a god who could not be bothered to send so much as a dream.

And here, at last, was a voice saying: *I saw. I heard every prayer. I kept count.*

**He heard them too,** the voice added softly, cruelly gently. **Every one. He lay in the dark beside me and heard you pray and could not answer, and do you know what he chose to spend his strength on instead, little soldier? Holding the seal. Holding *me*. Fifteen years of your devotion, poured into a lock, so that the thing beneath your city stayed beneath it. That is what your faith bought. That is what it was *for*.**

"And if I break the seal," Freya said, and her voice came out level, which she was privately proud of, "you go free. Into Corvale. With fifteen years of drunk prayers and executed witches in your belly."

**I go free,** the voice agreed, pleasantly. **And I am owed, Freya Hartwell. I am owed so much. But you have my word — my oath, sworn on the oldest law there is — that you and yours walk untouched. You, the quartermaster, the faithful of the undercity. My protection, freely given, binding as any chain. Ask Bram what a god's oath is worth.**

"Bram says a god's oath is worth exactly the god."

**Your Bram is a cynic with one eye and excellent taste in brandy.** The laughter rolled again, and it was warm, it was genuinely warm, and that was the horror of it, that after fifteen years of silence the first voice to laugh with her was this one. **I am not asking you to trust me, mercenary. I am asking you to do arithmetic. The seal fails regardless — a year, two years, perhaps less, now that the prayers come *answered* and the faithful grow bold and the Magistrate grows frightened and feeds me faster. When it fails, I rise unthanked, uninvited, and unfriendly. Or you open it now, on terms, with your name written first in my mouth, and your people sheltered under your bargain. Those are the choices. There is no third. There is no rescue coming. There is no one else.**

*Freya.* Vaskar's voice, barely a breath now, worn down to thread. *Freya, little one. There is a third.*

"Hush," she said automatically, and then stopped, and frowned, and looked up at the ceiling of the chamber as if she could see through it. "Wait. If you're conscious — if you've been conscious all this time — then you can hear me. Right now. You're *here*."

Silence from above. Silence from below. Even the Falls seemed to hold their breath.

**Careful,** the twin said, and the warmth had gone out of the voice entirely, and what remained was the cold vast patience of the deep places. **Do not reach for him through me, little soldier. That door opens both ways, and I am the door.**

Freya Hartwell sat on the wet stone in the glowing dark, fifteen years of grief in one hand and fifteen years of rage in the other, and beneath her heart the stolen heartstone beat like a second pulse, and she understood — with the terrible clarity that comes only at the worst possible moment — that whatever she said next, she was about to swear to something.

She thought about Marta, dragged screaming toward the water. She thought about the child at the wayside shrine, and the stone warming under her palm. She thought about a wall she had talked to for fifteen years, and whether a wall that listened was better or worse than a wall that didn't.

Then she lifted her chin, looked the folded storm full in its unseen face, and said:

"Before I swear anything to anyone — you're going to answer one question first. Truthfully. On your oldest law."

**Ask.**

"When Vaskar's silence began—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through, "—the night it began. What did the founders *take* from him? Because I'm wearing it around my neck, and I think you know."

The dark beneath the Falls laughed — low, delighted, hungry — and the heartstone against her chest flared white-hot, and somewhere far above, a god she had loved for twenty years began, very faintly, to scream.

"Ah," said Freya, to no one, to herself, to the whole rotten city overhead. "So it's *that* kind of answer."
