## 11. Old Wounds, New Oaths

Bram Odo Hessel had survived three wars, one plague, two marriages, and the dissolution of the Grey Company by the simple expedient of always knowing which way the wind was blowing and pretending he hadn't. It was a good philosophy for a quartermaster. Quartermasters who developed opinions died young, usually with their opinions still in their mouths.

But when Freya Hartwell came hammering on his door at the grey hour before dawn — hammering like the city was burning, hammering like a woman who had stopped caring who heard her — Bram opened it before the second knock landed, because some opinions you couldn't pretend away.

"Let me guess," he said, standing aside. "The heartstone talked back."

Freya pushed past him into the shop. She looked like a woman who had spent the night arguing with a god and losing on points. Her cloak was wet with river-mist. The stone at her throat glowed through her shirt like a coal refusing to die.

"It screamed," she said.

"Ah." Bram closed the door, shot both bolts, and considered whether to light the lamp. He decided against it. Some conversations went better in the dark, where a man could lie about his face. "Then I suppose you'll be wanting the whole story."

"I want the truth."

"They're not always the same thing, girl."

"Bram."

He heard her take off her sword belt and set it down. That was Freya's version of weeping — disarming herself in a one-eyed old man's back room. Fifteen years since she'd stood in his doorway, and she still did everything exactly the same way, as if the war had never ended, only gone quiet enough to ignore.

"All right," he said. "Sit down. And pour yourself something from that bottle on the shelf. Not the small one. The small one's for customers I don't like."

* * *

Bram's shop sat wedged between a tannery and a shuttered chandler's in Tanner's Row, which meant it smelled of nothing worse than old leather and older dust. In its day it had been something else entirely: a muster-house, an armory, a place where forty-one sworn soldiers kept their gear and their grievances and, once a year, their anniversary feast, at which Quartermaster Hessel invariably drank too much and sang the ballad about the miller's wife and got pelted with bread rolls until he stopped.

Forty-one sworn soldiers. Now the room held two, and one of them was dead in every way that mattered except breathing.

"The Grey Company wasn't founded to fight wars," Bram said. He lowered himself onto the bench by the cold stove, joints announcing themselves one at a time. "Everyone thinks it was. Mercenaries, swords for hire, all that. But the founders — Aldric and Veyra and the others, the first seven — they came out of Corvale itself, out of the years after the Magistracy rose. They'd seen what the new law did to anyone who still lit a candle for the old gods. Burnings. Confiscations. Children taken from mothers who taught them a prayer."

Freya said nothing. She had the bottle but hadn't poured. Bram watched her hold it like it might go off.

"So the seven of them made a company," he went on. "And the company made a bargain. Not with the Magistrate — with Vaskar himself. God of oaths and iron, god of the kept word. You remember what they taught us, first day of training?"

"'An oath is a blade,'" Freya recited, automatic, and then her voice cracked on the second half. "'—and a blade must have a hand.'"

"And a hand must have a heart," Bram finished. "That was the whole of our religion, girl. No temples. No tithe. Just that. We were Vaskar's hands, and he was our edge, and for two hundred years the arrangement suited everyone."

"But the seal."

"The seal." Bram rubbed his eye — the one he had left; the other had gone to a Corvale spearman thirty years ago, along with most of his patience for cities. "You've been down to the Falls. You've heard what's under there."

"I've heard something hungry."

"Hungry is a kind word for it." He leaned forward. "Vaskar has a twin, Freya. Same forging, different temper. Where Vaskar binds, Hollow-Vaskar breaks. Where Vaskar keeps faith, the Hollow one eats it. He's not evil, if that helps — gods aren't evil any more than winter is evil. But winter doesn't care whether your crops survive, and neither does he. The old stories say the twins were forged together at the making of the world, oath and its breaking, promise and the hunger that wants every promise unmade. And for a long time they balanced each other. A vow means nothing unless something wants it broken. That's theology, that is. Or it was, before the Wardens got their hands on it."

Freya poured finally. Her hand was steady. Bram noted it and admired it and hated how much it cost her.

"Fifteen years ago," she said. "What happened fifteen years ago?"

"The Magistracy happened." Bram took the cup from her and drank first, an old quartermaster's privilege. "The founders of the Wardens — not the captains, mind, the *founders*, the ones whose names are carved on the Hall of Justice — were men who understood exactly one thing: fear. They knew that people obey laws better when the laws are the only thing left to believe in. So long as the old gods answered prayers, the Magistrate's writ would always be second-best. Second-best isn't good enough for men like that."

"So they killed him."

"They couldn't kill him. Nobody kills a god. What you can do—" Bram held up three fingers, counting, an old habit from inventory days, "—is wound him, bind him, and steal from him. The Wardens' founders found the place where Vaskar's twin was chained — the Falls, girl, the Falls were always a prison before they were anything else — and they discovered something the old company records hinted at and never said plain. The binding wasn't stone or chain. It was an *oath*. A living oath, sworn by mortal hearts, renewed generation to generation by whoever carried the company's mark."

Freya touched her collarbone, where the old scar sat beneath the cloth. Every member of the Grey Company bore it — a blade crossed with a river, cut into the skin at swearing-in, healed with charm-work so it never faded. Bram's own had gone pale and soft as wax these last fifteen years, like a brand on cooling iron.

"They used the company," Freya said slowly. "They used our oath."

"They *wore* it. Aldric's line had kept the renewal rite for generations — every twenty years, the sworn hearts gathered at the Falls and gave the binding fresh blood and fresh word. The Wardens came to the last gathering with warrants and crossbows. They called it treason, consorting with forbidden powers. They killed the oath-keepers. All of them." Bram's voice went flat, which was how he sounded instead of angry; anger was a luxury for men with two eyes. "Except the rite needs a sworn heart freely given, and killing the givers broke the renewal, and a broken renewal means the binding starts to starve. So the founders did the clever thing. The cruel thing. They turned the binding inside out."

"Explain that to me like I'm a recruit."

"Gladly. Recruits are cheaper to educate." He set down the cup. "A living oath can bind a god — but it costs the swearer everything. Their voice in the god's ear. Their name in his mouth. Their magic, if the oath demands it. The founders couldn't get honest volunteers for that price. So they took a god instead. They wounded Vaskar at the Falls — drove the stolen piece of him, his heartstone, deep into the binding — and used his own divine weight as the mortar. His silence isn't absence, Freya. His silence is the *price being paid*. Every prayer he cannot answer, every faithful soul he cannot speak to — that's the wall holding his brother down. They built their law on a god's gagged mouth."

The silence in the room had weight. Somewhere outside, the tannery's early fires crackled, ordinary and obscene.

Fifteen years, Bram thought. Fifteen years this woman had prayed into a hole in the world and believed the hole was her own failing. He had known. He and the other survivors had known, and they had agreed — drunk, grieving, terrified — to let the silence stand, because telling her meant handing her a war.

"You knew," Freya said. Not a question. She was looking at him across the dark, and even half-blind as he was, he could feel her eyes like a hand on his chest.

"I knew."

"How long?"

"Since the night it happened. I carried the dispatches. I was supposed to deliver them to Captain Aldric's granddaughter at the Falls." He laughed, short and ugly. "I read them first. Quartermaster's privilege. Then I rode the other direction and told no one, and I've told myself every year since that it was mercy."

"Was it?"

"No," Bram said. "It was cowardice wearing mercy's coat. But I'm seventy-one years old, girl, and I've earned the right to name my sins accurately."

Freya stood. Paced. Stood again. He watched her wrestle it — the fury, the grief, the terrible arithmetic of fifteen lost years — and watched her do what she always did with unbearable things, which was fold them small and put them somewhere she could carry.

"He's conscious," she said at last. "Down there. All this time. Hearing me pray and unable to answer."

"Aye."

"Every charm I sold that didn't work. Every ritual I performed alone in the dark like a madwoman talking to weather."

"Aye."

"And now the seal's thinning, and the prayers are getting through, and every answered prayer tears the wall wider." She turned to face him. "So tell me the rest, Bram. The part you've been circling since I walked in. Only a sworn heart can renew it or break it. Whose heart? How many? And what does it cost?"

Bram looked at her for a long moment. Seventy-one years old, and here was the moment he'd been dreading since the heartstone first warmed, the moment he'd hoped with all his remaining strength would fall to someone younger and braver and less tired.

"There's the question," he said. "Sit down again. This part needs the bottle more than you do."

* * *

"The original binding took seven hearts," he said. "Seven founders, seven oaths woven together, each one carrying a seventh of the weight. That's why it held two hundred years — shared burden, renewed faithfully. The founders of the Wardens couldn't manage seven volunteers, so they substituted the heartstone and Vaskar's wound, and it's held fifteen years on borrowed divinity, and it's failing, and when it fails, Hollow-Vaskar walks out of that water and every oath ever sworn in this city comes unraveled behind him. Marriage vows. Warden commissions. The Magistrate's own authority. Everything built on the kept word goes soft as rotten rope."

"And to renew it properly?"

"One heart could do it. One sworn heart, given freely, knowing the full price." Bram met her eye and didn't flinch, though it cost him. "Your connection to Vaskar. Your name in his mouth. Your magic, all of it, permanently. And maybe more than that — the records are unclear whether the giver survives the giving. Aldric's journals suggest the seven founders lived out their days. But they were seven, sharing the load. Alone, alone, Freya—"

"I've done alone for fifteen years," Freya said. "It's the one discipline I've mastered."

"That's not discipline, that's just loneliness with good posture." He heard her almost laugh — a single rough breath — and counted it a small victory. "There's another way. Breaking it. Unbind the Hollow one deliberately, controlled, with Vaskar freed to fight his brother as he was always meant to. But that takes many sworn hearts, gathered, willing, and there's no time to gather them, and no guarantee the twins balance anymore after fifteen years of one starving in the dark while the other fed on leaked prayers. Break the seal and you might get balance. You might also get a god of broken promises loose in a city of ten thousand souls with nobody left who remembers how to fight him."

"So the choice is one sacrifice or a gamble with the whole city."

"Now you see why I drink."

Freya sat down across from him. For a while she was quiet, turning the cup in her hands, and Bram let her be quiet, because he'd soldiered beside her for eleven years and knew that her silences worked harder than most people's speeches.

"Sera," she said finally.

Bram's stomach dropped. Of course. Of course she'd already gotten there.

"What about her?"

"She's a sworn heart. Sworn to the Wardens now, but she wore the mark first. She swore the company oath same as me, same night, same blood." Freya's voice was very level, which Bram recognized as the sound of a woman walking toward a cliff and calling it a road. "If it takes more than one heart—"

"Stop."

"—then maybe she—"

"*Stop.*" Bram leaned forward, and his voice came out harsher than he intended, the old sergeant climbing up out of the quartermaster's grave. "Listen to me, Freya Hartwell, because I'm going to say this once, and then I'm going to say it again until you believe it. Sera Voss will not choose you. Do you hear me? Whatever she was, whatever you two were to each other — she put on that black coat and she buttoned it to the throat, and a Warden captain does not get to choose. That's the whole design of the thing. That's what the law is *for*. It chooses for you, in advance, so that when the moment comes you can tell yourself you had no hand in it. I watched better women than Sera hide behind that trick. I hid behind it myself."

"She hesitated. At the shrine raid. Three of her Wardens died and she stood in that wreckage looking at my charm-work like—"

"Like a woman reading a letter from home. Aye. I don't doubt she loves you. I don't doubt she hates what she does. Neither of those things will matter." Bram tapped the table, once, twice. "When the moment comes — at the Falls, with the water rising and the Magistrate shouting orders — Sera Voss will reach for her duty the way a drowning man reaches for a rope. Not because she doesn't love you. Because the law gave her permission not to, and fifteen years of obedience is a heavier armor than anything we ever wore in the company, and it doesn't come off just because someone you love is bleeding."

"You don't know that."

"I know it the way I know steel prices and bad harvests, girl. Thirty years of watching." He gentled, as much as his voice knew how. "Don't build your plan on Sera. Build it on yourself. If it must be one heart — make sure it's yours, chosen with open eyes, and not some desperate hope that your oath-sister will suddenly become the woman she was at nineteen. Hope's a fine ration but a poor foundation."

Freya was silent a long time. When she spoke, her voice had gone somewhere past anger into something colder and more useful.

"Then I need to know everything. The rite. The words. Where Aldric's journals are. If I'm going to pay this price, Bram, I'm going to pay it *correctly*, and if I'm going to break it instead, I'll break it correctly too. No more half-truths handed over one per visit like a miser counting coins."

"No more," Bram agreed. "Tonight. I'll open the strongbox. And I'll send word to the others."

She frowned. "What others? The company's dead."

"The company's *scattered*, which is different." Bram hauled himself up, joints complaining, and went to the wall where the old company banner hung under a sheet of canvas, grey sword on grey field, faded nearly to rumor. "Nine survivors, girl. Nine sworn hearts still beating, still bearing the mark. Old Maren keeping goats in the hill country. The Tallow brothers running a ferry. Little Osric, who's not little anymore and never forgave us for letting him live. None of them know the truth. All of them think their scars are just scars." He pulled the canvas free. Dust fell like grey snow. "But if the seal needs sworn hearts, then the Grey Company isn't finished. It was only waiting."

Freya stared at the banner. Bram watched her face and saw the exact moment the war re-entered her — saw her shoulders square, her chin lift, the mercenary's arithmetic running behind her eyes: assets, positions, odds.

"One more warning," Bram said quietly, folding the banner over his arm. "Before you march anywhere. The heartstone chose you, but the Hollow one knows your name now too. It spoke to you through the stone. It will keep speaking. It offers well, that thing — it offered the founders everything they'd ever lost. So when it whispers that Sera can be saved, that Vaskar can be freed without cost, that *you* can be the exception to every rule — you remember that its native language is the broken promise, and every word it says is shaped like a door out and opens like a trap."

"I know," Freya said. "I've been poor a long time, Bram. I know what doors look like when someone else holds the key."

Outside, far off toward the center of the city, a bell began to ring — not the dawn bell, not yet, but the sharp triple-stroke of the Wardens' summons, once, twice, three times, and then again, urgent as a fist on a door.

Bram cocked his head. "Raid," he said. "Big one, by the sound. They're ringing the general call." He looked at Freya, and Freya looked back, and he saw her arrive at the same conclusion he did: that the Magistrate had noticed the prayers getting answered, and that whatever came next would not wait for them to be ready.

"Strongbox," Freya said, already reaching for her sword belt. "Journals. Banner. Quickly, old man. I think the war just remembered where we live."
