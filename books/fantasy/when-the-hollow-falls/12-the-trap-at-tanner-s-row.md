## 12. The Trap at Tanner's Row

The boy was called Pip, which Sera was fairly certain was not his real name, and he cost her three coppers, a meat pie, and the last shred of her self-respect.

"Third stall past the broken pump," Pip said through a mouthful of pie. "The one what sells charms. She comes at dusk, always at dusk, like she's scared of the sun."

"You've seen her face?"

"I've seen her hood." Pip licked grease off his thumb with the thoroughness of a scholar annotating a manuscript. "But I seen her hands when she paid me to run messages. Left hand's got a scar across it, old one, like somebody tried to take her fingers off and gave up halfway." He looked up at Sera with the flat, transactional eyes of the undercity. "That worth anything extra?"

Sera looked at the scar on her own left palm — the oath-scar, twin to one she hadn't seen in fifteen years — and said, "No," in a voice that came out rougher than she intended.

Pip shrugged, unbothered. Money was money. Grief was somebody else's problem.

She should have walked away then. That was the thought that had been circling her for three days now, patient as a gull after a fishing boat: *you could still walk away.* She had the report half-written. *Charm-seller operating in the Tanners' district, supplying unlicensed devotional objects to known congregants. Identity unconfirmed.* She could have filed it, let some other captain draw the assignment, gone back to the clean arithmetic of Warden work where every criminal was a stranger.

Instead she'd spent three evenings standing in doorways smelling of tallow and river-mud, waiting for a woman she used to braid hair with on the eve of battles.

*You could still walk away.*

Sera Voss had never once walked away from anything in her life, and she wasn't going to start with this.

* * *

Tanner's Row earned its name honestly and its smell dishonestly — or rather, honestly too, which was worse. The rendering houses had closed years ago, but the stone remembered. Now the long low buildings housed weavers, bone-carvers, and the kind of quiet commerce that preferred no daylight and fewer questions. The Wardens called it a nest. The people who lived there called it home, and both parties were lying about something.

Sera went in with six Wardens and no torches. Torches announced you; the undercity read fire the way sailors read weather. She'd positioned two at the mouth of the Row, two at the far end near the drainage culvert, and kept the last two close, moving stall by stall down the crooked lane as the dusk bell rang and the market kindled itself into being — shuttered lanterns, murmured prices, the smell of fried onions doing valiant battle against the tanneries' ghost.

And there, third past the broken pump, exactly where a boy with greasy fingers and a mercenary's eye for detail had promised: a charm-stall. A folding table, a strip of cloth hung with knotted cords and stamped tin medallions, and behind it a figure in a gray hood, arranging her wares with the fussy precision of a jeweler.

Sera watched her for a long moment from the shadow of a doorway.

It was the hands that did it. The way the woman handled each little charm — not selling them so much as *checking* them, turning them over, testing knots, the way you'd check a comrade's armor straps before a march. Freya Hartwell had been the one who checked everyone's armor straps. She'd once made Sera re-buckle her sword belt four times before a skirmish at Redford Bridge, and when Sera had snapped at her about it, Freya had said, "I'd rather annoy you than bury you," and Sera had laughed until her ribs hurt, because they were twenty-two and immortal and the war was just a story they were telling each other.

The hooded figure finished arranging the charms, glanced up and down the lane — a soldier's glance, automatic, checking flanks — and Sera knew, knew in her teeth, before the woman ever turned her head.

"Hartwell," she said.

Freya went still. Then, with the unhurried calm of someone who had rehearsed this moment in her head more times than she'd admit, she began packing up her table.

"That's my name," she said. "Though I notice you're not wearing yours. Captain Voss." The title landed like a slap, deliberate. "They give you a hat yet, or do you have to earn it?"

"Sera Voss, by order of the Magistrate's Wardens, you are—"

"If you say 'under arrest,' I'm going to keep packing, and if you say the whole formal thing I'm going to laugh, and neither of those is going to go well for either of us."

"—required to accompany me for questioning regarding the distribution of unlicensed—" Sera heard herself saying the whole formal thing anyway, because fifteen years of law does not come off like a coat, and Freya did laugh, short and sharp, and slung her cloth of wares over her shoulder.

Two things happened at once.

The first was that the two nearest Wardens moved, well-drilled, cutting off the lane's south end. The second was that the market *changed*. It didn't scatter — scattering was loud, and loud drew attention. It simply dissolved, stall by stall, lantern by lantern, like breath off a mirror, until the only people left in Tanner's Row were six Wardens and one woman who had somehow become very alone in a crowd of empty doorways.

Except she wasn't alone. Sera felt it before she understood it — the prickle along her forearms, the same feeling she'd gotten in the chapel raid, in the moment before the god-flare had put Brandt and Oskan and young Merrick into the ground. Power in the air, like the charge before lightning.

"Freya," Sera said, and her hand found her sword hilt without any decision being made. "Whatever you're about to do—"

"Nothing." And there was something raw in Freya's voice under the flippancy. "I'm not doing anything, Sera. That's not mine. That's never been mine." A pause. "It's his."

"Whose?"

"You wouldn't believe me."

"Try me. I've had a bad month."

Something crossed Freya's face — hope, maybe, or its cruel cousin, the desire to be believed — and then the far end of the lane erupted, because the two Wardens at the culvert had spotted movement that wasn't movement, shadows peeling off walls, and someone shouted, and a thrown bottle burst against cobblestones, and Tanner's Row remembered it had teeth.

Later, Sera would piece together how badly the plan had gone. Her cordon had been built for one fleeing charm-seller, not for the undercity deciding, collectively and wordlessly, that the woman at the charm-stall was *theirs*. Smoke pots, probably stolen from a Warden armory months ago, bloomed in the narrow lane. The two Wardens at the south end got tangled in a fishmonger's net that nobody could later account for. And Freya Hartwell — veteran of a dozen campaigns, deserter of one — took the opening the smoke gave her and ran straight at Sera.

Not away. *At.* Because Sera was between her and the north end, and soldiers do the arithmetic fast.

The fight, when it came, lasted eleven seconds and Sera counted every one of them for the rest of her life.

One: Freya's shoulder hit hers, driving her back against a stall post. Two through four: blades out, steel ringing, the old company forms flowing out of both their bodies like water finding channels cut long ago. Five: Sera saw the opening — Freya overextended on a parry, ribs open, a thrust that would have ended everything — and took it, because duty, because law, because fifteen years.

Six: Freya turned the thrust. Not blocked — turned, blade sliding along blade, close enough that Sera saw her own reflection in the other woman's eyes, and Freya's free hand shot out and gripped Sera's wrist above the hilt, and held.

Seven through ten: they stood like that, locked, breathing hard through their teeth, smoke drifting around them, and neither blade moved another inch, and Sera realized with a lurch that Freya had not once, in eleven seconds, attempted a killing stroke. Every attack had been angles and wrists and shoves. Every opening Freya had created for herself, she had refused.

Eleven: Freya said, quietly, under the noise, "Look at your hand."

"What?"

"Your left hand. Look at it."

Sera looked. In the grip of combat her glove had torn, and her oath-scar showed pale in the smoky light — and beside it, pressed against it, Freya's bare hand, scar matching scar, and between the two scars something was happening. A warmth. A hum. Low and rhythmic, a cadence Sera had heard once, long ago, chanted by fifty voices around a fire before the company rode out at dawn.

The battle-cadence of Vaskar.

"Let go of me," Sera said, but her voice shook.

"No," Freya said. "You wanted the truth for questioning purposes. Here it is. Free of charge, since you never did learn to haggle."

The hum deepened. And the world — the smoke, the shouting, the lane — went soft and distant, and Sera fell, without moving an inch, into something else.

* * *

She stood on a hillside she knew. Knew the grass, knew the wind, knew the leaning waystone at the crest with the company mark carved into it — the spear-through-the-ring that she'd inked onto Freya's shoulder with a needle and lamp-black when they were both drunk enough to think it was a good idea.

But the sky was wrong. Torn. Above the waystone hung a wound in the world, a seam of darkness stitched with failing light, and around it — around it, holding it, *being* it — coiled a vast shape of storm and iron, wings folded around the tear like a man pressing his coat over a breach in a ship's hull. Vaskar. Not the distant warmth she remembered from the old prayers, but immediate, immense, and *bleeding*. Light ran off him like blood off a blade.

And she heard him. Faintly, terribly, the way you hear someone calling through several closed doors:

*Fifteen years. Fifteen years she prayed and I could not say her name. Tell her. Someone tell her I was not silent because I was absent—*

The vision broke.

Sera came back to herself in Tanner's Row with Freya still gripping her wrist, both of them on their knees in the muck of the lane without either having fallen, and the hum fading from between their matched scars like a tide going out.

Around them, the lane was quiet. The smoke pots had burned down to sullen wisps. The Wardens were regrouping at the ends of the Row, confused, wary, and — Sera noted distantly — conspicuously not advancing, because their captain was kneeling in the dirt with the fugitive and making no move whatsoever to arrest her.

"Captain?" called Dorn, the senior of the six, from the south end. His voice had the careful flatness of a man who has decided to see nothing. "Orders?"

Freya's eyes met Sera's over their joined hands. There was ash in the gray of them, and exhaustion, and underneath it all something that looked agonizingly like the girl who'd braided her hair at Redford Bridge.

"They'll write it up however you tell them to," Freya said softly. "That's the thing about you, Sera. You've always been able to make the ledger say whatever keeps you upright at night. So tell me — what's it going to say? *Fugitive escaped in the confusion?* Or *captain engaged target, target displayed anomalous capabilities, requesting access to founding archives*?" A beat. "Because if you want to know why three of your Wardens died in that chapel — if you want to know what your Magistrate is really feeding to the Falls next week — you already know which entry gets you there."

Dorn called again. "Captain!"

Sera released her wrist. Stood. Found her voice somewhere below her ribs and hauled it up.

"Fugitive escaped in the confusion," she said. "Fall back to the garrison. We'll re-form at dawn."

Behind her, in the settling smoke, she heard Freya Hartwell exhale — a long, careful breath, the sound of a woman who had bet her life on an old scar and won — and then footsteps, quick and light, retreating north past the broken pump.

Sera stared at her left hand.

The hum was gone. But the warmth stayed, faint as banked coals, and with it a phrase that was not hers, lodged behind her sternum like a splinter:

*founding archives.*

She'd need the key. The key was kept by Archivist Melden, who kept nothing without a countersignature, and a request to open the sealed founding records would travel upward faster than gossip in a garrison.

Sera looked down the empty lane where her sister-in-arms had vanished, then at her six puzzled Wardens, then at her own scarred palm, and began — with the grim, methodical calm that had built her entire career — to plan how to steal from her own government before dawn.

She was going to need a better lie than usual. Fortunately, she knew where to buy one: third stall past the broken pump, at dusk, like she's scared of the sun.

She hoped Freya would be there.

She hoped, for both their sakes, that Freya would be wrong about what happened next.
