## 13. What the Wardens Burn

The archives beneath the Hall of Wardens smelled of dust, tallow smoke, and the particular sweetness of old paper left too long in the dark — a smell Sera Voss had always found comforting, back when she believed that what was written down could be trusted.

She had come down twice before in her nine years of service. Once as a green recruit, sent to fetch a precedent for a property dispute between two dye-houses (the answer had been forty years old and made no sense to anyone, which was apparently the point). Once to file her own commendation after the Saltmarket raid. Both times, the archivist on duty had been a small, ancient man named Pell who spoke mostly in coughs and regarded every document like it might bite him.

Tonight there was no Pell. Tonight she had signed the ledger herself — *Captain Sera Voss, official inquiry, ref: Hartwell* — and the duty clerk had looked at her name, then at her face, and decided not to ask questions, which told her more about her own reputation than any commendation ever had.

The lamp guttered as she set it on the reading table. She waited until its flame steadied before touching the first box.

Three nights since Tanner's Row. Three nights since Freya had stood over her with a knife at her throat and a stone in her palm that had sung — *sung*, there was no other word for it — with a sound that had gone through Sera's ribs like a drumbeat through a door.

She had reported none of it.

That was the thing she kept turning over, the way a tongue worries a loose tooth. Not the fight, not even the stone. The report. In fifteen years of service she had never once failed to file. A Warden's word was only worth anything because it was complete, because it went into the record whole, because the law did not depend on what a person felt like saying on a given night. Her father — a customs clerk, a man who had loved ledgers the way other men loved horses — used to say: *the page doesn't care how you feel, girl. That's why we have pages.*

And three nights ago, sitting on the wet cobbles of Tanner's Row with her wrist sprained and her certainty cracked clean through, Sera Voss had looked at the blank form in her mind and found nothing she could write that would be true.

So she had come here instead. To the sealed shelves. To the founding archive, which no serving officer below the rank of captain was permitted to open, and which captains almost never opened, because the founding era was dull, everyone said so, all charters and land grants and correspondence with the Magistrate's office about drainage rights.

She pulled the first box toward her and broke the seal.

* * *

The early papers were exactly as advertised. Charters. Land grants. A truly astonishing quantity of correspondence about drainage rights, which Sera read with the grim patience of a woman determined to be bored, because boredom was safe, and safety had become a scarce commodity.

Then she found the letter dated the winter of the Founding — thirty-one years after the Magistrate took Corvale, eleven years before the Wardens were chartered — and stopped being bored.

It was written by Aldous Renwick. Sera knew the name from the murals: Renwick the Just, first Captain of the Wardens, the stern face above the motto *Order Is Mercy*. Every recruit learned his sayings. *A city without fear is a city without faith, and we supply both.*

The letter was addressed to a woman named Ilse Marrow. The handwriting was hurried, the ink blotted in places, as if written fast and by lamplight and possibly drunk.

*I have done a terrible thing and I find I cannot carry it alone and cannot confess it either, so I will put it in your keeping, Ilse, as you are the only soul I trust and you are far enough away that I can pretend you are not real.*

*You know what we told the Magistrate. You know what we told everyone. The gods are dead, or if not dead then gone, and what remains is superstition and the men who profit from it. We said it so often I half believe it still.*

*But the Falls were never empty. You know that. You were there.*

Sera's hand had gone cold around the page. She made herself keep reading.

*We did not seal the Falls to protect the city from the gods. We sealed it to protect us from one god, and we used another to do it. The wounded one held the hungry one down, and the wound was our making. Vaskar came to us in good faith — he heard our oath, he answered it, he fought for us when the twin rose in the flood year, and how did we repay him? We drove an iron oath through him while he was spent, while he was kneeling in the shallows with his brother's teeth in his throat, and we bound them together under the water and called it justice.*

*He has not spoken since. Of course he has not spoken. We took his voice to make the knot hold.*

*Every Warden since has been a jailer and called himself a priest. Every burning we have fed to the Falls — Ilse, you know what the blood does, you watched the water rise after the Harrow executions same as I did. It strengthens the seal. We built our law on feeding prisoners to a chained god and calling the chain holy.*

*I tell myself it holds something worse. I tell myself the alternative was the flood again, the drowned quarter, the screaming. Perhaps that is even true. But do not let them tell you it was mercy. It was theft. We stole a god's freedom and his voice and called it order, and now every prayer that goes unanswered in this city goes unanswered because we are holding his mouth shut.*

Sera set the letter down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

The lamp flame leaned and straightened. Somewhere overhead, boots crossed the hall floor — a patrol coming off shift — and the sound seemed to belong to some other life, some other city, one where the words *Order Is Mercy* meant what they said.

She thought: *forgery.* The thought arrived on schedule, dutiful, wearing its uniform. Documents lie. This is precisely why we verify. And then the next thought, quieter: *verify against what? Against the charter? Against the murals?*

Against the singing stone in Freya Hartwell's hand. Against the way three of her own Wardens had died in a chapel when a hedge-prayer flared true. Against the fact that the gods were waking now, after fifteen silent years, exactly as the seal weakened — and nobody had asked why they woke, only how to burn the people who noticed.

She pulled the next box.

* * *

There were others. That was the worst part, or the best part; Sera honestly couldn't decide. Renwick had not stopped writing to Ilse Marrow. For six years, in letters that grew shorter and harder and more sober, he kept accounts the way her father kept accounts — because the page doesn't care how you feel.

*The Magistrate's office suspects nothing. They do not want to suspect. Fear is cheaper than truth and we deliver it fresh daily.*

*We burned a hedge-priest of the Mother-of-Reeds today. The water rose four inches. Four inches, Ilse. I measured it myself like a man measuring grain. What am I becoming?*

*A warden came to me last month asking why the old battle-cadence is forbidden. I told him it invites disorder. He wrote it down. He believed me. They all believe me. That is the horror of it — I have discovered that if you give people a rule and a uniform, they will build a cathedral on top of anything, including a lie, especially a lie.*

The final letter was dated the spring of Renwick's death. It was four lines long.

*Ilse — the twin stirs. Vaskar spends himself holding it and grows fainter each year. One day the seal will need renewing and we have no sworn hearts left among the honest, only among the hunted. Pray the day comes after my time. I have no stomach to meet him again, and less to explain to him what we did with his gift.*

*Burn these after you've read them. Or don't. Perhaps someone should know.*

Someone should know.

"Someone knows," Sera said aloud, to the empty archive, and her voice sounded strange down among the boxes — young, and unsteady, and not at all like a captain's.

* * *

She worked until the lamp needed trimming twice more. There was more to find, and she forced herself to find it, because a half-truth was just a lie with better posture.

She found the original binding-oath, copied out in Renwick's careful clerk-hand: a mercenary company's oath, sworn by living voices, *freely given, freely held, freely ended* — the old formula, the one from before the Wardens existed, the one the hedge-faithful still used in their little illegal chapels. The founders hadn't invented anything. They'd stolen a covenant and dressed it in a charter.

She found the execution ledgers, and here she had to stop for a while, both hands flat on the table, breathing. Because the ledgers didn't just list names. Beside each name, in columns, neat as a grain-merchant's book: *water level before. Water level after.*

Fourteen hundred entries. Somebody had sat where she was sitting and measured the Falls after every single burning, for sixty years, and recorded the results, and filed them, and promoted themselves for it.

Her father's voice again, warm and useless: *the page doesn't care how you feel.*

No, Sera thought. But I do. Apparently I do. Apparently that's the problem.

She found one more thing, near dawn. A thin folder, misfiled — deliberately misfiled, she suspected, tucked inside a box of drainage correspondence where no honest reader would ever look — containing a single sheet in a different hand. Smaller, sharper, feminine. No signature, but a mark instead: a boar's head broken by a spear.

The old company mark. Ninth Spear Company. The company that had taken the binding-oath from the founders' hands and carried it, and whose roster Sera had burned in her own hearth two months ago rather than surrender to the inquest.

The note read:

*Captain Renwick asked the Ninth to swear the renewal-oath in perpetuity. We refused. We had already given our oaths once, to a god, and we do not sell the same goods twice. He took the heartstone instead, as surety. Tell whoever comes after: the stone remembers the true oath. If the day comes, it will show them the difference between a covenant and a cage.*

*— Q.M., Ninth Spears*

Quartermaster. Sera closed her eyes and saw a one-eyed old man behind a market stall of dubious luck-charms, who had once, years ago, over cheap beer, told her: *You and Freya both swore to the same god, girl. Only difference is she kept hers and you traded yours for a pension and a nice coat.*

She had laughed at the time. She remembered laughing.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the high slit windows, and Sera sat in it with everything spread out before her like a battlefield after the fighting: the letters, the ledgers, the oath, the note. Sixty years of a lie, documented, cross-referenced, and filed.

The law was her religion. She knew that; she'd known it for years, though she'd have denied it with a straight face. Other people had gods; she had procedure. When her mother died she hadn't prayed, she'd filed the paperwork correctly and on time, and it had helped, actually helped, the way ritual helps. When Freya had vanished from the Ninth — desertion, the company said, though Sera had never once believed it — she hadn't grieved, she'd transferred regiments, and the transfer form had a space marked *reason for departure*, and she had written *career advancement*, and it had helped, actually helped, the way a lid helps.

And now the lid was off, and underneath was a hole shaped like fourteen hundred names, and her religion was the instrument of it.

Because that was the thing. It wasn't that the law had failed. It was that the law had *worked*. Perfectly. Efficiently. Every mechanism functioning exactly as designed — the informants, the tribunals, the burnings, the ledgers, the beautiful complete records — all of it running smooth as a loom to weave a cage around a wounded god and a frightened city, and call the weaving holy.

You couldn't reform a system that was succeeding at being monstrous. You could only refuse it.

She thought about Freya's face at Tanner's Row. Not angry — that was what she kept returning to, the detail that wouldn't sit still. Freya had had her blade at Sera's throat and had looked *sad*, the way you look at a house you grew up in that's been sold to strangers. *They'll use your oath-sister's name to hunt me,* she'd said, *and you'll sign the orders, because signing is what you do instead of feeling.* And Sera had hated her for it, and reported nothing, and here she was, three nights later, sitting in the proof.

A knock at the archive door made her start so badly she knocked the lamp chimney against its stand.

"Captain?" The duty clerk's muffled voice. "Dispatch from the Hall. Priority."

She composed her face — the exercise of it felt suddenly obscene, arranging her features over the wreckage — and called "Enter."

The clerk was a nervous young man named Tally who admired her openly and had once asked her to teach him the longsword grip, and who handed her the dispatch with both hands like an offering.

She read it standing. Read it twice.

The Magistrate's office, countersigned by the Provost of Executions: requisition of the full holding cells, Grayfriar and Underhall both. Two hundred and eleven prisoners of faith-crime, currently detained, to be conveyed to the Hollow Falls within the fortnight for a *Rite of Public Confirmation*. Attendance mandatory for all senior officers. Purpose of rite: *restoration of civic stability following recent unauthorized manifestations.*

Unauthorized manifestations. As if the gods waking up were a plumbing fault.

Two hundred and eleven. The largest burning in the history of the Wardens. And the ledgers lay open on the table behind her, and she knew — she knew with a certainty that required no documents at all — what four inches of rising water looked like, and what two hundred and eleven would raise.

"Captain?" Tally said. "Is it — should I prepare the company muster?"

Sera looked at the boy. Nineteen, maybe twenty. Kept a picture of his mother over his bunk. Would stand at the Falls in his nice new coat and watch, because watching was duty, and duty was mercy, and mercy was order, and the whole beautiful machine would grind those words into paste before lunch.

"No," Sera heard herself say. "Tell the Hall the dispatch was received. Tell them I require three days to complete an official inquiry before mustering. Reference Hartwell." The name came out steady, which surprised her. "I'm still gathering evidence."

"Yes, Captain." He hesitated at the door. "Begging pardon, but — is it true the Hartwell woman was your sister-in-arms?"

"She was," Sera said.

"And now she's a criminal?"

Sera looked past him, up through the slit window, at the pale sky going pink over the rooftops, in the direction of the Falls.

"I don't know yet," she said, which was the most honest sentence she'd spoken in nine years of service, and Tally, bless him, wrote it down nowhere at all.

When he'd gone, she gathered the papers. All of them. The letters, the ledgers, the oath, the note from the quartermaster of the Ninth. She considered the archive fire — there was a brazier in the corner for destroying damaged documents, properly witnessed, properly logged — and dismissed it with something close to contempt. Properly logged. Everything was properly logged. Logging was how they'd done it.

Instead she took the papers up to her quarters, to her own hearth, the same hearth where a company roster had gone up in flames two months ago because some things deserved a private fire.

She fed them in a handful at a time. Renwick's confessions curled and blackened. The execution ledgers — she saved the ledgers for last — went up in slow, dense smoke, and she stood over them like a woman warming her hands at the only honest heat left in the city, and let her eyes sting, and did not pretend it was the smoke.

*Perhaps someone should know.*

Someone would. But not the Magistrate, not the Provost, not the machine that would take the truth and file it under *sedition*. Someone with a singing stone and a sad, furious face and fifteen years of unanswered prayers, who had once shared her bedroll and her bread and her god.

The last corner of the last ledger caught, flared, and died. On the hearthstone, unburnt, she'd left one page — the quartermaster's note, the boar's-head mark, the words *it will show them the difference between a covenant and a cage.*

Evidence for the defense. She allowed herself one dry breath that was almost a laugh.

Then she buckled on her sword, put the page in her breast pocket over her heart, and went out into the morning to find the most wanted woman in Corvale — not with a warrant this time, and not yet knowing what she was bringing her instead, except that it was heavy, and it was true, and it was going to change everything.

The Falls were roaring that morning. She was almost certain they'd never roared before.
