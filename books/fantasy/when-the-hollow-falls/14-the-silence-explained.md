## 14. The Silence Explained

Freya did not sleep that night.

She told herself it was the heartstone's fault — the way it sat on her table like a guest who refused to leave, humming its low, patient hum, warming whenever she came within arm's reach of it. She'd put it in a drawer. It had hummed louder. She'd wrapped it in wool and shoved it under her bed, and at some hour past midnight she'd lain awake listening to it pulse through the floorboards like a second heartbeat, and finally she'd gotten up, retrieved it, and set it on the table in plain view where she could glare at it properly.

"You could have said something," she told it.

The heartstone glowed faintly orange, the color of banked coals, and said nothing.

"Fifteen years," she said. "Fifteen years I lit candles for you. Fifteen years I kept your feast days alone in a rented room like a madwoman talking to empty air. I sold *charms*, Vaskar. Charms. Do you know what I used to be? I carried a war-axe the length of my arm and I sang the battle-cadence and you answered. You answered *every time*. And then one morning you didn't, and I spent fifteen years deciding whether I'd been abandoned or whether I'd never been worth answering in the first place."

The stone warmed. She felt it from across the room, a gentle pressure against her cheek like sunlight through a window.

"Don't," she said. "Don't do that. Don't be warm at me. You don't get to be warm at me yet."

She made tea she didn't drink. She sat by the window watching Corvale's undercity breathe below her — the cook-fires, the lantern-sellers, the little knots of people moving between shrines now that praying no longer meant pyres. The whole city was whispering to its gods again. And somewhere beneath all of it, beneath the Falls, two voices waited in the water: one wounded and familiar, one hungry and not.

She slept, eventually, sitting up, her hand on the heartstone without meaning to put it there.

* * *

The dream was an old one, or it should have been. For fifteen years her dreams of Vaskar had been the same: a battlefield at dusk, banners gone gray, herself standing in a field of the dead calling his name into a silence so complete she could hear her own blood moving. She'd stopped having the dream years ago. You stop dreaming of someone when they've become a wound instead of a presence.

But this time the field was different. The dusk was warmer. And the silence —

The silence had a shape.

She knew that shape. She'd stood inside it once before, at the Falls, listening to the water speak with two voices. The silence wasn't absence. It had never been absence. It was a hand pressed over a mouth.

*Freya.*

Her name, in the dream, spoken the way he'd always said it — not her whole name, never the whole thing, just *Freya*, clipped short, the way a commander says a soldier's name across a noisy camp. She woke with tears already drying on her face and dawn coming gray through the shutters and the heartstone blazing on the table like a small captive sun.

"Oh," she said. Her voice cracked. "Oh, you absolute bastard. You were *there*. All this time, you were—"

The stone pulsed. Not words. Not yet. But something like a nod, heavy and weary and unmistakably *him*, and Freya Hartwell, veteran of three wars, slayer of the Marsh-King's champion, sat down hard on her own floor and wept like a girl.

* * *

Bram arrived mid-morning, because Bram could smell a crisis the way carrion birds smelled a battle. He let himself in with the key he'd copied off her twenty years ago and had never, despite repeated objections, returned.

"I brought bread," he announced, one eye taking in the scorched scorch-mark on her table where the heartstone had flared overnight, the shattered teacup, and Freya herself, red-eyed, unwashed, and armed with a butter knife for reasons neither of them examined too closely. "And I see you're handling things well."

"He talked to me." She took the bread anyway. "In a dream. Or near enough. He said my name, Bram. My *name*. He's conscious. He's been conscious the whole — the seal, the binding, it doesn't just cage the twin. It cages *both* of them. He's been down there in the dark with that thing for fifteen years, awake, unable to speak, and every prayer that got answered since spring has been him bleeding through the cracks, and I—" She stopped. The butter knife trembled. "I thought he'd left me. I built my whole life around the shape of being left."

Bram set down his pack slowly. The old quartermaster moved carefully these days; the war lived in his joints now. "Sit down, girl."

"I am sitting down."

"Sit down *properly*, then, and drink something, because what I have to tell you goes better with tea and worse on an empty stomach." He lowered himself into her one good chair and studied her with his single eye, which had always been more perceptive than most people's two. "You want the full history. The part I held back."

"I want everything."

He nodded, unsurprised, and began.

"The company that became your company — the Grey Standard — started as nine soldiers who fought together in the Godswar back before Corvale had a Magistrate. They weren't holy folk. They were hired swords, same as us, same as everyone. But they served under a chaplain, a woman named Odile, who was sworn to Vaskar the way you were sworn — heart-deep, name-in-his-mouth sworn. And when the Godswar ended and the cities wanted peace, the gods wouldn't give it. Vaskar's twin — the hollow half of him, the half that's all hunger and no honor — wouldn't lay down arms. Couldn't. Hunger doesn't know how."

"So they bound him," Freya said. "I know this part. The Wardens' founding."

"They bound him," Bram agreed, "but here's what the archives leave out, what only the company knew, what got passed mouth to mouth down the roster until there was nobody left but me and you. A binding like that needs a sworn heart. A living oath, freely given, anchored on both sides. Odile gave hers — her bond to Vaskar, her name in his mouth, the whole weight of her devotion — and it anchored the seal from above. But a seal needs an anchor below too, or it's just a lid on boiling water. So Vaskar went down with his twin. Voluntarily. He took the other end of the oath into himself and let them weld the two of them together with his own godhood as the iron."

Freya set down the bread. Her hands had gone very still, which anyone who knew her would have recognized as the most dangerous thing about her.

"And when the seal weakened — when it started needing renewal —"

"It needed a new sworn heart. Yes." Bram's voice gentled. "Odile died old and safe in a house in the hill country, and the seal held anyway, because a god's side of an oath doesn't die. But the mortal anchor rots. Every generation it needs renewing, or the Wardens' founders found another way: blood. Pain. Feeding the Falls enough suffering to patch the cracks with fear. That's why they burn people, Freya. It was never about justice. It was maintenance."

"And Vaskar's silence. Mine. Fifteen years ago."

Bram looked away, toward the window, toward the sound of the Falls far off, a sound Corvale natives stopped hearing the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

"The seal was failing fifteen years ago. Badly. The founders' line had thinned, their oaths were rote words by then, nothing living in them. Something had to hold it, and there was exactly one thing left in the world with enough sworn weight to do it." He turned back to her. "Vaskar chose to go silent rather than let it break. His voice was the last thread of himself he hadn't already given. Speaking costs a god nothing, girl — it's breathing. But *your* god is welded to his twin, and every word he speaks, the twin speaks too, or feeds on the speaking, or slips a finger through the gap the word leaves. Silence wasn't abandonment. Silence was him holding the door shut with his body while the thing behind it clawed."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere below, a cart rattled over cobblestones. The heartstone on the table pulsed slow and steady, like something breathing in its sleep.

"He heard me," Freya said. It wasn't a question. "All those candles. All those rituals. He heard every one."

"He heard every one," Bram said. "And couldn't answer. And I'd wager — I'd wager the price of my other eye — that hearing them and not answering hurt him worse than the binding does."

Freya looked at her hands. They were steady again. That was the trouble with fury, in her experience: it didn't shake you. It *stilled* you. It had stilled her on battlefields a hundred times, that cold clean quiet right before everything broke open.

"Who knew," she said softly.

"What?"

"Who knew he was silent *on purpose*. Who let me believe I was forsaken. Who watched me sell luck-charms to desperate mothers for fifteen years and never said *he hasn't left you, girl, he's holding a door shut*." She looked up, and Bram, to his credit, did not flinch. "The founders knew. The Magistrate knows. Whoever keeps the sealed archives knows. And the Wardens burn people for praying to a god who went mute *saving them*, and nobody — not one soul in this city with the power to speak — said a word."

"That's about the size of it," Bram said quietly.

"Fifteen years." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing, and Bram, veteran of forty years of wars, shifted in his chair. "Fifteen years I thought I was unloved by my own god. There were nights I nearly walked into the sea, Bram. There were nights the only reason I didn't was that dead men can't keep feasts for absent friends, and I couldn't bear to make his silence total. And the whole time — the whole *time* — he was down there in the dark, awake, listening to me talk to him like a widow at a grave, and he couldn't say *I'm here, I'm here, I never left, this isn't your doing*—"

The heartstone flared. Not gently, this time. Heat rolled off it in a wave, and the humming rose to a frequency that made her teeth ache, and for one moment Freya felt it — felt *him* — pressing against the inside of his silence like a man pressing his palms flat against a wall from the other side. *I'm here. I'm here. I never left.* Not words. Just the truth of them, vast and wounded and furious on her behalf, which was somehow the worst part, because how dare he be furious for her, when she had all this fury and nowhere to put it except—

Except.

She breathed. Once. Twice. The stone dimmed to coals again.

"Bram," she said. "How much does the seal have left?"

"Not enough. The prayers are feeding it strength, strange as that sounds — every answered prayer is him spending himself to keep the door shut, buying time. But the Magistrate knows the ledger's running out. Why d'you think the burnings have doubled? Why d'you think they're filling the cells?" Bram's eye was grim. "Fear patches cracks. Blood patches better. And a great big sacrifice, a mass of the faithful burned at once at the Falls — that's not justice, girl. That's mortar."

Freya stood. She picked up the heartstone, and it settled into her palm warm as a living thing, and somewhere beneath the city a wounded god and his hungry twin both stirred at her touch, and she found she did not care.

"Then we're done letting them maintain their lie with other people's children," she said. "Get the roster. Get every name still living off the old rolls. If the seal needs a sworn heart, it can bloody well have mine — but it'll have it on *my* terms, at *my* choosing, and not as mortar for the Magistrate's gallows."

Bram rose, slower than he used to, and grinned the grin that had once preceded every disaster the Grey Standard ever survived.

"There she is," he said. "There's the axe-hand."

* * *

That night she dreamed of the battlefield again, but this time she walked it knowingly, banners gray, dusk warm, and at the far end of the field where the silence had its shape, she laid her palm flat against the air the way you'd press your hand to the far side of a wall, and she felt him press back.

"Hold the door," she told him. "I'm coming with reinforcements."

The silence, for the first time in fifteen years, felt less like abandonment and more like what it had always truly been: a promise, held shut with both hands.

When she woke, the heartstone was glowing steady and bright as a signal fire, and far off, beneath the sound of the Falls, something else had begun to stir — and this time, it was laughing.
