## 15. The Magistrate's Bargain

The Magistrate's study smelled of cedar oil and old paper, which Sera Voss had always taken for the smell of justice itself. Standing in it now, three days after Tanner's Row, she understood that it was only the smell of a very rich man who did not want anyone to know he burned things.

"Captain." He did not look up from his desk. He was a small man made smaller by the enormous chair the city had bought him, and he had a way of speaking that made every sentence sound like a favor being granted. "You've read the report on the chapel incident."

"I wrote the report on the chapel incident, Magistrate."

"And a thorough piece of work it was. Three Wardens dead. Property destroyed. And the cause of it all—" he tapped the page with one finger, "—listed here as *anomalous resonance.*"

"It's what happened."

"What happened," the Magistrate said, finally lifting his head, "is that the Falls are hungry. You knew this. I know this. The founders knew it when they built the first Warden garrison with its back to the water, like a man sitting in a chair with a wolf behind him and calling himself brave." He folded his hands. He had small, soft hands. Sera had once watched them sign a death warrant without pausing between names. "What the founders also knew — and what they wrote down, Captain, for those of us permitted to read it — is that the seal beneath the water is not maintained by law or prayer or good intentions. It is maintained by *weight*. By what we give it."

Sera kept her face still. She had gotten very good at that in fifteen years of wearing a captain's badge over an oath-sister's memory.

"The executions," she said. It wasn't a question. Everyone in Corvale knew the condemned went to the Hollow Falls to burn. That was the spectacle of it. That was the point.

"The executions," the Magistrate agreed pleasantly, "have been working beautifully for two hundred years. Every faith-burner, every hedge-witch, every charm-peddler we've fed to the water has gone down into that dark and held the door shut behind them. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Captain? Your entire career. Your garrison. Your *law*. It rests on the backs of the people you arrest." He smiled, and it reached his eyes about as far as winter reaches a cellar. "And now the door is rattling. Which means it needs more backs."

"How many?"

He slid a ledger across the desk. Not the execution roster — Sera had seen enough of those to last her several lifetimes. This was different. Thinner pages. A newer binding. She opened it and felt something in her chest go cold and quiet, the way it had gone cold and quiet the night she'd found Freya's charm-work in the wrecked chapel, threaded through the rubble like veins through a wrist.

Forty-one names.

Not forty-one *condemned*. Forty-one prisoners awaiting trial. Waiting, as the law promised them, for a hearing. For witnesses. For the slow, dull machinery of justice that Sera had spent half her life believing in even when it bit her hand.

"They haven't been sentenced," she said.

"No."

"Some of them haven't even been *charged*."

"Some of them," the Magistrate said, with the tone of a man correcting a clerk's arithmetic, "have been charged as of this morning. The paperwork is being drawn up. It will all be perfectly legal, Captain. That is rather the genius of our arrangement. We are not murderers. We are a court."

*We,* Sera thought. As if she were part of the sentence. As if she were ink.

She looked at the names. She recognized some. Old Marta from the tallow-row, who sold candles blessed by nothing at all and charged double because pilgrims expected to be cheated by someone. The twins from Cooper's Lane, sixteen years old if they were a day, arrested for singing a harvest hymn in the wrong key within earshot of a Warden patrol. A boy whose name she didn't know, listed only as *the bell-ringer*, and she thought: someone rang a bell for one of the old gods, somewhere, and this child was nearest to it when the world ended.

"This isn't justice," she heard herself say. Her voice came out level. Somewhere inside her, a hairline crack that had been growing since Tanner's Row widened another finger's width. "This is feeding people to a thing in the water so it doesn't eat us instead. And lying about it."

"Yes," the Magistrate said simply.

She'd expected denial. She'd prepared arguments — she'd lain awake half the night preparing arguments, the way she used to before battle, cataloguing angles of attack. She had not prepared for a small soft man to agree with her and keep smiling.

"That is precisely what justice has always been, Captain, everywhere, forever," he continued. "A bargain struck between fear and order, dressed in better clothes. The founders understood this. They stole a god to make their seal, they broke another god to hold it, and they wrote laws around the wound so that no one would ever look too closely at the scar. You have read the archives now. I'm told you have." His eyes sharpened slightly. "I'm told you requested founding documents after the incident at Tanner's Row. An unusual reading list for a busy officer."

"The truth seemed relevant."

"The truth is *inventory*, Captain. It sits on a shelf. What matters is what we do with it." He rose, which meant the audience was ending whether she was finished or not. Small men loved standing up during conversations; it was the only elevation available to them. "The rite is in six days. The prisoners will be moved to the Falls garrison under full escort. You will command the transfer personally."

"Me."

"You're the best officer I have. And your presence lends the operation... credibility." He came around the desk and stood close, close enough that she could smell the cedar on him, and lowered his voice as though offering her a kindness. "I know the Hartwell woman was your sister-in-arms. I know she's been seen in the undercity. Find her, don't find her — that's your affair. But these forty-one go into the water, Captain, and nothing walks out of that garrison with them. Am I understood?"

For a moment — one long heartbeat — Sera let herself imagine saying no. Let herself feel how the word would sit in her mouth. *No.* One syllable. Fifteen years of service balanced against one syllable, and the scales trembling.

Then she thought of Freya's face across drawn blades at Tanner's Row, holding out a stone that hummed like a war-drum, saying *look at it, Sera, look at what they built us on* — and she thought of the bell-ringer, whoever he was, sixteen maybe, waiting in a cell right now for a trial he would never receive.

"Understood, Magistrate," she said.

* * *

She did not go back to the garrison. She walked.

Corvale in the evening was a city pretending very hard to be ordinary. Lamplighters worked their rounds. A fishmonger hosed blood off his cart and argued cheerfully with his wife about it. Two children chased each other around a dry fountain, shrieking, and Sera watched them and thought: *in six days, forty-one people stop existing so that these children can keep doing that.* And then she thought, with a bitterness that surprised her: *and it won't even work. The door will rattle again. There will always be another ledger.*

She ended up, without deciding to, at the edge of Tanners' Row. Of course she did. Her feet had opinions about where she was allowed to grieve, and they had chosen this place — the scorched stretch of street where she'd put a blade to her own sworn sister and been shown mercy instead of a wound.

The buildings were still blackened. Someone had chalked a mark on the wall of the cooper's shop — a simple circle with a line through it, the kind of sigil hedge-folk used to mean *blessed ground* or *safe house* or *we remember*. Illegal, probably. Everything holy was illegal here; it was practically the city motto.

"Sergeant Voss is going to worry," said a voice behind her.

Sera turned. Corporal Dennick leaned against a post, off-duty, eating a meat pie with the unbothered air of a man who followed his captain to grim places because it was easier than letting her stand in them alone. He'd been with her nine years. He was the closest thing she had to a friend who hadn't known her before the badge.

"You followed me."

"I happened to be walking," Dennick said, gesturing with the pie at the entire opposite end of the city. "In this direction. Coincidentally."

"Dennick."

"Captain." He wiped his hands and straightened up, and his grin faded into something more careful. "Word in the barracks is there's a big transfer coming. Full escort, sealed orders, everyone walking on eggshells. Word also is you're commanding it." He paused. "Word additionally is that nobody's told anybody what the charges are, which is strange, because usually by now somebody's bragged about somebody."

Sera said nothing.

"Three Wardens died at that chapel," Dennick said quietly. "I carried two of them out myself. I'm not a soft man, Captain. But I've done transfers before, and I know what the Falls garrison means, and I know that forty-one people is not a number you move for a *trial*." He looked at her steadily. "So I'll ask you once, off the record, as a man asking a woman he's followed into fires for nine years. Is it what it sounds like?"

And there it was again, the crack, running the length of her now. Because she could lie to the Magistrate with her face. She could lie to the whole city. But Dennick had pulled bodies out of wreckage beside her and never once asked her to explain herself, and lying to him felt like spitting in a well.

"I read the founding archives," she said. "Everything the Wardens are — the law, the burns, the Falls — it's built on a stolen god and a wound we've been salving with executions for two hundred years. The seal is failing. The Magistrate means to feed it forty-one people to buy time." Her jaw tightened. "Some of them are children."

Dennick was silent for a long moment. Down the street, the fishmonger's argument resolved into laughter. The lamplighter moved on to the next lamp.

"So what does the captain do?" he asked.

"I don't know," Sera said, and it was the most honest sentence she'd spoken in fifteen years, and it terrified her more than the twin beneath the water ever could. "That's the problem, Dennick. I have spent my whole life knowing exactly what the captain does. The captain follows orders. The captain keeps her face still. The captain believes the law is the last wall between people and the dark." She looked at the chalked circle on the cooper's wall. "But the law *is* the dark. It built the dark. It feeds the dark. And I stood in front of it my whole life with my back straight thinking I was the wall."

Dennick nodded slowly, the way soldiers nod — absorbing information, filing it, planning around it.

"For the record," he said, "if the captain were to, hypothetically, lose certain sealed orders in a strong wind, I'd want to know where she drinks. So I could coincidentally be walking in that direction too."

"Dennick—"

"I'm just a man who likes pie," he said, picking up his half-eaten one as evidence. "Wherever it takes me."

* * *

She went back to her quarters and took out the company roster.

It was the fourth time that week. She knew the ritual by heart now: unlock the box, unfold the brittle pages, run her eyes down the names of the Ashen Company until she found the ones that mattered. *Kavanagh, Bramwell — quartermaster. Missing, presumed retired.* And below him:

*Hartwell, Freya.*

Her thumb found the name the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. Fifteen years ago, when she'd burned the rest of the roster to keep Warden investigators away from her old comrades, she had sat with this page in her hands for an entire night, quill poised over it, ink drying on the nib. Strike the name and Freya Hartwell ceased to exist in the eyes of the law — safe, invisible, free. Leave it, and leave a thread for any determined investigator to follow back through the ashes of the company to the woman selling charms in the night market.

She had left it. She had told herself it was oversight. She had told herself a great many things.

Now she unfolded the page and looked at the name and understood, finally, why she'd never been able to strike it. It wasn't sentiment. Or it wasn't *only* sentiment. It was that striking the name would mean admitting the oath was over, and the oath — *watch each other's backs, share the fire, tell no lies between sisters* — was the only law she'd ever believed in completely. The Warden code was rules. The oath was true. And somewhere in the last month, the rules had eaten the truth alive.

The Magistrate wanted her to herd forty-one souls to the water. Wanted her name on the transfer, her badge at the head of the column, her face — credible, trusted, *hers* — lending its weight to the lie. He wanted her complicity the way the seal wanted backs: silently, structurally, permanently.

And the Magistrate was right about one thing. It would all be perfectly legal.

Sera sat for a long time with the roster open, the lamp burning low, the city settling around her into its nightly hush. Then she did something she had never done in fifteen years of service.

She began, carefully, methodically, to copy the prisoner ledger out by hand. All forty-one names. Ages where she knew them. Cells where she could guess. The garrison rotations, the escort schedule, the route to the Falls — everything she'd memorized in the Magistrate's study while her face stayed perfectly, professionally still.

When she finished, near midnight, she looked at the two documents side by side: the official roster of the condemned, and her old company's page with Freya's name still living on it. One thread leading to the woman she'd betrayed. One list of the people she was being ordered to betray next.

She rolled the copied ledger tight, sealed it with plain wax — no badge, no sigil, nothing traceable — and wrote a single line on the outside in her careful captain's hand:

*To F.H., charm-seller, night market. From someone who owes her a debt she can't strike from any book.*

Then she pulled her cloak over her armor, left the badge on the table — just for tonight, just for this errand — and went out into the dark to find a smuggler who owed her money and didn't ask questions.

Six days, the Magistrate had said.

Sera Voss intended to make sure that when the hour came, she would already have chosen which side of the water she stood on — and this time, whatever it cost, she would not be standing with her face still.
