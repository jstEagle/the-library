## 16. Sisters in the Dark

The knock came at the third hour past midnight, and it came in a rhythm Freya knew better than her own heartbeat.

Two quick. One slow. Two quick.

She had taught Sera Voss that knock twenty years ago, on the walls of a besieged town whose name Freya had deliberately forgotten, when they'd been two spears in the same shield-line and had agreed that if either of them ever came to the other's door in the dark hours, it meant *trust me and ask nothing*. It was a soldier's code. It had no business surviving what came after.

Freya stood very still in her workshop, a half-finished warding charm dangling from her fingers, its thread of warm light pooling on the floorboards like spilled honey. She counted her exits out of habit — window, cellar hatch, the loose stone behind the coal bin that opened onto a smuggler's run she'd never actually used because it smelled like old fish — and then, with a sigh at her own stupidity, walked to the door and opened it.

Sera stood in the stairwell with a hood up, a sword at her hip, and an expression like a woman who had swallowed something bitter and hadn't yet decided whether to spit or swallow again.

"You look terrible," Freya said.

"You always did know how to make a homecoming warm."

"That wasn't a homecoming. Homecomings involve wine." Freya stepped back. "I've got nettle tea."

"I've been threatened by better offers."

"And yet here you are." Freya shut the door behind her, threw the bolt, and turned to study the woman in her lamplight. Sera had aged hard since Tanner's Row — not in years; in weight. There were new lines around her mouth, the kind carved by things said and unsaid, and her left hand kept moving toward her sword hilt the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. The fight on Tanner's Row sat between them still, unhealed. Freya's ribs remembered it. So, judging by the way Sera favored her right side, did Sera's.

"Nettle tea," Sera said, "then."

* * *

They drank it at the little table by the window, because neither of them could have told you why sitting across from each other made honesty easier, but it always had. Even in the company days, confessions had come out over camp cups, sideways, eyes on the fire instead of the sinner.

Sera set a folded paper on the table but didn't push it across. Not yet.

"I burned my report," she said. "The founding archives. Everything. I read it all, and then I fed it to the brazier in the Warden-house and watched it go, and I'd do it again."

"I know. Bram told me. He also told me you cried doing it, which I intend to hold over you for the rest of your natural life."

"He swore he wouldn't—"

"Bram swears lots of things. Mostly about his eye. He claims he lost it heroically. I've heard four different stories and my favorite is the one involving the bear and the wedding."

That earned her half a breath of laughter before Sera strangled it. Progress, Freya thought. On Tanner's Row there had been no laughter at all. There had been steel, and Freya catching Sera's wrist mid-thrust, and pressing the heartstone into her palm so the god inside it could shout truth straight down her arm. Freya would carry the memory of Sera's face in that moment for the rest of her life: the exact instant a lifetime of certainty cracked down the middle like river ice in spring.

"The Magistrate has forty-one prisoners," Sera said now. Her voice had gone flat, the way it went flat when feeling would otherwise drown it. "Hedge-witches. Shrine-keepers. A chandler who blessed his own candles. Two children who sang the old harvest hymns in the yard of the Lambing Street orphanage." She looked down at her tea. "He means to feed them to the Falls. Three nights hence. He calls it a Rite of Restoration. The paperwork says their deaths will 'renew civic sanctity.'"

"The paperwork," Freya repeated, "says that."

"The paperwork says a great many things I used to believe."

Freya reached across the table and took the folded paper. Sera let her. It was the execution roster — forty-one names in the Magistrate's neat clerk-hand, and beside them, times, positions, the order in which they'd be marched to the water. At the top, in Sera's own writing, a single line: *Warden deployment, full strength. Escape routes sealed.*

"Are those orders," Freya asked quietly, "or are they your plan for stopping anyone who tries?"

"They're orders I intend to follow badly."

Freya put the paper down and pressed both hands flat against the table, because if she didn't give them work they'd shake. Three nights. Forty-one names. She thought of Maren the hedge-witch — alive, then, dragged toward the Falls weeks ago and held instead of drowned, saved by some bureaucratic accident of scheduling, waiting in a cell for the water. Freya had done nothing that day in the market. She had watched, and hated herself, and gone home. Everyone in Corvale who had ever done nothing was about to find out what that silence cost.

"Why?" she asked. "Why come to me? You could burn this whole quarter down around my ears and be a hero. You could bury everything you read in the archives under my corpse. Nobody would ever know about the seal, the founders, any of it. That's still the smart play, Sera. Don't tell me you haven't thought of it."

"I've thought of it every night since Tanner's Row," Sera said. "Usually around the fourth watch, when the thinking gets ugly." She lifted her eyes. "But you showed me what was in the stone, Frey. I felt him. Your god shouted through my own hand, and he sounded—" Her voice caught, just once. "He sounded like the fires we used to sit beside. And I have spent fifteen years helping the people who did that to him. Who did that to *you*." She pushed back from the table abruptly and went to stand at the window, arms crossed, watching the empty street below as if arrest might be contagious. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I want to be clear about that. I don't get to ask that yet."

"Sera—"

"I'm asking for work. Give me the work and I'll do it until I've paid enough to deserve asking."

Freya looked at her — this proud, stubborn, law-bent fool standing rigid in her tiny workshop among drying bundles of rue and jars of tincture — and felt fifteen years of grief shift its weight, the way a wound shifts when the bandage finally comes off. It didn't feel good. It felt like air on raw skin. But it was air.

"All right," Freya said. "Sit down, Captain. We've got three nights and a stupid amount of work, and you're going to drink that tea while it's hot for once in your life."

* * *

The plan assembled itself in pieces, the way plans do — Bram's smuggler routes drawn in charcoal on the tabletop, the timing of the prisoner wagons, which Wardens could be trusted to be suddenly, catastrophically nearsighted at the crucial moment. Sera knew the deployment rotations by heart. Freya knew the undercity's bones, every shrine-cellar and chapel-loft where the faithful had been hiding since the raids began. Between the two of them they built a thing that might, with luck and the favor of at least one recently woken god, move forty-one people out of Corvale ahead of the water.

Somewhere past the second hour, Sera rubbed her eyes and said, "You've got rosemary in your hair."

"It's a charm ingredient. It's also possibly my entire hairstyle at this point. The gods woke up, Sera, not my standards of grooming."

"Fifteen years and you're still a disgrace."

"Fifteen years and you're still bossy."

"Fifteen years," Sera said, and the word landed differently the second time, heavier, and the humor drained out of the room like water through sand.

Silence. Freya turned her charcoal stick over in her fingers.

"We were oath-sisters," she said at last. "Properly sworn, at Vaskar's shrine outside Hallowmere, before the war took the shrine and the war took everything else. Do you remember the words?"

"I remember yours. You swore so fast the priest couldn't keep up. You'd been practicing."

"I had *not*—"

"You absolutely had. You wanted it so badly you glowed. I remember thinking, there's a woman who's going to die for me someday, and hoping I'd be worth it." Sera's mouth twisted. "And then the company broke, and I took the Warden's white, and I told myself oaths to gods were just words, and oaths to sisters were just words, and only oaths to law were real. Because the law fed me. The law gave me a roof and a rank and a reason to get up in the morning after—" She stopped. After the company broke. After whatever it was that had split them apart, the thing they had never once, in all these years, spoken of directly. "I chose the easy oath. The one that asked nothing back."

"The law asked plenty back," Freya said. "You gave it your whole heart."

"Yes. Well." Sera stared into her cup. "It turns out I gave it to liars."

Freya came around the table. She stood in front of Sera, and Sera looked up at her with an expression Freya had seen exactly twice before: once on a wall under arrow-fire, once at a graveside. Waiting to be judged by someone whose judgment mattered.

"There's a version of tonight," Freya said slowly, "where I tell you to go to the deep dark, and mean it. Fifteen years, Sera. Fifteen years I prayed into silence and called it faith, while you were out there lighting the pyres of people who prayed like me. I've rehearsed this argument. I've had it with you a hundred times in my head, and in every version you lose."

"In every version I should." No flinch. No defense. Just the flat, exhausted readiness of a woman who had already court-martialed herself thoroughly.

"But," Freya said, and knelt.

Sera went rigid. "*Frey*—"

"Shut up and listen. This is the part I practiced too." Freya took a breath, and found, to her enormous surprise, that the old words were right there, worn smooth by disuse but whole. "By bread and by blade, by hearth and by road. My hand for your hand, my wall for your wall, until the last ford is crossed and the last fire burns low. In Vaskar's sight, and in spite of him if need be." She looked up. "That last part's new. I added it tonight. I think he'd approve. I think he'd insist."

"Freya, you can't swear that to me. Not after what I—"

"I can, and I am, and here's why." Freya took Sera's scarred right hand in both of hers, the same hand that had held a sword to her heart on Tanner's Row. "Because the old oath was habit. We were young and we swore because that's what you did at shrines with your best friend, the way you drank together and complained about rations together. It cost nothing. This one costs everything. This one I'm swearing with my eyes open, knowing exactly what you did and what you are and what you might yet have to choose against me. That's the difference, Sera. That's the whole difference. An oath isn't true until it knows what it can be broken by."

Sera's jaw worked. For a long moment she said nothing at all, and Freya knelt on the cold floor of her own workshop and waited, and understood that she was witnessing the hardest thing she had ever watched another person do: accept mercy without arguing herself out of it.

Then Sera slid off the chair onto her knees, so they were level, eye to eye, and gripped Freya's hands hard enough to hurt, which was correct.

"By bread and by blade," Sera said, and her voice cracked on the second word and recovered by the fourth, "by hearth and by road. My hand for your hand. My wall for your wall. Until the last ford is crossed." She swallowed. "And I'll add my own line, since we're amending scripture tonight. My badge for your name. Whatever it costs."

"In Vaskar's sight," Freya finished.

"And in spite of him if need be."

The heartstone on the shelf above the workbench — Freya saw it clearly even though neither of them touched it — pulsed once, slow and warm, like a banked fire accepting new wood. Whether that was Vaskar's blessing or merely his attention, she couldn't say. She suspected, given everything, that it was attention. His voice would come later, faint and strained, when the seal let him speak. But he had heard. She was sure of it the way you're sure of weather coming.

They knelt there a moment longer, two gray-haired soldiers on a workshop floor smelling of rosemary and nettle tea, holding on to each other's hands like the last two planks of a wreck.

"This doesn't fix anything," Sera said into her shoulder, muffled.

"No."

"I'm still going to spend years earning this."

"Years," Freya agreed. "Decades, if I manage the ledger properly. I keep excellent accounts."

"You would." Sera pulled back, scrubbed her face with both hands, and became again the Captain of Wardens — spine straight, eyes dry, grief folded away somewhere it could be carried. "All right. The roster. Forty-one names, three nights, and the Magistrate wants the crowd penned in by dawn watch, which means his own guard will be stretched thin along Procession Street. If we're going to steal his executions out from under him, we start with the wagons."

Freya climbed to her feet, joints protesting, and reached for the charcoal.

"One more thing first," she said. "The tea. Drink it. I didn't stew a whole pot of nettles to watch it go cold out of Warden stubbornness."

Sera looked at the cup. Looked at her. And drank it — grimacing, obedient, exactly like the old days — while outside the window the city slept on, unaware that by the third dawn it would be burning or reborn, and quite possibly both.

"Now then," Freya said, laying the roster flat between them and pulling the map of the undercity close. "Let's talk about how we rob a Magistrate."

Above the workbench, quiet as a held breath, the heartstone pulsed again — twice, urgent — and far beneath the city, beneath the Falls, something vast shifted in its sleep and turned its ear toward the sound of two sisters plotting in the dark.
