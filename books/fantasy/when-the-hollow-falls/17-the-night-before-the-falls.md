## 17. The Night Before the Falls

### Freya

The safehouse behind the tannery smelled of wet wool, lamp oil, and Bram's pipe, which he had been forbidden to light and was therefore smoking anyway, holding the unlit bowl in his mouth on principle.

"Again," Sera said. "From the top."

Freya dragged her charcoal across the scrap of leather they were using for a map. "The prisoners go into the Falls yard at moonrise. Six Wardens at the gate, four on the wall, two on the scaffold. The Magistrate wants an audience, which means crowds, which means our people can stand close enough to touch."

"Close enough to get trampled," Sera said.

"Close enough to *help*," Freya said, and drew a line from the tanner's row to the drainage culvert that ran beneath the Falls yard. It was old. Older than Corvale's walls, older than the Wardens, older than the lie the whole city stood on. It stank of everything the city wished to forget, which made it, in Freya's long experience, the most honest road in Corvale.

Sera leaned over the map, and her braid slipped forward, and for a moment Freya saw not the Captain of the Wardens but the girl who used to sleep sitting up on watch because she didn't trust anyone else to do it right. The girl who'd once eaten a beetle on a dare and then dared Freya back, and Freya had actually done it, because what else do you do at seventeen when someone you love dares you?

"And after the culvert?" Sera asked.

"After the culvert, I go down." She kept her voice level. "To the seal."

"You go down," Sera repeated. "Alone. With a stone and a speech."

"With a stone and an oath," Freya corrected. "It's different. Speeches you can give badly and survive."

Nobody laughed. Even Bram didn't laugh, and Bram laughed at funerals as a matter of professional courtesy. He sat by the window with his one eye fixed on the street below, where the last of the lamplighters worked their way down Tanner's Row.

"The company will hold the gate," he said. "What's left of it."

"What is left of it?" Sera asked.

Bram counted on his fingers with great ceremony. "Me. Doss, who is seventy-one and blind as a mole but can still swing a hammer. The Fenwick twins, who owe me money. And Marta Oakes."

"Marta Oakes is dead," Freya said.

"Marta Oakes's *daughter*," Bram said. "Swears like her mother, hits like her mother, cries about it after like her mother too. Close enough. Seven swords, if you're generous about what counts as a sword."

"That's not a company," Sera said quietly. "That's a tavern argument."

"It was enough once," Bram said. "When we swore the first oath, there were nine of us and half of us were drunk. Gods don't count heads, girl. They count hearts." He knocked his pipe out against the windowsill, unlit, ashless, entirely symbolic. "Though it helps to have at least one sober heart among them, so I'll stay off the ale tonight. Someone has to be embarrassing tomorrow. I'd rather it was me."

* * *

They ate stew out of dented bowls, and it was terrible, and everyone had seconds.

Doss told the story of the siege of Harrow Ford for the fourth time that year, improving it each telling — there were now two giants and a haunted bridge — and nobody corrected him, because tomorrow some of them might die and a man ought to get his legends in order while he could. The Fenwick twins argued over dice and accused each other of cheating with such sincerity that Freya suspected they'd practiced it. Young Oakes sat near the fire sharpening a blade that was already sharp, the way soldiers do when their hands need something to love.

Freya sat apart, as she always did, turning the heartstone over in her palm. It was warm tonight. Warmer than it had ever been. Through the floor, through the foundations, through the bones of the city itself, she could feel it: a slow pulse under the world, like something enormous turning over in its sleep and beginning to dream of waking.

*Tomorrow,* she thought. *One more night.*

She caught Sera watching her across the room. Neither of them said anything. There wasn't anything left to say that hadn't been said with warrants and charms and fifteen years of silence between them. But Sera got up, crossed the room, sat down beside her without asking, and put a second helping of terrible stew in her hands.

"Eat," Sera said. "You'll want your strength for your speech."

"It's an oath, not a speech."

"To a god. With an audience. That's a speech, Frey." A ghost of the old smirk. "I've heard you give toasts. You'll want to rehearse."

And Freya laughed — a short, cracked, unexpected thing — and found that her eyes were burning, and blamed the stew, which was spicy, in the way that water is spicy.

* * *

Late, when the others slept, Sera woke her.

"Come look at this," she whispered, and led her to the window.

Below, in the dark street, a small figure was scratching chalk marks on doorposts. A child — the informant child, the one who'd sold Freya to Sera at Tanner's Row and had been weeping about it ever since, trailing the Wardens' plans to anyone who'd listen as though apology could be paid in installments. She marked six doors in a row, then looked up at the sky, then ran.

"She's counting houses for the Magistrate's men," Sera said. "Marking which families to seize as witnesses. They want the faithful in the crowd tomorrow. All of them. Watching."

"Hostages."

"Witnesses," Sera said. "He genuinely believes he's doing a rite. That's the worst part, Frey. He thinks the gods will thank him." She paused. "I burned my report today. The founding archives, the whole truth. I wrote it out fair copy and I fed it to the stove page by page, and I thought I'd feel free."

"Do you?"

"I feel like a woman standing on a roof in a storm," Sera said. "Everything's clear up here and none of it will hold." She turned, and in the dark her face was young again, terrified again, sworn again. "Tell me the plan works."

"No," Freya said honestly. "Plans never work. We work. The plan just gives us somewhere to be while we do it."

Sera huffed — almost a laugh. "You sound like Bram."

"I've had worse things said about me." Freya squeezed her hand. "Sleep, Captain. Tomorrow we give the Magistrate an audience he won't forget."

### Sera

She did not sleep. She sat on the roof of the tannery instead, in the cold, cleaning a sword that was already clean, and watched the city she had spent ten years defending prepare to murder forty people in the name of justice.

The Wardens' barracks would be lit all night. She knew the rhythm of it now: the quartermaster's carts rolling at midnight, the scaffold crews working by lantern, the chaplain's bell tolling the hours with a cheerfulness that made her teeth ache. Somewhere in that machinery, three hundred men and women who believed themselves good were oiling the gears of a thing that had never been good at all.

*How many of them would walk away,* she wondered, *if I read the archives aloud in the yard?*

Some. Not all. Enough? She didn't know. That was the terror of it — that truth turned out to be a weapon with no guarantee of aim.

Footsteps on the ladder. She didn't reach for her sword; only one person in this building walked like that, heel-first, apologetic even on stairs.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Freya settled beside her, wrapped in a blanket, hair loose and silvering at the temples in a way Sera pretended not to notice because noticing meant admitting time had passed while they weren't speaking.

"I was thinking about the day we met," Sera said. "You punched me."

"You stole my ration."

"You *shared* it with me after."

"Because punching you made me feel guilty." Freya smiled at the rooftops. "That's the whole secret of me, Sera. Guilt and stubbornness, held together with twine. Everything else is charm-work."

"Charm-work that answers prayers now," Sera said. "Half the undercity thinks you're a saint."

"The other half?"

"Thinks you're a demon sent to punish the Wardens. My own sergeant has a wager going."

"Which side is he on?"

"He's hedged. He's put coin on both." Sera shook her head. "Ten years ago that man would have reported a hedge-witch for selling luck-knots. Now he bets on miracles. The city's changing, Frey. Underneath everything, it's already changed. Tomorrow we're just making it official."

Freya was quiet a while. Below them, the child informant finished her rounds and vanished down an alley, and somewhere a night-bird sang its ugly song, and the pulse beneath the earth beat on, patient as tide.

"When it's done," Freya said at last, "if it works — I won't hear him anymore. Vaskar. Or anything. Whatever I am after tomorrow, I'll be deaf to all of it." She said it lightly, the way she said everything that mattered most. "Fifteen years of talking to a silent god, and now I finally get an answer, and the answer costs me the conversation forever. There's a joke in there. Bram would find it."

"Bram laughs at funerals."

"Someone has to. It's how you know they're funerals and not just Tuesdays." Freya pulled the blanket tighter. "Ask me if I'm afraid."

"Are you afraid?"

"Yes," Freya said. "But I've been afraid since I was nineteen years old and some fool girl stole my ration. Fear's just the price of loving things, Sera. I've paid it a long time. One more payment doesn't scare me." She stood, blanket and all, and looked down at the dark streets, at the lit barracks, at the falls roaring faint and constant beyond the walls. "Get some sleep, Captain. Tomorrow I need your sword arm and your terrible, beautiful faith in doing things properly."

"My faith isn't in doing things properly anymore."

"No," Freya agreed. "But you still fold your socks. Some things survive everything."

### Bram

Bram had gathered armies before. Real ones, once — four hundred spears at Greywater, God rest the lot of them. This was better work and worse: seven old soldiers in a smokehouse at dawn, passing around a jug of small beer and a map drawn on leather.

"Now," he said, tapping the culvert with his knife. "We hold the gate here. Not long. Ten minutes, maybe twelve if the twins argue with somebody. Long enough for the captain to open the yard and the Hartwell woman to get below." He looked around at their faces. Doss, ancient and grinning. The twins, identical in their worry. Young Oakes, who had her mother's jaw set exactly the way her mother set it before Harrow Ford. "I won't lie to you. This is a bad fight against good odds for a reason no one can prove. If any man wants to walk, he walks now, and drinks free on me forever, and I'll say nothing but kind words."

Silence. Then Doss said, "Is there beer at the end of it?"

"If we live."

"Then I'm in. Can't walk away from beer."

"I'm in," said twin one.

"In," said twin two, "but I want it noted I said we should have recruited more than seven people."

"Noted," Bram said gravely. "Oakes?"

Marta's daughter looked at the map a long moment. "My mother died believing the company owed a debt," she said. "To a god, to a promise, I never knew which. If this clears the ledger, I'm in. And if I die, someone tell the twins they're both cheats and it's not worth arguing."

"There," Bram said, throat tight, voice steady. "That's a war council. Nine drunks founded this company and seven mostly-sober fools are going to finish its business. Vaskar keep you—" he stopped himself, old habit, dead habit, and corrected it with something new. "*Whoever's listening*, keep you. And if no one's listening, then keep each other. That was always the real oath anyway. The god part was just the paperwork."

He poured the small beer into seven cups, and they drank standing, like soldiers, like ghosts, like the last of something that refused, out of sheer stubbornness, to be the last.

Outside, the sun came up over Corvale, gilding the smoke, the scaffolds, the waiting Falls. The hour of the rite was coming. The Magistrate's bells began to ring.

Bram picked up his hammer, the one he'd carried for fifty years, and smiled his funeral smile.

"Well," he said. "Let's not keep the gods waiting. They've waited long enough."
