## 18. The Rite at the Falls

The plan had exactly four moving parts, which Bram had said was three too many for anything involving soldiers, priests, or Freya Hartwell.

Part one: Sera falsified the prisoner transfer route so the condemned would cross the old tanner's bridge instead of the Processional. Part two: Bram and the seven ragged survivors of the Grey Company — a mercenary band that now consisted of one man with one eye, a cook, two brothers who'd never stopped feuding, and an archer who was mostly knees — would take the bridge. Part three: the undercity faithful would flood the Falls basin in numbers too great for the Wardens to hold. And part four, the part Freya had insisted on because nobody else could do it: she would carry the heartstone down to the seal and stand between the Magistrate's rite and the thing underneath.

Four parts. Simple. Clean.

It fell apart before moonrise.

* * *

Freya crouched behind a dye-merchant's shuttered stall at the lip of the Falls basin, the heartstone hot against her sternum where it hung on its cord, and watched the plan die in the order it was born.

First the bridge. There should have been an hour of darkness between the prisoners' carts and the rite; instead the carts came early, torches doubled, Wardens riding shoulder to shoulder like a wall of iron. Sera's reroute had been discovered — or betrayed. Freya's stomach did something complicated and painful at the second possibility, because there was only one person besides Sera who'd known the full route, and his name was Bram, and he was currently supposed to be standing on a bridge that now had forty Wardens on it and no quartermaster.

"He wouldn't," she whispered to herself, and then, because lying to herself had never taken: "He'd hate it, but he might. If he thought it would save more of them than it killed."

The cook, Jemma, materialized beside her out of the shadows smelling of onions and fear. "Bridge is blown," she reported. "Bram says fall back to the basin, make do."

"Make do," Freya repeated. The Grey Company's oldest battle plan. It had gotten them through the Marsh War and the Siege of Hallowford and one truly catastrophic wedding in Corvale's high quarter that Freya still refused to discuss.

"And part three?"

Jemma nodded toward the basin. Below, along the crescent of wet stone where the river tore itself over the Falls' edge, people were gathering. Not dozens. Hundreds. Charm-sellers and dock-hands and washerwomen and the beggar child Tam with her crooked crown of braids, all of them creeping out of the undercity like the tide coming in, quiet as prayer. They'd heard what was happening tonight. Word traveled faster than law in Corvale.

"They came anyway," Jemma said.

"Of course they did." Freya's throat ached. "Nobody ever needed a plan to be brave. Only to survive being brave."

Then the drums started.

* * *

The Magistrate had built a platform at the basin's heart, on the flat shelf of rock the fisherfolk called the Altar, close enough to the Falls that spray silvered its railings. Braziers burned on it with a flame that was the wrong color — white at the heart, gold-edged, hungry in a way fire shouldn't be. Freya knew that color now. She'd spent weeks learning everything the old texts and Bram's wine-loosened memory could tell her about the founding of the Wardens, and every account agreed on one detail: the founders fed the seal, and the seal drank.

Twenty-two prisoners were chained kneeling in a row on the platform. Freya found the hedge-witch Maren among them — gray-haired, stubborn Maren, whose stall the Wardens had torched a lifetime ago in chapter terms and a few months in real ones, whose sigils matched Freya's old company mark and had set all of this tumbling. Her face was swollen on one side. She held her chin up anyway. Freya loved her a little for it.

And there, climbing the platform steps in robes of office with the white-gold brazier-light making her shadow enormous against the mist — the Magistrate himself. Corvale had almost forgotten he had a body. He'd governed from the high tower for so long he'd become more rumor than man. Up close he was smaller than Freya expected, and older, with the papery look of someone who had spent decades indoors arguing with books, and eyes that were not confused about anything at all.

He raised his hands. The drums stopped. The crowd of hundreds went silent in a way no crowd of hundreds should.

"People of Corvale," the Magistrate said, and his voice carried impossibly, rolling across the basin without effort, pitched by something that wasn't lungs. "You have been told the old gods wake. You have been told their power can be shared, that prayer can be answered, that the law which kept you safe for two hundred years has grown cruel." He smiled gently, a schoolmaster's smile. "This is true. They wake. But you misunderstand what wakes *with* them."

The heartstone went cold against Freya's chest. Then hot. Then cold again, flickering like a guttering lamp.

"No," she breathed. "No, no, he doesn't know about the twin, he *can't*—"

"What better way to bind a god," the Magistrate continued, warm and reasonable, "than with worship? What better anchor than blood, freely spilled by the faithful? Tonight we renew the covenant our founders made. Tonight your devotion becomes the city's armor. You should be grateful."

"He knows," Jemma said flatly. "The smug old lizard knows exactly what's under there, and he's going to feed it."

Below, a Warden officer read the charges. Freya didn't hear them. She was already moving, shoving through the press of bodies toward the platform, because there are moments when a plan collapses and grief can wait and arithmetic can wait and the only thing that cannot wait is the distance between yourself and the disaster, and every stride closed less of it.

The first prisoner was brought to the block.

It wasn't Maren. It was a young man Freya half-knew, a charm-buyer, a boy who once paid for a fever-luck knot with three buttons and an apology. He was crying, and the crowd was praying — openly now, dozens of voices, then hundreds, a sound like rain beginning — and the Magistrate lifted the sacrificial blade, white-gold fire crawling up its length, and Freya was still twenty rows of terrified people away when it fell.

The boy's blood hit the Altar stone and the world *tilted*.

* * *

Later, Freya would struggle to describe it, because the senses refused to cooperate. It was a sound felt in the teeth. A light smelled through the skin. The whole basin lurched like a ship, though nothing moved — except below, beneath the thunder of the Falls, something vast rolled over in its sleep and began, at last, to wake.

The heartstone screamed.

Not hummed, not warmed, not any of the shy pulses it had offered her these past weeks. It *screamed*, a thin silver shriek only she could hear, and inside it two voices tangled: one deep and steady and wounded, one bottomless and delighted.

*"Blood,"* said the bottomless one, in a voice like a cave discovering it could speak. *"Oh, blood. Oh, they remembered. After two hundred years, they remembered me."*

"Hollow-Vaskar," Freya gasped, clutching the stone through her shirt. "Don't. Don't drink, don't—"

But the second voice, the faint one, the one she'd waited fifteen years to hear — Vaskar, her god, her quiet god — was straining against the first like a man trying to shout through a locked door, and what came through was not command or comfort but her name, torn nearly to nothing:

*"—reya. FREYA. The oath — the seal runs on oaths — theirs is a counterfeit — you must—"*

And the Falls themselves changed.

Anyone who has stood near the Hollow Falls knows their sound: a constant white roar, the city's oldest lullaby. The roar stuttered. Stopped. Started again, wrong, arrhythmic, like a heartbeat skipping. The waterfall's face bulged outward — water flowing uphill in ropes and sheets, dragged back against its own descent — and through the curtain of it Freya saw, for one impossible instant, a shape. Enormous. Shoulder-like. A suggestion of something rising off the riverbed far below with the slow patience of a mountain deciding to be an animal.

The crowd saw it too. Three hundred prayers died mid-word.

Then the screaming started, and it did not stop.

"RUN!" Freya bellowed, at everyone, at no one, shoving people toward the basin stairs. "GET OFF THE STONE! MOVE!" Because she'd read the founding accounts, and Bram had told her the rest, and one fact lived in her bones now like a nail: the seal was a wound, and wounds tear wider under panic and pressure, and there was nothing in the world that packed three hundred panicking human hearts tighter than a god-shaped shadow standing up in a waterfall.

The Wardens broke first. Whatever else you could say about them, they were trained on smugglers and hedge-witches, not on the dead rising out of rivers. Their line shredded. The Magistrate alone stayed on the platform, blade still raised, and Freya watched him watch the chaos with an expression she'd remember the rest of her life: not triumph anymore. Calculation. Recalculation. A bookkeeper discovering the ledger had been lying to him.

The second prisoner was already at the block — Maren, gray and unbowed — and whether it was the Magistrate's desperation or simple momentum or the white-gold fire acting on its own hungriest instincts, the blade came down before anyone could reach her, and Maren's blood joined the boy's on the Altar stone, and the Falls *shook*.

The shape below rose faster.

* * *

Freya reached the platform steps the same moment Sera did, from the other side, in full Warden plate with her helm gone and a cut across her brow painting half her face red. For one heartbeat they stared at each other across the last three steps — sworn sisters, hunted huntress, the whole wrecked history of them — and neither drew a weapon, because weapons had become a joke, a charming folk custom, irrelevant as buttons.

"The prisoners," Sera shouted over the roar. "I can't strike their chains — the locks are warded, the Magistrate keyed them to his own—"

"Then get them OFF THE PLATFORM, Sera!"

"I'm *trying*—"

The third sacrifice was dragged forward, and this time the crowd surged instead of fleeing, faithful and faithless together, washerwomen grabbing at Warden sleeves, Tam the beggar child sinking her small teeth into a soldier's gauntleted hand with a war cry that would have done the Grey Company proud. Bram's voice rose somewhere in the crush — *"FORM UP, YOU BEAUTIFUL DISASTERS, COMPANY OF GHOSTS ON ME"* — and against all sense, against every plan that had already died, seven battered veterans and three hundred citizens pushed toward the platform to save twenty-two strangers from a god's dinner.

Freya took the steps three at a time.

She reached the Altar stone as the Magistrate turned to face her, blade dripping, white-gold fire streaming from it like a banner in wind. Up close his calm had finally cracked; sweat cut channels through the dust on his face.

"Hartwell," he said. "The quartermaster's records were very thorough about you, once I knew to ask."

"Funny. Yours weren't thorough enough." She pulled the heartstone from her collar and held it up between them, and it blazed, silver-white, and the Magistrate flinched from it as if from the sun. "Two hundred years of feeding people to a hole in the world, and you call it covenant. You call it *law*."

"It IS law." His voice shook, and steadied. "It held for two hundred years. What will your truth hold? Look at it, woman. Look what's coming. You'll unmake the seal with revelations and good intentions, and when it tears open — when IT is loose — you will wish—"

The fourth blow never fell, because there was no fourth blow. Instead the river left.

That was how Freya's mind recorded it afterward, in the simple grammar of shock: the river left. The whole thundering weight of it, the water above the Falls and the churning plunge below, heaved upward and outward in a single groaning exhalation, hanging in the air like a curtain drawn aside, and for the first time in living memory the riverbed showed itself — black stone, ancient channels, and a seam of white-gold light running down its length like a scar that had been stitched shut by desperate hands.

And standing in the scar, gripping its edges with hands the size of ferry boats, was the twin.

It looked like Vaskar the way a forest fire looks like a hearth. Same broad lines, same warrior's frame, but hollowed — chest cavernous and empty, face a smooth blank oval where a god's eyes should have been, and yet somehow seeing, somehow finding *her*, the way a needle finds north. When it spoke, every soul in the basin heard it in their own skull, in the voice of their own worst night:

*"SISTER'S CHOSEN. LITTLE OATH. YOU SMELL OF HIM."*

The heartstone burned so hot Freya cried out and nearly dropped it. And through the pain, through the terror, Vaskar's voice broke through — stronger than she'd ever heard it, because fear for her was doing what fifteen years of silence couldn't:

*"FREYA. HEAR ME NOW, AT THE END OF WAITING. THE SEAL IS OATH AND BLOOD. THEIR BLOOD HAS FED THE WRONG PRISONER. IF HE RISES PAST THE SCAR, NO OATH BELOW CAN REACH HIM. YOU MUST MEET HIM AT THE BRINK. YOU MUST BE SWORN, AND WILLING, AND—"*

The twin pulled.

The scar of light stretched. Somewhere in the city behind them, bells began to ring — all of them, every tower, no one ordering it, just the city's whole brass throat screaming at once. Masonry dust sifted down through the suspended water like snow. Jemma was shouting something. Bram was shouting something. Sera had reached the platform and was laying about her with the flat of her sword, clearing prisoners back from the block, and she caught Freya's eye across the chaos and mouthed one word, and it wasn't a plan or a prayer.

It was: *Don't.*

Which was, of course, exactly the wrong thing to say to Freya Hartwell, who had spent fifteen years being told by everyone — by circumstance, by law, by silence itself — what she couldn't do.

The heartstone cooled suddenly in her fist. Settled. Went quiet and clear, the way a sword goes quiet in the hand of someone who has finally stopped pretending they won't use it. And Vaskar said, softly, in the voice that had once told her at dawn that she fought well and meant it:

*"THEN COME, MY FREYA. COME TO THE WATER. I CANNOT HOLD HIM AND SPEAK BOTH. BUT I CAN DO ONE WITH YOU BESIDE ME."*

The twin's blank face tilted downward. The hollow chest opened like a door.

Freya Hartwell ran toward the edge of the Falls.
