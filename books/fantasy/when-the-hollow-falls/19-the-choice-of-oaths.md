## 19. The Choice of Oaths

The world had gone to noise.

Freya Hartwell stood at the lip of the Hollow Falls with blood on her sleeves that was not all her own and a scream in her ears that was not entirely of this world. Behind her, the Magistrate's rite had collapsed into chaos — prisoners scattering, Wardens shouting orders no one obeyed, Bram bellowing something about the east stair in a voice that could have called cattle across three counties. Above it all, rising out of the water like smoke given teeth, was the twin.

Hollow-Vaskar.

He did not look like Vaskar. That was the thing she'd never understood until now, standing close enough to feel the spray of him against her face, cold as grave-water. Vaskar, when he had still spoken to her — when he had been more than fifteen years of silence and a worn heartstone on a cord — had felt like a hearth. Like the moment after a battle when you were allowed to sit down. The twin felt like the moment before one. He was hunger wearing her god's shape, a Vaskar with everything warm burned out of him, and he was *rising*, and the chains the founders had forged were snapping one by one like wet rope.

"Freya!" Sera's voice, somewhere behind her, raw from shouting. "Freya, the binding won't hold — we have to fall back—"

"We can't fall back." She said it quietly, and somehow the quiet carried. It always had, between them. "If he comes up through the Falls, he comes up under the city. Under the chapel district. Under the night market where Maren sells her apples and the children pray at the shrine because nobody ever told them not to."

She stepped forward. One step. The stone at the brink was slick and ancient, carved with the old company marks worn nearly smooth — she could feel them through her boots, faint as scars.

And there, at the edge, with the twin's shadow spreading across the water like an oil fire, Freya Hartwell finally understood.

It came to her the way most truths come: too late, too simply, and obvious in hindsight. The seal required a sworn heart. Not a sacrifice dragged screaming to the water. Not blood spilled by another's hand — the Magistrate's rite had proven that much, feeding the Falls a dozen deaths and waking the thing instead of starving it. A sworn heart, *freely given*. An oath was not a chain. An oath was a door held shut from the inside by someone who chose to hold it.

That was why the founders had needed a company of mercenaries. Not for their swords. For their word.

"Oh," she said aloud, to no one, to the water, to whatever was left of her god listening beneath it. "Oh, you absolute bastards."

* * *

Fifteen years.

Fifteen years she had knelt in rented rooms and alley shrines and once, memorably, a pig shed outside Corvale during the worst winter of her life, and performed the rites of Vaskar the Unbroken into silence. Fifteen years of charm-work powered by nothing but her own stubborn talent and the dregs of what the god had poured into her before he went quiet. Fifteen years of telling herself stories. *He sleeps. He is testing me. He has gone to war somewhere far away and cannot spare a word.* Fifteen years of the particular loneliness of a soldier whose commander has stopped answering dispatches, and who cannot decide whether to desert or die at the post.

And now she knew. He hadn't abandoned her. He had been down here, in the dark, pressed body to body against his own hungry reflection, holding the door shut with the last of his strength, unable to speak because every syllable spent was a plank pried off the seal. His silence had been his loyalty. Her faithfulness had been, all along, the thing keeping the lock oiled.

She laughed. It came out cracked and wet and slightly mad, and she didn't care. Somewhere behind her, Sera was calling her name again, and Bram was roaring, and the twin was rising, and Freya Hartwell stood at the edge of the Hollow Falls laughing like a woman who has just found the receipt for something she paid for twice.

"You could have *written*," she told the water. "A note. A dream. One bloody dream, Vaskar. I'd have taken a dream."

The water shivered. And beneath the twin's roar — faint, threadbare, but unmistakable, in the old battle-cadence she had marched to across half the known world — she heard him answer.

*Freya.*

Not a command. Not comfort. Just her name, in her god's mouth, for the first time in fifteen years, and it undid her more thoroughly than any blade ever had. She went to her knees on the wet stone without deciding to, and pressed her palm flat against the brink, and felt the hum of the seal running up her arm like a heartbeat that wasn't hers.

"Don't," she whispered. "Don't talk. Save your breath, you stubborn old hearth-fire. I know what has to be done. I've always known. I just needed someone to tell me the price, and apparently the price was finding out myself, because everyone in this entire cursed history of ours would rather die than simply *explain things*."

The cadence stuttered. If a god could be flustered, Vaskar was flustered.

"Yes, I'm angry," she said. "I'll be furious later. I've scheduled it. Right now I'm busy."

* * *

The twin noticed her then.

She felt it the way you feel being watched in a dark room — a turning of attention vast enough to have weather in it. The roar changed pitch. The spray went still, hanging in the air like held breath, and when Hollow-Vaskar spoke, his voice came from everywhere, from the water and the stone and the marrow of her bones.

*Little oath-keeper. You smell of my brother. You smell of* want.

"I do," Freya agreed. She stayed kneeling, which was not submission; it was simply easier to talk to enormous ancient horrors from a stable position. Bram had taught her that. "I want plenty. Fifteen years of wanting, banked up. He never gave me answers. You're offering them, I know. I've heard your sales pitch already, down in the dark. Very generous terms. Answers, power, acknowledgment." She tilted her head. "You know what else I want? For Maren's boy Tam to stop coughing through the winter. For the hedge-witches of the undercity to stop burning. For my oath-sister to forgive herself for the ten years she spent enforcing a lie. None of those are in your catalogue, are they?"

Silence. The interested kind. The kind that was measuring her.

*You would give your brother's bond,* the twin said, and it was not quite a question. *Your name in his mouth. Your oath. All of it, into the water.*

"All of it," Freya said. "But hear me, hollow thing, and hear me well, because I will only say this once and I am a mercenary and mercenaries do not repeat themselves for free." She rose to her feet. Her knees ached. Everything ached. She found she didn't mind. "I am not giving my oath to *you*. I am giving it to the *door*. You are not the master of this binding. You are the cargo. The difference matters, and if you don't understand the difference, that is precisely why you're going back in the box."

The roar that answered shook pebbles off the brink. The water reared up like a rearing horse made of night. And Freya Hartwell, charm-seller of Corvale, late of the Grey Company, stood her ground on legs that wanted very badly to run, and began to speak the words.

* * *

They were old words. Older than the Wardens, older than the Magistrate, older than the city itself. The Grey Company had carried them in its bones for four generations, and Bram had drilled them into her when she was nineteen and thought they were just a marching song with pretensions. She had sung them drunk around fires. She had sung them over graves. She had never once imagined she would sing them alone, at the edge of the world, as a weapon.

*"By the hand that holds and the hand that holds fast—"*

The seal woke under her feet. She felt it take her measure — not her blood, not her pain, though she was bleeding and in considerable pain, thank you very much — but her *word*. Oaths have weight. Every promise kept adds a grain. Every vow honored in the dark when no one would know otherwise. Fifteen years of faithful service to a silent god, offered up daily with no proof it was heard, no reward, no acknowledgment — she had thought it was wasted. It turned out it had been accruing interest.

*"—by the bread shared and the blade turned aside—"*

The twin struck at her. Not physically — worse. He reached into her with that vast cold attention and showed her things. Her mother's kitchen, gone these thirty years. The faces of the dead of the Grey Company, young again, whole again, saying her name. Vaskar's voice as it had been before the silence, warm as a hearth, saying *well fought, little one*. Everything she had lost, offered back, if only she would stop. If only she would let go of the words halfway and reach instead.

"That's cheating," she said, voice shaking, and kept speaking. "*"—by the word given in the dark and kept in the light—"*

"And it won't work, because I have spent fifteen years learning the difference between a gift and a bribe, and you, my friend, are a *bribe*."

* * *

Sera reached her then.

She came out of the smoke with her sword drawn and her face streaked with ash and blood, and skidded to a halt at the sight of Freya standing at the very lip of the Falls with her arms raised and the air around her humming like a struck bell.

"Freya." Not Captain Voss. Not Warden. Just *Freya*, the way she'd said it across ten thousand campfires. "Whatever you're doing — the water's rising, the whole brink is going to go, you have to move—"

"I can't move. I'm rather the point of the exercise."

"Then I'm staying."

"No, you're not." Freya looked at her — really looked, taking the extra two seconds the words cost her, because some things you pay for even at the end of the world. Sera Voss, who had held her hair back over sick buckets in three countries. Who had taught her to read a battlefield and cheat at cards. Who had spent ten years being the law's best soldier because she genuinely believed the law was good, and who had burned her own report and opened the founding archives and walked into this catastrophe with her eyes open anyway. "This needs a sworn heart freely given, Sera. One. Mine. If yours gets mixed in, the binding tangles, and tangled bindings are how we got this mess in the first place."

"Freya—"

"Besides." Freya grinned at her, wild and bright, and it was the grin from the old days, the one that preceded either glory or disaster, usually both. "Someone has to survive to tell them what happened. Someone has to make sure the covenant holds. Someone has to look after Bram, gods help whoever that is. You're the only one of us with any administrative talent whatsoever."

Sera made a sound that was half laugh and half sob, and sheathed her sword, and stood beside her anyway, one hand on Freya's shoulder, which was not part of the binding and did not need to be and she was doing it regardless, because that was who Sera was.

*"—by the name I bear and the name I give back—"*

Here it was, then. The last clause. The true price, laid bare at last, and Freya looked down into the churning dark and spoke it plainly, the way you say anything important: simply, and looking right at it.

"Fifteen years I served you, Vaskar the Unbroken. In silence, without sign, without reward. I give you back those years. I give you back my oath. I give you back my name from your mouth — keep it, spend it, let it be the last coin in your purse, and buy the door with it." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "You were a good god to me. Better than I deserved, and quieter than I could bear. Whatever's left of you after this — rest. That's an order. I'm still a sergeant of the Grey Company and you never revoked my rank."

Beneath the roar, faint as a candle seen through a closing door, the battle-cadence sounded one last time. Not words. Just the rhythm. *Well fought, little one. Well fought.*

Freya Hartwell closed her eyes, and gave the last line to the water.

*"—by a heart that chooses: I bind, I hold, I stay."*

* * *

The world went white, then quiet, then very, very heavy.

She felt the oath leave her the way you feel a tooth leave your jaw — a wrongness, a gap, a tongue that keeps returning to the absence. Fifteen years of faith, drawn out of her like thread from a spool, and woven down into the dark, and wrapped around the twin, and knotted. She felt him fight it. She felt the founder-oaths wake to help hers, ghost-hands joining hers on the rope. She felt Vaskar's last strength flow into the knot alongside her own, his warmth going out of the world like a hearth banking down to embers, embers to ash.

And she felt, distinctly, unambiguously, the exact moment the door closed.

The water fell. All at once, like a curtain dropping — the rearing black shape folding down into the Falls, the roar cutting off mid-note, the pressure lifting off the city so suddenly that somewhere behind her a hundred people staggered. The spray came down gentle as rain. The hum in the stone faded to a low, steady pulse. A heartbeat. Held. Contained. *Kept.*

Freya exhaled.

Then her knees informed her, formally and in writing, that they were done, and the brink of the Hollow Falls came up to meet her, and the last thing she saw before the dark took her was Sera catching her — arms around her, ash-streaked and crying and laughing all at once, saying her name over and over.

Her name. In a human mouth. Which was, she thought, drifting down into the deep soft black, exactly as many gods as she needed.

Somewhere above, distantly, Bram was shouting something about needing a stretcher and also whether anyone had thought to bring food, because victories were hungry work.

And somewhere below, behind a door sealed with a sergeant's word, something old and patient settled down to wait — and far above, on the smoking brink, Captain Sera Voss lifted her fallen sister in her arms and turned to face the Magistrate's men, who had just arrived to find their god-food drowned and their law standing in the balance of a woman's two hands.
