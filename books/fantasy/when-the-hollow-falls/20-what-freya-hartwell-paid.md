## 20. What Freya Hartwell Paid

Sera Voss had carried wounded soldiers before. She had carried them out of burning farmhouses and off frozen riverbanks and once, memorably badly, out of a pigsty in Duncarrow that a company mule had decided to defend. She knew the weight of an unconscious body the way she knew the weight of her own sword: intimately, unhappily, and without any illusion that carrying was the same as saving.

Freya Hartwell weighed almost nothing.

That was the first wrong thing. Fifteen years ago, when they'd shared bedrolls on the Marches road, Freya had been all lean muscle and elbows, a woman built like a drawn bow. Now she was lighter than her own armor, which Sera had already stripped off and left lying on the smoking stone like the shed skin of something that had finally finished molting. Freya's head lolled against Sera's shoulder. Her breathing was slow and even and entirely too calm for a woman who had just shouted herself hoarse at two gods and won.

And she was warm. That was the second wrong thing, or perhaps the first, depending on how Sera counted. Freya's skin had always run cold; it was one of the hundred small facts Sera had catalogued during those years and then spent fifteen years pretending she hadn't. Now heat poured off her like a banked forge, like something enormous had passed through her on its way out and taken the door off its hinges behind it.

"Freya." Sera shifted her grip. "Hartwell. If you're faking this to get out of Bram's cooking, I want you to know I respect it completely."

No answer. Of course no answer. But Sera said it anyway, because saying things to unconscious people was a habit from the war, and because the alternative was to look up.

She looked up anyway.

The Magistrate's men were coming down the processional stair in column, maybe sixty of them, halberds gleaming wetly in the light of the fires still eating their way along the Falls district. Behind them, at the top of the stair, the Magistrate's own banner — the scales, the seal, the tidy geometry of a law that had been feeding people to a hole in the world for three generations — snapped in the wind off the water.

Sixty men against a sergeant, a quartermaster with one eye, a handful of freed prisoners who could barely stand, and a captain whose commission was, as of approximately ten minutes ago, a lie she had chosen over the truth she'd been handed.

Sera did the arithmetic the way she always did, quickly and without sentiment.

Then she laid Freya down — gently, on the flat stone where the shrine had been, away from the worst of the smoke — and stood up.

"Voss!" The shout came from the head of the column. Commander Aldous Reeve, the Magistrate's hand, his polished breastplate reflecting fire. He'd been her instructor once, briefly, before she'd learned everything he knew and half of what he didn't. "Step aside. Prisoners are to be secured. The rite is postponed, not cancelled."

"The rite is cancelled," Sera said.

Reeve stopped. Around him, the column stopped, sixty pairs of boots striking stone in ragged unison, and in the sudden quiet Sera could hear the Falls themselves, that vast low breathing beneath the city, quieter now than she had ever heard it. Sealed. Sleeping. Held shut by a woman lying on a shrine-stone with soot on her face.

"You are confused," Reeve said, in the tone of a man offering a colleague a ladder back onto a wall she'd fallen from. "You've had a trying night. Everyone has. Hand over the prisoners and the Hartwell woman, and we will call tonight a misunderstanding."

"Call it whatever you like," Sera said. "But you'll do it from up there."

"Sergeant—" Bram appeared at her elbow, breathing hard, one eye wide. "There's about sixty of them and about nine of us, and two of ours are asleep, one is a child, and I have a crossbow with one bolt, which I am saving for myself if it comes to that."

"Noted," Sera said.

"Good. Just so we're agreed on the tactical picture."

"Completely agreed." She stepped forward, past Bram, out onto the open stone between the fallen and the column, and raised her voice to the register she'd used for fifteen years to make hardened killers stop hitting each other. "Commander Reeve. Before your men take one more step, I'm going to read them something. It'll take four minutes. After that, anyone who wants to arrest me can try. Anyone who wants to go home can go home."

Reeve's jaw tightened. "This is theater."

"It's testimony," Sera said. "Under Warden code, section one, paragraph three. Which I know you know, because you taught me the citation. Any citizen may demand public reading of sealed founding archives where those archives bear on a matter of life and law. You made us memorize it so we'd never be afraid of documents." She smiled, and felt it come out crooked. "I'm not afraid of documents anymore. I've read yours."

* * *

The founding archive of the Wardens was a single leather folio, and Sera had it in her satchel because she had stolen it six hours ago from a locked drawer while her hands shook and her whole understanding of her own life lay in pieces around her on an office floor. She had meant, at the time, only to read it again. To check. The way you touch a bruise.

Now she opened it in front of sixty armed men and the wind tried to tear it out of her hands twice before she got it braced against her forearm.

"'Record of the Founding Covenant,'" she read. Her voice carried better than she expected. Smoke made voices carry; there was probably some proverb in that. "'In the year of the Silence, the undersigned did bind the Hollow One beneath the Falls by the following means.'" She paused. Reeve had started forward. Two of his men hadn't followed him, which was interesting, and one of them had lowered his halberd entirely, which was more interesting.

"Keep reading," said someone from the crowd of freed prisoners, and someone else shushed them, and Sera kept reading.

"'We could not kill him. We could not hold him. So we took his brother.'" A breath. Somewhere in the column, a man made a small sound, quickly swallowed. "'The god Vaskar we took alive, and wounded at the root of his voice, and sealed alongside his twin, for a bound god may not speak and a silent god may not be prayed to, and a god no one prays to is a god the city need not fear. The silence of the gods is our work. Let every Warden after us keep it.'"

She let the words sit. Wind off the Falls. Fire crackling somewhere above. Sixty men not moving.

"Fifteen years ago," Sera said, and now she wasn't reading, she was just talking, and her voice cracked on the number and she let it, "the prayers stopped being answered. Every priest, every charm-seller, every child at a wayside shrine. And the Wardens told you it was because the old gods were dead or false or never real at all. Because magic was a crime, and praying was magic, and the people who grieved were criminals, and the Falls were hungry, and someone had to feed them." She looked up, straight down the length of the column. "Your founders starved a god and called it peace. They chained his voice shut and called it law. And every execution at these Falls for three generations wasn't justice. It was dinner."

"That's enough." Reeve's voice had gone thin. "Men, secure the—"

"Commander." It was the Warden who had lowered his halberd. Young. Sera didn't know his name, which would have bothered her a week ago and didn't now. "With respect, sir. My mother prayed. Every night, my whole childhood. At the little shrine on Cooper's Lane, before you lot burned it." His voice didn't shake. That was the frightening thing about it. "Who exactly was she committing a crime against?"

Reeve turned to answer him and found, visibly, that he had no instrument in his kit for answering that question. The law had never needed one. The law had never imagined being asked.

"Read the rest," said Bram loudly, from the shrine-stone, in the tone of a quartermaster who has audited worse ledgers than this one. "There's signatures at the back. Very tidy handwriting. Four of the signatories' family names are currently on street signs."

* * *

It didn't end cleanly. Sera had fought enough battles to know that nothing ended cleanly; endings were just the part of the fight where everyone agreed to stop pretending it was still going. Three of Reeve's men charged anyway, out of loyalty or terror or the simple momentum of a life spent obeying, and Sergeant Okonwe — awake after all, bleeding from the scalp, leaning on a freed prisoner for support — broke the charge with the flat of his blade and a great deal of shouting, and nobody died, which Sera counted afterward as the single miracle of the night she intended to remember.

The rest of the column wavered. Then the young one — Cooper's Lane, mother, shrine — planted his halberd butt-down on the stone like a man planting a flag, and said, "I witness the reading," in a clear voice, and the words were Warden formula, oath-formula, the oldest sentence in their code, and forty other men heard it and understood what it cost and what it started.

One by one, then in twos and threes, halberds came down. Not surrendering. Witnessing.

Reeve stood alone at the head of his dissolving command for a long moment, a general discovering mid-parade that the parade had become a crowd. Then he looked at Sera, and whatever he saw in her face made him turn and climb the stairs alone, which she supposed was its own kind of confession. The law, walking away from itself.

Sera exhaled for what felt like the first time since sundown.

"Captain," Bram said beside her.

"Don't." 

"Begging your pardon, but—"

"Don't call me Captain. I resigned. Or mutinied. Whichever sounds better at trial."

"Mutinied," Bram said promptly. "Trials love a good mutiny. And for the record, I never doubted you for a moment."

"You told me an hour ago I was 'about to do something stupid and historical.'"

"I said both those words with great affection."

* * *

They made camp of a kind on the shrine terrace, because nobody was going anywhere — the stair was blocked with abandoned halberds, the upper city was burning in the polite, contained way cities burn when their law has just died of embarrassment, and moving Freya seemed, to all of them, unspeakable. Okonwe organized the freed prisoners into watch rotations with the serene efficiency of a man who had waited twenty years for someone to put him in charge of something. The young Warden — his name was Tam Vess, and he gave it shyly, like handing over contraband — sat apart with his helmet off, staring at his hands.

Sera knelt beside Freya.

She was still breathing. Still warm. But when Sera took her hand, Freya's fingers didn't close around hers, and Sera realized she had been waiting for that without knowing it — the grip, the squeeze, the mercenary's shorthand for *still here*. Nothing. The hand lay in hers like something set down rather than something resting.

"She paid," said a voice, low, from the shrine-stone itself.

Sera's knife was out before she'd decided to draw it. The stone was just stone. But the air above it moved the way air moves over deep water, and the voice came again, faint as a hearth heard through a wall, worn thin as a coin rubbed fifteen years between worried fingers.

*Fifteen years I lay beside my twin with his hunger in my ears and my voice nailed shut,* it said. *Fifteen years I watched her pray to me and could not say: I am here, I am here, I am not gone, I am only caged. Do you know what that is, Warden? To hear your faithful call and to be made of silence?*

"No," Sera said honestly. "But I think she does now."

A pause. Something in the air might have been laughter, or grief, or the two of them sharing a body the way twins share everything.

*She gave me the only thing I had left to give her. Her oath. Her name in my mouth. The bond between us — fifteen years of it, unanswered, unbroken, stubborn as a badger. She poured it into the binding and shut the door with it.* The voice frayed. *She will not hear me again. Not me, not any god. She has traded every voice but the human ones.*

"Is she—"

*Alive. Whole. Herself.* Firmly, now, like a god setting down a foot. *Poorer by one god and richer by everything else. See she knows it was not a theft. It was a purchase. She chose the price with her eyes open, and I have been silent for fifteen years, and I will spend eternity being loud about this one fact if I must: she chose.*

The presence thinned. Went. The Falls breathed beneath the city, deep and sealed and, for the first time in living memory, merely water.

Sera sat back on her heels. Her eyes were wet, which was unacceptable, and she wiped them with the heel of her hand, which solved nothing.

"Was that—" Tam Vess had crept close, pale. "Did the stone just—"

"Go get some sleep, Vess."

"Yes, Captain."

"*Don't*—"

"Sorry. Habit."

* * *

Near dawn, the fires in the upper city burned down to embers and the sky went the color of pewter, and Freya Hartwell woke up.

Sera knew it before her eyes opened, the way you know rain coming — some shift in the quality of the stillness. Then grey eyes blinked open, unfocused, found the sky, found the smoke, found Sera, and narrowed.

"How much," Freya rasped, "of that did you all see."

"All of it," Bram said cheerfully, from somewhere nearby. "Very dramatic. There was kneeling. Okonwe knelt, don't let him deny it."

"There was no kneeling," Okonwe said, from further away, having clearly been kneeling.

Freya tried to sit up. Failed. Tried again, with Sera's arm behind her shoulders helping, and managed a sort of seated sprawl against the shrine-stone, and stared out at the Falls with an expression Sera couldn't read — until she could, and wished she couldn't. It was the expression of a woman listening to a room she knew should have music in it, and hearing only the room.

"Oh," Freya said quietly.

"Freya—"

"It's quiet." Not bitter. Worse than bitter: factual. A report from the field. "All of it. Even the—" Her hand drifted toward the stone, toward the deep water below, and stopped halfway, hovering, finding nothing to hover over. "Everything's just... weather now. Water's water. Stones are stones." She laughed, one short breath with no humor in it, and pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth. "Fifteen years I'd have given anything for five minutes of quiet. Careful what you pray for. That's going on my next charm."

"Freya." Sera took her hand — the human gesture, the grip, the squeeze — and Freya squeezed back, hard, reflexively, and Sera held on. "He talked to me. While you were under. The stone. Him. Whatever." She stumbled through it, all of it: the wound at the root of the voice, the cage, the fifteen years of watching and silence, the last thing the god had said. "*She chose,*" Sera finished. "He wanted you to know it wasn't a theft. He said he'd spend eternity being loud about it."

Freya was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone rough. "That's the most words that man has ever said in a row. Typical. Waits until I'm unconscious."

"He also said you're poorer by one god and richer by everything else."

"Mm." Freya leaned her head back against the stone and closed her eyes, and Sera watched her take inventory — hands, breath, the gray morning, the smoke, the sister holding her hand — and watched her arrive, visibly, at the sum. "Bram."

"Here."

"Do we still have the company fund?"

"Technically it's in a wall in Tanner's Row. Technically."

"And the roster?"

"Burned," Sera said. "By me. Months ago. Mostly. Your name wouldn't come off."

Freya opened one eye. "Sentimental."

"Administrative error."

"Mm-hm." Freya looked out at the Falls, at the stair littered with grounded halberds, at the prisoners and deserters and one very tired sergeant all camped together on holy ground that was, as of last night, no longer a feeding trough. Somewhere up there, the Magistrate was learning what his law had been built on, and somewhere beyond him the whole city would learn it by noon, because forty witnesses with grounded weapons was not a secret that kept. "So," she said. "What happens now? Nobody's ever done this before. No law for it. No god running it. Just..." She gestured vaguely at all of it, the ash and the dawn and the people.

"Just us," Sera said.

"Gods help us."

"No," Freya said, and there was something new in her voice, something unbowed, almost amused, the voice of a woman opening shop on the first morning of a world with different rules. "As of about four hours ago — I really wouldn't count on it."

Above them, on the high stair, small against the pewter sky, a runner appeared — Magistrate's colors, panting, waving a sealed scroll — and stopped dead at the sight of the terrace full of witnesses, and stood there, caught between two worlds, deciding.

Bram reached for his crossbow. Sera put a hand on his arm.

"Let him deliver it," she said. "I want to see what they think we are now."

The runner swallowed and began, carefully, to come down the stairs.
