## 21. A City Without Warden Law

The Magistrate's fall was less dramatic than his rise had been, which Sera Voss thought said something damning about the kind of man he'd been.

She had expected — she had *braced* for — barricades, loyalist companies making a last stand on the Magistrate's Stair, the sort of blood-soaked afternoon that history books would later pretend was inevitable. Instead, three days after the binding at the Falls, a delegation of guildmasters and grain-merchants and two extremely nervous priests of no particular god climbed the Stair together and informed Lord Aldous Corvel that the city would prefer to be governed by someone who had not recently attempted a mass human sacrifice in the name of public safety.

He had protested. Of course he had. He had used the word *order* eleven times by Sera's count, and the word *chaos* seven, and at some point he had begun crying, which nobody had expected, least of all him.

Then they'd walked him down his own stair and put him in a boat.

"Where are we sending him?" Sera had asked one of the guildmasters, a barrel-chested woman named Odile who smelled permanently of tallow.

"The northern lakes," Odile said. "There's an abbey. Very quiet. Very far from anything flammable."

"And if he comes back?"

"He won't." Odile's smile was thin as ledger-paper. "The abbot owes me money."

And that was it. That was the whole revolution. Sera stood on the Magistrate's Stair afterward, in the drizzle, watching the boat dwindle on the grey water, and thought: *fifteen years of burnings, and it ends with a debt called in.*

Maybe that was how most evil ended, actually. Not with swords. With paperwork.

* * *

The trouble with toppling a magistrate was that somebody had to govern after, and the city, having spent three days in giddy, terrified liberty — shops shuttered, then unshuttered, then shuttered again out of habit — began casting about for someone with a uniform.

They found Sera.

"You're the captain who read the truth aloud," people said. She hadn't done it alone; there'd been a clerk named Pell with a voice like a cracked bell, and four freed prisoners willing to stand in front of armed Wardens, and Freya Hartwell unconscious in her arms while Hollow-Vaskar's roar still rattled the shutters of every house in Corvale. But stories simplify. Stories wanted a captain.

By the fifth day, a crowd had gathered outside Warden House — not an angry mob, worse: a *hopeful* mob, which was far harder to disperse. They held up petitions. They held up children. One man held up a goat, which Sera chose not to investigate.

Captain-General Halloran, second chair of the Warden bench, met her in the map room where she'd once plotted raids on chapels, and offered her the captaincy with all the warmth of a man handing over a live snake because it had bitten him first.

"The bench has voted," he said. "You have the confidence of the rank and file. The merchants trust you. Even the undercity trusts you, gods help us. Take the white sash, Captain Voss. Keep the peace. That's all anyone is asking."

"That's not all you're asking," Sera said. "You're asking me to keep the law as it stands."

"The law kept this city standing for two hundred years."

"The law burned a hedge-witch named Maren Ostler for selling cures to pregnant women." She watched him flinch. Yes. He knew the name. He'd signed the writ. "The law fed prisoners into a waterfall to keep a stolen god chained. The law built its house on a lie, and the house fell, and you want me to sweep up the rubble and call it a foundation."

"What would you do instead?" Halloran spread his hands. "Abolish justice?"

"No," Sera said slowly, and heard herself say the thing she had been turning over in the dark hours since the Falls, since she'd sat on wet stone with Freya's head against her shoulder and felt the moment the last god went quiet in the world like a lamp guttering out. "I'd rebuild it from the oath up. And I wouldn't wear your sash while I did it."

* * *

Refusing the captaincy took longer than accepting it would have, because everyone in Corvale assumed refusal was a negotiating tactic. It took Odile three visits, a letter from the guilds, and one memorable afternoon in which Sera physically removed the white sash from her own shoulders and set it on the table between them before the message landed.

"You understand," Odile said, "that if you fail, there will be nothing above you. No bench. No writ. Just you and whatever you decide justice looks like."

"Yes."

"And when you get it wrong — and you will, girl, everyone does —"

"Then I'll be wrong where everyone can see me," Sera said. "That's the point."

Odile studied her for a long moment, tallow-scented and shrewd. "You know what you sound like? You sound like a woman describing a court that doesn't exist yet."

"I know," Sera said. "That's the part that frightens me."

* * *

They built it in an old granary.

Sera had wanted somewhere that wasn't Warden House — couldn't stomach the idea of holding judgment in a building whose cellars still smelled of the ash chute — and Bram, of all people, had solved it. The old quartermaster had turned up at her door a week after the Falls, one eye and both eyebrows raised, and announced that the company's pension fund had quietly purchased a granary off Tanner's Row thirty years ago as a smuggling front and that it was high time the asset did something honest.

"You're telling me my new courthouse is laundered money," Sera said.

"I'm telling you it's *clean* laundered money," Bram said. "There's a distinction. Ask any quartermaster."

So the Covenant Court of Corvale opened on a raw spring morning in a building that still smelled faintly of barley, with benches scavenged from three demolished chapels, a floor Sera had scrubbed herself because no one else could be trusted to reach the corners, and no sash, no seal, and no gallows.

That last detail had caused the most argument.

"No gallows," Sera repeated to the assembled council — Odile; Pell the clerk, who had volunteered so fast he tripped over his own chair; Mother Anthea of the river-shrines; a tanner named Joss Kell who represented the undercity and distrusted everything including chairs. "We don't kill. Not for theft, not for assault, not even for murder. Restitution, labor, exile, or confinement. If a person cannot be made safe among us, we make them safe *from* us. That's the covenant."

"And what stops a murderer from doing it twice?" Kell asked.

"Locks," Sera said. "Walls. The same things that stopped them before, minus the theater. Kell, I've stood at the Falls. I've watched men die for praying wrong. I am never again going to confuse killing with justice just because killing is faster."

Mother Anthea, small and wrinkled and older than the city walls, tapped her cane once — her signal, Sera had learned, that she agreed but wished to be dramatic about it. "And oaths?" she asked. "The old courts swore on the Magistrate's seal. Your new court swears on what?"

Sera had thought about this for weeks. She looked at the center of the room, where they'd left the floor bare, no altar, no shrine-stone, nothing.

"On each other," she said. "Every witness swears on whatever they hold sacred — their god, their dead, their own name. And the court swears back. Both directions. That's what makes it a covenant instead of a commandment."

"Hm," said Mother Anthea. "Ambitious. Foolish. Good." She settled back, satisfied, which Sera had learned was her highest category of praise.

* * *

The first case was, of course, a disaster.

A cooper named Willet had struck a neighbor in a dispute over a drainage ditch, breaking the man's nose, and both parties arrived drunk, related to half the audience, and convinced of their own martyrdom. Pell read the charge in his cracked-bell voice. The audience jeered. The neighbor's mother heckled Willet's mother, who heckled back with impressive stamina.

Sera sat in the plain wooden chair at the front and thought: *this is it. This is justice. A smelly room full of shouting cousins.*

Somehow, it was the thought of Freya that steadied her. Freya, who would have found all of this hilarious. Freya, who was three streets away selling charms in the night market with steady hands and a silence inside her that Sera could not look at directly without something in her chest tearing.

She got the cousins quiet. She heard the ditch grievance — genuinely ancient, going back apparently to a fence Willet's grandfather had moved — and she ruled: Willet would repair the drainage properly, at his own cost, and work the neighbor's cooperage through the busy season, and the nose was paid for in coin, and both men would shake hands or spend a night in the lockup to think about it.

They shook hands. Badly. Like two dogs agreeing a truce. But they shook them, and the audience — gods help her — *applauded*, and Pell leaned over and whispered, "Captain— sorry. What do I call you now?"

"Voss," Sera said. "Just Voss."

"That's going to be confusing. Half the city is named Voss. Well. Named *after* you. There's a baby on Cordwainer Street—"

"Pell."

"There's also a tavern sign," Pell said. "It's not flattering, but it's affectionate."

* * *

The freed prisoners came on the third day.

Not to bring cases. To sit. They filled the back benches in their patched clothes — the ones who'd survived the execution roster, hauled off the Magistrate's carts at the Falls while the twin's roar split the sky. They didn't speak. They just sat, every session, at first like mourners and then, gradually, like witnesses, which Sera understood was exactly what she'd asked them to be when she'd stood in front of the Warden line and read the founding archives aloud: *the truth, the whole rotten truth, sworn before the city.*

Today, one of them approached the bare patch of floor after session. A woman with burn scars up both arms, a chapel-keeper from the undercity named Dagny whom Sera had once arrested herself, two years ago, and handed toward the Falls.

"Keeper Voss," Dagny said.

"Just Voss."

"Mm." Dagny looked around the granary, at the chapel benches, at the bare floor. "You know what's strange? This is where I always imagined dying. Not here specifically. Somewhere like it. Some cold room where men in good coats decided I was fuel." Her eyes were level, not accusing — past it, maybe, or choosing to be. "And now you want me to help judge."

"I want you to watch," Sera said. "Judging comes later, if you ever want it. Watching comes first. Somebody has to remember what it looked like from the other side of the chair."

Dagny considered this. Then she sat, not at the back, but in the second row, close enough that Sera could hear her breathing through the next case, a contract dispute between a fishmonger and a very litigious widow. Close enough to see.

It was, Sera thought, the closest thing to absolution anyone had ever offered her, and it wasn't absolution at all. It was better. It was *attendance*.

* * *

Bram found her at dusk, after the benches emptied, sweeping. He claimed a chair, put his boots up on a bench, and produced from somewhere in his coat a bottle of plum spirit that Sera strongly suspected had been company property since before the war.

"To the new regime," he said, pouring two tin cups. "May it outlive the plum brandy."

"Low bar."

"Best kind of bar." He drank, squinting at her with his one eye. "You're waiting for it to fail."

"Every day."

"Good," Bram said. "Only fools build things sure they'll stand. The rest of us build things and *check the joints*." He turned the cup in his scarred fingers. "Company used to run like this, you know. Before your time. Any man could call a muster-court. Any man could be judged by it. Old Harrow himself stood trial once — stole a pig, would you believe, when we were starving outside Kelver's Ford. Paid it back in labor. Never lived it down."

"You never told me that."

"Never asked." He set down the cup. "Point is, girl, you think you invented this. You didn't. You *remembered* it. There's a difference, and the difference is that remembered things have already survived being lost once. Makes them hard to kill."

Sera let that settle. Outside, Tanner's Row was lighting its lamps, and somewhere beyond the rooftops the Falls muttered to itself, bound and quiet, and would be forever, because Freya Hartwell had bought forever with the only coin she had.

"How is she?" Bram asked quietly.

Sera scrubbed the same clean spot on the floor. "She laughs at the right times. She sells charms. She makes jokes about being the only charm-seller in Corvale whose wares are honestly rubbish." A pause. "Last week I caught her standing outside the rebuilt shrine on Bellows Lane. Just standing. Listening to people pray. Like a woman listening at a door she no longer has a key to."

"She knew the price," Bram said.

"I know she knew the price. That's what makes it unbearable." Sera straightened, leaned on the broom. "She saved every faithful soul in this city, Bram, and the reward is that she's the only one who can't be faithful anymore. The covenant holds because she can't touch it."

Bram was silent a while. Then, in the tone of a man setting down something heavy he'd carried too far: "You might ask her what she actually wants, instead of deciding what she's owed. Company rule. Nobody carries grief that isn't theirs."

"I'm not carrying—"

"You've been carrying it since the Falls. You carry it like a rucksack. Badly packed. One strap." He stood, retrieved the brandy, and headed for the door, pausing with his hand on the frame. "Court sits tomorrow. First real murder trial. Kell's been telling the whole undercity. There will be a crowd, and half of them will want blood no matter what you rule, and the other half will want mercy no matter what happens. Whatever you choose, half this city calls you a fraud tomorrow night."

"I know."

"And supper after," Bram added. "Freya's cooking. Which means it's terrible. Attendance mandatory." He tipped an imaginary hat and was gone into the lamplight.

Sera swept out the last of the barley dust, set the broom against the wall, and stood alone in the granary that was now, impossibly, a court. No sash. No seal. No gallows. Just a bare patch of floor where people swore on whatever they held sacred, and swore back, both directions, and hoped.

Tomorrow, half the city would call her a fraud. Let them. Fraud, tyrant, fool — she'd answer to all of it, in this smelly room full of shouting cousins, for as long as they let her.

She blew out the lamps and went out into the spring dark, toward Tanner's Row, toward terrible supper, toward the small stubborn light of the night market where a charm-seller with empty hands was, at that very moment, laughing at something a customer said — and Sera walked faster than she strictly needed to.
