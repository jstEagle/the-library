## 22. The Unanswered Prayer

The night market in Corvale had learned to be loud again.

That was the first thing Freya noticed every evening when she unfolded her stall beneath the crooked awning on Tanner's Row — the noise of it, easy and unafraid. Lantern-sellers calling their prices. A fiddler three stalls down who only knew four songs and played all of them badly. The smell of fried onions and tallow smoke and, faintly, the cold mineral breath of the river coming off the Falls, two streets away, where it had always come from.

Fifteen years she had worked this market with her collar up and her voice down, selling luck that was mostly copper wire and confidence. Now her sign read *Freya Hartwell — Charms, Blessings, Honest Work* in letters Sera had painted for her, because Sera's handwriting was better and because Sera had insisted, with the grim stubbornness of a woman who had once held a bridge against forty raiders, that Freya was done being invisible.

The magic was lawful now. Anyone could feel it in the air, like weather changing. Prayers rose from the undercity every evening, small and large and greedy and holy, and sometimes they were answered, and nobody burned for it.

Freya sold charms. Real ones, mostly — warming-stones for dockhands' hands, salves blessed at the rebuilt shrine by a priestess who had survived the purges hiding in a flour cellar. Her own gift, the old bright hum of Vaskar's cadence in her palms, was gone. Spent. Poured out at the lip of the Falls like the last of a flask she'd been saving for an emergency, and the emergency had come, and she had paid, and that was that.

Most days she didn't miss it. Most days.

"Charm-woman." A voice at her elbow, piping. It was Pip, the cooper's daughter, six years old and missing both front teeth, which made her negotiations ferocious and adorable in equal measure. "Mam says do you have anything for chickens."

"Anything for chickens," Freya repeated gravely. "That's a broad commission. Are they sick?"

"They're *sad*. Mam says it's because of the fox, but Tam says it's because Bessie ate a beetle that was cursed."

"Bessie."

"The brown one. She's been staring at the fence all day and not laying."

Freya crouched behind her stall and made a show of sorting through her box, letting the girl watch her reject a knot-charm (for sailors), a hush-charm (for new parents, and she sold more of those than anything), and a finding-charm shaped like a tiny brass hand (for people who lost things, which was to say, everyone). At the bottom sat what she wanted: a simple warding twist of rowan and red thread, nothing god-touched about it at all, just craft and patience.

"For the coop door," she said. "Hang it inside, where the fox can't chew it. And tell Tam that beetles aren't cursed. Annoying, maybe. There's a difference."

"How much?" Pip asked, already doing arithmetic with her whole face.

"Two coppers. Or one copper and you tell me whether Bessie lays tomorrow."

The girl slapped a coin into her palm with the solemnity of a treaty signing and ran off into the lantern-light, red thread trailing. Freya watched her go and felt the thing she felt most evenings now, the one that still surprised her: she didn't want to be anywhere else.

* * *

Sera found her there an hour later, as she often did, still wearing half her Warden grey even though the Wardens were no longer Wardens, exactly. The captaincy had been dissolved along with the Magistrate's court, and Sera had refused the new command so hard — three times, formally, in writing — that they'd finally stopped asking. What she'd built instead had no uniform, technically, though she wore enough of the old grey that everyone knew what she was. The covenant-court held session in the shrine-house twice a week. Faithful and faithless alike brought their grievances to it: a stolen pig, a contested blessing, a man who claimed his neighbor's ghost was moving his fence-posts. Sera judged them all with the same flat stare and the same maddening habit of being right.

"You have onion on your tunic," Sera said, sitting down on Freya's stool without asking, as she had not asked for anything in thirty years of friendship except once, at the Falls, and Freya still didn't like to think about that.

"It's charm residue."

"It's onion."

"Charm *onion*." Freya brushed it off anyway. "Any judgments today?"

"Widow Merrin's goat vs. the Aldouse boy. Found for the goat." Sera accepted a cup of cider from the stall-keeper's passing jug, drank, and looked out over the market with an expression Freya had learned to read the way she'd once read weather off the mountains. "The shrine-priestess asked after you again. Evening prayers are thin. People see you at them and they come."

"I'm not much of a congregation."

"You're a *symbol*, Frey."

"I'm a woman who sells chicken wards." But she said it gently, because she knew what Sera wasn't saying: that half the undercity told the story of the Falls now with Freya Hartwell at the center of it, oath-given, god-paid, and that no amount of onions would make her anything else. "I'll come to prayers," she added, before Sera could deploy the stare. "Tomorrow. Not tonight."

"Why not tonight?"

Because tonight the shrine would be full, and the singing would start, and Freya would stand in the back with her empty hands and her silent heart while around her the faithful glowed with answered hope, and she would be glad for them, truly, and also ache somewhere below the ribs like a tooth pulled long ago that the tongue kept finding. Because fifteen years of silence had ended in one terrible afternoon of hearing Vaskar's voice at last — *daughter, forgive me, I never left, I could not speak* — and then choosing, freely, eyes open, to give even that away.

She missed him. Gods, she missed him. Not the power. The voice. The certainty of being known.

"Frey," Sera said quietly, and put her hand over Freya's where it rested on the stall rail. Sera's hands were scarred and warm and steady. "You don't have to explain. You never did."

"That's a lie. I explained constantly. You just never listened until we were standing over an apocalyptic pit."

"That's true love, that is." Sera drained the cider and stood. "Bram sends his regards. He says if you don't come by the warehouse this week he's going to start telling the story of the Tanner's Row fight his way, and his way involves you crying."

"I didn't cry."

"He says his way involves *sobbing*."

"Give him my warmest regards," Freya said, laughing despite herself, and Sera squeezed her hand once and went off down the Row toward whatever needed judging next, grey coat and straight back, and Freya watched her go the way she had watched her go across twenty years and two wars and one very bad winter, with the quiet astonishment of a woman who had somehow kept everything that mattered.

* * *

It was full dark when she closed the stall, and she took the long way home, as she usually did, past the rebuilt shrine.

They had raised it on the old foundations near the Falls, but smaller than before, and humbler — a round stone building with a slate roof and no Warden-watch post beside it, which was the part that mattered. The founders' seal was broken; Hollow-Vaskar lay bound beneath the water under an oath Freya had paid for with everything she had, and above it the city had built something no law could outlaw: a place where anyone could pray to anything, or nothing, and be left alone about it.

There were candles burning inside. Late, for prayers. Freya slowed, meaning to pass by.

Then she saw the child.

A boy, small, seven or eight, alone at the prayer-rail, kneeling with the particular desperate stillness children have when they're asking for something too big for them. His coat was too thin for the season. His shoes had been mended with string. Freya knew that coat. She'd worn its predecessor through her own seventh winter, on the wrong side of Corvale's mercy.

He was praying aloud. Children did that here — no one had taught them yet to be ashamed of it.

"*Please,*" he was saying, to the stone, to the candle-flames, to whatever might be listening in the dark under the world. "*Please. My mam's cough won't stop and Da says the physic costs too much and I'm being good, I'm being so good, please make her better. Please.*"

His voice cracked on the last word and broke Freya's heart cleanly, the way only children's voices could.

And the shrine stayed silent.

She felt it — the absence, the great patient quiet where an answer should have been. No warmth in the stone. No hum. The candles guttered and rightened and gave their ordinary light, and the god or gods who owed this boy a debt stayed wherever they were, busy or distant or simply gone, and the boy knelt there getting smaller, learning early the lesson Freya had spent fifteen years learning: that faith does not guarantee an answer, and the silence is not a punishment, it is just silence, vast and indifferent and terribly, terribly lonely.

Freya stood in the doorway with her empty hands and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like the old battle-cadence, that this was the moment. This was what it had all been for. Not the seal. Not the oath. Not her name in a god's mouth. This.

She came forward and knelt beside him on the cold floor.

"Hey," she said softly. The boy startled, wiped his face with his sleeve, eyed her sword-callused hands. "What's your name?"

"Toma." Then, wary, hopeful, the way children asked: "Are you a priestess?"

"No. Worse. I'm a charm-seller." That got a wet, confused blink. Freya smiled at him. "Toma, I'm going to tell you something true, and I want you to hold onto it, because it took me a long time and a lot of trouble to learn. Sometimes the gods are listening. Sometimes they aren't. Sometimes they can't be — and it isn't because you weren't good enough. It's never that. Do you hear me? It is *never* that."

"Then who answers?" His voice was very small. "If it's not them."

"We do." Freya reached out and turned his chin gently toward her. "People answer. That's the covenant now — no law wrote it, no god signed it. We look after each other, and that's the whole religion, and it's enough. It has to be enough, because it's what we've got, and Toma—" she leaned close, conspiratorial, "—it turns out it's quite a lot."

"But my mam—"

"Your mam is going to be fine, because you prayed, and I heard you." She stood, and held out her hand. "Come on. Where do you live?"

He lived in the undercity, in a room off Cooper's Lane, and they walked there together through the lamplit streets, and Freya bought physic on the way — the good physic, the expensive kind, from the herbalist who tried to refuse her money and was overruled — and cough syrup and honey and a heavy wool blanket off a cart that was closing up. Toma carried the blanket like a king carrying treasure.

His mother was a thin woman with a woodcutter's shoulders and a racking cough, and she wept when she saw the physic, and Freya sat with her and showed her how to steep it and promised — honestly, because she was done selling false promises — that the cough would break within the week, because it would, because it was a lung-fever and the physic was good and the woman was strong. She left the blanket. She left a warming-stone too, a real one, and when the woman tried to pay, Freya pressed the coins back into her hand.

"Someone prayed for you," she said. "Very loudly. In a shrine. It worked."

At the door, Toma caught her sleeve. "Will you come back?"

"Every few days," Freya said. "To check the physic's working. That's how covenants work — somebody shows up."

He grinned at her, gap-toothed like Pip, and shut the door, and Freya walked home through the sleeping city with the river's breath on her neck and her hands empty and her heart, impossibly, full.

* * *

She passed the Falls on the way home. She always did, now. Some habits were older than grief.

The water thundered down in the moonlight, white and cold and ordinary. Beneath it, deep, the twin lay bound — she felt the binding like a scar-ache some nights, fainter each month, holding, holding. And beneath *that*, or beside it, or woven through it all in ways she would never fully understand, perhaps something of Vaskar endured too, silent by choice now rather than necessity, keeping his own kind of watch.

"Goodnight," she told the water, the way she did. "Still here. Still mine."

No answer. Of course not.

But her hands were warm in the cold air, and behind her in the undercity a mother slept under a good blanket with medicine on the shelf, and in the morning there would be a market, and chickens to inquire after, and Bram's terrible storytelling, and Sera judging goats, and prayers rising like smoke from a hundred small shrines, most of them unanswered and all of them heard — because hearing was a thing human beings had finally learned to do for themselves.

Freya Hartwell walked home through the dark, humming an old battle-cadence she no longer had the gift to feel, and found, to her lasting surprise, that it sounded almost like a lullaby.
