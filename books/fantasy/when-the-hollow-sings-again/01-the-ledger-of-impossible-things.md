## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The candle had burned down to a stub the color of old honey by the time Mirabel Lockridge reached the third page of the grudge.

He worked nights because nights were when the Gilded Hollow was honest. By day, the guildhall wore its gilding like a mask—brass fittings polished to a shine, brokers in dove-gray silk murmuring valuations over velvet trays, everything arranged so that a visitor might believe impossible things could be bought politely. But after the lamps guttered and the last broker climbed the stairs to his rooms, the Hollow told the truth about itself: it was a warehouse with pretensions, full of crates that whispered.

Mirabel sat at the long ledger desk beneath the gallery, quill moving in small precise strokes, and entered the night's acquisitions into the Book of Impossible Things.

*One (1) bottled storm, summer squall, captured over the Weeping Downs. Contents volatile. Do not shelve near the saint's shadow.*

He glanced up at the shelf in question, where a squat glass jar sat wrapped in three layers of oilcloth and chain. Even through all of it he could feel it—not hear, exactly; the gift didn't work that way—but *feel*, the way you feel weather coming in an old scar. The storm was sulking. Storms always sulked when they were caught. Somewhere under its thunder it was still trying to rain on a hillside that no longer existed, and Mirabel wrote in the margin, in his smallest hand: *unhappy. turn jar quarterly or it will sour into hail.*

Nobody read the margins. That was rather the point of them.

He dipped the quill again. The ink was good—guild ink, iron-gall cut with something Odile Crane refused to name—and it took the light of the candle and made each word look wetter than it was.

*One (1) second shadow, belonging to Saint Averil of the Ford. Provenance disputed. Shadow claims (by posture only) to have outlived its saint. Sold as-is.*

That one had cost him an afternoon. Shadows couldn't speak even to him—they weren't alive enough for the gift to catch—but they *postured*, the way dogs postured, and this one had spent its first week in the vault pressing itself flat against the wall whenever anyone passed, which Mirabel had eventually understood to mean *I am not for sale, I am in mourning.* He'd written that in the margin too. He doubted the buyer would care. The buyer was a collector from the coast who wanted the shadow because it was rare, the way a man wants a stuffed owl.

And then there was the grudge.

It lay before him in a lead-lined box the size of a bread loaf, and it was heavy in a way that had nothing to do with lead. Grudges sold by weight at the Hollow—the older and better-fed, the heavier—and this one had been fattening in a farmwife's chest for thirty-one years, ever since her neighbor's pigs broke into her orchard the night before her daughter's wedding. She had sold it that morning with tears running down her face and both hands shaking, and now it sat in its box radiating something Mirabel could only describe as *vinegar and grief*, and his job was to assign it a catalog number.

*One (1) grudge, aged 31 years, weight 4 lb 2 oz. Origin: Kettlemarsh district. Note: unusually well-preserved. Recommend storage away from forgiving persons, as forgiveness will degrade the goods.*

His quill paused.

There it was again—that small cold feeling at the base of his skull, the one that arrived every time he wrote something true about a thing that ought not to be spoken to. Because the truth was that the grudge wasn't goods. It was a wound someone had pried out of themselves and boxed, and somewhere in Kettlemarsh a woman was sleeping tonight without it for the first time in three decades and probably felt hollow as a dropped eggshell, and here it sat, priced by the ounce.

Mirabel wrote the number anyway. 4,417. He always wrote the numbers. Numbers were safe. Numbers were what kept a man like him invisible inside the most dangerous building in the realm.

Because the ledgers of the Gilded Hollow did not merely record impossible things. They recorded the *trade* in them, and trade meant contracts, and contracts meant the guildmaster's private correspondence—and Odile Crane's private correspondence was precisely where a beast-speaker should never, ever look.

He looked anyway. Every night. In small doses, like a man drinking poison to build tolerance.

* * *

The rats came at midnight, as they always did.

They came up through the gap behind the cold hearth, six of them on ordinary nights, more when the kitchen had been generous, and they arranged themselves along the wainscoting in a rough half-circle with the air of a congregation that has heard the sermon many times and come mostly for the warmth.

"Well?" said the oldest, a gray-muzzled doe who lived somewhere in the foundation stones and had opinions about everything. "Is he dead yet?"

"Who?"

"The fat broker. Tallow-hands. You said he ate something that disagreed with him."

"That was Tuesday, Grandmother. He recovered." Mirabel kept his voice below the hearing of anything with two legs. To the rats it hardly mattered—he could have mouthed the words and they'd have caught them, the way his kind always could—but he spoke aloud anyway, softly, because after twenty years of silence on the subject it was a luxury to make any sound at all. "You'll be pleased to know he's back to stealing from the pantry."

"We knew that," said a young buck from the cellar. "We watch him do it. He's very bad at it."

A ripple of rodent laughter went down the wall, a sound like dry leaves being crushed one at a time. Mirabel smiled despite himself, despite the hour, despite everything, and pulled the guildmaster's correspondence from the locked drawer where it did not belong to him.

This was the ritual. This was the whole rotten architecture of his double life: by day, Mirabel Lockridge, keeper of ledgers, a gray, forgettable man whose gift—if he even had one, said the rumor he'd started himself—was arithmetic. And by night, the rats of the Gilded Hollow received a full accounting of the guild's business, translated faithfully into the wide simple grammar of the First Tongue's low dialect, the tongue of gutters and grain-stores and things that lived under floors.

Not the whole First Tongue. Never that. What Mirabel had was a splinter of it, inherited from a grandmother who had died denying she'd ever had it—a splinter sufficient to speak with beasts and be understood, sufficient to understand their answers, insufficient to do any of the great forbidden things the Wardens' manuals warned about. He could not command. He could not bind. He could not call anything up out of the hollow places between. He could only *listen*, which was the crime the law punished least and forgave never.

The Sundering had ended all speech between the realms, three hundred years ago. The covenant that once bound human voice to beast voice had shattered—or been shattered, depending on which banned history you believed—and the First Tongue had died everywhere except in the mouths of criminals. The Crown called speakers abominations. The Wardens called them breaches. The guilds, who would have paid handsomely for one if they could find one, called them nothing at all, officially, while unofficially keeping a list of rumors.

Mirabel intended to remain a rumor until he died of old age.

"The guildmaster's letters," he told the rats, breaking the seal on the topmost letter with his thumbnail. "Are you listening? Attendance, please. Grandmother, wake the others."

"I'm awake," said Grandmother. "Everyone's awake. There was cheese."

"There is always cheese. Very well." He smoothed the letter flat. "Item one. Odile Crane writes to a broker in Corvane regarding the bottled storm. She asks whether the coastal collectors are paying more this season. She says—" he read ahead, and slowed, and read it twice, "—she says *the market for weather improves when harvests fail.*"

A silence went down the wainscoting. Rats did not understand markets, but they understood tone.

"What does that mean?" asked the young buck.

"It means," Mirabel said carefully, "that our guildmaster may be pleased when crops die. Write it down. Someone remember it. Item two." He unfolded the second letter. "She writes to a man with no signature concerning a shipment expected within the month. She describes it as *small, live, and rare beyond appraisal.*"

"Another storm?"

"No. Storms aren't live. She means a creature." His own voice sounded strange to him. "She says it must be kept quiet, and warm, and that if the Wardens come asking, the cellar was sealed for damp."

Grandmother's whiskers twitched. "We live in your cellar, bookkeeper. Nothing has been sealed."

"I know," Mirabel said. "I'm telling you what she intends to say. There's a difference." There was always a difference with Odile Crane; that was what made her guildmaster. He set the letter down and rubbed his eyes, and the candle flame leaned in the draft from the hearth-gap, and for a moment the ledger desk seemed very small and the dark behind him very large.

Twenty years. Twenty years of marginalia and midnight translations and lying with his whole posture, every waking hour, to every person with two legs. He was good at it now. That was the worst part. He'd gotten so good at being no one that some mornings he woke and had to lie still for a moment and remember which lies were load-bearing.

"Do the humans know?" the young buck asked sometimes, in the wondering way of the young. "Do any of them know what you are?"

"You're what," Mirabel would answer. "I'm a clerk."

But tonight the question lodged, as questions did when the candle got low. He looked at his hands—ink-stained, steady, unremarkable—and thought about the shelf of bottled weather, and the mourning shadow, and the boxed grudge, and the whole glittering inventory of a guild that traded in everything except the one impossible thing sitting quietly at the ledger desk translating its secrets to vermin.

Loneliness was a strange thing in a crowded house. He was surrounded, every hour, by voices—the rats, the mice in the pantry wall, the guard-dogs in the yard with their simple loyal worries, the horses, the ravens on the roof who gossiped like washerwomen. He had never once been alone with his ears open. And he had never once, in twenty years, been able to answer a single one of those voices in daylight, in front of a witness, as himself.

"You've gone quiet," observed Grandmother. "Humans go quiet when they're scheming or sad. Which is it?"

"Neither. Both. Go to bed, Grandmother."

"We live in a wall. All beds are our bed." But she gathered her congregation with a flick of her tail, and they poured back down through the hearth-gap in a brown ribbon, and the hall was silent again except for the candle and the distant, chained muttering of the storm on its shelf.

* * *

He finished the grudge entry. He re-inked the pen. He was reaching for the next acquisition—a crate of imported dreams, per the manifest, contents "mildly haunted"—when the guard-dogs began to sing.

Not bark. Sing. That low rising keening that dogs gave only when something crossed the boundary of their world, something that smelled of nowhere they knew.

Mirabel's quill stopped mid-word.

He listened past the dogs, the way he could listen past everything, spreading his attention through the dark like oil on water. The yard: two dogs on the gate-line, voices climbing. The kennels: three more, taking it up. The horses stamping in the stable, rolling their eyes white. The ravens lifting off the roof in a clatter of black wings.

And underneath it all, faint as a coin dropped in another room, something he had not heard in twenty years and had stopped letting himself dream of.

Words.

Not barking. Not the posture-and-scent grammar of beasts. *Words*, in the high clear cadence of the First Tongue's elder register, the one in his grandmother's banned books, the one no living throat should have been able to shape—

—and every animal within a mile of the Gilded Hollow was turning toward the front gate to answer it.

Mirabel stood up so fast his chair went over. The candle drowned in its own wax. And in the sudden dark, through the frost-rimed window, he saw the yard gates swing open, and a small barefoot silhouette walk calmly between two snarling, kneeling guard-dogs, toward the lighted door of the Gilded Hollow, as though it had been invited.

As though, some part of him whispered, in a language older than invitations, it *had* been.
