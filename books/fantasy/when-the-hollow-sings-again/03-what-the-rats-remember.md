## 3. What the Rats Remember

The order came down at midmorning, delivered in Odile Crane's voice the way all her orders came: pleasant as honey poured over a blade.

"Lock him in the blue room," she said to Brannick, the door-warden, while Mirabel stood at the ledger desk pretending to reconcile a column of figures that refused to be reconciled. "Feed him, keep him warm. He's not a prisoner, he's an *asset*. We'll begin appraisal tomorrow."

*Appraisal.* The word sat in Mirabel's ears like a stone dropped into still water. He knew what the Gilded Hollow appraised. Bottled storms, weighed by the ounce of their thunder. A saint's second shadow, kept folded in cedar because its owner had died owing debts and the shadow was all that remained to collect against. Grudges, sold by weight—there was a whole shelf of them in the vault, each grudge sealed in wax and labeled with the name of the one who'd nursed it and the name of the one it was meant for.

He had never once heard the word applied to a child.

The boy had been with them two days now. Two days of bare feet on cold flagstones, of the guard-dogs refusing their kennels to sleep outside whatever room he occupied, of the kitchen cats abandoning their posts by the hearth to sit in a silent ring around him while he ate. Two days of sounds drifting down from the nursery corridor that made the hair on Mirabel's arms rise—not crying, not laughing, but *speech*, in a register no human throat should have owned, and every time it came, something answered. A crow on the sill. A mouse behind the wainscot. Once, impossibly, the old cart-horse in the stable below, a low groaning note through three floors of stone.

Mirabel had spent thirty years making himself small around his gift. He knew exactly what it looked like when someone else's gift was too large to hide.

Now Odile wanted it appraised.

"You'll want to log the acquisition," she said, turning to him, and her smile was the one she used on clients who were about to be overcharged. "Provisional entry. Unknown provenance, unknown nature. I'll dictate particulars once we've established what he is."

"What he *is*," Mirabel repeated, and was proud of how flat his voice came out. "He's a child."

"He's a *phenomenon*, Lockridge, wearing a child the way a pearl wears grit." Odile's eyes were pale gray, the color of coin silver, and they did not soften. "You saw the dogs kneel. You heard the reports from the night watch—the whole forest went quiet when he arrived, then loud again all at once, like a held breath let out. Things like this don't wander out of the old wood barefoot in the first frost by accident. Something sent him, or something *drove* him, and either way there is a buyer in this realm or another who will pay a fortune to know which." She tapped the ledger desk twice with one ringed finger. "Entry. Provisional. Today."

Mirabel wrote it. His hand did not shake; thirty years of practice held even now. *One (1) male child, approx. seven years, provenance unknown.* The ink looked obscene on the page, sitting between the entry above it—a love potion recalled for false advertising—and the entry below, six pounds of grave-moss, damp but viable.

When Odile swept out, he sat for a long moment with his pen hovering over the wet line, and understood that he had just passed the first gate of something, though he could not yet see which side of it he stood on.

* * *

The cellar rats were the best-informed creatures in the Gilded Hollow, which Mirabel suspected Odile knew and tolerated, because a guild that traded in secrets ought to have some of its own listening from the walls.

He went down after the evening meal, carrying a heel of the good bread—the rye with caraway, smuggled from the kitchen under his coat—and a candle stub he didn't light, because rats saw poorly anyway and fire made them nervous. The cellar ran the length of the guildhouse, low-ceilinged, stacked with casks of pickled lightning and crates marked in dead languages. In the far corner, beneath a loose flagstone the masons had never bothered to seat properly, lived the colony: forty-odd brown rats with the glossy coats of animals who ate well and knew it.

They came to him the moment he knelt. They always did. There was no loyalty in it—he'd learned long ago that beasts felt nothing so abstract—but there was habit, and appetite, and something older than either: the plain relief of being *understood*. Every beast-speaker learned early that most animals lived their whole lives untranslated. It made them greedy for conversation. It made them, sometimes, terribly honest.

He broke the bread and scattered it, and the colony flowed over it, whiskers twitching, and he opened the quiet channel at the back of his mind where words became smell-became-meaning, and asked:

*The small one. The boy. You've seen him.*

The eldest doe came forward. She was gray about the muzzle, missing half an ear to some ancient trap, and she had lived under the Hollow longer than Mirabel had kept its ledgers. She was also, he had long suspected, a gossip of the first rank.

*Seen,* she said, in the way rats said things—which was less language than a cascade of images pressed nose-to-nose into his mind. *Seen, smelled, heard. All the colony has seen. He comes to the grate in the blue room and talks to us.*

Mirabel's stomach tightened. *Talks. How?*

And the doe showed him.

Not words—rats had no use for words—but the shape of them. The boy crouched at the cellar grate in the blue-room floor, barefoot, his breath fogging in the cold, and the sounds he made were the sounds Mirabel had spent his life hearing at the edges of things: the speech beneath birdsong, beneath wind, beneath the creak of old houses settling. The First Tongue. Not learned. Not mimicked. *Native*, the way a river is native to its bed. And the colony had gathered at the grate—all forty of them, pressed shoulder to shoulder in the dark, not afraid, not feeding, just *listening*, the way starving men press against the window of a lit house.

*What does he say?* Mirabel asked.

The doe hesitated. Rats did not hesitate. That alone frightened him more than anything that followed.

*He asks our names,* she said. *No one has ever asked our names.*

Then she gave him the rest, and the rest was worse.

She gave it as rats gave everything—in pictures, layered and simultaneous, and Mirabel knelt in the dark cellar with bread crumbs on his knees and let the images pour through him like cold water.

The boy walking out of the old forest. Not lost—*leaving*, deliberately, glancing back over his shoulder the way you glance back at a house you do not expect to return to. Bare feet black with frost that did not bite him. The trees of the old wood bending inward along his path, not with wind—with attention, the way a crowd turns its head.

And behind him—

The doe's memory shied from it, and Mirabel gentled the link, slowed it, made her show him anyway, because he needed to know.

Something vast following. Not walking. *Present*, the way weather is present. A pressure moving through the forest behind the boy, tall as the treetops or taller, shapeless in the way of things seen at the edge of sight. Where it passed, the doe said—and here she gave him the sensation rather than the image, the rat-sense of it—the *quiet* moved with it. Birds stopped. Insects stopped. The small busy sounds of living things simply ceased, as if the vast thing drank silence the way other creatures drank water. It had followed the boy to the treeline and no farther. It had stood there—at the edge of the last field, at the border of the frost—through the whole of one night, facing the guildhouse lights.

Then it had gone back into the wood. Or seemed to. The doe's opinion, offered with the flat certainty of a creature who had survived eight years by trusting her own nose: *it has not left. It is waiting. It waits the way winter waits.*

Mirabel sat back on his heels. The candle stub rolled from his fingers and clacked against the flagstone, and none of the rats startled, because they were all still looking at him, waiting to see what the man would do with what they'd told him.

*Thank you,* he said, and gave them the rest of the bread, and climbed the stairs with his heart going like a smith's hammer.

* * *

He tried to work. That was the thing about ledgers—they demanded arithmetic, and arithmetic left no room for anything else, and for thirty years that had been their secret gift to him. He entered the grave-moss. He entered the returned love potion, noting the refund and the surcharge for handling. He got as far as a new consignment of bottled fog from the coast before the pen stopped moving.

Here was the fork in the road, and it was not marked, and both directions looked like ordinary corridor.

If he reported—if he took the rats' testimony to Odile, dressed up as intelligence gathered through means he could not safely explain—then he fed the appraisal. He confirmed the boy's value. He put a number on a child, and Odile Crane would find that buyer, in this realm or the Winter Court beyond it, and the boy would end his days as the Hollow ended everything: catalogued, sealed, and sold. But he would have done his duty, protected his place, kept his gift hidden another year, another decade, until he died quietly with his secret intact and his conscience filed away in a drawer.

Or he said nothing. And Odile found out anyway—Odile always found out—and then the question would not be what Mirabel knew but why he hadn't reported it, and a guildmaster who sold grudges by weight would know exactly what to do with a clerk caught concealing contraband knowledge. There were buyers for beast-speakers too. There always had been. That was why the gift had been outlawed since the Sundering, why his grandmother had taught him to lock it behind his teeth, why he had spent his whole life translating contracts to rats in empty rooms at midnight, the only conversations where he was entirely himself.

*It is waiting,* the doe had said. *It waits the way winter waits.*

And the frost had come early this year. The frost had come wrong.

Mirabel set down the pen. He looked at the provisional entry drying on the page—*one (1) male child, provenance unknown*—and thought: I am the provenance now. Whatever happens to him happens through these books, through my hand, through my silence or my speech.

There was a third option, of course. There was always a third option, the one that wasn't safe in either direction: go up to the blue room, look the boy in the eye, and ask him—speaker to speaker, in the only tongue that mattered—what he was running from, and what he needed.

It was cowardice dressed as caution, telling himself he needed more information before choosing. He knew that. He chose it anyway, tonight, and hated himself a little for the relief of it.

* * *

The blue room was at the top of the east stair, named for wallpaper gone the color of drowned sky. Two guards had been posted; Odile trusted Brannick, and Brannick trusted no one, which amounted to the same architecture. But Brannick's post was the stair, and the blue room's window overlooked the stable roof, and the stable roof, as any smuggler worth the guild's salt knew, overlooked the town wall, and the town wall overlooked the world.

Mirabel climbed with a covered dish—supper, he'd told the kitchen, for the asset—and Brannick waved him through with the weary indifference of a man four hours into watching a corridor.

The boy sat cross-legged on the bed, which was too big for him, wrapped in a blanket that was too big for both of those together. Up close he was painfully ordinary—scraped elbows, a cowlick, the solemn round face of any seven-year-old—except for his eyes, which were the exact gray-green of deep water over stones, and except that the kitchen cat was curled on the windowsill inside a locked room, having appeared there by means no one could explain, and was watching the boy with the fixed devotion of a pilgrim.

The boy watched Mirabel come in. Did not shrink. Did not speak.

Mirabel set the dish down and crouched, slowly, the way you crouch near a strange dog, and opened the quiet channel—and for the first time in thirty years, he did not open it toward an animal.

He didn't need to. The boy's chin lifted. Those water-colored eyes widened a fraction, and his mouth parted, and what came out was soft and wondering and utterly impossible: a phrase in the First Tongue, pitched perfectly for a human throat, the greeting children used among the hollow places between—*I see you seeing me.*

Mirabel's whole body went cold and hot at once. No one had spoken that to him since his grandmother died. No one alive should know it existed.

"I see you," he whispered back, in plain trade-tongue, because his First Tongue was rusted to almost nothing, a handful of pebbles at the bottom of a dry well. "My name is Mirabel. Can you tell me yours?"

The boy considered him for a long moment. Then he reached out and touched Mirabel's wrist—two small fingers, warm as banked coals—and spoke again, and this time the words came doubled, First Tongue underneath and meaning rising through it like heat through floorboards, and what Mirabel heard, what he would carry for the rest of his life whether he meant to or not, was this:

*It is coming behind me. It is patient. When the singing stops, it will finish walking.*

Outside the window, across the rooftops, far off in the direction of the old forest, every dog in the town began to howl at once—and then, all together, stopped.
