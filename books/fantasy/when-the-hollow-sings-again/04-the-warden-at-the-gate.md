## 4. The Warden at the Gate

The Warden arrived three days after the frost, riding a grey mare that had been white once and would be again, if the winter allowed anything to be itself for very long. Sera Vance saw the guild before she saw its gate: the Gilded Hollow rising out of the river mist like something that had been dropped there by accident and decided to stay—tall, crooked, gilded at the eaves where the light caught it, shabby everywhere the light did not bother to reach.

She had been told it traded in impossible things. She had assumed this was a figure of speech.

It was not. The manifest pinned to her saddlebag—a courtesy copy from the provincial archive, annotated in some clerk's cramped hand—included, among other entries, *one storm, bottled, provenance disputed* and *a shadow, second-hand, formerly belonging to a saint*. Sera had read the manifest four times on the road and each time had come away with the same conclusion, which was that the Crown's interest in the Hollow was less about what it sold than about what it knew. Impossible things required impossible knowledge. And knowledge was the one commodity the Crown had never figured out how to tax.

She stopped at the gate. The mare stamped; her breath came out in two pale flags. Above her, the guildhouse windows were dark except one, high up under the eaves, where a candle burned with the stubbornness of someone doing accounts at an hour when honest people slept.

"Warden Vance," she said to the door, after knocking. "Crown service. I'm expected."

The door opened on a woman with ink-stained fingers and the look of someone who had been interrupted in the middle of a calculation and resented it deeply.

"You're early," said Odile Crane, guildmaster of the Gilded Hollow. "I'd budgeted another day of pretending we weren't expecting you."

"Then I'll try to be worth the expense."

Odile looked her over the way Sera imagined she looked over everything: as an entry in a ledger, awaiting valuation. "Two days," she said. "That's how long since the letter came down from the provincial seat. You rode through the night." It wasn't a question, and it wasn't admiration either. "Come in before your horse decides she's done being loyal to you. She's been telling you so for the last mile."

Sera kept her face still. "You speak with the animals?"

"I speak with everyone," said Odile Crane. "It's cheaper than listening to them." She stepped back from the doorway. "The ledger-keeper will see you. Mirabel Lockridge. He handles our correspondence. If anyone here knows about letters that shouldn't have been written, it's him."

*If anyone here knows*, Sera thought, following her into the warmth, *that is precisely the wrong thing to hand me on a plate.* Unless, of course, it was meant to be exactly the right thing. Guildmasters who gave you a name that quickly were usually giving you a sacrifice. She filed that away with everything else.

* * *

The interview room smelled of tallow and old paper. Mirabel Lockridge sat behind a desk built for a larger man, or perhaps for a smaller room, and he stood when she entered the way men stand when they've rehearsed standing and are hoping it reads as natural.

"Warden," he said. "Can I—there's tea. I could have tea brought."

"No tea." She sat opposite him without being invited, because invitations were a formality she had learned to stop extending to herself. "This won't take long. I hope."

He folded his hands on the ledger in front of him. His fingers were stained the same ink-brown as his mistress's. A clerk, then, really a clerk, down to the callus on his middle finger and the way his eyes went first to the book and only afterward to her face, as though the book might contradict him.

"You received correspondence from the provincial seat," she began. "Regarding reports of forbidden speech in this district. Are you familiar with the statute?"

"The Sundering Edicts," he said. "Yes."

"Recite them for me. Briefly."

His throat moved. "Speech between the realms is prohibited. Human voice to beast, beast to hollow place, hollow place to human. Any person found practicing the old binding-tongue—" he paused, "—is subject to seizure, examination, and, upon confirmation, the quiet sentence."

"The quiet sentence," Sera repeated. "Which is?"

"Nobody knows," he said. "That's rather the point of it."

Something in the flatness of the answer made her look at him more carefully. He wasn't being clever. He was frightened, and hiding it badly, the way a man hides fear behind precision—the exact recitation, the careful hands—and she had interviewed enough liars to know that the good ones never gave you the statute verbatim. The good ones paraphrased, approximated, got one word deliberately wrong so you'd correct them and feel superior and relax. This man gave her every word in order, like a boy reciting scripture to keep from thinking about what was under the floorboards.

"When did you last hear the First Tongue spoken?" she asked.

"Never."

"Never?"

"It's a dead language, Warden. It died with the Sundering. That's what 'dead' means."

"That isn't what dead means," Sera said. "Dead means no longer spoken. It doesn't mean unheard. Things can be heard without being spoken." She let that sit. "Have there been any unusual occurrences at the guild recently? Animals behaving strangely? Livestock? Birds?"

The pause lasted half a breath too long. "We're a trading house. Everything here behaves strangely. Last month a man tried to sell us a grudge by weight."

"And did you buy it?"

"He wanted too much per ounce."

She almost smiled. She didn't. Instead she opened the folder she'd carried in against her chest, the one with the provincial seal, and laid out—not the report, just a blank sheet, watching his eyes track the paper and not the words. "Three nights ago," she said, "in the town below this hill, every dog within earshot of the Hollow began howling at moonrise. All together. Then they stopped, all together, mid-howl, as though something had closed a door on them. The constable wrote it up as weather."

"Dogs do that."

"They don't," Sera said. "In nineteen years of service I've heard dogs start together when there's fire, or wolves, or a death. I've never heard them stop together. Stopping takes agreement." She leaned forward slightly. "And two nights ago, a child came to this door. Barefoot. In a hard frost. Unafraid of your guard-dogs—which, I'm told, is putting it mildly. Tell me about the child, Master Lockridge."

She watched him decide to lie. It happened in his face the way weather happens on a plain: visible from a great distance, arriving slowly, impossible to stop. "There's no child here," he said.

"There is a child here."

"I think you've been misinformed."

"Master Lockridge." She kept her voice level, almost gentle, which was worse. "Your guildmaster sent me to you within a minute of my arrival. Not to her factor, not to her guard-captain—to you, the keeper of her correspondence. Either she believes you know nothing, which makes you a strange choice, or she believes you know everything, which makes you a sacrifice. I haven't decided which. But I promise you that one of us in this room is going to leave knowing the truth, and I would prefer it to be both of us, because the alternative is that I find it on my own, and I am much less pleasant when I'm finding things."

Mirabel Lockridge looked at her then—really looked, for the first time, past the uniform to whatever was underneath it—and Sera had spent nineteen years making sure there was as little underneath as possible, and hated him a little for the effort it took.

"There was a child," he said finally. "He came to the door. He was cold. We took him in. He hasn't spoken a word since."

"Since when?"

"Since we took him in."

*Since we took him in.* Not *since he arrived.* She turned the phrase over and found the seam in it. "He spoke before, then. At the door."

"I didn't say that."

"You said *since*. Words mean things, Master Lockridge. You're a clerk. You of all people should respect them." She let the silence work. Outside, wind moved along the eaves, and somewhere below them a dog began barking and was hushed by a handler. "What did he say?"

"Nothing I understood."

That, at least, was true—she could hear the truth of it, the small relief in it—but it was true the way a locked door is true: accurate and useless. He hadn't understood. Which meant he had heard. Which meant—

"Thank you," she said, closing the folder. "You've been helpful."

"I've been helpful?" He sounded genuinely startled.

"People tell me everything by trying to tell me nothing. It saves time." She rose. "I'll want to see the child. Tomorrow, perhaps, once he's rested."

"I'll ask the guildmaster."

"Don't ask," Sera said, at the door. "Askers get answers prepared for them. I'll simply come."

* * *

That night she did not sleep well, and the reason had a name, and the name was Cadence.

Not the town. Her sister. Cadence, who at nine years old had sat in the barn behind their father's farm talking to the horses—not *to* them, the family joke went, but *with* them—and who at eleven had been taken by two Wardens in a cart with barred windows while Sera, fourteen and furious and held back by their mother's arms, screamed the only word that mattered: *she's not hurting anyone.*

Cadence, who had gone to examination in the capital and come back eighteen months later as a quiet, polite stranger who flinched at the sound of her own voice, and who had died at twenty-two in a fever so mild the physician called it *a wasting of the spirit*, and whose last words to Sera, whispered through cracked lips, were: *They didn't take it out of me, Ser. They took the rest of me out around it.*

Sera had joined the Wardens the following spring. Everyone assumed grief. Grief was permitted. What was not permitted, what she had told no living soul in nineteen years, was the truth: that she had joined to learn how the machine worked, so that one day she could stand inside it and refuse. Nineteen years of refusals deferred, each one smaller than the last, until here she was—a Warden of the Crown, assigned to investigate forbidden speech, lying awake in a guildhouse bed that smelled of other travelers, listening to the wind, knowing that somewhere under this roof was a child who talked to animals and a clerk who lied about it badly and a guildmaster who had handed him over like a coin she didn't need.

And knowing, worst of all, what the quiet sentence meant. Because she had read the sealed annexes, the ones Wardens of her rank weren't supposed to request, and she had requested them anyway, and now she knew what *examination* meant, and why Cadence had come back hollowed out and smiling politely at nothing.

*One more refusal deferred,* she thought. *Or not.*

She got up. She dressed. She took the corridor toward the cellars, because Odile Crane had said the child was lodged "somewhere safe," and guildmasters who said *safe* meant *locked*, and Sera had learned long ago that the fastest way to find a locked thing was to follow the sound of the lock.

She did not make it past the kitchen passage.

A fox sat in the middle of the corridor. Not caged, not fleeing—sitting, upright, composed, with the amber patience of a creature that had been expecting her. Its eyes caught the lamplight and held it, and there was something in those eyes that was not animal, and Sera's hand went to her sword hilt before her mind caught up with her training.

"You're a long way from the woods," she said, stupidly, because one speaks to fill silence even when the silence is looking back at you.

The fox tilted its head. Then, distinctly, in a voice like dry leaves arranging themselves: "And you're a long way from believing in what you're doing, Warden. I could hear it from the stairs."

Sera did not move. Every instinct she owned was screaming that this was the anomaly, the violation, the thing she had ridden two days through frost to seize—and beneath that screaming was another voice, quieter, older, wearing her sister's cadence: *they didn't take it out of me, they took the rest of me out around it.*

"You should run," she managed.

"Where?" said the fox. "Everyone interesting is in this building." It stood, stretched with theatrical elegance, and trotted past her toward the cellar stairs. At the third step it paused and looked back. "The child is asleep. The clerk is sitting with him, pretending to read. He's better at ledgers than lies, your clerk, but he stays all the same. You might want to see that before you write your report." A pause, dry as dust. "Or don't. I'm told duty is very comfortable, once you stop feeling it."

Then it was gone, down into the dark, and Sera Vance stood alone in the corridor with her sword half-drawn and her whole career balanced on a point the width of a hair.

She sheathed it. Slowly.

In the morning, she decided, she would see the child. And whatever she found—whatever the law demanded and whatever the law was for—one thing was already certain, settling over her with the cold certainty of the frost outside:

Someone in this house was going to lie to the Crown. She only had to choose whether it would be her.
