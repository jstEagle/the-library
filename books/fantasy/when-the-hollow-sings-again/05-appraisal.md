## 5. Appraisal

The summons came at dawn, written in Odile Crane's own hand on paper the color of old teeth: *Bring the boy to the long cellar after first bell. Bring the ledgers. Tell no one.*

Mirabel read it three times by candlelight, and each reading sat worse in his stomach than the last. *Tell no one.* Odile's orders always came with that clause, but this time it had a different weight—less the habitual secrecy of a guildmistress and more the hush of something being buried alive.

The boy—Ember, the kitchen girls had already begun calling him, for the way his hair caught light like struck flint—was awake when Mirabel came for him. Of course he was. He was always awake. He slept like a bird, in short shallow sips, and Mirabel had learned to recognize the small sounds he made in the dark: not words, not quite, but the First Tongue murmured low against his own collarbone, the way other children hummed.

He was sitting cross-legged on the cot in the locked storeroom where Odile had him kept, feeding crumbs of yesterday's bread through the gap under the door to a mouse that had, against all sense, become his friend.

"You shouldn't do that," Mirabel said gently. "She'll have the traps reset."

The boy looked up. His eyes were the trouble. Everything else about him could be explained away—a foundling, a changeling story, a village too poor to keep him—but those eyes were the color of deep water over deep stone, and when they fixed on you, you felt *catalogued*. Known down to your debts.

"He was hungry," Ember said, in the trade-tongue, accented strangely, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "So I fed him. That is not a thing that needs forgiving."

Mirabel crouched, took the offered crust, and set it aside. "No," he agreed. "It doesn't." And then, because he could not help himself, because the gift in him was a tongue that moved without permission, he said it again in the language beneath language—the one the rats spoke, the one the dogs dreamed in, the one outlawed since the Sundering. *That is not a thing that needs forgiving.*

The mouse went still. Then it stood up on its haunches, tiny paws folded, and looked from Mirabel to the boy and back with an expression Mirabel had no honest name for except *recognition*.

Ember smiled. It was the first time he'd smiled at anything but Nettle.

"Don't," Mirabel whispered, in the trade-tongue now, urgent. "Not here. Not where the walls have ears that aren't mine. Do you understand me? Whatever happens today—whatever she asks of you—keep that part of yourself shut behind your teeth."

The boy tilted his head. "Why?"

Because the world had burned once already because of voices like yours. Because there were men whose whole careers were built on finding them. Because Mirabel had spent thirty years learning to be quiet and knew exactly how loud a silence had to get before it killed you.

"Because I'm asking," was all he said.

Ember considered this with the grave deliberation of a child who has never once been lied to and therefore weighs every request as if it might be a riddle. Then he nodded once, and fed the rest of the bread to the mouse, and stood up to take Mirabel's hand.

His hand was warm. That was the worst part, Mirabel thought later. Everyone expected the uncanny to be cold.

* * *

The long cellar ran the whole width of the Gilded Hollow, beneath the counting rooms and the vaults, and it was where Odile kept the inventory that couldn't be shelved upstairs. Mirabel had catalogued it often enough that he could walk it blindfolded: the bottled storms racked in rows like wine, each labeled with its date and its body-count; the saint's second shadow, pressed between glass panes, still faintly warm; the grudge sold by weight, which was really just a lead box that grew heavier every year and which no buyer had ever dared open twice.

Today Odile had cleared the center of the floor.

There were cages. Six of them, arranged in a half-circle around a single chair, and Mirabel's stomach dropped before his mind caught up to what he was seeing, because he knew every animal in those cages by name and by ledger-entry and by the particular sound each made when it was frightened.

A marsh-owl called Sister Quiet, taken in trade from a fen-witch two winters past. A pine marten named Copper, quick and mean and clever as a tax collector. A gray goose with a broken wing that should have been released months ago and hadn't been, because Odile never let go of anything she might someday sell. A hare. A crow with a chipped beak. And in the largest cage, hunched and miserable in straw, a young wolf with a foreleg mended crooked—Briar, who had been brought in half-dead from a Warden snare and whom Mirabel had personally nursed through the fever, whispering to him every night in the forbidden tongue until he stopped trembling.

*Tell no one,* the note had said. Now Mirabel understood what he had not been told.

Odile Crane stood at the head of the arrangement like a general reviewing troops, and beside her, taking notes, was Master Quillon, the guild's appraiser—a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the soul of a scale, who could put a price on anything and had once, famously, valued a widow's grief at eleven marks.

"The boy sits there," Odile said, indicating the chair. "You bring him in, you stay, you watch. You write down what happens. In the ledger, Mirabel—not in whatever private cipher you imagine I don't know about."

"I don't keep a cipher," Mirabel said, which was true, and also a lie, because what he kept was worse than a cipher: he kept the truth.

"Mm," said Odile, in the tone of a woman filing his denial away for later use.

He brought Ember down the stairs. The boy took in the cellar with one slow turn of his head—the bottled storms, the shadow, the cages—and Mirabel watched something go out of his face. Not fear. Worse than fear. *Recognition*, of a kind. The look of someone seeing a room they had seen before, in some other life or some older ruin, and knowing exactly what rooms like this were for.

The animals saw it too.

Sister Quiet swiveled her great moon-face toward the boy before his foot touched the bottom stair. The crow began a low rattling call it only made before storms. Briar stood up. All six creatures turned toward Ember at once, the way iron filings turn toward a lodestone, and Master Quillon wrote *subject exhibits preternatural effect upon specimen animals prior to contact* in his neat little hand, and Mirabel wanted to take the pen and break it against the man's teeth.

"Sit," Odile told the boy.

Ember sat.

"What we're doing today," Odile said—and her voice had changed, Mirabel noticed; it had gone smooth and public, the voice she used for buyers—"is a simple matter of valuation. Every item in this guild has a price. You are an item in this guild. I apologize for nothing; the alternative to having a price is having no worth at all, and no worth means the street, and the street in winter means a shallow grave. So. We establish what you are, and then we establish what you cost, and then, if you are very lucky, we find someone willing to pay it."

She nodded to Quillon. Quillon opened the first cage.

* * *

It went like this:

They opened the marsh-owl's cage, and Quillon prodded her out with a padded stick, and Sister Quiet—half-blind, ancient, gentle as ash—hopped onto the cellar floor and stood blinking in the lantern light while a strange child watched her from a chair.

And the boy began to cry.

Not loudly. That was the horror of it. He cried the way children cry who have learned that crying loudly brings worse things: silently, steadily, tears sliding down a face that stayed perfectly composed, hands gripping the edges of the seat. And as he cried, he *reached*—Mirabel felt it happen, felt the boy's grief go out of him like heat off a stone, shapeless and enormous and seeking—

—and Sister Quiet threw back her head and screamed.

Owls do not scream. They hoot, they hiss, they clack their beaks. But this owl screamed, a raw human-sounding shriek of pure distress, and Copper the marten took it up from his cage in a chittering wail, and the goose began to honk in broken frantic bursts, and the hare thumped and thumped and thumped against the cage floor, and the crow made a sound like a hinge tearing loose, and Briar—the wolf, poor crooked-legged Briar—threw himself against the bars of the big cage howling, howling, howling, a sound with words in it if you had ears to hear, and Mirabel had ears to hear, and the words were *help, help, help, help.*

Every animal in the cellar, screaming in unison.

Quillon's pen scratched on, delighted. "Synchronized vocalization across five species," he murmured. "Six, if we count the wolf separately. Remarkable. Note the latency—under two seconds from stimulus. This is not training. Training leaves latency. This is resonance."

"It's a child crying," Mirabel said, and his voice came out flat and strange. "You're writing down a child crying as though it were a property of livestock."

"I'm writing down what sells," Quillon said, without looking up.

"Enough." Odile raised one finger and the owl's cage was closed, the marten's, the hare's. The screaming wound down by degrees, species by species, until only Briar was still making noise, pressing his ruined muzzle against the bars, staring at the boy with yellow eyes gone wild with sympathy.

Ember had stopped crying. He sat very straight now, very still, and his eyes had gone somewhere far away, and Mirabel recognized the look because he had worn it himself at seven years old, at ten, at twenty-five: the look of a person closing every door inside themselves, one by one, so that nothing could get in and nothing could get out. The look of someone learning to be quiet.

"Again," Odile said.

"No," Mirabel said.

The word dropped into the cellar like a stone into a well. Odile turned to him slowly, and her face did not change at all, which was how Mirabel knew he was in more danger than he had ever been in his life.

"No?" she said.

"He's seven years old. He's been here four days. You've got your valuation—resonance, latency, six species, write it up, frame it, sell it to whoever buys. What you want now isn't data. It's the boy's *depth*, and the way you measure depth is you drown him a little further each time and see what floats up. I've read enough contracts in this building to know what appraisal becomes when nobody says stop."

Silence. Quillon had stopped writing. Even Briar had gone still.

Odile crossed the floor unhurried and stood close, closer than she ever stood to anyone, and looked up into Mirabel's face. She was a small woman; people forgot that, and regretted it.

"Four days," she said softly. "And already you love him. I can see it. It's all over you like cheap perfume." She glanced back at the boy in the chair. "Good. Love makes people predictable, Mirabel. Predictable people can be managed. Now pick up the wolf's cage key, carry it back to the rack, and remember whose ledger your name is written in."

He picked up the key. He carried it to the rack. His hands did not shake, and he hated them for it.

But as he passed the boy's chair, Ember reached out—quick, small, deliberate—and touched Mirabel's wrist, two fingers, the way you'd touch someone to say *I see you standing in the fire too.*

And in the First Tongue, so quietly that no one heard it but Mirabel and perhaps the wolf, the boy said: *Don't be afraid. I know what she is going to do. I have been sold before.*

* * *

He wrote the report that afternoon because refusing entirely would only make Odile assign it elsewhere, to someone with less access and fewer scruples, and Mirabel had already decided—he did not name the decision even to himself—that whatever happened next, he would be the one holding the pen.

*Subject responds to distressed fauna via unknown mechanism. Effect is involuntary and appears empathic rather than commanding. Subject exhibits no aggression. Recommend restricted handling. Recommend removal of subject from proximity of livestock, working animals, and stable stock, as effect observed to generalize.*

All of it true. None of it useful. He left out the part that mattered most—the part Quillon had missed, because Quillon listened with his pen and not his spine: that the animals had not screamed *at* the boy. They had screamed *with* him. That was not a trick. That was not a talent. That was the covenant itself, frayed to its last threads, still passing its current through anything alive enough to feel it.

The boy wasn't exhibiting an ability. He was *leaking*. The way a dying fire leaks smoke. The way a cracked bell leaks silence into every note.

Mirabel sealed the report, delivered it, and then went down into the walls.

The rats had a parliament chamber behind the third-floor pantry, in the gap between the old chimney and the new, a nest of shredded ledger-pages (he tried not to take this personally) where the boldest of them held court. He knelt on the cold stone and put his mouth to the crack and asked, in the old tongue, the question he'd been carrying since the cellar: *What have you seen? What do the walls say about the small one?*

The answer came back in fragments, rat-thoughts being what they were—all smell and motion and grievance—but coherent enough, assembled:

*Small one does not sleep. Small one walks the storeroom at night and puts his palm flat on the floor and the floor remembers being forest. Small one speaks to the locks and the locks grow confused and one night soon a lock will forget its work entirely. And behind the small one, far behind, out past the walls, out past the town, something vast has been walking since the frost came. Slow. Patient. It does not come closer. It waits the way winter waits. The birds fly over it and will not land behind it.*

Mirabel sat back on his heels.

Something vast, following the boy out of the old forest. Something patient. He had told himself the child was a refugee, a stray, a foundling washed up on the Hollow's doorstep by ordinary catastrophe. He had told himself a great many things in four days.

Now he did the arithmetic he had been avoiding. Odile had cleared the cellar. Odile had summoned Quillon. Odile had ordered the wolf kept caged instead of sold, though the wolf's price had been quoted twice this season. These were not the preparations of a woman deciding whether to keep an asset.

These were the preparations of a woman composing a letter.

And Mirabel knew her correspondence list by heart, because he copied it every quarter-day: the merchant princes of the Salt Coast, who would pay in gold. The Archonate of the Court, whose inquisitors paid in favors and whose favors were chains. And at the bottom of the page, in Odile's smallest hand, the entry she believed he could not read because it was written in the Winter Court's own script—a script he had taught himself at nineteen, from a book in this very cellar, because a beast-speaker who cannot read what hunts him dies of it:

*One (1) speaker, juvenile, untrained. Covenant-grade resonance confirmed. Price: survival. Terms negotiable.*

The Winter Court. Not the Crown. Not the merchants. She meant to sell the child to the Court that had ended the world once already, the Court whose hunger had forced the covenant into being, the Court that had spent three hundred years looking for exactly this: a living anchor, young enough to train, strong enough to hold, small enough to cage.

The long winter falling across the realm wasn't weather. It was a searchlight.

Mirabel knelt in the dark behind the pantry with the rats' warm eyes gleaming at him through the crack, and he felt the guilt arrive on schedule—the guilt of four days of obedience, of carrying the boy his meals and locking the door behind him, of writing neat reports while the noose was measured. Guilt was familiar. He had lived on it for thirty years, the way other men lived on bread.

But tonight, for the first time, it hardened into something with an edge.

He thought about the ledger. About the pages and pages of impossible things he had catalogued without comment—bottled storms, stolen shadows, grudges by weight—and how none of it had ever made him sick until now, because none of it had ever been a *child*. Odile traded in everything, but she had never yet traded in a person, and Mirabel realized, sitting in the rat-dark with his heart going slow and enormous in his chest, that she was about to, and that the line was about to be crossed in his handwriting, with his valuations, under his seal.

*Price: survival. Terms negotiable.*

Well. Two could negotiate.

He rose, brushed the dust from his knees, and walked back down through the sleeping guild, past the counting rooms, past the vault, down to the locked storeroom where a small warm shape breathed in the dark. He did not go in. He stood outside the door a long moment, listening—to the boy's slow bird-sleep breathing, and beneath it, faint as a pulse through stone, the vast patient thing far to the north, waiting the way winter waits.

Then he went to his own room, took out a fresh sheet of paper, and began, in the trade-tongue, in plain honest letters anyone could read, to draft a lie.

It was the first lie he had ever written for someone else's sake, and it came easily, which frightened him more than the rats' news had.

Across the hall, in the wardens' quarters lent to the Crown's investigator, a candle still burned. Sera Vance kept late hours. Sera Vance asked questions that weren't on any official list. Sera Vance had watched him in the corridor yesterday with those gray unblinking eyes and said, *You count things, Master Lockridge. Counters always know where everything is. Even the things that aren't supposed to exist.*

Mirabel dipped his pen. Outside, the first false snow of midsummer drifted past the window, silent as ash, and somewhere below the floor, a lock that had spoken to a child began, very quietly, to forget its work.
