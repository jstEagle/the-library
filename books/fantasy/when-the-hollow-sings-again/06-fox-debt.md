## 6. Fox-Debt

Nettle smelled the child before she saw him, and the smell was wrong.

Not wrong the way a corpse is wrong—corpse-smell is honest work, the body telling the truth at last. Wrong the way a river running uphill is wrong. The boy in the cellar below the Gilded Hollow carried a scent like frost on green things, like the space between a lightning strike and its thunder, like the old forest before the Sundering had taught it to be afraid. Nettle had not smelled that since before her bargain went sour, and the memory of it made her tail ache in a way tails should not ache.

She lay along the rafter above the kitchen anyway, because lying was what foxes did best and because Odile Crane's summons had been specific.

*Watch him,* the guildmaster had said, standing over the ledger desk with her rings clicking against each other the way they did when she counted money in her head instead of out loud. *Don't speak to him. Don't let anyone else speak to him. And for all the saints' second shadows, Nettle—if he says anything back to you, you come tell me first.*

*And if I don't?*

Odile had smiled then, and Nettle had seen the village girl under the silk, the one who'd watched winter eat her neighbors one by one. *Then you'll owe me more than you already do. And we both know what happens to debts that grow too large.*

So: watching. Nettle was good at watching. She had spent ninety years being watched herself—caged, measured, appraised—and she knew every trick of it from the inside.

The boy sat on the cellar floor with his back against a wine rack, knees drawn up, barefoot even though the stone was cold enough to make a human child cry. He wasn't crying. He was listening—to the walls, to the pipes, to the lock on his door, which had begun whispering to itself the day after they locked him in, forgetting how to hold its tongue.

That was the thing about the First Tongue that humans never understood. It wasn't a language you spoke *to* things. It was the language things already spoke, all of them, always—the groan of timber settling, the rust of iron dreaming, the slow argument between root and stone. Before the Sundering, everyone could hear it the way everyone can hear rain. After, only the hollow places kept it, and the beasts, and the things like Nettle who were made of it.

And this child, who should have been as deaf as any other human, sat in a cellar speaking it back to the dark like it was his mother's name.

Nettle dropped down through the gap between rafters without a sound, because startling him would be cruel and because she wanted to see what he did when a fox-spirit unfolded out of shadow into the lantern light.

He looked at her. He didn't flinch. He didn't reach for her fur the way human children always did, hands full of grabby wonder. Instead he tilted his head—her own gesture, stolen clean—and said something.

The words came out of him small and round and old. Nettle felt them land on her ears like snow landing on snow.

*What are you called,* he had said. Not "what is your name." In the First Tongue there was no difference between a thing and what it was called, and so asking someone's name was asking them to show you themselves, and it was rude to do it first.

Which meant the child had just handed her his own self unasked, open as a door.

Nettle's hackles rose. Ninety years of indentured servitude, ninety years of keeping every part of herself behind her teeth, and a barefoot boy of seven had undone it in four syllables.

"You shouldn't do that," she said, in human speech, rough from disuse. "Talking to strangers."

The child considered this. Then, in the old words: *You aren't a stranger. You smell like home.*

"Home," Nettle said, "burned down before you were born, little anchor. Everything smells like home when you're the last of something."

But she sat down beside him, because her tail had decided before her head did, and tails knew things heads took longer to learn.

* * *

They played a game that night. Nettle told herself it was reconnaissance.

The game was an old one—*which-hand-holds-the-winter*, the kits used to play it in the hollow hills before the Sundering closed them, before the covenant bound every voice in both realms so neither could devour the other whole. You hid a pebble in one paw and asked a question in the First Tongue, and the pebble answered true or lied, depending on whether the question was honest.

Humans had their own version, Nettle supposed. They called it gambling, and they ruined their lives with it.

The boy was good at the game. Better than good. When he asked the pebble *is the sky still mine*, and the pebble said *yes*, Nettle had to turn away and groom her shoulder until her eyes stopped doing whatever they were doing.

"That's not how it works anymore," she told him finally. "The sky belongs to the Crown now. They put it in ledgers."

The child frowned at her, and the frown was ancient. *Why would anyone write the sky down?*

"So they can sell it."

He thought about that with the terrible seriousness of children, and then he asked the question Nettle had been dreading since she climbed down through the rafters:

*Who bound you?*

"Not who." She curled her tail over her paws. "What. A bargain. That's all a binding is, little anchor—a bargain where one side forgets to read the fine print. Your kind wrote the covenant so no one could ever make that mistake again. Every voice in both realms, tied to every other voice, so that when one sang, all heard it, and none could swallow another in the dark where no one listens." She bared her teeth, not quite a smile. "It worked beautifully. For a while. Then your ancestors got frightened of hearing too much, and they broke it, and everything since has been the long echo of that breaking. Famines. Silences. Winters in midsummer. Me."

*Me?*

"Debts don't die, child. They wait. My bargain was struck before the Sundering, unpaid, and when the covenant held, the debt slept in me like a splinter. Now the covenant's fraying, and the splinter's waking up. Every year I feel a piece of myself go quiet. Last spring it was my shadow's voice—I used to be able to send it hunting without me. This year—" she flexed her forepaws, watched the lantern light pass through the left one slightly wrong, slightly late— "I don't know what goes next. That's the price of broken bargains. You pay in pieces, and the pieces get taken from the middle out, so you never know which parts you're missing."

The child reached out and touched her paw where the light came through late. His hand was warm. Human-warm, which was the warmest kind there was, back when the realms still traded warmth freely.

*When the covenant comes back,* he said, *will you be whole?*

Nettle looked at him for a long time.

"No one knows what comes back," she said. "No one's reforged it before. There's a story that the binding needs two voices—one from each realm, willing, tied together. But willing voices are rare, and the price—" she stopped, because the price was the one thing foxes didn't talk about, the superstition older than the Sundering: *name the price and it becomes yours to pay.*

The boy waited. Children were patient with silence in a way adults never managed; they lived in it.

"The price," Nettle said at last, quietly, "is being heard by both realms at once. Everything you are, laid open, everywhere, forever. No hiding. No keeping parts behind your teeth." She touched her muzzle to his hair, an old gesture, an aunt's gesture, one she hadn't made since her den burned. "You understand why nobody volunteers."

He nodded slowly. Then he said, in the First Tongue, words that went through Nettle like cold water through cloth:

*I would. If it would give you your shadow back.*

* * *

She was still sitting with him near dawn when the knock came at the cellar door—three soft taps, and then a pause, and then a fourth tap that did not belong to any guard's rhythm.

Nettle flowed into the rafters and became shadow. The boy, bless him and doom him, simply watched the door with interest.

Mirabel Lockridge slipped inside carrying a covered tray he had no business bringing to a prisoner. He set it down, crouched to the boy's level, and glanced around the room once—the glance of a man checking whether walls had ears, which in the Gilded Hollow they sometimes did.

"Eat," Mirabel said. "The bread's not drugged. I checked the kitchen myself. Well—one of the kitchen's less formal residents checked it for me."

The boy cocked his head. Then he answered—not in human speech, but in the old words, soft and wondering: *The rats say thank you. They say you translate the contracts so they know which walls will fall.*

Nettle, folded into darkness above, felt her ears stand straight up.

Mirabel Lockridge went very still.

"I don't know what you mean," he said, in the flat voice of a man lying badly, and Nettle almost laughed, because she'd heard better lies from oysters.

The boy smiled—the first smile Nettle had seen on him, wide and gap-toothed and seven years old—and repeated it, gentler, the way you repeat things to someone who cannot hear how loud their own heart is: *The rats. Thank you.*

For a moment the cellar was silent except for the lock muttering to itself, forgetting its work.

Then Mirabel Lockridge sat down on the cold stone across from a child who had just named his secret aloud, and Nettle watched—from her rafter, in her shadow, with her fading pieces—watched him decide. She saw it happen in his face the way weather happens: fear first, then calculation, then something underneath both that was neither, something that looked, gods help them all, like relief.

"They say that?" he asked quietly. "The rats?"

*All of them,* the boy said. *Even the ones in the walls of the wardens' rooms. Especially those. They say the gray woman counts in her sleep.*

Above, in the dark, Nettle curled her tail tight around herself and thought about bargains.

She owed Odile Crane a debt nine decades old, and debts grew teeth when ignored. She had been ordered to watch the child and report, and she had instead taught him games and given him her grief and let him promise her impossible things. Any one of those facts, sold to Odile, might buy back a piece of her—maybe even the shadow, maybe even the rest of what was going quiet.

And below her, a man who translated contracts for rats was learning that the child could hear every kindness he'd ever done in secret, and a boy who smelled like the world before it broke was smiling at him like he was worth knowing.

*Two willing voices,* the story said. *One from each realm.*

Nettle pressed her muzzle into her tail and began, very softly, to plan her treachery—which was to say, her honesty, which for a fox was the same thing wearing different clothes.

She was still planning when boots sounded overhead, heavy and doubled, and Odile Crane's voice came down through the floorboards like a blade slid between planks:

"Wake our guest. The Court's envoy arrived early. We appraise at noon."

Above the cellar, in the hall, a candle that had burned all night guttered out—and Sera Vance opened her door.
