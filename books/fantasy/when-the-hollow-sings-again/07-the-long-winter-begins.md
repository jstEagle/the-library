## 7. The Long Winter Begins

The snow began at noon, in the middle of the wheat harvest, and Sera Vance watched it fall from the window of the Warden garrison at Thornmere with her tea going cold in her hands.

It was not the soft, apologetic snow of a freak storm. It fell with intent—fat, unhurried flakes that settled on the ripe gold heads of wheat until the whole field bowed, then bent, then broke. She could hear it from the open casement: a sound like a thousand small sighs as stalk after stalk gave way under a weight no harvest was ever built to carry.

Below in the courtyard, Corporal Iven stood with his bare hand out, catching flakes, laughing. He was twenty-two. He had never seen anything die that he hadn't killed himself.

"Corporal," Sera said, not loudly. The window carried her voice the way stone carried cold. "Get your hand inside before it blackens. And get every sheaf within reach under cover. If we can't stop this, we can at least be less useless than the weather."

Iven's laugh died. He looked at his palm, where the snow was melting into red-tinged water—the first bite of frost already chapping skin that should have been sweating—and went to do as he was told without another word.

Sera set down her tea. It had gone bitter anyway.

Three provinces. That was what the dispatch said, when it came an hour later, sealed with the double crown of the Office of Anomalous Affairs and stamped in wax so dark it looked like dried blood. Snowfall in Vessing, Callowmere, and the lowlands of Harrow Fen. Midsummer snow. Rivers icing from the banks inward, as if the cold were walking out of the ground rather than falling from the sky. Livestock standing motionless in fields, unresponsive to whip or voice or fire.

*Unresponsive to voice.* She read those three words twice, and something in her chest went tight and cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season.

She had been a Warden for eleven years. She knew what orders looked like when they were written by frightened men. This one was written by terrified ones, and terrified men did not write careful laws—they wrote wide nets.

*By decree of the Crown, all anomalous persons, creatures, and phenomena are to be seized, catalogued, and conveyed to the capital for assessment. No anomaly is to remain at large. Mercy is hereby suspended.*

*Mercy is hereby suspended.* As if mercy were a garrison you could garrison and ungarrison. As if it were a thing with a gate.

She read the third page more slowly, because the third page was the one that mattered. Attached was a list of reports, gathered from informants and toll-keepers and the kind of people who sold their neighbors' secrets for a copper a word. Most of it was the usual harvest-season nonsense: a two-headed calf, a well that sang, a woman in Callowmere who claimed her shadow walked ahead of her on cloudy days.

Then, near the bottom, in a clerk's tidy hand:

*Item 14 — Gilded Hollow trading post, north road. Unregistered arrival approx. one month past: male child, age six to eight years, origin unknown. Arrived on foot, barefoot, unharmed by frost. Reportedly caused livestock and working animals within earshot to behave abnormally. Animals described as "answering" the child. Current status: held at the Hollow by guildmaster Odile Crane, pending appraisal. No further details. Appraisal value: pending.*

Pending. Odile Crane was holding a child the way she held everything else—until the price came in.

Sera sat very still. The window light was going strange, white-gold and wrong, summer sun shining down through falling snow, so that the dust motes in her office seemed to hang suspended between seasons, belonging to neither.

She thought about Mirabel Lockridge's face when she'd asked him about the boy. She had interviewed forty people in her career who were hiding something, and she had developed a taxonomy of their tells: the liars blinked too fast, the guilty over-explained, the ashamed went flat and quiet. Mirabel had done none of these. He had done something worse. He had told her the truth wrapped around a lie like a husk around a seed—*yes, there is a boy; yes, he arrived on the frost night; yes, the dogs behaved strangely*—and left the seed itself unsaid, the thing underneath, the actual matter. *What is he? What did you hear when he spoke?*

And she had let him keep it, because she had seen something else in his face that she recognized the way you recognize a scar pattern—on yourself.

Now the Crown was sending its net across three provinces, and the seed was on the list. Item fourteen. Pending appraisal.

Sera reached for her ink and stopped, pen hovering, for a long moment, over the report form.

Here was the arithmetic of her life, laid out plain: eleven years of service. A posting to Thornmere that everyone in the capital understood for what it was—a shelf, a place to store a Warden whose last investigation had ended badly, whose questions had gotten too loud, whose name still surfaced in certain rooms followed by a certain silence. She had taken the posting because the alternative was resignation, and resignation meant admitting the thing she could not yet admit: that she had loved the law more than it loved her back, and that some part of her was still waiting, like a kicked dog, to be called good again.

If she filed the report accurately—if she wrote *item fourteen confirmed, subject present at Gilded Hollow, recommend immediate seizure*—then riders would come north within the week. Wardens with writs and chains. And whatever the boy was, whatever the animals heard when he opened his mouth, he would go to the capital in a cage to be *assessed*, and Sera knew exactly what the Office of Anomalous Affairs did with things it assessed during a national panic. She had read the older files. The ones from the Sundering's aftermath, when the First Tongue was outlawed and the speakers were rooted out root and branch. The files did not use the word *mercy* even once, which was perhaps the most honest thing in them.

If she filed it accurately, she was obedient. And obedience, right now, wore the face of a seven-year-old boy in a cellar.

If she delayed it—even a day, even with a plausible clerical excuse, *verification required, informant unreliable, awaiting corroborating report*—she was something else. Something she didn't have a clean word for. Traitor was too grand. Coward was too small. There should have been a word for a person who simply could not make her hand move the way the Crown wanted it to move, but the Crown's language, she had noticed long ago, was rich in words for disobedience and poor in words for conscience.

Her pen touched paper.

She wrote: *Item 14 — details inconsistent with primary source. Informant (toll-keeper, Harrow road) known unreliable. Recommend verification prior to action. Requesting assignment to investigate personally given proximity of Thornmere garrison.*

It was true, mostly. The toll-keeper *was* unreliable. She *was* the closest posted Warden. And investigation was, technically, what she intended to do. The lie lived in the space between the lines—in the fact that she already believed every word of item fourteen, and that verification was not her purpose. Her purpose was to get there first. To stand between the net and whatever it was closing on, for as long as standing there was possible, and to tell herself the whole way north that she was only gathering facts.

Facts. She signed the report, sanded it, sealed it, and felt the seal press cool against her thumb like a small verdict.

Outside, the snow kept falling on the broken wheat, and somewhere below, Corporal Iven was shouting for tarps, and summer was dying three months early with no one to file a report on its behalf.

*

She rode out at dawn with two troopers, because going alone would have been a statement and statements got Wardens recalled. Iven and Dree rode behind her, young and loud and glad of any errand that broke the garrison routine, and Sera let their chatter wash over her while she watched the country come apart.

The first farm they passed had abandoned its fields entirely. Wheat lay flattened under a crust of ice that gleamed in the morning sun like the scales of some enormous sleeping fish. The farmer stood at his fence watching them pass, and he did not beg, which frightened her more than begging would have. He just looked at them with the flat patience of a man doing sums he doesn't like, and Sera understood that he was counting how many days of stored grain stood between his family and the hunger, and that the answer was not a large number.

"Strange weather," Dree offered as they rode on. "My gran always said the old kings could call snow like this. Curse-weather, she called it."

"Your gran also said eels were the souls of tax collectors," Iven said. "Curse-weather. It's weather. Cold snap. Happens."

"In midsummer?"

"Happens."

"It does not happen," Sera said quietly, and both of them shut up, because she almost never spoke on the road and when she did it counted double. But the truth was that it did not happen, and never had, not in any chronicle she had read, not in any elder's tale, not in the deep archives she had once been permitted to access back when she was permitted to access things. Weather did not fall in straight obedience to a border, and this cold did. The dispatches said Harrow Fen was white to the river while Vessing's southern parishes still had green grass, as though someone had drawn the line of winter with a ruler.

Weather obeyed no one. Which meant this was not weather. Which meant it was a phenomenon. Which meant it was hers—hers and every other Warden's—to seize, catalogue, and convey to the capital, per decree.

She wondered, riding through the white fields, how exactly one seized a season.

They made the Gilded Hollow's outer road by late afternoon. Even before the guildhouse came in sight, Sera saw the signs along the way that made her spine prickle: birdsong, absent. Not quiet—absent, the way a room is absent of furniture. She had grown up in Harrow Fen among marshbirds that sang from before dawn until after dark; she knew what a living countryside sounded like, and this was not it. The hedgerows were full of shapes—sparrows, wrens, a crow that watched them pass from a fencepost with one round eye—but the shapes made no sound at all. Once, crossing a frozen stream, they flushed a hare, and it ran in perfect silence, and Iven actually reined up and stared after it with his mouth open.

"Even the hares are quiet," he said.

"The hares are fine," Dree said, but he didn't sound sure.

The Gilded Hollow rose out of the whitened landscape in its usual fashion: gilded. Guildmaster Crane had gilded everything that would hold gold leaf—the sign, the door hinges, the weathervane, the teeth of the guard-dogs' collars—and in the strange doubled light of sun-on-snow the whole building glittered like something that had been buried in a king's tomb and dug back up. The dogs were in the yard. They did not bark at the riders.

That was new. On her previous visits they had sounded off at fifty paces, deep-chested and professional. Now they stood at the gate in a row, four of them, perfectly silent, and as Sera dismounted they all turned their heads—not toward her, toward the guildhouse itself, toward some point below it, the cellars maybe—and held there, listening to something she could not hear.

"Steady," she murmured, to herself or the dogs, she wasn't sure which.

Odile Crane received her in the front room among the impossible inventory—a bottled storm turning slow circles behind glass, a saint's second shadow folded in a cedar box like a bolt of black silk, a ledger-clerk in the corner weighing a grudge on a brass scale with the concentration of a man weighing saffron. Odile herself was exactly as Sera remembered: silver-haired, sharp-faced, dressed in gray silk worth more than the garrison's annual budget, with the smile of a woman who has already calculated what you'll pay and finds the figure amusing.

"Warden Vance." Odile spread her hands. "Twice in a season. The Crown flatters us."

"The Crown investigates." Sera did not sit. "There's a decree out. You've seen it."

"I've seen snow." Odile glanced at the window, where the flakes drifted past the glass, unhurried, patient, endless. "The decree I've merely heard about. Anomalies, is it? Seize and convey?" Her smile sharpened by a degree. "You've come to appraise my stock, then. I warn you, my prices are famous."

"I'm here about the child."

Something moved behind Odile's eyes—quick, controlled, and gone. It was not surprise. It was the look of a merchant hearing a customer quote a price she'd hoped to set herself. "Child? We have several children pass through. Apprentices' brats, refugees. The roads being what they are."

"A boy. Six to eight. Barefoot on the frost night. The dogs knelt to him." Sera paused. "The dogs are kneeling now, Mistress Crane. They've been facing your cellar since I rode through the gate. I'd like to know what they're listening to."

For a moment the front room was very quiet. The bottled storm turned. Somewhere in its glass, lightning flickered like a trapped pulse. The grudge-weigher had stopped weighing and was pretending, badly, to study his scale.

"You've been talking to my toll-keepers," Odile said. "They exaggerate. The boy is a foundling, half-frozen, simple-minded. He makes noises. Birds being birds, they make noise back. There's your anomaly, Warden—a child and some sparrows." She said it smoothly, each word placed like a coin on a counter, and it was a good performance, and it was wasted on Sera, because Sera had spent eleven years learning to hear the pause underneath the sentence, and Odile's pause had lasted half a breath too long, and half a breath was where merchants hid their fear.

"Simple-minded foundlings don't arrive barefoot through a killing frost," Sera said. "I want to see him."

"No."

The word came out flat and fast, stripped of silk, and Sera heard it land in the room like a dropped blade, and knew, in the same instant Odile did, that it had cost her something to say it. Odile recovered fast—her smile reassembled itself piece by piece, a mask going back together—but the order of operations had been revealed. The smile was the costume. The *no* was the body underneath.

"Mistress Crane," Sera said, gently now, because gentleness opened more doors than force, "three provinces are under snow in midsummer. Rivers are freezing from the bottom up. Every animal between here and Harrow Fen has gone silent. My superiors believe these events are connected to anomalous speech—the forbidden kind—and they are seizing everything that might be connected. Including, if necessary, guildmasters who obstruct a Crown investigation." She let that sit. "Help me help you. Show me the boy, tell me honestly what he is, and I will write a report that keeps the heavy writs away from your door. Hide him, and the next visitor won't be me. It will be Captain Rennick, and Rennick does not buy, trade, or negotiate. He confiscates. Ask anyone who's dealt with him."

Odile studied her for a long moment. Outside, the snow ticked softly against the glass, filling the silence the way sand fills an hourglass.

"You may interview the household," Odile said at last. "Not the boy. The boy is under appraisal, and appraisal is a protected guild process—chartered rights, Warden, older than your Office. You may speak to my clerk, Mirabel Lockridge. He handled the intake. He can tell you everything relevant." A thin pause, threaded with something that might have been malice or might have been warning, delivered with perfect ambiguity. "*Everything relevant.*"

"That will do for a start," Sera said.

Odile rang for the clerk, and while they waited she poured two glasses of plum wine with the serenity of a woman who had just moved a dangerous piece two squares and dared the board to object, and Sera drank hers because refusing would be a statement, and hated it, and smiled the whole time.

Mirabel came when called. He was exactly as she remembered: lean, careful, ink-stained to the second knuckle, with the watchful stillness of a man who has spent his whole life being overlooked on purpose. His eyes went to her, then to the window, then—briefly, involuntarily—to the floor, in the direction of the cellar, and back up again, and the whole journey took less than a heartbeat, but Sera caught it, because catching things like that was the one skill she'd never been able to unlearn.

"Master Lockridge," she said. "Warden Vance. Again. I have questions about the foundling."

"Of course, Warden." His voice was level. His hands were not quite; he folded them behind his back, which was either deference or concealment, and she suspected both. "Whatever's useful."

Behind him, faint through the floor, from the deep places under the guildhouse, Sera heard—or thought she heard—a sound. Small, high, wordless. A child's voice, singing something with no words in it at all.

Every dog in the yard began, very softly, to howl.

Not in alarm. That was the terrible part. In harmony.

*

She wrote her report that night in the guild's guest room, by candlelight, with the snow still coming down past the window like a curtain that someone, somewhere, was drawing slowly across the world.

The report was short. She read it five times. *Investigation ongoing. Subject not observed. Household accounts consistent with foundling of impaired faculties. No evidence of forbidden speech obtained. Recommend continued observation. Requesting extension of assignment.*

Every sentence defensible. Every sentence, together, a wall.

She thought about the oath she'd sworn at nineteen, hand on the crown-stone, in a hall full of banners: *to hold the line between the realms and the ruin.* Everyone had assumed the ruin would come from outside—from the wilds, the hollow places, the old powers. No one had imagined the line running through the middle of a report form, through the middle of a Warden, so that on one side of her signature lay duty and on the other lay a child singing wordlessly in a cellar while the dogs sang with him, and she had to choose which side of herself to stand on.

She thought about her last great case, the one that had shelved her in Thornmere—the evidence she'd found, the names she'd spoken, the way the Office had thanked her by burying the findings and burying her with them. She had told herself, these two years, that she'd learned her lesson. That truth-telling in the Crown's service was a coin you spent once.

But the coin was warm in her pocket tonight, and the snow kept falling, and somewhere beneath her feet a child was singing a language older than the Crown, and the animals were answering, and winter was walking across three provinces like something returning to claim a debt.

Sera dipped her pen, signed the report, and sealed it.

Then she took out a second sheet, unsigned, unstamped, and wrote a single line on it in her own hand, addressed to no one, meant for herself alone, the kind of document that gets people hanged if it's ever found:

*If the boy is what the silence says he is, then the law is on the wrong side of the line, and I know which side I swore to hold.*

She folded it small and put it in her breast pocket, over her heart, where oaths are kept.

Down in the cellar, faintly, the singing went on. Sera blew out her candle and sat in the dark, listening, and did not sleep.
