## 8. Running the Hollow

The ledger's pages burned the way all lies do—quickly, and with a smell that lingered.

Mirabel worked by the light of a single tallow stub in the counting room, the guild asleep around him except for the rats, who had gone quiet at his request. *One hour,* he had told them through the wainscoting. *One hour of silence, and I'll owe you the whole wheel of cheese from the third shelf.* They had agreed with the brisk indifference of creatures who knew exactly how much a man's desperation was worth.

Before him lay the record of the child.

*Item: one boy, age indeterminate, acquired by unknown means, held for appraisal. Value: pending.*

Odile Crane's neat hand. Odile Crane's neat cruelty. Mirabel had watched her write it two days ago and said nothing, and every hour since, the silence had grown teeth.

He turned to the death-records section—the oldest part of the ledger, where the Gilded Hollow recorded its losses with the same care it recorded its gains. A guild that traded in impossible things needed impeccable books. Bottled storms had serial numbers. Saints' shadows were notarized twice. And when something died in the Hollow's keeping, the entry was written in ink mixed with ash, because the Crown's auditors trusted grief more than they trusted merchants.

Mirabel fed three pages into the candle flame and caught them in an iron dish, crumbling them fine. Then he ground the ash into the inkstone with the back of a spoon, added a drop of the tallow, and stirred until the mixture went grey and honest-looking.

His hands were steady. That surprised him. Inside, everything was screaming in a language even the rats would have understood.

*Item: one boy, age indeterminate. Deceased. Cause: winter-fever, sudden. Remains: disposed per custom.*

He wrote it in his own hand, which was the point. If anyone came asking—and someone would come asking; Sera Vance had eyes like a hawk that had been cheated once—he would swear the child had died of the cold that was coming down out of the north weeks too early. Fever took the weak first. Everyone knew that. Everyone wanted to know it.

The lie required a body, or the absence of one explained. He solved it the way smugglers solved everything: with the tunnel.

* * *

The smugglers' passage ran beneath the Hollow's east wing, older than the guild itself, older maybe than the town—a throat of packed earth and old brick that surfaced half a mile away in the roots of a dead oak on the moor road. The guildmaster knew about it. The guildmaster knew about everything, which was why Mirabel had spent three nights learning what she did *not* know: that the second turning of the tunnel had collapsed in spring, and that the collapse could be crawled through if one was small, or thin, or willing to bleed a little at the shoulders.

The child was small. Mirabel was willing.

He came for him after midnight, carrying a cloak that smelled of camphor and a satchel of bread, dried apples, and the ledger's forged page folded against his heart like a second confession.

The cellar was cold enough that his breath showed. The boy sat in the corner of his cell with his knees drawn up, and around him, impossibly, the mice had gathered—not fleeing him but huddled near him, a grey ring of small bodies, as though he were a hearth.

They scattered when Mirabel's boots rang on the stone. The boy looked up. He didn't speak—not in any tongue Mirabel's ears could parse—but something moved across his face that needed no translation. *You came back.*

"I came back," Mirabel whispered, and then, because lying to this child felt like lying to weather, he told the truth in the only way he could. He knelt, opened the cell lock with Odile's stolen key, and said, "We're leaving. Tonight. Now. Can you be quiet?"

The boy nodded. Then he pointed at Mirabel's chest—at the pocket where the forged page lay—and made a sound. Low. Questioning. The vowels of it bent in a way human throats weren't shaped for, and yet the boy made them easily, the way other children whistled.

Mirabel didn't understand the words. But he understood the grammar of suspicion; he'd written enough of it.

"Yes," he admitted. "I lied. To keep you alive." He hesitated. "I'm sorry."

The boy studied him for a long moment with eyes the colour of deep water over stones. Then he reached out and touched Mirabel's wrist, once, deliberately, and the touch said something the words hadn't: *I know what lying is. I know what it costs. Do it anyway.*

Later—much later—Mirabel would understand that the child had just forgiven him in the First Tongue, and that forgiveness, in that language, was binding.

* * *

Nettle was waiting at the tunnel mouth, which meant either that fox-spirits heard everything or that Mirabel had been far less subtle than he'd believed.

She sat on an overturned cask in the dark, tail curled around her feet, amber eyes catching the lantern light like coins at the bottom of a well. Her indenture-collar glinted at her throat—cold iron worked with the guild's seal, a bargain made visible.

"You're doing it tonight," she said. Not a question. "During the frost-watch change. Bold. Stupid. I approve of both."

"Nettle—"

"You'll want me along." She hopped down, light as an insult. "The tunnel's second turn is collapsed, and your plan for that is presumably *hope*, which is not a plan. Also, the boy likes me. Also—" and here her voice lost some of its lacquer, "—I owe him. From the games. From before your Warden arrived and everyone started looking at him like inventory." She bared her teeth, which on Nettle was not quite a smile and not quite a threat, and often both. "Settle my debt, bookkeeper. Strike it from your ledger. That's how your kind does things, isn't it? Everything written down so it becomes real?"

Mirabel looked at her for a long moment. Debt-bound she might be, but debts ran both ways—if he freed her of this one, she'd owe *him*, and fox-spirit debts compounded like interest.

"One condition," he said. "If I fall, you take him the rest of the way. North, past the moor road, to the old forest. Whatever follows you, whatever hunts—you don't stop walking."

Nettle's ears flattened. "Charming. You've thought about failing."

"Constantly."

"Good. The ones who haven't are the ones who freeze." She jerked her chin toward the tunnel. "After you, then. Hero."

The word landed harder than she probably intended. Mirabel picked up the lantern and stepped into the dark.

* * *

The tunnel was a lesson in the guild's own history. Brick gave way to older brick, gave way to raw earth threaded with tree roots like the veins of a buried hand. Their breath fogged. Somewhere above, frost was writing its white script across the moors, and the long winter that had begun in midsummer tightened another notch.

The boy walked between them without a sound. Even his footsteps made less noise than they should have—as if the earth were listening politely and he was minding its sleep. Twice, Mirabel felt the pressure of attention pass over them from somewhere far off and vast, like the shadow of a bird at extreme altitude, and both times the boy stopped, tilted his head, and waited until it passed.

"What is that?" Mirabel breathed, the second time.

Nettle's fur had risen along her spine. "Don't ask questions the answer has to walk through you to reach."

At the second turning, the collapse was exactly as bad as the rats had reported—no, worse, because the rats had not mentioned the smell of old rot in the broken brick, nor the way the darkness beyond seemed to have texture. Mirabel set down the lantern, got on his knees, and looked at the gap. A body's width. Barely.

"I go first," Nettle said. "I'm the smallest and the least breakable. Then the boy. Then you, hero, sideways, bleeding acceptably."

It went almost according to her plan. Almost. The boy slipped through like water finding a crack. Nettle flowed after him. Mirabel went last, and the brick took its toll from his shoulders and tore his sleeve and the skin beneath, and he lay gasping in the farther dark with blood on his collarbones and the absurd, giddy laughter of the nearly-caught rising in his chest.

Then the lantern, passed through the gap behind him, illuminated the figure standing at the tunnel's end.

Sera Vance.

She stood with her back to the faint grey light of the exit, sword sheathed, Warden's coat buttoned to the throat, and her expression was the expression of a woman who had been waiting in the cold for hours and had used every one of those hours deciding something.

Mirabel came up off the ground with nothing in his hands and nowhere to go. Behind him, the boy pressed close to Nettle's side. Above them, muffled by earth and brick, the Hollow slept on, unaware that its bookkeeper was standing in a smugglers' tunnel facing the law with a forged death record against his heart.

"Warden," Mirabel said. His voice cracked on the second syllable. "I can explain."

"No, you can't." Sera's voice was flat and tired. "That's rather the problem, Lockridge. You lie badly. You've always lied badly. It's the only reason I'm here instead of Captain Dree." She took a step forward, and Mirabel flinched, and she stopped, and something complicated crossed her face. "I searched your quarters yesterday. Your ledgers. I found the ash-ink. I found the missing pages. I've known for two days that the child was going to die of a fever that hasn't happened yet."

The silence in the tunnel was total. Even the boy's breathing had gone quiet.

"So run," Sera said.

Mirabel stared at her. "What?"

"Run. Now. While I decide how long it takes me to raise the alarm." Her jaw was tight, her hands fisted at her sides, and up close Mirabel could see what exhaustion and duty had been doing to her—she looked like a rope fraying under load. "Every instinct I have says seize you both. Every instinct I have also says that what Odile Crane intends for that child is worse than anything the law will do to me for letting him walk. I served the Crown for eleven years, Lockridge. I'd like to believe there's still a difference between the law and *that*." She flicked her eyes toward the boy. "Give me a reason to keep believing it."

Mirabel's mind raced through lies and found, at last, that he was done with them. "There's something following him," he said. "Something vast. The covenant—the old covenant—is failing. The winter isn't weather. It's a death rattle. And that child may be the only thing in the world that can answer it." He swallowed. "I don't fully understand it. But I know the Court wants him caged, and I know Odile wants him sold, and I know that if we stay, he ends up as an entry in somebody's ledger forever. That's my reason. It's a poor one. It's all I have."

Sera looked at him for a long time. Snow-cold air drifted down the tunnel from the moor. Somewhere far above, a bell marked the frost-watch change.

"The dead oak on the moor road," she said finally. "Half a mile east, there's a shepherd's bothy, ruined. Nobody uses it. I'll be slow reporting my patrol. Slow by a day, maybe two." She turned as if to leave, then paused, her back to him. "Lockridge. If I find out you've hurt that child—if any of this was ever anything but what you say it is—I will hunt you to the edge of the map. That's not the law talking. That's me."

"I'd expect nothing less," Mirabel said honestly.

She climbed the worn steps to the tunnel mouth and was gone, and the grey pre-dawn light she'd blocked came flooding in after her like water.

Nettle let out a breath she'd apparently been holding for several minutes. "Well," she said. "Either we've just gained an ally, or we've just acquired a very patient predator. With Wardens it's genuinely difficult to tell."

"She let us go."

"She let us go *and told us where she'll be sleeping*." Nettle's grin flashed sharp in the lantern light. "Bookkeeper, that woman is following us. She's simply too proud to call it that."

The boy tugged Mirabel's sleeve. He pointed up the steps, toward the moor, toward the dying oak and the open world beyond it—and then he pointed at Sera's fading footprints in the frost-dust, and made a sound. Two notes. Warm ones. In any language, in every language, unmistakable:

*Bring her.*

Mirabel looked at the footprints a moment longer. Then he shouldered the satchel, took the boy's hand, and climbed up out of the earth into the freezing dawn, with a fox-spirit at his heel, a forged death cooling against his heart, and a Warden's tracks leading north ahead of them like an invitation neither of them had dared to name.

Behind them, far off across the dark fields, the first true snow of the long winter began to fall—soft, silent, and listening.
