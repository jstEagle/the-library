## 12. Wardens on the Wind

Sera Vance had learned to read weather in a soldier's way—not by clouds but by birds. When the crows lifted off a frozen field all at once, without cause, she'd been taught to assume cavalry. Tonight, on the white road north of Harrow Fen, the crows went up in silence, and there was no cavalry. There was something worse: nothing at all. The forest on either side of the track had gone quiet in that particular way quiet went when men were moving through it who did not wish to be heard.

She knew that silence the way she knew her own name.

They were two days out from the Silent Herd valley, and the child—Ember, now; Nettle had named him over a fire of wet alder wood, and the name had stuck like burrs—had begun to talk in his sleep. Not in the First Tongue, or not only. In fragments of ordinary speech he could not have learned from any of them, words in an accent a century dead, calling for someone called *Maren* to come out of the snow. Sera had heard it twice through the thin leather of her tent before she stopped pretending it didn't chill her.

Mirabel walked beside her at the head of their little column, ledger-satchel still slung across his chest as though the guild might come collecting even here, forty leagues into blighted country. Nettle trotted behind with Ember on his back, the fox-spirit's coat gone from rust to a strange frost-pale gold as the winter deepened around them, as though the season were claiming him by inches. Sera had decided not to ask about that. There was a limit to how many impossible things one Warden could interrogate in a single week.

The road bent along the shoulder of a hill where the pines grew thick and black, and Sera felt the wrongness arrive before her mind assembled it. A smell under the snow-smell. Oiled steel. Horse sweat. Woodsmoke snuffed rather than burned.

She had grown up on garrison bread and drill-yard shouting. She had served six years in the Crown's Fourth Wardens, and she knew exactly what a prepared ambush smelled like, because she had helped set enough of them.

"Off the road," she said quietly. "Now. Don't run. Running tells them we know."

Mirabel looked at her with the particular blankness of a man whose entire education had been conducted indoors among ink and animals. "Wardens?"

"Maybe." She was already moving, angling them down into a fold of ground where the snow lay deep over bracken. "Maybe worse."

It was not maybe. It took them another quarter mile of careful, breath-fogged movement before she saw what she had already known she would see: lantern-shields glinting through the trees behind them, closing the road they'd left, and ahead—a whistle, low and three-noted, the old Fourth's signal for *box formation, take them alive*.

Take them alive. That was almost funny. Someone wanted the child breathing, which meant someone above a captain had signed for this, which meant this was not a dead-end posting anymore and neither was she.

"Sera." Mirabel's voice was very steady, which she was learning meant he was terrified. "How many?"

"Twelve, if it's a full file." She drew her sword. The sound of it leaving the scabbard seemed enormous in the hushed wood. "And I know every one of them by name."

* * *

Their names came back to her in the worst way, which was one at a time, attached to faces.

Dren Halloway broke cover first, because Dren always broke cover first; he'd been doing it since they were both nineteen and getting chewed out for it in equal measure. He came through the pines with his shield high and his visor up, because he'd never once worn his visor down in her presence, some old joke about her aim, and when he saw her standing in the snow with her blade drawn between him and the fleeing shapes of a clerk, a fox, and a child, his face did something she would remember longer than the wound she was about to take.

"Vance?" His voice cracked on the second syllable. "Sera, what in the Crown's name—" He caught sight of Ember over her shoulder, small and dark-eyed against Mirabel's side, and the crack hardened. "Oh," he said. "Oh, you're with *it*."

"It" was a seven-year-old boy standing barefoot in the snow because Nettle hadn't been able to find boots his size in a burned-out village, and the snow around his feet, Sera noticed with a lurch, had melted in a neat circle, as though the cold itself were afraid to touch him.

"You have orders," she said. Her voice came out flat, parade-ground flat, the voice she'd hated in her superiors and now heard coming out of her own mouth. "I'd love to hear them. Whose seal?"

"Captain Roke's. Countermanded upward—this goes past Roke, Sera, this comes from the Citadel itself. Anomalies are to be seized. Alive." Dren's eyes flicked past her, counting. "There's a bounty clause. For cooperation."

"For turning over a child."

"For turning over the *cause* of the winter." He said it like a man reciting scripture he half believed, and that was the horror of it, that was the thing she would carry: Dren Halloway was not a cruel man. He was a believing man, and the Citadel had spent the last month giving soldiers something to believe in that was easier than starvation. "Three provinces under snow in midsummer, Sera. People are eating their seed grain. Their *dogs*. And the reports all say the same thing—it walks out of the old forest and everything with ears answers it. You've seen it. You've *seen* it."

"I've seen it," she said, "sing a dying horse to sleep."

"That's not—" He stopped. Behind him, the file was spreading through the trees in the box formation, twelve shields, crossbows unslung, and she saw the moment the arithmetic finished in his face, the moment duty became simpler than doubt. "Sera. Step aside. Please. I'll write it up that you resisted. It'll be cleaner for you."

Everything in her life had been building, apparently, to this exact sentence, delivered by a man with his visor up in a frozen wood.

She thought about the campfire outside Harrow Fen, four nights ago, when Mirabel had put his hand on a broken-down mare's neck and spoken to her in a language no court had outlawed so much as failed to understand, and the mare had lain down in the snow like a child being tucked in, and died without fear, and Sera had stood there holding her own sword and understanding that everything the law had told her about people like Mirabel Lockridge was a lie built by frightened men on top of a truth they couldn't afford. She thought about the silent herd, hundreds of animals standing motionless in a valley like a congregation waiting for a sermon, and the stag lowering its head to a child's palm.

"Cleaner for me," she repeated.

She turned her sword toward the Fourth Wardens of the Crown.

* * *

Later—much later, in the fevered arithmetic of memory—she would count the fight in heartbeats instead of moves.

One: Dren's face going gray, because he understood, in the space of a single breath, that she wasn't bluffing, and that whatever happened next could not be taken back by either of them.

Two: the crossbowman on the left flank, Vess, loosing early, the bolt hissing past Sera's ear close enough to part hair, because Vess had always been the best shot in the file and had just deliberately missed—or had he? She would never be sure, and the not-being-sure would keep her awake for years.

Three: her blade meeting Dren's shield-rim, the shock of it jarring up through her wrist into a shoulder that had never fully healed from the fall at Colder's Bridge.

Four through nine: noise. Shouting. Ember's voice rising behind her in that other language, high and clear, and the temperature dropping so fast her breath froze mid-air, and two of the crossbows icing over in their owners' hands, and someone screaming that the boy was doing *something*, and Sera shouting over her shoulder, "Run! The ravine! All of you, RUN—"

Ten: Captain Roke's voice, arriving like a landslide. She hadn't seen him come up the road, and that was her failure, hers alone; she'd counted twelve and stopped counting, because twelve was a file and files were what she knew. Roke did not fight like a file. Roke fought like a verdict. He came through the pines on a gray courser with his greatsword already moving, and he did not announce himself, and he did not hesitate, and the first thing she knew of him was Dren shouting her name—

Eleven: she got her parry up. Mostly up. Steel met steel and then steel met the gap below her pauldron, and the world went white and then red and then very, very quiet, the way the forest had been quiet, and she was on her knees in the snow watching her blood make a small dark flower on the white and thinking, distantly, professionally, *left side, below the arm, deep, bad, very bad.*

Twelve: Nettle.

She had never seen the fox-spirit move at speed. He was always ambling, always grinning, always three steps behind the action with a comment ready. What came out of the treeline was not ambling. It was a smear of frost-gold fury, and it hit Captain Roke's horse like a thrown torch, and the horse screamed and went down, and Roke went down with it, and Nettle stood over Sera in the wreck of the ambush with his teeth bared at eleven armed men and his fur burning with cold-fire and said, in a voice she had never heard him use, a voice like ice cracking on a lake:

"The next one of you who touches her, I will show you what my kind did before your covenant. I remember it. Do you want to remember it too?"

And the Fourth Wardens of the Crown—the pride of the Citadel, the terror of the borderlands—looked at a bleeding traitor, a screaming child, a clerk with a ledger-satchel, and a fox, and they *hesitated*, and the hesitation was the whole battle, right there, won and lost in the same instant.

"Go," Dren said hoarsely. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the snow around Ember's feet, at the circle of melt, at the small boy staring at the armed men with an expression that held no fear at all and far too much sorrow. "Go, Sera. Before Roke gets up. *Go.*"

She tried to stand. The left side of her body declined the invitation, politely and absolutely, and the snow came up to meet her face, and the last things she registered were Mirabel's arms under her shoulders, swearing in three languages including one that made the nearby pines shiver, and Nettle saying something in the First Tongue that made the ravine open its shadows like a door, and then the dark took the accounting out of her hands.

* * *

She dreamed of the drill yard at the Citadel. Snow falling on the practice squares. Dren at nineteen, laughing, visor up. Her instructor's voice: *A Warden's word is the wall, Vance. Everything else is weather.* And then the dream changed, the way dreams do, and the wall was the covenant-stone she'd never seen, and it was cracking, and through the cracks came not water but silence, silence pouring out over the fields like floodwater, and everything it touched forgot how to speak, and she was trying to hold the wall together with her hands and her hands were full of snow that wouldn't stop bleeding—

She woke to pain like a struck bell and a ceiling of woven branches.

Someone had made a shelter. A proper one—cut boughs laid over a frame, packed snow in the gaps, a fire burning low and smokeless in a stone ring, the kind of fire you built when you didn't want to be found. Her left side was bound tight, bandages stiff with some resinous salve that smelled like pine and something older underneath, something that made her wound hum instead of scream. She was lying on cloaks. Someone had given her the cloaks.

"She wakes," Nettle said. He was sitting cross-legged by the fire, and in the firelight his frost-pale coat had gone back to rust, mostly, except at the tips of his ears, which stayed white, and she suspected would stay white forever, a mark of the night's spending. "Don't sit up. If you sit up, I'll have to be impressive again, and I'm very tired of being impressive. Do you know what it costs me to throw a captain's horse? No, of course you don't. Nobody ever asks about the costs. Everyone asks about the tricks."

"How long?"

"A day. Maybe two days of lying here while your insides argue about whether to stay inside." He fed a stick to the fire. "You lost a great deal of blood, Warden. Fortunately, you had a great deal to lose. Very overstocked. Like a granary."

Through the shelter's opening she could see dusk, and snow, and Mirabel sitting a little apart with Ember asleep against his side, the boy's bare feet tucked into Mirabel's coat. Mirabel looked up, and across the distance between them he did something she had never seen him do in all the weeks since the gate: he bowed his head. Not a courtier's bow. The other kind. The kind you give someone who has paid for you.

"He hasn't slept," Nettle said, following her gaze. "Neither has the cub. The cub keeps asking if you're going to die, and Mirabel keeps saying no, and the animals keep telling the cub things about your body that Mirabel can't hear anymore without wanting to be sick, so it's all been very restful." He glanced at her sidelong, firelight catching the amber of his eyes. "Do you want the honest version of your situation, or the Warden version? They differ mainly in adjectives."

"Honest."

"Honest is: another day of riding and the wound opens and you die in the snow, and the cub feels it happen through every bird in ten miles, and I don't know what that does to a child who is apparently the last thread of the oldest bargain in the world, and I don't want to find out. Honest is also: there is a place. Half a day north. Under a hill. The old folk called it a listening-house, back when there were old folk who remembered such things." Nettle's tail curled around his feet, and for once the grin was entirely absent from his face. "My grandmother's grandmother denned near it, before the Sundering. She said there was a woman there who kept a lamp lit for travelers who spoke the First Tongue. She said the woman was old *then*."

Sera stared at him. "That was centuries ago."

"Yes," Nettle agreed pleasantly. "Isn't it wonderful? Pack the sled at first light, Warden. You're going to meet someone older than my family's grudges, and believe me, that is *old*."

Beyond the fire, Mirabel shifted, and Ember murmured in his sleep—that dead-century voice again, calling for Maren—and Sera lay still in the borrowed warmth and understood that the wall she had been all her life, the law, the file, the word of the Crown, was behind her now, broken open, and everything else was weather.

Half a day north, something under a hill was keeping a lamp lit.

She closed her eyes, and let the pain carry her toward it.
