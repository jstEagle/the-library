## 13. The House Under the Hill

The hill did not look like a door until it was one.

Mirabel had walked past such mounds all his life—sheep-grazed hummocks scattered through the northern fells, the kind children dared each other to climb and shepherds used as windbreaks. This one sat a little apart from its neighbors, wearing a crown of rowan trees that should not have survived this far above the treeline, and it wore them the way an old woman wears her last good shawl: deliberately, and against all sense.

Nettle stopped at the base of the slope, Sera a dead weight across his back, and sniffed the air with an expression Mirabel had learned to distrust.

"She knows we're coming," the fox-spirit said. "She's known since the Warden bled on her threshold-moss, actually. She's deciding whether to be annoyed."

"Can you blame her?" Mirabel shifted Ember higher on his hip. The boy had grown lighter these past weeks in a way that frightened him—not thinner so much as *less*, as if the long winter were drawing him out of the world thread by thread. He slept too much. When he woke, he spoke less and less in any tongue Mirabel knew, and more and more in the First Tongue, murmuring to things that were not there. Or perhaps, Mirabel thought grimly, they *were* there, and simply starving like everything else.

"Blame is for people with choices," Nettle said, and started up the hill.

There was no door. There was a hawthorn growing at an angle no honest tree would choose, and when Nettle pushed past its branches the hill opened along a seam Mirabel's eyes kept sliding off—a passage lined with stone so old it had gone soft at the edges, worn into curves like river rock. Warmth breathed up from below. Not the desperate heat of a banked fire, but the patient warmth of earth that has been tended.

Lamps burned in niches along the descending stair. Real lamps, with real oil, in a realm where three provinces' worth of winter had swallowed midsummer.

"How?" Sera rasped from Nettle's back. Her wound had been festering despite Mirabel's poultices; her voice came out sanded down to the grain.

"The house keeps what the world forgets," Nettle said. "Warmth. Oil. Names. She's been banking this place like a hearth for—" he paused, calculating in that way he had that made centuries sound like grocery debts, "—a very long time."

The stair opened into a round chamber, and Mirabel stopped breathing.

Every wall was covered in thread.

Not tapestries—*threads*. Thousands upon thousands of them, fine as spider silk, running from pegs and combs and the teeth of old harps, crossing and recrossing the circular room in patterns that shifted when he wasn't looking directly at them. Each strand caught the lamplight differently: some gold, some gray, some black as a closed eye. And threaded through them all—one among thousands, but he saw it the moment he entered, the way you see your own name written on a page—was a single filament of light that pulsed.

Faint. Thready. Stubborn as a candle flame cupped in two hands in a gale.

"That's it," Mirabel said. His voice cracked. "That's the covenant."

"It's one thread of it," said a voice from the room's far side. "I've kept the others alive by remembering them. But memory is not substance, young man, and I am nearly out of substance myself."

She was smaller than her voice. Ancient beyond argument—skin like vellum gone amber, eyes milk-pale but fixed on them with terrible accuracy, hands knotted around a spindle that held no wool. She sat in a chair grown from the living root of the hill itself, and she looked at each of them in turn: Nettle, whom she greeted with a nod of grudging respect; Sera, whose wound she assessed in one flick of those blind pale eyes; Ember—

At Ember she went very still.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor lamb. You're Maren's."

"You know the child," Sera managed.

"I knew his mother." The old woman rose, joints crackling like green wood in fire, and crossed the room. Ember slid down from Mirabel's hip and stood before her, and the two of them regarded each other—the last speaker of the old world and whatever the boy was, which even now Mirabel could not put a word to—and then she gathered him in with both arms and held him, and Ember began to cry. Not the dead-century voice. A child's crying, seven years old and no older, and Mirabel had to turn away because it hurt worse than anything the Wardens had done.

"He has been calling for her in his sleep," Mirabel said quietly, when he trusted himself. "For Maren."

"For all of us." She stroked the boy's hair. "He calls in the Tongue, and the Tongue does not distinguish between the dead and the absent and the sleeping. Sit down, all of you. There is broth. There will be answers. And then," her mouth folded into something too dry to call bitter, "there will be the part where I tell you what it costs, and one of you is foolish enough to pay it. There always is. I've watched it happen before."

The broth was barley and roots and tasted like the best thing Mirabel had ever eaten, because everything did, lately; hunger had become the fifth member of their company. They ate in a lower chamber where the walls were plain stone shelved with jars—oil, salt honey, dried apples from a summer that felt like rumor—and the Last Speaker, who told them to call her Maren's grandmother and nothing else, watched Ember demolish a third bowl with an expression Mirabel recognized. He wore it himself, every time the boy slept through a night.

"His mother came to me nineteen years ago," she said. "Pregnant, half-frozen, hunted by both realms for reasons neither would explain to her. She had done something forbidden: she had fallen in love across the boundary. A man of the hollow places—yes, Warden, they exist, your Crown simply files them under *unconfirmed*—and from that union came Ember, born with a voice that belongs wholly to neither world and therefore wholly to both."

"The anchor," Sera said. Her color was bad but her mind was working; Mirabel was learning to read the pain behind her economy of words. "The covenant needs an anchor. That's why the Court wants him."

"The covenant needs *anchors*. Plural." The old woman drew a thread of yarn from her pocket, held it taut between two knotted fingers. "When I was young—when the world was younger—we spoke the binding every generation, renewing it, two voices, one called from each side of the veil, willingly bound for the speaking. My partner died. I did not renew. I thought one missed generation would matter little." The thread snapped. She let the halves fall. "It mattered. The covenant began to fray the way a rope frays—strand by strand, invisibly, until the load comes and the whole thing parts. Your silent beasts. Your midsummer snow. The famine-wraiths. All symptoms of a rope going slack between two worlds that were never meant to crush each other, and now, without it, may."

"And the ritual," Mirabel said. His heart was already ahead of him, dreading the shape of what was coming, the way you dread the last pages of certain kinds of stories. "To reforge it."

"The words must be spoken in the Hollow's heart, at the covenant-stone, by two voices." She looked at Ember. "One of each realm. One must belong to the hollow places—by blood or by bond. He does. And one must belong wholly to this world, and here is the terrible arithmetic, child:" she turned those blind eyes on Mirabel, and he felt seen straight through, ledgered, appraised, "the voices must be *willingly bound*. Bound to one another for the speaking. Two strands twisted into one rope, taking the whole weight of two realms. A trained Speaker spends forty years learning to hold a single thread safely."

Ember cannot do it alone. She didn't say it. She didn't have to.

"A single untrained voice," Mirabel said, very evenly, "would shatter."

"It would burn out of him like a flood through a dry streambed," she said. "It would take everything he is and scatter it into both realms, and the winter would go on, and worse than winter." She let that sit. Then, almost gently: "Unless a second voice binds to his and shares the load. Someone of this world. Someone willing. Someone who can learn enough of the Tongue to answer when he sings—to *hold*, while he pours."

Sera was already shaking her head. "Then we teach someone. You. You've kept a thread of it alive for—"

"I am ninety-one years old, Warden, and I have been dying politely for a decade. I could not walk to the Hollow's heart, let alone speak there." Her mouth quirked. "And no one else in this room qualifies. The fox is of the other realm. You are Crown law down to your marrow, and the binding would taste that lie and reject you."

"What about—" Sera stopped. Looked at Mirabel. Stopped again.

Mirabel set down his bowl.

He had known, he realized. Since the Silent Herd, maybe. Since the night Ember first answered the dogs. Some part of him had been walking toward this hearth for weeks, counting the cost in advance the way he counted guild debts, and had arrived at the total long ago and filed it away under *later*.

"I'm a beast-speaker," he said. "Illegally. Since I was six years old. I hid it my whole life because the Sundering made my gift a hanging crime, and now you're telling me the only thing standing between the world and eternal winter is a Speaker of this realm who can learn the First Tongue fast enough to stand beside him." He looked at the old woman. "How fast?"

Her expression did something complicated—pity and respect and grief braided together.

"Words of the First Tongue are not learned," she said. "They are *paid for*. Every word takes a memory to root it. Small words, small memories. The deep grammar—the words you'd need at the covenant-stone—those take larger payments. It is how I have lived so long and remember so little of why." She spread her knotted hands. "You would arrive at the stone fluent, and hollowed, and if the binding holds you would survive it, changed. If it fails—"

"If it fails, there won't be anyone left to file a complaint," Nettle said lightly, from the corner where he was washing his paws. He wasn't looking at anyone. His ears were flat.

"Mirabel." Sera's voice. Low. "Don't answer yet. Don't—"

"There's a child asleep upstairs holding a rope that's about to snap," Mirabel said. "There's no version of *yet* that helps."

He climbed to the upper chamber alone that night, after Ember woke crying for Maren and would not settle for anyone else.

The threads filled the dark with faint light, and the covenant-filament pulsed among them, weaker than it had been at noon. He could feel it now, having stood this close to the truth—like standing near a door left ajar in January. Cold coming through. The boy sat wrapped in blankets in the old woman's chair, hiccupping the tail end of his tears, and reached for Mirabel without a word.

Mirabel lifted him onto his lap. For a long time neither spoke. Below, he could hear Sera and Nettle arguing in low voices—he caught his own name twice, and the word *fool* once, and couldn't tell which of them was aiming it where.

"Ember." He hesitated. Then, in the handful of First Tongue Nettle had taught him on the road—words that had cost him a childhood afternoon and the smell of his first workshop—he asked, clumsily, *are you afraid?*

The boy considered this with grave, ancient attention. Then he answered in kind, slow, shaping the sounds carefully so Mirabel's halting ear could follow:

*Yes. But grandmother says afraid and ready can live in the same house.*

Mirabel laughed—something cracked open in his chest and came out as laughter—and pulled the boy close.

"Then we'll build it together," he said in his own tongue, knowing Ember understood anyway. Outside, snow fell on the rowan-crowned hill, steady and endless, and somewhere north beneath the ice, the Hollow's heart waited with its stone, and its price, and its arithmetic.

In the morning, the Last Speaker spread her hands over a spindle and said, "We begin. Give me a memory, child—something small. Show me you understand what you're buying."

Mirabel closed his eyes, reached into the crowded ledger of his life, and chose the first line to strike.
