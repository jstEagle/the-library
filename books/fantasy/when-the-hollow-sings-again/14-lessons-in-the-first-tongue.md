## 14. Lessons in the First Tongue

Nettle had lived three hundred years by one rule: never teach anyone anything for free.

It wasn't stinginess. It was arithmetic. Every bargain a fox-spirit ever struck began with a gift given too freely, and Nettle's own debt to the Gilded Hollow had begun exactly that way—a favor for a traveler, a kindness on a frozen road, and then Odile Crane holding the paper with his name on it like a knife held at the throat of everything he was. So he hoarded. He hoarded secrets and songs and the small clever tricks that kept a fox alive in a world built of traps.

Which made it strange, even to him, that he taught Mirabel Lockridge the first word without being asked.

* * *

The shrine under the hill smelled of cold stone and older prayers. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of a thing that had been counting since before the Sundering. Sera Vance lay fevered on a pallet of pine boughs near the shrine's inner wall, her bandages changed twice a day by hands that pretended not to tremble, and Ember sat beside her for hours at a stretch, humming to her in the First Tongue as though sound alone could knit flesh.

Perhaps it could. The fever broke a little each dawn.

But the child could not do what needed doing alone. The Last Speaker had been clear about that, in her voice like dry leaves settling: *two voices, willingly bound.* And Mirabel Lockridge—ledger-keeper, liar of no talent, man who flinched when dogs barked—was going to have to learn to speak a language that had died with the old world.

Nettle found him in the shrine's outer chamber on the third evening, sitting cross-legged before the Last Speaker's stone, turning a copper coin over and over in his fingers. A habit, Nettle knew. Bookkeepers touched money the way other men touched talismans.

"You're going to ask me," Nettle said, "and I'm going to say it can't be done, and you're going to ask again, and eventually I'll say yes, so let's skip to the part where I say yes."

Mirabel looked up. In the shrine's dim amber light—the light came from nowhere, from the stone itself, from the memory of fire—he looked hollowed out and stubborn all at once, which Nettle was learning was simply his face.

"The Last Speaker says I need the First Tongue. Really need it. Not the handful of sounds I've picked up from Ember like a magpie picking up buttons."

"She's right."

"Then teach me."

"There's a price."

"I know there's a price." Mirabel set the coin down flat, deliberately, the way he closed a ledger. "There's always a price. I keep books, Nettle. I've never once seen an entry that didn't balance."

"No," Nettle said, circling him once, fox-quick even in his man-shape, "you don't understand. You think it's coin. You think it's years. You think it's something you can count and pay and walk away from." He stopped in front of the man and let his eyes go amber, let the shape of him waver the way it did when he wasn't bothering to hold it still. "The First Tongue isn't learned. It's remembered. It's the language both realms spoke before they split, and it doesn't live in minds anymore—it lives in the space between them. To speak it, you have to open that space inside yourself. And the space has to come from somewhere."

"From where?"

"From you. One memory per word. The tongue takes what holds your life together and unpicks a thread for every stitch it gives back."

Mirabel was quiet for a long moment. The drip of water counted through the silence.

"How many words?"

"For the binding?" Nettle laughed, low and bitter. "The Last Speaker used nine. She's had sixty years to forget how much she paid. I'd guess you'll need more. You're not her. You weren't raised half in both worlds."

"Then we start now," Mirabel said, "while Sera sleeps, while the road is quiet, while the winter hasn't finished eating the world." He spread his empty hands. "I've read every contract in the Gilded Hollow, Nettle. I know what it looks like when someone signs away more than they understand. So explain it to me until I understand. Then I'll sign anyway."

And that—that was the moment Nettle decided the lesson would be free after all. Not free of price; nothing was. Free of *charge*. He would take no cut, no favor, no paper with a name on it. Some debts you paid forward because someone once paid yours badly, and you wanted the story to end differently this time.

"All right," he said. "First word. Give me something small to start. A memory you won't miss."

Mirabel smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin and brave. "No one misses anything until it's gone. That's rather the point. But—" he thought, "—my seventh birthday. There was a cake with candied violets on it. My mother sang. I remember the song better than the day, honestly."

"Small," Nettle agreed. "Cheap. Good for practice." He knelt, mirroring the man's posture, and held out one hand, palm up. "Take my hand. Say the word after me, and when it settles, let the memory go. Don't fight it. Fighting makes it take two."

"What's the word?"

"*Vessh,*" Nettle said, and the syllable moved through the chamber like something waking. The dripping water stuttered. The amber light leaned toward them. Somewhere behind the walls, in the dark veins of the hill, something old turned over in its sleep.

"It means *listen*," Nettle said. "Or *hear-truly*. The distinction matters less than you'd think."

Mirabel took his hand. His grip was cold and steady.

"*Vessh.*"

The word left Nettle's mouth and entered Mirabel's like a key entering a lock—and Nettle felt the lock turn, felt the man's mind open along a seam he hadn't known was there. He watched, with a fox's clear sight, as the memory rose: a kitchen, steam, a small boy in a too-large shirt, a cake studded with purple flowers, a woman singing off-key and unashamed. It hung between them for three heartbeats, whole and warm and utterly ordinary.

Then it unraveled.

Not violently. Thread by thread, like a sweater caught on a nail, like fog lifting off a pond at morning. The singing went first, then the faces, then the taste of sugar, and last of all the simple certainty that such a day had ever happened at all. What remained was a smooth blank place in Mirabel's history, a socket where a tooth had been, and into that socket slid the word *vessh*, glowing faintly, fitting perfectly, home.

Mirabel opened his eyes. They were wet, but he was smiling.

"That's it? That's the price?"

"That's the price for the smallest word in the language." Nettle released his hand. "Say it back."

"*Vessh.*"

The water in the walls resumed its counting. The light settled. And Nettle heard—not with ears, with the older sense—the word sit in the man's chest like a swallowed coal, warm and permanent.

"One," Nettle said. "Down. Now give me another memory, and this time don't pick the cheapest one. Pick one you're willing to lose *knowing* you'll lose it. The tongue can tell the difference between a sacrifice and a discard. It charges extra for discards."

Mirabel stared at him. "You're joking."

"I never joke about prices. Ask anyone who's ever regretted dealing with me."

* * *

They worked through the nights, because nights were when Sera slept and when the hill itself seemed to lean in and listen.

The second word cost him the face of the schoolmaster who had first taught him letters—an old man named Pellin, ink-stained, patient, dead thirty years. Mirabel described him carefully beforehand, the way a man might describe a photograph he intended to burn: the wart on his left knuckle, the way he tapped the slate twice before scolding, the smell of chalk and pipe smoke. Then he spoke the word—*sauren*, *to-open*—and watched the description become a stranger's description, accurate and meaningless, a face he could name but no longer see.

The third word took the name of the street he'd grown up on. The fourth, the taste of the first apple of every autumn of his childhood—all the apples at once, which he hadn't anticipated, and which made him laugh in a startled, wounded way that echoed strangely off the shrine stones.

"Apples," he said, shaking his head. "Of everything. Apples."

"The tongue takes what's woven deepest," Nettle said. "You don't get to choose the threads, only offer the cloth."

By the fifth night he had seven words. By the ninth, eleven. Ember would sometimes sit at the edge of the lamplight, watching, and when Mirabel mastered a particularly difficult sound—one that required the throat and the breath and something behind the breastbone to move together, a sound with no letters—the child would clap his small hands once, silently, and grin.

Sera slept through most of it. When she woke, she noticed.

* * *

It happened on the twelfth night. She had insisted on being propped up against the wall to watch, gray-faced, one arm bound tight against her chest, her Warden's eyes doing what Warden's eyes did: cataloguing.

"You look different," she said to Mirabel.

"Do I?"

"Calmer." Her gaze narrowed. "Lighter. Like a house with the furniture sold off." She turned to Nettle. "What is it costing him? Exactly. I want the number."

"You couldn't read the number," Nettle said. "It's not in any ledger."

"Try me. I audit people for a living."

So Nettle told her—or some of it. The birthday. The schoolmaster. The street. The apples. He watched her face as he listed them, watched the Warden-coldness crack along familiar lines, because Sera Vance had spent her career pricing things too: sentences for crimes, years for treasons, blood for oaths. She understood ledgers. She just hated this one.

"That's barbaric," she said quietly.

"It's honest," Nettle said. "Every magic in the world lies about its price. This one writes it down where you can see it. I respect that."

"And how many words does he need?"

Nettle looked at Mirabel, who was watching the exchange with an expression of polite, terrible readiness, the expression of a man reading the terms of a contract he fully intends to sign.

"More than he has memories to spare," Nettle said. "That's all any of us need to know tonight."

Sera was silent a long time. Then she said, to Mirabel, "When you can't remember why you're doing this—because that day will come, Lockridge, I've seen men pay past the point of purpose—look at the boy. He's the reason. Write it somewhere if you have to."

"He can't write it anywhere," Nettle said softly. "Paper burns. Ledgers burn. That's rather the problem we're running from."

"Then look at him," Sera said. "Every day. Before you forget why looking matters."

Mirabel met her eyes, and nodded once, and Nettle thought: *there it is. The shape of the ending. He's already seen it, and he's walking toward it anyway.*

Foxes know that walk. Foxes make that walk themselves, into traps, into winters, into mouths. The difference between courage and stupidity is only whether the thing you walk toward is worth more than the thing you leave behind, and Nettle had spent three centuries unsure of the arithmetic.

He was not unsure anymore. Watching Mirabel open his mouth for the thirteenth word, watching him offer up—deliberately, clearly, with his eyes open—the memory of his mother's voice, Nettle felt the old grief of his own bargains rise in his throat like smoke.

Because he knew what came next. He had seen the Last Speaker's face. He had heard the emptiness beneath her words, the vast quiet rooms where a life used to be. The First Tongue did not stop taking when you had enough words. It stopped taking when there was nothing left but the speaking.

And Mirabel Lockridge, thirteen words richer, twelve memories poorer, smiled at Ember across the lamplight and said, in a language older than hunger:

*Tomorrow. We continue tomorrow.*

Behind them, far to the north, the wind changed—and carried, faint and wrong, the sound of many boots moving through snow.
