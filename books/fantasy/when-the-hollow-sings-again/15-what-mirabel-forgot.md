## 15. What Mirabel Forgot

The shrine's back chamber had become a schoolroom, though no school in the realm would have recognized it. A single candle stub on the floor. A circle of salt and ash drawn by the Last Speaker's own trembling hand. And Mirabel Lockridge kneeling at its edge with his palms flat against the stone, paying.

The word was *vessh*. It meant, roughly, *the moment before the door opens*—anticipation, threshold, the held breath between what is and what comes. Nettle pronounced it for him three times, tail curled around her feet, her amber eyes watchful in the way of a creature who has watched too many bargains go wrong.

"Again," she said.

"*Vessh.*"

The memory left him like water through cupped hands.

He didn't feel it go—that was the cruelty of it. He only felt the shape of the hole afterward, running his mind along the gap the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. Something had been there. Something about a door. He was almost certain it had mattered to someone, once.

"Nettle." His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "What did I just lose?"

She looked away. "I don't ask anymore," she said. "It's kinder that way."

* * *

He kept a list. It was the ledger-keeper in him; he could not help it. On the flyleaf of a stolen account book, in his small precise hand, he recorded each payment:

*Day one: the smell of rain on the guildhouse roof. Day two: my seventh birthday—the cake was honey, I think. Day three: Master Ellory's face. He taught me numbers. I remember being taught.*

Twelve entries, then fourteen. The words came easier now, sliding into him like keys into oiled locks, and the memories went easier too, small bright coins spent without counting. He told himself he was choosing carefully. He told himself he could afford these.

Then Ember began asking questions he couldn't answer with simple words, and Nettle said, gently, that he needed the grammar of grief, and the grammar of grief cost more than nouns ever had.

*My mother's voice.*

He sat with that one for a long time before he paid it. He made himself say the memory whole, first, alone in the dark of the side chapel where they'd laid Sera's bandages and spare blades: her voice, low and cracked from winter coughs, singing the threshing song off-key because she always came in half a beat late. *Row and bind, row and bind, the field remembers what we find—*

"I'll keep the song," he told the dark. "I'll keep the words of it. Just not—not the sound of her."

The First Tongue did not negotiate. It took the sound and left the lyric, a tune with no instrument, a letter with no hand that wrote it. Afterward he knew that his mother had sung to him. He knew she'd been off-key. He knew he had loved it.

He could not, for the life of him, remember why.

* * *

They noticed. Of course they noticed. You cannot hollow out a man quietly; the quiet itself is the tell.

Sera saw it first, because Sera saw everything. She was mending again—Nettle's carrying had saved her life but torn the wound wider, and she healed slow and furious, pacing the shrine's upper room like a wolf in too small a pen. She watched Mirabel across the evening fires with an expression he'd learned to read as Warden arithmetic: threat assessment, ongoing.

"You laughed at nothing tonight," she said, on the fourth evening after the mother-memory. They were alone; Ember slept curled against the warm flanks of the stag that had followed them out of the silent valley and never left. "Ember dropped his bowl. You laughed. Then you just—stopped. Like you'd forgotten what laughing was for halfway through."

"Did I?" Mirabel fed the fire a stick. "I'm tired, Sera. We're all tired."

"That's not what this is." She shifted, wincing, her hand pressed below her ribs. "I've interrogated men who sold their own children. I know what a person looks like when they're spending something they can't get back." Her voice dropped. "What is it costing you? Truly. Not the version you gave us."

And here was the terrible ease of it: the lie assembled itself without effort, smooth as a balanced column of figures. He had lied badly to her once, at the Hollow's gate, and she had seen straight through him. But that Mirabel had still been whole, all his guilt and fear rattling loose inside him where anyone could hear.

This Mirabel had fewer loose parts.

"The lessons are hard," he said. "That's all. The language doesn't sit in the mind easy. It reshapes things around itself." True enough. True enough to pass any test she'd know how to give.

Sera studied him for a long moment, firelight moving across her scarred face. He met her eyes steadily. Calmly. He was aware, distantly, of how calm he was, the way a man is aware of standing on ice.

"All right," she said finally. But she didn't believe him—he could see that she didn't—and she let it lie anyway, which frightened him more than an accusation would have. Wardens filed things away. Wardens waited.

When she'd gone up to her bedroll, he added a new line to his hidden ledger, beneath the words already written there:

*Day seventeen: the reason I hid my gift.*

That one hadn't been his choice. The grammar of binding demanded it—the old covenant's syntax required a speaker who understood concealment, who knew in his bones why a gift like his must stay buried, or the binding words rang false. So when the lesson called for it, he had reached for the deepest thing he owned and paid it over without letting himself think.

He knew he had hidden his gift since boyhood. He knew there had been a reason—a specific reason, sharp enough to teach a child silence.

The page where that reason lived was blank now. He ran his fingers over the empty space and felt nothing at all, and it was the nothing that scared him, when anything still could. Grief, at least, was a kind of remembering.

* * *

"He's fading," Nettle said.

She said it to Sera, in the shrine doorway at dawn, not bothering to lower her voice, because fox-spirits hold that privacy is a debt owed only to those who pay it back. Mirabel stood at the well in the yard below, drawing water, and heard every word.

"Not dying," Nettle went on. "Worse. Dying leaves a body. This leaves—" her muzzle wrinkled, searching for the word, "—room. He's making room in himself, Sera. For the words. And the room doesn't fill back in."

"How many has he given?"

"Counting last night? Twenty-six. He started with pebbles—birthdays, faces, small hurts. Now he's paying in stones from the foundation." Nettle's tails lashed once, sharply. "There's a price past which the man who walks into the Hollow's heart won't be the man who walked out of the guildhouse. And he knows it. That's the worst part. He's doing the sums. He's decided the answer is acceptable."

Sera was silent a long moment. Below, the well chain clinked, rhythmic and unhurried.

"And we just let him?"

"We need him." Flat. Bitter. "Two voices, one of each realm, willingly bound. The Last Speaker was clear. Ember can't carry the covenant alone—one untrained voice will shatter, and take the world's winter with it, or worse. We have exactly one other speaker. I have exactly one debt I can't walk away from." Nettle looked at her own paws. "I indentured myself to save my own hide once. I know what willing costs. He's about to learn it better than I ever did."

"Then we make him stop."

"With what?" Nettle's laugh was dry as frost. "Chains? He'd slip them. Pity?" Her amber eyes cut upward. "You've seen how he looks at Ember. You'd take the boy's whole future rather than spend one of his?"

Sera didn't answer. Below, Mirabel set down the full bucket, straightened, and looked—at the doorway, at them, though the angle was wrong and the light was bad—with that new, untroubled calm, and smiled, and lifted a hand in greeting.

Neither of them waved back.

* * *

That night, Ember climbed into his lap by the fire, the way he did now, boneless with trust in the way of children and animals and no one else.

"Tell me the story," the boy said, in the First Tongue. "The one about the fox who stole the sun."

So Mirabel told it—in the old language, fluently, beautifully, twenty-eight payments deep—and somewhere in the middle of it he reached for the part where his own mother used to tell him that same story, and found the shelf bare.

He knew there had been telling. He knew it the way you know a room has been lived in after the furniture's gone: marks on the floor, pale rectangles where pictures hung. Ember watched his face and something in the boy's expression flickered—the beasts and the beast-speakers both knew when a voice went hollow mid-word.

"Mira," Ember said softly, using the name he'd made for him. "You went away."

"Just for a moment." He smoothed the boy's hair. "I'm here."

But it wasn't entirely true, and the child knew it, and Mirabel knew the child knew it, and none of them said anything, because there were only so many nights left and the road north was long and the wind was rising.

Later—when Ember slept, when Sera kept her watch with her sword across her knees and her eyes on Mirabel instead of the treeline—he opened his little ledger one final time that night and read down the column of losses. Twenty-eight lines. Some of them were just labels now; the things themselves were gone, and even the labels felt like they belonged to a stranger's inventory.

At the bottom, in fresh ink, he wrote the next day's intended payment, because a ledger-keeper plans ahead:

*Day twenty-nine: the face of the woman I—*

He stopped. Stared.

There was a woman. There was a debt of feeling, vast and specific, attached to a face he could no longer see. Love, certainly—love of some kind, some shape. But whose face? A sweetheart from his youth? Someone he'd failed? The memory of the feeling remained, orphaned, pointing at nothing.

He searched backward through himself and could not find her name. Could not find whether he'd ever spoken it aloud. Somewhere in the last week, unlisted, unwitnessed, the First Tongue had taken a payment he had never agreed to—or one he'd agreed to so casually he'd never thought to write it down—and it had taken more than he meant to give.

His hand, he noticed, was completely steady.

That was the dread, then. Not fear—he still knew what fear was, the way you know a country from a map—but the absence arriving early, filling him room by room, preparing him to be a vessel instead of a man. The Last Speaker's face rose in his mind: those vast quiet rooms behind her eyes. He had pitied her once.

He was becoming her, one unremarkable night at a time, and he had perhaps a dozen payments left before the man named Mirabel Lockridge was all ledger and no goods.

He closed the book. He banked the fire. He lay down beside the sleeping child who might be the covenant's last anchor, and he made his plans with perfect calm, and far above the shrine, through the broken roof, the stars wheeled north toward the Hollow's heart.

In the morning, Sera stood in the doorway watching him saddle the horses with efficient, gentle movements, and she said his name—just his name, nothing else—and the way she said it was the way people speak at gravesides.

"Ready?" she asked.

"Of course," Mirabel said, smiling. "I've never been more ready for anything."

And it was true. That was the horror of it. It was entirely, peacefully true.

Behind them, on the southern wind, came the first smell of smoke.
