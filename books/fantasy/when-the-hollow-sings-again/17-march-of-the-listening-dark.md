## 17. March of the Listening Dark

Sera Vance had been a Warden long enough to know what a forest sounded like when it was pretending.

This one was good at it. Wind moved through the black pines in long, rehearsed sighs, and somewhere off to the left a creek muttered under its lid of ice, and if you didn't know better you'd have called it peaceful. But she'd stopped knowing better around noon, when the birds had gone quiet all at once—not scattered into silence the way they did when a hawk crossed the sun, but all together, like a choir cut mid-note—and hadn't started again.

The cold was wrong too. It had a texture now, a grain, like something growing. Three days north of the shrine and the frost no longer melted off her cloak by midmorning; it thickened instead, feathering out from her breath in shapes that almost looked like writing.

"Ember." She crouched beside the boy at the edge of the treeline, keeping her voice level the way you did around spooked horses and lit fuses. "Tell me what you hear."

Ember tilted his head, listening with more than his ears. He'd done it constantly since the shrine, since the Last Speaker had pressed her thread of the old covenant into his small hands and told him—gently, terribly—that he was the last living anchor of everything. He listened like someone who'd just learned the world had been talking about him his whole life.

*They are hungry,* he signed, then spoke it aloud in the trade-tongue, halting but clear. Nettle had been drilling him. *Not men-hungry. Hollow-hungry.*

"The famine-wraiths," Mirabel said behind her. He stood with his ledger satchel slung across his chest like a bandolier, which would have been funny if the man wasn't so plainly terrified. "The things born where the covenant's fraying. The Last Speaker said—"

"I remember what she said." Sera rose. The wraiths came from the places between, the speaker had told them, from the seams where human voices and beast voices used to meet. They were what hunger looked like when you took the hunger out of a wolf and the grief out of a winter and let them walk. "How close?"

Mirabel closed his eyes. She'd watched him do this a dozen times now, reach out along whatever invisible threads the law said didn't exist, and every time her oath turned over in her chest like a stone in a boot. Every time, she let him.

*Rats,* Ember added, pointing down. *Under the snow. They say the dark thing walks ahead of us. And behind.* His face did something complicated. *Behind is closer.*

* * *

They made two miles before the first blade rang.

It came out of the trees at the frozen creek—a hired blade, Odile's make, the kind of mercenary who wore mismatched armor and killed for coin with a craftsman's economy. Then a second. A third. Not Wardens. Sera noted that with a strange, sour relief even as she drew steel: these were guild knives, bought and paid for by the woman whose ledger Mirabel had burned pages of to fake a child's death.

"Down!" she shouted, and the fight opened like a wound.

She took the first man on the parry and put him into the creek ice, which held, which was worse for him. The second circled toward Mirabel and the boy, and Nettle got there first—the fox-spirit moving in that wrong, liquid way of hers, all suggestion and teeth, her borrowed shape fraying at the edges as she snarled something in a language that made the mercenary's horse scream and bolt.

Sera had fought skirmishes before. Border actions. Suppressions. This was not that. This was three of them against eight, in snow that swallowed sound, guarding a seven-year-old who was supposed to be the salvation of two realms and couldn't yet tie his own laces without help.

She was holding the line—barely, ugly, one shoulder already bruised purple under her mail—when the child screamed.

One of the mercenaries had gotten past her, past Nettle, straight for Ember, and the boy had thrown up both hands and spoken.

He spoke the First Tongue.

Sera had heard Mirabel use small pieces of it, the way a man might hum a bar of a song half-remembered. This was not that. This was a single word that went through the air like a nail through boards, and the world obeyed it.

The cold *answered*.

Frost detonated outward from the boy in a ring, white fire in reverse, and the mercenary froze—froze—mid-stride, one boot lifted, sword raised, his shout dying in a throat gone solid. Ice climbed him like ivy, silver and swift, sealing his eyes, his mouth, the fury in his face into something smooth and terrible. A statue of a man caught reaching for a child. Two more attackers, turning to flee, locked the same way, their terror preserved in crystal for anyone to read.

Then the silence.

Sera's breath came out in shreds. Her sword arm had gone numb—not from cold but from something older, some animal part of her brain that had been screaming at her to run since the word left the boy's mouth. Across the churned snow, Nettle had flattened herself to the ground, hackles up, ears back, staring at Ember the way you'd stare at an avalanche pausing to consider you.

Even Mirabel. Even Mirabel had stepped back. Half a step, unconscious, his hand pressed flat over his mouth—but she saw it, and she knew he'd see that she saw, and she watched the guilt land on his face like a dropped stone.

"Ember." Sera holstered her sword slowly, deliberately, the way you moved around a drawn bow. "Hey. Look at me. It's over. You're safe."

The boy was shaking. The frost ring had stopped exactly where it wanted to, a perfect circle, and he stood at the center of it with his hands still raised and tears freezing on his cheeks. *I didn't mean—* His signs were ragged, frantic. *I only wanted him to stop. I only wanted—*

"I know." She crossed the ring. Made herself cross it, because the boy needed to see a human being walk into the place where a man had just become an ice sculpture, and keep walking. She knelt, took his small hands, lowered them gently. They were cold as river stones. "I know. But listen to me. That power—" She searched for words that wouldn't break him. "It listens to your fear, little brother. So we have to keep the fear small. Can you do that? When it comes, you find my voice and hold onto it."

He nodded against her shoulder. Over his head, her eyes met Mirabel's, and something passed between them that neither of them said aloud: *the boy isn't just in danger. The boy is the danger.* And every soul hunting them—the Wardens, the guild blades, the Winter Court's veiled envoys—they weren't chasing a child anymore.

They were chasing a weapon that loved people.

* * *

They ran after that. No more camping in the open, no more fires. The Hollow's heart pulled at them through the trees—Sera could feel it now too, a low ache behind the sternum like a tooth remembering it had once hurt—and everything with teeth pulled at them from behind.

The Wardens found them at dusk.

Not all of her former unit. A picket—six riders, outriders of the main column, torches bobbing through the pines like will-o'-wisps with discipline. Sera heard the hoofbeats and made her choice in the space of one heartbeat, the same choice she'd been making since the gate of the Gilded Hollow: *not them. Never them.*

"Split," she hissed. "Nettle, take Ember east through the deadfall. Mirabel, with me. We give them something to chase."

"You'll be chased," Mirabel said.

"That's the idea." She shoved him toward the treeline. "Go. I'm very chaseable. It's a professional skill."

The plan worked for ninety seconds, which in her experience was the natural lifespan of any plan. Then the wraiths arrived, and plans stopped mattering entirely.

Sera felt them before she saw them—the temperature dropping so fast her breath crackled, the dark between the trees going darker, *hungrier*. They came low across the snow like smoke poured from a broken jar, four of them, shapeless and starved, and where they passed the frost wrote itself into spirals and the silence deepened until her own heartbeat sounded obscene.

The Warden horses broke instantly. Torchlight bent toward the wraiths and went out.

And here was the thing Sera would carry for the rest of her life, whatever life she had left: the lead rider, a young Warden she half-recognized from the training yards at Carreth, turned his mount directly into the path of the nearest wraith rather than away from it. Because the wraiths were flowing toward the child. Toward the seam in the world. And his orders, Sera understood with a lurch, were to take the anomaly alive—which meant standing between the famine-wraiths and their feast.

His torch died. The smoke-shape rose over him like a wave deciding.

Sera did not think. She thought about thinking, later, and couldn't find the moment of decision anywhere. There was just her body moving, her blade swinging through smoke that was not smoke, and the wraith coming apart around her steel with a sound like a starving animal finally, finally eating—

—and pain, white and total, everywhere at once, as if the wraith had taken its toll out of her arm, her ribs, the marrow of her, before dissolving into a smell like old cellars and old winters.

She heard shouting. Heard Mirabel's voice, cracked and desperate, saying words that bent the air. Heard hooves, a lot of them, retreating—Wardens running from a battlefield where their enemy list had gotten too long to parse. Then arms under her, wrong-armed, fur-armed, Nettle's voice cursing fluently in three languages.

"Don't you dare," Sera managed, as the sky tilted. "Don't you *dare* carry me like a—"

"Hush," said the fox-spirit. "Debts are settling tonight, Warden. Yours included."

The last thing Sera saw before the dark took her was Ember, running alongside through the snow, small and lit-from-within, singing under his breath in the First Tongue—and the forest, the whole frozen listening forest, singing quietly back.

* * *

She woke to firelight and agony and Mirabel's face above her, drawn and sleepless.

"You're alive," he said. "Barely. Ribs, arm, and something the healer at the shrine would call 'a wraith's interest,' which I gather is bad. Nettle carried you four miles. You owe her more than she'll ever let you pay."

"Wardens?"

"Gone to ground or gone home. For now." He hesitated, and in the firelight she watched him weigh whether to lie to her, and watched him decide not to. "There's worse news. Ember's feverish. The power—it's not sitting right in him since the fight. Like a bell cracked, still ringing but wrongly. He freezes things in his sleep now. Small things. A cup. Nettle's tail, once." He rubbed his eyes. "Sera—he can't reach the heart like this. If we bring him to the stone while he's this frightened, this *unmoored*, the binding won't forge. It'll shatter. Through him."

Sera stared up at the pine branches swaying against the stars, each breath a negotiated settlement with her broken ribs, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the shrine. Two willing voices, one from each realm. The child willing but untrained, cracking under his own gift like river ice under a cart. And behind them, gathering in the dark like weather: the rest of her unit, Odile's blades, the Winter Court's patience.

"How far?" she asked.

"Half a day. Less, if we don't stop."

"Then we don't stop." She gripped his sleeve and hauled herself upright against every protest her body owned, teeth bared, and beyond the firelight the forest leaned in to listen. "Because I've fought Wardens, and I've fought mercenaries, and I just learned I can kill a wraith and live. But Mirabel—" Her breath fogged, spiraling faintly, almost like writing. "If that boy breaks before we reach the stone, there won't be anything left worth saving. Move."
