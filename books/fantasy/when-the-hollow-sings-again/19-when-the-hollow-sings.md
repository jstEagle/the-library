## 19. When the Hollow Sings

The covenant-stone had been waiting longer than any of them.

Mirabel felt it before he saw it—a pressure in the chest, like standing too close to a struck bell. The forest opened without warning, trees drawing back as if in deference, and there it stood at the center of a bowl of frozen earth: a stone the size of a cottage, black as wet slate, carved over every surface with script that hurt to look at. Not because it was ugly. Because it was *listening*.

The snow here did not crunch. It hummed.

"Ember." Nettle crouched beside the child, her fox-shape flickering between fur and girl and something older than both. "This is where I stop being clever. This is where you have to be brave instead."

Ember looked up at her, then at Mirabel. Seven years old, barefoot still—the boy had refused shoes since they fled the Hollow, and none of them had ever asked twice—and his breath came out in silver ribbons that did not dissipate but drifted upward, threading into the branches like the forest was inhaling him.

Behind them, Sera stood with her sword drawn and her back to the treeline, watching the way they'd come. The snow was red in places. Her own blood, mostly. She had not let anyone bind the wound properly; she said she wanted to feel it if the Wardens arrived again.

"They'll come," she said now, flat. "Odile's warning bought us a day, no more. Whatever you're going to do—do it."

Mirabel knelt in the humming snow.

He could hear everything. That was the cruelty of this place, that his gift burned brightest here at its heart: the stone's slow pulse beneath the frost, the famine-wraiths keening somewhere north beyond the ridge, the mice in their burrows praying in their small gray way for spring, and Ember—Ember loudest of all, a voice like dawn heard from inside a mountain. The First Tongue lived in the child the way fire lives in coal. All Mirabel had to do was listen.

All he had to give was everything else.

*Two voices,* the Last Speaker had said, in the house under the hill. *One of each realm, willingly bound. The child is willing. But a single untrained voice will shatter against the stone like glass on iron. He needs an anchor. He needs someone who can answer.*

*And what does the anchor pay?*

She had not lied to him. She had only smiled, old and terrible and kind, and said: *The same thing everyone pays who is heard by both realms at once. You will speak a language you no longer understand, in a voice that will not come back. What remains after—that is what remains. No one can promise you more.*

Nettle had argued. Sera had argued harder. Ember had simply taken Mirabel's hand and pressed it flat against his own small chest, and through skin and bone Mirabel had heard the boy say—not in words, in weather, in tide, in the green push of things growing—*I'm scared. Stay with me.*

So he had stayed.

Now he drew the last of Nettle's lessons up out of himself, word by word, each one leaving a hole where it had lived. He had already paid for most of them. His mother's voice—gone; he knew the fact of her singing but not the sound, and the absence sat in him like a tooth pulled clean. The reason he'd first hidden his gift—gone, and stranger for its going, because now hiding it seemed merely sensible rather than necessary. A birthday. A teacher's face. The taste of honey cake. Small coins spent early, and he'd been grateful, then, that the price started small.

It had never once stopped climbing.

"Ember," Mirabel said aloud, because his human voice still worked and he meant to spend it while he could. "When I nod, you sing. Don't stop. No matter what you hear me say—no matter how strange—I am answering you. Do you understand? The strange words are me."

The boy nodded, solemn as a priest.

Sera turned from the treeline just long enough to look at him. Snow caught in her lashes; the wound in her side had soaked through its wrapping again. "Mirabel Lockridge," she said, and for once there was no Warden iron in it. "Come back."

He smiled at her. "I've never been good at lying to you. So I won't start."

Her jaw tightened. She turned back to the dark.

***

Sera Vance had broken oaths before. It never got easier; she'd only gotten faster at it.

She watched Mirabel kneel at the stone and thought about orders. *Seize all anomalies. Lethal force authorized.* Somewhere south of here, Captain Voss was marching with forty men and a writ signed in the Regent's own hand, and every law she had sworn to uphold said she should put her sword through Mirabel's spine right now and drag the child back in chains, and winter would be *managed*, and the Crown would endure, cold and eternal and safe as a tomb.

The Crown would endure. She had believed that once the way other people believed in saints.

Ember began to sing.

There was no preparing for it. Sera had heard the boy speak to animals, had heard him calm a dying horse with three syllables, had heard him wake a valley of silent beasts with a touch. None of it was this. This was a sound that came up through the ground and out of the sky and through her own ribs, a note that had been waiting under the world since before the Sundering, patient as ice, and when it finally sounded every frozen thing for ten leagues answered. The trees shuddered off their snow. The wraiths beyond the ridge went quiet mid-keening. Even her wound seemed to hold its breath.

The stone woke. Its carvings filled with light like veins filling with blood.

And Mirabel—Mirabel opened his mouth and answered in a language Sera did not know, had never known, though she realized with a lurch that she almost *almost* recognized it, the way you recognize a face from a dream. The words poured out of him steady and low, call and response, the boy's song and the man's answering, weaving together into something that was neither human nor beast nor hollow-place-between but all three at once, bound at the seam.

*Willingly bound,* the Last Speaker had said. Sera understood the phrase now. It wasn't a ritual requirement. It was a description of what she was watching: two people choosing each other in front of God-knew-what, with nothing held back.

Snow began to fall upward.

***

Nettle had made bargains all her long life, and she knew the shape of a price being paid better than anyone born human ever could.

She saw it happening to Mirabel in real time. With every answered verse, something left him. She couldn't see memories go—those were inside—but she saw the rest: the color draining not from his face but from his *presence*, the way a lamp dims as the oil burns down. His gift, the thing he'd spent his whole life hiding, was being poured into the stone word by word, spent as fuel, and he sang on anyway, steady as a millwheel, giving the language back to the world that had outlawed it.

*Fool,* she thought, with enormous tenderness. *Magnificent fool. You never even haggled.*

The fox in her wanted to run. The fox in her had always wanted to run; it was why she owed debts in the first place, why she'd been bound to the Gilded Hollow, why Odile Crane held her leash. Running was the oldest magic she knew.

Instead she stepped forward into the light and added her voice—not the First Tongue, she didn't have it, but a spirit's keen, high and wild, a third thread braided through the other two. If this was going to work, it would work with all of them in it. If it was going to fail, it would fail with all of them in it. Debts worked both ways.

Above them, the upward-falling snow tore open, and the sky showed winter's engine: a vast slow turning of gray light, the mechanism of the long season, grinding on toward a spring it had no intention of releasing.

The stone flared white.

***

Mirabel heard the world.

Not pieces of it. Not the guild-rats' gossip or the guard-dogs' boredom or the horses' complaints—all the small translations he'd spent a lifetime eavesdropping on. He heard the whole chord at once: every beast within a hundred miles waking and lifting its voice, the silent herd in the far valley crying out together, wolves and sparrows and the fish under the ice and the worms in the frozen soil and Ember, Ember above all, the boy's song rising like sunrise, and Mirabel answering, answering, his borrowed tongue pouring out of him faster now, the stone drinking it, the sky's gray engine stuttering, catching, *breaking*—

The winter cracked mid-word.

He felt it break. Felt the long cold fail all at once like a held breath released, felt warmth move through the ground beneath him like blood returning to a numb limb, and the sound the world made when it happened was the sound the covenant had always been trying to make and never quite managed: not a command, not a binding, but a *greeting*. Both realms, saying hello.

He kept singing. There wasn't much left. He could feel the edges of himself going soft and thin, words leaving him he hadn't known were words, things he hadn't known he was. He was aware, distantly, calmly, that he could no longer remember what his mother looked like—only that someone once had sung to him and he had loved her, and that was enough, that had always been enough, love was the part that stayed—

The last word left him like a bird leaving an open hand.

Silence.

Not the dead silence of the blighted farms. A living silence, the kind that comes after music, when the air is still shaped by what passed through it. Mirabel knelt in the melting snow at the foot of the singing stone and tried to say something—anything—to Ember, who had stopped singing and was staring at him with huge dark eyes full of tears and terror.

Nothing came.

He reached, the way he had reached his whole life, for the voices around him—the mice, the birds, the boy, the great slow heartbeat of the stone itself.

Quiet. Total, ordinary, human quiet. For the first time since he was four years old, Mirabel Lockridge heard nothing that was not said to him in words.

*What remains,* the Last Speaker had promised. *That is what remains.*

He did not know. Kneeling in the thaw, with the winter breaking overhead in sheets of rain and the first true warmth in a year moving across his face like a hand—he did not know what remained. He knew his name. He knew the faces around him: Sera, pale and swaying on her feet with her sword still drawn; Nettle, fox-eared and weeping without shame; Ember, running to him, crashing into his chest, small fists gripping his coat, sobbing words in the First Tongue that were surely beautiful and that Mirabel could not understand at all.

He put his arms around the boy and held on.

That, at least, needed no translation.

***

They came down off the ridge an hour later, when the rain had gentled and the forest dripped and steamed. Sera walked at the rear, watching their backtrail out of habit, though nothing followed—Voss's men had been a mile south when the binding broke, and whatever they had seen in the sky had sent them running. She'd deal with them later. Or the Crown would deal with her. Either way, later.

Ahead, Nettle carried Mirabel piggyback through the slush, the man limp as a shed skin, breathing, blinking, answering questions in a whisper. Alive. Emptied. She kept checking his pulse like counting coins she didn't trust.

"He keeps trying to talk to the birds," she reported when Sera caught up. "Keeps forgetting he can't. Keeps smiling when he remembers." Her ears flattened. "I don't know if that's worse or better."

"It's Mirabel," Sera said.

Ember walked between them, holding Sera's hand with one small fist. Every few steps he glanced back at Mirabel and made a small wounded sound, and every few steps Mirabel, half-conscious on Nettle's back, smiled at him without understanding a single thing the boy said—and the boy kept talking anyway, chattering his bright unknowable syllables at the man who had bought the world's spring with the ability to hear them.

South, far off, bells that had been silent for a year began to ring in the river towns. Thaw-water roared in every stream. Somewhere behind them the covenant-stone stood dark and finished, its work done, its script gone smooth as riverstone.

And on the road ahead, where the mud was softening and the first green shoots were pushing up through the ruin of the snow, Sera saw hoofprints. Fresh. Many horses. Headed not away from the Hollow, but toward it—toward Odile Crane, and whatever bargain she had made, and whatever price was now coming due.

Sera tightened her grip on Ember's hand and quickened her pace.

"We need to get home," she said. "Before someone else decides what we're worth."
