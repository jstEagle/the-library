## 20. Aftermath of Voices

The thaw came the day after the singing, as if the world had only been waiting for permission.

Sera Vance watched it happen from the high shoulder of the Hollow road: snow sliding off branches in wet sheets, streams bursting from their ice like birds from cages, the whole frozen country exhaling at once. Mud rose to meet them. It was, she would think later, an appropriately undignified road for a miracle.

Mirabel rode behind her on the requisitioned Warden horse, wrapped in her own cloak because he had shivered for two days straight and refused to say so. He had not spoken since the covenant-stone. Not to her, not to Nettle, not to the child. Sera had caught herself three times in the first night turning to ask him something—a direction, a preference, whether he wanted the last of the dried plums—and each time the question had died in her throat, because asking him felt like pressing on a wound to see if it still bled.

He was alive. He ate what she put in his hands. He slept, when he slept, with the terrible stillness of the exhausted. But the man who had knelt in the frost-melt talking to a dying horse as if it were an old friend—that man had left something in the Hollow's heart, and no one could tell her what remained.

Ember knew. That was the worst of it.

The child rode in front of Nettle, who had resumed fox-shape somewhere north of the shrine and refused all discussion of returning to human form—"Four legs carry grief better than two," she'd said, and that had been the end of it—and Ember spent the miles south leaning against her fur, whispering. At first Sera had assumed he was whispering to the fox. Then she'd realized he was whispering *past* her, toward Mirabel, in the First Tongue, in the small bright cadences of a child telling his father about his day. Telling him about the crows he'd met at noon, about the stream that had thanked him, about the shapes the clouds were making.

Telling him things Mirabel could no longer hear.

On the third morning Ember stopped whispering. He sat rigid on Nettle's back with his fists in her ruff, and when they halted at midday he slid down, walked to where Mirabel sat sharpening a knife he no longer needed, and stood before him with the terrible directness of children.

*Can you hear me?*

He signed it first, small hands shaping the old gestures—the ones beasts understood without tongues—and then said it aloud anyway, in the First Tongue, and then, in a cracked whisper, in Common, a word he had clearly learned only recently and only for this:

"Please."

Mirabel set down the knife. He opened his arms and Ember went into them like something falling, and over the boy's head Mirabel looked at Sera with eyes that were calm in a way that frightened her more than panic would have, and shook his head once. *No.* He couldn't hear the boy. Not the words, not the voice beneath the voice, nothing.

But he held on. He held on until Ember's sobs went quiet, and then he pulled back, took the child's small hands in both of his own, and pressed them flat against his own chest—over his heart, over the silence there—and then moved Ember's fingers, slowly, deliberately, through a shape in the air.

It was not a word Sera knew. But Ember's face changed as he traced it, grief cracking open into something else, and he answered in sign and speech together, and after that he did not stop talking to Mirabel for the rest of the day, and Mirabel listened with his whole body, watching the boy's hands the way other men watched a fire.

That evening, making camp under a rock overhang with the rain finally warm instead of killing, Sera asked Nettle about it.

"He told him he can still understand," the fox said. She lay with her chin on her paws, one ear tracking the dark. "Not the First Tongue. Him. The boy says the hollow places don't live in your ears." Her amber eye rolled toward Sera, sardonic as ever. "The child is teaching him a new language. Slowly. With his hands."

"That'll take years."

"Yes," said Nettle, with enormous satisfaction. "Isn't it wonderful?"

* * *

The Crown found them at Ford Aldery, which was less a town than a bridge with ambitions, three days south of the thaw-line. Sera saw the banners first—the black stag of the Crown Wardency snapping above a cluster of tents on the far bank—and reined in so hard the horse skittered.

"How many?" Mirabel asked. His voice, when he chose to use it, had settled into something quiet and even, a bell with the clapper removed.

"Two tents' worth. A captain's company." Sera counted again, cold and professional, the old habit steadying her. "Twenty, thirty swords. And those are negotiation colors, not arrest colors. They've pitched facing the bridge, not around it."

"They know we're coming."

"Everyone knows we're coming," Sera said. "We walked out of a fairy story and broke a winter that killed half the harvest. There are probably songs already, and I promise you every one of them is wrong."

She looked at the tents for a long moment. Then she looked at Mirabel, and at Ember asleep against Nettle's flank, and made the decision she had been carrying since the ambush in the frozen forest, since she'd turned her sword on Halric Dane and the others she'd bled beside for nine years, since the moment on the Hollow road when she had chosen, irrevocably, to follow instead of command.

"I'm going in first," she said. "Alone."

"Sera—"

"You're a forbidden speaker with no gift and a burned ledger, he's the anomaly three provinces of superiors want in a cage, and she"—Nettle opened one eye with theatrical menace—"is a spirit with an outstanding warrant dating back forty years. None of you can negotiate. It has to be me, and it has to be now, before someone senior arrives who doesn't owe me anything."

"What do you owe them?" Mirabel asked quietly. "Or they you?"

Sera looked at the black stag snapping in the wind and thought of Halric going down under the famine-wraiths with her name in his mouth—not as a curse; as a plea—and felt the old scar of it ache the way it always did, duller now but never gone.

"That," she said, "is exactly what we're about to find out."

* * *

The captain's name was Ilse Varrow, and Sera had never met her, which was either very good or very bad. Varrow received her in the command tent with tea she didn't pour and dossiers she didn't open, and studied Sera across the camp table with the flat patience of a woman who had read everything twice before the meeting began.

"Warden Vance. Third Circle, decorated twice, reassigned to the Gilded Hollow posting four years ago for reasons that are sealed at the ministerial level." Varrow folded her hands. "Your former unit reported you dead at the March of the Listening Dark. Your former unit is also, per the testimony of eleven witnesses and one guildmaster of questionable character, the party that ambushed you first. Would you like to revise either statement?"

"No, Captain."

"Mm." Varrow turned a page she hadn't needed to open. "You are aware that harboring a Class One anomaly carries a life sentence. That abetting a forbidden speaker carries twenty years. That the creature accompanying you has been property of the Crown's debt-registry since before you were born."

"I am aware of all of it."

"And yet here you sit, asking for amnesty."

"I'm not asking for mine," Sera said. "Do what you like with me. I'm asking for theirs."

She laid it out on the table the way she had rehearsed it on the road, night after night, while the others slept: the Wardens' ambush, documented in Halric's own recovered orders, signed by a deputy minister whose name made Varrow's jaw tighten almost imperceptibly. Odile Crane's sworn deposition, dictated in the guildhall with a lawyer present because Sera had insisted, in which the guildmaster detailed the Winter Court's offer—the child in a cage, the winter made permanent, the realm saved by being ended. The envoy's letters, intercepted. The names of every Crown official who had known and priced and bargained.

And beneath it all, the thing that was not paper and could not be forged: the simple, staggering fact that the winter was over. That whatever the law called Mirabel Lockridge, the world outside the tent was running with meltwater, and every farmer between here and the capital knew why.

"You're asking me to believe," Varrow said slowly, "that the man who ended the Long Winter did it using the exact practice my ministry has spent two hundred years calling abomination."

"I'm not asking you to believe it. You can watch him do nothing at all—he has no gift left. The child, though." Sera met her eyes. "The child you will have to decide about eventually, Captain, and I am begging you to decide correctly. Because there are two ways this goes. Either the Crown learns that speakers are the reason anyone is alive to be governed—or some clever minister decides the answer to the covenant problem is another cage, and in fifty years we do all of this again, worse, and there won't be a second anchor left to pay for it."

Varrow was silent for a long time. Outside, the meltwater roared under the bridge like applause from nowhere.

"The sealed file on your reassignment," she said at last. "I've read it. Unsealed, in my capacity. You reported your own commanding officer for burning a village of suspected speakers." She let that sit. "They didn't promote you or hang you. They buried you at a trade guild in the middle of nowhere and waited for you to resign."

Sera said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't true, and the true version had taken her years and she wasn't giving it away free.

Varrow closed the folder. "Amnesty for Lockridge, conditional. Full pardon for the child—provisional guardianship to be determined. The spirit's warrant I can convert to a registered contract; she'll hate the paperwork. You—" and here the corner of Varrow's mouth moved, the smallest possible distance, "—you I'm reinstating. Fourth Circle. Oversight of the Gilded Hollow's reformation, effective immediately. Congratulations, Warden. You are now the ministry's problem instead of its embarrassment."

"That's not justice," Sera said. "Halric's orders came from above him too, but he drew on us. Someone should—"

"Someone will." Varrow gathered the documents with brisk finality. "Just not quickly, not loudly, and not fairly. That is what justice looks like when it's negotiated instead of won, Warden Vance. You'll learn to live inside it. Most of us do."

* * *

Ember would not come into the camp until Mirabel went to fetch him, and even then he crossed the bridge with his hand fisted in Mirabel's sleeve, glaring at the soldiers with a ferocity that would have been funny if Sera hadn't known where it came from. Cages. Appraisal. A boy who had walked out of an old forest with something vast following behind, who had been appraised before by people with ledgers.

Nettle, presented with her converted contract, read it twice, pronounced it "an insult to the art of debt," signed it anyway, and then spent the rest of the afternoon teaching Ember insults in the First Tongue that made the camp's horses pin their ears, which she claimed was proof of scholarship.

It was near dusk when Sera found Ember alone at the bridge rail, watching the water run brown and triumphant beneath. She leaned on the rail beside him. For a while neither spoke. Below, the river carried the last of the ice south in broken white rafts, grinding and singing against the stones.

"He used to hear me," Ember said at last, in Common—careful, halting, a language he was building word by word, the way Mirabel was building a language of hands. "Everything. Before I talked, he heard."

Sera considered lying to him, decided instantly against it, and was surprised how easy the decision was. "Yes. He did."

"Now the words go past him. Like wind." The boy's jaw worked. "Nettle says he still knows me. She says the hollow places don't live in ears. But I miss him." His voice dropped, furious and small. "He's standing right there and I miss him."

"I know," Sera said.

"Is it my fault? He paid for me."

Sera turned to look at him fully—at the thin shoulders, the eyes that had frozen men mid-stride and wept over crows, the terrible weight of a question no seven-year-old should have to carry, carried anyway, because the world did not check ages before it handed out its prices.

"Listen to me," she said. "A year ago I believed with my whole heart that what Mirabel does is abomination. What *you* are, what you speak—it was my job to put it in chains. And then I watched a dying horse choose to lie down, and I watched a valley of silent animals wake up, and I watched him walk into the dark under the hill knowing what it would cost, and go anyway." She crouched to the boy's level, forearms on her knees, close enough that he had to see her. "He didn't lose his hearing because of you. He lost it because the covenant was dying and someone had to hold the door. You didn't push him through it. He walked. Do you understand the difference?"

Ember's eyes were wet and fierce. After a moment he nodded, once, the way soldiers nod.

"Good. Then here's what you do about missing him." Sera glanced back toward the tents, where Mirabel sat by the fire with a slate across his knees, patiently copying out the shapes Ember had taught him that morning, getting them wrong, being corrected with a seven-year-old's merciless precision. "Don't stop talking to him. Not ever. Make him learn you. Make it take years. He's got time now—all the time he bought. Spend it."

Ember looked at the fire, at the bowed head, at the slate. Something in his face unclenched.

"He gets the signs wrong on purpose sometimes," he said. "So I have to keep teaching."

Sera laughed before she could stop herself—the first real laugh since the frozen forest—and Ember's mouth twitched in answer, and down in the water the ice sang south toward the sea, carrying winter out of the kingdom one white piece at a time.

* * *

They rode out from Ford Aldery on a morning so mild it felt like a forgery. Varrow had given them papers, an escort of four sensible soldiers who asked no questions, and a letter of transit that described Ember, with magnificent bureaucratic delicacy, as *a ward of the Crown under linguistic instruction.*

By midday the road ran green at its edges. Actual green—the first shoots shouldering up through the mud with the blind audacity of things that had survived. Nettle trotted ahead in fox-shape, tail high, conducting an animated census of every field mouse within a quarter mile. Ember rode with Mirabel now, chattering in two languages and signing in a third, and Mirabel answered in his careful, borrowed gestures, wrong half the time, corrected relentlessly, laughing—actually laughing—in a way Sera had never heard from him in all their weeks of flight.

She fell in beside him as the road bent south.

"The guildmaster's deposition named a deputy minister," she said quietly, when the boy's attention was on a hawk. "Varrow will bury it. Odile will testify anyway; she's already rewriting her ledgers, God help us all. There's going to be a reckoning of sorts. Ugly, slow, incomplete." She paused. "You'd have grounds to want nothing to do with any of it. The Crown hunted you. The guild sold you. You could take the boy and vanish into the new spring and no one alive could blame you."

Mirabel rode in silence for a dozen strides. When he turned to her, his hands left the reins and shaped the signs slowly, deliberately, so she could follow—and she realized, with a jolt, that Ember had taught her more of this language than she'd noticed, that she was reading him now the way she'd once read terrain.

*I tried vanishing,* he signed. *For fifteen years. It doesn't suit me.*

Then he smiled, that same quiet bell of a smile, and nodded ahead—to the road, to the south, to the distant smudge on the horizon where the Gilded Hollow waited with its impossible ledgers and its rewritten debts.

"There's something else," Sera said, and hesitated, because she had held this back for three days and could hold it no longer. "Odile's letter arrived this morning. It's addressed to you. But there's a second sheet inside, sealed separately." She drew it from her coat, heavy paper, wax stamped with the sigil of the Last Speaker's shrine. "It's from the old woman. Under the hill. And Mirabel—" Sera turned the seal toward him in the sunlight. "It isn't addressed to you."

She watched him read the superscription, watch his face change as he sounded it out, letter by letter, in a language of hands that only one person in the world had been teaching him.

The letter was addressed to Ember.
