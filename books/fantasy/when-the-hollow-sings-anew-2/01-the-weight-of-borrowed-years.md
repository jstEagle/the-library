## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

There are three rules to stealing a memory, and I break none of them, ever, because the one time you break them is the time you stop being a thief and start being something the wardens burn at the crossroads.

Rule one: never take from a waking mind. A waking mind knows what it's missing, even if it can't name the shape of the hole. It'll spend years touching that gap like a tongue probing a missing tooth, and eventually it'll go looking for a tooth-puller, and then someone like me ends up in a cell with iron bands around her skull.

Rule two: never take what keeps a person standing. First steps, mother's face, the name of their god — those are load-bearing memories. Take them and the whole house comes down, and the collapse is loud, and loud things get investigated.

Rule three: never keep what you steal.

I want you to notice that rule three is not about ethics. Rule three is about survival. A kept memory hums in your skull like a stolen bell, and there are people in this world who can hear bells. But I'd be lying — and lying to myself is the one lie I try not to tell — if I said survival was the whole reason. The truth is uglier and softer: kept memories go stale. They're like bread in that way. Fresh out of someone else's head, they're warm, they're alive, they taste of somebody. Keep them too long and they're just crumbs, and you're just a person eating crumbs alone in the dark, pretending it's a meal.

So. Rules intact, here is how I came to be standing in Lord Aldous Vey's bedchamber at half past two in the morning, holding his first kiss in my cupped hands like a moth I'd caught.

* * *

The Vey estate sat on the shoulder of the Thornfell foothills like a fat man leaning on a fence — comfortable, entitled, entirely unaware of how easily he could be pushed. I'd cased it for six days. Six days of selling ribbon trimmings at the market gate, of learning which servants gossiped and which servants counted silverware, of discovering that Lord Aldous drank plum wine after supper and slept like a man who had never once been afraid of anything, which told me everything about him before I ever saw his dreams.

The wards were the usual merchant-grade nonsense: threshold charms keyed to bloodline, a dream-snare over the master bedroom tuned to catch intruders' intentions. Intentions. As if I intended anything. That's the beautiful joke of my trade — the wards look for wanting, for greed, for the hot grabby shape of desire, and I walk in wanting nothing. Empty-handed, empty-hearted, or close enough to pass. You can't catch a thief who isn't reaching.

I stood at the foot of his bed and let my eyes unfocus until the room went soft and gold-edged, the way rooms do when you stop looking at them and start listening. Every sleeping mind makes noise if you know how to hear. Most people snore in colors. Aldous Vey dreamed in slow blue waves, deep and untroubled, a pond nobody had ever thrown a stone into.

Rich men dream shallow. That's not cynicism; it's hydrology. Nothing has ever pressed hard enough on Aldous Vey's life to carve depth into it. His fears were small and well-fed. His joys were the same. Somewhere in that placid blue water floated the memory I'd been hired to fetch — no, wait. Let me correct myself, because the correction matters.

Nobody had hired me to fetch this one.

The commission was ordinary: Lady Sarelle Vey, the lord's aunt by marriage, wanted her nephew's memory of a certain conversation removed. Something about debts, something about a promise made over cards, something she'd paid four hundred crowns to make unmade. Fine work, clean work. I'd already lifted it — an hour ago, a gray little memory smelling of cigar smoke and panic, folded up small and tucked into the lead-lined case against my ribs where stolen things go to sleep. By tomorrow, Aldous would remember agreeing to nothing, owing nothing, and his aunt would sleep better, and the world would be exactly as dishonest as it was yesterday, just tidier.

But here's the thing about being inside a sleeping mind's gravity. You don't get to choose what drifts past you.

His dreams turned over like a sleeper rolling in bed, and the blue water shifted, and up from underneath rose a memory so bright it hurt to look at — the way a coin at the bottom of a clear stream hurts, all flash and promise. I hadn't gone looking for it. It came looking for me. Dreams do that, near a thief. They show off. Or maybe that's vanity, maybe I flatter myself, maybe every thief believes the loot wants to be taken because the alternative is admitting we're just vultures with good posture.

It was a garden. Summer, some years ago — the memory wore its age lightly, but I could feel the dust on it, the way old memories have a texture like linen washed too many times. A girl in a yellow dress under a plum tree — plum again, even his romance was flavored by his wine — laughing at something he'd said, and then not laughing, and then the kiss. His first. Sixteen years old, heart going like a rabbit, the taste of plums and terror, her hand finding his collar, the whole world narrowing to the warm inch between mouths.

And then the moment after. The moment nobody remembers but the person it happened to: the pure astonished *oh* of it. The feeling of having been, for one heartbeat, a different and better person than he'd ever been before or since.

I should have left it. It was load-bearing. Rule two, black and white, written in my own hand in the front of my own ledger: *never take what keeps a person standing.* And God knew Aldous Vey was standing on very little else. A man's first kiss at sixteen, especially a man whose life has gotten no better than that — that's not a memory, that's a foundation stone.

But it was *warm*. Do you understand? When you do my work, you handle other people's warmth all night long, and you give all of it away, every scrap, because rules one through three, and you walk home at dawn with hands so cold you can't feel your own fingers, and you tell yourself that's the price of the trade, that's the arithmetic, cold hands for warm coin.

The memory sat in my palms and pulsed like a small captured sun, and I stood there in a rich man's bedchamber and hated him a little, and hated myself more, and did the thing I always do, which is the wrong thing done competently.

I took it.

* * *

Let me tell you how theft actually feels, since poets get it wrong. Poets write about memory-theft like it's mist, like it's grief, like it's some pale spiritual transaction. It's not. It's physical. Lifting a memory is like lifting a live coal out of a fire with your bare hands — except the coal doesn't burn, it *knows*. For the second or two it sits in your grip, it thinks it's still happening. The girl in the yellow dress kissed me — kissed *him*, kissed Aldous, whoever — and for one shining second I was sixteen and terrified and in love in a stranger's garden forty miles and eleven years away from my own body.

Then I folded it. Folded is the word; there's no better one. You compress the memory down along its seams — every mind sews its memories with seams, most thieves never learn to find them — until it's small enough to fit through the needle's eye of the extraction, and you draw it out through the crown of the skull like thread through cloth, and the sleeper sighs, and it's done.

Aldous sighed. Rolled over. The blue water of his dreaming closed over the place where the memory had been, smooth as if nothing had ever sunk there.

That's the part that always gets me. Not the taking — the closing. Minds heal around absence so fast. He'd wake tomorrow and the garden would be gone and he wouldn't mourn it, because you can't mourn what you don't know you had. He'd just feel a little colder in summer, a little older at sixteen-year-old distances, and he'd blame the wine, or his knees, or the weather. Absence without a shape. Grief without an address.

I stood there longer than I should have. Long enough that the dream-snare overhead stirred once, tasting the air for greed, finding only the tired gray residue of a job already finished, and settled back down. Long enough for the candle I'd pinched to gutter and relight twice.

Then I did the professional thing. I walked back the way I'd come — window, trellis, wall, drop, hedge, road — and I did not look back at the sleeping house, because looking back is how thieves die, in stories and in fact.

* * *

The safehouse was a rented loft above a cooper's shop in Millbrook, chosen for its forgettable staircase and its roof access, paid for in a dead woman's name. I climbed the stairs two at a time out of habit, though there was nothing behind me but night, and let myself in, and lit the lamp, and sat down at the table with the ledger.

The ledger is rule three made flesh. Every memory I take gets sold within a week — that's the business — but before it's sold, it gets *written*. Not the content. Never the content; writing down what a memory holds is halfway to keeping it, and keeping is dying. No — I write the weight of it. The color. The temperature. One line, sometimes two. It's how I prove to myself the thing existed, and it's how I set the price, and it's — fine, yes, it's also how I remember them. All of them. Every scrap of other people's lives has passed through my hands for nine years now, and not one scrap has stayed, and the ledger is the closest thing I have to a life of my own. A biography made entirely of other people's property. Read it cover to cover and you'd learn everything about the kingdom of Vael and nothing about Lena Underhill, which suits me, or would suit me, if suiting me were something I still knew how to do.

I dipped the pen. I opened the ledger to the next blank line, and I wrote:

*Ninety-one. First kiss, male, ~16 yrs. Garden, summer, plum tree. Warm all the way through. Price: whatever the buyer's got.*

Ninety-one. Ninety-first memory of the year, and we weren't out of the spring frosts yet. Good season. Bad sign. I've learned to read my own output like weather — heavy volume means light purse means hungry months ahead, because the kind of clients who buy memories in bulk are the kind who pay in promises.

I looked at the line I'd written and the pen hovered over the page, because there was one more thing to record and I didn't have a column for it. The *oh*. That moment-after. I'd carried it out of the garden along with the kiss, fused to it, inseparable — you can't fold a memory without folding what it meant — and somewhere between the Vey estate and Millbrook I'd made a decision I hadn't noticed making.

I wasn't going to sell it whole.

This is the kind of thinking that gets thieves killed, so let me be precise about what I planned, because precision is the only virtue I own outright. The buyer for a first kiss pays for the kiss — the sensation, the sweetness, the marketable romance of it. Nobody pays extra for the *aftermath*. So I'd split it at the seam. Sell the garden, the girl, the plums, the mouth-warmth. Keep the *oh* — not keep-keep, not rule-three-broken keep, just... hold it overnight. An hour. However long it takes to sit with a feeling that isn't mine and pretend, for a little while, that warmth can be borrowed the way coats can, the way names can, the way entire lives can.

Borrowed. That's the word I use. Borrowed implies giving back. I'm very big on giving back.

I split the memory at the table, quick and clean, the way you snap a wishbone. The garden went into the lead case with its eighty-nine siblings-in-trade. The *oh* stayed in my hands — small now, smaller than it had any right to be, a spark the size of a knucklebone — and I cupped it, and I let it think it was still happening.

For a minute, maybe two, I was loved in a garden in summer. It wasn't mine. It didn't fit; it fit *terribly*, the way rich clothes fit a beggar, all the seams in the wrong places, because the girl in the yellow dress had never laughed at anything I'd said and no one had ever leaned across a plum tree toward me with that particular soft catastrophe in their face. The memory didn't care. Warmth is not discerning. Warmth just pours itself into whatever container is coldest.

The lamp flickered. Outside, far north, past the dark shoulders of the Thornfells, something hummed — the border wards, singing their low endless note, the note I've heard my whole life and that only a handful of us hear at all. Some nights it sounds like machinery. Some nights, like tonight, it sounds almost like a choir warming up.

I gave the spark back. That's the discipline. You open your hands and you let it gutter out into the dark of the rented room, unowned, unwitnessed, and you watch your own palms go cold again, and you write nothing down, because some entries don't belong in ledgers.

Then I banked the lamp, lay down on the cot in my boots, and did what I always do at the end of a working night, which is recite the rules like a prayer to a god I don't believe in:

Never take from a waking mind. Never take what keeps a person standing. Never keep what you steal.

Three rules. Nine years. Ninety-one memories sold this year alone, and not one of them mine, and not one of them missed me.

I was almost asleep when the thought arrived, the way bad news arrives — quietly, already true, waiting to be noticed: the wards had changed their note. Not louder. Not softer. *Wrong.* Like a singer who's lost the key and is covering for it, smiling through it, hoping nobody notices before the song falls apart.

I lay in the dark and listened, and told myself it was the wind, and knew it wasn't, and slept anyway, because tomorrow there'd be a buyer for a garden and a road north through the Thornfell pass, and a thief's job is to keep moving while the music holds.
