## 2. Smuggler's Arithmetic

There are three kinds of arithmetic on the Thornfell pass, and I've done all of them: the kind you do with coin, the kind you do with blood, and the kind you do with memories. Only one of them spends clean.

I'd climbed two days from the lowlands, following the smuggler's road as it switchbacked up through the black pines, and by the time I reached the waystation at Kestrel's Rest my boots had given up any pretense of being waterproof and my ribs ached in the place where the baron's housekeeper had introduced me to her fireplace poker. Worth it, mind. The baron's memory of his mother's lullaby fetched a fine price in Dunmarrow — grieving sons pay well for their mothers' voices — but the getting of it had involved a locked pantry, an unfriendly dog, and the poker. I was not at my best when I pushed open the waystation door and smelled woodsmoke, wet wool, and the particular sourness of cheap ale spilled into old rushes.

Brannoc was holding court at the corner table, the way he always did, taking up more room than a man that size should reasonably need. He's got shoulders like a cart-horse and a beard going to iron-gray, and he was in the middle of telling some story with both fists for emphasis when he saw me. The story stopped mid-word. The whole table turned to look.

"Well," Brannoc said, and his voice went carefully, elaborately neutral. "If it isn't the ghost of Lena Underhill. We thought the wards ate you."

"The wards don't eat people, Brannoc. They hum. It's entirely different."

"Six months." He said it like an accusation, because it was one.

"Five and a half." I dropped onto the bench across from him, which required displacing a skinny poacher named Tam who knew better than to argue, and held out my hand for his cup. He gave it over without thinking about it, which is the thing about Brannoc — twenty years of knowing someone wears grooves into a person, and you roll along them whether you mean to or not. The ale was terrible. It was also the best thing I'd tasted in a week, and I told him so.

"It's horse piss," he said. "You've been eating trail food."

"I've been eating *other people's* trail food."

That got a laugh out of the table, and just like that I was folded back into the warmth of the room, the way you slip into a bath that's gone lukewarm — comfortable, and faintly aware that comfort is not the same as warm. That's what belonging among outcasts is like. Nobody here asks where you've been, because nobody here wants to answer the same question. We're all of us running from something or toward something or, in most cases, away from something while calling it toward. But there are grooves, and I fit mine, and for an evening that was nearly enough.

Nearly.

* * *

Here's the thing you should understand about me, before we go any further, because everything else follows from it: I steal memories. Not metaphorically. Actually. There's a craft to it — a real craft, older than the guilds that pretend they invented it, older than the wards on the border, maybe older than the border itself — and I'm good at it. Better than good. When I put my hands against a sleeping temple and *listen*, I can hear a mind the way you hear a house at night: the creak of old griefs, the rattle of unshuttered joys, the small animal sounds a person makes in the rooms of themselves when they think no one's listening. And I can lift things. A first kiss. A dead father's laugh. The exact blue of a sky over a specific summer field.

The rule — my rule, the only one I keep religiously — is that I never keep what I steal. I take a memory out of your head whole and intact, wrap it in thought-stuff the way you'd wrap a lamp in wool, and carry it to market. The seller forgets; the buyer remembers; I go home empty-handed but full-pursed. Clean arithmetic. What passes through my hands doesn't stick, because if it stuck I wouldn't be a thief anymore, I'd be a magpie, and magpies end up with nests full of other people's lives and nothing of their own.

I tell myself that's why I travel light, inside. Discipline. Professional standards.

Some nights I suspect it's just loneliness wearing a workman's apron.

* * *

The bargaining started after midnight, when the fire had burned down to its red bones and the rest of the table had staggered off to whatever lean-tos and haystacks passed for beds in their lives. Brannoc sent Tam off with a jerk of his chin — Tam went, because everyone goes when Brannoc does the chin-jerk, it's practically a law of nature — and then it was just the two of us and the fire doing its slow arithmetic of embers.

"You want the high pass," he said. Not a question.

"I want the high pass."

"High pass is closed."

"High pass is never closed. High pass is *expensive*. Those are different words."

He turned his cup around on the table, once, twice. I watched him do it and felt the first cold finger of real trouble touch the back of my neck, because Brannoc doesn't fidget. Brannoc is a rock with opinions. If he's turning his cup, it's because he's deciding how much truth to spend, and Brannoc haggles over truth harder than he haggles over anything.

"They closed the low road yesterday," he said finally. "Warden patrol came through at dawn, doubled up, riding pairs. Wouldn't say why. And last night—" He stopped. Started again. "Last night the wards sang, Lena. Not hummed. *Sang.* Woke every soul in this station sitting straight up in bed. Old Marta down the hall thought it was her dead husband come back and tried to set a place for him at breakfast."

My turn to be still.

The border wards run the whole spine of the Thornfells, old as anything, older than the kingdoms they divide, and nobody living remembers who laid them. They hum. That's the word everyone uses — hum, like bees, like a struck string fading — and most folk live their whole lives under that sound without ever properly hearing it, the way you stop hearing a river if you live beside it long enough. But I hear it. Always have, since I was small, since before I knew what my hands could do. Some nights it's a lullaby-quiet drone under the wind. Some years it's louder. This year—

This year I'd been hearing words in it. Or near-words. Something trying to pronounce itself through thirty miles of standing stone and failing, the way a man tries to speak through a shut window.

"Louder than usual?" I asked, aiming for casual and missing by a country mile.

"Louder than the year the quake brought down Halfway Tower. Louder than anything." He leaned forward, and the firelight did something unkind to his face, carving the worry deeper. "And here's the part I didn't want to say in front of the others. Dovan's boy came down from the ridge line three days ago. Said the stones up top are *warm*. Not sun-warm. Warm like a beast that's been running. And the singing's coming from between them now, not just from them. Like the air itself has caught the tune."

I sat with that. Outside, the wind worried the eaves, and beneath the wind — faint, patient, enormous — the wards hummed their almost-song. I realized I'd been hearing it for days without letting myself notice, the way you don't notice a headache until someone names it.

"What aren't you telling me?" I said. "Out with it. You're spending truth, so spend it all."

He looked at me a long moment. "There's a caravan wants through the high pass. Before the week's out. Rich merchant out of Vael-side, paying stupid money, won't say what he's carrying or why the hurry. I've turned him down twice. Tonight he offered enough to buy this station outright." His jaw worked. "And I'm tempted, Lena. That's what scares me. I've been running this road twenty years and I know every rule of it, and the first rule is you don't take rich fools through a pass that's singing. But the money—"

"How much?"

He told me. I whistled.

"Yeah," he said. "So. Arithmetic. He pays me that to guide him through, and either the wards are just having a loud year and we all come through fine and I'm rich, or something up there is wrong in a way I can't name, and I bury half a caravan. That's the sum as I see it. Unless."

"Unless," I said, "you bring a passenger who can listen to the wards properly and tell you which it is."

His grin was slow and crooked and exactly as infuriating as I remembered. "Unless that."

"And what do I get out of it? Besides the pleasure of your company, which I can get free anytime I want by lying face-down in a ditch and hallucinating."

"Passage. Safe road, my escort, no questions at the checkpoints. And—" he hesitated, and I understood suddenly that this next part cost him, "—and the merchant's offered a bonus for a memory-wright. Says he's got goods need carrying careful. Thought maybe you'd—"

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"Brannoc. No." I kept my voice level, mostly. "I don't carry private cargo. You know that. I sell what I take and I keep nothing, and I don't ferry other people's keepsakes across borders for men who won't say what they are. Last wright who did courier-work for a masked buyer ended up—"

"I know how it ended up. Everyone knows how it ended up." He rubbed his beard. "Look. I'm not asking you to take his cargo. I'm asking you to walk the pass with us and use those ears of yours. If the wards are wrong, you'll know it before any of us, yes? You've said yourself you hear them sing. So sing back. Tell me if the song's a cradle-song or a war-song."

It was a fair offer. It was, I noted with professional detachment, even a clever one — flattery wrapped around utility, the oldest wrapping paper there is. And underneath it, threaded through it, was the thing Brannoc would never say plainly: *stay with the convoy, Lena. Stay where people can see you. Something's coming and I'd rather you weren't alone on the road when it does.*

Old friend. Old fool.

"One condition," I said.

"Name it."

"There's a woman in Dunmarrow. Mid-forties, dyed hair, calls herself Maren the Lacewright. She's been shopping for a childhood — a specific one. Her sister drowned when they were girls, and Maren's spent forty years unable to remember her sister's face, grief burned it right out of her, and now she's got money saved and she wants to buy the memory of a summer day from somebody who'd have been that sister's age. Somebody from Harrowmere village, on the coast, summer of the big regatta." I pulled the folded slip of parchment from my coat — I'd written it out weeks ago, details in my own cipher — and slid it across the table. "I lifted one last month. Girl's childhood, age nine, regatta day, sister alive and laughing in every frame of it. Perfect match. I've been carrying it since Dunmarrow waiting for a buyer who could pay, and Maren can't pay what it's worth. But she can pay passage-equivalent. You get her memory, she gets her sister's face back, I get a clean ledger and you get your bonus. Everybody eats."

Brannoc stared at the parchment. Then at me. "You're selling me a child's summer to buy your road toll."

"I'm selling *Maren* a child's summer. You're just the middleman. And the child in question is fifty years old now and will remember selling it fondly, because I pay sellers double rate and I explain exactly what they're losing. She cried when she sold it. Then she laughed and bought a goose. People are strange, Brannoc. I deal in the strangeness."

"That's—" He shook his head slowly. "That's cold, girl."

"That's *clean*," I said, and heard the edge come into my own voice and couldn't file it off. "Nothing sticks to my hands. Nothing I carry belongs to me. The moment it's sold, it's gone out of my head and into hers and I sleep fine. Can you say the same? Can anyone on this road? At least my trade is honest about what it is. We eat each other's pasts here in the borderlands and call it economy, and I'm just the one with the decency to itemize."

The fire cracked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard groaned under a foot that couldn't sleep. And under it all, so faint I might have imagined it, the wards hummed on — that vast, patient, almost-melody, like something behind a door learning the shape of the latch.

Brannoc looked at me for a long time. Twenty years of grooves, both of us rolling in them.

"All right," he said finally. "Maren gets the memory at your price. You get passage and the bonus-share. And Lena—" he tapped the table once, hard, "—when we're on that pass, if your ears tell you the song's gone wrong, you say so. Loud. No heroics, no hanging back to listen prettier. You *say so.*"

"On my life," I said, which was, I reflected later, an interesting thing to swear by, given what my life was worth and how it was denominated.

We shook on it. His hand swallowed mine whole.

* * *

I slept badly in the waystation loft, which was standard, but I dreamed strangely, which wasn't. In the dream I stood on the high pass with snow coming down that wasn't snow — it was pale, and it fell upward, and where it touched the ground the ground forgot to be ground — and somewhere above me the wards were singing a word. Just one. One word, huge and slow, taking the whole night to pronounce itself, and I strained to catch it, because a thief knows value when she hears it, and that word was the most valuable thing I had ever heard in my life.

I woke with it on the tip of my tongue and lost it before I opened my eyes, the way you lose the name of someone in a dream, and lay in the dark feeling robbed. Which was absurd. I was the robber. I was always the robber.

But I lay there a while longer anyway, listening to the wind and the humming underneath it, and I thought about Maren the Lacewright, who would get her sister's face back tomorrow and weep over a stranger's borrowed summer, and I thought about the girl who'd sold it, fifty years on, laughing over her goose. And I wondered — not for the first time, but with unusual force — whose memory I would have to sell someday to buy my own safe road, and whether I'd cry, and whether there'd be anyone left who remembered me fondly enough to bid.

Outside, far up the black shoulder of the mountain, the wards sang on. Louder tonight, I thought. Definitely louder.

Like something practicing.
