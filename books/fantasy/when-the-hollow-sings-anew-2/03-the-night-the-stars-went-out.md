## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

I want to say I saw it coming, because a thief likes to believe she sees everything coming. But the truth is that on the last night Vael existed, I was lying in Brannoc's wagon with my boots off — an act of trust I still regret — trying to decide whether to steal a nap or just have one honestly.

The wards had been singing all evening.

That's not a thing most people notice. The border wards hum the way a kettle hums before it boils, low and constant, and if you've spent your life slipping under them like I have, you learn their moods. Most nights they grumble at me personally. *There she goes again,* they seem to say, in a tone of bureaucratic disappointment. But that night they weren't grumbling. They were singing high and thin and frightened, like a child humming to keep the dark away, and every few minutes the note cracked.

Brannoc sat by the fire turning a spit with nothing on it, which is his way of thinking. "They've been loud for a week," he said.

"They've been loud for ten years," I said. "Tonight they're scared."

"You can hear fear in a ward?"

"I can hear fear in everything. It's a professional hazard."

He grunted. Brannoc's grunts are a whole language. This one meant *you're being dramatic, girl,* and I let him think so, because the alternative was admitting that the hair on my arms had been standing up since sundown and that I'd checked three times whether the memory-satchel against my ribs — the one holding the nobleman's kiss and half a stranger's childhood I hadn't sold yet — felt heavier than usual.

It did. Everything did. It was the kind of night where the world holds its breath, and I'd been in enough sleeping houses to know what comes after the held breath.

* * *

It happened a little past midnight, and it happened fast, and then it happened slow, which is the worst possible way for anything to happen.

First there was a sound. Not loud — that's the part nobody believes when I tell it. No thunderclap, no scream of breaking magic. It sounded like glass cracking somewhere very far away, or like ice on a pond giving its first warning groan under a skater's weight. A single note from the wards, higher than any note should go.

Then silence.

I sat up so fast I banged my head on the wagon roof. Brannoc was already on his feet, staring north through the trees toward the pass, toward the border we'd been paralleling for two days.

"Tell me you heard that," he said.

"I heard it."

"Tell me it's nothing."

"I'm a thief, Brannoc. Lying is what I do. Ask me something easier."

And then the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. There were no clouds; I remember the sky being insultingly clear, because I remember thinking, absurdly, *of course the sky would pick tonight to be beautiful.* One moment the stars were there — the Shepherd, the Seven Sisters, the pale scatter of them like salt spilled across black cloth — and the next moment they simply weren't. All of them. Everywhere. As if someone had reached up and pinched out every candle in creation at once.

I have stolen a lot of things out of a lot of heads. I have seen what a mind looks like when a piece is taken — the little stutter, the way a man reaches for a name and finds smooth wall. I know exactly what absence feels like from the inside.

That sky was absence. The whole sky, absent, all at once, and the dark that rushed into the space where the stars had been wasn't empty. It was *full*. It pressed down like water. I could feel it against my skin, cold and attentive, the way a hand feels hovering just above your face while you sleep.

"Lena," Brannoc said quietly.

"What?"

"How many kingdoms are there?"

I laughed, because it was such a strange question, and then I stopped laughing, because he wasn't smiling. He was looking at me the way you look at someone when you're checking whether *they're* mad or whether you are.

"Count them," he said. "Go on."

"Ashkar," I said slowly. "The Marchlands. Osterhale. Corvane across the strait." My mouth kept moving and nothing kept coming out. There was a shape in my mind where the next kingdom should be, a shape with borders and roads and mountains I knew by heart, and the shape was there but the *name* wouldn't come, the name slid off it like rain off glass, and worse — the roads wouldn't come, the mountains wouldn't come, the whole country behind us was going soft and gray like a face in a fogged window, like a word repeated until it stops meaning anything—

"Brannoc," I said. "What's south of here?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. His hands, big as hams, turned over once in the firelight, empty.

"That's not funny," he said.

"No," I said. "It isn't."

Because I knew what was south of here. I knew it the way you know your own teeth. South of here was Vael.

The name came to me whole and bright, and the moment it arrived I understood, with a lurching, sickening clarity, that it shouldn't have. That it was the only name of its kind left anywhere in the world, sitting in my head like the last ember in a burnt house, and that everything around it — the kingdom itself, the people in it, the roads and the songs and the graves — was being *eaten*.

* * *

We weren't the only ones awake. By the time I got my boots on, the rest of the camp had gathered at the tree line, maybe thirty souls — smugglers, fence-menders, a tinker family, old Marta who read fortunes badly and charged fairly for it. Nobody spoke. Thirty people staring south at a darkness that had texture, that had *weight*, that seemed very slightly to move the way deep water moves.

"It's the wards," said Doss, one of Brannoc's cousins, whose entire personality was confidence. "Wards blew. Happens. We'll—"

"The wards are gone, Doss," Marta said. Her voice was flat and terrible. "I've lived under them sixty years. You don't hear silence where a song was and call it a song."

"Well, what then? The country just—" He gestured, furious, at the darkness. "What, it just *went* somewhere?"

"Yes," I said, before I could stop myself.

Everyone looked at me.

I should have kept my mouth shut. Rule one of my trade: never show anyone what you can do, never show anyone what you know. But my heart was hammering and the dark was pressing closer and I could feel the name in my skull glowing like a coal in ash, the one warm thing in a freezing world, and I heard myself say:

"There was a kingdom south of here. It was called Vael. And it's gone."

"Gone where?" someone whispered.

"Eaten," I said. "Or unwritten. Or—" I groped for words and hated how small they were. "You know how a memory looks when it's been lifted? The hole where it was? Smooth around the edges, like nothing was ever there? Look at the sky. Look at the south. That's a hole. Something took a bite out of the world, and Vael was the bite."

Silence. Then Doss laughed, one sharp bark, and said, "Girl's been at the poppy wine," and several people nodded gratefully, because a madwoman was much more comfortable than the truth.

But Marta looked at me, and Brannoc looked at me, and neither of them laughed.

Because here's the thing about me, the thing I don't put in the sales pitch: I don't take memories out clean. Taking is easy — any hedge-witch with a silver needle and no conscience can scoop a memory loose. What I do is *carry*. I hold what I take inside my own head, whole and undamaged, until I sell it. Which means my mind has learned, over years, to tell the difference between a thing that belongs to me and a thing that doesn't. It means I notice holes. It means that when the world sprang a leak, I felt the draft.

And it means — though I didn't understand this yet, not fully — that whatever was eating the country couldn't eat what I carried. My head was already a smuggler's wagon. Full of other people's goods, sealed tight, marked *do not touch.* The queen's name, or whoever's name it was, rode in my skull like contraband in a false-bottomed chest, and the thing outside couldn't find it.

Lucky me. The sole survivor of a nation, and the reason I survived was that I was a criminal.

* * *

The vertigo hit me later, walking back to the wagon, and it hit me hard enough that I grabbed a tree and hung on like a drunk.

It wasn't fear. Fear I can handle; fear is an old colleague. It was something worse. It was my own memories rearranging themselves around the hole.

I thought: *the capital, the city with the white terraces above the river.* And my mind supplied the image perfectly — and simultaneously informed me, calmly, helpfully, that there was no river there, that rivers needed names and names needed a country and the country didn't exist. I thought of my sister's voice singing a song from the southern provinces, and I could hear the tune, and I could see her face, and I could not for the life of me say where the song was from, because *from* was a place, and places were being subtracted.

Do you understand what that's like? Every memory has roots. Pull out the root — the kingdom, the ground truth underneath everything — and the branches don't fall right away. They hang there, green and vivid, attached to nothing. I was full of green branches. I could see faces of people who now had never been born. I could taste food from kitchens that had never cooked. Somewhere behind that wall of dark, if there even was a *behind* anymore, there might be people — alive, dead, mid-laugh, mid-scream — and every fact connecting me to them was dissolving like sugar in rain, and the only thing keeping any of it real was that one stubborn name burning in my head like a coin too heavy to spend.

Vael.

I stood there saying it over and over, silently, like a prayer, like a thief counting her take in a dark alley. *Vael. Vael. Vael.* Because I had a sudden, animal certainty that if I stopped saying it, even for a minute, I'd lose it too, and then the country would be gone twice — once from the world and once from me — and there would be nothing left anywhere, no witness, no record, no proof it had ever sung.

Somebody had to remember. Apparently the universe had audited everyone in creation and decided the job went to me, which told me everything I needed to know about the universe's judgment.

* * *

Near dawn, the Hollow — though we didn't call it that yet; we called it *it*, in the lowered voices people use for things that might overhear — passed near the camp.

I felt it before anything else. My satchel of stolen goods went cold against my ribs, all of it at once, every borrowed memory flinching like horses before a storm. Then the darkness at the tree line *thickened*, gathered, leaned. There was no shape to it. That was the horror of it — my eyes kept trying to find edges and finding none, the way they slide off an illusion. It was less like looking at something than like looking at the place where looking stops working.

Nobody screamed. Screaming would have required breath, and every person in that camp had forgotten how to breathe for a good ten seconds. We just stood there while it drifted along the edge of the firelight, patient as tide, and I felt it *taste* us. Not our bodies. Our pasts. I felt it brush against my mind the way a customs officer pats down a coat, searching seams, and find the false bottom, and pause—

—and move on.

When it finally withdrew, folding itself back into the starless south like ink running backward into the pen, old Toma the tinker said, in a small voice entirely unlike his usual bellow:

"I had a dog. Didn't I? I had a dog, a brown one, what was his—"

He never finished. I watched it happen. Watched the question die on his face, watched him frown, shrug, and turn back to the fire, already forgetting he'd asked. Whatever the Hollow had found in him, it had taken payment on its way past.

I didn't sleep. Obviously. Instead I sat in the wagon with my knees drawn up and my arms around the satchel, doing inventory like my life depended on it, because it occurred to me that it might.

The nobleman's first kiss: intact. Half a stranger's childhood: intact. A soldier's homecoming, a baker's pride, a widow's last conversation with her husband — all intact, all safe, all *mine to guard now*, because the people those memories came from were south of that darkness, and south of that darkness was a country that had never existed.

Which raised a question I didn't want to look at directly, so naturally I looked at it directly:

If the kingdom was erased — if every map, every mind, every gravestone had been scoured — then who else remembered? Was I really the only one? Out of however many hundred thousand souls? The wards had shattered, the stars had died, and the whole vast machinery of a nation had been wiped like chalk from a slate, and the sole surviving copy of its name was rattling around in the skull of a memory-thief with a fake name herself, sleeping in a smuggler's wagon on the wrong side of the border?

If I were writing this as fiction, I'd call it implausible.

Brannoc found me at first light. He didn't ask if I was all right, which I appreciated; men who ask that question want you to lie so they can stop worrying. He just handed me tea and sat down heavily and said:

"You still have it. The name."

"Yes."

"Don't say it again. Not out loud. Not till you know what's listening." He stared into the gray morning, jaw working. "My daughter," he said, after a while. "She lives in the Marchlands now, married a cooper. Good woman. Sends letters." He paused. "I tried to think of her face just now, and it came, but it came *wrong*, Lena. Like a portrait of her instead of her. Like something painted from a description." His hands closed around his cup hard enough to whiten the knuckles. "Whatever ate that country is still hungry. And we're standing in the pantry."

I drank my tea. It tasted of ash and pine, and I thought about my rules — never keep what you steal, always deliver, always pay your debts — and about how every rule I'd ever lived by assumed a world that still existed to enforce them in.

South of us, where Vael had been, the darkness waited. North of us, the road climbed toward Thornfell and whatever was left of the world. And somewhere in between — because there's always an in-between, and in-betweens are where I live — I'd begun, without quite deciding to, to turn the problem over the way I turn any locked thing: not with dread.

With appetite.

Something had stolen a whole kingdom. And I don't know how to explain this without sounding like a monster, but the feeling that moved through me as the sun came up on a starless world wasn't only grief.

It was recognition. Somebody out there was doing my job on a scale that made mine look like picking pockets. And a thief knows one thing better than anyone alive:

Anything that can be taken can be taken *back*.

I just had to figure out who to rob.
