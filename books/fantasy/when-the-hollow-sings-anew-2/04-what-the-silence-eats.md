## 4. What the Silence Eats

There is a particular arithmetic to running a smuggler's camp on the Thornfell pass, and I have spent thirty years perfecting it. One part cargo, one part lies, one part knowing which of your people will bolt at the first sign of trouble and pre-emptively chaining their boots to the tent pegs — metaphorically, of course. Brannoc Hale does not chain people. Brannoc Hale merely arranges circumstances so that leaving becomes more bother than staying.

I tell you this so you understand that when the silence came through camp that night, my first instinct was not fear. My first instinct was bookkeeping.

* * *

It started with the fire going wrong.

Not dying — fires die all the time up here, the wind comes off the scree like a debt collector and snuffs them without ceremony. This was different. The flames kept burning and stopped making light. I sat there watching orange tongues lick at birchwood, and the orange went gray, and the heat stayed in my knuckles while my eyes insisted nothing was happening at all. Like watching a memory of a fire instead of a fire. Which should have told me something, given what came next, but you'll forgive an old man his slowness. I've been slow before. I was slow when Lena first turned up at my fire eight years ago, half-frozen, full of lies about being a wool merchant's clerk, and I let them stay anyway because a camp needs someone who can count past ten and lie past twenty.

"Brannoc." That was Marta, our cook, standing over me with a bowl of barley soup I had no memory of her ladling out. She'd been beside me a moment ago. Or she hadn't. Time had gone soft around the edges, like bread left in milk. "Your girl's asking for you."

"Marta, if this is about the salt again—"

"No. Your girl. Pip."

And here is where I have to be honest with you, because whatever else I am — fence, liar, keeper of other men's contraband and my own worse secrets — I am not a man who lies about what matters.

I did not know who Pip was.

The name went into my head and found nothing there. Nothing. You know how it is when you can't recall a word, when it sits on the tip of your tongue like a stone you swallowed? This wasn't that. This was a door opening onto a wall. *Pip.* I said it back to myself, testing it. It sounded like a name. It sounded like a name I should know. And behind it — nothing, just the smooth blank face of a thing never planted, as if some gardener had come along and raked the whole bed flat.

"Your daughter, Brannoc," Marta said, gentler now, because Marta has watched enough hard men go soft to recognize the moment it happens. "Pip. She's by the far tents."

Daughter.

I want to be precise about what happened inside me then, because later, when everything was ash and screaming and Lena was looking at me with those thief's eyes gone wide as coins, I needed to know exactly what had been taken and when. What happened was: I believed her. The way you believe a stranger who tells you your bootlace is undone. Some part of me — the part that isn't memory, the part that's just *me*, the stubborn old mule of it — knew that Marta didn't lie, that I had a daughter, that her name fit a shape in my life the way a key fits a lock even after the lock's been melted down for scrap.

But the shape was empty. Someone had reached into the house of my head and carried off my daughter, and left the doorway standing, and I could see straight through to the winter sky beyond.

* * *

I found her by the woodpile. Of course I did — she's always by the woodpile, that child, splitting logs like they owe her money. Thirteen years old and built like a whip handle, with her mother's black hair hacked short for convenience and her father's — my — temper held on a very short leash.

"Pip," I said, and my voice came out strange, careful, the voice of a man walking on river ice.

She looked up. Axe across her shoulder. "What?"

"I—" And here's the thing, here's the humiliating, unbearable thing: I didn't know what to say to her. Not awkward-dad don't-know-what-to-say-to-a-teenager. I mean I stood there in front of my own child and searched for one single shared thing — her mother's name (dead, I knew that much, grief apparently weighs too much to steal), the day she was born, why she has a scar on her chin, whether she likes eel, whether she *hates* eel, anything — and the search came back empty. Every shelf bare. Every drawer pulled out and left gaping.

"You're looking at me funny," she said. Suspicious. Good instincts, my girl. Wonder where she got those.

"Just tired," I said, which was true in the way that most of my lies are technically true. "Long day on the pass."

"The caravan from Duskmoor?"

"What caravan from Duskmoor?"

She lowered the axe. Slowly. The way you lower a weapon when the person in front of you stops being a threat and starts being a problem. "Brannoc," she said — she calls me Brannoc when she's worried, Pip when she's furious, Da when she forgets herself and loves me out loud — "the Duskmoor caravan. Wool and tin. You negotiated the toll yourself Tuesday last. You made the Duskmoor factor cry."

Had I? There was a flicker there, a ghost of smugness, the shape of a factor's blubbering — and then even that guttered, and I was grasping at smoke. "Did I."

"Daddy." She dropped formal address entirely, which frightened me more than anything else that night. "Say something only you would know."

And I opened my mouth, and I reached, and I reached, and what came out was: "You were born in spring."

Her face crumpled. Because it wasn't wrong — I could feel that it wasn't wrong, the way you feel rain coming in your knee — but it was the answer of a stranger who'd read my file. A fact without a floor under it. No labor, no screaming, no midwife swearing, no tiny red furious face. Just *spring*, floating loose, attached to nothing, mine the way a coat in a pawnshop window is yours.

"That's not you," she whispered. "That's a calendar."

* * *

Now. About that same time, across camp, things were getting worse for everyone else, though I didn't know it yet because I was busy having the worst conversation of my life. I learned the shape of it afterward, pieced together from the others, and since I'm telling you this story I might as well tell it whole.

Lena was there that night. They'd come down off the high trail two days ahead of schedule, mud to the eyebrows, complaining about their boots and eating my soup like they owned the pot. They'd taken the corner spot near the rockfall, the one we keep clear because Lena won't sleep with anyone at their back — thief's habit, or worse than habit, though none of us asked and Lena never told. When the fire went gray, Lena was already awake. Thief's hours. And according to Marta, who tells it better than I could bear to, Lena sat bolt upright with a look on their face like a woman hearing a burglar in the next room, except the burglar was everywhere.

"It's not in the camp," they said. "It's in the *air*. Listen."

Nobody listened, because nobody heard anything, and that was rather the point.

Then Old Wenna, our forger, seventy years of forged seals and forged genealogies and one genuinely excellent forged papal dispensation she keeps under her mattress for morale — Wenna stood up in the middle of the camp and began reciting. Names. Her sons, three of them, drowned or dead or dispersed, she'd never say which. She recited them every night like a rosary; everyone knew the ritual, nobody ever mentioned it. Except tonight she got halfway through — *Aldous, Tam, and* — and stopped. Stood there with her mouth open, fishing.

"Aldous and Tam," Marta prompted gently.

"And..." Wenna's face worked. "There was another one. Wasn't there? There were three. I set three bowls."

"There were three," Marta said.

Wenna looked at the third place setting at her own fire, the cup still sitting there from supper, and her expression is the one I will carry into whatever grave the Hollow leaves me: not confusion. Recognition of loss without any loss to attach it to. Grief with the photograph burned out of the frame. "I must have set it for someone," she said. "I wouldn't set a cup for no one. I'm not senile. I set it for *someone*."

And Lena — Lena went white as the scree and grabbed Wenna by both shoulders and said, "What was his name? Think. Don't try to remember, that's like handing it to them. Feel for the hole. Where does it hurt?"

Which is either madness or wisdom, and that night it turned out to be wisdom, because Wenna pressed her hand flat against her sternum like a woman with heartburn and said, in the smallest voice I ever heard from her: "*Corvin*. His name was Corvin. Why do I only remember him when it hurts?"

Because, as we would all learn before dawn, the hurt is the only place they didn't take.

* * *

Here is what Lena figured out, crouched in the middle of our camp while the darkness walked through it, and here is what I want written down somewhere permanent in case the writing itself gets eaten: memories are food now. The Hollow doesn't destroy them. It eats them. And like any eater, it's sloppy. It leaves crumbs. It leaves the edges of things — the pain, the love, the weight — because maybe those parts taste wrong, or maybe they're gristle, or maybe a silence that devours remembering can't digest what remembering is made of underneath. Wenna couldn't hold her son's name or his face or his voice. But the love was too dense, too well-cured, too much a part of the bone. So she remembered that she loved someone, the way you remember a meal you can't picture eating, and the hunger of that not-knowing nearly folded her in half.

I tested it myself, that night, sitting across from my daughter with nothing in my head where she should be. I felt for the hole, like Lena said. And oh, there was a hole. It was shaped exactly like thirteen years of a girl by a woodpile. It hurt like a tooth pulled wrong. And in the hurt, fragments: small hands too big for the work they wanted. A laugh through a wall. The specific fury of watching her climb something she shouldn't. None of it connected. All of it true. Crumbs from a table where something vast and patient had dined.

"Do you know who I am?" Pip asked me. She'd put the axe down and taken my hands, which were shaking, which embarrassed me, which is the sort of feeling that survives anything, apparently. Embarrassment. Wonderful. The Hollow ate my daughter and left me my vanity.

"No," I said. Honest to the last, God rot me. "But I know I'd walk into a fire for you. I know it the way I know my own hands. Something took the rest, and it can't have you, because whatever it left behind — that's not going anywhere."

She cried. I cried. We were a fine pair by the gray fire, and over her shoulder I saw Lena watching us with an expression I'd never seen on that thieving face before. Not pity. Calculation. But calculation with teeth behind it, the look of a merchant discovering the market has changed and deciding to change with it — no. Better than that. Worse than that. The look of someone realizing that a thing they'd treated as trade goods all their life was suddenly the only food in the world, and that they were standing in the pantry, and that the thing doing the eating was going to come for the pantry eventually, and that they were the only soul in camp who understood the currency.

* * *

The silence passed over us sometime in the smallest hour. I know because the stars came back — dimmer, I'd swear it, like lamps turned low to save oil — and the fire remembered how to be bright, and sound returned to the world in a rush: wind, scree, Wenna sobbing into Marta's shoulder, someone's mule complaining with tremendous conviction about nothing.

We gathered at the main fire, all nine of us, because that's what you do after a wolf walks through the flock. You count.

Marta had lost the recipe for her own soup — the actual recipe, in her head, gone, though she could still cook it, hands remembering what mind couldn't. Wenna had lost Corvin entire except as an ache. Two of the Droste twins had lost each other's faces and kept addressing each other by the wrong names with increasing politeness, like ambassadors between countries that no longer existed. Hask lost six years and his wife both, and sat staring at his own wedding ring like a riddle in brass.

And me. I sat there with my daughter's hand in mine, holding on like she was the last rope on a cliff, and I inventoried. Thirty years of running this pass. Every face, every favor, every grave I'd dug and friend I'd buried and lie I'd told. Most of it still there. The Hollow hadn't stripped the camp clean — it had fed, selective, like a heron drinking. Taken a mouthful here, a mouthful there. Tasting us.

Or — and this was the thought that arrived cold and stayed — *stockpiling.*

"Everyone listen," Lena said. They were standing, which they almost never do, that creature lives permanently at a slouch like the world's a chair they're declining to sit in. "Listen carefully, because I'm only saying this once and then we're deciding what to do about it, and yes, there's an 'about it.' This wasn't random. It moved through camp like a current, and currents have sources. It followed the wards' weakness up the pass — I heard the wards singing wrong for three days, I told Brannoc, he said I was imagining things—"

"You told me the wards sounded *hungry,*" I said. "Forgive me for not building a shrine."

"—and it ate selectively, which means it chooses, which means it can be chosen *against*." Lena's eyes swept the circle, and I saw them doing what thieves do: casing us. Casing reality. "Memories are food now. That's the rule of the world as of tonight. Everything we are is on the menu. And the first rule of surviving a market where you're the commodity—" the ghost of their old smirk, there and gone, terrible and wonderful, "—is learn the buyers. Learn the sellers. Learn the roads. I steal for a living. Well. From tonight, the profession's changed. Now I find out what's hunting us, where it dens, and what it wants. Because it wants *something.* Hunger always does."

"And until then?" Hask asked, bitter, twisting his ring. "What do we do tomorrow?"

"We write everything down," Pip said.

Every head turned to my daughter. Thirteen years old, chin scarred, motherless, robbed, and steady as a keel. "Everything," she said. "Names. Faces described in words. Who loved whom. Put it on paper and hide the paper, because whatever that thing is, it eats what's in our heads, and I haven't seen it eat ink yet. If it takes more tomorrow, we'll know what we lost. We'll have receipts." Her jaw set, and it was my jaw, my late wife's stubbornness wearing my daughter's face, and even hollowed out as I was I knew — knew in the ache where the rest of her lived — that I was proud past all bearing.

"Receipts," Lena repeated softly. Something kindled behind their eyes, that ledger-look, that counting-look. "Gods help me, the child's right. Write it all down. Every kindness. Every debt." A pause, and then quieter, almost to themselves: "Start a ledger. Of everyone the silence takes. Names, if we can find them. If we can't—" they glanced at Wenna, at the third cup, "—then holes. We mark the holes. Nobody gets eaten clean. Not while I'm breathing."

* * *

Later — much later, when the fire had been rebuilt honest and orange and Pip slept against my side with her axe in reach, a habit she has that I no longer remember teaching her but am proud of regardless — Lena came and sat across from me. In the dark. Watching.

"Ask," I said.

"Your daughter. What do you still have?"

"Crumbs. Small hands. A laugh through a wall. Climbing things." I looked at the sleeping shape of her. "Enough to know I'd burn the world down to fill the hole. Not enough to know what I'd rebuild after."

Lena nodded slowly, and in the firelight their face did something complicated — guilt and greed and resolve all fighting for the same ground. "Brannoc. When I came down the pass two days ago. Did I seem wrong to you?"

"Wrong how? You're always wrong. It's your best feature."

"I mean—" They stopped. Started again, and their voice had dropped its wry coat entirely, and underneath it was something young and frightened that thirty years of smuggling had never once shown me. "I heard the wards sing. Three days ago. And I thought: hungry. And I thought: that's odd, wards aren't alive. And then I thought—" a breath, "—I thought I recognized the tune. Brannoc, I have stolen ten thousand memories and I have never once known where the hunger in them comes from. Tonight I watched that thing eat my friends and I thought: I know that appetite. I've *carried* that appetite. For years. Little pieces of it. In my head."

The wind came off the scree. Somewhere above us, faint as a hairline crack in a bell, the wards sang their wrong note.

"So what are you telling me, thief?" I asked. "That you're on the menu?"

Lena looked into the fire for a long moment. Then at me. Then, briefly, unbearably, at my sleeping daughter — at the hole in me shaped like her, at the crumbs I was guarding like coals.

"No," they said. "I'm telling you I might be the reason it came."
