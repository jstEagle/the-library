## 5. A Door Out of Nowhere

Three days after the world forgot itself, I learned that grief has a flavor. It tastes like river water and burnt oats, which is to say it tastes exactly like breakfast in a refugee camp, eaten standing up, watching a woman try to remember her own husband's face by drawing it in the dirt with a stick.

She'd gotten the nose right. The eyes kept coming out wrong — too kind, she said, or maybe not kind enough — and every time the stick moved, I could see her second-guessing herself, editing the dead man into someone he'd never been. That's how memory works when you're alone with it. It doesn't hold still. It's a thief too.

I finished my oats. I did not cry. Crying costs salt, and salt was currency now, along with needles, rope, and — this is the part that would have made me laugh three days ago, back when laughing was a thing I did — memories.

I want to be clear about what I am, since the world seems determined to make me its protagonist. I am not brave. I am not chosen. I am a thief with excellent posture and one marketable skill, and everything I've done since the stars went out has been aimed at a single, unfashionable objective: staying alive. If you were hoping for a hero, there's a woman over by the well who gave away her wedding day for two loaves and a blanket. Start there.

* * *

The rumor arrived the way rumors do in places like Thornfall camp: sideways, thirdhand, and smelling faintly of liquor.

I heard it from a carter named Dulcet — not his real name, but he'd forgotten his real one and had picked a new off a crate of plums — who heard it from a pilgrim who'd come down out of the high passes ahead of the darkness. The pilgrim, Dulcet said, had talked about a door. An exodus gate. A way *out*.

"Out of what?" I asked.

Dulcet looked at me like I was simple. "Out of *this*," he said, and gestured with his cup at everything: the gray sky where stars used to be, the camp full of people clutching their heads like men with toothaches, the treeline where the birds had stopped singing because — and here's a detail I can't shake — the birds couldn't remember their songs. They opened their beaks and nothing came out but breath. "Out of the country that isn't."

"Vael isn't gone," I said. "It's just—" and then I stopped, because I didn't know how to finish that sentence. Vael wasn't anywhere. Vael was a word only I seemed to know, a shape pressed into my skull like a seal in wax, and every day fewer people flinched when I said it aloud. In another week they'd look at me the way you look at a madwoman shouting at empty air. In another month I might look that way too.

"It's just what?" Dulcet asked.

"Just misplaced," I said, and drank my terrible tea.

But I made him tell me about the door twice more, once for the details and once for the lies, because those are different lists. The drowned city of Cael Morath, south and east, past the Lethe-wood if you believed the pilgrim, down where the river Sarn used to meet the sea before the sea got greedy. A gate beneath the water. Old imperial work, older than Vael, older than the maps. A door that opened *elsewhere* — not to another kingdom, mind, not to some neighboring realm where the sky still worked, but elsewhere entirely. Out from under the silence altogether. You walked through and whatever ate Vael couldn't follow, because you'd no longer be anywhere it could reach.

"And people have used it?" I asked.

"People have *tried*," Dulcet said. He had the particular cheerfulness of a man who has decided the world ended on someone else's watch. "Pilgrim said three families went down last spring. Said none came back." He shrugged. "But nobody comes back from anywhere these days. Could mean the door works."

Could mean the door eats people, I thought. But then, so does everything now. At least the door would do it quickly, and honestly, and without making me watch it happen to someone I liked first.

* * *

Here is something nobody tells you about being the last person who remembers a country: it makes you rich and poor at the same time.

Rich, because everyone wants what's in your head. By the second evening I'd sold the same three facts about Vael four times — the color of the palace roofs (green copper), the name of the capital's best noodle shop (the Bent Ladle, and yes, I stole that memory fair and square from a cook who wept into his stockpot, but he sold me a kiss once so we're even) — for bread, boots, and a knife that held an edge. People paid to hear me say the word *Vael*. Not because it meant anything to them anymore. Because it sounded like it should mean something, and hunger of that kind pays better than the belly kind, though it lasts shorter.

Poor, because every time I said the word, I spent a little of it. I don't mean that mystically. I mean each telling wore the memory smooth, the way a stair wears hollow underfoot. The green of the roofs was going gray-green. The Bent Ladle's smell — steam and scallion and wet wool — had lost the wool. Somewhere inside me, the country I'd never loved properly was dying by inches, and I was the one holding the pillow.

So when I say I decided to find the door, understand the arithmetic. Not rescue. Not restoration. I'm not the queen's champion; I'm the queen's pickpocket, if that. I wanted through the door because through the door was the only direction left that wasn't *behind* me, and behind me was a silence that ate grandmothers mid-sentence.

Self-preservation. There. I've written it plainly so you can't romanticize it later. Whatever else happens in this story, remember that it starts with a thief running away.

* * *

I spent a day preparing, which in thief's terms means I spent a day stealing.

Boots first — proper ones, waxed leather, taken off a sleeping drover whose feet were frankly better served by the exchange, since he also got a dream of a warm kitchen inserted where the ache of losing them had been. Yes, I can do that. Give as well as take. It's the one generous thing about my trade, and I use it mostly to tip.

Then food, wrapped in oilcloth: hard cheese, dried plums, a heel of black bread I traded a stranger's memory of learning to swim for. The swimmer will miss it someday, in the way you miss a room you can't picture. I've made peace with that. Or I've built a wall between me and it. From outside, they look identical.

Last, the ledger. My ledger, the little oilskin book I've kept since my second year in the trade, where I write down everything I take: whose memory, what it was, what I was paid. It's not conscience, exactly. Conscience would have stopped me years ago. It's bookkeeping. You don't survive as a thief without knowing your inventory.

That night, by the light of a fire that burned with a strange blue patience — wood doesn't crackle anymore, did you notice? Even fire has gone quiet — I read through the ledger and found the entry that mattered:

*Borrowed from vault beneath the Amber Hall, autumn before last. Contents unknown. Payment: forty crowns and passage. Note to self: never again rob a royal vault, the locks are rude.*

Contents unknown. I'd gone in for coin and come out with a sealed glass vial, warm as a held hand, humming against my sternum ever since. I'd assumed it was some noble's treasured afternoon. A coronation, maybe. A lover's face.

Now the whole sky was dark, an entire country was a hole in the world, and the thing in my head hummed louder every night, like a struck bell refusing to finish.

Coincidence, said the part of me that wanted to walk toward Cael Morath and stop thinking.

Obviously not, said the part of me that had survived nineteen years of thievery by never once believing in coincidence.

I closed the ledger. I decided, in the manner of all wise people facing unbearable conclusions, to decide later.

* * *

Leaving Thornfall was easier than it should have been, and that ease shamed me in a low, persistent way, like a stone in a boot.

Nobody tried to stop me. Nobody asked me to stay. An old woman pressed a twist of dried fish into my hand and said, "You're the one who remembers," not as thanks, not as blessing — just as identification, the way you'd point out a well or a bridge. I was already becoming a landmark instead of a person. Best go while I was still useful enough to feed.

At the camp's edge I paused, because the carter Dulcet was there with his mule, loading bales, and he nodded at my pack and said, "Cael Morath?"

"How did you—"

"You checked the road south twice yesterday and asked the pilgrim's cousin about the Lethe-wood crossing. You're many things, girl, but subtle's not fighting for the title." He spat, companionably. "Take the eastern bank. Western bank's gone strange — trees drinking names, or so they say. And if you meet anyone wearing gray and singing, walk the other way. Fast. Don't listen long enough to learn the tune."

"Singing," I repeated. "Singing's bad now."

"Everything good went bad somewhere around Tuesday," Dulcet said. "You'll adjust."

He turned back to his mule, and I stood there a moment longer than dignity allows, wanting — absurdly, stupidly — to be told good luck, or safe roads, or even *don't die*, any of the small coins people pay each other at partings. But he'd already forgotten I existed. I watched it happen. His eyes slid off me like rain off waxed canvas, and I understood that to him I'd been a conversation, and conversations end, and there was no room left in the world for remembering that they happened.

I walked south with the fish in my pocket and my heart doing its level impression of a locked door.

* * *

The land between Thornfell and the coast was Vael's, once. I keep having to write sentences like that, and I hate every one.

I passed a shrine to the Lady of Wells on the second morning — I knew it was hers because I'd robbed a priestess's devotion to her once, and the shape of the feeling was still in me, sweet and cool as well water. The shrine was intact. The statue was intact. The name carved beneath it had been smoothed blank, not chiseled away but *unwritten*, the stone unscarred, as if the letters had simply reconsidered and withdrawn. Moss grew in the hollows of the goddess's face. She looked like a woman trying to recall why she was standing there.

I put a stolen copper in her offering bowl. Habit. Also insurance. Gods may forget themselves, but I've found they keep grudges with remarkable fidelity.

Past the shrine, the world got quieter by degrees, the way a house gets quiet when someone dies in it. No birdsong. No insects. Wind moved through the grass and the grass bent and neither of them commented on it. Twice I saw other travelers on the far side of the fields, walking the same southward pull I felt in my own feet — the whole remnant population drifting toward the coast like iron filings toward a magnet nobody could see. The exodus had begun without a proclamation. Grief, it turns out, flows downhill, and downhill ends at the sea.

On the third evening I made my mistake.

I'd found a ruined mill to sleep in — roof half-gone, millstone split like a rotten fruit — and I'd done the sensible thing, which is to say I'd warded the door with a thread strung with bells and settled in to eat my cheese and count my remaining memories like a miser counting a diminishing purse. And somewhere between the second bite and the third, I caught myself talking aloud. To nobody. To the dark.

"—and the harbor at Vael-smere smelled of pitch and cinnamon, and there was a bell-buoy off the breakwater that rang flat, always flat, and sailors said it was haunted but really it was just cracked, and—"

I stopped. Listened to my own voice die in the empty mill.

I hadn't meant to say any of it. The memories were leaking. The vial-thing behind my sternum was warm, warmer than body-warm, and it pulled at the rest of me like a drain pulls water, and every fact of Vael I owned came loose and drifted toward it, and I sat in a ruined mill in a country that had stopped existing and realized, with the clarity that only arrives at the worst possible moment, that I was carrying something enormous.

Not stolen treasure. Not a noble's fond afternoon.

A keel. A cornerstone. The kind of thing that holds up a sky.

"No," I said to the dark, conversationally. "No, absolutely not. I don't do quests. I do jobs. Jobs have exits."

The dark declined to argue, which I chose to interpret as agreement.

* * *

I reached the edge of the Lethe-wood on the sixth day, and that's where the road ran out of road.

The trees began politely enough — birch and alder, ordinary, gray-barked, minding their own business. But the deeper lines of the forest had gone strange, the way Dulcet warned. The trunks nearest the path were smooth and pale and *featureless*, and when I looked at them too long I felt a tug behind my eyes, gentle as a hook through wool, like something tasting the hem of my name. I touched the nearest tree and my fingers came away remembering nothing of bark. Just smoothness. The tree had drunk the texture of itself.

Beyond the first ranks, the forest thickened into shadow that didn't behave — shadows pooling uphill, shadows moving when the wind didn't — and somewhere in that deep green dark I could hear, very faintly, a sound like a crowd murmuring in a language made entirely of things forgotten. Names, maybe. Or the spaces where names used to be.

The pilgrim's directions, as retailed by Dulcet, as garbled by drink: *follow the eastern bank until the trees lean, then cut through where the leaning stops, and the sea will find you before you find it.*

The trees leaned ahead of me like a crowd craning to watch an execution.

I stood at the treeline a long time, doing the honest math. Through the wood lay Cael Morath, the door, survival. Around the wood — if there was an around — lay weeks I didn't have, because behind me, patient as winter, the silence was walking. I'd felt it pass in the night, once, a mile west: a pressure like the moment before thunder, and every sound for a minute simply *declining to occur*. When it moved on, the crickets near my fire didn't start again. There were no crickets near my fire anymore. There would not be crickets anywhere again, ever, unless somebody fixed whatever I was carrying in my chest.

Somebody. Ha. Somebody with a spine and a cause and a clean conscience. Somebody who wasn't me.

I tightened my pack straps. I checked my knives. I took out the ledger and, on a fresh page, wrote the entry I'd been avoiding:

*Borrowed from the vault beneath the Amber Hall: contents possibly the true name of the Kingdom of Vael, spoken by its dying queen. Possibly load-bearing. Possibly the reason the sky broke. Payment: forty crowns, which I intend to spend before the universe sends an invoice.*

Below it, after a pause long enough for my hand to steady, I added:

*Plan: reach Cael Morath. Find the door. Go through it. Do not save anything. Do not fix anything. Be alive on the other side, which is the whole job description, and has been from the start.*

Then I looked up at the leaning trees, and the tasteless trunks, and the dark that murmured in forgotten tongues, and I thought about what the Hollow would do to a thief carrying the last word of a dead queen through a forest that drinks names.

It occurred to me, not for the first time, that I was probably the most valuable object in the world, walking around unprotected in boots I'd stolen off a snoring man.

And it occurred to me, for the first time, that I might not be the only one who'd done that math.

Because that was when I saw the tracks — fresh, hours old at most — leading into the wood ahead of me. Boot prints. Even stride. And beside each print, at regular intervals, small scorched circles in the moss, like marks left by a warder's staff keeping rhythm.

Someone else was going to Cael Morath. Someone careful. Someone armed. Someone who either didn't know what I carried —

— or knew exactly, and had been sent to collect it.

I stood at the edge of the Lethe-wood, listening to the murmur of everything forgotten, and followed the tracks in.
