## 6. The Warder on the Road

There are three things a warder learns before the Choir gives him his gray cloak: how to track a thief across country that no longer wants to be tracked across, how to read the residue of a stolen memory like a butcher reads a carcass, and how to stop asking what happened to the person you were before.

I learned all three in the order given. The third one took longest.

The village was called Hobb's Rest, or had been, once — the sign over the well still said so, though the paint had gone the color of old bone and someone had scratched a word beneath it that might have been *help* if you were feeling generous, or nothing at all if you weren't. I wasn't feeling generous. Six days on the road with two novices who kept asking questions had sanded most of my generosity down to splinters.

"Sir Sef." The younger one, Pellin, stood at the edge of the square with his hood up against the drizzle, which was a waste of a hood, because the drizzle wasn't rain anymore. It was the fine gray sift that came off the Hollow when it passed through a place and left the place behind. It didn't wet you. It just made you feel slightly less certain of your own name. "Is this one dead?"

"No," I said. "Worse."

The village was empty, but not abandoned. Abandonment has a look to it — doors swinging, crockery left mid-meal, the small archaeology of panic. This was different. This was tidy. Doors shut. Shutters drawn. A broom leaned against a fence post as though its owner meant to come back for it in an hour and had instead been gently, completely deleted from the world's ledger.

The Hollow had passed through here the way weather passes through. And then something else had come after it, picking at the bones.

"Spread out," I told them. "Look for residue. You know what you're smelling for."

They did not, actually, know what they were smelling for — memory-thieves leave a scent that isn't a scent, more a wrongness in the air, like a room where a fire burned years ago — but telling them that would have meant explaining, and explaining would have meant admitting that I could smell it too, sharp and sweet under the ash-smell of the Hollow's passage. So they spread out, and I stood alone in the square of Hobb's Rest and let the truth arrive on its own schedule.

It arrived fast.

* * *

Here is what the Gray Choir is, since nobody outside it knows, and half the people inside it have forgotten: we are what remains of Vael's warding orders, reorganized after the stars went out, sworn to the silence that ate our country because the silence promised us order. That sounds mad. It sounded mad when Mother Vesper said it to me, kneeling in the white hall with her voice like cold water poured slowly, and it still sounds mad now, and I have stopped caring. Mad things can be true. The wards failed. The sky went dark. Every map forgot the kingdom it was drawn for. Somebody had to keep order in the aftermath, and the Hollow was the only institution left standing.

So we keep order. We feed the Hollow when it must be fed — carefully, ritually, from those who consent, or from criminals, which the paperwork assures me is the same thing — and we hunt the ones who make the hunger worse. Memory-thieves. Gleaners. The little vultures who spent generations picking at the minds of sleeping nobles and now, in the famine years, pick at everything. Every stolen memory is a wound the Hollow can widen. Every thief is an arsonist in a burning country. That is what I believe. I believe it the way you believe a rope will hold your weight, because the alternative is looking down.

I walked the square of Hobb's Rest and read the carcass.

A thief had been here, recently — within a day, maybe less. Not one of the feral ones who rip and run; the takings were clean. At the house by the smithy, an old man's memory of his wedding day, lifted whole, edges neat as a surgeon's sutures. In the tavern cellar, the brewer's recollection of his dead wife's laugh, taken so gently he'd probably never miss it, which was the cruelest part. And at the last house on the lane, a child's — no. I crouched there a long moment. Not taken. *Given.* Someone had sat with a hollowed woman, one the Hollow had already eaten down to her last few memories, and had refused to take what little she had left, and had instead left something. A deposit. A single bright thread of borrowed warmth tucked into the ruin of her mind like a coin under a beggar's tongue.

Thieves don't do that. Thieves take. I have catalogued four hundred thefts in three years and I have never once seen a thief put anything back.

But I knew the hand. That was the thing sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone. I knew the lightness of the touch, the way the taking left no bruise, the particular courtesy of it — the signature of someone who'd been taught that what you steal from another mind should leave it better than mere absence, someone who'd turned violation into a kind of craft so refined it almost wasn't violation anymore. Almost.

I had seen that signature once before, years ago, in a nobleman's townhouse in a city whose name I was no longer permitted to remember, and I had stood in the bedroom doorway looking at the sleeping man and the neat absence where his first kiss used to be, and I had thought: *whoever did this loved doing it well,* and then I had thought: *and whoever did this once kissed me exactly like that, carefully, like something worth leaving unbruised.*

Lena Underhill.

The name rose in me the way names rise in places the Hollow has touched — reluctantly, against the grain, like a splinter working itself out of flesh. I hadn't spoken it aloud in six years. I wasn't sure I still could. But I knew it, and knowing it felt like holding a coal.

* * *

"Sir Sef." Pellin again, at the tavern door, holding something wrapped in oilcloth. "Found this inside. Thought you'd want it."

It was a memory-jar — a gleaner's tool, glass blown thick and clouded, designed to hold a recollection in suspension for transport. Professional work. Inside it swam a dim gold shimmer, and even without opening it I could tell what it held: not loot. A gift. Someone had bottled a memory deliberately, labeled it in grease-pencil on the glass in a quick slanted hand:

*For the woman in the blue house. She asked me to keep this safe until she could carry it herself. Don't sell it, don't drink it, don't be a bastard about it. — L.U.*

L.U. Initials, like a signature, like a taunt. Lena Underhill had always signed their work when they wanted to annoy me specifically, which was a habit they'd developed at nineteen and apparently never outgrown, and which I would have found charming in another life, in another country, in a version of the world where the stars still burned.

"You know the thief, sir?" Pellin asked. He had that novice eagerness, the hunger to be trusted with something.

"I know the work," I said.

"It's good work?"

"The best I've ever seen," I said, and hated myself a little for the honesty. "That's what makes them dangerous."

Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't escape, standing in the drizzle in a dead village with a jar of somebody's wedding day in my hands: the best thief in Vael — in what had been Vael — was loose in a country where every stolen memory was a meal for the thing that had eaten our sky. And the Choir's orders were clear. Gleaners were to be found, questioned, and rendered — their hoards confiscated into the Hollow's keeping, their own memories assessed for contamination. Rendered was a soft word. I had watched renderings. I had performed two. You did not come back from a rendering as anyone you'd recognize.

And somewhere east of here, moving fast along smuggler roads only they knew, was the one person alive whose hands I could identify blindfolded, by touch alone, the way you know a lover's handwriting.

Duty said: report the signature. Request tracking assignment. Follow the trail.

Something older than duty, something I'd buried under the gray cloak and the oath and the slow erosion of the man I'd been, said: *you loved them. You loved them for eleven months and two weeks and you counted, you absolute fool, you counted the days, and then you let the Choir sand that out of you because sanding things out is what the Choir does, and it didn't take, did it? It never takes, not all the way. Here it is, six years later, sitting in your chest like a stone you swallowed, warm on one side.*

"Sir?" Pellin was watching me. "You've gone strange."

"I'm deciding," I said, "which way the trail runs."

* * *

I did not report the signature. Not fully. I wrote my evening dispatch by lantern-light in the tavern while the novices slept, and I described the thefts accurately, because lying badly is worse than not lying, and I described the residue accurately, and when it came time to name the probable identity of the gleaner I wrote: *signature consistent with an experienced professional, possibly guild-trained, identity undetermined.*

Guild-trained. There was no guild anymore. The memory-guilds had been dissolved, absorbed, erased — but the phrase would buy me time. Bureaucracy is a kind of mercy, if you know how to wield it. Somewhere in the Choir's archives a clerk would file *identity undetermined* and move on to the next village, and the hunt for Lena Underhill would belong to no one in particular, which is the closest thing to safety this country offers.

Then I sat with my lie and examined it, the way I'd examine any other residue, and I did not like what I found.

Because it wasn't mercy. Let's be honest, since honesty is the one luxury the Choir hasn't taxed yet. I hadn't protected them out of kindness. I had protected the trail because I wanted to follow it myself. I wanted to stand in front of Lena Underhill again and see whether the person looking back at me was the sharp-eyed, sharper-tongued thief who used to steal kisses from nobles' dreams and then steal actual kisses from me on the loading docks behind the tannery, laughing, saying *relax, warder-boy, I'll give it back* — or whether six years of famine and silence had worn them down into just another gleaner with a jar and a knife. I wanted to know if the person who'd left a gift-memory in a dying woman's head was still in there somewhere.

Or maybe — and this was the thought I turned over last, the one with teeth — maybe I wanted to catch them myself so that no one else would. Because I had seen what the Choir did to gleaners, and I had done it twice, and I could not — I tested the thought the way you test ice, putting weight on it slowly — I could not do it a third time. Not to them.

Which meant I was already compromised. Which meant I was already, in the Choir's precise vocabulary, *contaminated.*

Mother Vesper says the Hollow eats memory, but that's not quite right, and I've had time to learn the difference. What the Hollow eats is *meaning* — the connective tissue, the reason one memory matters. You can keep the facts of a thing and lose the loving of it. The Choir took my oath that way. I remember swearing it; I remember the words; I cannot find, anywhere in myself, what it felt like to mean them. They left me the sentence and ate the soul of the sentence.

But some things resist. Some things go deep enough, grow roots enough, that when the hollowing comes they survive like seeds survive winter — not dead, only waiting. Whatever Lena Underhill was to me had gone that deep. I'd checked, over the years, quietly, the way you check a wound. Still there. Still warm on one side.

I closed the ledger. Outside, the gray sift had stopped, and for a moment — I want to say it was a moment, I need it to have been a moment — the clouds over Hobb's Rest thinned, and something behind them flickered. Not a star. Nothing so kind. Just the memory of where a star used to be, the ache in the sky's socket.

Tomorrow we'd ride east. I'd tell the novices we were following reports of feral gleaners, which was true in the general sense that all lies contain some sediment of truth. And somewhere ahead of us, on roads only outlaws remembered, a thief with courteous hands was carrying a jar of somebody's wedding day and whatever else they'd lifted from the ruins, moving toward Cael Morath if my instincts were worth anything, and my instincts were worth a great deal, they were the one inheritance the Choir hadn't managed to confiscate.

Find them first, I told the dark. Find them before the Choir does. Find them before the Hollow does. And then decide — finally, after six years — which side of the oath you're standing on.

The dark, being the Hollow's country now, said nothing at all.

But far off to the east, past the black hills, I thought I heard — faint, impossible, unmistakable — the wards singing. And underneath the song, threading through it like a signature in grease-pencil on glass, a melody I knew.

One I'd only ever heard hummed by a thief, on a loading dock, in a country that no longer existed.

They were heading for Cael Morath. And so, gods forgive me, was I.
