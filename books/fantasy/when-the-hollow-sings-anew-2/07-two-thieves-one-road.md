## 7. Two Thieves, One Road

I want it noted, for whatever ledger the universe keeps of such things, that I did not scream when the rope came down over my shoulders.

I want it further noted that I *considered* screaming, and decided against it on purely professional grounds — namely, that in a dead country where nobody remembers anything, screaming is a service with no market. So instead of shrieking like a startled hen, I did what any self-respecting thief does when snared: I went loose, dropped my weight, and started cataloguing my captor's mistakes.

He'd anchored his line to a birch that was younger than me. He'd left his left side open because he favored his right hand. And he smelled — this was the detail that stopped my heart and restarted it out of rhythm — he smelled of cold iron and juniper oil, which is what warders use to keep their instruments from remembering things they shouldn't.

"Sef," I said.

The rope tightened. "Thief."

"You're supposed to be dead." I twisted enough to see him step out of the dark, and there he was, all six feet of him, older around the eyes and harder everywhere else, wearing the gray livery of people I had very recently learned not to trust. His hair was shorter than it had been the last time I'd watched him pretend not to look at me across a guild hall. His hands were the same. That felt obscene, somehow, that his hands could stay the same while everything else got rewritten by something that ate countries.

"Everyone's supposed to be dead," Sef said. "The whole kingdom. You included. Imagine my surprise."

"The wards fell three days ago. Give grief a moment to find its boots."

"Grief found mine already." He hauled the rope, spinning me, and pinned my arms against my back with one hand — efficient, impersonal, the grip of a man arresting merchandise rather than a person. "You're going to tell me what you took."

"What I—"

"Don't." The word came out flat as a blade laid on a table. "The village at Harrowmere. Twelve houses burned, but nothing looted except the memory-house. Every jar cracked, every shelf swept clean. The Choir says it was scavengers. Scavengers don't work that neatly. Scavengers don't leave a signature like a bow after dinner." His breath moved the hair near my ear. "That's your work, Underhill. Clean as surgery. So you're going to tell me why a cutpurse who never keeps anything decided to empty an entire vault of other people's lives."

Here is what I should have said: *nothing*, obviously, *you great gray idiot*, because saying anything else puts a target on me shaped exactly like the thing I carry. Here is what I actually said, because three sleepless nights and a sky full of missing stars will strip the caution out of anyone:

"Why do you care? You serve the ones eating us."

His grip didn't change. But something behind me changed — some small tectonic shift, the kind a mountain makes before it decides whether to be scenery or landslide.

"I serve order," he said.

"You serve a silence with a payroll."

"I serve—" He stopped. I felt him stop, the way you feel a door close in a house you're standing in. When he spoke again his voice had gone somewhere quieter, which frightened me more than the shouting would have. "Three days ago I had a warrant book with four hundred names in it. Now the ink's blank and the names are gone and the only reason I know they were ever there is that my hand remembers writing them." The rope loosened a fraction. "You remember things, Lena. Everyone says so now, the few who are left. They say the thief remembers. So I'm going to ask you once more, and then I'm going to do my duty regardless of how much it costs me, and we'll both have to live with the arithmetic. What did you take?"

And here is the trouble with being asked honest questions by men whose hands you used to hold: you answer.

"A memory I stole years ago," I said, "from a royal vault I broke into for coin, without knowing what it was. A single recollection, sealed in glass, humming louder than anything I've ever lifted. I think—" My throat tried to close. I made it behave. "I think it might be the only reason any of this still exists. Whatever Vael was. Whatever it's about to stop having ever been."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight, like wet wool. Somewhere above us a bird made a sound and I flinched, because these days birdsong is either birdsong or bait.

Then Sef laughed. It was a terrible sound — a laugh with all the joy amputated, just the mechanics of it, humor run through the Choir's gray mill until what came out the other end was only rhythm. He let go of my arms but not the rope.

"You're lying," he said.

"I've been told I lie beautifully. This isn't one of those times."

"People don't steal kingdoms by accident."

"No," I agreed. "They steal them by being poor, and good at their work, and too tired to ask why one little jar hums like it's got a choir inside it." I turned my head as far as the rope allowed, enough to catch his eye. Up close he looked like a man who hadn't slept since the stars went out, which made two of us, and I hated how much comfort I took from that. "Let go of the rope, Sef."

"Duty says—"

"Duty's dead. You said so yourself. Your warrant book is blank paper now. So the question isn't what your orders were; it's what you're going to be, now that nobody remembers giving them to you."

* * *

He didn't let go of the rope. But he did sit me down by his fire, which I chose to count as progress, and he fed me barley soup that tasted like obedience, and for a long while neither of us spoke. The firelight did unkind things to his face — showed me the gray threading his temples, the new scar through one eyebrow, the way his jaw sat clenched even at rest, like a man permanently braced against a door.

"You've changed," I offered.

"So have you." He didn't look up. "You used to laugh at your own jokes before you finished them."

"I used to have a country to be funny in."

"That's not it." Now he looked up, and his eyes were doing the thing they'd always done, the thing that used to make me feel like a locked door someone was patiently learning to pick. "You used to be hungry. The good kind. Hungry like the world owed you something and you meant to collect. Now you're hungry like—" He shook his head. "Like something's eating you from the inside."

I thought of the jar in its wrappings against my ribs, warm as a second heart, singing its one note into my bones every hour I failed to understand it. I thought of Brannoc forgetting his daughter mid-sentence. I kept my face still, because I have always been better at keeping faces than keeping promises.

"Everyone's hungry lately," I said. "It's the fashion."

"Lena."

"Don't use my name like you've got a claim on it."

"I don't." Something crossed his face — fast, gone before I could price it. "I gave up claims when I put on the gray. Which is why I'm going to say this as a warder and not as whatever I was to you." He set down his bowl. "There's a gate. Cael Morath. The drowned city. The Choir believes the exodus door there can move what's left of the population out before the Hollow finishes the job. They've assigned me to secure the route."

"And?"

"And the route runs through country that has no borders anymore. No wards. No shape. The Hollow unmakes geography the way it unmakes everything else — the road I walked yesterday may not exist tomorrow, or may exist where it didn't before. I need someone who can feel the seams." His jaw worked. "Someone who hears the old wards sing."

My skin prickled. "Who told you that?"

"You did. Years ago, drunk, on the Thornfell pass, when you thought I was asleep. You said the wards sang to you, that you could walk a smuggler road blindfolded because the stones hummed where it was safe. I filed it away as drunk nonsense." He met my eyes squarely. "It wasn't nonsense, was it? Because I've walked those roads since the sky went black, and they don't sing to me. They sing to no one. But the refugees talk about a thief who moves through dead country like she knows where the bones are buried."

"They exaggerate."

"Do they." It wasn't a question. It was barely even a sentence. He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the iron at his belt. "Here is my offer, thief. Come with me to Cael Morath. Guide me through country that doesn't keep its own shape. In exchange, I don't report you to the Choir. I don't report what you took, or what you might be carrying. As far as my superiors know, you died at Harrowmere with everyone else whose memories you emptied."

"That's it? Passage and silence?"

"For now."

"And what happens when we reach the gate? Do I get to walk away, or does the arithmetic change?"

Sef looked into the fire for a long time. Long enough that I counted his breaths — eleven of them — before he answered.

"I don't know," he said, and the honesty of it landed harder than any lie would have. "I'd like to tell you yes. I've learned not to promise things the world might eat first."

* * *

We negotiated terms until well past midnight, and if you want to know what trust looks like between two people with our particular histories, it looks like a list. I wrote it. He watched me write it. We both initialed it like merchants, because merchants is what we'd become — he selling safe passage, I buying it with skills instead of coin, and neither of us willing to extend credit.

Item one: I guide, he guards. Item two: he asks no questions about what I carry, and I ask none about what the Choir ordered him to do with it if he found it. Item three — I insisted on this one — either party may end the arrangement with a day's notice, no knives involved, and the other walks away without following.

"You think I'd hunt you," he said, reading item three.

"I think you'd *want* to hunt me, and I think you'd hate yourself halfway through, and I'd rather spare us both the poetry."

Something almost like a smile moved under the scar. "Fair."

Item four was his: no thefts along the road. Not of goods, not of coin, and above all not of memories. Not from refugees, not from corpses, not from anyone.

"You're asking me to starve," I said.

"I'm asking you to remember what your trade does to people. I've seen the villages you work, Lena. I've stood in the memory-houses after you've gone through them and watched families come home to minds with holes where their mothers used to be. You call it borrowing." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Nobody borrows. Things taken from minds don't come back. You know that better than anyone. You just never had to watch it happen."

I wanted to throw the soup at him. I wanted to tell him about the nobleman's first kiss, lifted three weeks and one apocalypse ago, still wrapped in silk in my pack because my rule — *never keep what you steal* — had nowhere left to return it to. The man it belonged to might not exist anymore. His kiss was an orphan now, and so was I, and so was the whole idea of giving things back.

But I didn't tell him any of that, because item two cut both ways.

"Agreed," I said. "No thefts. Except—"

"There's an except."

"There's always an except. Except when a life depends on it. If someone's dying and the only thing between them and the dark is a memory I can pull, I pull it, and you can bill me later."

He stared at me for a long moment, and I watched him weigh it — duty against mercy, rule against exception, the whole gray machinery of him grinding against something older and less obedient. Finally he said, "Except when a life depends on it," like a man signing a contract he already regretted, and I felt a small vicious satisfaction, because I'd gotten him to agree to break his own rules on day one, and that boded well for my chances of surviving him long-term.

* * *

We broke camp at gray dawn, if you could call it dawn, the sky being the color of unwashed linen and showing no particular opinion about morning. Sef doused the fire with practiced economy. I checked my wrappings — the jar still warm, still humming, still patient — and slung my pack, and we stood facing each other across the ashes like two duelists who'd misplaced their reasons.

"Which way?" he said.

I closed my eyes and listened. It's hard to describe what I hear, and I've stopped trying to explain it to people who don't. The old border wards had been built into the bones of the land itself — every stone wall, every river ford, every boundary oak carried a thread of the working, and when the threads were whole they made a music, low and constant, the way a hive hums. Three days ago the music had shattered. But shattered glass still sings if you touch the edges, and I could feel those edges now, faint and broken, running off toward the southwest like a snapped harp string pointing at its own ruin.

And underneath it — quieter, wronger, coming from everywhere and nowhere — something else. A listening. A patience. Like the pause before a word that will undo you.

"Southwest," I said, opening my eyes. "Along the seam where the ward-line tore. The Hollow follows the same wound we do, so we stay a ridge east of it and move at night."

"At night. Through unmapped country. Toward the thing eating the map."

"Toward the drowned city, which is southwest, which means the wound, which means—" I shrugged. "Everything worth reaching is past something that wants to eat you. You knew that when you hired me."

"I didn't hire you. I arrested you and then failed to turn you in."

"Same paperwork, different mood." I stepped past him onto the trail, deliberately taking the lead, deliberately making him follow. If he noticed the maneuver, he had the grace not to comment. "Keep up, warder. And Sef?"

He grunted.

"When this is done — when we reach Cael Morath and you've got your gate secured — you and I are going to finish the conversation we started three years ago at Midwinter, and you're going to tell me why you actually left the guild, and I'm going to decide whether to forgive you. Those terms aren't negotiable. Put them on the list."

For a moment he said nothing, and I heard behind me the specific quality of silence that means a man is deciding between several answers, most of them lies. Then:

"Noted," he said.

We walked. The dead country stretched out ahead of us, gray and patient, full of roads that might not exist by evening and villages that remembered themselves imperfectly, doors opening onto rooms where furniture had forgotten its purpose. Somewhere behind us the Hollow moved through what used to be Vael, eating the last of it bite by bite. Somewhere against my ribs, the queen's last word hummed its one stubborn note, waiting for me to understand it.

And somewhere ahead — I couldn't have said how I knew, except that the broken ward-song seemed to bend toward it the way water bends toward a drain — something waited in the drowned city that had opinions about thieves.

I adjusted my pack and lengthened my stride.

First rule of thieving: when the whole world becomes a locked house, you don't stand in the yard admiring the locks.

You pick one, and you go in.
