## 8. Memories Are Currency

Ashfall got its name the honest way: from the sky.

The refugee camp sat in a valley where the mountain had once wept fire, and now the wind carried the gray residue of that old grief down onto the tents and cookfires and the long queues of hollow-eyed people who had walked out of whatever Vael used to be. I arrived at dusk with an empty purse, an empty belly, and a skull full of stolen goods, which is more than most of them could say. Most of them had nothing left at all except the memories they were standing in front of me selling.

You want to understand what my trade looks like when it isn't wearing its good coat? Come to Ashfall. Come stand in the bread line.

* * *

I'd heard about the market before I reached it — every smuggler road has its rumors now, and most of them are true, which is the worst thing about the end of the world. It's bad enough when your lies come home. When your truths start doing it, you know the arithmetic has changed.

The market was a ring of blankets and crates in the shadow of the old slag-heaps, lit by tallow lamps that guttered whenever anyone wept too loudly nearby. There was no coin. Coin had stopped meaning anything the week the stars went out — you can't eat gold, and you can't remember with it either. What they traded here was lighter and heavier both.

A memory for a loaf. A memory for a blanket. A memory for medicine, if the memory was good enough.

I stood at the edge of it with my hood up and my thief's hands tucked into my sleeves like a respectable woman's, and I watched a man in a fishmonger's apron hand over his mother's face to a broker with brass scales and a ledger. The broker touched two fingers to the man's temple, murmured something that sounded almost like courtesy, and drew the memory out in a thin silver thread that coiled into a glass vial like smoke that had been taught manners. The man swayed. Blinked. Asked his own son who he was.

The son didn't answer. The son was next in line.

"That's Harrow," said a voice beside me. "He runs the scales. Used to be a tithe-collector out of Durnvale. Says the work's the same, only now people line up grateful."

I turned. Brannoc stood there with a sack of turnips over one shoulder and the expression of a man who had been expecting to find me somewhere exactly this stupid.

"You followed me," I said.

"I *traded* for you," he said. "Gave a woman three days of my youth for the information that you'd come through Ashfall's south gate looking like a wet cat with a secret. Worth every year." He looked out over the market, and his jaw worked. "I told you the roads would fill up. I hate being right. It's the only thing I've never been able to sell."

"How much have you given them?" I asked, because Brannoc's face had lines in it that hadn't been there in autumn, and I don't mean the kind you earn honestly.

"Enough." He shifted the turnips. "Not enough to miss. That's the trick, Lena. You give them the things that hurt to carry anyway. My father's funeral — sold that first week, best trade I ever made, slept like a stone afterward. A fever I had when I was nine. The name of the boy who bullied me at school." He smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Turns out half a life is ballast. You can throw it overboard and float just fine."

"And the other half?"

"The other half is why you're standing here instead of running for the coast like a sensible criminal."

* * *

I should explain something, since we're being honest tonight, and honesty is a currency I've rarely had reason to stock.

I have rules. Had rules. Rule one: never keep what you steal. Rule two: never steal from the desperate. Rule three: never steal twice from the same head, because a mind remembers being robbed even when it can't remember what was taken, and there are wounds that don't show but fester all the same.

Ashfall broke rule two just by existing. Every soul in that market was desperate, and every one of them was selling, and the terrible truth — the one I stood there chewing on while my stomach gnawed at my spine — is that I understood them. Completely. Without reservation. I knew exactly what it felt like to hold a memory you couldn't afford to keep.

The difference was that mine paid better.

"Come on," Brannoc said. "There's stew. Well. There's liquid that resents being called stew. You can insult it properly."

* * *

We ate by his fire with a dozen other travelers who had the specific silence of people conserving themselves. And that was where I saw her.

She was young — younger than the wedding band she twisted around her finger, which was the only jewelry she had left and the last thing she would ever sell, though she didn't know that yet. She sat across the fire with a boy asleep against her shoulder, maybe four years old, with ash in his hair no amount of washing would shift, because it fell fresh every day in Ashfall, gift of the mountain. She had the queue-ticket of the memory-broker folded in her fist, and she kept unfolding it and folding it again, as though she could wear the decision soft enough to make.

Her name, I learned later, was Maren. At the time she was just the woman with the ring and the boy, and I watched her for the better part of an hour without knowing I was watching a mirror.

When her turn came, she woke the boy gently, stood, smoothed her skirt with both hands the way you do when you're trying to borrow courage off the fabric, and walked to Harrow's scales with her chin up like a queen going to a coronation, or an execution. Same posture, those. I'd know. I've attended both.

Harrow weighed her offering on his ledger-pen and shook his head. "Wedding day," he read off her whisper. "Full sensory. That's a half-loaf, maybe two-thirds if the weather was fine."

"It rained," Maren said. Her voice had a crack down the middle of it that she was standing on either side of. "It rained all morning and my husband said the rain was lucky, that his grandmother always said rain at a wedding meant the marriage would—" She stopped. Folded the ticket again. "It was a good day. It was the best day. Please."

"Best days fetch best prices," Harrow said, not unkindly. He was a collector, not a monster; the distinction matters less every year. "But I'll be straight with you, missus. Sell the wedding, and you won't grieve it. Won't miss it. Memory doesn't leave a hole — that's the mercy and the horror of it. You'll just be a woman who was never married, with a ring she can't explain and a boy she—" He glanced at the sleeping child. "Well. You keep the boy. Mothers' love for their living children holds deeper than the brokers can reach. Everyone knows that."

Everyone knows that. The four most expensive words in Vael.

"I'm not selling him," she said sharply. "I'm selling a *day*. One day. So he eats through the winter."

And I stood there in the firelight with a vault-queen's name rattling around my skull like a coin in a beggar's cup — a memory worth more than every loaf in Ashfall, worth the sky itself, though I didn't know that yet, wouldn't know it until a crow with delusions of grandeur told me so — and I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do since the border fell.

How many of the memories in my hoard had come from heads like hers? Not desperate ones — I had my rules — but *tired* ones. Nobleman's first kiss, lifted from a sleeping lord who'd have traded it for a night's peace anyway. A merchant's childhood summer. Small treasures, lifted light-fingered from the comfortable, fenced to collectors who liked to *feel* things secondhand. Harmless, I'd told myself. Victimless. The comfortable could afford the loss.

Watching Maren twist her ring, I understood that there is no such thing as a victimless theft of memory. There is only theft whose bill arrives late. Somewhere in some collector's cabinet, some lordling was savoring a borrowed kiss, and the man who owned it had woken that morning unable to say why his wife's smile made him feel like a stranger. That's the shape of my crime. It doesn't bleed. It just *unhappens*.

"You're making that face," Brannoc muttered.

"What face?"

"The one where you're about to do something noble and then talk yourself out of it and then feel rotten either way. I've seen you wear it into three card games and one bar fight."

"I don't do noble," I said. "Noble's a rich man's hobby. I do survival. Survival's cheaper."

"Uh-huh." He handed me his bowl. "Then walk away."

* * *

Here is what happened, and I'll tell it plainly because dressing it up would be a lie, and lies are the one currency I've never been able to counterfeit well.

Maren sold her wedding day.

I watched her sit in Harrow's chair. I watched her close her eyes, and I watched her mouth move once — shaping a word, maybe a name, maybe *wait* — and then Harrow touched her temple and the silver thread came out of her, glittering faintly with rain, actual rain, little beads of it caught in the memory-stuff like flies in amber. It was beautiful. That's the obscenity of it. Stolen things are always beautiful going into the vial. It's only after, looking at the empty space they leave behind, that you see what beauty costs.

Harrow sealed the vial, set it in his strongbox among dozens of others — a whole box full of rain and music and first mornings, enough happiness to drown in, priced at bread — and counted two loaves and a strip of dried apple into her hands.

Maren stood. Straightened her skirt. Looked down at her ring finger, where the band still sat, and frowned at it the way you'd frown at a splinter — something foreign, something to see to later. Then she gathered her sleeping son and walked back toward the tents, eating as she went, and she passed within arm's reach of me, and her eyes slid over my face with no recognition whatsoever, and why would there be? She'd never seen me before.

Neither, anymore, had her husband. Wherever he was. If he was. If "husband" was even a word that still applied to her life, with the day that made it true sitting in a glass box under a tarp.

I didn't stop her.

Let that sit a moment, because I want to be fair to myself, and fairness is a discipline I practice badly. I didn't stop her because stopping her was impossible — she'd already signed, already sat, and the thread was drawn; you can't stuff smoke back in a skull, not cleanly, not kindly. I didn't stop her because I had no bread to offer in its place and no right to offer judgment from inside a hood full of contraband. I didn't stop her because the world had broken and everyone in Ashfall was bleeding and I am a thief, not a healer, and thieves survive by knowing which fires to walk past.

All true. All reasons. None of them excuses. I've learned the difference the hard way, and the hard way is the only school I ever finished.

What I actually did was follow her.

Not far. Just to the edge of the tent rows, where she paused to shift her son's weight, and I stepped out of the dark like the ghost of her own conscience, and I said, "Your ring. Don't take it off. Whatever it costs, however strange your hand feels wearing it — don't take it off."

She stared at me. "Who—"

"A thief," I said. "Take it from one who knows. The day's gone. The ring remembers for you. Bodies are honest that way — they hold what minds let go. Some night that band will warm on your finger for no reason you can name, and that warmth will be real, and it will be yours, and no broker in Vael can weigh it." I pressed a coin into her hand — an actual coin, worthless, starless, the last of my useless money — "Buy him something sweet someday. Sweetness helps glue what's left together. Trust me. I'm an expert in what's left."

She looked at the coin, and at me, and something moved behind her eyes — not recognition, but the animal wariness of a mother with a child, deciding fast whether I was danger. Then the boy stirred and murmured something in his sleep, a word, half a name, and her arms tightened around him, and she nodded once, sharp, and walked away into the ash-fall with her loaves and her blank ring and her unexplainable warmth-to-come.

Brannoc appeared at my shoulder. "That was nearly decent of you."

"Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation."

"You gave her your last coin."

"I gave her a *souvenir*. Sentimental value only."

"You're broke."

"I've been broke before." I watched the lamps of the market gutter and flare. "Broke's a place. I've lived worse places."

He grunted, unconvinced, and we started back toward the fire, and that was when I felt it — the prickle at the nape of my neck, the one every cutpurse knows, the sensation of being *watched* with intent. Not Harrow's appraising eye. Not a beggar's envy. This was older and colder, patient as frost, and it came from the darkness beyond the lamplight where the ash fell thicker and the shadows pooled like spilled ink.

Somewhere out in that dark, something was keeping a ledger of its own.

I told myself it was a wolf. I told myself it was the Choir, whose gray-cloaked agents had begun haunting the roads asking questions about thieves and names. I told myself a lot of things that night, huddled by Brannoc's resentful stew with my back to the dark and my hand curled protectively around the satchel where a dead queen's last word slept on.

None of them helped. Because under all my telling-myself, one thought kept surfacing, cold and clear as the thread of Maren's rain:

*I know how it feels to be the thing in the dark, watching someone decide what their memories are worth.*

And whatever was out there had decided mine already.
