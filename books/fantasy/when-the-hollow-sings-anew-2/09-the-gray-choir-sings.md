## 9. The Gray Choir Sings

The Gray Choir does not sing, which was the first lie I ever loved.

I learned it on the night they took me in, eleven years ago now, when I was a warder with a dead queen's country behind me and nothing in front but road. They found me at a shrine outside Thornfell — or rather, they let me find them, standing among the broken candles like mourners who had arrived late and were too dignified to admit the funeral was over. Seven figures in gray wool, faces soft as unfinished sculpture. The one who spoke for them had a voice like water finding its level.

"You've lost something," she said.

"Everything," I said. Because Vael was gone — not conquered, not burned, *gone*, unwritten, a hole shaped like a country — and I was one of perhaps a dozen souls alive who still remembered it, and carrying that memory was like carrying a stone that grew heavier every day you failed to put it down.

"We can take the weight," she said. "Not the memory. Never the memory of what you serve. But the doubts, the griefs, the small treacherous voices that ask *why* at three in the morning. We will hollow those out, and you will be able to walk again."

I want to be fair to her here. I want to record, in whatever ledger this confession becomes, that she did not lie about what she offered. She offered exactly that: an absence where my doubts used to live. What she did not say — what I have spent eleven years declining to notice — is that doubts are load-bearing. Hollow them out and you do not get certainty. You get a man who can walk anywhere, because nowhere weighs anything.

They call the process *taking vows*. There are actual words. I remember them the way you remember a scar — not the pain, just the shape.

*What is a kingdom? A promise kept aloud.*

*What is order? Grief, organized.*

*What are you?*

And the answer, spoken by seven voices including mine, in a room where the candles did not flicker no matter how the draft moved:

*A note in the choir. Nothing sung alone.*

They took my doubts after that. Physically — there was a bowl, and my breath over it, and things coming off me in gray wisps like heat-shimmer off summer stone. My doubt that the Choir served anything but itself. My suspicion that the Hollow was not a punishment but a hunger. My certainty — and this one hurt worst going, I remember clutching at it like a man at a ledge — that whatever had erased Vael deserved to be fought, not filed under.

Afterward I felt *clean*. That's the word I'd have used, then. Clean. The way a room feels when the body's been carried out.

Eleven years I have been clean.

* * *

Here is how a report works, for those fortunate enough never to have filed one.

You kneel — kneeling is optional, technically, but the stone floor of the relay-house has a way of suggesting it — and you speak your findings into a brass horn mounted in the wall, and somewhere far away, in the white city that appears on no map because maps are made by people who remember places, someone writes it down. You report movement. You report thefts. You report names.

Above all, you report memory-thieves.

Because the Hollow eats memory, and thieves handle memory, and somewhere in the Choir's arithmetic — an arithmetic I have never been permitted to check — thieves are either food or traitors, depending on which column the auditor is filling out today.

My territory is the Thornfell passes. Smuggler roads, refugee trails, the long gray vein of people flowing away from the wound where their country used to be. I am good at this work. I tell myself that means something. I have told myself that for eleven years, and telling yourself things is a skill the Choir leaves intact, because otherwise the whole apparatus would collapse by breakfast.

Three days ago, in a looted village called Marrow's End, I found a house that had been robbed the way only one person robs: precisely, gently, leaving everything of value except what was taken. The family's silver sat untouched in its chest. Their grief sat untouched on every surface. But certain memories had been lifted from their sleeping minds — I could read the work in the residue, the faint sweetness in the air like a wine cellar after a careful burglary — and the thief had left payment. Actual coin, tucked under a child's pillow, as if conscience could be settled at market rates.

Only one person I have ever known stole memories like a surgeon and paid like a penitent.

Lena Underhill.

I stood in that ruined parlor for a long time, doing arithmetic of my own. Duty said: file the name. The Choir has wanted Lena Underhill for years — a thief talented enough to matter, unaffiliated enough to vanish, and lately, since the wards fell, seen moving toward Cael Morath with a purpose nobody could parse. Report the location and the apparatus grinds forward, and some gray-faced clerk adds a line to a ledger, and in a month or a year Lena Underhill stops being anyone's problem, including mine.

That was three days ago. I am reporting tomorrow.

I have spent three days not deciding.

* * *

Tonight I sit in the relay-house at Ashfall's edge — refugee camp, three thousand souls, smell of woodsmoke and boiled leather — and I watch the fire, and I try to reconstruct the man who took the vows.

That's the trick of being hollowed. You can't miss what's gone, because missing requires a place for the missing to land. But sometimes I catch echoes. Tonight the echo came from a girl across the fire who was arguing with her brother about whose turn it was to carry the water, and something in my chest went tight and aching around a shape I couldn't name, and it took me an embarrassing length of time to identify the shape.

It was a memory of an argument. Mine. Years ago. Lena and I, on the pass above Highmoor, shouting at each other about whether stealing from sleeping strangers was mercy or arrogance — Lena saying *they'll never miss it, I leave them better than dreams do,* and me saying *that's not yours to decide,* and both of us so young and so sure, and neither of us winning, and neither of us wanting to.

I don't know if that memory survived the hollowing. I think it must have, because I'm feeling its ghost right now, the way an amputee feels weather. Or maybe it didn't survive and this ache is just scar-tissue, the body remembering a shape the mind was emptied of.

Either way: I watched the children argue, and I thought of Lena, and I thought, with a clarity that felt almost like pain, *I am going to lie tomorrow.*

Not might. Am. I could feel it settling over the decision like snow over a footprint — not erasing it, covering it, keeping it fresh for whoever comes next.

* * *

Let me explain the mechanics of lying to the Choir, since I appear to be writing a manual.

You cannot simply omit. Omission gets noticed; the clerks cross-reference patrols, sightings, the gossip that flows through camps faster than water through sand. If Lena Underhill was seen at Ashfall's edge and I report nothing, the silence itself becomes evidence, and silences get audited by Mother Vesper personally, and Mother Vesper audits the way surgeons cut — thoroughly, and without any particular interest in your comfort during the procedure.

No. The lie has to be built like a bridge: same river, wrong crossing.

So tomorrow I will kneel at the brass horn and report that I found Marrow's End worked over by a professional hand, signature consistent with Underhill, and that the trail leads south along the Lethe-wood margin — which is true, as far as it goes, because Lena did pass south, four days ago, heading for the drowned city. And then I will add that the trail enters the wood and dissolves, as all trails do there, because the trees drink identity and even tracks forget what made them. Which is also true. And then I will recommend that the Choir dispatch searchers to the eastern ford, forty miles from where Lena actually is, because the eastern ford is the logical next crossing for a thief making for Cael Morath.

Logical. Wrong by forty miles and by intent. Every word load-bearing and every word false in aggregate, the way a building is made of honest bricks and yet is a lie if you build it across someone's door.

I practiced it tonight, murmuring into my cupped hands by the fire. It came out smooth. That frightened me more than the lie itself — how easily the hollowed mouth produces falsehood, how little friction there is. Eleven years ago I would have sweated through my shirt. Now the lie sits in my chest like a pebble in a boot: present, ignorable.

Except.

Except there is one thing the hollowing cannot reach, because the Choir took it before they took anything else, back when they still believed they were collecting oaths. They asked me, on induction night, to surrender my attachments. Lovers, debts, grudges — *all weights.* And I gave up the grudges gladly, and the debts reluctantly, and then they came to the last name and I said no.

I said no to the Choir. Me. A starving warder with a dead country in his head, saying *no* to seven gray faces in a candleless shrine.

They studied me for a long time. The water-voiced woman tilted her head like a bird considering a worm that had suddenly grown teeth.

"You may keep it," she said at last. "Attachment to one person is a small weight. We find it useful, actually. Love makes men predictable."

I should have understood then. I should have heard the trap in it — love makes you predictable, therefore we will use your love as a handle. But I was twenty-four years old and hollowing hurts less than grieving, and I walked out of that shrine lighter by a whole soul's worth of doubt, carrying one single warm coal cupped in my hands, and I called it a bargain.

The coal was Lena Underhill.

It still is. That's the thing I discovered in Marrow's End, standing in the residue of their careful theft. Whatever else the Choir carved out of me, they never got the coal. Maybe they couldn't. Maybe attachment isn't stored in the same rooms as doubt. Maybe they left it deliberately, the way you leave a stake in the ground to which a dog can be tied.

Doesn't matter. It burns. Three days it has burned, ever since I recognized the signature, and tomorrow I am going to betray eleven years of clean service to keep it burning.

* * *

A complication, because there is always a complication, and complications in my life arrive wearing Choir gray.

Mother Vesper is at Ashfall.

She arrived this evening — I saw the outriders first, then the palanquin, plain as a coffin and about as cheerful — and word moves through camp the way cold moves through a wall. By full dark, half of Ashfall knew that the Choir's highest voice had come to the borderlands in person, and the other half pretended not to know, and everyone bartered a little harder and slept a little worse.

Vesper does not travel without reason. Vesper travels the way weather arrives: because conditions are right, and someone is about to have a very bad day.

I presented myself at her tent within the hour, because not presenting myself would have been the louder statement. She received me seated, hands folded, face lit from below by a brazier of coals that gave her skin the color of old paper. She looks kind. That is her great weapon and she maintains it like a blade. She looks like someone's grandmother, if grandmothers collected regrets instead of buttons.

"Warder Sef." Her voice is warm bread. "Thornfell's loneliest post. You do us proud."

"I do the work," I said, which is the approved response, meaning both *thank you* and *please stop.*

"The work." She turned her hands over, studying her own palms as if reading something written there. "We have a theft, Warder. A delicate one. A memory that walked out of a vault it should never have left — before the vault stopped existing, which is the sort of bookkeeping error that gives the faithful indigestion. You've encountered the thief's work. Marrow's End. Tell me what you found."

So it was already known. So my discovery had been reported by someone else — a scout, a listening-post, the crows, who knows — and my three days of not-deciding had been spent inside a net I hadn't seen.

I gave her the truth, carefully portioned. The signature. The gentleness. The coin under the pillow. At *coin* her eyebrows moved a fraction, the way a still pond moves when a fish turns under it.

"They pay," she said. "Curious. Thieves with consciences. Do you know what I do with curious things, Warder?"

"Keep them," I guessed.

"Study them. Until they're no longer curious, only useful or gone." She smiled, and the smile reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "This thief carries something. Something old, something royal, something the Hollow wants back the way a river wants the sea. Find them, Warder. Not to harm — we rarely need to harm the curious ones. Just find them. Bring me a location, and the Choir will do the rest, and you may go back to your quiet passes and your clean reports."

*I will do the rest.* Not we. I noted that, the way you note a door left unlocked — not entering it, just knowing it's there.

"Yes, Mother," I said.

And here is the thing I want recorded, in whatever ledger receives confessions from hollowed men: I meant to lie to her. Standing in that tent, coals painting my face, I intended to give her the eastern ford and forty miles of beautiful wrongness. I rehearsed it against the inside of my teeth while she smiled her grandmother's smile.

But when I opened my mouth, what came out was: "There is a complication. The thief has protection."

Her eyes sharpened. "Protection?"

"Terrain. Companionship. Luck." Each word chosen like a stair-step in the dark. "Thieves this careful are rarely alone, and alone is how I'd prefer to meet them. Give me three days, Mother. Unannounced. If the Choir moves openly, the thief will feel it — they hear the roads sing, this one. Everyone says so. Let me hunt quiet, and I will bring you more than a location. I will bring you the thief, and whatever they carry."

Silence. The coals ticked and settled. Somewhere outside, a child cried and was hushed, and the hushing sounded like the whole camp holding its breath.

"Three days," Vesper said at last, warmly, terribly. "How generous of you to offer what I was about to demand. Go, Warder. And Sef—" I paused at the tent flap. "The Choir forgives many things. It keeps accounts of all of them."

Outside, the night smelled of ash and pine, and the stars — the few stubborn ones that had come back since the wards fell, scattered like dropped coins across a dark table — looked down on me with the indifference of things that have never had to choose.

I had lied. Not with words — every word I spoke her was defensible, deniable, technically true. I had lied with *shape*, with intention, with three days of stolen time, and my heart was hammering like a first-time burglar's, and beneath the hammering, underneath eleven years of clean hollow silence, something was singing.

That's what I'd forgotten. That's what the hollowing took and I never had language for until this moment, standing in the cold with Vesper's account-book opening behind me: that betrayal of the Choir feels exactly like coming home.

The Choir promised order. Order is grief, organized. I have been organized grief for eleven years, a note in a choir that sings nothing, and tonight, for the first time since that candleless shrine, I am discord. I am the wrong note. I am walking out of key and toward a thief on smuggler roads, carrying a lie in my mouth like a coal, and the song I am breaking is the only one I ever chose.

Three days. Then Vesper sends others, or comes herself, and the arithmetic changes.

Three days to find Lena Underhill before my own masters do — and to decide, finally, which of us is hunting which, and what I will do when duty and the coal in my hands demand opposite things.

I know what the vows say. *Nothing sung alone.*

Watch me, Mother. Watch me learn to sing solo.
