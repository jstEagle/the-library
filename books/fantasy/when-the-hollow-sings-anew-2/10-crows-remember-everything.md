## 10. Crows Remember Everything

The crow had been following us for three days, and I knew it because crows are the only creatures in the Thornfell that travel with an audience in mind.

It started as a shape at the corner of the sky, a smudge of black against a sky that had no business being black anyway — the stars still hadn't come back, and the sun rose these days like something being reluctantly remembered rather than seen. The smudge kept its distance on the first day. On the second it found higher branches, closer ones, and by the third morning it was sitting on a dead pine not twenty paces from our fire, watching me with the particular patience of a creditor.

"That bird's been staring at you since dawn," Sef said. He was sharpening his warder's blade against a whetstone, which he did whenever he was nervous, which was often, which meant his blade had never in its life been sharper.

"It's a crow."

"It's been *following* you."

"Everything follows me lately. It's the age of following." I tossed another branch on the fire and tried very hard to look like a person who had not noticed the crow at all. This is a skill. Most of my life has been built on skills like this: looking unremarkable, looking harmless, looking like someone who has definitely never robbed a sleeping nobleman of his first kiss. Looking, in short, like nobody.

The crow did not buy it. Crows never do.

Sef went back to his whetstone. Since Ashfall we had fallen into a rhythm that wasn't quite trust but had stopped being quite suspicion, which for two people like us amounted to a marriage proposal. We walked. We didn't talk about the Lethe-wood ahead of us or the Choir behind us. We shared a fire. It was, I suspect, the closest either of us had come to intimacy in years, and neither of us would have survived saying so aloud.

The crow hopped down from its pine.

Now, I want to be clear about what happened next, because I have replayed it enough times to know how impossible it sounds even to me, and I once watched a man forget his own daughter mid-sentence, so my bar for impossible sits somewhere in the clouds.

The crow walked into our camp like it owned the deed. It stopped at the edge of the firelight, tilted its head — that mechanical, hinge-like tilt they do, as though their necks were installed by a suspicious carpenter — and looked directly at me. Not at Sef with his sword. Not at the food. At me. At, more specifically, I would learn, the middle of my skull.

Then it spoke.

"You," it said, in a voice like a rusted gate remembering it used to swing, "are carrying stolen goods."

I did the dignified thing. I screamed, fell off my log, and put the fire between me and the bird.

Sef was up with his blade out before I'd finished falling, which I suppose is something. "Lena. What — did that crow just—"

"I heard it too, yes, thank you, I'm not mad, we're both mad together, that's very comforting."

The crow regarded us both with the weary disdain of a tutor faced with particularly slow pupils. It was bigger than most crows, its feathers gone slightly gray at the wingtips like a man going silver at the temples, and one eye was clouded — old, then, or old in the way that mattered. When it spoke again, the words came out in clusters, oddly accented, vowels rounded in a way I couldn't place and yet somehow could, the way you can recognize a song from your childhood without knowing its name.

"Stolen goods," it repeated. "In your head. I can hear it from the trees. It hums."

"I don't know what you're talking about," I said. This is my second-best skill: lying fluently. It failed completely, the way it always fails when it matters, because my voice came out an octave too high and the crow actually laughed — a dry clicking sound, like dice in a cup.

"Thieves," it said, with enormous satisfaction. "You always say that. Every thief since the world was young. *I don't know what you're talking about.* My mistress heard it a thousand times from men with gold in their boots and blood on their cuffs." It hopped closer to the fire, and I noticed its feet were scarred, one toe missing entirely. "I am Ossifrage. Last familiar of the royal aviary of Vael. And you, little magpie, are sitting on the queen's last word like a hen on an egg that isn't yours."

The forest got very quiet. Or maybe I got very quiet, and the forest stayed exactly as loud as it had been; after the night the stars went out I no longer trust my own ears to tell the difference between the world changing and myself doing so.

Sef's blade didn't lower. "There is no Vael," he said flatly. "Vael is gone. There's a hole where Vael was. Ask anyone."

"Anyone," said Ossifrage, "has forgotten. That is rather the entire point, and you'll forgive me if I don't weep over the consensus of the amnesiacs." He — and it was a *he*, I realized, some quality in the irritation of his voice — ruffled his feathers and fixed his good eye on me again. "But she remembers. Don't you, magpie. There's a thing in your skull with a name inside it, and names don't hum unless they're true ones."

My hand drifted to my temple, which was absurd, because the thing he was talking about wasn't somewhere I could cover. It sat behind my eyes like a coin swallowed years ago: the memory I'd lifted from the royal vault in Vael, back when Vael existed, back when I thought I was stealing a queen's signet and some loose jewels and maybe, if the gods were generous, the combination to her wine cellar. Instead I'd come away with a memory that felt like nothing — a single moment, a dying woman's lips moving around a sound. I'd carried it for three years without knowing what it was. A word I couldn't quite read. A name spoken once, at the end.

I had never told Sef about it. I had never told anyone. Rule three of theft: never keep what you steal. But rule three had bent the day the wards shattered, because that nameless memory was the only thing in the world that still remembered the country it came from, and I could no more give it away than I could give away my own heartbeat.

"I don't know what you're—"

"If you finish that sentence," said Ossifrage mildly, "I will follow you for the rest of your natural life, and I have outlived several dynasties, so I'm good for it."

Sef made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. He lowered the blade a fraction. "It wants the memory back?"

"It wants," said Ossifrage, "what belongs to the crown of Vael, which is to say, everything, eventually, but let us begin with the small items." He hopped twice more and settled onto a stone across the fire from me, folding himself up with the primness of a judge taking his chair. His clouded eye caught the light strangely. In the fire-glow it almost seemed to glow faintly, like a jar with a drowned star in it — but that was nonsense. Probably. These days I kept a running list of things that were probably nonsense, and the list had gotten embarrassing. "Sit down, girl. You look like a rabbit that read its own fortune."

I sat. Slowly. Keeping the fire between us, mostly out of principle.

"Here is what happens now," Ossifrage continued. "You give me the memory. I carry it home. Home being, at present, a hole in the world, but I am a patient bird. And you go free, lighter by one burden you never understood anyway."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I peck you," said Ossifrage, with terrible calm, "in the eye."

I blinked. "That's it? You talk like a herald and your ultimatum is *a peck in the eye*?"

"Crows remember everything, magpie. Including every fool who laughed at the aviary." His feathers tightened along his neck. "We were not pets. We were keepers. When the queen needed a message carried beyond any ward, any wall, any warder's oath — she sent us, because a crow cannot be bribed, cannot be bound, and cannot be made to forget, for we keep our memories outside ourselves. In the rookeries. In the songs. In the bones of the mountain." His good eye glittered. "Do you understand what I am telling you? When your Hollow ate this country, it ate the minds of men. It did not eat the crows. We remember Vael. All of it. Every field, every ford, every face. We are the only archive left, and I am the last librarian, and you are squatting in my reading room."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, something howled in a way I chose not to think about.

"The queen's last word," I said carefully. "What does that mean? What's in this memory?"

Ossifrage went very still. Stillness in a crow, I've since learned, is worse than any noise they make. "That," he said, "is not for thieves to know."

"It's in *my head.*"

"Which is precisely the problem, yes." He clicked his beak, once, sharp. "You stole a vault three years ago. Do you know why the vault held that memory? Do you know why a dying queen spent her final breath speaking one word into a single recollection and sealing it in iron?" He leaned forward, and his shadow stretched long across the ground toward me, longer than a crow's shadow had any right to be. "No. You don't. You picked a lock and took a pretty humming thing and ran, and now the country it belonged to has been unwritten, and the only thread left tying Vael to existence is lodged in the skull of a cutpurse who can't even pronounce it."

Each sentence landed like a stone dropped from a height. By the end I was cold all over, and not from the mountain air.

"I couldn't read it," I said. My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "It's a word. I've looked at it a hundred times. It won't — it doesn't resolve. Like writing underwater."

"Of course it doesn't. It isn't written for you. Names aren't." Ossifrage softened, barely, the way a cliff softens into scree. "It isn't punishment, girl. Some memories are doors, and doors only open for the hand they were made for. You've been carrying a key for three years without knowing what it unlocked. That's not your crime. Your crime is that when the door finally shows itself, you might be tempted to run with the key anyway. Thieves always are. I've watched your kind for a long time."

"She's not running anywhere," Sef said.

I turned to look at him. He was standing there with his ridiculous perfectly-sharpened sword, this boy I'd loved before either of us could afford it, this warder of a kingdom that no longer existed, and he said it with such flat certainty that something in my chest twisted sideways.

"You don't speak for me," I said.

"No," he agreed. "But I've seen what happens to people who run from this thing. I've been dragging them back to the Choir for two years." He didn't look at me when he said the Choir's name; he never did anymore, not since the confession at the ford, not since I learned what he'd done and decided — against every scrap of sense I owned — to keep walking beside him anyway. "Running stops working. Sooner or later everyone turns around."

"Everyone turns around," Ossifrage echoed, and there was something under the echo, some older grief, that made me look back at him. The clouded eye caught the firelight again. Definitely glowing. Definitely nonsense. I added it to the list.

"So what's your plan, librarian?" I asked. "Peck my eye out and fly the memory to a hole in the world?"

"My plan," said Ossifrage, "was to frighten you into cooperation, and failing that, to follow you until you grew fond of me. Crows are very good at both. The second works better than you'd think." He preened once, fastidiously, ruining his own gravitas completely. "Also, I know the roads you need. The drowned city of Cael Morath lies past the Lethe-wood, and the Lethe-wood has eaten eleven parties since the stars went out. Twelve, if you count the choir singers, which I prefer to. Without me, you will walk in circles until the trees drink your names. With me—" he spread his wings, showing ragged edges and missing feathers, a banner in poor repair flown proudly regardless, "—you will merely nearly die, several times, memorably."

"He's not wrong about the wood," Sef murmured.

"You've been to it?"

"I've stood at the edge of it. I watched it take a patrol I trained with. They walked in arguing about whose turn it was to cook and came out silent, holding hands, asking each other who they were." He finally sheathed the blade. "If the bird knows the way through, we take the bird."

"*The bird* has a name and a title," said Ossifrage coldly.

"The bird threatened to peck out my friend's eye," Sef said, "so the bird can wait on ceremony."

*Friend.* He said it casually, tossed off like small change, and I watched him not watch me while he said it. Two years ago that word between us had meant something different and simpler and warmer. Now it was a careful, rebuilt thing, load-bearing, placed exactly where it needed to go. I hated how much it steadied me.

"All right," I said. "Here's my counteroffer, your librarianship. You travel with us. You guide us through the wood. And nobody takes anything from anybody's head until we reach Cael Morath and I decide what this memory actually is. If it truly belongs to your dead queen, fine. But I've been robbed of enough context for one lifetime. I'm not handing over the only piece of Vael left in the world on the say-so of a crow with a grudge and one working eye."

Silence. The fire cracked. Ossifrage studied me for a long moment with that single bright eye, and I had the uncomfortable sensation of being appraised not as a threat but as a specimen — a strange artifact dug up from somewhere, being tentatively dated.

"Fond of you already," he said at last, disgusted. "Very well, magpie. Terms accepted. But hear the price of my company, and mark it well: I will ask you questions as we go. About the vault. About the night you stole. About what you felt when the stars went out. And you will answer honestly, because crows know lying the way fish know drowning, and I will peck you in the eye."

"That's genuinely your only threat?"

"It has worked on kings," said Ossifrage.

He hopped closer to the fire then, closer than he'd yet come, and settled at my left side — deliberately, I understood later, at the side where the memory hummed, close enough that I could feel a faint warmth from him, like sitting near a banked oven. His good eye half-closed. For a moment the comic menace fell away entirely and he looked simply ancient, and tired, and alone in the way that only a last-of-anything can be alone.

"There is something else," he said quietly. "Something you should know before the wood, because the wood will strip you down to what you know deepest, and you should have this at the bottom of you where it counts." He opened the good eye fully, and it burned. "That memory you carry, girl — it isn't just the queen's last word. It is the last time Vael's true name was spoken *by anyone*. Not written. Spoken. A country lives in its name the way you live in your body. When the last mouth that knew it falls silent, the country becomes..." He searched for the word and visibly disliked each candidate. "...editable."

The word went through me like cold water through cloth.

"You're saying the Hollow—"

"I am saying," said Ossifrage, "that whatever devoured the stars did not find a door into Vael. It found a door into silence, and Vael was standing nearest to it. And I am saying that the thing in your skull is not loot, and never was. It is a seed." He tucked his head down, preparing to sleep, the way birds do — abrupt, total, as though the conversation had simply been switched off. His last words came muffled through his own feathers.

"Guard it better than you guard your heart, magpie. Though from what I hear in that hum, I suspect the two have gotten tangled already."

* *

I didn't sleep. I sat by the fire with a crow breathing softly at my side and a warder snoring with the disciplined economy of a man who can wake at a whisper, and I reached inside myself the way I had a thousand times before, to the shelf at the very back where I kept the one thing I'd ever broken my own rules to keep.

The memory unfolded the way it always did: a chamber of dark iron, cold enough to burn, and a woman dying on a bier of state with her ladies fled and her guards gone and only the silence keeping vigil. Her lips moved. The sound came out shaped like a word I could see and not read, like light behind frosted glass, like a door—

Except tonight, for the first time in three years, something was different.

Tonight, with the crow asleep beside me, the frosted glass thinned. Just at the edges. Just enough that I caught a flicker of what lay behind it — not the word itself, not yet, but its weight, its *shape*, vast and warm and old as hills — and underneath it, faint as a pulse, I heard the wards of the Thornfell singing their nightly song.

And for one held breath, the wards and the word sang the same melody.

Across the fire, Ossifrage's clouded eye slid open, glowing faintly in the dark, watching me watch the inside of my own skull.

"Yes," he murmured, drowsy and satisfied, a librarian closing up shop. "Now you begin to understand what you've stolen, magpie. Tomorrow I'll tell you how the queen came to hide it — and why she trusted a crow with the hiding, and a vault with the keeping, and no one at all with the knowing."

He tucked his head away again. Then, muffled:

"Assuming, of course, that the trees let us keep our tongues long enough to tell it."

Beyond the firelight, in the direction of the Lethe-wood, something in the dark began — very softly, very patiently — to sing back.
