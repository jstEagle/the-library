## 11. The Queen's Last Word

The crow made us wait.

Of course it did. Crows are theatrical by nature, and Ossifrage was a crow the way a cathedral is a building — technically true, and missing the entire point. It had led us off the road at dusk, through a stand of pines that smelled like resin and old snow, to a hollow where a fire could burn unseen. Then it had hopped onto a flat stone, folded its ragged wings with the air of a magistrate taking his seat, and said nothing for the better part of an hour.

Sef paced. I sat. The crow watched us both with one eye, then the other, the way you'd appraise two horses you suspected were lame.

"You're enjoying this," I said finally.

"I am *resting*," Ossifrage said. "I have flown since before your grandsires drew breath, thief. I have outlasted dynasties, famines, three separate attempts on my life by chefs of varying competence, and one very determined falconer whose name I still curse in my dreams." He — it was definitely a he, though how I knew that I couldn't say; crows don't wear their pronouns like banners — ruffled his feathers. "I will rest as long as I please."

"We don't have time—" Sef started.

"You have less time than you think," the crow said, "and none of it belongs to you. Sit down, warder. You'll want to be seated for this anyway. Most people do."

Sef didn't sit. He was the sort of man who treated every instruction as a negotiation he intended to win. But he stopped pacing, which from Sef was practically prostration.

I fed the fire another branch and waited.

Here is what I knew going into that night: I was a memory-thief who had broken exactly one rule in her life — the big rule, the only rule, *never keep what you steal* — because seven years ago I'd lifted a whole armful of memories from the royal vault of Vael and one of them had sunk its hooks into me so deep I couldn't give it up. I'd told myself for years it was just a fine piece of work, a vintage worth savoring. A collector's weakness. Every thief has one.

I told myself that right up until the stars went out and the country they hung over stopped existing in anyone's mind but mine.

What I did not know — what no one knew, apparently, except a four-hundred-year-old bird with a grudge against chefs — was what I was actually carrying.

Ossifrage began, as all terrible storytellers begin, with the weather.

* * *

"It was raining," he said, "the night the queen died. It is always raining in the telling, so it may as well have been raining in the fact. She had ruled Vael for sixty-one years, and she had done it well, which is the cruellest kind of preparation for grief. A bad queen dies and people shrug. A good queen dies and the whole country holds its breath, waiting to see if the world will notice the absence."

He spoke differently when he told stories. His voice lost its dry rasp and took on weight, like a river deepening. Sef had gone very still across the fire. Even his hands had stopped moving, and Sef's hands were always moving — adjusting straps, testing edges, touching things to know where they were.

"The queen's name was Maerlin Vael," Ossifrage said. "Not the country. The woman. People forget there's a difference, or rather they forget there isn't one — the land took her family's name four hundred years before her birth, and by the end of her reign, the borders of her body and the borders of the kingdom had grown to rhyme. When she hurt, the harvests thinned. When she slept badly, the wards hummed flat. Everyone pretended this wasn't true, because acknowledging it would mean admitting the country had no plan for the day it stopped being true."

"And then?" Sef asked quietly.

"And then she got sick. Not dramatically. Slowly. The kind of sickness that gives you years to make arrangements and takes them back one by one while you're making them." Ossifrage turned his head toward me. "She was not a fool, thief. She knew what she was. The land held its shape because she held hers, and when she let go, everything she was holding would come loose. So she made preparations. Not for her death — for her country's survival after it."

"The vault," I said. My mouth had gone dry.

"Patience." He said it the way you'd flick water at a cat. "In her last winter, she summoned the keepers of the royal aviary. That was us, in those days — forty birds, ravens and crows and one insufferable magpie who is thankfully dead. We were not pets. We were the kingdom's second memory. Birds remember, thief. We remember in a way people can't, because we don't need our memories to make sense of ourselves. A woman forgets her wedding day and wonders who she is. A crow forgets a wedding day and simply finds another one. Memory sits lighter on us."

I thought about that. I thought about what it would be to carry the past like weather instead of like wounds.

"She gathered us in the vault beneath the palace," Ossifrage went on, "on the longest night of the year, with the rain coming down like the sky had decided to finish the job itself. And she gave us a charge. She said: *when I am gone, the name will go with me unless someone carries it. Names are heavier than you think. A name isn't what a thing is called. A name is why it keeps being.*"

Sef made a small sound. I looked over. His face in the firelight was doing something complicated — the look of a man hearing a story he'd been told fragments of all his life by people who only half-remembered telling him.

"She spoke it once," Ossifrase said. "Just once. Into a single memory — one moment, held perfectly, the way you hold water in cupped hands. She chose the vessel carefully. Not a bird. Birds live long but fly far, and far is dangerous. She chose a human memory, sealed in crystal and glass and silence, and she laid it in the deepest shelf of her vault, behind the regalia and the ransom-gold and all the glittering things kings collect to convince themselves they'll last forever. And she said to us: *guard the door. Forget what's inside. Forgetting is the lock.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere up the slope, a pine dropped its load of snow with a soft whump, and I nearly came out of my skin.

"So we forgot," Ossifrage said. "On purpose. All of us, together — we plucked the knowledge out of ourselves like feathers and left only the duty. Guard the vault. Trust no keeper. And if ever the country should fail—" he paused, and his voice did something I hadn't heard from him yet; it cracked, just slightly, like old ice, "—if ever the stars should go out and the land should vanish and the silence should come walking — then find the memory. Find whoever carries it. Because the name cannot be spoken twice into the world by accident. It must be carried home and given freely, or it will break again."

Silence, for a while. Real silence, not Hollow-silence — the ordinary kind, full of fire-crackle and wind and Sef breathing carefully through his nose.

"Seven years ago," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened, like a coin run over by a cartwheel. "There was a job. Deep vault, old locks, wards nobody had touched in decades. I went in for the ransom-gold. I came out with—" I gestured vaguely at my own skull, that crowded little warehouse. "I came out with a lot of things. I never inventoried them properly. There was one that wouldn't let go. One moment I couldn't give away no matter how hard I tried, and believe me, I tried, because keeping is against every rule I—"

"What does it look like?" Ossifrage asked sharply. "The moment. Describe it."

And here's the thing — I opened my mouth to describe it, and found I couldn't. Not the way you describe a stolen thing. Every memory I've ever lifted, I can turn over and examine, catalogue, price. This one I could only *be inside*. Rain on glass. An old woman's hands, spotted and steady. The sense of a word sitting in the room like a third person, enormous, patient, waiting to be needed.

"Rain," I said. "Hands. A word I can't hear and can't forget."

Ossifrage closed both eyes. When he opened them again, something had gone out of them that I suspect had been keeping him aloft for four centuries.

"That's her," he said softly. "That's the queen's last word. You're carrying it, thief. You have been carrying it for seven years, through smuggler roads and border towns and a hundred little thefts, humming to yourself about rules." He laughed, and it was a horrible sound. "We searched for decades. We tore the vault apart after the fall — what was left of us, what remembered enough to search. Nothing. The Choir got to the palace first and ate their way through the archives. And all the while the name was walking around in a cutpurse's head, getting rained on, getting drunk on, getting *haggled over*—"

"I traded a childhood for a horse once," I said, mostly because someone had to say something and it clearly wasn't going to be dignified. "Not mine. But still."

"*A horse*," Ossifrage repeated, in the tone of a man reading his own eulogy.

Sef had finally sat down. He was staring into the fire, and his face had gone the particular gray-white of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. I knew that face. I'd been wearing versions of it for weeks.

"Lena," he said. "If the queen hid the name in a memory, and the memory is in your head, and the Hollow eats memories—"

"—then I'm not a courier," I finished. "I'm a lunchbox."

"A what?" said the crow.

"Never mind."

But it wasn't a joke, and none of us pretended otherwise. Because I'd felt it — I'd been feeling it for weeks, that faint cold attention at the edge of the dark, the way the Hollow always seemed to find us a day later than it should have, a mile closer than it should have been. I'd told myself it was coincidence. Thieves are great believers in coincidence; it lets us sleep at night.

It wasn't coincidence. I was the loudest thing in a silent country. I was a struck bell trying to hide in a field of deaf men.

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I sat by the dying fire and turned the moment over and over, trying to see its edges, and every time I reached for it, it reached back.

That's the part I haven't confessed to anyone, so I may as well confess it here, to the dark, where confessions are cheap. All these years, I told myself I kept that one memory because it was beautiful. Collector's weakness. Fine vintage. And some of that was even true — it was beautiful, the way a mountain is beautiful, in a way that makes you feel briefly honest.

But underneath the wry accounting, underneath the professional detachment I wore like armor plate, there was a simpler truth, and it was this: it was the only thing I'd ever stolen that felt like it had chosen me back. Every other memory I'd lifted had been a stranger's — warm for a moment in my hands, then gone, handed off, spent. This one had settled into me and stayed, patient as the queen's own hands. As if somewhere under the rain and the glass, an old woman had looked at the thief who would someday come stumbling into her vault, and decided: *her. She'll do.*

Kings trust gold. Queens trust thieves. We're the only ones motivated to be careful.

Toward dawn, Ossifrage hopped down from his stone and stood at the edge of the firelight, regarding me with that judicial eye.

"You should know the rest of it," he said. "The part that isn't in the pretty version."

"There's a part that isn't in the pretty version? I feel like the pretty version ended with 'you're a lunchbox.'"

"The queen didn't just hide the name," the crow said. "She priced it. Magic that matters always costs, thief — anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. To speak her word aloud, at whatever door stands between silence and song, the speaker must pay in the only coin the land recognizes. Not blood. Not gold." His head tilted. "*Memory*. The name was spoken from a life, and it can only be returned by one. Whoever speaks it gives up what they love most — and the door takes it all, not a piece. Everything warm. Everything kept. The speaker walks through the other side of that door a stranger to their own heart."

The fire had burned down to coals. I looked at them for a long time.

"Well," I said at last. "Good thing I'm not planning to speak it. I'm just the delivery service. Find the heir, hand it off, collect my fee, retire to the coast. That's the whole job."

"Of course," Ossifrage said, with a dryness that suggested four hundred years of watching humans say exactly that.

Across the ashes, Sef stirred in his blanket-roll. Awake, then. Had he heard? The light was too gray to tell, and his face was turned away, and I was too tired, too full of rain-on-glass and spotted hands and the weight of a word shaped like a country, to check.

We broke camp in silence. The pines dripped. Somewhere north, past the drowned city and the dead roads, the First Door was waiting, and now I knew what waited with it: an invoice.

I'd spent my whole life pricing other people's memories. Warm moments, bitter ones, first kisses and last words — I could put a number on any of them and sleep soundly after.

For the first time, standing at the treeline with a crow on my shoulder and a kingdom in my skull, I wondered what mine would cost.

Ahead, the road bent toward Cael Morath, and the black water beyond it lay flat as a held breath.
