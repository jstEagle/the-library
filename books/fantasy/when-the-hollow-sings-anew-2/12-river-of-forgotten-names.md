## 12. River of Forgotten Names

* * *

**Lena**

There are three rules to crossing the Lethe-wood, and I learned all of them from a woman who'd forgotten she taught me.

One: don't drink the water, even if your throat burns like you've been chewing embers — especially then, because that's not thirst, that's the trees advertising.

Two: keep talking. A voice is a rope thrown back across a gap. Stop speaking, and the gap widens until you can't hear yourself on either side of it.

Three: if someone starts telling you who they are in the past tense, hit them.

I was explaining these to Sef at the treeline when he said, with the flat confidence of a man reading an official notice, "The Gray Choir has files on this place. It's classified as a low-priority anomaly."

"Files," I said.

"Documented observations."

"Sef. The Choir keeps records of a forest that eats names?"

He didn't answer, which was its own kind of answer. He'd gone quiet in a particular way since the ford — since his confession and my fury and whatever strange, harder thing we'd built on top of the wreckage of both. He talked less about himself now. Which, given where we were standing, was roughly equivalent to a man walking into a fire wearing paper.

The Lethe-wood did not look like much. That's the trick of it. From outside it was just birches, pale as bone in the gray light, with a river running silver between them — narrow enough to spit across in places, slow everywhere, moving without any current I could see. Water that flows uphill of nothing. The sound it made was almost speech. Almost. Like a word you'd heard in a dream and couldn't pull back into waking.

"It drinks identity," Sef said. "That's the file summary."

"The file summary," I repeated, "is wrong, or at least incomplete. It doesn't drink identity the way I steal memories, quick and surgical. It sips. All day. Every day. You walk through and come out the other side missing things you won't notice are missing." I looked up into the white branches. "These trees are full of people. Little pieces of them, anyway. Somewhere in there is my grandmother's laugh, probably. Somebody's first snow. A king's middle name."

"And you know this how? You've crossed before?"

"I grew up on the Thornfell roads, Sef. Everybody who smuggles knows somebody who went into the Lethe-wood and came out calling their own mother 'that woman.'" I tightened the straps on my pack, checked the wax-sealed jar of honey I carried for no reason I could name anymore — some habit of mine that had outlived its reason, which felt grimly appropriate. "We cross at the shallows by the split stone. We talk the whole way. Loud. And we hold hands."

His eyebrows went up. Even now, even hollowed-out and half-sworn to monsters, he had expressive eyebrows. It was one of the forty things about him I was never going to tell him I'd catalogued.

"Not romance," I said. "Tethering. If one of us drifts, the other feels the grip go slack."

"Of course," he said, too quickly, and looked at the river.

I took his hand anyway. His palm was rougher than I remembered, and I remembered everything, which was becoming less a blessing and more a job description every day.

"One more thing," I said. "If I start talking about myself in the past tense — hit me."

"With what?"

"Whatever's closest. You're a warder. Improvise."

* * *

**Sef**

The first hour was manageable.

Lena talked the way water runs downhill — constantly, finding every crack, impossible to dam. They narrated. That was the only word for it. They gave the wood a running commentary on itself: the crooked birch that looked like a man mid-flinch ("bad omen, ignore it"), the moss shaped like a handprint ("good omen, also ignore it"), the way the river-sound changed pitch when you stopped listening for words in it. They were performing, I understood that much. Lena performed competence the way I performed duty: as armor, as offering, as the only self we were sure anyone wanted.

But under the performance they were working. Every so often their fingers would tighten around mine — hard, deliberate, a pulse of pressure timed to nothing I could see — and I began to understand they were checking. Measuring how much grip it took to feel real. Calibrating the tether before we needed it.

Thief's arithmetic. Everything measured twice, stolen once.

"The water's rising," they said around what must have been the second hour. They were right. The silver river had widened without seeming to move, the way night widens. Where we'd planned to cross at the split stone there was now a channel hip-deep and broad as a road, and the split stone stood on a small island of pale roots like the last tooth in a jaw.

"That wasn't in the file," I said.

"The file," Lena said sweetly, "can bite me."

We waded. The water was cold in a way cold isn't supposed to work — not temperature but absence, cold like forgetting warmth rather than lacking it. It climbed my shins, my knees. And with every inch it climbed, something in my head got quieter.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was the last discipline the Choir left me and I cling to disciplines the way drowning men cling to anything. It wasn't voices vanishing. It was edges. My mother's face — I have my mother's face, I know that the way you know your own hands, and yet somewhere in the water's reach the specific fact of her softened, blurred, became a shape labeled *mother* instead of a woman with a chipped front tooth who sang off-key on purpose to make her sister laugh.

Wait. Whose sister?

Whose tooth?

"Lena," I said, and heard my own voice come out level, which frightened me more than shaking would have. "Talk faster."

They were already turned toward me, gripping my hand hard enough to grind bone. "Sef. Your eyes."

"What about them?"

"They went gray. Not dark — gray. Like someone poured milk in them." Their free hand came up, hovered near my cheek without touching. "Say your name."

"My name is—" 

And here is the thing I will remember for the rest of my life, however long the Hollow lets me keep it: there was a gap. A clean, polite, well-lit gap, like a display case in an empty museum. I knew I had a name. I knew people had spoken it ten thousand times. I reached for it and found the little brass plaque that should have held it, and the plaque read: *name withheld.*

"My name is," I finished, "withheld."

Something happened in Lena's face that I had never seen there in all our years of knowing each other, all the years before the world ended and the years since. Fear, yes, fear I'd seen. But this was fear arranged around something else. This was terror holding a lantern for love to see by.

"No," Lena said. "No no no, you don't get to do this, you absolute— okay. Okay." Their voice dropped, steadied, became the voice they used for talking people off ledges. "Rule three, Sef. I'm allowed to hit you. But first — listen. Listen to me."

"I'm listening."

"Do you remember the granary?"

I blinked. Milk-gray world, cold water climbing, and this stranger-shaped person asking me about a granary.

"What granary?"

"The one in Harrow's Bend. Harvest festival, six years ago. You were there. I was there." They stepped closer in the water, close enough that I could see their breath, and their grip on my hand was the only warm thing left in the universe. "You were nineteen and wearing a warder's coat two sizes too big because you'd just passed your trials and nobody in your family believed in tailoring. And there was a thief in the rafters."

"You were the thief," I said. The words came out of me from somewhere below memory, below thought — from the sediment, the bedrock, the part of a person the water hasn't reached yet.

"I was the thief," Lena agreed, grinning, and oh, that grin was a rope too, thrown across the gap. "And you caught me. Do you remember catching me?"

"You dropped a candlestick."

"A candlestick."

"On my head. From thirty feet. You robbed the festival vault and assaulted a junior warder with silverware, and when you climbed down to check if I was alive — which, incidentally, is the worst rescue plan I have ever witnessed—"

"—you were laughing," I said. The gap in me was still there, the name still withheld, but something else was surfacing now, rising through the cold like a drowned lamp coming up lit. "You were hanging upside down by your knees from a rafter beam, holding a stolen candlestick over my unconscious body like a trophy, and when I opened my eyes you laughed so hard you fell."

"I fell on you."

"You fell on me. And then you apologized by stealing my dinner."

"And you let me."

"I let you," I said, "because you were the most interesting thing that had ever happened in Harrow's Bend, and I was nineteen, and I had already decided I would follow you off the edge of the map." I stopped. The water pressed against my ribs. "Which appears to have been prophetic."

Lena's eyes were wet. In a forest that drank identity, standing in a river that erased names, the thief from the rafters was crying over a candlestick.

"There you are," they whispered. "There you are. Hold onto that. Hold onto me. The name will come back — the shallow stuff goes first, the deep stuff holds, you just have to—"

The river rose.

It didn't surge. Rivers here don't do violence; violence leaves evidence. It simply was suddenly higher, waist-deep, and the cold closed over the last warm places in me like a book closing, and I felt the granary start to slide. Not vanish — slide. The candlestick tilting. The laughter going distant. Lena's face in the rafters beginning to blur at the edges, becoming *a thief*, becoming *someone*, becoming the brass plaque again, *memory withheld*.

And underneath it, rising up from the deepest water of all, something older than the granary surfaced — not a memory but an oath, the Choir's cold liturgy, *order above all, order above name, order above self* — and I understood with perfect clarity that the river hadn't taken my name at all.

The Choir had sold it first. The river was only collecting the debt.

"No," I said aloud.

"Yes," said the water, in the voice of every tree at once, patient as rot. *Give it here. You weren't using it.*

"Lena." My voice cracked down the middle. "I can't find myself. There's nothing — the files took everything, the oath took everything, and now I can't even find the boy with the big coat, and I don't know what's left to hold onto—"

"Then hold onto this."

They kissed me.

In the middle of the Lethe-wood, waist-deep in the river of forgotten names, the thief I had followed off the edge of the map kissed me like an argument they intended to win. And it worked — gods forgive me, it worked — because a kiss is not information. A kiss cannot be filed, summarized, redacted, collected. It doesn't live in the head at all. It lives lower and deeper, in the marrow, in the pulse, in the animal fact of *someone chose you, someone is choosing you right now, in the teeth of the thing that unchooses everyone.*

My hand closed on theirs. My name — *Sef*, short for nothing, given by a mother with a chipped tooth who sang off-key — came back like blood returning to a numb limb, painful and hot and entirely mine.

When we broke apart, both gasping, the river had stopped rising. Whether the trees were satisfied or merely patient, I couldn't say.

"Don't," I managed, "ever do that again."

"Noted," Lena said. "Will absolutely do it again."

* * *

**Lena**

Here's what I learned in the Lethe-wood, and I'll write it down later because writing things down is apparently the only magic that works reliably anymore:

Memory is not the strongest anchor. I always thought it was — I built my whole rotten trade on the assumption that what's kept can't be lost. But Sef's memories of us nearly washed away, and one kiss held. Because a kiss isn't stored. It's *made*, fresh, in the moment, by two people insisting on each other. The river can drink everything you're keeping. It can't drink what you're making.

We came out the far side an hour past dusk, soaked to the sternum, and collapsed on the bank among ordinary honest pines that wouldn't touch our names with a stick.

Sef lay on his back breathing like a bellows. Then, quietly: "Thank you."

"For the kiss?"

"For knowing what I was before I was anything official." He stared at the sky. No stars. Still no stars, three weeks now, and I doubted I'd ever stop flinching at it. "The Choir told me my history started at my induction. I believed them, because believing them hurt less than wondering. But you remember a boy in an oversized coat, and that boy existed, and therefore I existed, and therefore they lied." A pause. "They lie about everything, don't they. I think they lied to me about bigger things than coats."

I sat up. Something in his tone snagged on me like a nail in cloth.

"Sef. What do you mean, bigger things?"

He was quiet long enough that the almost-speech of the distant river seemed loud by comparison. When he finally spoke, he wasn't looking at me. He was looking south, toward Cael Morath, toward the drowned city and the gate and the name I carry like a coal in my skull.

"When we get to the First Door," he said, "there's something you need to know about why it will open. And I need you to promise — before I tell you — that you'll remember I'm still the boy from the granary."

The coal in my skull went very cold, and very bright.

"Sef," I said slowly, "what did you hide?"
