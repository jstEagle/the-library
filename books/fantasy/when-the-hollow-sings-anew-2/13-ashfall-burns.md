## 13. Ashfall Burns

I have a theory about sleep in refugee camps. It goes like this: nobody actually sleeps. We just agree to lie down at the same time so the night feels less like an ambush and more like a negotiation we're losing politely.

I was mid-negotiation when the dogs started.

Not barking — Ashfall's dogs had gone quiet days ago, the way animals do when something wrong moves through the world and they'd rather not be introduced. This was the other kind of sound, the one that comes before: a low, collective whine, forty throats at once, rising like wind through a broken flute.

I was awake before I knew why, hand already under my bedroll for the knife I kept there out of habit even though knives are nearly useless against everything that matters now.

Sef was already up. Of course he was. The man slept the way cats sleep — technically, and with one ear deployed.

"No," he said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You were going to ask if it's the Choir."

"Is it the Choir?"

He stood in the tent flap, silhouetted against a sky that still had stars in it, which meant whatever was coming wasn't the Hollow itself. Small mercies. He tilted his head, listening to something I couldn't hear — his ward-sense, the sixth sense the Gray Choir grew in its people like a pearl around a wound. His face did something complicated.

"It's the Choir," he said.

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about Ashfall, so you can understand what we lost.

It wasn't a town. Towns have names, and names were getting expensive. Ashfall was a decision — four hundred people deciding, in the absence of any country telling them what to do, to build their tents on the same hillside and call it enough. There was a bread line that ran on barter and rumor. There was a woman named Odji who'd set up a forge out of a cart wheel and stubbornness and could mend anything except time. And there were the memory-tokens, little knotted cords the camp used instead of coin, because coin requires a kingdom behind it and ours had been unwritten.

And there was Maren.

Maren was the mother I've told myself I don't think about. Tall, hollow-cheeked, with two children she shielded the way a wall shields — constantly, unglamorously, by simply being between them and everything. Eight days ago, in the bread line, she'd sold her wedding day for flour. I'd watched her do it. Watched the broker's man lay his palm on her temple and draw out twenty years of one ordinary June like thread from a spool, watched her blink afterward with the faint bafflement of someone who can't remember why her hands smell of lilies.

She'd smiled at her daughters anyway. She always smiled at them anyway. That was the arithmetic of Ashfall: love divided by hunger, and hunger never quite won.

I steal memories for a living. Have since I was fifteen and learned that grief fits in a jar as easily as joy. And I want to tell you I felt uniquely horrified by Maren's sale, that it cut me differently than the trade I'd practiced all my adult life.

It didn't. It cut exactly the same, which was the problem. I recognized the transaction the way a pickpocket recognizes another pickpocket. Same hands. Same shrug afterward. *The memory is gone but the bread is real.* I'd told myself that sentence ten thousand times in ten thousand markets.

Watching Maren smile without her wedding day, I understood for the first time that the sentence has a second half nobody says out loud: *and someday you'll reach for who you were, and find only bread.*

* * *

"East perimeter," Sef said, pulling his gray coat on. The coat. The Choir colors. He wore it like a scar he'd decided not to explain anymore.

"How many?"

"Hard to say. The wards I strung on the ridge—" he stopped, jaw working. "They're not singing anymore."

"That bad?"

"Lena." He turned and looked at me in the dark of the tent, and his eyes had the flat shine they got when duty and something older were having one of their wrestling matches. "If the Choir is here, it's here for the name-bearer. It's here for you. And everyone in this camp is going to stand between us whether they know it or not."

"Then we wake them up."

"They're not soldiers—"

"They're not dead either. Yet." I was already moving, stuffing Ossifrage's travel satchel — yes, the crow had a satchel; no, I will not apologize; he has opinions about luggage — grabbing the ledger I'd started keeping, the maps, the little tin of salt-ward chalk that worked about as well as prayers but cost less.

Sef caught my arm at the tent flap. "There's a version of tonight where we run east right now and live."

"There's a version where we run east and live forever," I said, "and every night some part of us hears those dogs."

He let go. That's the thing about Sef I couldn't hate him for, however hard I tried: when it came down to it, he always, always chose the version of himself he could stand.

We went toward the fire.

* * *

The Choir didn't come screaming. Screaming is for people who need to feel something.

They came in a line, twelve of them down the eastern slope, torches held high and perfectly level, like a metronome. Their faces were the worst part, if you can rank parts of a nightmare — smooth-eyed, calm, mouths soft as men at prayer. The Gray Choir didn't wear masks. Masks would imply there was something left underneath worth covering.

Behind them, the tents began to burn. Not all at once — in order, west to east, methodical as a clerk ruling a ledger line. They weren't trying to kill the camp. Fire was punctuation. Fire was the question mark at the end of: *Give us the name-bearer.*

Ashfall woke up screaming after all. Not the Choir's kind of noise — the human kind, all ragged edges and love in it, mothers hauling children into the dark, old Odji coming out of her forge-cart swinging a hammer at a man in gray who simply stepped aside, unhurried, and set her cart alight.

I've done cruel things. I want that on the record, because what follows isn't a war story, it's a confession wearing one's clothes.

I moved through the smoke doing what I do.

A Choir swordsman had a family cornered against the ravine fence — father, son, a grandmother clutching a bundle that was probably everything she owned and definitely less than she'd lost. He raised his blade with the terrible patience of a man weeding a garden.

I slipped behind him and put my fingers to his temple and took.

You should know what taking feels like when you're in a hurry and angry. Sloppy. I didn't select. I hooked into the nearest bright strand and pulled, and whatever it was — a mother's voice singing? a river he'd swum as a boy? the taste of the last honest meal he'd ever eaten? — it came out of him in a rush and he dropped his sword and sat down in the ash and began to cry without knowing why.

The grandmother hit me over the head with her bundle in thanks. Or possibly in general. Refugee camps don't have time for nuance.

"Run," I rasped at the family, pointing west. "Ravine path, single file, don't stop for anything that talks."

"Anything that *talks*?" the father said.

"It'll make sense later," I lied, and ran on.

* * *

I found Maren near the bread-line awning, which had caught fire and was raining embers like orange snow. Her daughters weren't with her, which meant they were safe somewhere, because Maren's whole architecture was built around putting her girls elsewhere than danger.

She was standing in front of a Choir officer. Not fighting. That was the thing that stopped my blood — she wasn't fighting, and Maren fought weather.

He had his palm raised toward her temple, and I saw the gesture for what it was, because I'd made it a thousand times in a thousand darker rooms. A reading. A search. The Choir had gotten better at theft than I'd ever been, and they were riffling through her like a drawer.

"She doesn't have it," I called, stepping into the ember-light. "Whatever you're looking for. She sold everything interesting months ago. Believe me, I checked."

The officer turned. Under the gray hood his face was ordinary, which is the Choir's deepest obscenity — not monsters, just men with the doubt filed off.

"The name-bearer," he said. Conversational. "You're carrying something that hums."

"Funny. My mother said the same about me."

He gestured. Two choristers peeled off the burning line and came for me, unhurried, and I did the arithmetic I've done my whole life — exits, distances, odds — and hated the answer.

Then Sef fell out of the smoke onto the nearer one.

I want to be fair to Sef here, and fairness means saying plainly: he fought like a man settling accounts. No flourish, no anger he'd admit to, just gray-coat efficiency turned inside out, every technique of the Choir used against its own grammar. The first chorister went down confused. The second got a ward-sigil drawn in the air so fast it looked like a bird startled off a branch, and then got thrown backward by nothing at all.

The officer looked at Sef and did something worse than attack him.

He saluted.

"Warder," he said. "The Choir remembers your service."

"And I remember its price," Sef said. "Walk away from this camp."

"You know the doctrine. The name cannot be permitted to exist outside the Silence. You know what she carries."

"I know what she carries." Sef's voice didn't shake. I don't know how his voice didn't shake. "I'm asking you to walk away anyway. Once."

The officer considered. Behind him Ashfall burned, tent by tent, orderly as scripture. Somewhere Odji was still hammering at something. Dogs howled at a sky that still, insultingly, had its stars.

"No," the officer said, gently, the way you refuse a child.

So I stole his memory of the last hour.

All of it — the march down the slope, the orders given, the name-bearer hunt entire. I put my hand on his neck while Sef held his arms and I drank the way I've never drunk, greedy and graceless, tearing the evening out of him in ribbons. When I finished he swayed, blinking at the fire like a man who'd wandered into a disaster he had no part in, and I shoved him hard and he stumbled back up the hill the way you do when your body knows to flee even when your mind has lost the reason.

It bought us minutes. Minutes are a currency too. Ask anyone in Ashfall.

* * *

I found Maren's daughters by finding Maren, because she was running toward the fire while everyone else ran from it, and I grabbed her around the waist and hauled her behind the bread ovens where the girls were huddled under a tarp with Odji's grandnephew, all of them soot-faced and impossibly alive.

"Maren." I shook her shoulders. She was shaking worse. "The ravine path. West. Single file. Don't stop for anything that talks — trust me on this, it's become relevant advice."

She stared at me. And then she did the thing I will carry longer than most of my memories, which is saying something, given my profession.

She said, "Take it back."

"Take what back?"

"My wedding day." Her hands closed on my wrists, and her grip was iron and grief. "The broker's man. He kept it — they always keep them, they resell, I found out, I asked — you're one of them, aren't you, you have the hands, take it back, bring it home, please—"

"Maren—"

"My daughters ask me about it." Her voice cracked down the middle like drought ground. "Little Kess asked me what Papa looked like when he was happy, and I couldn't — I sold the only — I remember *that* I loved him, I remember it like a number, but I can't *see* him, Lena, and they're burning everything, and if I die tonight without it—"

The camp collapsed another notch. A storage tent folded into itself with a sound like a sigh, and sparks went up in a column, and against that column the Choir's line re-formed, patient, inevitable, twelve minus the ones we'd stopped becoming eleven becoming a problem we could not solve twice.

"I can't," I said.

It came out small. I've said a lot of hard things in my life with a grin on them — threats, lies, prices — but this came out bare.

"I can't get it back tonight. Tonight I can get *you* back. To your girls. Down the ravine. That's the whole of what I have, Maren, and I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I have stolen a hundred things worth more than your wedding day and I'd trade every one to—"

I stopped. Because I heard myself. Because the sentence finished itself somewhere below language and showed me its shape.

*I'd trade every one.*

Every one.

Hold that thought. Hold it the way I'm holding it now, because it changed the shape of everything that came after, though I didn't know it yet. All I knew in that burning moment was that Maren let go of my wrists, nodded once — mothers nod like that, filing their grief for later because later is a luxury and children are now — and gathered her daughters, and ran for the ravine, alive, diminished, alive.

And I stood in the ember-snow of a camp I'd slept in for nine nights and understood that I was done.

Done stealing for coin. Done stealing for survival. Done with the arithmetic where the past is currency and the poor pay in themselves. The Hollow eats memory and the Choir harvests it and brokers bottle it and I — I had spent my whole life telling myself I was different because I never kept what I took.

Never keeping it wasn't innocence. It was cowardice with clean hands.

Some things shouldn't circulate. Some things should be carried home.

* * *

By the time the eastern sky went the color of old dishwater, Ashfall was a field of chimneys and questions. Four hundred people scattered into the hills like spilled seed. The Choir withdrew — they hadn't found what they came for, and the Choir doesn't waste effort on mayhem for its own sake; mayhem was only ever the envelope, and they'd delivered the message: *we know roughly where you are, and we are patient.*

Sef found me sitting on the cold bread ovens, watching smoke write nothing in particular against the paling sky. Ossifrage landed on my shoulder — the crow had spent the raid, as far as I could tell, supervising disapprovingly from a tall pine — and pressed his horrible wise head against my cheek, which is crow for *you are distressed and also I am hungry.*

"You saved them," Sef said. "Most of them."

"I couldn't save her memories." My voice sounded scraped. "She sold her wedding day for flour, Sef. Flour. And the broker still has it, sitting in some vault like — like a jar of honey nobody's going to eat. And I stood there and told her I couldn't get it back, and it was true tonight, but it doesn't have to be true."

He sat down beside me. Didn't touch me. Sef never touched me unless the touching was load-bearing.

"Lena," he said carefully, "if you're about to propose robbing the Choir's memory-vaults across half a dead kingdom, I should tell you the roads are—"

"Not the vaults." I looked at him, and the thing below my ribs had stopped being guilt and hardened into something with edges, something you could build with or cut with. "Everything. Sef. Every jar, every cord, every stolen June in every market between here and the sea. The Hollow is eating memory because Vael forgot its own name. Fine. Then memory is the whole war — not the name alone, all of it, every wedding day and sister's face and first kiss anyone ever sold out of desperation. Somebody has to start carrying them *home.*"

Silence. Smoke. Ossifrage made a sound like a rusty hinge, which is crow for *continue, this interests me.*

"That's madness," Sef said softly.

"Yes."

"The Choir will hunt you for the name and now you want to add every broker and fence and Choir quartermaster on the continent—"

"Yes."

"You'd need a network. Safehouses. People. You'd need—" He stopped. I watched the realization move across his face like weather, and something in him flinched from it and something older in him leaned toward it, and I understood I'd just recruited him without meaning to, the way you recruit anyone: by wanting something impossible out loud where the right person can hear.

"You'd need a thief," he said quietly.

"A very good one." I stood, and ash sifted off my coat, and behind us Ashfall went on smoking over its empty hearths like a candle blown out mid-prayer. "But first we cross the Lethe-wood, and first we find Cael Morath, and first I return one specific wedding day to one specific impossible woman, because if I can't keep that promise, the big ones are just vanity wearing armor."

Sef rose too. He was quiet a long moment, looking west toward the ravine path where four hundred strangers were learning to be refugees again.

Then he said, in a voice I didn't know yet and would learn to dread:

"There's something you should know about how the Choir found this camp so fast."
