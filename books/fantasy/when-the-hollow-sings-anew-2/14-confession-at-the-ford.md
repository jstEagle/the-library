## 14. Confession at the Ford

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that warders learn early, and it has nothing to do with wards. It is the arithmetic of what you can carry. How much guilt fits in a man before it starts to slosh over the sides and stain everything he touches. I had been doing the sums wrong for years, carrying too much and admitting none of it, and by the time we reached the ford below the Lethe-wood, I had been doing sums all night without once arriving at a number I could live with.

Lena crossed the water ahead of me, boots in hand, trousers rolled to the knee, picking their way over the stones like a heron that had learned to steal. The river ran shallow and quick here, silver-gray in the pre-dawn, and it made a sound like someone whispering a list. Given where we were headed — given that the wood on the far bank drank names out of passing travelers — I did not find the whispering comforting.

"You're brooding," Lena said, not turning around. "I can hear it from here. It sounds like a millstone with opinions."

"I don't brood."

"You've been brooding since Ashfall. You brood in iambic. It's very tiresome." They hopped the last stone, shook a foot at the cold, and sat on the bank to pull their boots back on. "Sit down, Sef. Whatever it is, you might as well say it while my hands are occupied and I can't gesture dramatically."

I stayed standing. Cowardice enjoys a good vantage point.

The truth was this: three nights past, in the reek of Ashfall's burning stables, with smoke still climbing the sky and the mother's sold wedding day still a raw wound between us, I had knelt at a gray shrine — one of the Choir's way-altars, a flat stone marked with a circle that meant nothing and everything — and I had spoken our route into it. Not all of it. A thief's confession, partial, hedged: *they move toward Cael Morath, by the north road or the Lethe-wood, depending on the water.* That was all. A sentence. It had taken less time than tying a bootlace.

I told myself it was strategy. Feed them a little, steer them wrong later. Every spy learns the litany. Every spy believes it, right up until the moment the thing they fed comes back with teeth.

The Choir had come to Ashfall within two days of my report.

They had burned the camp looking for the name-bearer — for Lena — and I had stood in the smoke with ash on my face telling myself the timing was coincidence, that the Choir had other ears, that a gray altar is just a stone. And then last night, while Lena slept and I kept watch, I had felt the Choir's nearness in my oath-bone like a struck bell, and I had understood, with the terrible clarity that only arrives when it can no longer be useful, that no one else had heard us. No crow had sung of us. No refugee had sold us. I had done it. One sentence, kneeling, and a camp full of strangers had paid for it in fire.

And the mother — the one whose wedding day Lena had watched get traded away like a coat in winter — she had run into the dark barefoot, alive, but everything else she'd sold was gone forever, and some portion of that ledger bore my handwriting.

"Sit," Lena said again, patting the grass beside them. "You're making the morning nervous."

I sat. I did not say anything for a while. The river listed its names. Somewhere behind us, the Lethe-wood waited with its patient drinking trees, and somewhere ahead of us was a drowned city and a door and a name I wasn't supposed to know mattered, and all of it — every step of the road behind and ahead — was contaminated now, because they knew. They knew where we were going. They had known since the smoke.

"Lena," I said. "At Ashfall. Before the fires."

"Mm."

"There's something you should know, and there's no version of it that isn't awful, so I'm going to say it plainly and then you can do whatever you like."

That got their attention. They stopped wrestling with a bootlace and looked at me, and I want it recorded that their eyes were steady, that they gave me the courtesy of their whole attention before I earned none of it.

"I reported you," I said. "Three days ago. There's a Choir way-shrine in the stable quarter — you probably walked past it a dozen times, they're underfoot everywhere in that camp, half the refugees step on them without knowing. I knelt at it and I told them our route. Toward Cael Morath. By the north road or the ford." My voice did something embarrassing on the last word; I pushed through it. "Two days later they burned the camp."

The silence afterward was the longest I have ever stood inside. Even the river seemed to hold its list.

Lena looked at me for perhaps ten heartbeats. Then they looked away, out at the water, and began — slowly, deliberately, with enormous care — to tie their bootlace. Their hands were not shaking. I almost wished they were. Steady hands meant they were somewhere far past shaking.

"The mother," they said finally. "The one who sold her wedding day. She got out because I dragged her. She lost everything else because—" They stopped. Started again. "Because you knelt on a stone and said a sentence."

"Yes."

"How long have you known? That it was you?"

"Since last night."

"A whole night." They laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard in weeks, and I have heard the Hollow eat a village. "A whole night to rehearse. Was this the draft that won? *I reported you, and also, good morning?*"

"Lena—"

"No. You had your turn. You said it plainly, very brave, very tidy, and now I get mine." They stood up. They were not tall, Lena; they never needed to be. "Do you know what I thought, when Ashfall burned? I thought, *the Choir is thorough.* I thought, *they have informants everywhere, of course they found us, of course.* I blamed crows. I blamed refugees. I blamed myself for sleeping too deep, for humming, for letting the stolen memory pull at me like a bell in my skull. I lay awake inventing enemies." Their voice cracked along its middle seam. "And the whole time, the informant was holding my horse."

"I held your horse because I was going to tell you."

"Oh, well. Points for scheduling."

"I was trying to find a way to say it that didn't—" I stopped, because the honest end of that sentence was *that didn't cost me anything*, and Lena was already nodding, having clearly arrived at the same destination by a faster road.

"That's the trade, isn't it?" they said. "That's always been the trade. You want to confess without paying for it. Everyone does. I've robbed forty houses and never once apologized to a sleeper, because apologizing wakes them up." They kicked a stone into the river. It went in without a splash, swallowed whole. "But here's what you don't understand about theft, Sef, and you of all people should understand it by now. When you take from someone, you don't just take the thing. You take their certainty. Every person I've ever robbed sleeps differently afterward, even if they never find out. Some part of them knows the world is thinner than they thought. That's the real theft. And you—" they jabbed a finger at me, and their hand was shaking now, finally, uselessly late, "—you took my certainty. I trusted my instincts around you. My instincts are the only thing I've ever owned outright. Everything else is borrowed, stolen, secondhand. But my instincts were *mine*. And they said: the warder is true. The warder chose us over his masters. And my instincts were singing a song you'd already sold."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you're standing there wearing your shame like it's a fine coat. Look at me, look how sorry I am, aren't I noble for saying it. Saying it isn't the payment, Sef. Saying it is just the bill."

The river whispered. Far off, too far to matter yet, I could feel the Lethe-wood in my ward-sense — a dull pressure, like weather. Or like an audience.

"What happens now?" I asked. "You need my ward-sense for the crossing. The trees will drink you hollow without me reading the safe paths. And I—" I swallowed the rest, which was *and I cannot do this without you*, because it sounded too much like pleading and not enough like penance.

"Now?" Lena sat back down on the bank, as if their legs had simply filed a complaint and been granted leave. They laced the second boot with savage precision. "Now nothing changes, which is the cruelest thing I can think of. We cross the wood, because the wood is the only road they don't expect, except they'll expect it now too, won't they, because you told them north road or ford." A bitter little laugh. "We go on. We find the door. We restore a country. And every hour of every day, I will be doing sums in my head — *is this the moment, is this the stone, is this the dark road where he finishes the job* — and I will hate myself for the sums, because I'll know most of them are nonsense, and I'll keep doing them anyway. That's what you've stolen. Not my route. My peace."

"I would rather you hit me," I said quietly.

"I know you would. Violence settles accounts. It's clean. This is messier, so you get the mess." They finally looked at me again, and there was something in their face that hadn't been there before — not forgiveness, nothing so generous, but a kind of hard, clear appraisal, the look of a professional evaluating goods. "Tell me why. Not the strategy. Not *I fed them a false trail*. The real reason. The reason you knelt."

And here is where I could have lied, and I considered it, briefly, the way a drowning man considers air. But Lena Underhill steals memories for coin, and if anyone in this broken country could spot a counterfeit, it was them.

"Because I'm still theirs," I said. "In my bones. The Choir took my oath and put something where my doubts used to be, and most days I can't tell which impulses are mine and which ones are theirs, wearing my voice. Kneeling at that altar felt — it felt like breathing after holding your breath for months. It felt like relief. And I hate that, and I did it anyway, and then I spent two days constructing reasons why it had been clever." I looked at the water. "I don't know if I left the Choir or the Choir left a Sef-shaped hole where I used to stand. That's the real reason. Not strategy. Habit. I knelt because kneeling is what's left of me."

Lena was quiet a long moment.

"Well," they said at last, and their voice had changed — not warm, nothing so easy, but the fury had drained out of it and left something more durable underneath, bedrock under topsoil. "That's the first true thing you've said since the shrine, I'd wager. It's ugly enough to be true. Pretty confessions are always lies."

"Mostly."

"Mostly." They pulled their knees up. The light was strengthening now, gray going to pale gold, and the Lethe-wood across the water showed its true colors: black trunks, white leaves, and between the trees, if you looked too long, shapes that might have been shadows or might have been people who'd walked in full and walked out abbreviated. "Here are the harder terms, warder, so listen, because I'll only say this once. First: no more altars. If the urge to kneel comes on you, you tell me. Out loud. Even if it's mid-river. Especially mid-river."

"Agreed."

"Second: you don't protect me from information again. Not to spare me, not for strategy, not ever. I'd rather drown knowing the depth than sail blind. Third—" they hesitated, and for a heartbeat they looked very young, and very tired, and I understood that this third term was going to cost them more than it cost me, "—third. When this is over. If there's an *over*, if any of us walk out of the drowned city with a country in our mouths. You don't get to apologize your way back to whatever we were on the road before Ashfall. You earn whatever comes next. Slowly. With receipts."

"With receipts," I repeated. It was so absurdly, perfectly Lena that my chest hurt.

"Every kindness documented. Every debt entered in the ledger. I'm a thief, Sef; I know how books get balanced. Nobody balances them with good intentions."

The river whispered its list. I thought about standing, about offering a hand up, and decided against it — some gestures would read as presumption now, and I had spent my allowance on presumption for the season. So instead I took off my own boots, rolled my own trousers, and prepared to cross beside them like an equal, which is to say: like two people who had agreed to keep walking together while carefully not touching.

"For what it's worth," I said, "the ledger idea — writing things down so the Hollow can't take them — I've been keeping one. For you. Since Ashfall."

That did stop them. They turned, one boot in hand, and stared. "You've been — what?"

"Entries. Things you did. Things you said. Small things, mostly. The way you checked on Ossifrage's wing twice a night even though the bird hates you. The bread you gave the mother before she asked. Your handwriting slants left when you're tired." I shrugged, feeling suddenly naked in a way that had nothing to do with boots. "Paper doesn't forget. It seemed like the only weapon we had that the Hollow can't eat."

Lena looked at me for a long time. The fury was gone entirely now, and what replaced it I couldn't read — something moving behind glass, something not yet ready to be handled.

"Add one more entry," they said finally, turning toward the water. "This morning. *Lena forgave nothing, and crossed the river anyway.*"

They stepped off the bank onto the first stone, and the current parted around their ankles, and on the far shore the Lethe-wood stood waiting with its thousand white leaves and its patient thirst, and that was when Ossifrage came out of the reeds like a thrown black coat, wings hammering, and screamed at us in a voice like a slammed crypt:

*"They're in the wood already! Gray cloaks, six of them, walking the dry paths — they knew the ford, they knew the ford, someone TOLD them—"*

Lena went very still on the stone, mid-river, and did not look back at me.

They didn't have to.

* * *

We crossed the rest of the water at a dead sprint, boots strung around our necks, Ossifrage cursing overhead in three registers and two dead languages. And as the first white leaves closed over our heads, I understood exactly what kind of day this was going to be: the trees would drink at our names, the Choir would hunt us through paths only I could read, and the only person in either world whose trust I had just finished demolishing was about to learn whether my sense of direction was worth more than my word.

The trees began to whisper, and I recognized, with a chill, the sound of my own voice among them.
