## 15. The Hollow's Anatomy

The library didn't exist.

I want to be precise about that, because everything else that night depended on it. The building had never stood where we found it — Sef and I checked the map twice, then the ground, then each other's faces for signs we'd both gone soft in the head. There was no library at the bottom of the ravine off the old pilgrim road. There was a scorch mark shaped like nothing in particular, a scatter of foundation stones that geology could not account for, and a door.

The door was oak, iron-banded, and standing alone in the rain like a monument to someone who'd forgotten to build the rest of the house.

"You see it too," I said. Not a question. In the weeks since the stars went out I'd learned to ask that constantly, because the alternative was trusting my own skull, and my own skull had recently proven itself a bad landlord.

"I see a door with no wall," Sef said. His hand rested on his sword the way it always did when his duty and his judgment were having one of their arguments. "Which means either it's a trap, or it's a door."

"Those are usually the same thing on this road."

He almost smiled. He'd been doing that more lately — almost-smiling, catching himself at it, filing it away somewhere behind the Choir-gray of him. I'd stopped pretending I didn't notice. Some things you keep even when keeping them is stupid.

The door opened before either of us touched it. That should have been my first warning. My second warning was the smell: dust and lamp-oil and vellum, the particular warm-paper scent of every archive I'd ever robbed, which meant somewhere inside, something was remembering what libraries were supposed to smell like, because the actual air out here smelled of wet ash and nothing.

I went in anyway. Brannoc used to say I had two speeds: careful and curious, and curiosity always got the last word. He wasn't wrong. He also wasn't here anymore, and there are holes where he used to be that ache like pulled teeth, so I try not to think about him directly. You learn to think around grief. It's a thief's skill, really. We're trained from the first lift to move through a room without disturbing anything.

Inside, the library existed after all.

Not rebuilt — remembered. Shelves rose into darkness that had no ceiling above them, just more shelves, receding upward like the idea of shelves. Lamps burned without flame. Books lined every surface, thousands of them, tens of thousands, spines cracked and titles gilt-faded, and when I pulled one down at random it came apart in my hands like wet bread, because paper can only hold so much forgetting before it gives up.

A woman sat at a reading desk beneath a lamp that cast light but no warmth. She was translucent the way breath on glass is translucent — there and not-there, depending on how hard you insisted. She wore scholar's robes gone gray as her skin, and she was reading a book that no longer had any words in it, turning pages that were only texture now, and she looked up at us with the expression of someone interrupted mid-sentence by the end of the world.

"Oh good," she said. "Visitors. Do you know, I've been sitting here for — I want to say eleven years? The calendar stopped being useful some time ago." She set the blank book down. "You're not Choir. Thank all the small gods. Come in, come in, mind the third shelf, it's mostly suggestion by now."

Sef's hand hadn't left his sword. "What is this place?"

"The Royal Athenaeum of Vael," she said, "or the memory of it, or the ghost of its memory. Ilvane. Senior archivist, junior lecturer, and currently the only member of staff." She gestured at the vast dark. "Everyone else evacuated when the wards fell. I stayed to catalog the loss. Someone has to write down what's being erased, even if the writing erases too. It's the principle of the thing."

"And you're dead," I said.

"Mostly. It's a spectrum." She peered at me, and something in my face must have interested her, because her eyes narrowed the way a scholar's do when they've spotted an error in a manuscript. "You're carrying something heavy."

"I beg your pardon?"

"In your skull. Something dense. Old. It hums." She tilted her head. "You've been near the Hollow a great deal and it hasn't eaten you, which means whatever you're carrying is either poison to it or food it's saving for last. Both options interest me enormously. Sit down, thief. I don't say that as an accusation. I say it as a diagnosis. You have the walk of someone who lifts things. Also your boots are wrong for a pilgrim and right for a burglar, and you keep glancing at the shelves the way other people glance at jewelry."

I sat. Sef did not, but he did stop looking quite so much like he was about to run me through, which from him was practically a hug.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the Hollow, going in: it eats memories. Here is what everyone knew about the Hollow, going in: it eats memories, it moves like weather, it has no face except borrowed ones, and it came the same night the border wards shattered and Vael stopped existing everywhere except in the heads of people like me who were outside the borders when the lights went down.

That's not much. That's a child's description of a wolf. Fur, teeth, comes at night.

Ilvane gave me the anatomy.

"First error everyone makes," she said, pacing between the shelves — her feet made no sound at all, which I noticed the way a thief notices everything, professionally — "is thinking the Hollow is a creature. A beast that wandered in from somewhere else. People want it to be an invader, because invaders can be fought, expelled, killed. But the Hollow isn't from outside Vael." She stopped. She turned, and her ghost-light flickered. "It's from inside. It's ours. We made it, the way a body makes a fever."

"How does a country make a monster?" Sef asked. Flat voice. I'd learned by then to hear what lived under the flatness — this time it was fear, tightly filed.

"By refusing to grieve properly." Ilvane resumed her pacing. "Let me tell you about the queen's death, since apparently nobody tells anyone anything anymore. Queen Marenthe, last of the Aureline line, died four years ago. Not suddenly — slowly, badly, a wasting sickness the physicians couldn't name. And here's the thing about a queen: she isn't a person, she's a load-bearing wall. The whole architecture of the kingdom rests on her. The border wards aren't stone, boy warder, you know this better than I do — they're oaths. Spoken words, renewed each generation, anchored in the royal bloodline. The wards sing because someone taught them a song."

"The wards sing," I said quietly. I hadn't told many people that I heard them. Ilvane caught it instantly, of course. Scholars and thieves, we're both paid to notice.

"Yes. And when Marenthe lay dying, she did something unprecedented. She tried to speak the land's true name into a single vessel — one memory, sealed in the vault — so that the name would survive her, so the succession would have something to stand on. The true name of Vael isn't a word, it's a *keystone*. Every oath, every ward, every boundary and birthright hangs on it. Normally it passes crown to crown, mouth to ear, living to living. Marenthe was the last living mouth." Her voice thinned. "She spoke it. It was sealed. And then the court did the most human thing imaginable."

"They hid it," Sef said.

"They hid it, and they *grieved wrong*. Oh, they mourned — there were forty days of bells, the whole performance. Public grief. Ceremonial grief. Grief as state theater. But the true thing, the raw thing, the scream underneath — nobody let that exist. The court couldn't afford to fall apart, so it didn't. The heir was spirited away for protection. The vault was sealed. The name was locked away where no one could speak it, and every soul in that palace swallowed their sorrow whole and went on administering tariffs." She spread her translucent hands. "Grief doesn't vanish because you refuse to perform it. It goes underground. It ferments. And a kingdom's grief is not a private matter — a kingdom's grief has *weight*, and weight has consequences, and four years of unsaid mourning built up behind the walls of the world like floodwater behind a dam."

"So when the wards broke—" I started.

"The wards didn't break. They *drowned*. The Hollow is the water. It's every unspoken word, every swallowed sob, every memorial that was held in a whisper because speaking loudly felt dangerous. It's Marenthe's death, unwept. It's a country that lost its mother and was told to keep working." She said it simply, without drama, the way you'd read a diagnosis aloud. "The Hollow isn't eating memories out of malice. It's eating them because that's the only way it knows to grieve. It takes what it cannot express. It swallows remembrance because remembrance hurts. And it will not stop, it *cannot* stop, until someone speaks the name again — until the keystone is set back and the country can finally, properly, out loud, mourn its queen and live."

The lamp above her desk guttered, though there was no wind. I realized my hands were shaking and made them stop, because a thief's hands obey orders or the thief doesn't eat.

"You're telling me," I said, "it's not a predator. It's a wound."

"It's an abscess," Ilvane said. "And you can cut it open all day long, warder, you and your Choir with your gray hoods and your tidy protocols — you can carve it smaller, chase it across the map, burn villages to starve it of rememberers. You'll buy time. You will not heal it. Abscesses don't respond to amputation. They respond to drainage. To letting the poison *out*." Her eyes fixed on me again, and this time there was no mistaking it: she was looking through me, at the thing I carried, the humming weight I'd been hauling around for years without knowing what it was. "And that requires the name. Which requires the vessel. Which is currently lodged, if I'm any judge, in the head of the young woman drinking my tea that isn't there."

I looked down. There was, in fact, a cup in front of me. I hadn't picked it up; the library had simply provided it, the way a dream provides furniture. I hadn't drunk from it either, but somehow I felt warmer, which told me exactly how deep I was in a place that ran on rules older than physics.

"I stole it," I said. Because lying to a ghost seemed pointless, and lying to myself had stopped working somewhere around the night the stars went out. "Years ago. A job in the capital. Vault job. I took a dozen memories off the inventory and sold nine, kept three, and one of them was — noise. A word in a dying voice. I kept it because it felt expensive. That's my rule. Never keep what you steal, except when the goods feel expensive, and then I keep them like a magpie, like a dragon, like an idiot."

"Do you know what you've been carrying?" Ilvane asked softly.

"A word."

"The word. The last living utterance of the true name of Vael, spoken by its dying queen into a sealed memory so it would survive her." She leaned forward. "Every warder, every Choir agent, every hollowed thing in this broken country is hunting for that vessel whether they know it or not. The Hollow wants it because it wants the grief to end and doesn't know how. The Gray Choir wants it because control of the name means control of the country's rebirth — imagine the leash you could hold. And you—" she pointed one insubstantial finger at my chest— "you have been walking around with the entire kingdom folded up in your pocket like a stolen watch, selling stranger's childhoods for passage money, complaining about your loneliness."

"That's unfair," I said. "I complain very little."

"Lena." Sef said my name like a warning, but I waved him off. What was he going to do, arrest me? For what? Grand theft, sovereign nation?

* * *

We talked for hours, or the local equivalent of hours, in a place where the lamps never needed trimming. Ilvane answered everything — she was an archivist, answering questions was her whole skeleton, and I suspect part of why she'd stayed behind to haunt a demolished building was precisely this: the hope that someday someone would come asking the right questions, and she could finally hand over the catalog of the loss.

The Hollow, she explained, wears faces because it ate them. It offers memories back because it remembers offering, remembers comfort, remembers being a person who once dried someone's tears — it's a chorus of everyone it consumed, singing the only song it knows, which is *here, take this back, don't cry.* It grows stronger near places of concentrated forgetting. It cannot cross running water that has been named. It fears — and this she said with real scholarly relish — it fears *witnessed speech*. Things spoken aloud, in front of others, with witnesses who repeat them. Memory locked in one skull is fragile. Memory shared is armor.

"That's why it hunts the name-bearer first," she said. "One skull. One point of failure. If you die, Lena Underhill—" I hadn't told her my name either, but the library apparently read its guests the way I read rooms— "if you die, the name dies with you, and Vael becomes permanent silence. Not destroyed. Just... unspoken. Forever. The difference matters. Destroyed things can be rebuilt. Unspoken things can't even be missed."

Sef had gone very still during that part. I thought it was soldier's discipline. Later I'd understand it was something worse — a man hearing his own biography described as a state secret. But that night I just saw a tired warder listening to a dead librarian, and I thought: we're all carrying more than we say.

"One more thing," Ilvane said, as we turned to leave. The door was still there, patient, though I'd have sworn the rain outside had stopped and started twice. "The heir. Marenthe's heir. Everyone assumes the line died with the succession, but blood doesn't need paperwork to persist. Blood remembers. Somewhere in this broken country walks a person whose veins still hold the old oath, who could open the First Door at Cael Morath — could open it *and pay its price* — and set the name back where it belongs. Find the vessel. Find the heir. Speak the name at the Door, with witnesses, out loud, in grief and in defiance. Do those three things and the Hollow drains, the wards relearn their song, and the stars come home."

"And if we fail?" I asked.

"Then keep walking," she said, "and enjoy the quiet. It's the only thing the Hollow leaves."

* * *

Outside, the rain had genuinely stopped. The ravine was dark and ordinary and the door was gone — not closed, *gone*, no hinge-scar, no foundation stones, just hillside, as if the hill had decided to stop pretending. I checked anyway. Habit.

Sef stood beside me in the wet grass, and neither of us spoke for a long moment, because there are conversations that need the silence around them to hold their shape.

"The heir," he finally said. Careful. Too careful. "Could be anyone. Could be dead."

"Could be," I agreed. "But Ilvane said blood remembers. And I've been thinking — the Choir keeps its people close, doesn't it? Takes them young? Swears them in before they're old enough to know what they're swearing away?"

His jaw tightened. In the dark I couldn't read his eyes, and I'm very good at reading eyes; it's half my trade. That blankness was the loudest thing he'd ever not said.

"There's a legend," he said at last, each word placed like a foot on ice. "Among the Choir. About an heir who was taken in, protected, raised in gray. Most of us think it's a story to keep the ranks loyal. A fairy tale about mercy."

"And the rest of you?"

He didn't answer. He started walking toward the road, and I followed, and the question walked with us, patient as the Hollow, all the way down into the dark.

Somewhere behind us, in a library that had never been built, a dead woman turned another blank page, cataloging our departure.
