## 16. To Steal a Kingdom

I had spent my whole life learning how to take things out of heads. Nobody had ever taught me the reverse.

That was the problem with Ilvane's revelation, sitting in me like a swallowed stone: it wasn't a plan, not yet. It was just a terrible fact wearing a plan's clothes. The Hollow wasn't a monster that had come for Vael. It *was* Vael — or the grief Vael had never spoken aloud, the queen dying nameless in her vault-dark room while her country went on singing songs with the wrong words and calling itself by whatever name the maps had settled on. A kingdom that forgot to say its own name at its own bedside. And grief unspoken doesn't dissipate. I knew that better than most. Grief unspoken gets stolen, hoarded, traded in back alleys for bread. It grows teeth.

So there we were, Sef and I, in a library that had stopped existing, being lectured by a ghost who kept adjusting spectacles she no longer technically possessed.

"You're saying," I said slowly, "that if someone speaks the true name at the First Door—"

"The wound closes," Ilvane said. "Or rather, the wound is *answered*. A name spoken by a willing voice, carried from the queen's own memory, offered rather than taken." Her ghostly finger tapped the lectern, passing through it slightly on the downbeat. "The Hollow has been waiting to be heard, child. Not fought. Heard."

"And if nobody hears it?"

"Then it keeps eating until there's nothing left to eat, and then—" she spread her hands, an elegant shrug that had probably ended debates in this library when the library still had walls, "—there is no *then*."

Sef hadn't moved in a long time. He stood near the doorway of the non-existent library with his arms crossed, which I'd learned was his way of holding himself together when his insides were arguing. Warders did that. Everything squared away, everything guarded, even their own doubt.

"No," he said.

I turned. "No what? I haven't asked anything yet."

"You're about to ask whether we can carry the memory to the heir and speak the name. And the answer is no." His jaw worked. "Lena. There *is* no heir."

"There's always an heir. Kingdoms are very good at producing them."

"Not this one." He said it flatly, the way you'd say *the well is poisoned* or *the bridge is out* — facts you don't argue with because arguing wastes breath you'll need for running. "The royal line of Vael ended the night the queen died. That's not Choir doctrine, that's history. The succession failed. The regents fought for eleven years, and then the Choir came offering order instead of blood, and everyone was so tired of blood they said yes." He looked at me, and there was something underneath the flatness, something moving like a fish under ice. "The heir is a story we tell new recruits. A cautionary tale. *The heir who chose chains* — that's what Mother Vesper calls them. Someone who supposedly abandoned their birthright and swore to the Choir instead. It's a lesson about treachery. About how bloodlines make bad oaths."

"And you believe the story?"

"I *was* the story," he said, too fast, and then caught himself, and I watched him decide to be careful again. "I mean — I've heard it enough times."

Hmm.

Now, I steal memories for a living, so let me tell you something about people: they don't lie with their mouths nearly as often as they lie with their pauses. Sef's pause had a whole second life in it. But I filed it away, because there were larger fish, and also because the ghost was still talking.

"The First Door stands in Cael Morath," Ilvane said. "The drowned city. Your smuggler's rumor is accurate, thief — the exodus gate, the old door, the first threshold the founders of Vael ever spoke a name across. It will open for two things only: royal blood freely given, and the queen's word truly spoken." She looked at me over her absent spectacles. "You carry the word. You have carried it for years without knowing, which is the only reason the Hollow hasn't simply taken it from you. A thing unknowingly held casts no shadow."

"Lovely. So all I have to do is walk into a drowned city, find a door, and produce a member of a dead royal family." I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin as tin. "Ilvane, I'm a thief. My skill set is picking pockets and climbing through windows. My five-year plan used to be *don't get caught*."

"Your skill set," she said mildly, "is carrying things that aren't yours to places they need to go. You have been doing exactly that since the night the stars went out. You simply didn't know you were the courier."

I hated her a little for that. Hated how neatly it fit. Because here's the truth I'd been avoiding since the Lethe-wood, since Ashfall burned, since Brannoc — no. Not yet. Since the cairn under the blank stone — here's the truth: I had been planning to run. Cael Morath, the exodus gate, all of it — I'd told myself it was about survival. Get through the door, get out of the country-that-wasn't, live small and safe somewhere the Hollow couldn't follow. Self-preservation. Honest motive. The one clean thing about me.

And now the ghost had gone and made my escape route into a delivery route, and there was no version of the map where I could look at myself in the morning and pretend I hadn't seen it.

Because I'd made a vow over a pile of stones with no name carved on it. *I will remember everyone the Hollow takes.* Well. Here was everyone. All of them. An entire kingdom, taken, and the only person who could give it back was standing in a library that didn't exist, complaining about her skill set.

"Sef," I said. "Talk to me about the heir. If the story's true — if somewhere there's someone with royal blood — who would know? Where would they be?"

He was quiet long enough that Ilvane's ghost flickered, impatient.

"They'd be dead," he said finally. "Or they'd be ours." And when I looked at him: "Choir legend, Lena. The heir who chose chains. If any blood of the queen survived, the Choir found them first. That's the point of the story. *We got there before hope did.*" His voice did something strange on the last word, fraying at the edge. "It's why the tale works. You can't rescue someone who already surrendered."

"Then the story's a cage," I said. "It's designed so nobody ever goes looking. 'Don't bother, the heir's one of us now' — that's not a warning, Sef, that's a lock. And I'm a thief. Locks are kind of my whole personality."

"It's not a lock. It's a grave."

"Graves I can work with too."

He stepped toward me then, close enough that I could see the exhaustion carved under his eyes, the ward-sense always humming behind them like a man listening to a sound no one else can hear. Which, to be fair, he was.

"Lena." Low. Almost gentle, which was worse than shouting. "You want to steal a kingdom. Do you understand what that means? The Hollow isn't a rival guild. It isn't the Choir, with rules and ranks and things you can bribe. It is the accumulated grief of a dead queen wearing the faces of everyone it has eaten. Brannoc is in there. The mother from Ashfall's wedding day is in there. Half the people you've ever sold memories *about* are in there. And you want to walk up to it and hand it a reason to stop?"

"Yes."

"Why?" And there it was — not cruelty, genuine bafflement, a man whose entire moral architecture was built on duty trying to locate the load-bearing wall in mine. "What's in it for you? You could be through the gate in a week. Free. Safe. You said it yourself — you never keep what you steal. So *don't keep this one*. Deliver it to the door and walk away. Why would you hand the Hollow your one bargaining chip?"

I thought about lying to him. I'm good at lying; it's most of theft. But he'd been reading my ledger entries back to me at night, every kindness I couldn't remember earning, and there was something about a man who keeps records of your goodness that makes lying to him feel like spitting in a well.

"Because of the wedding day," I said.

He blinked. "What?"

"In Ashfall. The mother who sold her wedding day for bread. I watched her do it, and I told myself it was her choice, her arithmetic, none of my business. Then the Choir burned the camp and I got her out, but I couldn't get the memory back, and she looked at her own children like they were lodgers." My throat tightened around it. "I've spent ten years telling myself taking memories doesn't hurt anyone, because the people I took from were asleep and rich and would never feel the gap. But the Hollow does what I do, Sef. It just does it to everyone, forever, and calls it hunger instead of trade. And if I run through that gate with the queen's name in my pocket — if I save myself and let Vael stay eaten — then I'm not better than the Hollow. I'm just smaller."

Silence. Even Ilvane's ghost had gone still, in the way ghosts have of making silence feel attended.

"That's not a reason," Sef said at last, roughly. "That's penance."

"It's both. Most honest things are."

*

He didn't agree. That matters; I want to be accurate about this, because later — if there is a later — I don't want the story softened. He didn't agree. What he did was stop arguing, which in warder is a different animal entirely.

We left the non-existent library as the light did whatever light does in a country that's been unnamed — gray seeping into grayer, dusk without a schedule. Ilvane faded at the threshold with a last instruction: *the Door tastes intent, child. Come to steal, and it stays shut. Come to give, and we shall see.* Helpful. Very helpful. Ghosts and riddles, the two great constants of my life lately.

We walked. The road toward Cael Morath ran along the spine of a hill that might once have been called something, past farms whose names had been eaten so thoroughly that even the fences seemed unsure of their purpose. Sef walked half a step ahead, the way he always did, positioned to take the first hit of whatever came. I'd stopped commenting on it. Some chivalries you just let stand.

"So this heir," I said, after a mile of quiet. "Play the game with me. Suppose the Choir's story is true and the heir lives. Sworn to the Choir. Hollowed of doubts, like you were. What then? We stroll up to a Gray Choir officer and say *excuse me, are you secretly royalty? Mind opening this door?*"

"They'd kill you before you finished the sentence."

"For asking nicely?"

"For knowing." He kicked a stone off the path with unnecessary force. "The Choir doesn't leave loose threads, Lena. If the heir exists, they've spent their whole life being watched. Every friend vetted. Every memory audited. Their own lineage cut out of them like a tumor — because that's what the Choir does, it doesn't just take oaths, it takes the *knowing*. You can't betray a bloodline you don't remember having." He glanced sideways at me. "How do you think I got recruited? They found me at fourteen, an orphan with ward-sense, and they gave me order and purpose and a place to sleep. They hollowed my doubts. Do you know what I doubted, before? Neither do I. That's the trick. They don't leave you angry about what they took, because they take the anger too."

We walked another stretch.

"But you lied about my location," I said. "In the beginning. When you reported me and then didn't. A man with no doubts doesn't lie to Mother Vesper."

His step hitched — barely, a stutter in the rhythm of him, like a skipped heartbeat you'd only catch if you'd spent weeks learning his walking. "That was different."

"How?"

"It just was." And then, quieter, almost to the hills: "Maybe the hollowing doesn't take everything. Maybe some things grow back. Like—" He stopped himself. Again with the stopping. This man was a hedge maze with a sword.

"Like what, Sef?"

"Nothing." He lengthened his stride. "It doesn't matter. The heir is a legend. Legends don't open doors."

"Legends are just memories somebody decided to keep," I said. "I'd know. Keeping them is my whole crime."

He didn't answer, but he slowed his stride until we were walking together, shoulder to shoulder, and I counted that as a partial victory. With Sef you learned to bank partial victories. They compounded.

*

We made camp in the lee of a ruined waystation, and I lay awake doing arithmetic, because that's what thieves do instead of praying.

Here's the sum. One stolen memory, seven years in my skull, humming like a struck bell whenever the wards used to sing. One drowned city, three days' walk. One door requiring royal blood and a spoken name. Zero known royals. One possible heir, location unknown, existence doubtful, allegiance worse. One warder who flinched at the word *heir* like a dog at a whistle only it can hear.

One crow, missing since the ambush, presumably alive because Ossifrage had the survival instincts of a tax collector and the ego of a king.

And one vow, carved into me deeper than the cairn stones: *remember everyone.*

I sat up. Sef was on watch, naturally, sitting with his back against the waystation wall, whittling nothing in particular out of a stick. He'd been doing that since Brannoc died. I think his hands needed a job that couldn't fail.

"If the heir is real," I said into the dark, "and the Choir has them — then the Choir knows what the heir is. Which means Vesper knows. Which means the offer she made me—" the easy ending, Vael restored but empty, a husk dressed in my memories "—wasn't generosity. It was containment. She doesn't want the name spoken. She wants it *buried*, because a restored Vael with a living heir is a kingdom she can't rule."

Sef's knife paused. "Vesper serves the Hollow."

"Does she?" I'd been turning it over for days, and it finally clicked into place the way a lock clicks when you stop forcing it. "Or does she serve herself, using the Hollow the way I use a sleeping nobleman? Think about it. The Hollow eats indiscriminately. The Choir harvests selectively. Those aren't the same religion, Sef. Vesper's not a priestess. She's a *middleman*." I grinned, and felt the grin come out feral. "And middlemen, warder, are the best marks in the world. They always think they're the smartest thing in the room."

"You're proposing to con the woman who commands an army of hollowed soldiers."

"I'm proposing to *steal a kingdom*," I said. "You said it like an accusation earlier. I've decided to take it as a compliment. Look — every heist I've ever run had the same shape. Find the thing that's hidden. Learn who wants it kept hidden. Become the person who decides where it goes. The queen hid her name. Vesper wants it buried. The heir, whoever they are, needs it given. Three players, one object, and I'm the only courier in play." I spread my hands. "This is just a job, Sef. The biggest one I've ever run. But it's still a job, and jobs have steps."

"And the step where you pour your own memories into a door? Is that in the ledger too?"

I hadn't told him about that part. Ilvane's warning — *a name spoken by a willing voice* — and the price the First Door would ask, the irreplaceable thing, the whole hoard. I'd kept it folded up small, the way I keep everything that hurts.

But he was looking at me in the dark with those ward-sensed eyes, and lying to him had gotten expensive.

"The door will want a price," I said. "A real one. Names don't cross thresholds for free — that's the oldest rule there is, older than guilds, older than kingdoms. I don't know yet what it'll ask. But I know it won't take coin." I pulled my knees up. "Whatever it asks, I'll pay it. That's the step you're not allowed to argue about."

"I'm a warder. Arguing about prices is literally my vocation."

"Then argue with the door."

He almost smiled. In the dark, I saw it happen — a small crack of light in a closed shutter — and then it sealed itself again, because he was still carrying whatever it was he wouldn't say, the fish under the ice, the heir-shaped hole in his story.

"One condition," he said.

"Thieves don't accept conditions."

"This one you will." He set down the stick. "If it comes to choosing — the name or yourself, the mission or your skin — you choose yourself. You run. You've earned running. Whatever vow you made over Brannoc's cairn, I'll remember the rest for you. I'm good at remembering. It's the one thing the Choir never managed to hollow out of me." His voice went low and steady, oath-voice, the voice he probably used for swearing things he meant. "Promise me, Lena. Promise you'll run."

And here's the thing about promises: I've made a career of extracting them from other people and wriggling out of my own. But he was watching me with the whole frozen lake of himself held very still, waiting, and I thought about a ledger full of kindnesses I couldn't recall, kept by a man who remembered me better than I remembered myself.

"I promise," I said, "to consider running."

"That's not—"

"It's the best offer on the table, warder. Take it or leave it."

He left it. But he picked the stick back up, and his shoulders came down half an inch from his ears, and when I lay back down I heard him murmuring to himself — counting, maybe, or memorizing, or doing whatever quiet devotional thing warders do with the dark.

I stared at the nonexistent stars — the black sky where Vael's constellations used to wheel, the hole in heaven shaped exactly like home — and I did my final sum.

Not escape. Restoration. Carry the queen's word to the lost heir, speak the name at the First Door, and pay whatever toll the crossing demanded. Steal a kingdom back from grief itself, and give it away to everyone.

It was, objectively, the worst heist plan in the history of theft. No exit strategy. No fence for the goods. No client except an entire country of the forgotten, and no payment except the one thing I'd built my whole life never to spend.

I fell asleep smiling anyway.

Somewhere east, black water waited, and under it a city lit by drowned stars, and under that a door with an appetite. Somewhere in the Choir's ranks, a legend walked around wearing royal blood and not knowing it. And somewhere in my skull, a dead queen's last word hummed against the walls of its hiding place, patient as a seed, waiting for spring.

Three days to Cael Morath.

I just hoped the heir, whoever they were, was easier to find than they were to forgive.
