## 18. Market of Secondhands

The road to the bazaar didn't exist, which was how we knew we were on it.

You walked north out of Ashfall's ash-line until the trees went gray as old dishwater, and then you stopped walking toward anything and started walking *between* things — between one hill and its own shadow, between a thought and the moment you'd finish having it. Sef called it "the seam." Brannoc had called it worse, but Brannoc was three days behind us with the cart and the mule and his grumbling, and I'd told him to stay put because the bazaar didn't like crowds.

It barely liked people.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Sef said. He walked with his warder's staff slung across his back like a soldier's pike, which in the seam was either comforting or provocative depending on who saw it. "The mother sold her wedding day. That's done. Sold memories don't come back."

"They come back here."

"That's a story thieves tell other thieves."

"It's a story *I'm* telling you, and I've never lied to you about the trade." This was true, mostly. I'd exaggerated prices in my time and once described a client's wife as more forgiving than she was, but lying about the mechanics of memory — no. The trade has rules older than any guild, and one of them is this: nothing is ever truly destroyed. A memory sold goes somewhere. It sits on a shelf. It waits for someone with coin.

Or, in my case, for someone with something better than coin.

The seam opened the way a mouth opens when it decides to speak — reluctantly, all at once. One step there were gray trees, and the next there was light that came from no direction in particular, and stalls, hundreds of them, pitched in a market square that had no ground so much as a suggestion of cobblestone, laid down wherever someone had bothered to remember what cobblestones looked like.

The Market of Secondhands. Every thief knows about it the way sailors know about the sea that eats ships. We say the name quietly and pretend we'll never need it.

It smelled like rain on hot stone and old paper and, underneath, faintly, like the inside of a locked room. The crowd moved slow, hunched, polite the way mourners are polite. Vendors sat behind tables of the intangible made briefly tangible: jars of first snowfalls, folded laughter in linen, a boy's whole year of being nine hanging from a hook like a coat. Somewhere a woman was selling the sound of her mother's voice by the syllable. Somewhere else, a man had laid out his regrets in neat rows and nobody wanted them.

"You look pale," Sef said.

"I look *seasoned*. There's a difference."

"Lena."

"I know." I straightened my collar, checked my knife, left it — knives are rude in the bazaar, they imply negotiation of the wrong kind. "We go to the broker's row. We ask after a wedding day, bride named Maren of Ashfall, groom a cooper, sold eight days ago for bread. We pay whatever they ask. We walk out. Simple."

"And if what they ask is too much?"

I looked at him. He looked back, and I watched him understand, because Sef is slow the way glaciers are slow — enormous, inevitable, and arriving exactly where he means to arrive.

"No," he said.

"It's my hoard. My choice."

"You've been keeping those memories since before I knew you. You told me—" He stopped himself, jaw working. Whatever I'd told him, he wasn't going to quote it in front of the market. Markets listen. Especially this one.

*Keep your dearest ones close,* is what I'd told him, once, drunk on cheap wine outside Ashfall. *They're the only wealth that can't be stolen from a careful thief.* Which is true. Nobody can steal them.

But they can be *spent*. And I was about to spend like a dying lord.

* * *

The broker's stall was easy to find because it was the largest and the emptiest. The others crowded the edges of the square; this one stood near the center under a canopy of woven dusk, and the broker herself was neither young nor old, tall as a door and thin as the space beneath one. Her face kept almost resolving into someone familiar — a teacher, an aunt, a woman who'd once sold me an apple — and then sliding away again. Brokers wear borrowed faces the way I used to wear borrowed gloves.

"Underhill," she said, and my name came out tasting like dust. "The memory-thief. We wondered when you'd come. Word travels even through seams."

"Then you know what I want."

"A wedding." She smiled without warmth or coldness, just precision. "Maren of Ashfall. A fine vintage, actually. Twenty-three years of marriage distilled into one afternoon. The vows, the dancing, the groom stepping on her foot twice. She sold it clean — no residue, no regret-bitterness. Buyers fought over it." Her eyes, which were the color of a ledger's margins, narrowed slightly. "It's already spoken for."

The floor of my stomach dropped through the suggested cobblestones into whatever lay beneath suggestions.

"Spoken for," I repeated.

"We hold auctions. Yours is the counter-offer. If you can meet the reserve."

"Which is?"

She turned and lifted a small glass bell from a shelf behind her, and inside it — oh, inside it — there was sunlight. Real sunlight, gold and specific, falling across a courtyard I knew too well. A girl of maybe nineteen laughing at something off-glass, sleeves rolled, flour on her hands, and a boy beside her saying something clever and getting hit with a dish towel for it. The image turned slowly in its prison like a fly in amber, and every rotation showed me another detail I'd forgotten I remembered: the crack in the courtyard tile shaped like a river delta. The way she tucked her hair behind her ear with the hand holding the towel so she had to use her shoulder for the other side. The exact pitch of the boy's laugh.

My first meeting with Sef. In the queen's vault corridor, both of us nineteen, both of us pretending to belong there, both of us failing beautifully.

That wasn't on auction. That was the *price tag*.

"No," Sef said again, from beside me, quieter now, which meant louder in every way that counted.

"That's not the reserve," the broker said. "That's an example. The reserve is *three*. Three of comparable warmth. Comparable weight. The buyer bidding against you is a collector of first meetings — rare category, deep pockets, sentimental in the way collectors always are, which is to say: ruthlessly. To outbid him you must offer three memories of equal or greater value, surrendered willingly, and willingly is the operative word. Forced payment sours the goods." She set the bell down between us like a chess piece. "So. What have you got, Underhill? And do think carefully. The market sees everything you carry. It's very hard to hide warmth from a broker."

And that was the trap inside the trap, because of course the market could see everything I carried. Of course it knew precisely which three would hurt most. That's how ruinous costs work: they're not ruinous because they're large. They're ruinous because they're *accurate*.

* * *

We stepped away from the stall, far enough for the illusion of privacy, which the bazaar permitted because it found privacy entertaining.

"You walk away," Sef said. "Right now. We find another way. There's always another way."

"There isn't, and you know it. Residue fades. In a month that memory gets bought, decanted into some collector's vault, and Maren goes to her grave never knowing what she gave up — or worse, knowing and grieving it forever. I've seen sold people. They smile at their children and feel nothing and don't know why they're crying." I rubbed my face. "I took a stranger's childhood off Brannoc's debt. I've taken kisses and christenings and last words off sleeping minds for coin. I have *never once* given anything back, Sef. Not to anyone. Because giving it back was never possible, and because—" I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Because I liked keeping them. Even the ones that weren't mine. They filled up the quiet."

He said nothing. Sef's silences have architecture.

"So this is the first honest thing I've ever done in the trade," I said. "Let me do it badly."

"What three?" he asked finally, and his voice had gone careful, the voice of a man defusing something. "Lena. What three does it want?"

I closed my eyes and let the market look at me, since it was going to anyway.

There was the night my sister Nell and I slept on the roof during the heat-wave and she talked about running away to the coast until we both fell asleep mid-plan. There was the morning I came home with my first stolen purse, age eleven, terrified and triumphant, and instead of a scolding my mother taught me how to pick a lock, saying *if you must be a fox, be a good one*. There was honey — the taste of honeycomb stolen off a hive when I was nine, stung twice, worth every welt.

Warm ones. The warmest. Exactly the kind a collector of first meetings would bid against.

"Not the vault," Sef said. He'd read my silence like a page. "Not our—"

"It's not offering that one. Yet. Maybe it saves it for the next purchase. Maybe that's how it hooks you — one wedding day, and then you keep coming back, buying back pieces of other people with pieces of yourself, until there's nothing left but the ledger." I opened my eyes. "Which is why this is the last time. One purchase. Then I retire the habit forever."

"You're lying to yourself in a market that sells self-deception by the pound."

"I know. Isn't it wonderful? I'm contributing to the local economy."

* * *

The transaction itself was almost gentle, which I hadn't expected. I'd imagined pain. Instead the broker simply asked, "Willingly?" and I said, "Willingly," and meant it down to the marrow, and the market believed me because meaning it was the entire currency.

The roof came first.

Nell's voice went like a candle pinched — there, warm, gone, and where it had been was a smoothness, a blank patch shaped exactly like my sister talking about the sea. I reached for the memory of reaching for the memory and found the edges already sealing over, skin growing smooth across a wound I couldn't locate anymore. I knew — abstractly, the way you know a country exists that you've never visited — that I had a sister, and that we had loved each other, and that something about a roof mattered enormously.

I started crying and didn't know what about. The broker handed me a cloth with professional courtesy.

The lockpicking lesson went second, and losing it hurt differently, because it wasn't just the scene, it was my mother's voice teaching me, and when it went I lost the sound of her entirely — I still knew she'd existed, still knew she'd loved me, but the voice was a room with the furniture removed. I stood in it, echoing.

The honey went third, and by then I'd learned the trick of it, which is that you stop fighting. You open your hand. You let the warm thing leave, and you watch it go into the glass bell where it turns slowly, golden, someone else's treasure now, and you notice that the sting of the welts stays even though the sweetness doesn't. The market keeps the pain free of charge. Very generous.

Three bells on the shelf. A wedding day in a fourth, unsealed, mine to carry.

"Receipt?" the broker asked.

"Do I look like someone who wants proof of what I just did?"

"You look," she said, tilting her almost-familiar face, "like someone who will be back within the year. They always say otherwise." She wrapped Maren's wedding day in gray silk and pressed it into my hands, and it was lighter than bread and heavier than anything I'd ever held. "For what it's worth, thief — the collector bid high because first meetings are rare. But willing sacrifice? That hasn't crossed my counter in eleven years. The market remembers kindness longer than it remembers coin."

"The market can remember it for both of us," I said. "I won't be able to."

* * *

Outside the stall, Sef took one look at me and steered me to a bench that might not have existed five minutes earlier but was solid enough when I needed it to be.

"How many fingers," he said.

"Four. How many faces do you have right now, because from here you're doing that thing where you look like everyone I've ever failed."

"That's the broker's influence wearing off." He crouched in front of me, forearms on knees, close enough that I could see the Choir-gray still faded at his temples from the Lethe-wood. "What did you give?"

"Does it matter?"

"Yes."

I told him. Roof, lockpicks, honey. His face did something complicated at each one — grief for the roof, something like awe for the lockpicks, and at the honey, a small helpless laugh that broke halfway through.

"You told me that story," he said. "Third night out of Ashfall. You stole honeycomb at nine and got stung twice and ate it anyway, crying, and you said it was the best thing you ever tasted and the worst deal you ever struck."

"Did I." The words landed in me like stones into a well I couldn't hear the bottom of. "That sounds like something I'd say."

"Lena—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't do the thing where you memorize my losses out loud. It's kind and I hate it. Just—" I looked down at the silk bundle. "Help me get this to Maren. That's the job. That's the whole job now. Everything else is inventory shrinkage."

He helped me up. And as we walked back toward the seam — past the woman selling her mother's voice, past the rows of unwanted regrets, past a vendor shouting that today only, laughter was half price — I caught Sef glancing sideways at me, quick and recurring, the way a man checks a wound.

At the time I thought he was worried I'd collapse.

Later — much later, when I'd learn about the little book he carried, the one with my name on the cover and my life written inside in his careful hand — I'd understand what he was really doing in that market.

He was counting what was missing. And starting, entry by entry, to write it back.
