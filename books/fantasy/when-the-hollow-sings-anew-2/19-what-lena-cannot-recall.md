## 19. What Lena Cannot Recall

I learned early that the best way to catch a thief is not to chase them. It is to watch what they touch.

Chase a memory-thief and they vanish like smoke off the coals. But watch them — watch their hands hover over things they shouldn't care about, watch them flinch at nothing, watch them reach for a name that isn't there anymore — and you learn everything. You learn where they've been. You learn what it cost them.

So I watched Lena Underhill. I had been watching them for three weeks, ever since Ashfall, and by the time we made camp in the ruins of a shepherd's cot below the Lethe-wood's black tree line, I had compiled a list of things wrong with them that had nothing to do with knives or lies.

It was not a short list.

* * *

The trouble started with honey.

We'd scavenged half a jar of it from the cot's cold larder — real honey, amber and slow, a small miracle in a country where miracles were being eaten wholesale. Lena dipped two fingers in, licked them clean with the theatrical relish of someone performing enjoyment rather than feeling it, and said:

"Huh."

"Huh," I repeated.

"It's sweet." They frowned at the jar like it had lied to them. "Obviously it's sweet. Honey's sweet. Everyone knows that." A pause. "But it's — I don't remember it being sweet. Like that. Like a whole thing. Like something your grandmother kept for feast days and doled out on bread with a spoon she'd rap you with if you asked twice."

"Did you have a grandmother like that?"

"I don't know," Lena said, and there it was — the flat, careful tone of someone stepping onto ice and listening for the crack. "I don't know if I don't remember because it never happened or because it happened and it's gone. That's the problem, isn't it? The Hollow doesn't leave holes. It leaves smooth places. You can't even tell where the floor used to be."

I said nothing. In the Choir we were taught that silence was discipline. That night, sitting awake while Lena slept, I decided the Choir could go hang, took out my knife, and cut a strip of parchment from the bottom of my map.

On it I wrote: *Honey. L. remembers the word, not the taste. Do not let them pretend otherwise.*

Then I folded it up small and put it in my breast pocket, next to my heart, which I was beginning to suspect had opinions of its own.

* * *

The sister came three days later, and she did not come gently.

A refugee column passed us on the road — maybe forty souls, gray-faced, moving south with the shuffling patience of people who had forgotten where south was good. And among them a woman with Lena's jaw, Lena's crooked way of standing, Lena's habit of counting her fingers when nervous, which I knew about only because I'd spent an embarrassing number of nights watching Lena count theirs.

Lena stopped dead in the middle of the road.

The woman looked at them. Looked through them. Kept walking. She had a child on her hip and grief worn down to a nub, and whatever flickered behind her eyes when she saw Lena's face never surfaced into recognition.

"That's—" Lena started. Stopped. Their hand went to their sternum, pressing, as though holding a door shut against wind. "Sef. Who is that? Why does looking at her feel like—"

"I don't know," I lied.

"You're a terrible liar for a man whose entire profession is truth-adjacent."

"Memory-adjacent," I corrected. "And no one's profession is truth anymore. Truth got eaten along with everything else."

But that night Lena sat across the fire from me, knees drawn up, and said, very quietly: "I had a sister. I'm almost sure I had a sister. I can't see her face. I can't hear her voice. All I've got is the shape of loving her, like a footprint after the bird's flown." They looked up. "Did I? Did I have one?"

The honest answer was: I didn't know. The useful answer was also: I didn't know. So I gave the third answer, the one that cost me something.

"If you had a sister," I said, "she'd be lucky to have been loved like that. Whatever shape of it is still in you — it's big. I can see it from here."

Lena stared into the fire long enough that I worried I'd said something unforgivably tender. Then: "You keep records, warder?"

"What?"

"You've got ink on your fingers. You always have ink on your fingers. And you write at night when you think I'm asleep. What are you writing? Reporting to your Choir?" There was no venom in it. That was worse. Venom would have meant they still believed I might choose them over duty; without it, they were just tired.

"Ledger," I said.

"Of what?"

"Of you."

That surprised a laugh out of them — short, cracked, real. "Of me. Gods. I'm worth more dead than alive and more recorded than remembered. Fine. Write this down: subject complains constantly, snores like a rockslide, steals blankets—"

"—has not stolen anything since Ashfall," I said, "which for you must be a kind of agony."

The laugh died. Lena looked at their own hands. "Yeah," they said softly. "It is, actually. Thanks for noticing."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about watching someone unravel: you start keeping score without meaning to.

By the tenth night I had filled six pages. Not a report — reports are weapons, and this was not that, whatever I told myself at two in the morning when the fire guttered and I told myself I was gathering intelligence. This was something else. This was a man copying out a book he loved before the library burned.

*Remembers the word "honey," not the taste.*
*Does not remember having a sister. Remembers the shape of having one. Counts fingers when grieving. Does not know why.*
*Left-handed, but reaches for things right-handed first, as if trained out of it and grown back around it.*
*Hummed four notes of a Thornfell road-song today. Could not finish it. Was angry about it for an hour.*
*Called me by my full name — Seferian — once, in sleep, and looked stricken awake. Does not know why they know it.*

That last one I wrote slowly. My name. My whole name, the one I hadn't spoken aloud since the Choir took my oath and filed the rest of me under *warder, expendable.* Lena had known it. Somewhere in the smooth places of them, some part of them still knew me — knew the boy who'd stood in the palace orchard while a thief-in-training picked his pocket and left a plum in its place, because, they'd said, laughing, *a trade should hurt both ways or it isn't fair.*

They didn't remember the plum. They didn't remember the orchard. They remembered my name the way you remember a song you heard once through a wall.

I sat with that until the fire burned down to red eyes in the ash, and then I added a seventh line:

*Knows things about me they cannot account for. Whatever else the Hollow has taken, it has not taken how they felt about me. I intend to find out what that was. For the ledger. Obviously for the ledger.*

* * *

Morning brought frost and a discovery.

Lena was up before me — rare — crouched over the ashes of last night's fire, turning something over in their fingers. When I approached they held it up without turning around.

"This was in my pack," they said. "Bottom seam. Sewn in. I found it because I was checking for — well, because old habits." They unrolled it: a scrap of oilcloth, and inside it, a pressed sprig of rowan berries gone dark as garnets, and a strip of paper in handwriting I recognized because it was mine, from years ago, from another life: *For L.U., so you can't say Vael never gave you anything. — S.*

"I don't remember this," Lena said. Their voice was very level, which is how I'd learned to recognize when they were closest to breaking. "I have a keepsake sewn into my pack from you, from before, from — from whenever — and I don't remember it. I've been carrying it through the end of the world and it means nothing to me except that it should mean everything, and Sef, I am so tired of reaching into myself and finding rooms with the furniture sold off."

I crouched beside them. Up close their eyes had the particular shine of someone refusing to cry on schedule.

"So keep it anyway," I said.

"That's madness."

"No. Madness is letting the Hollow decide what you're allowed to carry." I took the sprig from them, turned it over. "Look. Rowan. Warder's tree. We plant it at doors to keep out hunger. Whoever gave you this was telling you: whatever comes, this door holds." I handed it back. "Maybe you don't remember the person. Keep the sentence."

Lena closed their hand around it. For a moment neither of us moved, and the frost ticked in the branches overhead, and I thought: *say something true, you coward, you've written seven pages of true, say one of them out loud.*

What I actually said was: "Also, you snore like a rockslide."

"Recorded for posterity?" they managed, wet-eyed, grinning despite themselves.

"Page three."

* * *

That day I bought ink.

Not really bought — traded, in a half-dead market village clinging to the foothills, a week of my rations for a stoppered pot of oak-gall ink, a bundle of quills, and a bound book of blank pages that the shopkeeper swore had belonged to a customs clerk who'd forgotten his own job title and wandered east. The irony was not lost on me. Nothing was lost on me anymore; that was the whole point. Somebody in this partnership had to remember, and Lena was hemorrhaging themselves by the hour, smooth place by smooth place, and I —

Well. I had made my choice somewhere back on the road, quietly, without ceremony, the way all the real choices happen. The Choir had hollowed my doubts years ago and called it order. But they'd missed one doubt, buried deep, shaped exactly like a thief with quick hands and a crooked way of standing. And a doubt, tended properly, grows into a conviction.

I began the new book that night by the fire while Lena pretended to sleep and I pretended to believe them.

*Ledger of Lena Underhill,* I wrote on the first page. *Kept by S., warder, traitor (pending). Purpose: so that when the Hollow is done eating, somebody can set the table again.*

Then, beneath:

*Entry the first. Today L. gave their bread ration to a child who wasn't hungry, just lonely, and claimed afterward it was an accident. It was not an accident. It is never an accident. They have done this every time food crosses their hands since Ashfall, and they will deny it every time, and I will record it every time.*

*Entry the second. L. whistles when frightened. Badly. On purpose, I think, so the fear has nowhere to hide.*

*Entry the third. L. touched the rowan sprig eleven times today. Counted. It helps. Write that down too: it helps.*

I wrote until the candle drowned in its own wax. Across the dead fire, Lena's breathing had gone suspiciously even, the evenness of someone listening hard.

"You're doing it again," they said finally, to the dark.

"Doing what?"

"Loving me in shorthand."

The words landed in the silence between us like a dropped coin, ringing. Neither of us reached to pick them up. After a long moment, Lena rolled over, and I saw by the ember-light that they weren't smiling.

"Don't stop," they said. "Whatever it is you're writing — don't stop. Because here's the arithmetic, warder, and I need you to check my figures: every day I lose something I can't name. Every day. And I can feel it going, like water through fingers, and I can't even grieve properly because I don't know what I'm grieving. But you—" their breath caught, steadied. "You look at me like I'm a book worth copying out. So copy. Copy everything. Copy the snoring. Copy the accidents with bread. Because someday — if there's a someday — I want to read it and meet the person I apparently was."

"Someday's a long way off," I said, "and the road to Cael Morath runs through country that eats names."

"Then write fast."

I wrote fast. I wrote through the Lethe-wood's approach, through the nights when the trees at the tree line seemed to lean toward our fire like listeners. And on the last page of my first night's entries, after Lena's breathing finally went genuinely slack, I added a line I did not read aloud and did not show anyone:

*Note to self: when this is over — if there is an over — give them the book and run, because they'll want to kiss you and you will want to let them and neither of us has earned that yet. Note amended: maybe earning isn't the point. Note amended again: shut up and keep writing.*

* * *

Two mornings later, the crow found us.

It dropped out of a sky the color of dirty wool and landed on a boundary stone ahead of us with the insolent precision of a creature that had never once been late for anything. Black as a sealed letter. One eye like a drop of molten gold.

"Well," it said, in a voice like dry pages turning. "There you are."

Lena went for a knife. I went for a warding sign. The crow regarded us both with vast disappointment.

"Oh, put those away. If I wanted you eaten I'd simply talk longer." It hopped closer, head tilting, and fixed its golden eye on Lena — on, I realized with a cold slide in my gut, not Lena's face but Lena's temple, the space behind it, as though it could see straight through bone into the smooth places. Into the one thing hidden there that hummed.

"There's a name sleeping in that skull," Ossifrage said. "A royal name. A last word. And I have flown a very long way over a very empty country to collect it — or," and here the crow's feathers lifted in something that was absolutely a shrug, "to find out why a common thief is carrying it and hasn't yet been devoured for the privilege."

Behind me, I heard Lena's breath go shallow. Ahead of us, the black line of the Lethe-wood waited, drinking.

I reached into my coat and put my hand flat against the ledger over my heart, and I thought, with a calm that surprised me: *whatever this is, whatever it wants — the record survives. Whatever happens to either of us, the record survives.*

I just didn't yet know how much of the record we'd both have to pay to make it true.
