## 20. The Choir's Offer

The trap was set with tea.

I should have known. Nobody in a war offers you tea unless they want something, and nobody in this particular war offers you tea unless they want the one thing you have left that matters. But I was tired, and cold, and the tent was warm, and there are limits to how suspicious a person can stay when their hands won't stop shaking.

So that's how Lena Underhill, memory-thief of no particular renown and even less particular virtue, came to sit across from Mother Vesper with a porcelain cup in her fingers and the fate of a kingdom cooling in her skull.

The tent itself was the first lie. Gray canvas outside — everything about the Choir was gray, gray robes, gray hoods, gray voices like ash falling on snow — but inside it was all velvet and lamplight, carpets thick enough to swallow footfalls, shelves of books I itched to touch because books mean memories and memories mean trade. A little parlor of the world as it used to be, pitched in the middle of nowhere, guarded by people who had sold their doubts for a promise of order.

Vesper poured without asking. She was older than I expected, or maybe she just wore age the way other people wear authority — comfortably, like a coat cut to fit. Her face was kind. That was the second lie, or maybe the worst one: kindness, real kindness, deployed like a knife.

"You're thinner than your file suggested," she said.

"I'm flattered you keep files."

"We keep everything." She smiled. "That's rather our whole tragedy."

I didn't drink. I held the cup and let the steam climb past my face, and I watched her watch me not drinking, and neither of us pretended we weren't both doing exactly what we were doing.

"Where's Sef?" I asked.

"Your warder is being spoken with." The way she said *spoken with* made my stomach drop half an inch. "Separately. It's nothing sinister, Miss Underhill. We simply find that people negotiate better when they aren't glancing at each other for permission."

"People negotiate worse," I said. "That's the point."

"Mm." She settled back into her chair, hands folded, and studied me the way a jeweler studies a stone she already knows is flawed. "Do you know what I admire about thieves? Honesty of appetite. A thief knows what she wants. Priests pretend. Kings pretend. Soldiers pretend so long and hard they forget what's underneath. But a thief — a thief looks at the world and says, *that is mine because I can take it.* There's a clarity in that. Almost a purity."

"You're going to offer me something," I said. "Get to it before I start liking you. That would ruin us both."

Something moved behind her eyes — amusement, maybe, or grief wearing amusement's clothes. She set down the teapot with a small ceramic click that sounded, absurdly, like a door closing.

"Then I'll be direct, since you've asked so prettily. You carry a memory. One specific memory, stolen years ago from a vault you never understood, humming in your head like a struck bell you've never been able to silence. You've felt it, haven't you? All these years. The name-shaped weight behind your eyes."

I had. Of course I had. Every thief knows some thefts sit wrong — too heavy, too warm, too *aware* of being carried. Most of us learn to stop noticing. I'd told myself for years it was just a nobleman's hoard of family secrets, dusty and valuable and none of my business. But late at night, on cold passes, I'd feel it turn over in my sleep like something dreaming.

"The Hollow wants it," Vesper said.

"The Hollow doesn't want things. It eats them."

"It wants this one." She leaned forward, and the lamplight found lines in her face I hadn't noticed before — fine ones, around the mouth, the kind that come from smiling at people while deciding whether they live. "It has wanted it since the night the wards fell. Everything else it takes is appetite. This is hunger with a purpose. And here is the truth no one else will tell you, Miss Underhill, because everyone else is either too frightened or too noble: the Hollow cannot be fought. It cannot be starved. It cannot be out-stolen, though I see you considering it, and it's very charming. It can only be *fed*. Fed properly. Fed what it actually wants. And then—" she spread her hands, an elegant little gesture, a conjurer revealing empty palms— "it stops."

"And if it stops?"

"Everything returns."

The words hung there. I made myself breathe slowly, the way Brannoc taught me — in for four, hold for two, out for six, because panic is just bad arithmetic. Somewhere outside the tent, wind moved through dead trees, and under the wind I could hear the faintest hum, the Hollow passing through the world like a tide through gravel.

"You're telling me," I said carefully, "if I give it the queen's last word — the true name — willingly—"

"Not even willingly. *Gladly*." Vesper's voice went soft, and softness from her was more frightening than shouting ever could have been. "There's a difference. The door between worlds doesn't open for sacrifice. Sacrifice is just theft pointed inward. It opens for surrender. For a gift given freely by someone who understands what she's giving away. The Hollow has been fed by terror since the beginning, and terror spoils the meal. It needs one honest offering."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange," she said, "you get yourself back."

She reached into her sleeve and produced a small gray book — ledger-sized, plain-bound, the kind every thief keeps and no thief shows anyone. She laid it on the table between us without opening it.

"Every memory you ever stole and kept. Every one you gave away to buy passage, buy bread, buy Brannoc's road. Every one the Hollow took on the night the sky went dark. The Choir collects, Miss Underhill. We have collectors who were working the border towns the night the wards fell. We have your hoard. We have the nobleman's first kiss. We have the stranger's childhood you traded to a smuggler for dry socks and safe passage — yes, we know about that, Brannoc talks in his sleep, bless him. We have all of it, catalogued and preserved, waiting in jars that do not leak."

My throat had gone tight. I hated that. I hated her for making it tight.

"And the sister?" I heard myself ask, and immediately wished I hadn't, because asking tells the seller what the buyer already owns. My sister's face had gone out of me somewhere on the road, traded or taken, and I'd been walking with the hole ever since, touching my tongue to it like a missing tooth.

Vesper's expression gentled further, which should have warned me.

"Restored. Whole. She'll remember you, which is more than most of the world can say right now."

"And Vael?" The word came out of me before I could stop it, and I saw her register that — saw her note that the thief cared about more than herself, and file it away for later use.

"Returned," she said simply. "Stars and wards and songs. Your country back from the wound it fell into. Everyone who was eaten, remembered again. Every map corrected, every mind mended. The war over before winter. No one else dies. No more camps burning. No more mothers selling wedding days for bread." She paused, delicately. "You've seen what that costs. You paid for one yourself, I'm told. Three warm memories. A ruinous price, the bazaar-keeper said. He was quite impressed."

The tent felt smaller. The lamplight felt colder. I looked down at my tea and realized my hands had stopped shaking, which somehow frightened me more than the shaking had.

"What's the price," I said. Not a question. A price was coming; there's always a price; I'd built my whole life on knowing that before anyone told me.

"Just the one memory," Vesper said. "The name. Given gladly. To the Hollow, directly, from your own mouth — well, your own mind. We'll arrange the mechanics. You won't feel a thing. Most donors report the experience as restful."

"That's it. One memory, and everything comes back."

"One memory," she agreed warmly. "And everything comes back."

And there it was. The easy ending, served up on porcelain with steam rising off it. Give up the one thing I'd never been able to put down anyway — the bell in my skull, the weight behind my eyes, the theft I'd never chosen and never wanted — and get back everything I'd lost. My sister's face. The taste of honey. Sef's laugh, the real one from before. Brannoc alive somewhere in the restored arithmetic of the world, complaining about his knees. Vael whole. The stars lit. An entire kingdom handed back because one tired thief finally agreed to hand over the loot.

Every instinct I had said *take it*.

Every instinct I had also said *nobody gives away a kingdom for one memory*, and that instinct had kept me alive through eleven years of smuggler roads and buyer's markets and men who smiled exactly like Vesper was smiling now.

I set down the cup. Untouched. I wanted her to notice.

"Say the rest of it," I said.

"There isn't any rest of it."

"There's always a rest of it. You're a choir, not a charity. What does the world look like after? Paint it for me. Who wakes up? What do they wake up *to*?"

For a moment — just a moment — the kindness flickered. Something underneath it looked out at me, something old and patient and very tired, and then the kindness came back on like a lamp relit, and I understood that both faces were real, and that was the most frightening thing yet.

"Afterward," she said, "there is peace. Order. The Hollow fed is the Hollow quiet, and quiet is what every soul in this broken country prays for whether they know it or not. The wards will rise again — ours, this time, tuned properly, maintained by the Choir. Memory will be governed. Curated. Nothing will be *lost* anymore, Miss Underhill. Can you imagine? A country where nothing is forgotten, where grief is managed, where every recollection sits in its proper jar on its proper shelf. No more thieves. No more hunger. No more—" her hand made a small, weary circle that took in the tent, the war, the darkened sky, the whole unraveling century— "*this*."

"No more forgetting," I repeated.

"No more forgetting," she confirmed.

"And who decides what everyone remembers?"

The pause lasted half a heartbeat. In a negotiation, that's an eternity. I'd have caught it in a market stall; I caught it here.

"The alternative," Vesper said, "is that everyone forgets forever. Every mother, every child, every song, until the last person who remembers anything dies alone in the dark and the world ends not with fire but with silence. Those are the only two endings on the table tonight. Managed memory or no memory. Order or oblivion. I know which one I'd choose for my children."

"You don't have children."

"No," she said, and something in her voice made me wish I hadn't said it. "But I remember wanting them. Vividly. It's one of the things the Choir permits me to keep."

*Permits.* There it was. The word slipped out of her like blood from under a bandage, and I watched her hear herself say it, and I watched her decide not to care.

I thought about Sef. Being *spoken with* somewhere in the gray dark, probably being offered his own version of this cup. I thought about him kneeling to the Choir once already, hollowing out his doubts so he could serve without flinching, and how he'd lied to them for me anyway, one small treachery at a time, like a man smuggling light out of a dark house one match at a time. If they got their managed world, there'd be no more matches. There'd be no more him. Just a warder in gray who'd once loved someone and no longer knew why that mattered.

I thought about the mother in Ashfall who'd sold her wedding day for bread, and how she'd looked at her husband after, polite and puzzled, like a woman being introduced to a stranger she was told she loved.

That was the catch. That was the whole catch, sitting right there in Vesper's beautiful pitch, wearing its best clothes. A Vael that came back *empty*. Restored in shape and starlight, warded and catalogued, every citizen returned to their body with their memories issued back to them by permit, curated, approved, filed. A kingdom of jars. People waking up remembering only what the Choir allowed, loving only whom the records said they'd loved, grieving on schedule. The Hollow wouldn't be defeated. It would be *employed*. Promoted from devourer to archivist. And the queen's true name — the one wild unspeakable word that had made Vael *Vael* and not just territory — would be locked in a vault deeper than the old one, kept by people who thought a country was a filing system.

"They'd come back," I said slowly, testing it aloud, "but they wouldn't come back *whole*."

"They'd come back safe," Vesper said. "Whole is a luxury, Miss Underhill. Safe is a policy."

"And the name itself? Once I give it up? Where does it live?"

"In trust," she said smoothly, too smoothly, and we both knew it.

"In a jar."

"In a vault."

"Run by the Choir."

"Run by *order*," she said, and for the first time her voice sharpened, and I saw the true believer underneath the negotiator, the woman who had genuinely made this bargain with herself years ago and never once looked back. "Someone must hold the wild things still, or they burn the house down. You of all people should understand. You steal memories for coin. You've seen what untended remembering does to people — the grudges, the griefs, the loves that curdle. You've profited from it. Don't stand in my tent and tell me the unmanaged heart is sacred. You've made your living picking its pocket."

And that landed. I won't pretend it didn't. Because she was right about the profession and wrong about everything else, and the worst lies are always built on a true foundation.

I stood up. My legs held, which surprised me.

"You're right," I said. "I've seen what remembering does. I've also seen what forgetting does. I watched a woman look at her husband like a stranger. I watched a man lose his daughter mid-sentence and keep talking because he couldn't tell anything was gone. I watched a whole country fall out of the sky because one word went unspoken." I leaned on the table, close enough to smell the tea and the lamp oil and, beneath it, something faintly gray, like ash that hadn't finished being smoke. "You're not offering to end the famine, Mother Vesper. You're offering to nationalize it. Same hunger, better ledgers. And I'm a thief, so believe me when I tell you: I know a fenced good when I'm shown one."

Vesper looked up at me for a long moment. Then she did something I didn't expect. She laughed — quietly, genuinely, a sound with real warmth in it, which was somehow the cruelest thing she'd done all evening.

"Oh, you're good," she said. "You are *very* good. Do you know how many bearers have sat in that chair? How many said exactly what you're saying?" She rose too, unhurried, and began gathering the teacups with the ease of a woman closing up shop after a slow day. "They all took the offer eventually. Some took days. One took an hour. It's not weakness, dear. It's arithmetic. Eventually the road gets long enough, the nights get cold enough, and one memory versus a whole kingdom stops being a hard sum." She glanced at me over the rim of the stacked cups. "Yours is nearly done already. I can hear it. The bell's gone quiet in you — you've been carrying it so long you've started to think of it as part of your head instead of something in it. Another week on the road, another burned camp, another friend in the ground, and you'll walk into my tent and pour the tea yourself."

"Maybe," I said. "But not today."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "Not today."

At the tent flap I stopped, because there was one more question, and it had been sitting in my chest since she'd mentioned the jars, and I needed to know even though knowing might break something.

"The memories you're holding," I said, without turning around. "Mine. The hoard. If I said yes tomorrow, would I get back the ones I *chose* to give away? The wedding day I bought for that mother? The childhood I spent on Brannoc's road?"

Silence. The kind with weight in it.

"Those were transactions," Vesper said gently. "Completed ones. The Choir honors completed transactions."

Meaning no. Meaning those memories were gone into the Choir's coffers, spent, spent *by me*, and no restoration would undo what I'd done with my own hands. She'd have let me find that out after signing. After the door closed.

"Goodnight, Mother Vesper," I said.

"Goodnight, Miss Underhill. Do travel safely. The road ahead of you is worse than you think, and I'd hate for the arithmetic to finish early."

Outside, the night was black from horizon to horizon, no stars, no moon, just the wind and the far-off hum of the Hollow moving through the dead trees like a tide that had forgotten the moon. Sef was waiting at the edge of camp, gray-hooded escorts peeling away from him, and even in the dark I could see something had happened to his face — something offered and refused, or accepted, I couldn't tell yet, and his expression was doing that terrible careful thing it did when duty and everything else were fighting behind his teeth.

"They talked to you," I said.

"They talked to me." His voice was flat and strange.

"Sef. What did they offer you?"

He looked at me for a long time, and above us the starless sky pressed down like a lid, and somewhere in the distance the Hollow sang its silent song, patient as winter.

"They showed me my file," he said finally. "All of it. Everything the Choir knows about Lena Underhill." He paused, and his jaw worked once. "Lena — there's a page in it about me. About *us*. And it's dated three years before I met you."
