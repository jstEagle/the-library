## 21. Ledger of Small Kindnesses

I had never intended to become a man who kept accounts for someone else. In the Choir we were taught that memory was a burden properly laid down, that the clean soul traveled light. Mother Vesper said forgetting was mercy in its work clothes. I believed her for six years, which is either an indictment of my intelligence or of my loneliness, and I have stopped pretending it isn't both.

Then Lena Underhill started coming apart in front of me, one warm thing at a time, and I discovered I would do anything — anything at all, including treason, including arithmetic — to keep some record of them on this earth.

The ledger was Brannoc's idea, technically. Or mine, prompted by his grumbling. We had made camp three nights out from Ashfall's ashes, in the lee of a rock shelf where the wind couldn't get its teeth into us, and Lena had asked — for the third time that day — why the porridge smelled like honey when there was no honey in it.

"Because you told me once," I said, "that everything tastes better if you imagine honey in it. You said your mother did that during the famine winters."

Lena looked at me with those grey eyes gone suddenly unmoored, the eyes of someone standing in a room they're fairly sure is theirs but can't find the door out of. "My mother?"

And there it was. Another door closing quietly somewhere inside them.

Brannoc spat into the fire. "Boy," he said to me, in the tone he reserved for stating the obvious to people pretending not to see it, "you'd better write that down before it goes the way of the rest."

So I did. I bought a ledger in Ashfall before it burned — gods forgive me, I had been buying supplies while the Choir's riders gathered at the tree line; I have stopped auditing that hour of my life — and I filled the first pages with my own cramped hand, the hand the Choir taught me for copying oath-texts, which is fitting, because this too is an oath of a kind.

*Lena Underhill hates honey-cakes but loves the smell of honey.*

*Steals only from sleepers. Never from children. Once went hungry rather than lift a dream from a dying woman.*

*Laughs at their own jokes before finishing them.*

*When frightened, hums. Badly.*

That last one I had learned on the road through the Lethe-wood, when I was coming apart myself and Lena anchored me with a story about the day we met — the day they stole my fear of drowning, or so they claimed, though what they actually stole that day, I think, was my attention, permanently, and without ever filing a receipt.

Now, tonight, with rain coming down like the sky meant to drown us all on principle, I opened the ledger and read aloud.

* * *

Lena sat across the fire with their knees drawn up and their chin on them, looking smaller than a person who has carried a kingdom's name in their skull has any right to look. The rain drummed the hide awning Brannoc had rigged. Ossifrage hunched on a stone like a feathered judge, one eye closed, listening with the particular intensity of a creature who remembers everything and finds most of it disappointing.

"Entry the ninth," I said. "Dated last autumn. *Today I stole the color of a harvest festival from a farmer outside Dellhollow. Paid him double because he cried in his sleep. Then gave the coin back to his barn as a blessing from some saint I invented on the spot. Saint Fenna of the Loose Coin. She doesn't exist. Someone should invent her.*"

Lena blinked. "I don't remember that."

"You told it to Brannoc at the time. He laughed so hard he fell off his mule."

"I remember falling off the mule," Brannoc offered from the darkness. "The rest is fog."

"Entry the twelfth," I went on. "*A boy in Thornfell village sold me his memory of his brother's face — needed money for medicine. I took it, took the coin, and then taught him to draw until he could rebuild it himself. It took four days. His drawing is terrible. His brother apparently had a nose like a doorknob. He wept anyway. So did I, after I left.*"

"Stop." Lena's voice came out strange.

I stopped.

"That one I don't remember at all." They said it flatly, the way people report weather, which is how I've learned to recognize Lena's grief: not in tears first, but in that terrible administrative calm. "Four days. Teaching a child to draw. That's — that's the kind of thing that's supposed to be load-bearing, Sef. That's the kind of thing a person is *built out of.* And it's just — gone. A hole with my name on it."

"Lena—"

"No." They held up a hand. Their fingers were trembling and they looked at the trembling as if it belonged to somebody else. "Keep reading. If you've written me down, then read me. All of it. I want to know exactly how much of me is missing."

So I read. Gods help me, I read.

*Entry the fifteenth. Gave my boots to a refugee girl at the pass. Walked two days barefoot and lied about it. Told Sef I lost them gambling, which Sef believed, because Sef believes the worst about me on principle.*

"I don't believe the worst about you," I said.

"You wrote it down in your own voice," Lena pointed out, and I had no defense except that they had smiled when they said it, a small crooked smile, and I understood that being known by them — even the secondhand, even the borrowed version of them that lived in my handwriting — was a gift I hadn't earned.

*Entry the nineteenth. The crow is insufferable and I would die for him. Do not tell him either of these things.*

Ossifrage opened his good eye. "Too late," he said. "Noted. Filed. Will be invoked at the appropriate moment, which is now not, because even I can see this is a moment for the mammal feelings."

*Entry the twenty-third.* My voice caught on this one, and I had to clear my throat, and Brannoc looked at me sharply, because Brannoc misses nothing except, lately, pieces of his daughter. *Sef reads at night when he thinks no one watches. He reads like a man doing penance. I want to tell him the war ended years ago and nobody won, but he looks so serious about it, like guilt is the last uniform he has. I hope someone tells him someday that he was never the thing he's apologizing for.*

I stopped reading. The fire popped. Rain ran off the hide in ropes.

"That one," Lena said slowly, "I don't remember writing. But I remember thinking it. Just now, hearing it — I remember *being* the person who thought it." They looked up at me across the flames, and their eyes were wet, and their voice cracked straight down the middle. "He sounds like someone I'd like. This person in your book. This person who gives away boots and invents saints and hopes things for strangers. I keep waiting to recognize myself and I keep meeting someone better instead."

"They're not better than you. They are you."

"No, Sef. That's exactly what they're not." Lena wiped their face with the heel of their hand, angrily, the way you swat a fly. "Don't you see it? Every entry you read, I paid for. Not with coin — with *myself*. The Hollow takes and takes and I let it, I keep spending myself down to buy miles and bread and time, and what's left over —" They gestured at the ledger. "What's left over is apparently generous. Kind. The sort of person who teaches a boy to draw his brother's nose. And I can't remember earning any of it. I'm sitting here reading the biography of a good thief and taking her word that she's me."

Brannoc stirred the fire without needing to. Old men always stir fires when silence gets heavy; it gives everyone somewhere else to look.

"Lass," he said. "I've known you eight years. Stolen with you, drunk with you, buried friends with you. And I'll tell you something for nothing: the kindness wasn't ever in the remembering. It was in the doing. You did those things when you were whole. Whatever the Hollow eats, it can't eat what you *did*. Things done stay done. That's the one law even this cursed country still honors."

Lena stared at him. Then at the ledger. Then at their own hands, turned palm-up, as if checking whether the instruments of all those small mercies were still attached.

"The mother at Ashfall," they said quietly. "The one who sold her wedding day. I bought it back for her, didn't I. In the bazaar. You told me — no. You *read* me — no, wait." Their face worked. "I can't tell anymore which memories are mine and which ones you've given me secondhand. Everything's the same weight now. Everything's paper."

"It's not paper," I said.

"It is if it's written down." And then Lena began to cry — really cry, not the angry wiping but the deep, graceless, full-bodied grief of someone mourning themselves, and I did the only thing I knew to do, which was to cross the space between us and sit down beside them and put the ledger in their lap.

"Then hold the paper," I said. "It's yours. Every word. When the Hollow comes back for more, it'll have to get through my handwriting first."

They laughed through the tears, that broken hiccuping laugh, and clutched the book to their chest like a shield, and Ossifrage muttered something about "mammal feelings, plague of the age," and Brannoc pretended very loudly to be asleep.

For a while, that was enough. The rain eased. Lena's breathing steadied against my shoulder — I recorded that fact nowhere, but I promise you it is truer than anything in the ledger.

* * *

Later, when they slept, I sat up with the fire and read my own entries again, and found the one I had not shown them. The last page, dated yesterday.

*Lena forgot the taste of honey today. They forgot their mother's voice last week. The rate is increasing. I am running out of ink and out of ways to pretend this ends anywhere but where it ends. If the Hollow takes them entirely, I will keep this book. I will read it to whoever Lena becomes. I will tell a stranger about a saint named Fenna of the Loose Coin until the stranger believes in her, and in that way, maybe, I can steal them back. It occurs to me that I have become a memory-thief myself, working the opposite direction. Lena would laugh at that. I will write down that they would laugh, so that someday I can show them why.*

I closed the book. Across the dead fire, Brannoc's eyes were open.

"You didn't read them the last one," he said.

"No."

"Sensible. Cruel and sensible. That's usually the Choir way, boy." He said it without heat. From Brannoc, that was nearly affection. "You going to tell them the other thing? About the route?"

I went cold. "What do you know about the route?"

"Scouts on the ridge tonight. Gray cloaks, no fires, moving like they already know where our camp is." He nodded east, toward the black line of the hills, where the rain had stopped and the stars — Vael's returned stars, the ones Lena bled to bring back — were wheeling bright and indifferent overhead. "They didn't find us by luck, Sef. Somebody sang."

I thought of my reports to the Choir. The ones I'd falsified. The one I hadn't. The confession I still owed Lena, sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone, heavier every day, and the Singing Stones standing between us and Cael Morath like an accusation waiting to happen.

"Get Lena up," I said. "Wake the crow. We move before moonset — and Brannoc, whatever happens at the Stones, whatever you hear me say or fail to say—" I picked up the ledger, Lena's whole surviving self, and held it against my heart. "This leaves that camp intact. Whatever else they take. Promise me."

Brannoc looked at me for a long moment, and then at the sleeping thief curled around a book of their own forgotten goodness, and he said, quietly, "On my daughter's name," — and did not notice, and I did not tell him, that he could no longer remember what it was.
