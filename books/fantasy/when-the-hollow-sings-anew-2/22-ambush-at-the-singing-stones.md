## 22. Ambush at the Singing Stones

### Lena

The Singing Stones earned their name honestly, which in my experience made them rare as honest coin. They stood in a broken ring on the shoulder of the pass — nine pillars of gray granite that caught the wind and hummed through their cracks like a choir of old men who'd forgotten the words but remembered the tune. The wards of Vael had been built to that melody once, Ossifrage claimed. Now they just sang it to nobody.

We camped inside the ring because Sef said the stone-song muddled a tracker's ear, and because Brannoc said any place with a good echo was a place worth defending, and because I said nothing, which by then everyone understood to mean *I agree with whoever's less likely to get us killed.*

Three days out from Ashfall's ashes. Three days of Ossifrage riding my shoulder like a judgment I couldn't shake off, croaking warnings about the Choir's reach, about Mother Vesper's web tightening around the pass. Three days of Sef walking half a step behind me, close enough to catch me if I stumbled, far enough to pretend he wasn't watching. And three days of me not sleeping properly, because every time I closed my eyes I saw the mother from Ashfall mouthing her wedding day at the sky, selling it over and over to no one.

So when Brannoc shook me awake in the black hour before dawn, part of me was almost grateful for the interruption.

"Up," he whispered. "Company."

I came awake the way thieves do — all at once, hand already finding the knife under my bedroll, mind already cataloguing exits. The stones hummed around us. The wind had died. That was wrong. The wind never died on this pass; it lived here the way lichen lives on rock.

"The song changed," Brannoc said. He crouched beside me, big as a door, gray-bearded, smelling of pipe smoke and road dust and forty years of smuggler's arithmetic. His eyes were on the dark beyond the ring. "Listen."

I listened. Under the granite's drone there was something else now. A second note. Thin. Wrong. Like someone humming along with the stones who didn't know the song and didn't care.

"Gray Choir," I breathed.

"Or worse." He grinned at me, and it was his old grin, the one he'd worn the day we met, when he'd caught a fourteen-year-old pickpocket lifting his purse and instead of breaking my fingers had bought me dinner and asked, curious, what it felt like to take things. "Good thing you're awake, little thief. You'd hate to miss your own funeral."

"Sef," I hissed across the dead fire. Nothing. I hissed again.

"I'm up." Sef rose out of the dark like he'd never been asleep at all, which knowing him, he hadn't. Warders slept with one ear open and both hands ready. His eyes were already moving, reading the night the way I read a locked room. "Six of them. Maybe seven. They've been circling since midnight."

"You knew?" I kept my voice low. "And you let us sleep?"

"You needed it. And they weren't coming yet." He drew his ward-blade — a thin gray thing that drank light instead of reflecting it. "They're coming now."

Ossifrage burst off my shoulder in a clatter of feathers, wheeling up through the dark. "*Fly!*" the crow screamed, in its voice like a hinge that needed oil. "*Fly, fly, name-bearer, fly south, fly NOW—*"

But the ring only had one gap wide enough for a horse, and even in the dark I could see shapes filling it. Gray robes. Gray hoods. And where faces should have been, smooth blankness, like eggs, like river stones, like the places on a wall where pictures used to hang.

Hollowed ones. The Choir's hands. People who'd given everything up to the silence and now did its errands wearing what was left.

"Brannoc," I said. "Your axe."

"Already in my hand, girl." It was. It always was. There was a reason the Thornfell clans still told stories about him, and most of them ended with somebody else running away.

What happened next happened fast, and I'll tell it fast, because slow would be lying.

They came through the gap in a silent rush — the Hollowed don't scream, that's the worst of it, they come at you quiet as debt collectors — and Sef's blade went up and a ward flared blue-white across the ring's mouth, and two of them hit it and recoiled like moths off a lamp. But wards are arithmetic, and six against three is bad math no matter how you carry the numbers.

One slipped around the ring's edge. Through the crack between two singing stones, where the song was thinnest.

It came for me.

I want to say I fought well. I want to tell you I moved like smoke and struck like lightning and all those lies thieves tell about themselves after the third drink. What actually happened is that I got my knife up, took a forearm like cold marble across my shoulder, and went down hard on the granite with the taste of blood and the whole stone-ring ringing through my skull like a bell someone had murdered.

And the Hollowed one leaned over me, faceless, patient, and reached for my head with fingers that had no warmth in them at all, and I thought — absurdly, in the last moment — *not the memory. Anything but the memory.* Because whatever else I'd lost, whatever I'd sold or spent or forgotten, the queen's word still burned in me like a coal wrapped in wool, and if they took that, Vael stayed dead forever, and everything — Ashfall, the Lethe-wood, Sef's palm bleeding on the First Door, all of it — would have been for nothing.

Then an axe came out of the dark and took the Hollowed one apart at the shoulders.

Brannoc stood over me, breathing hard, beard swinging, and hauled me up by the collar like I weighed nothing, which we both knew was a lie but a kind one.

"Little thief," he said. "You're bleeding on my boots."

"There's blood on your boots anyway," I managed. "There's blood on everything you own."

"Aye." He laughed. Gods, he laughed, deep and easy, while the ward-light strobed blue across his face and Ossifrage screamed overhead and Sef held the gap alone. "That's the trade, isn't it? Everything costs. I told you that on the pass, first day. Smuggler's arithmetic."

"Brannoc—"

"More coming round the north side." He wasn't looking at me anymore. He was looking at the dark between the stones, where more gray shapes were flowing, silent, patient, endless. "Sef can't hold both gaps. Somebody has to hold the north side."

"Somebody has to—" My stomach dropped through the mountain. "No."

"Somebody has to," he agreed pleasantly. "And I'm the biggest somebody here, and I'm the oldest, and I've had the best run of anyone I ever met, and I will not stand here and watch them eat a girl who owes me one childhood and half a bottle of very bad plum brandy." He shoved me toward the south gap, toward Sef, toward the narrow dark path down the mountain. "Go."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are, though." Still pleasant. Still grinning. But his eyes were wet, and I'd known this man eleven years and I had never once seen his eyes wet. "Because if you stay, I'll just have to save you again, and I'm saving up my strength. Go find your kingdom, Lena. Go put the stars back. And when you do—" he paused, and the grin softened into something older and sadder and truer, "—when you do, remember me loud. Remember me so hard the Hollow chokes on it."

The Hollowed poured through the north gap.

He met them like a landslide meets a road.

I saw the first swing take two of them down. I saw Sef scream something — his voice cracking the ward open for one heartbeat to shout *BRANNOC* — and I saw Brannoc glance back at me, just once, over his shoulder, axe rising and falling, rising and falling, buying seconds with years.

"Lena!" he roared, above the stone-song, above the ward-light, above everything. And then he said a name.

Not *Lena.*

Another one. Older. True. A name that landed in my chest like a key turning in a lock I hadn't known was there — a name that fit some hollow place in me perfectly, a place I'd carried all my life without knowing it was shaped like anything, and the name rang through that emptiness and *filled* it, one golden instant, before—

Before the gray hands took him.

I won't write down what the Hollow does. Not because I don't remember. Because some remembering is a weapon you shouldn't leave lying around.

The stones stopped singing.

All nine of them, all at once, mid-note. The silence that fell was total and soft and hungry, and in it I heard — faintly, horribly — the sound of Brannoc's laughter being unwound from the world, thread by thread.

Then Sef's hand closed on my arm and we were running, down the south path, into the dark, with Ossifrage overhead screaming *fly fly fly*, and the name Brannoc gave me echoing in my skull like the last coin in an empty purse.

I knew my real name for exactly as long as it took us to reach the tree line.

Then, like water through a thief's fingers, it was gone.

### Sef

I have killed people I loved. This is the sort of sentence the Choir teaches you to say flatly, and I said it flatly for years, and I believed I understood it.

I did not understand it until I dragged Lena down the mountain with Brannoc's death still standing in my eyes like an afterimage, and heard them stop dead on the trail and say, in a small, scraped-out voice:

"What was it? What did he say? At the end?"

"You heard it."

"No." Their hands were shaking. Blood on their temple, ash in their hair, and their eyes — their eyes were the worst thing I'd seen all night, and I had watched a good man die. "No, I heard it, and then it went. Like — like a word in a language I don't speak. Sef. What was my name?"

And I opened my mouth to tell them.

And found nothing there.

I searched the way you search a room you know — left pocket, right pocket, under the bedroll, behind the loose stone — and the room was empty. Whatever the Hollow had taken from Brannoc in those last moments, it had taken the name out of the world entire. Out of him, out of Lena, out of me. Out of the air itself. A name spoken is a name given, and the Hollow had snatched it out of the giving like a pickpocket working a crowd.

The irony of that did not escape me. It has not escaped me since.

"We'll find it again," I said, which was a lie, and Lena looked at me, and I watched them decide to let me keep the lie because the truth was too heavy for that trail, that hour, those shaking legs. Eleven years of loving someone teaches you the shape of their mercies. They extended me one.

Ossifrage dropped onto a branch above us, feathers ragged, one wing held wrong. The crow's eyes — black as vault doors — fixed on Lena with something I'd never seen in them before. Grief. Real grief, bird-shaped and ancient.

"He gave it away," Ossifrage rasped. "Clever fool. Clever, stupid, brave fool. A true name spoken in love cannot be eaten clean, name-bearer. It leaves a scar in the shape of itself. Somewhere in you, there is a hole with edges. Edges can be traced."

"Then trace it," Lena whispered.

"Not tonight." The crow's head drooped. "Tonight we are wounded, all three of us, and the Choir is not done, and grief makes poor mortar. Tonight we walk."

We walked. Down through the black pines, past a stream that ran too quietly, past a shrine to some road-god whose statue had been defaced by weather into featurelessness — and Lena flinched at it, and I pretended not to see, and the pretending cost me more than the seeing.

Here is what I did not say, walking behind them in the dark: I remembered Brannoc's last words. Not the name itself — that was gone, scooped out of me like marrow from a bone — but the *fact* of them. The shape. The way his mouth moved around two syllables, maybe three, tender as a christening. And I understood, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in, that the Hollow had not merely killed him.

It had robbed the funeral before the body was cold. Taken the eulogy out of the mourner's mouths. Stolen the one gift he had left — the truth of who Lena was — from everyone alive who might have carried it.

The Choir calls this *tidying.*

I have served order my whole life. I have told myself that the gray work was mercy, that unburdening minds was kindness, that a world without dangerous memories was a world without wars. I knelt in white halls and swore oaths and let them hollow my doubts out of me like pulp from a fruit, and I called the emptiness peace.

Standing in that dark wood, listening to Lena breathe wrong — the quick shallow breathing of someone holding themselves together with habit — I finally understood what I had sworn to.

Not order.

Erasure. Tidied, catalogued, sanctified erasure. And Brannoc — loud, generous, impossible Brannoc, who fed strays and forgave debts and once carried a lame mule four miles on his own back — Brannoc was just the latest entry in a ledger ten thousand names long.

My ledger. My oath. My tidy gray sin.

"Sef." Lena had stopped. Turned. In the dark their face was mostly shadow, but their eyes caught what little light the starless sky allowed. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stand very still and judge yourself so hard the rest of us can feel it." They stepped closer. "Stop it. He chose. You heard him choose. If you make his choice into your guilt, you're stealing from him too, and you're a better thief than that."

A wet laugh came out of me that I hadn't authorized. "Did you just lecture me on theft ethics?"

"Somebody has to. You're terrible at it." But their voice cracked on the last word, and then they were leaning into me, forehead against my collarbone, shaking, and I put my arms around them and held on the way you hold something you have already lost once and been given back and cannot bear to lose twice.

Above us, Ossifrage murmured into the dark, low and rhythmic — and I realized the crow was reciting. Names. A long litany of names, going back and back, every soul the aviary's last familiar had watched the Hollow swallow, hoarded feather by feather against the silence.

"...and Brannoc of the Thornfell clans," the crow finished, "who paid the whole price, and paid it laughing."

Lena lifted their head.

"When this is over," they said, and their voice was different now — scraped clean, tempered, the way a blade is different after the forge — "when Vael stands again and the stars come home, I am going to carve his name into every border stone in the kingdom. Every single one. And if I can't find his name, I'll carve the space where it goes, and I'll make the space so loud that no one who passes it can ever forget that something was taken."

"That's not how memorials usually work," I said.

"I'm not usually a memorial person." They wiped their face with the heel of their hand, briskly, businesslike, a thief closing a job. "I'm a ledger person. And I just opened a new page."

Far behind us and above us, on the shoulder of the pass, the Singing Stones began to hum again — the old melody, the ward-melody, the one the country was built on. Whether it was the wind returning or something else, something answering, none of us said.

But Ossifrage turned toward the sound, and ruffled, and said softly, in a voice I had never heard the crow use before — reverent, almost frightened:

"They're singing for him. Nine hundred years those stones have sung, and they have never once changed the tune."

The melody shifted. Rose. Resolved, at the end, into two syllables — maybe three — that sounded almost, almost like a name.

And then the wind took it, and the dark took the wind, and somewhere down the valley a horn sounded: the Choir, regrouping, hunting, patient as winter.

We had a drowned city to reach, a door to open, and a name to give back to the world.

We walked.
