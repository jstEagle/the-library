## 23. Burial Without a Name

The stones were still singing when we started digging.

That was the worst of it, I think — or one of the worst things, and by that hour the worst things had begun queuing politely, like supplicants at a guild office. The Singing Stones kept up their low harmonic thrum, the same note they'd held since before the Choir came over the ridge, since before Brannoc put himself between us and a dozen gray-robed singers with knives made of quiet. The stones didn't know he was dead. The stones would go on not knowing forever.

"Shouldn't we make them stop?" Sef asked. He stood at the edge of the clearing with his ward-sense prickling off him like heat off a forge, hands half-raised as if he might shush the mountains personally.

"No," I said. "Let them sing. He liked it here."

It was true. Brannoc had stopped at this pass every season for thirty years, long enough that the smuggler routes had names for him the way they had names for landmarks: *Old Brannoc's Bend*, *Brannoc's Nap Rock* (a flat stone exactly the shape of a man who has decided the cargo can wait). He used to say the Stones sang the oldest song in Vael, older than wards, older than roads, and that anyone who couldn't sleep to it was carrying too much guilt for the road to hold anyway.

He'd said it to me the night before he died. That was the kind of luck I had now. Every kindness anyone did me came pre-loaded with its own funeral.

Sef dug. I dug. Ossifrage perched on a boulder and offered commentary, because the crow was constitutionally incapable of silence when silence was what the moment demanded.

"You're digging too shallow," Ossifrage observed. "Corvids inter our dead far deeper. Also we do it with more dignity. Also sometimes we eat them, which is efficient."

"Not helping," Sef said through his teeth.

"I wasn't aware I was employed."

I let them argue. It filled the hole faster than shovels. There is a particular arithmetic to grief-work: you dig, and each shovelful is heavier than the last, not because the ground hardens but because you know what it's *for*, and somewhere around the fourth foot your arms start doing the work while your mind stands off to the side holding its hat. My mind had been holding its hat since dawn.

Brannoc lay under his own cloak on the pine boughs Sef had cut. They'd cleaned him as best we could with snow and river water. The bolt from the Choir's arbalest had gone in under the collarbone, quick, and I clung to *quick* the way a drowning man clings to driftwood that is technically also part of the problem. Quick. He hadn't lingered. He hadn't been hollowed. Whatever else the Hollow and its Choir took from this world, they hadn't gotten to eat him from the inside out. They'd only gotten all of him at once, which was somehow both better and infinitely worse.

His face was wrong, though. Dead faces are always wrong — the person leaves and the face stays, like a house with all the lights on but nobody home — but his was wronger than most, because his mouth was open slightly, mid-word.

Mid-*name*.

His last word had been my name. My real name, the one I'd traded away years ago in a vault full of sleeping nobles, the one even *I* hadn't been able to remember until a dying man spent his final breath handing it back to me like a coat check ticket.

*Lena.* Just Lena, actually. That was the joke of it. The grand secret name, the thing I'd built my whole guarded heart around losing, and it turned out to be four letters I could have guessed. All that weight for something so small. Or maybe that was the point. Maybe names are small the way keys are small.

"He should have run," I said. I don't know who I was talking to. The shovel, maybe. "I told him to run. He was faster than me. He's *always* been faster than me."

"He was buying time," Sef said quietly.

"I know what he was doing. I know the arithmetic." I threw another shovelful over my shoulder. "I just — he traded his whole life for about forty seconds. Forty seconds. That's a terrible exchange rate. He'd have haggled it down to nothing. He'd have called me an idiot for accepting terms that bad."

Sef didn't answer, which was correct, because there wasn't anything to answer with. We'd buried enough people in the last months to have developed a protocol of silences: the silence for strangers, the silence for comrades, and now this new one, the worst one, the silence-for-people-who-died-saying-your-name.

* * *

By late afternoon the grave was deep enough, and we lowered him down, and then came the problem of the marker.

Smugglers get markers. It's one of the few dignities of the trade. A cairn, usually — a pile of stones any traveler can add to, so the dead man keeps growing even underground — and a carving, a name, so the road remembers. The road remembering matters more out here than anywhere. Out here memory isn't sentimental. It's infrastructure. Lose the memory of a safe ford and people drown; lose the memory of a friend and the next traveler starves where he'd have been fed.

Sef found a flat stone, good and wide, and got out his knife. He looked at me. "What do I carve?"

And I opened my mouth to say *Brannoc*, and discovered I didn't know how to spell it.

Not in the way you fumble over spelling — *one n or two?* I mean the letters wouldn't come at all. I knew the shape of the word the way you know the shape of a room you grew up in, warm, familiar, full, and when I reached for its parts they slid away like fish. I tried again. *B-r-a—* nothing. The Hollow had eaten through this valley two nights ago on its way to the ambush, and it had grazed as it went, the way a locust swarm grazes, and somewhere in its passage it had taken the spelling of my oldest friend's name out of my head along with, apparently, the taste of smoked trout, the layout of Brannoc's camp at Thornfell, and the sound of his laugh, which I noticed missing only because I went looking for it and found a hole shaped exactly like it.

"Carve—" I started. Stopped. Started again. "Sef. What was his laugh like?"

Sef went very still. Warders notice things; it's their whole profession, noticing, and I watched him notice this. "Low," he said carefully. "Like a cart going over a bridge. You told me once it was the best sound on the pass."

"Yeah," I said. "I would've said that."

I sat down on the cold ground next to the grave and laughed, once, horribly, because the alternative was screaming and I'd learned that screaming attracts the Hollow the way spilled honey attracts everything else. "It's eating him twice. Do you understand? It ate him with a bolt, and now it's back for seconds. It gets the whole meal. It gets the grave goods. In a year nobody will know his name, and in ten years nobody will know there was ever a man here at all, and in fifty years some kid will trip over this cairn and think it marks a border or a badger den, and that will be Brannoc's entire legacy. Forty seconds of my life. Bought with all of his. And the Hollow doesn't even have to chew."

Ossifrage ruffled his feathers. For once the crow had nothing clever to contribute, which from Ossifrage was practically a eulogy.

"No," I said. And the word came out of me with force I didn't know I'd stored. "No. That's not how this ends."

I stood up. My legs were shaking — I'd been shaking since the ambush, honestly, some animal part of me still running from those gray robes — but I stood up anyway and walked to the flat stone and took the knife out of Sef's hand.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

"Something worse than stealing," I said. "Giving something away for free."

I carved into the stone. Not his name — I couldn't, and pretending otherwise would be a different kind of lie. Instead I carved the things I still had. Every fact about Brannoc Underhill — no, not Underhill, that was mine, borrowed, whatever — every fact I could still hold:

*Bought passage with stories when he had no coin.*

*Knew forty-one ways through Thornfell and claimed forty-two, to keep the count interesting.*

*Hated the taste of honey. Loved the smell of it.*

*Called his mule "Your Honor" as an insult and the mule believed it.*

*Sang badly and always, especially the warding songs, especially off-key, on purpose, because "the wards need humbling."*

*Died facing north. Died buying forty seconds. Died saying a name.*

I carved until my fingers bled, and then I carved the last line, the one that mattered, the one I cut deepest:

*HE WAS REAL. HE WAS HERE. THE ROAD KNOWS IT EVEN IF YOU DON'T.*

"There," I said, when I finished, and my voice cracked straight down the middle. "Let the Hollow come read it. Let it try to un-carve stone. Stone remembers longer than skulls do. Ask any mountain."

Sef put his hand on my shoulder. He didn't say anything wise. Wise was beyond either of us that evening. But he stayed, and staying was its own language, one I was — slowly, suspiciously, against all my professional instincts — learning to translate.

* * *

We built the cairn as the light failed. Sef laid the foundation stones square, with a warder's precision; I stacked the rest with a thief's contempt for symmetry; Ossifrage contributed one entirely unnecessary rock from a great height, striking Sef on the head, which Brannoc would have found hilarious, so we counted it as a libation.

Then I stood in front of it, and I made the vow.

I want to be honest here, since honesty has become the only currency I trust anymore: I am not a person who makes vows. Vows are for people with faith in outcomes. I deal in exits. Every job I ever ran, I ran with a door mapped and a second door behind it. Promising things felt like leaving the second door locked.

But Brannoc was under six feet of Thornfell granite because he'd stood in a doorway for me, and I thought: fine. One door. Locked. Here's what's on the other side.

"I'm going to remember them," I said. My voice carried strangely in the Stones' singing, harmonized with it almost, one thin human thread woven into the old note. "Everyone the Hollow takes. Everyone. Not just the ones I loved — everyone. The mother in Ashfall who sold her wedding day. The boy who forgot his sister mid-sentence. Every hollowed soldier, every emptied village, every name scraped off every map. I can't bring them back yet. Maybe I never can. But while I'm standing, they're recorded. I'm a thief. Taking things is what I do, and the Hollow thinks it's taking everything." I pressed my bleeding palm flat against the top stone. "It picked the wrong country to rob. I steal *back*."

Sef watched me with an expression I couldn't file. Something between awe and fear and the look of a man watching someone walk out onto ice he's already tested.

"That vow will cost you," he said finally. "You know that. Memory isn't free storage. Carrying theirs alongside yours —"

"I know what carrying costs." I looked at him. "I'm the one who's been paying retail."

"And if it fills you up? If there's no room left for *you*?"

"Then somebody had better keep a ledger," I said, and saw him flinch, because he already was, had been for weeks, that little book in his coat full of me, and I hadn't meant to hand him permission so much as confess how much I needed it. "Come on. It's getting dark, and dark is theirs."

We gathered camp. We didn't light a fire — smoke draws the Choir, and grief draws the Hollow, and I refuse to feed my best friend's ghost to either. We ate cold rations in the lee of the cairn, which felt right. Brannoc would have approved of a wake with no fire and no songs. He'd have said fire wastes warmth and songs waste breath, and then he'd have sung anyway, badly, on purpose.

Ossifrage settled onto the topmost stone like a gargoyle taking a post. "I'll watch him," the crow said, without preamble, without irony. "Crows remember everything. I told you that. Now it's load-bearing." He tucked his head under his wing, and if crows could look solemn, he managed it, mostly, except for one eye, which stayed open and fixed on the northern dark.

* * *

I slept in fits. Somewhere past midnight I gave up and climbed out of my bedroll and sat against the cairn with my knees drawn up, talking to the stones in a whisper, telling Brannoc everything that had happened since he died, because that's what he'd have wanted — news first, mourning later, and only if the news was bad enough to justify the expense.

I told him about the plan. Cael Morath. The First Door. The queen's name sitting in my skull like a coal that wouldn't cool. I told him Sef was the heir, though Brannoc had known before I had — old smuggler read bloodlines the way he read weather, and he'd squinted at Sef the first day and said, *"That one's got royal posture and no idea why,"* which I'd dismissed and which I now owed him an apology for, posthumously, along with everything else.

I told him I was scared. That was the real wake gift. Brannoc was the only person alive who'd ever seen me scared and not treated it as either weakness or leverage. He'd just handed me tea and waited.

"Forty seconds," I whispered to the stones. "I'm going to make them worth a kingdom. You terrible, generous old man. Watch me."

The Stones hummed. The wind moved. And far off across the black ridges, faint as a scar, something vast and silent shifted its weight — the Hollow, turning in its sleep, or waking, or listening.

But underneath the silence, if I held my breath and listened the way Brannoc taught me, I could hear it: the wards of Vael, broken and scattered, still trying to sing. Off-key. Badly. On purpose, maybe, to keep themselves humble.

Getting louder.

Toward morning Sef shook my shoulder gently. "There's light on the eastern road," he said. "Refugee column. And Lena — there's a rider ahead of it, moving fast. Gray cloak."

I stood. My palm had scabbed over the carved stone's imprint, and I flexed it once, like a promise flexing.

"Well," I said, "he bought us forty seconds. Let's see how far we can stretch them."
