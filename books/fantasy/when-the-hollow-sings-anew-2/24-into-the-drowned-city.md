## 24. Into the Drowned City

The black water found us before we found the city.

We'd been walking three days through country that wasn't country — the mapless stretch where Vael used to be, where the ground remembered roads but wouldn't commit to them. Sef walked ahead with his ward-sense pricked like a cat in a thunderstorm. Ossifrage rode my shoulder, heavier than any crow has a right to be, muttering directions in a voice like a hinge that had learned to be bitter about it.

"Left at the dead elm," he said. "No, your other left. Saints preserve me from bipeds."

"You said right at the dead elm," I said.

"I said left at the dead elm an hour ago, and right at the birch, and you argued about the birch too. This is why I outlived the monarchy. No respect for avian authority."

I let him have it. When a bird who remembers a kingdom tells you which way the drowned city lies, you don't quibble over elms.

Sef stopped so abruptly I walked into his back, which was becoming something of a habit, and neither of us was pretending anymore that we minded.

"Listen," he said.

I listened. Wind. The creak of half-real trees. My own pulse doing its usual workmanlike thudding. "I hear nothing."

"That's what I mean." He turned, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen since Ashfall burned — not fear exactly. Appetite's cousin. "There's no silence here. There's *held* silence. Like a note someone's keeping in their chest."

Ossifrage shifted on my shoulder. For once he didn't snark. "She feels it too," he said quietly. "Look at her face."

I hadn't known I was making a face. But my hands had gone cold and quick the way they always did before a job, before a vault, before anything worth the risk of me. Because here's the thing nobody tells you about thieves: we're not really after coin. Coin's just the receipt. What we're after is the moment a locked thing opens. Every heist I ever ran, every nobleman's sleeping mind I slipped into, every rule I broke — it all came down to that one held breath between *sealed* and *open*.

And somewhere past the ridge, something sealed was waiting to open.

"Come on," I said, and my voice came out hungrier than I meant it to.

* * *

The water appeared the way grief does — gradually, then all at once. First a dampness in the air that tasted of old stone. Then puddles that weren't reflecting the sky, because there wasn't a sky to reflect, just the gray nothing the Hollow had left where heaven used to be. Then a shoreline, black sand glittering faintly, and beyond it a sea without waves, lying flat as poured ink under our feet and away to a horizon that didn't bother existing.

"Cael Morath," Ossifrage said, and for the first time since I'd known him his voice cracked down the middle. He ruffled his feathers and looked away, out over the water. "They called it the City of Jars. Built on the shallows so the tide could sing under the floors. The queens kept their drowned stars there."

"Their *what*?" Sef asked.

But I'd stopped listening, because the city was rising.

It didn't surge up like something breaching. It rose the way a memory rises — slowly at first, a suggestion, a line of rooftops surfacing through the black water like a thought you almost lost. Then towers, slender and crooked as fingers uncurling. Then spires, hundreds of them, each one crowned with glass, and inside each globe of glass a light.

Stars. Actual stars, or near enough that my eyes stung looking at them. They burned underwater-bright through the glass, throwing long trembling beams across the flat sea, so that the whole city stood in a lattice of light like a spiderweb made of lanterns. The water beneath them glowed green-gold where the beams touched it. Somewhere under those floors, I realized, the tide really was singing — a low chord, patient, older than any song anyone had sung to me.

"Oh," I said. Just that. Just the smallest word there is, falling out of me because everything bigger had been stolen already.

Sef was staring too. His hand had drifted up, half-reaching toward the light, the way you reach toward a fire when you're cold enough to stop caring about dignity. "The Choir told us Cael Morath was a ruin," he said. "A sunk wreck. A warning."

"The Choir says a lot of things," Ossifrage muttered. "That's rather their entire profession."

We walked down onto the black sand. Where our feet touched the waterline, the sea parted around us — politely, the way a crowd parts — and held a dry path of gleaming stone just wide enough for three. Or two and a crow, if the crow was going to sulk about being counted last.

"It knows us," Sef said softly.

"It knows the name you're carrying," Ossifrage said, nodding at me. Not at Sef. At me. And there was something in the way he said it — careful, weighted, like a man setting down something fragile — that I filed away and didn't look at directly. Filing things away and not looking at them is most of my skill set. It's also, I'm beginning to suspect, most of my problem.

* * *

The streets of Cael Morath were canals. We walked the raised walkways between them, and the water below was full of light — drowned stars had drifted loose over the centuries, or been released, and they hung suspended in the black depths like fruit in dark jelly. Some pulsed slow, like sleeping hearts. A few flickered fast, frantic, and Ossifrage went silent whenever we passed those, and hurried us along.

"What are the fast ones?" I asked finally.

"Dying," he said. "A star in a jar lasts as long as it's tended. Untended—" He snapped his beak. "The keepers are gone. Everything's running down. That's what a kingdom dying looks like from the inside, thief. Not fire. Not war. Just lights going out one jar at a time while everyone forgets there was ever supposed to be someone whose job was tending them."

I thought of Brannoc. Of course I did; I think of him now the way you touch a bruise, compulsively, to check it still hurts. His cairn back under the Singing Stones with a blank stone on top. I'd promised to remember everyone the Hollow took. So I did what I do. I took out the ledger Sef keeps for me — no. Wait. The ledger I keep now. Mine. Sef started it, but it's mine; that's what he says anyway, in that careful voice, handing it over each morning like it might bite.

I wrote: *Cael Morath stands. Lights still burning. Counted forty-one jars on the first street alone.* Because if the city could rise out of a sea that shouldn't exist, then maybe remembering wasn't as hopeless as it felt. Maybe things could come back. Maybe that was the whole point of stars in jars — somebody believed light could be saved.

"Look at this," Sef said. He'd stopped at a doorway set into a wall of white coral-stone. Above it, carved deep, worn smooth by water that had never actually touched it, was a sigil: a crown made of seven interlocking rings.

"That's the royal mark," Ossifrage said. His voice had gone very quiet and very old.

"No," Sef said. "It's not. The royal mark is five rings. This is seven." He was frowning, touching the stone like a man reading by fingertip. "Why do I know that? Why does the difference matter to me?"

Neither of us answered. Neither of us knew. But Ossifrage looked at Sef for a long moment with his head tilted, the way birds look at things they've decided not to comment on yet, and my stomach did a small cold somersault that I chose, professionally, to ignore.

* * *

We lodged that night — if night meant anything here, where the only darkness came from the gaps between star-jars — in a keeper's house built out over a canal. Round windows. A hearth that lit itself when Sef's blood-warm hand brushed the mantel, which it did casually, absently, the way he kept doing things that should have been impossible for a Gray Choir warder, and which I kept not mentioning, because we had an arrangement, Sef and I, and the arrangement was mostly *not asking yet.*

From the round window I could see the whole spread of the city, the lattice of light, the flat black sea holding it up like cupped hands. And I felt something I hadn't felt since before the night the stars went out. Not hope exactly. Hope's a word for people with better odds. This was smaller and more stubborn. Wonder. Plain, childish wonder, sitting down inside me like a traveler who intends to stay.

Here's my confession, since this ledger seems to want honesty: I stole memories for eleven years and I never once stole a sunset. Never a wedding, never a lullaby, never anything beautiful unless someone paid me. Beauty was other people's property. I dealt in secrets and shames, the inventory of the locked rooms. I told myself that was discipline. Rules. Never keep what you steal.

Standing at that round window with the drowned stars burning underneath me, I understood for the first time that it was never discipline. It was fear. Beautiful things, kept things, are the things that hurt worst to lose. So I never let myself hold any. And now I was standing in a city built entirely out of held light, and the hollow space where my sister's face used to be ached like a missing tooth, and I thought: *no more.* Whatever this costs. Whatever the door asks. I have carried other people's treasures my whole life. Time I learned to carry my own.

Below, in the canal, one of the fast-flickering stars guttered — and steadied. Brightened. Held.

All along the canal, one by one, other jars began to burn a little stronger. As if the city had heard me. As if tending were contagious.

Sef, behind me, went very still. "Lena," he said carefully. "What did you just do?"

"Nothing," I said. "I just looked at them."

"Yes," he said. "Exactly."

And far off across the black water, at the heart of the city where the tallest spire climbed toward nothing, a door-shaped outline began to glow — pale gold, patient, unmistakable.

The First Door, waiting. And something beside it, small and dark, that hadn't been there a moment ago.

Something shaped very much like a woman, standing very still, watching our window.
