## 25. The First Door

There is a particular kind of quiet that lives underwater even when the water has gone elsewhere, and Cael Morath was full of it.

We had been in the drowned city three days by then, picking our way down through its terraces like lice through the seams of a coat, and I had learned that the silence here wasn't empty. It was *stored*. The whole city felt like a jar with a lid on it — everything that had ever been said in these streets pressed down under glass, waiting for someone to unscrew the dark.

Lena didn't feel it that way, of course. Lena never felt anything the way anyone else did. They walked ahead of me along a boulevard that had once been a canal bed, boots crunching on salt-cured cobbles, humming a tune they almost knew, and said, "You're doing the face again."

"What face?"

"The one where you're thinking something noble and planning to be insufferable about it later."

"That's just my face."

"It's your *choir* face," they said. "I've seen it on the others. It's the face of a man who's about to sacrifice himself for the greater good and wants credit."

"I want," I said, with dignity, "to find a door."

And we did. That was the trouble with Lena. They were right about nearly everything, which would have been fine if they'd ever learned to stop saying so.

* * *

The First Door sat at the bottom of the city, which made sense, because everything important in Cael Morath had been built at the bottom, where the founders figured grief would pool.

Getting there meant descending the Spine — a stair cut into the cliff beneath the old palace, four hundred steps, each one carved with a name. Not kings. Not heroes. Just names. A cooper. A girl called Tamsin who apparently liked eels. A midwife. The founders had put their whole city on the stairs, as if to say: *you do not reach the door of this country except over the shoulders of everyone in it.*

Ossifrage rode my shoulder most of the way down, heavier than a crow has any right to be, muttering commentary.

"Four hundred and six," he croaked at step four hundred and six, because he'd been counting wrong out loud for an hour purely to irritate me. "In my day we counted honestly."

"In your day," I said, "you were a bird in a palace telling jokes to queens."

"And she laughed at every one," he said, "because she was gracious. Unlike some warders I could mention."

Behind us, Lena laughed — a short, surprised sound, like they hadn't authorized it. I'd been collecting those sounds for days now without meaning to. It was becoming a problem. There was a ledger in my pack with entries about Lena Underhill in my own handwriting, and lately I'd caught myself writing things no ledger should hold. *They smiled at the jar-lights today like the jars were old friends.* That sort of rot.

At the bottom of the stair was a plaza, and in the plaza was the door.

I'd imagined it wrong. I'd expected something vast, something that ate the sky — the way the Choir described gates, all teeth and architecture. But the First Door was barely taller than a man. Old wood gone black as bog-oak, set in a frame of white stone that the sea had never managed to stain. It stood free, unattached to any wall, in the middle of the plaza like a headstone someone had planted facing the wrong direction.

Carved above the lintel, in letters worn shallow by salt and centuries:

**SAY WHAT WAS LOST. PAY WHAT IT COST.**

"Well," Lena said. "Cheerful."

"It's a door," Ossifrage said. "Doors are honest. It's doors' one virtue."

Lena walked up to it and put their hand flat against the wood, the way they touched everything — thieves touch first and ask after, it's how they take the measure of a thing. I watched their fingers spread. Watched them go very still.

"It's warm," they said quietly. "Sef. Why is a door warm?"

Because there was a heart behind it, I wanted to say. Because Vael isn't gone, only folded, and folded things are warm where the creases are. Because I have known what this door wants since I was nineteen years old and kneeling in a gray chapel while Mother Vesper held a knife to my oath.

What I said was: "Residual warding. Old magic holds heat."

Lena looked back at me over their shoulder. They have eyes the color of river ice over dark water, and they are far too good at using them. "You've been lying to me since the Lethe-wood," they said. "Small lies. Little ones. 'I'm fine.' 'I don't remember that village.' 'Nothing's wrong.'"

"Have I?"

"Don't do the face."

"Lena—"

"The *face*, Sef." But they let it go, which was worse than pressing. When a thief lets something go, it's not mercy. It's marking where you keep it.

* * *

We camped that night in the shadow of the door, because there was nowhere else and because — though nobody said it aloud — none of us quite trusted it to still be there in the morning. Things in Cael Morath had a habit of rearranging themselves when you weren't watching. The drowned stars in their jars drifted through the streets at night like patient lantern-fish. Whole avenues migrated. Once I'd watched a fountain walk.

Ossifrage slept — or pretended to sleep — with his head under his wing, which was his way of pretending not to listen.

Lena sat across the small fire from me, feeding it splinters of a drowned bench, and turned the stolen memory over in their skull the way they always did when they thought I wasn't looking. I could see it happening. Their pupils did something strange, a flicker, like a lamp guttering. The queen's last word, sitting in their head behind their eyes, wearing them like a borrowed coat.

"Ask me," they said suddenly.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you're not asking. You've got a question with teeth. It's been chewing on you since the stair."

I fed the fire instead of answering.

"Sef."

"There's a price," I said. "On the door. Beyond the name."

"I can read, warder. *Pay what it cost.* Cost of what? Coin? Memories?" They gave a thin smile. "Memories I can manage. I'm practically liquid."

"Not memories." I heard my own voice go flat, Choir-flat, the voice I used for delivering truths that hurt other people. "Blood. Royal blood. The door was keyed to the queen's line — it's a vault-gate, not an exit. It opens for her blood and her word together. One without the other is just a dead woman's furniture."

Lena stared at me. The firelight moved in their face.

"So we find the heir," they said slowly. "The lost heir. The one Ilvane's ghosts keep whispering about. The queen's blood, walking around somewhere in this mess of a world, probably wearing a gray robe and singing hymns to the thing that ate our country—"

"Probably," I agreed.

"You know something." It wasn't a question. They were leaning forward now, firelight and jar-light fighting over their features. "You've known since the library. Since Ilvane said *the heir lives.* You did the face then too, and I was too tired to call it, and I am calling it now, Sef. What do you know about the heir?"

Here is what I have learned about secrets: they are heavy in exactly the way memory is heavy. You carry them the same place, behind the sternum, and they press on the same nerve, and eventually you start building your posture around them. I had been carrying mine for eleven years. My spine had opinions.

"I know the Choir has hunted the heir since the night Vael fell," I said. Each word placed like a stone in a wall. "I know Mother Vesper calls the hunt the Long Errand. I know that whoever carries the queen's blood could open this door tomorrow — and that if the Choir found them first, they wouldn't kill them. They'd hollow them. An empty key is still a key, but it doesn't get ideas."

"That's not knowing something," Lena said. "That's knowing nothing with extra steps."

"It's knowing what matters."

"No." They stood. Firelight jumped. "Knowing what matters is *telling me* —"

"*Telling you what?*" And it came out of me before I could weigh it, hot and sharp: "That I spent six years hunting people like you for the thing that murdered my country? You know that. That I reported you once and got Ashfall burned? You know that too. What is it you think I'm hiding, Lena? What's left?"

They went still in a way I'd learned to dread. Stillness, in Lena, is the drawn-bow moment.

"Something," they said softly. "Something with your heartbeat on it."

Then they rolled into their blanket with their back to me, and the conversation was over, and Ossifrage opened one eye, considered me with the entire judgment of a dead monarchy, and closed it again.

I lay awake most of the night doing arithmetic. This is what I am, underneath the sword and the oath and the face Lena keeps naming: an arithmetician of sins. *If I tell them, the Choir hears it through whatever channels remain. If I tell them, Lena looks at me differently — not with less trust, but with use. People look at keys with use.* And underneath all of it, the true sum, the one I refused to carry up out of the dark where I kept it: *if I tell them whose blood the door wants, they will understand immediately what it will cost to pay, and they will try to stop me, and they might succeed, and then Vael stays dead forever.*

Some secrets are cowardice. I told myself mine was logistics.

I have always been a good liar. It's the one skill the Choir sharpened rather than stole.

* * *

Morning came gray through the jar-stars, which dim politely at dawn, having better manners than most suns.

Lena was already up and dressed, standing before the door with their arms crossed, and I knew before I reached them that the night had changed something. Thieves metabolize betrayal fast; it's fuel to them.

"We're going in," they said. "Today."

"The name-memory—"

"Is ready or it isn't. We don't get to choose. Every hour we stand here, the Hollow gets one hour closer, and Brannoc bought us time with his *life*, so we don't waste it standing in a plaza admiring carpentry." They turned to me. "You'll do the ward-work. Wards are yours. The speaking is mine."

"And the price?" I asked, and hated how carefully casual I made it.

"Is royal blood." They shrugged. "Which we don't have. So here's my plan, and you're going to love it: we knock anyway. We put the name against the wood and we see what the door does with a thief's voice and no pedigree. Because I've been thinking about what Ilvane said. *The door is a wound.* And wounds, Sef—" they tapped the scar on their own jaw, the one they couldn't remember earning, "—wounds don't care whose hands are on them. They just want to be opened."

It was a bad plan. It was a plan built out of hope and spite and a crow's secondhand theology, and it ignored the single most important fact on this side of existence, a fact currently beating inside my own wrist.

"It might work," I said.

"You're doing the face again."

"I don't have a face."

"Everyone has a face," said Ossifrage, dropping onto my shoulder with a thump. "Yours is merely better defended than most. Like the rest of you."

Lena dug the memory out — I watched them do it, watched them reach into themselves with those thief's hands of theirs and draw the queen's last word up into their throat like hauling a bucket from a deep well. Their eyes went distant and ancient and terribly sad. For a moment they stood there breathing, and the plaza seemed to lean in, every drowned star in every jar brightening by one degree, the whole dead city holding what remained of its breath.

They stepped toward the door.

And I — eleven years of gray discipline, six years of hunting, a lifetime of arithmetic — I did the only thing left that the sum required.

I drew my knife and opened my palm before they could turn around, and let my blood fall on the threshold stones, where it steamed, and sank, and *answered.*

The door lit like a struck match.

The carving above the lintel blazed. The black wood warmed from hearth-warm to furnace-warm in a breath, and every name on the four hundred stairs behind us began, faintly, to sing — coopers and eel-girls and midwives, the whole city rising up its own throat — and Lena spun, memory still glowing in their mouth, and looked at my bleeding hand, and then at me, and I watched them arrive at the truth the way you watch someone fall: completely, and in slow motion, and all at once.

"Your blood," they whispered. "Sef. *Your blood answered the door.*"

The First Door began, very slowly, to open — and stopped, half an inch, quivering, hungry, waiting for the word that my blood alone could not give it.

Lena hadn't spoken yet. And they were looking at me the way people look at a locked room they've finally found the key to — and I understood, with perfect clarity, that I had run out of arithmetic.
