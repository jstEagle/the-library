## 26. Vesper's Web

The crow had been gone for two days when the web finally showed itself.

I should explain that Ossifrage disappearing was not, in itself, alarming. He was a royal familiar with the manners of a tax collector and the loyalty of a cat who has decided, provisionally, to live with you. He came and went as he pleased. He insulted my cooking. He perched on Sef's shoulder specifically because he knew it annoyed me and delivered his commentary in the flat, aristocratic drawl of a bird who had once advised queens.

But two days was long, even for him. And on the morning of the third day, we found the feather.

It was wedged in the doorframe of the shepherd's hut we'd been sheltering in — a single black feather, too large for any honest crow, and pinned there not by wind but by *intent*. Someone had driven it into the wood with a sliver of silver wire, and wound around that wire was a thread of gray silk so fine I nearly missed it.

Sef saw it before I did. His hand closed over mine before I could touch it.

"Don't," he said. "Choir silk. It drinks."

"Drinks what?"

"Whatever you're most afraid of losing." He said it lightly, the way you'd mention the weather, but his eyes weren't light at all. "It's a message. Not a trap. If they wanted you dead or taken, they wouldn't have bothered with theater."

I looked at the feather a long moment. The gray thread caught the dawn and held it wrong — swallowed it, gave nothing back.

"They have him," I said.

"Yes."

"And they want me to know it."

"Yes."

I pulled my hand free of his and crouched by the doorframe, careful not to touch anything, and studied the arrangement like the professional I used to be. The feather wasn't just a flag. It was an invitation. Underneath the wire, scratched into the wood in a hand so elegant it made my teeth ache, were three words:

*The stones remember.*

The Singing Stones. Half a day north, a ring of standing rocks where the old wards pooled like water in a cupped hand. A place where sound carried strangely and lies carried further.

"She wants a meeting," I said.

"Mother Vesper doesn't want meetings." Sef's jaw did the thing it did when he was swallowing words he thought would hurt me. "She wants audiences. There's a difference. One is a conversation. The other is a play where you find out your part halfway through."

* * *

We argued about it for exactly as long as it took me to finish packing, which is to say, not very long, and not because I won.

"You're walking into her hands," Sef said, shouldering his pack with the grim economy of a man who had decided to come along and hated himself for it. "You understand that. This is what she does. She builds a box out of things you love and then asks you to close the lid yourself."

"I know how cages work, Sef. I've picked enough of them."

"Not ones built for you."

He had me there, and he knew it, and I knew he knew it, and neither of us said anything else for the first mile. The Thornfell foothills rose around us, gray and patient, and somewhere above us the border wards still hummed their broken half-song — fainter now than they'd been weeks ago, like a singer running out of breath.

Here is what I didn't say to him: I wasn't going for Ossifrage. Or rather, I was, but not only. Ossifrage was the last living creature who remembered Vael whole — its aviaries, its vaults, its queen's dying breath. Every day he flew free was a day the country's memory stayed alive somewhere outside my skull. And my skull, lately, had been leaking.

That was the thing I hadn't told anyone. Since Ashfall burned, since the ford, since the argument in the dark before the First Door — small gaps had started opening in me. Nothing important. The name of a river I'd robbed along. Which pocket I kept my picks in. Whether I liked honey. Small thefts, and I recognized the signature on them, because they were mine: something was picking my pockets while I slept, and it was very, very good.

The Hollow didn't need to catch me. It just needed to wait.

So yes. I walked north toward Mother Vesper knowing it was a trap, because a trap requires you to be worth catching, and I intended to spend that worth buying back something that mattered.

* * *

The Singing Stones stood in a bowl of moorland, nine of them, leaning together like old conspirators. The wind moved through them and they answered in voices that weren't quite wind — low harmonics, almost words, the residue of every ward-song ever sung nearby. When I was young and stupid and new to the trade, I'd stolen a memory here from a wandering pilgrim, and for a month afterward the stones had hummed it back to me at night until I gave it up just to sleep.

They remembered everything spoken between them. That was the point. That was also, I suspected, the problem.

She was already there when we crested the rise.

Mother Vesper did not look like the leader of an order of memory-devouring zealots. She looked like someone's grandmother, if your grandmother had been carved from chalk and dressed in mourning. Gray robes, gray hair coiled tight, gray eyes that had the particular shine of frost over deep water. She sat on a fallen stone with her hands folded in her lap, and beside her — hobbled by a cord of that same drinking-silk, wings bound with wire — sat Ossifrage.

He looked terrible. His feathers were dulled, one wing bent at a bad angle, and his eyes — those ancient, contemptuous, queen-taught eyes — were half-lidded with pain. But when he saw me, he lifted his head and said, in that dry drawl, barely above a whisper:

"Oh, wonderful. The thief comes to the parley. Do try not to steal anything on the way down, dear. Your reputation precedes you."

"Ossifrage." My voice cracked on the second syllable and I hated it.

"He's quite comfortable," Vesper said. Her voice was soft. That was the worst thing about it. Everything she said sounded like a kindness being done to you without your consent. "A little silk, a little wire. Birds are simpler than people. They don't complicate captivity with hope."

"Let him go."

"Of course." She smiled, and the smile reached all the way to her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "That is rather why we're here."

* * *

I want to record, for whatever ledger survives me, exactly how she did it. Because it was masterful, and because I nearly fell for it, and because someday someone might need to recognize the shape of the thing.

She didn't threaten. She *grieved*.

"Child," she said, rising from the stone with the stiffness of age, crossing the grass toward me with her hands open, empty. Behind me I felt Sef go rigid. "Do you know what you carry? Truly? Not the story the crow told you — stories are birds' currency, and birds inflate. I mean do you *know*?"

"The name of Vael," I said. "Spoken by its dying queen. Hidden in a single memory in her own vault. Stolen by a thief who didn't know what she had." I shrugged. "Me. I've read this chapter."

Something flickered across her face at *she*. A tiny correction, filed away. Vesper missed nothing; that was her whole horror.

"And do you know what happens when you speak it?" she asked gently. "At the First Door? Have you asked yourself why the door demands a price? Why the queen hid the name instead of crying it from the battlements?" She shook her head slowly, sorrowfully. "Because a kingdom named aloud is a kingdom *bound*, child. Bound to its grief. The Hollow isn't a monster. It's a symptom. Vael died of what it refused to say, and if you speak the name, you don't cure it. You *enshrine* it. You nail the wound open forever and call it a country."

"That's a lovely speech," I said. "Did you write it yourself?"

"I buried forty years of sisters to earn the right to say it." No anger. Just weight. "The Choir began as healers, Lena Underhill. We eased the dying. We learned that memory is the cruelest organ — it keeps beating after everything else stops. We learned to take the pain out. And then we learned that some pains are too large for one mind, and the world taught us what it does with unspoken grief." She gestured upward, at the gray sky, at the absence where stars should be. "You want to restore Vael. So do I. Truly. But there are two ways to wake a sleeper, child. You can call their name — or you can let them dream on, painless, until the dream ends gently on its own."

"And the difference?"

"In one, they wake screaming. In the other, no one suffers at all." Her gray eyes held mine, and I will be honest: for three full seconds, I wanted it. I wanted the easy ending, the painless ledger, the world where nobody had to pay. "Give me the name. Give it freely, and I will release the crow, call off the Choir, and walk away from you forever. Vael can rest. You can rest. Everyone stops hurting. Isn't that what thieves want, in the end? To stop running?"

And there it was. The web, complete. Not silk and wire — those were just for the bird. The real web was built out of my own exhaustion, spun strand by strand across the whole road from Ashfall to here: the burned camp, the mother's sold wedding day, Brannoc's blank cairn, the gaps opening in my own skull like doors into dark rooms. She hadn't set any of it. She'd just *waited* for it, the way spiders wait, and now she offered to make it mean something.

Behind me, Sef said quietly: "Lena."

One word. Just my name. But the way he said it — I heard what was underneath, which was the memory of the ford, and the confession, and the harder terms we'd rebuilt trust on. *Trust that checks the arithmetic,* he'd told me. *When someone offers you everything for one small thing, count the small thing twice.*

I counted.

"Here's my problem with your offer," I said. "You said Vael can rest. But rest isn't what the dead want. I've stolen memories from the dying, Vesper. Dozens. And you know what they clutch hardest? Not the painless days. The painful ones. The fight with the daughter. The night the crops failed. The name of the person who broke their heart. Because pain *witnessed* is a life, and pain erased is just — silence with good posture." I took a step closer, and I watched her stillness sharpen. "You're not offering Vael peace. You're offering it oblivion with a eulogy. And you're offering me the same deal you gave Sef's doubts when the Choir hollowed them out. I know how your mercy works. It works by subtraction."

For the first time, Mother Vesper's face moved. Not much. A tightening around the mouth, a cooling behind the eyes. The grandmother shell thinned, and underneath it I glimpsed the thing that had buried forty sisters and called it healing.

"You are a thief," she said, "who has never once kept what she steals. And yet you lecture me on what the dead want."

"I never keep what I steal," I agreed, "because keeping is how you become *you*, Vesper. And look at you."

The silence after that had edges.

Then Ossifrage laughed.

It was a horrible sound — a dry, rasping, delighted caw, the laugh of a bird who had outlived queens and found the whole business increasingly funny. "Oh, *well struck*," he wheezed. "Vesper, dear, I did tell you. I told you she was the wrong thief to invite to tea. Queens always know. It's the first thing they teach you in the aviary: never bargain with someone who has already priced their own soul. They'll always bid higher than you expect—"

"*Quiet*," Vesper said, and the word came out with the silk in it, and the cord around Ossifrage's body *tightened*.

What happened next happened fast, and I only understood it afterward, so let me lay it out in order.

The silk drank. That was its function — Sef had warned me. It fed on what its captive most feared losing. And Ossifrage, last familiar of the royal aviary, keeper of the queen's last confidence, feared losing exactly one thing: the memory he guarded. The true name, humming in my skull, which he had sworn to protect and had failed to reclaim from me.

The silk drank *toward* it. And a drinking-cord, pulling hard against the will of a creature who had spent ninety years refusing to give up a queen's secret, met something it had not been built to handle.

The wire snapped. The silk burst apart in a spray of gray ash, like a lie caught in daylight. And Ossifrage — ragged, bleeding, one wing hanging wrong — launched himself off the stone and into the air with a cry that was half triumph and half agony.

Vesper moved faster than a grandmother has any right to move. Her hand came up, fingers crooked, and I felt the Choir's craft reach out — that cold subtraction, that gentle erasure — grasping for the bird mid-flight.

So I did the only thing I know how to do.

I stole.

Not from her — I'm not suicidal. From the stones. Nine standing rocks full of every word ever spoken between them, humming with centuries of ward-song and oath and argument, and I reached out with the oldest trick in my book, the one I'd learned at fifteen with shaking hands in a nobleman's bedroom, and I *pulled*. I ripped a fistful of the stones' memory loose — a century of accumulated echo, raw and undifferentiated — and I threw it into the space between Vesper's reaching hand and Ossifrage's fleeing body.

Sound detonated. Every stone screamed at once with a hundred years of everything, and Vesper flinched — flinched, the great spider, the merciful executioner, flinched like any person does when the past shouts at her — and in that flinch, Ossifrage was gone. Up, over the rim of the moor, a black smear against the gray sky, flying crooked but flying.

Gone.

* * *

We ran. Of course we ran. Sef grabbed my wrist and hauled me downslope while the echo-storm still roared behind us, and we didn't stop until the Stones were a mile behind and the light was failing and my lungs felt like borrowed goods.

We collapsed in the lee of a boulder, gasping. For a while neither of us spoke.

"She'll come for us now," Sef said finally. "No more invitations. No more webs. She's done being subtle."

"I know."

"You threw a hundred years of memory at her bare-handed. Do you have any idea what that could have—" He stopped. Looked at me properly. "Lena. What did it cost?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. I took inventory, quick and clinical, the way you check your pockets after a cutpurse brushes past. My sister's face — still there, thank the dark. Honey — still tasteless, still missing, old loss. The name of the river—

Gone. The river's name, the one I'd robbed along in my first year, the one I'd been fond of. Gone, smooth and clean, a gap with no edges.

Small thefts. Still small. Still mine to notice.

"A little," I said. "Nothing that matters."

"Liar," Sef said, without heat. He dug into his pack and brought out the ledger — his ledger, the one he kept of me, pages dense with his cramped careful handwriting — and made a note by feel in the fading light. I didn't ask what he wrote. I was afraid I knew.

Above us, far off, a black speck wheeled once against the last of the light. Crooked flight. Still flying. Heading south, toward the drowned city, toward the Door — or maybe just away, anywhere away, a wounded thing making for cover.

"Hold on, you ridiculous bird," I murmured. "Hold on, and get there."

Sef touched my shoulder. "Lena. What she offered you — the easy ending. Did you believe her? Even for a second?"

I thought about lying to him. We had a whole history of it, recently amended.

"For three seconds," I said. "Maybe four."

"And now?"

"Now?" I watched the speck vanish beyond the ridge, and I felt the gaps in me like missing teeth, and I thought of Brannoc's blank cairn and the mother's wedding day and a queen who died whispering her country's name into a single memory because she trusted *one creature* to keep it. "Now I'm done counting what I've lost. Now I want to know what she's so afraid I'll say."

Sef was quiet a moment. Then: "There's something you should know. About the Door. About why she's truly afraid." He hesitated, and I saw him weigh it, and I saw him decide — the new Sef, the one who checked his arithmetic. "It's about blood. Mine. And we need to talk tonight, because tomorrow we reach Cael Morath, and after tomorrow there won't be time for truths."

The wind came down off the moor, and in it, faint and broken, the wards sang their half-finished song.

"Then start talking," I said.
