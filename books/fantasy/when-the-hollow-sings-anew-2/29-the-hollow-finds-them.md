## 29. The Hollow Finds Them

### Lena

I have been chased before. I want that on the record, in some celestial ledger where thieves' accomplishments are noted with the appropriate grudging respect: Lena Underhill has been chased by dogs, by wardens, by a tax collector with an unreasonable crossbow arm and a personal grudge. I know what pursuit feels like — the hot breath of it, the drumbeat of it.

This was not pursuit. This was *arrival*.

The Hollow did not come at us the way a hunter comes. It came the way weather comes, or grief, or dawn — inevitable, total, indifferent to anything you might prefer. One moment Sef and I were standing in the dark before the First Door, our breath still ragged from the argument, my hand still stinging from where I'd slapped him hard enough to leave a mark I intended to feel guilty about later. The next moment, every sound in the world stopped.

Not quiet. Quiet is a thing with edges. This was the absence of sound's very possibility — as if someone had reached into the world and taken out not the noise, but the capacity for noise. My heartbeat went on. My breathing went on. But they went on inside me only, sealed off, like a candle burning under glass while the whole night outside forgets what fire was.

Then it showed itself.

I don't have words that fit, so I'll use the wrong ones and let you do the arithmetic. It was vast the way a cathedral is vast and the way a debt is vast. It stood over the valley below the First Door — or rather, it didn't stand, it simply occupied, the way fog occupies, except this fog had *shape*, and the shape was wrong in the specific way that a face is wrong when the eyes are painted on. And across that vastness, everywhere at once, were faces.

Faces of everyone it had eaten.

The first one I recognized was Brannoc. Of course it was. The Hollow knows exactly which knife to hold up to the light.

His face floated on its surface like a lamp under ice — big, craggy, half-smiling the way he always was, the smile of a man who'd made peace with the world's terms years ago and found them negotiable anyway. His lips moved. No sound came out, because sound was not currently a thing the world offered, but I read his mouth easily enough, because I'd watched him say these exact words a hundred times around campfires:

*Passage costs passage, little thief.*

My legs wanted to run. That's the animal truth of it; everything above my knees voted to flee and everything above my waist couldn't move. Because behind Brannoc's face there were others. A woman with a burn-scarred jaw from Ashfall. A warden I'd robbed in Thornfell city whose name I never learned. Children. Dozens of children, their faces small and bright on that enormous dark, like stars that had been peeled off the sky and worn.

And I understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only at the worst possible moment, that the Hollow wasn't showing me these faces as a threat.

It was showing them as a *menu*.

The silence pressed against me like a palm against a window. Then, inside the silence, something spoke — not in my ears but in the place where memories live, which meant it could speak without breaking its own rule about no sound, because it was speaking directly into the vault.

*Lena Underhill.* Not a voice. An impression of one, the way a footprint is an impression of a foot. *You keep things.*

"I return them," I said. My voice sounded absurdly small, a coin dropped in a cathedral. "That's the rule. I never keep—"

*You kept.* The impression rippled, almost amused. Almost. If hunger can be amused. *A kiss from Lord Aldous Verren, aged nineteen, orchard wall, the girl with the crooked tooth. You returned a copy. You kept the original. Thieves lie to themselves best of all.*

Heat climbed my throat. It was true, and worse than true — I hadn't just kept it, I'd visited it. On bad roads, in cold camps, I'd opened that memory like a jar of summer and breathed it in. First kisses weren't mine to hoard. I knew that. I'd known it and done it anyway, because loneliness makes excellent arguments and terrible bookkeepers.

"Fine," I said. "Guilty. Add it to the pile."

*I intend to.*

And then it gave me back the orchard.

There was no transition. One moment I stood in the dark before the First Door; the next I was seventeen again, or rather I was standing inside a borrowed nineteen-year-old's chest, tasting cider and nerves, watching the girl with the crooked tooth lean in with her eyes half-closed and her courage visibly failing her twice before it held. The bark of the wall rough under my palms. The smell of late apples going soft in the grass. Her breath hitching — that hitch, that ridiculous perfect hitch —

It cut off.

I staggered back into the present, gasping, my cheeks wet, my hands shaking. The Hollow hung there, patient as arithmetic.

*I can give them all back,* it said. *One by one. Every theft you ever returned. Every kindness you ever witnessed and filed away. Your sister's wedding. The taste of your mother's bread. The first time you understood what your gift was and wept because it felt like being given a key to other people's houses. I ate them all, Lena Underhill. Nothing is gone. Nothing is ever gone with me. I am the most honest keeper you will ever meet.*

"That's not keeping," I said, and my voice cracked on it. "That's hoarding."

*Is it?* The faces shifted. Brannoc drifted closer, or seemed to. *Your friend sold his daughter's lullaby to buy you road. I have it here. I can play it for you. Would you like to hear how she laughed at the end of it? He hasn't heard it in thirty years. I could give it back to you both.*

"Don't." The word came out raw. "Don't you dare wear him."

*I am not wearing him. I am what remains of him. There is a difference, and the difference is that he is mine now, and everything he loved is mine now, and you are standing in front of a door holding the last piece of Vael's soul in your skull, and I would like it back.*

There it was. All the seduction stripped away, the ledger laid open. It wanted the queen's name-memory. Everything else — the faces, the offers, the terrible generosity of it — was bait on the hook, and we both knew it, and it knew I knew, and it offered anyway, because the Hollow had learned something important in its long gray existence: people rarely refuse what they're starving for, even when they can see the hook.

Behind me, I heard Sef draw breath — the first sound either of us had managed since it arrived, which meant the Hollow was letting us, which meant even our voices were on loan.

"Lena," Sef said quietly. "Don't listen to the menu. Look at the restaurant."

"What?"

"It's showing you what it has," he said. "Ask it what it needs."

And oh, that was such a Sef thing to say — warder-brained, always reading the structure underneath the surface — and I hated how much steadier it made me feel, and I turned back to the vast dark wearing my dead friend's face, and I asked.

"What do you need?"

The silence went very still. Somewhere in its depths, a child's face blinked.

*Grief,* said the Hollow. *Unspoken. Unspent. I am what Vael could not say before it died. Every year the wards sang louder because the country was trying to tell someone it was hurting, and no one listened, and the listening ran out. I need the grief finished. I need the name spoken to someone who can hear it. Or I need to eat the last person who remembers, and then it will finally be quiet.*

"You'd rather be quiet than healed?"

*I would rather stop hurting,* said the Hollow, with the naked honesty of something too large to bother lying. *Wouldn't you?*

And the awful thing — the thing I will carry in whatever replaces my memories after tonight — is that I almost said yes.

* * *

### Sef

I have faced the Choir's discipline trials. I have knelt on stone for three days reciting oaths until the words wore grooves in me. I once held a bridge against six raiders with a cracked shield and a dislocated shoulder. None of it prepared me for watching the Hollow offer Lena their own life back, piece by piece, like a merchant laying out cloth.

Because that's what it was doing. I saw the pattern within minutes — I'm built for patterns, it's the one thing the Choir left sharp in me. It fed Lena the orchard kiss and watched them break. Then it offered the taste of honey-bread, and Lena's whole body leaned toward it like a plant toward light. Then their sister's laugh, and Lena made a sound I hope never to hear again: joy and grief fused into one note, the sound of getting something back that you already mourned.

Each gift cost nothing. Each gift was free. And each gift made the next refusal harder, because the Hollow was re-teaching Lena what they'd lost, one warm thing at a time, until refusing would mean losing it all over again — freshly, knowingly, in full color.

It was crueler than torture. Torture at least lets you hate it.

I should have been afraid for myself. Part of me catalogued that fact with distant interest — the part the Choir hollowed, the part I've spent months quietly rebuilding from smuggled scraps. The larger part of me was doing arithmetic I despised: if the Hollow took me now, mid-offering, it would break Lena's concentration, and Lena needed to reach the door. So I did the only tactically useful thing available to a man with no weapon that mattered.

I stepped between them.

The vastness regarded me. Faces turned across its surface like leaves on black water — and then, horribly, familiar ones surfaced. My training master at the Choir's cloister, old Mother Hesk, who taught me to bind wards with thread and patience. The recruit who'd died in my arms during the Cael Morath expedition, young Pellin, who'd joked about the drowned city's stars right up until the water took him. The Hollow wore them the way a soldier wears a coat taken from the dead.

*Sef,* it said, using Mother Hesk's mouth-shape. *Warder. Oathkeeper. You stand beside a thief who stole from your queen's vault and never told you until a crow forced it out of them. You stand beside the door your blood alone can open. Tell me, oathkeeper — what does duty say?*

"Duty says protect the realm," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "All of it. Including the parts you ate."

*The parts I ate ARE me. To restore them is to unmake yourself. You were made by the Choir to prevent restoration. You swore it.*

"I also swore an oath at seven years old to a country I can't remember," I said, "and I've recently learned that oath was stolen along with everything else. So forgive me if I'm reviewing my contracts."

Something moved through the Hollow — not laughter, nothing that alive, but a shift, an attention. And in that shift, I felt it probe me. Felt it slide against the place where my lineage sleeps, the royal blood the Choir stole the knowledge of, the blood I'd cut into the door's seam an hour ago and lied about. It tasted me the way the lock had tasted me.

And it smiled. I felt it smile. With my mother's face, which I have never seen, which it apparently has, because it eats everything and everyone, including — the realization landed like a stone dropped down a well — including perhaps the woman herself, wherever she fled to when the Choir took her son.

*Heirling,* it said softly, and the endearment in it was the cruelest thing yet, because it sounded genuine. *Little heir of a nameless land. You carry the key and the lock and the wound, all three. I could give her back to you. Your mother's face. Her voice singing the wards awake. I hold every moment of her you never got to have. Ask me.*

My knees nearly went. I'll write that honestly, whoever reads this — if anyone reads this — my knees nearly went, because there is a hole in me shaped like a mother and the Hollow fit itself into it perfectly, the way it fits into everyone, because it IS everyone's holes, it is the sum of everything Vael never got to say goodbye to.

"No," I said.

*Why not?*

"Because you're offering it to stop me," I said, "and my mother would rather stay eaten forever than be used as a leash on her son. I don't remember her. But I'm betting on her anyway."

The Hollow went still in a new way — a considering way. Behind me, Lena's hand found my sleeve. Didn't grip it. Just touched, the way you touch something to confirm it's still there.

"Sef," Lena whispered. "It's not going to stop asking, is it."

"No."

"And it's getting stronger the longer we stand here."

I extended my senses toward the door at our backs — the First Door, ancient and star-veined, drinking Sef's blood from its seam, waiting with the patience of stone for a name. The wards in the mountains, miles away, were screaming. I could feel them through my ward-sense like a dog feels thunder: the whole border coming apart, and the Hollow swelling with every scrap it swallowed, growing vaster, more patient, more generous.

"It feeds on the unfinished," I said. "Every minute we don't act, it gets more of the world to wear. Lena — it isn't here to kill us. Killing us ends the memory. It's here to *wait*. It can wait longer than we can breathe."

*True,* said the Hollow pleasantly, in Brannoc's mouth this time, and I flinched because Brannoc had died buying us hours and here it was spending him like small change. *I am very good at waiting. I waited for the queen to die. I waited for the vault to be robbed. I waited for a crow to gossip and a thief to grow a conscience. I can wait for you to choose. Everyone chooses eventually, heirling. Hunger sees to it.*

It began, then, the final cruelty. It didn't attack. It *settled* — spread itself across the valley like a second night, folded its thousand faces closed like hands folding, and became simply present. A pressure. A patience. The temperature of grief.

And into the silence it dropped gifts like coins into a well. For Lena: the smell of their childhood kitchen. For me: a fragment of lullaby in a voice I'd never heard and recognized instantly, blood knowing blood. For Lena: their sister saying *you're my favorite disaster*. For me: the queen's hand on a small boy's hair.

For both of us, together, unbidden, unprompted, devastating:

A market day in Vael's capital. Sunlight. Two children — a boy in warder's grey, a thief-child with quick fingers and quicker grin — colliding over a fallen apple, laughing, neither knowing yet what they'd become to each other. The Hollow played it once, gently, and then withdrew it, and the absence rang like a struck bell.

"That one was ours," Lena said. Their voice was wrecked. "We didn't steal that one. We lived it. You don't get to — that one wasn't *food*—"

*Everything loved is food eventually,* said the Hollow, almost tenderly. *Unless it is finished. Unless it is spoken. Unless someone stands at a door and pays the whole price instead of bargaining with me for pieces. Choose, thief. Choose, heirling. The stars you want back are in my belly, and I give nothing freely — I only lend.*

The pressure of it settled over us like snowfall that intends to continue until spring never comes. Against my sleeve, Lena's hand curled into a fist.

And I watched them turn — slowly, terribly, beautifully — to look at the First Door. I watched them understand what I'd understood in the river-wood and the burning camp and every hard mile between: that the Hollow was right about one thing. There would be no fighting it. There would be no outrunning it. There was only the price, and the paying, and the question of who had the courage to pay it whole.

"Lena," I started. "Whatever you're thinking—"

They looked at me. And their expression was the expression of a thief standing in front of the greatest vault in creation, calculating not what they could take from it, but what they could afford to put in.

"I'm thinking," they said, "that I finally know what I stole from myself."

The Hollow leaned in — all of it, every face, every grief, hungry and hopeful and waiting — to hear the answer.
