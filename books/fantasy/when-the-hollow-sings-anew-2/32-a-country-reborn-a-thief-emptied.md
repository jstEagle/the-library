## 32. A Country Reborn, A Thief Emptied

The door opened, and the world poured through it like water through a broken dam.

I don't remember falling. I remember light — not the gray nothing of the Hollow's country, but real light, gold and rude and everywhere, the kind that makes you squint and swear at it. I remember sound: a rushing, a roaring, a great low hum that started somewhere below my boots and climbed up through my teeth until my whole skull rang like a struck bell.

And then stars.

They came back all at once, the way a held breath comes back — thousands upon thousands of them wheeling overhead in their old bright courses, as if they'd never left, as if the sky had only been pretending to be empty for our benefit. The wards sang along the ridgelines, high and clear, threads of silver-blue fire stitching themselves from mountain to mountain faster than I could follow. I knew what they were. I knew what everything was, in that first raw moment. That was the last time I would.

Because then the door closed behind us, and I looked at Sef — at his bleeding palm, at his face, which was doing something complicated and wet and wonderful — and I felt absolutely nothing.

Not grief. Not joy. Not recognition.

Nothing.

* * *

Here is what I understood, standing on the threshold stone with my hands empty and my head emptier:

Vael had come back. I could see it happening, could feel it like heat off a forge. The black wound where a country used to be was filling in — hills first, then rivers, then roads threading across the land like veins remembering their route to the heart. Somewhere far off, a city assembled itself out of nothing, tower by tower, bell by bell, and when the bells finished existing they began to ring, all of them at once, a sound like the sky clearing its throat after a long illness.

Songs came next. That's the part people won't believe when I tell it later. The songs came *before* the singers — rising up out of the fields and the gutters and the mouths of open windows, half-remembered melodies completing themselves mid-note. Harvest songs. Lullabies. A drinking song so bawdy that even I, knowing nothing, felt my ears go warm on principle.

A kingdom un-erasing itself. Every stolen name returning to every stolen thing.

And in the middle of it, me. Lena Underhill, memory-thief, name-bearer, the person who had carried a whole country inside their skull for months without knowing it. I had done the thing. Spoken the word. Paid the price.

I just didn't remember any of it.

* * *

"Lena." Sef said my name and caught my elbow, and I want you to understand how strange that was — how he said it like it meant something enormous, like the two syllables were worth more than the kingdom reassembling itself behind him, and I stood there looking at his hand on my arm thinking, *who is this man, and why does he look at me like I'm a miracle?*

"You did it," he said. His voice cracked down the middle. "You spoke the name. Lena, you—"

"I did?" My voice came out flat and small. "Then why can't I remember it?"

He went very still. It's a particular kind of stillness, the kind I'd learn to read later the way you read weather. In that moment I didn't know him well enough to know that stillness meant fear.

"What do you remember," he said carefully.

I cast around inside myself the way you pat your pockets after leaving a tavern, hoping the coin purse will turn up. "Thornfell," I said slowly. "There's a pass. There's a man called Brannoc who — no. No, there's just the word Brannoc, like a label on an empty jar." I kept digging. "Crows. There's supposed to be a crow. Ashfall? Something burned." Nothing. Nothing but shapes without substance, rooms swept bare, doors opening onto other doors opening onto nothing. "Sef," I said, and my own voice frightened me, because it sounded the way a stranger sounds asking directions. "Why do I know your name?"

He didn't answer right away. Above us the stars turned in their new-old circles, indifferent and gorgeous. Down the slope behind the threshold stone, the First Door was already folding itself back into the hillside, its work done, taking with it whatever evidence remained that anything had ever been missing.

"I loved you," Sef said finally. Not *do*. Past tense, or maybe he thought past tense was safer. "That's — that's the shortest version."

"Was I good at it?" I asked. "Loving you?"

Something moved across his face, quick and pained, gone before I could name it — except I couldn't have named it anyway, could I? I didn't know his face. I didn't know any faces. I was a stranger wearing my own skin, standing in a country that owed me everything and knew it, and I couldn't remember one single reason why.

"You were terrible at it," he said, and his voice broke again, differently this time. "You were the worst. You stole my hat the day we met and you never gave it back."

"That doesn't sound like something worth dying for."

"It wasn't dying," he said. "It was paying. And yes. Yes, it was worth it."

* * *

They came for us before dawn — not soldiers exactly, though some wore swords, and not courtiers exactly, though some wore silk, and every last one of them weeping.

That's what I couldn't get over, in those first hours inside returned Vael. The weeping. Grown men and women in fine coats dropping to their knees in the mud of the palace road, sobbing like children at the sight of me — *me*, mud-spattered, hollow-eyed, carrying nothing now but hunger and a headache like the inside of a bell. An old woman pressed her forehead to my boot and said words I couldn't parse in a language that made my bones ache with familiarity and nothing else. A young guard took off his helmet and just stood there crying, thanking me by title after title, most of which I've since learned were invented on the spot, because nobody knew quite what to call the person who had given a country back and forgotten to keep the receipt.

"Name-bearer," someone said.

"Queen's thief," said someone else, and a murmur ran through the crowd like wind through wheat, because apparently that was true too, apparently I had robbed the royal vault years ago without ever knowing what I'd taken, and fate or luck or the queen's own clever ghost had decided that the best place to hide a kingdom's name was in the head of a girl who forgot everything she stole.

Except I hadn't forgotten this. I'd *given* it. That distinction mattered to everyone around me enormously, and to me not at all, because either way the vault between my ears stood empty, and I was tired, and I did not know a single soul in the entire shining country that had risen from my sacrifice like morning from night.

Sef stayed close. He'd explain things when the crowds overwhelmed me — who the old woman was (the queen's archivist, returned with everything except her archive), why the guards wept (they remembered dying, in the sense that they remembered a hole where their deaths should be), what the bells were singing (the Coronation Anthem, which hadn't been sung in living memory because living memory had been erased). He was patient and thorough and kind, and every kindness landed on me like rain on glass.

At some point he tried to tell me about a crow.

"There was a crow," he said. "Ossifrage. Last familiar of the royal aviary. Terrible creature, bit everyone, quoted proverbs wrong on purpose. He recognized the name-memory in you before anyone. He should be here somewhere —" and he scanned the ruined-and-restored courtyards for a bird I could not remember meeting, and found nothing, and I watched hope flicker and gutter in his face like a lamp running dry.

"Sef," I said, when he'd gone quiet. "Stop."

He stopped.

"You keep handing me pieces of myself like you're dealing cards," I said. "A crow. A hat. A road through the Lethe-wood where you nearly drowned in yourself. I can hear that these things are important to you. I can't feel that they're important to me. And every time you say one, you look worse, like each word costs you blood." I made myself hold his gaze. It was hard. His eyes were the kind of brown that expects things. "So stop. Please. Tell the historians instead. They'll write it all down properly, and maybe someday I'll read it like a story about somebody else."

"Lena—"

"Somebody else," I repeated, and heard how cold it was, and couldn't help it. "That's who did the brave thing, Sef. Whoever she was. I hope she was happy. I hope it was worth it to her. But she isn't here, and I am, and I'm so tired of being thanked for a gift I don't remember giving."

* * *

They put me in a room in the palace that night — my room, apparently, or a room they'd decided was mine, hung with curtains in colors that meant nothing to me and furnished with a bed that smelled of cedar and someone else's life. Through the window, Vael glittered under its returned stars: rooftops and watchfires and the silver thread of the wards singing along the hills, everything humming with existence, everything humming at a frequency just below hearing, everything except me.

I sat on the edge of the bed and took inventory, the way a thief takes inventory. It's habit. It's the only habit I have left.

Possessions: one knife, dulled. One coat, burnt at the hem. Boots, mine, apparently, though the scuffs told stories I couldn't read.

Memories: Thornfell, faint as breath on glass. The word *Brannoc*, heavy and unexplained. The word *Ossifrage*. The word *Ashfall*. A sister — I knew I had a sister, the knowledge sat in me like a splinter, but her face was a smear of paint, her voice a language I'd never learned.

Debts: one kingdom, paid in full by parties unknown.

I lay back and stared at the ceiling and did not cry, mostly because crying requires feeling something first and I had checked, thoroughly, and found the shelves bare. This was the victory, I thought. This was what it had bought: stars over everyone's heads but mine, songs in every throat but mine, a country reborn and a thief emptied, standing in the middle of her own triumph like a hired guest at someone else's wedding.

Somewhere below the window, the bells began again. The Coronation Anthem. People were singing it badly and loudly and with their whole hearts, and I lay there listening, trying to catch the melody, trying to make my own mouth shape the words, and failing, failing, failing.

A knock. Soft. Two taps, then a pause, then one more — a rhythm, I realized, a little pattern, like a code, like something someone had done before at this same door on other nights I couldn't recall.

I didn't answer. The door opened anyway.

Sef stood in the lamplight holding something against his chest — a book, bound in gray leather, worn soft at the corners from handling. He looked like a man arriving at his own execution and finding it canceled and being disappointed about it, if that makes sense. It probably doesn't. Nothing makes much sense tonight.

"You said tell the historians," he said. "I'm not a historian. But I kept records."

He held the book out to me. On the cover, in handwriting I didn't know and that seemed, somehow, to know me, were three words:

*Ledger of Lena.*

"I don't want it," I said, which was a lie, or would become one, or had always been one — I genuinely couldn't tell anymore, and neither, I think, could he.

"No," he agreed quietly. "But you'll want to know who you were. And I'm the only one left who was paying attention." He set the ledger on the table beside the bed, straightened it so its spine faced the door, and paused there with his hand flat on the cover, not quite letting go. "Get some sleep, Lena. Tomorrow we start putting you back."

"We?"

"You didn't think I walked out of the Hollow's country just to watch you wander around inside your own life lost," he said. "I know every road you ever traveled, Underhill. I'm going to walk you down all of them again."

He closed the door softly behind him. I picked up the ledger. I opened it to the first page, and in the dark, by starlight that I owed everything for and recognized nothing of, I read the very first line written about myself, in a stranger's careful hand:

*Lena steals memories and keeps none. Watch the hands, not the eyes. She thinks nobody notices when she's lonely. Everybody notices.*

I read it twice. Three times.

Nothing came back. Nothing stirred.

But somewhere under the emptiness — deep, deep down, in the flooded cellar of me where the name had lived and died and bought a world — something turned over in its sleep.

I put out the lamp and did not sleep at all.
