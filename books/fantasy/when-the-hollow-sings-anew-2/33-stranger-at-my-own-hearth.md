## 33. Stranger at My Own Hearth

The first night, Lena tried to leave.

I found them at the door of my quarters in the restored palace — quarters they had never seen, in a palace that had not existed a month ago, wearing borrowed clothes and an expression I recognized the way you recognize a scar on your own hand. That flat, polite blankness. The look of someone calculating the exits.

"The bed's more comfortable than the road," I said. "That's all this is. You don't owe me anything."

"I know." They said it gently, which was worse. Lena Underhill had been many things to me over the years — infuriating, brilliant, impossible — but they had never once been gentle about owing me nothing. "It's just that I don't know why I'm here."

"You're here because Vael came back and you made it come back, even if you don't remember doing it. Because half the court would build you a shrine if you stood still long enough. And because—" I stopped myself. Old habit, keeping things behind my teeth. The Choir had trained me well; it turned out the training outlived the Choir. "Because you need somewhere to be while you figure out who you are. And so do I. We can be strangers together. It's efficient."

Something flickered across their face. Not recognition — I'd have known recognition. It was more like the shape recognition leaves behind, the dent in a cushion after you get up.

"Strangers," they repeated, tasting the word. Then, with a ghost of the old dryness: "You always talk this much?"

"Worse," I said. "Ask anyone."

They didn't ask anyone. They didn't know anyone to ask.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about grief when it arrives sideways: it doesn't feel like loss. Loss has edges. This was worse — it was grief with the wound removed, mourning without a grave, loving someone who was standing right in front of me, breathing, alive, and gone.

Lena had poured everything into the First Door. Every kept memory, every stolen year, every warm thing they'd hoarded against the cold like a dragon hoarding candle-ends. The kingdom came back. Stars, wards, songs, rivers remembering their courses. And the person standing in the wreckage of their own skull was — I want to say *nobody*, but that's a lie and Lena deserves better lies than most. They were somebody new. Somebody with the same hands, the same crooked smile waiting for material, the same instinct to check a room for exits before checking it for friends.

They were somebody who didn't know me.

And underneath all of it, underneath the grief like groundwater seeping through everything, there ran a thread of something I couldn't name yet. Something that looked at the empty space where Lena used to keep themselves and thought: *I can fill some of this. Not all. But some.*

The ledger helped. The ledger was the whole reason any of this worked at all.

* * *

"You wrote this?" Lena asked, turning the small book over in their hands. It was the third one. I had filled two already since Vael's return — cheap paper, bought from the market stalls that had reappeared along with the city itself, as if the shops remembered where they'd been even if the people didn't.

"Every night," I said. "Since Ashfall."

"Why?"

Because I loved you. Because you were forgetting yourself in pieces and someone had to hold the shape of you. Because when you sold three of your warmest memories for a stranger's wedding day I watched you do it and said nothing and I have been trying to make up for that silence ever since. Because—

"Because you kept forgetting things," I said. "Your sister's face. The taste of honey. What Brannoc sounded like when he laughed. I thought—"

I stopped. Lena waited. They were good at waiting now; patience is one of the few things amnesia gives you free.

"I thought someone should remember you," I finished, "even if you couldn't."

Lena looked down at the ledger. Their thumb moved over the cover the way it used to move over lockpicks — absent, assessing, tender with tools.

"Read me some," they said.

* * *

I started at the beginning, because beginnings seemed kindest.

*"Day one. Subject: Lena Underhill, memory-thief, professional liar, terrible cook. Observed eating an entire honey-cake in four bites while claiming not to be hungry. Subject claims to have no interest in helping anyone. Subject then spent two hours teaching a refugee child to pick locks 'for educational purposes only.'"*

"That doesn't sound like me," Lena said.

"It sounds exactly like you."

"But I don't remember it."

"No," I agreed. "But it happened. That's the point of the book."

* * *

We fell into a rhythm. Nights, mostly, because nights were hardest. Days were manageable — there was work, there were court functions Lena dodged with an instinct so deeply buried it had survived the Hollow itself, there was the slow strange business of a country un-learning its own erasure. But nights were when the gaps got loud.

So every evening I would light the lamp, and Lena would sit in the chair by the window — always the same chair, they'd claimed it the second week without knowing why, and I never told them it was the chair they'd sat in the first night, exhausted, and refused to give up — and I would read them themselves.

Some entries were small. *"Subject insists crows cannot be trusted. Subject may be right."*

Some were harder. *"Brannoc died today. He died saying Lena's real name, which Lena did not know they'd lost. Lena does not know yet that he called them by it. I have not told them. I will tell them someday, when it will hurt less. Or I won't. I haven't decided which is kinder."*

Lena went very still when I read that one.

"He knew my name," they said quietly. "My real one?"

"Before the door took it, yes. He gave it back to you with his last breath. You carried it right up until you spent it."

"Spent it on what?"

"On us," I said. "On everyone. On the door."

Lena was silent a long time. When they spoke again their voice was steady, which I recognized as effort, which meant it was costing them, which meant somewhere under all that blankness the old Lena was still in there — the one who paid for other people's griefs with a joke and a straight spine.

"Well," they said. "Tell me his laugh sometime. If you remember it."

"I remember everything about him," I said. "He'd have hated that. He'd have found it funny too."

"Yes," Lena said, and then frowned, because they had no idea how they knew that. "Yes, somehow. He would have."

* * *

There were bad nights.

One in particular. Rain against the windows, the kind of autumn storm that made the new-old wards sing faintly in the stones, and Lena sitting in the window chair looking smaller than I'd ever seen them — smaller than the day we met, smaller than the day they'd sold their sister's face away without blinking.

"How much of me is left?" they asked. No preamble. Just the question, laid open between us like a gutted fish.

I put down the ledger.

"Lena—"

"Don't. Don't soften it. I'm a thief, apparently. Thieves appreciate honest accounting." Their hands were shaking. They hid it by gripping the arms of the chair. "How much?"

I thought about lying. God help me, I thought about it seriously. I could feel the lie sitting right there, ready-made: *most of you, nearly all, just the edges.* And then I thought about what they'd said — *thieves appreciate honest accounting* — and I understood that the person asking me wasn't really asking about percentages. They were asking whether they were worth staying for. Whether the stranger in the chair was worth the trouble of the man reading to her.

"Less than half," I said. "Of the memories. Of the *recorded* life, if you want to be technical. But that's not the same as how much of *you* is left, and I'm not going to let you confuse the two, because I've watched you for weeks now and I can tell you exactly what survived."

"What?"

"You flinch when people raise their voices, but you step *toward* danger. You steal bread from the kitchen and leave coins, and you think no one notices the coins. You sit facing doors. You can't sleep unless you know where I am in the house — you pretend it's habit, but I've watched you relax when you hear me moving around downstairs. You hate being thanked. You love being trusted. And every single time I read you something kind that you did and don't remember, you get this expression—" I tapped the corner of my own mouth, "—right there. Like the kindness surprised you. Like you'd caught yourself doing something good and were embarrassed to be proud of it."

Lena stared at me. The rain filled the silence.

"That's not memories," they said finally. "That's just... me?"

"That's just you," I said. "Memories are the record. You are the handwriting."

They cried then. Quietly, furiously, the way people cry when they've forgotten how — badly, in gasps, furious at the tears for existing. I didn't touch them. I wanted to. Instead I picked the ledger back up and read, because reading was the thing I could do, the one skill I had that mattered:

*"Day nineteen. Subject gave away their boots to a woman with frostbitten feet and then complained for three days about cold toes rather than admit generosity. Subject's complaints were, per usual, extremely entertaining."*

And through the tears, wet and cracked and unmistakable, Lena laughed.

* * *

Ossifrage came back in the fourth week, flying crooked, one wing still healing from whatever Vesper had done to him before the end. He landed on the windowsill of Lena's room like he owned the deed, fixed me with one glittering eye, and said:

"You're still reading to them. Good. Crows remember everything, warder. Someone ought to remind you humans of your duties."

"Come to mock me?" Lena asked from the chair. They'd taken to the crow faster than to any human — perhaps because Ossifrage demanded nothing, expected nothing, simply *was*, and treated Lena's blankness as irrelevant rather than tragic.

"Crows don't mock," Ossifrage said. "Crows *assess*. And my assessment is that you've gotten thinner and the warder reads to you like a priest at a deathbed instead of a friend at a fire, which is inefficient. Ask him about the ferry."

"The ferry?" Lena glanced at me.

"There was no ferry," I said quickly.

"There was *absolutely* a ferry," Ossifrage said with relish. "River crossing, day eleven or twelve. The warder here haggled with the ferryman for an hour over two coppers, lost the argument, and then Lena stole the ferryman's memory of the price and made him believe he'd already been paid. Teamwork. Beautiful to watch. The ferrymen of three kingdoms still tell stories."

"You *what?*" Lena turned to me, and there it was — the grin, the actual true grin, surfacing through the blankness like a fish rising. "You lectured me about honesty. I *know* you did. I don't remember it but I know it, you have a face for lecturing."

"I contain multitudes," I said.

"You contain criminal tendencies and a lecture-face." They were laughing now, really laughing, and I sat there holding the ledger and feeling something crack open in my chest that I didn't dare examine, because hope is a currency I'd learned not to carry — but gods, the weight of it, the warmth.

"Read another one," Lena said, wiping their eyes. "A good one. Apparently I was entertaining. I'd like to meet her."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the lamp had burned low and Lena had finally gone to sleep in the bed they'd grudgingly admitted was more comfortable than anywhere else — I sat at the desk with a fresh page and the pen hovering.

This was the part nobody saw. The nightly entry, written after the reading, when the house was dark and honest.

*I read to them again tonight,* I wrote. *They laughed at the ferry story. Ossifrage told it before I could stop him. She laughed like she used to — no. Not like she used to. Like she does now. I keep making that mistake, comparing. There is no "used to." There is only her, whichever version wakes up tomorrow.*

*She asked how much of her is left. I told her the truth. I think it was the right call. She cried. She also laughed. Both counts I'm calling victories.*

*Trouble: the court physicians want to examine her. Ilvane's notes suggest memories poured into the First Door aren't destroyed but transformed — the scholar-ghost used the word "chorus" again, which I don't like, because I heard singing on the wind last week and I haven't told anyone. Haven't told her either. She has enough ghosts.*

*Trouble, larger: Mother Vesper's body was never found. The Choir is scattered but scattered isn't gone. And there are whispers — servants' whispers, market whispers — of gray-robed figures at the edges of the rebuilt wards, standing very still, listening.*

*Also: sometimes at night I hear the wards humming a melody I almost recognize, and I catch myself humming the missing line without knowing where I learned it. I should write that down properly. I will. Tomorrow.*

*Personal note, not for reading aloud: she checked for me tonight before sleeping. Third time this week. She thinks I don't notice. She relaxed when she heard me on the stairs.*

*Whatever she was before the door, whoever comes back, whoever doesn't — I'm going to be here. That's not duty. The Choir taught me duty and I burned that oath in front of witnesses. This is the other thing. The thing without a name in the ledger.*

*I'll find the word eventually.*

I set down the pen. Downstairs, a floorboard creaked — Lena, restless, checking the house, finding me where I always was — and settled.

Outside the window, far off toward Thornfell, the wards hummed their almost-familiar song. And beneath it, so faint I might have imagined it, something else answered: a second voice, threading through the melody like a hand slipping into a hand.

I told myself it was the wind.

I wrote it down anyway.
