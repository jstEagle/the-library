## 34. One Memory Kept

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that no one teaches you in the Choir's arithmetic lessons, which is to say there is no such thing as Choir arithmetic lessons, because the Choir does not believe in numbers that don't serve an immediate purpose. Numbers exist to count heads, count losses, count how many villages have gone quiet on the eastern road. I learned real arithmetic from a thief.

Lena Underhill taught me that everything has a price, and that pretending it doesn't is just theft with better manners. They taught me it over smuggler's fires and at fords where we argued about whose turn it was to stand watch, and they taught me it most thoroughly the night I watched them pour every warm thing they owned into a door so that a country could exist again.

And then I did the worst, kindest thing I have ever done. I cheated them out of one memory.

I have been carrying the guilt of it for three weeks now, folded up small behind my ribs like a coin you're not sure you're allowed to spend. Every night I sit by their bedside in the restored palace of Vael and read them their own ledger, and every night I think: tonight I'll tell them. And every night I look at that blank, patient face — patient the way weather is patient, the way stone under water is patient — and I think: not yet. One more night. One more entry. Let them learn to laugh again before I hand them the bill.

Here is what happened at the door. Here is what only I know, because Ossifrage was bleeding in the corner and the Hollow had already turned its thousand borrowed faces away, and because dead men keep secrets well and I was very nearly one.

* * *

The First Door stood in the dark like the idea of a door rather than a door itself — a tall seam of pale light in a wall of nothing, humming with the queen's old wards, waiting with the terrible patience of things that have waited three hundred years. To open it wanted two payments: blood of her line, and a name spoken true. But doors are greedy the way all thresholds are greedy, and this one had been starved for centuries. It didn't want a sip. It wanted a flood. It wanted everything warm the speaker carried — every memory that made them *them*, poured out and drunk down, so that what walked through would be new-made and empty and could carry Vael's name without the weight of anything else.

I knew that part. I'd known since the library-that-wasn't, since Ilvane's ghost explained it in her dry scholar's voice while Lena took notes on paper that kept trying to forget it was paper. What walks through must be unburdened. The lock eats the self to make room for the country.

I told myself I had accepted it. I told myself that when Lena knelt before the seam of light and began — gods, the sound of it, like watching someone bleed out from the inside — I told myself I had made my peace. Their memories went out of them in a long silver thread, and I saw each one flicker past: their sister's face, honey on bread, the Thornfell pass at dawn, Brannoc laughing, the nobleman's stolen first kiss that started this whole disaster, and then — and this is the part that broke me even though I thought I had no more breaking left in me — *me*. Our whole history, going into that light. The day they lifted a memory from me on a dare and apologized for a week. The night in the Lethe-wood when they anchored me to myself with a story about rain. Every small ordinary hour, feeding a door.

And the door drank, and Lena knelt there getting lighter and lighter, and I understood that when this was finished I would stand next to a stranger who had once been the person I loved best in any world, and they would not know me, and Vael would be saved, and everyone would call it a victory.

So I did what thieves do. I made a substitution.

It wasn't planned. I want that on the record, if there is a record. I didn't stand there scheming like some villain in a ballad. It happened the way a hand happens — you reach, and it's done. As the last of their memories rose toward the seam — the oldest one, the deepest one, the first time they ever saw me, a skinny warder's apprentice dropping an armful of ledgers in a palace corridor while a thief in a borrowed uniform laughed at him and picked the ledger off the floor and handed it back and said, *you dropped your whole life, warder* — I caught it.

Not with my hands. You can't catch a memory with your hands, though I swear to this day my palms still ache like I did. I caught it the way the Choir taught me to catch things: the Gray Choir's whole art is handling memory that isn't yours, cupping it without drinking, carrying it intact. All those years I hated them for hollowing me, and it turns out they taught me the one skill that mattered. I cupped that memory — our first meeting, bright and small and ridiculous, smelling of ink and dust and Lena's crooked grin — and I pulled one of mine out of my own chest in exchange, and I fed the door *mine* instead.

The door didn't notice. Doors are hungry, not discerning. It took my memory of my mother singing — the one thing I had left of her, the song she hummed over the cradle, gone now into a lock forever — and it never knew the difference.

The memory of us sat in my hands like a live coal. And then, because I am apparently the sort of man who commits one crime and immediately plans the cover-up, I pressed it deep, deep down, beneath the oath-scars and the Choir's old hollowing, into a pocket of myself so far under that even I couldn't feel it anymore. I buried our first meeting inside me the way you bury a seed, and I told myself I didn't know why, and I lied.

Then Lena spoke the name. Then the world came back.

* * *

Three weeks later, and here is my nightly ritual, and I perform it with the solemnity of a priest because that's what it is:

"Entry forty-one," I read, holding the little book close to the candle. "Today Lena Underhill ate an entire pear and declared it the finest pear in the history of fruit. When informed that pears are not historically interesting, they said, 'Everything is historical if you're the first person to taste it after the end of the world.' The gardener agrees with them. The gardener is wrong."

Lena lies propped against the pillows in the guest chamber that someone decided should be theirs, watching me with those eyes that are polite and curious and utterly vacant of me. "Do I talk like that?"

"You talk exactly like that. It's exhausting."

"It sounds exhausting." A pause. The candle gutters. "Sef."

"Yes."

"Do I ever remember things? Little ones? Not — not the big stuff. Little ones."

I set down the ledger. This is the question. This is the question every night, wearing different clothes. Do the gaps fill in. Will I wake up knowing your face. Will I know why you look at me like I'm something precious that got broken.

"The healers say the door took what it took," I say, which is true. "They say memory doesn't grow back like a limb. But—" and here I give them the honest half, because they deserve honesty even if they can't recognize it as payment owed— "but you're making new ones. Fast. Faster than anyone expected. Whatever was taken, whatever's missing, you're building around it. You built a whole personality around liking pears in three weeks."

That gets the almost-smile. The almost-smile is not their old smile but it lives in the same house, uses the same furniture. I have learned to love its lodger.

"I wish I remembered you," Lena says suddenly, plainly, the way they say everything now — no armor, because armor is made of memories and theirs were spent. "Everyone keeps telling me stories. The crow yells at me like I owe it money. The court treats me like a saint who fell down some stairs. And you—" they gesture at me, frustrated, gentle, "you look at me like I'm a letter you keep reading. I wish I knew what it says."

I look at the ledger. Entry forty-one. Pear.

"Maybe someday," I say, and my voice does something I don't authorize, goes rough at the edges. "Maybe the words wear grooves. Maybe if I read enough of you back into yourself, some of it takes."

"Is that how it works?"

"No idea. I'm a warder, not a healer. My professional expertise is keeping things *out* of places." I tap the book against my knee. "This is me doing it backward. Badly."

"Sef." They sit up. Candlelight moves on their face, and for a moment — a heartbeat, no more — something flickers behind their eyes, quick as a fish under ice. "Say the thing about the ledger again. From the beginning. Why do you write it?"

I know why I write it. I write it because a woman named Ilvane once told me that a thing written down is harder to eat. I write it because Brannoc died saying their name and I swore on his cairn that no one else would go unnamed. I write it because love, stripped of everything else, is record-keeping — it's *I was here and you were here and it mattered* — and I will keep the records if it kills me.

But what I say is: "Because somebody should."

They study me for a long moment. Then they lie back down, satisfied or tired or both, and I read them entries thirty through thirty-six until their breathing goes slow, and I snuff the candle, and I sit in the dark doing the arithmetic.

Here is the sum as I work it: they gave everything. A kingdom's worth of everything. Sister, sister's face, honey, home, the pass at dawn, the grief, the joy, the whole crooked shining inventory of themselves. And I stole one piece back. One. Out of all of it, the smallest possible theft, the tiniest crime on the books: the day they met me. That's all I kept. Their first sight of me, tucked under my ribs where it hums like a ward at midnight.

Some nights I tell myself it's nothing. Some nights I tell myself it's everything. Most nights I understand it is exactly what it is — a thief's arithmetic, a cheat, a mercy, the one dishonest column in an otherwise honest ledger — and I carry it anyway, because the alternative was a world where not one single grain of what they were survived anywhere except in other people's telling, and I could not bear that. I could not bear to be the only one who remembered them. Memory kept alone curdles. Memory shared — even secretly, even stolen, even kept from the very person it belongs to — stays alive.

Ossifrage knows. Of course the crow knows; crows remember everything, it's the entire insufferable point of them. He sat on my windowsill two nights ago, feathers still ragged from Vesper's cage, and looked at me with one black eye and said, "You're heavier than you were, warder."

"Crows are rude."

"You're carrying something that isn't yours."

"It's mine," I said. "I paid for it."

He tilted his head. "With what?"

"With my mother's song." And the awful thing, the thing I haven't said aloud to anyone, is that I don't miss it. Or I miss it the way you miss a country — abstractly, achingly, without being able to conjure the tune. The door has my mother's voice now, singing somewhere in the hinges of the world, and maybe that's not the worst place for it. Maybe every time the door swings — and Vael's doors swing open a lot these days, gates unbarred, borders singing — she sings a little.

The crow considered this. Crows consider everything; it's how they justify the yelling. Then he said, "Keep it well, then. Seeds grow in the dark." And he flew off through the window I hadn't opened, because crows also enjoy showing off.

Seeds grow in the dark. I've turned that over every night since. Because here is what I haven't told anyone, not the healers, not the court, not the blank-faced, pear-loving, almost-smiling person sleeping ten feet from me: lately, when Lena looks at me, the vacancy cracks. Just for a heartbeat. Just a fish under ice. Last night they said my name before I introduced myself. Two nights ago they hummed four notes of a tune and stopped, frowning, and asked me why their chest hurt. And yesterday — yesterday, in the great hall, when the restored court gathered and someone mentioned the memory-guild, the old thieves' guild, dissolved and disgraced — Lena went very still, and then they said, quietly, to no one, to the air:

"Somebody should rebuild that. Somebody should make it honest."

And every head in the hall turned toward them, because everyone remembers what Lena Underhill was, even if Lena doesn't.

I sat there with our first meeting burning under my ribs like a coal refusing to go out, and I thought: it's growing. Whatever I planted, whatever leaked through the seal I made — it's taking root in them, or they in it, or some third thing that belongs to neither of us and both.

Tomorrow the queen's chamberlain comes with formal word: the restored court will grant the name-bearer any boon they ask. Lands, titles, treasure, anything. Everyone assumes Lena will ask for a house, a title, a quiet life. Everyone assumes wrong. I've seen the look gathering on their face for days now, the same look they used to get standing outside a vault, and I know — I *know*, the way I know ward-lines under snow — exactly what they're going to ask for.

They're going to ask for the guild.

And I am going to have to decide whether to tell them the truth about what I did at the door before they march off to remake the world in the image of a self they can't remember having — or whether some seeds, once planted, are better left to grow in the dark.
