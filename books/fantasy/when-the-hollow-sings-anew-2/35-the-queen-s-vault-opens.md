## 35. The Queen's Vault Opens

They gave me three days to recover before they told me I could have anything.

I spent those three days doing what I do best: nothing useful. I sat in a guest chamber in the restored palace of Vael — a room I had apparently burgled twice in my old life, according to a very polite steward who pretended not to enjoy telling me — and I tried to remember why I'd done it.

Nothing came. Of course it didn't. The door had eaten my hoard down to the marrow: every memory of Sef, every stolen year, the taste of honey, my sister's face. What remained was a person shaped like Lena Underhill with most of the furniture repossessed.

The strangest part wasn't the absence. Absence is just quiet. The strangest part was everyone else's faces when they looked at me. Gratitude has a weight to it, and the whole country was wearing theirs like armor too heavy for the parade.

On the third morning, Sef came to walk me down to the vaults.

"You don't have to come," he said, which meant he very much wanted me to, which meant I went.

* * *

The Queen's Vault sits beneath the oldest wing of the palace, behind a door that had been sealed so long the stone had grown around its edges like bark over a wound. When Vael came back — when the name rang out through the wound in the world and the stars reignited one by one like lamps being carried up a dark stair — everything that had been erased returned. The wards. The songs. The roads. And the vault, with everything still inside it, waiting patiently the way only locked things can.

Two warders of the new court worked the seals while we waited. They were young, both of them, born after the queen died, and they handled the door with the reverence of people who had grown up on stories and never expected the story to need dusting.

"You're sure you want to see this?" Sef murmured.

"I robbed this place," I said. "Seems rude not to attend the inventory."

"You didn't know you robbed it."

"That's what makes it rude. At least when I steal things awake, I have the decency to feel guilty."

He almost smiled. He'd been doing a lot of almost-smiling lately, each one a small act of faith that the man I used to love would recognize the gesture even if I didn't recognize him. He was wrong about that, mostly. But I was starting to learn his face the way you learn a language by living in it rather than studying it — slowly, imperfectly, with moments of sudden fluency that took my breath sideways.

The last seal broke with a sound like a held breath released. The door swung inward on darkness that smelled of cedar, cold stone, and something older underneath both: the particular hush of a room where a secret has lived alone for years and gotten comfortable with silence.

Lamps kindled themselves as we crossed the threshold — dead queens' magic, patient as debt. And there it was.

Shelves of glass and leaded crystal, floor to ceiling, receding into the dark like the ribs of some enormous sleeping animal. Every vessel held a memory. I could feel them the way you feel heat from a banked fire — hundreds of them, thousands, humming at slightly different pitches. Grief. Joy. A coronation. A betrayal, probably; royal vaults always hold at least one betrayal, kept as evidence or warning.

And on a plinth of black marble at the center, empty now, a single setting shaped for a memory the size of a country's name.

"That's where it sat," Sef said quietly. "That's what you took."

"I thought I was stealing a nobleman's signet ring and got greedy."

"You were nineteen. You got lost. You took the first thing your hands found because you heard guards coming." He paused. "You told me that once. On the Lethe road."

I looked at him. "I don't remember telling you."

"I know." He said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "I'm keeping it for you anyway."

* * *

The court received us — received *me*, specifically, though Sef refused to be more than two steps from my elbow — in the Hall of Reckoning, which turned out to be an actual hall where actual reckoning happened, and not, as I'd assumed from the name, a tavern with ambitions.

Queen Maerlin the Second sat her grandmother's throne. She had been a girl of nine when the Hollow took Vael, a princess remembered by no one, and now she was a young woman with a crown that fit her like it had been keeping itself warm all these years for exactly this skull. She had the queen's eyes — Ossifrage had told me that, perched on my shoulder during the coronation, croaking commentary like a disapproving herald. She also had, I noticed, her grandmother's habit of looking directly at the thing everyone else was politely avoiding.

Which was, at present, me.

"Lena Underhill," she said, and the whole hall went silent the way halls do when they've rehearsed their silences. "Vael owes you its name."

"Technically I owed *it*," I said. "I stole it first. We're square."

A few courtiers gasped. The queen's mouth did something at the corner that suggested she'd have laughed if laughter were permitted on the third day of a reign. "The crown does not see it that way. You carried what no one else could carry. You paid—" she hesitated, and I watched her choose honesty over ceremony, which I decided then and there I could work with, "—more than anyone should have. So. Name your price. Land, title, gold, pardon for whatever the ledgers say you did before—"

"There are ledgers?"

"*Extensive* ledgers," she said dryly, "which the crown is prepared to lose in a fire. Ask."

Here is the thing nobody tells you about having everything taken away: it clarifies. For years I'd wanted things — coin, safety, Brannoc's laugh across a campfire, Sef's hand in mine without either of us flinching. Wanting with holes in yourself is just hunger wearing ambition's coat. But standing in that hall, emptied out, offered anything, I found that the want had changed shape entirely.

I couldn't ask for my memories back. The door had fed on them; that account was closed, and Mother Vesper's offer had already taught me what a restored Vael costs when you buy it cheaply. I couldn't ask for a title. I'd spent my whole life avoiding being owned by anything larger than myself, and a title is just ownership with better tailoring.

But there were other holes. Ones that weren't mine.

"How many people," I asked, "lost memories to the Hollow? Not the ones it ate forever — the ones scattered. Sold. Traded for bread in camps like Ashfall. Stolen by thieves like me and sitting in jars somewhere right now, humming, ownerless."

The queen blinked. Beside me, Sef went very still.

"The Hollow scattered what it didn't swallow," I said. "We saw it. Mothers selling wedding days for bread. Crows guarding royal secrets for decades because nobody came to collect them. There are memories out there that belong to people who may not even know they're missing anything — who just feel a cold spot in their chest and call it grief and don't know grief's proper name." My voice was doing something embarrassing. I let it. "I was a thief. I was a good thief, which means I was a terrible person with excellent craft. Give me the craft and take the rest of the job description."

"Say it plainly," the queen said.

"Rebuild the Guild of Mnemosyne." The old name came out of my mouth and tasted like someone else's — but right, like a key that fits a lock you didn't cut. "Not as thieves this time. As healers. Finders and returners. Let us go into the gray places and the refugee roads and the markets of secondhands, and bring back what was sold, and teach people how to hold onto their own heads. Let the guild be the thing that stands between Vael and the next silence — because there will be a next one. Grief doesn't stop existing just because you sang it into a chorus."

The hall was quiet. Somewhere near the ceiling, a raven-shaped shadow shifted on a beam — Ossifrage, attending court as was apparently his ancient right, listening with one polished eye.

"And you would lead this guild?" the queen asked. "You, who cannot remember your own sister's face?"

"That's exactly why," I said. "Every thief in the old guild stole because they were missing something and thought someone else's memories would fill the hole. It doesn't work. I'm living proof — I had a vault full of other people's years and I was still the loneliest creature on the Thornfell pass. But healers?" I shrugged, and it came out steadier than I felt. "Healers start by admitting what's broken. I can do that. I'm very qualified. I'm broken in *alphabetical order*."

This time the queen did laugh — briefly, inelegantly, gloriously — and half the hall gasped again, and I decided that if I accomplished nothing else with my second life, I would teach the court of Vael that laughter was permitted on solemn days.

"It will take years," she said. "It will take charters and funding and fighting the remnants of the Choir in the courts of neighboring kingdoms, whose masters will not love a guild that un-sells their merchandise."

"Then it'll keep me busy," I said. "Your Majesty, I have a head full of blank pages. I intend to fill them with work."

She rose, and the hall rose with her, and she spoke the words that made it law — and I stood there, a thief in borrowed clothes, watching a country decide that its memory-thieves would become its memory-keepers, and felt something turn over in my chest that I chose to believe was purpose and not indigestion.

* * *

Chartering a guild turns out to be ninety percent paperwork and ten percent arguing with archivists, and I loved every minute of it, which surprised me more than anyone.

The first recruit was obvious. Brannoc's daughter — Wren, twenty-two, sharp-eyed, built like her father and twice as stubborn — arrived at the palace gates two weeks after the charter was signed, carrying his old ledger and refusing all offers of hospitality until she'd read me every entry in it.

"He wrote you down," she said. "After the Singing Stones, I mean. Before. All through the journey — he kept notes on you the way Sef does. Said somebody had to, since you kept giving pieces of yourself away like they were free."

"He charged me passage for a stranger's childhood once," I said. "Free wasn't really his style."

"No," she said softly, turning a page. "But he wrote that you were. 'Gives away what she can't spare, keeps nothing, the fool, the fool.' " She looked up, and she had his eyes, and I had to grip the table. "He'd have joined your guild, you know. If he'd lived. He'd have hated the charter meetings and loved the work."

"Then he joins posthumously. First name on the roll. Founder's honors."

"He'd have hated that too."

"Good. Then it's accurate."

Wren became our first Warden of Returns — the officers who track sold and scattered memories through the gray bazaars and refugee roads. Within a month we had eleven more: ex-Choir defectors seeking penance, Ashfall survivors who knew the memory-trade from the selling side, two reformed burglars of my old acquaintance who wept with relief at being given a job where taking things was no longer the point.

Sef worked beside me, though technically he held a court position — Warden-Regent, heir in fact if not yet proclaimed, a truth that had finally been spoken aloud in that same Hall of Reckoning after I'd yelled at him in front of the First Door. His lineage was public knowledge now. His blood had opened a door between worlds; there was no putting that back in the jar.

He was strange about it, in the way Sef was strange about everything — quietly, dutifully, as if inheritance were a form to be filed. But at night, in the guildhall we were building out of the old Mnemosyne safehouse on Candlemaker's Row, he would sit with the ledgers open and read to me. My entries. His entries. The record of a person I used to be, reconstructed page by page like a mosaic from shards.

"Taste of honey," he read one evening. "Lena's favorite. Bought a jar in Ashfall and ate the whole thing with her fingers and said, quote, 'this is what the rich people are hoarding, and honestly, fair.' "

"I sound insufferable."

"You sounded *happy*." He turned the page. His voice did the careful thing it did when the entries got close to the parts about him. "Entry forty-one. 'Sef hums when he thinks no one's listening. Badly. Do not tell him.' "

"I'm telling you right now: badly."

"Noted," he said gravely, and reached for the ink to add it to today's page, because that was how we did things now — the ledger grew daily, my new life recorded in his neat hand, so that future-Lena would have past-Lena's mistakes available for study.

* * *

The vault itself became the guild's first great project.

Queen Maerlin granted us custody of it — the shelves, the vessels, the humming thousands — and for a season we did nothing but catalog. Wren managed the inventories. The defectors handled the delicate work of identifying provenance. And I walked the rows every day, learning the collection the way I'd once learned locks: by touch, by patience, by respect for the mechanism.

Most of the vault's holdings were royal memories — coronations, treaties, the queen's own childhood, preserved against exactly the catastrophe that had eventually come. But mixed among them were others. Confiscated thieves' hoards, centuries of them. Memories seized from smugglers and spies and, once, heartbreakingly, from a child, labeled only *too dangerous to return* in an archivist's cramped hand.

"We return those too," I said, when Wren hesitated over that shelf. "Especially those. Dangerous to whom? Some minister who didn't like what the child saw?"

"Some things were sealed for mercy," she said.

"Then we'll judge case by case. That's the difference between us and the old guild, Wren. They had rules. We're going to have judgment. It's slower. It's supposed to be."

Ossifrage supervised, naturally. The crow had been appointed Keeper of the Aviary Archives — a real office, with a real stipend that went entirely toward walnuts and grudge maintenance — and he perched on the vault's central plinth, the empty one, and offered commentary on our technique in a voice like a filing cabinet falling downstairs.

"In my day," he croaked, "apprentices catalogued for *five years* before touching a vessel. Five! And they were grateful!"

"In your day the country disappeared," I said. "Standards lapse."

"In my day we had *respect for the name*." But his eye followed me down the rows with something that wasn't quite disapproval anymore. One evening, as I locked up, he said, quieter: "The queen would have liked you. My queen. She hid the name because she believed ordinary hands could carry extraordinary things safely, if the hands chose to." A pause. "She was right. It annoys me."

"High praise, from you."

"Don't preen. You drop things when you preen."

* * *

Winter came early that year, the first true winter of the restored Vael, and with it came the reports.

They started as rumors on the trade roads — villages where the wards flickered, not failing, but *stuttering*, like a singer clearing her throat. Then a Returns team brought in a vessel recovered from a Choir cache in the borderlands, and the memory inside it was wrong: not damaged, not faded, but *hollowed*, scooped clean, its shape intact and its substance gone. Eaten from within.

"Vesper," Sef said that night, laying the vessel on the guildhall table between us like an accusation.

"She fell into the Hollow," I said. "We watched it take her. Gently. Like it was owed."

"And now it sings with her voice." He turned the empty vessel in the lamplight. "The Choir isn't dead, Lena. It's headless and hungry and it remembers everything she knew — including every route into this kingdom, including the vault, including *us*."

Outside, far to the north, the border wards hummed their nightly song. Lately I'd noticed something in it I couldn't name — a new note, low and wrong, threading under the melody like a finger dragged through wet ink.

I looked at the hollowed vessel, and at Sef's face above it, and at the ledger lying open beside them, full of everything we'd rebuilt and everything we might yet lose.

"Well," I said. "We'd better find out what it's singing."

The wards answered before he could — a single discordant note rolling down from the north, faint but unmistakable, like a chorus learning a new song in the dark.

One that sounded, horribly, almost like my name.
