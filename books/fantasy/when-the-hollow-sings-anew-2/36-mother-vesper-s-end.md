## 36. Mother Vesper's End

I had been a weapon for so long that I'd forgotten weapons rust.

That was the thought I carried up the tower stairs of the restored palace of Vael, three days after the coronation-that-wasn't-quite-a-coronation, with a lantern in one hand and Lena's hand in the other and no sword at my hip for the first time in eleven years. The Choir's old quarters had been sealed since the door opened, since Vael came flooding back into the world like water into a dry channel, and nobody — not the court, not the guards, not me — had wanted to be the one to open them again.

Which was exactly why we should have known she was still inside.

"She'll be dead by now," Lena said. Not cruel. Just doing sums, the way thieves do. "Half-hollowed, alone, no one feeding her. Three months."

"You don't know her."

"I know the Hollow." They squeezed my hand. "It doesn't kill quickly when it wants to watch."

The stairwell smelled of dust and tallow and something else underneath, something that made the back of my teeth ache — the smell of a room where a lot of remembering had been done badly. At the top stood the door to the Choir's sanctum, black oak banded in gray iron, and across its face someone had scratched words in the old warder's cipher that only two people left alive could read.

*COME ALONE AND I WILL BE CIVIL.*

"It knows we can both read it," Lena said.

"It knows *you* can read it," I said. "I'm an afterthought. A footnote." I looked at the scratch, at the neat, patient strokes. "She says she'll be civil."

"Mother Vesper's idea of civil is what most people call an atrocity with table manners."

"Yes," I said. "But she's asking. She's never asked before."

Lena looked at me for a long moment, lantern-light moving on their face, and I saw them decide something — the same way they used to decide things over a lock, weighing the tumblers against the risk.

"Then we answer the invitation," they said. "But we answer it together, and if she objects to the plus-one, I'm stealing her teeth."

* * *

The sanctum was smaller than I remembered, or perhaps I had simply been smaller when I served in it, and more afraid, and therefore everything had seemed vast. Rows of gray robes hung on hooks like shed skins. The oath-table still stood in the center, scarred with the marks where each of us had pressed our palms and given away our doubts. And at the far end, in the chair that had been hers, sat Mother Vesper.

She had been beautiful once, in the way that winter is beautiful — precise, austere, composed entirely of edges. The Hollow had taken half of her and left the other half sitting upright out of what I could only assume was spite. Her left side was smooth and faintly translucent, like wax held near a flame, and where her left eye had been there was a soft gray shimmer that moved when she spoke, rippling with something almost like sound.

"Sefron," she said. My name in her mouth sounded like an indictment being filed. "And the thief. How domestic."

"Vesper." I kept my voice level. Eleven years of practice, keeping my voice level in this room. It didn't work as well as it used to. "You were ordered to leave."

"I was ordered by a boy king's regency council that doesn't remember I exist." Her good eye found mine. "Which is the trouble with building your life on taking memories, Sefron. Eventually everyone forgets to fear you, and then you have nothing."

"You could have left," Lena said. They'd drifted a step to my left, casual as anything, putting themselves between Vesper and the row of hanging robes — I knew their habits well enough now to know they were checking whether the robes were empty. "There's a whole country out there. Bread, sunlight, people who've never heard of you."

"There is a whole country out there," Vesper agreed, "because you two pried open a door with a dead woman's last word and a boy's blood, and poured a kingdom back into the world." She smiled, and even diminished, even half-erased, it was a smile I had seen preside over a hundred oaths. "Do you know why I wanted it gone? Truly?"

"Enlighten us," Lena said.

"Because a named thing can grieve." Her voice dropped, and for a moment the gray shimmer in her ruined eye stilled. "The Hollow was grief, girl. Grief with nowhere to go, born when Queen Marenne died unnamed and unmourned and her whole country forgot how to say its own sorrow. I fed it. I organized it. I gave it structure, purpose, a choir instead of a scream. And you—" the good eye flicked to me— "I gave you a place to put your grief too, didn't I, Sefron? You came to us hollowed-out already. I only made it official."

"That's not what this is about," I said, and hated how much it cost to say.

"No," she agreed softly. "It isn't. This is about the fact that I am half a person, Sefron, and the half that remains remembers every oath I ever administered, and cannot bear them." She rose from the chair. She was unsteady on her left side, listing like a ship with a broken keel, and she steadied herself on the oath-table with a hand that was mostly memory already. "So I am going to finish the work. I am going to speak the unmaking backward, here, at the heart of the wards, and I am going to take this whole singing, grieving, resurrected country apart again — because a country that does not exist cannot make anyone mourn it."

* * *

Later, Lena would ask me why I didn't draw steel — except I wasn't carrying steel, which was rather the point, and anyway there was no time. What happened happened fast, the way the worst things always do.

Vesper began to speak.

Not words. Not quite. She spoke the *shape* of words, the anti-name that the Hollow had whispered through her for years, the syllables that meant *forget*, and the lantern-flames bent away from her mouth, and along the walls of the sanctum the old gray wards woke up — the ones the court thought were dead, the ones the Choir had threaded through the palace foundations in secret all those years — and began to drink the light.

I felt it happen. That's the part I will never manage to explain properly. I felt Vael start to thin, felt it the way you feel a fever coming on, a wrongness behind the eyes. Somewhere below us the bells of the restored city faltered mid-peal. Somewhere beyond the windows, the new stars — the returned stars, Lena's stars — guttered like candles in a draft.

"*No,*" Lena said, and it wasn't a shout. It was quiet and absolute, the voice of someone who had once stolen a kingdom's name and knew precisely how much weight a single word could carry. They stepped forward into the failing light and did the most Lena thing I have ever witnessed.

They talked back.

"You're doing it wrong," they said. "You've always done it wrong. You think grief is a hole. It isn't. I've been inside the hole, Vesper. I lived in it for a year. It doesn't want silence. It wants *witness*. You can't unname a country out of mourning — you have to give the mourning somewhere to go."

"And where would that be?" Vesper rasped, still speaking the unmaking between the words, still thinning the world.

"A choir needs a conductor," Lena said. "Or it's just noise."

And I understood — later than they did, because I am a slow study in all matters of the heart — what they meant. What we had learned at the border in those first days after the door opened, when the Hollow had come to the edge of the reborn Vael and found, instead of a wound, a country full of people who finally knew what they had lost and were saying so, out loud, together, in every square and kitchen and shrine. We had thought we were safe because the name was spoken. But the deeper truth was stranger: the Hollow had been *fed*. Fed honestly, fed grief willingly given, witnesses' tears and mourner's songs instead of stolen memories. It had come to the border ravening and it had left humming.

It was not our enemy anymore. But it was still hungry, and it was still owed.

And standing in front of us, unraveling the world syllable by syllable, was a woman who was already half its property — who had given it decades of service and doubt and self, who had nothing left to feed it but the remainder.

"*Finish it,*" I said to the dark.

Not to Vesper. To the other presence in the room, the one that had been there the whole time, the one whose silence had texture, the gray shimmer in Vesper's eye that was not an eye at all but a window. I had spent eleven years serving it and three months learning to speak to it, and in that moment I did the last thing my oath had ever prepared me for.

I addressed it as a colleague.

"She's yours," I said. "She was always yours. Take her gently. She's owed gentleness — she just never knew how to ask."

* * *

I want to tell you there was a battle. Some part of me, the part raised on soldiers' stories, still wishes there had been — steel drawn, wards flaring, a worthy antagonist struck down at the last. Closure, the ballads call it, and they usually mean somebody dies loudly.

What actually happened was quieter than snowfall.

Vesper stopped speaking mid-syllable. The unmaking died in her throat, and the gray in her eye spread — slowly, without hurry, like dawn coming up over a field — down her cheek, along her jaw, across the rigid architecture of her shoulders. And as it went, her face changed. The edges softened. The austerity melted off her like frost off glass in a warm room.

"Oh," she said.

Just that. A small word, almost silly, from the mouth of the most feared woman in two kingdoms. She sank back into her chair, and the Hollow gathered around her the way a family gathers around a deathbed, and I swear to every god whose name survived the unmaking — it was tender. It held her the way you hold something you have waited a long time for. Because it had waited. All those years she thought she commanded it, it had been waiting for her to stop commanding and simply arrive.

"The song," she whispered. Her good eye found mine, and then found Lena, and for the first time in the eleven years I had known her, Mother Vesper looked *unarmed*. "Sefron. Is it true? Do they sing to it now?"

"Every evening," I said. My voice cracked on the second word and I let it. "You'd have heard it if you'd come out."

Something moved across her dissolving face that I will spend the rest of my life deciding whether it was regret or relief. Probably both. Most things are both.

"I built order," she said, fading, the words going soft and gray at the edges the way she was. "Out of grief. It was the only kindness I knew."

"I know," I said. "It was mine too."

And then she was gone — not violently, not with any tearing or screaming, but the way a note ends when the singer chooses to end it, cleanly, on purpose, having finished the phrase. The gray shimmer folded closed. The chair was empty. On the oath-table where her hand had rested lay her signet ring, the gray iron band of the Choir's high office, cooling like a stone that had recently been held.

The bells below us picked up the peal where they had faltered, right on the beat, as though they'd only paused to breathe.

* * *

We stood in the sanctum a long while afterward. Neither of us spoke. Outside the narrow window, Vael went on existing — rooftops and smoke and market-carts and a guard changing shifts in the courtyard below, all of it stubbornly, magnificently ordinary — and the stars were steady overhead, and somewhere across the city the evening chorus was gathering in the squares, hundreds of voices preparing to sing their grief to the sky the way they now did every night, the way that had turned a devouring silence into something almost like music.

Lena picked up the signet ring. Turned it over. Their thumb found the worn sigil — a circle with a line drawn through it, the mark of the unspoken name.

"What do we do with this?" they asked.

"Melt it down," I said. Then, because it was Lena, and because I had learned something in the last year about what this particular thief did with things: "Unless you've got a better idea."

They were quiet for a moment. Lantern-light, starlight, the far-off sound of the chorus tuning up.

"I keep thinking about what she said," Lena murmured. "*A choir instead of a scream.* She thought that was mercy. Organizing the grief. Containing it." They turned the ring once more in their fingers, and then — deliberately, watching themselves do it — slipped it into their coat pocket, into the same pocket where they kept their ledger. "Maybe it started as mercy. Most terrible things do. But she never learned the second verse, did she. The one where you let the grief go."

"No," I said. "She never learned anything she couldn't take from someone else."

Lena looked up at me, and there was something in their face I was learning to recognize — the expression they wore when they were about to say a true thing and resented it.

"You told it to take her gently," they said. "'She's owed gentleness.' That's the man the Choir made, saying that? Or is that somebody new?"

I thought about it honestly, because they deserved honesty and I had promised it to them at the ford and never revoked the promise.

"Somebody new," I said. "Somebody who read a ledger aloud every night for a month to a stranger with my whole heart's history written on their face. He's new. He's maybe four months old. But he's keeping the job."

"Good," Lena said softly. "Because the court's going to ask us, you know. Tomorrow, at the assembly. *What became of Mother Vesper.* And I refuse to lie in a rebuilt country. It feels sacrilegious."

"Then we tell them the truth," I said. "That the last High Warden of the Gray Choir died in her chair, in the room where she did her worst work, and that the thing she spent her life feeding took her the way a river takes rain. Without anger. Like it had been waiting for her all along."

Lena nodded slowly. Then they snorted — a small, wet, unmistakably Lena sound, grief and laughter sharing a blanket.

"'Like a river takes rain.' Listen to you. Eleven years in a murder cult and you come out with poetry."

"I had a good teacher," I said. "Someone once described a heist to me as 'lending, with paperwork.'"

"That was slander," Lena said, "and I stand by every word of it," and they took my hand, and we went down the stairs together, past the empty robes and the scarred table, out of the last dark room in Vael.

Behind us, in the empty sanctum, the evening breeze through the narrow window stirred the hanging gray robes — and for just a moment, passing beneath us on the stair, I heard it. Faint. Almost nothing. A hum, low and layered, rising from the direction of the Choir's old quarters like warmth from a hearthstone.

The Hollow, singing.

Not the ravenous silence. Not yet the full chorus that would one day stand at the borders of the country and harmonize with every mourner in Vael. Something between. Something practicing.

Lena heard it too. They stopped three steps from the bottom, head tilted, and I watched them listen with the expression of a thief appraising a lock they'd never seen before — professional interest shading into something warmer, something almost like recognition.

"Sef," they said quietly. "It's trying to learn the melody."

"I know," I said.

"It's *close*."

"I know," I said again, and pulled them gently onward, down into the lamplit streets, toward the sound of the city gathering to sing — neither of us yet understanding, either of us, that the note the Hollow could not find, the one missing line it was reaching for night after night at the edge of the kingdom, was a melody only two people alive had ever heard entire.

And that one of us had been carrying it all along.
