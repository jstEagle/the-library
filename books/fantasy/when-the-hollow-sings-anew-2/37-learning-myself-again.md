## 37. Learning Myself Again

The first thing I learned about learning myself again is that it's humiliating.

Nobody warns you about that part. The healers at the rebuilt court — my guild now, gods help me, though the paperwork still lists me as "consultant" because apparently even resurrection comes with a probationary period — they talk about reclamation like it's a gentle thing. Like memories are birds returning to a roost at dusk. What they don't mention is that some of those birds come back and immediately start pecking you.

Take my sister.

Wren Underhill stood in the doorway of my quarters on my third morning in Vael-the-returned, holding a basket of plums, looking at me like I was both the answer to every prayer she'd ever whispered and also an enormous inconvenience, which — knowing us — is probably accurate.

"You're shorter than I remembered," she said.

"I'm exactly your height."

"No," she said, setting down the basket with great ceremony, "I remember you being taller. You were taller when I was nine."

"Wren, you were taller than me when you were nine. You've always been a weed. I'm the garden's only dignity."

And then she crossed the room and hit me. Not hard — a flat little smack on the arm, the kind of blow that carries no force and all the meaning in the world. Then she grabbed my face in both hands and pressed her forehead against mine and shook, and I held her, and I didn't know why we were crying except that apparently our bodies remembered what my mind had sold.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you: grief doesn't need the memory. It just needs the shape of one. My skull had a Wren-sized hole in it and when she walked in, everything around the hole lit up like lamps coming on in windows of a house where the family never left.

"Do you know what I did," she said into my shoulder, "when the stars went out? When everyone forgot there'd ever been a Vael?"

"Tell me."

"I didn't forget." She pulled back and looked at me, red-eyed and furious and alive, so alive it hurt to look at. "That's the thing, Lena. Nobody outside the border could forget what never existed for them. But inside — inside, we were all hollowed. Everyone woke up not knowing their own names, wandering around like — like sleepwalkers. Except me. Except a few. We couldn't say why we knew something had been lost, but we knew. I spent three years writing your name on things. Every day I'd write LENA UNDERHILL somewhere new — doorposts, fence rails, the inside of my boot. Because I couldn't tell you who you were, but I knew there'd been someone worth remembering, and I refused — I *refused* —"

She stopped. Breathed. Picked up a plum and thrust it at me like a weapon.

"Eat. You're too thin. Thieves are supposed to be well-fed, it's practically the whole point of thieving."

I ate the plum. And here's the second thing I learned: the taste meant nothing to me, and everything anyway. Some other Lena had loved plums — I could feel the ghost of the loving, the groove worn smooth by years of enjoyment — and so I ate it and let that stranger's pleasure be mine on loan, which, if you think about it, is just theft with better manners. Old habits.

* * *

The guildhall used to be the Vault. Our queen's Vault, the one whose deepest shelf I'd robbed blind years ago without ever knowing what I carried out. Now it has windows cut into it — actual windows, because the new Vael decided that hoarding memory in the dark was how you got HOLLOWs, and honestly, fair — and long tables where people sit across from each other and trade.

Not steal. Trade.

That was the whole argument, the first month. Half the restored court wanted the memory-guild burned to the ground and its members run out of the country, and I can't say I blamed them; I'd been one of the jackals picking at the kingdom's pockets since before I understood whose pocket it was. But Sef stood up at the council — Sef, who had every reason to want me forgotten twice over, once as thief and once as the person he'd lost — and said, "You want the skill to die or the sin to die? They're separate things. She knows that better than anyone." He called me *she* in front of the whole council, and I didn't correct him, because I genuinely couldn't remember if I minded.

Turns out I don't. Turns out there are a lot of things I don't mind that old-Lena would have bristled at, and vice versa. Rebuilding yourself from ledger entries and other people's testimonies is like wearing a coat sewn by committee: mostly warm, occasionally bizarre.

Ilvane supervises the training now, when the ghost feels like manifesting. A scholar-ghost haunting a library that doesn't exist anymore, teaching thieves to be healers — if you'd told me two years ago that this was my future I'd have picked your pocket out of spite. But Ilvane's first lesson is a good one, and I repeat it to every new recruit:

"A stolen memory is a wound in two bodies. The keeper bleeds absence. The taken bleeds presence. Our work is sutures, not acquisitions. Say it back."

They say it back. Some of them mean it eventually.

* * *

My friends came back to me the way weather comes back to a valley — gradually, and with opinions.

Brannoc I couldn't get back at all. That debt sits in me like a stone in a shoe; I've stopped trying to shake it out and started trying to walk well with it instead. His daughter Maren visits the guildhall sometimes, tall and quiet with his same crooked grin, and trains under me, and pretends she isn't grieving in front of the woman her father died buying time for, and I pretend I don't notice her pretending, and between the two of our performances we manage something almost like honesty. She keeps his dice. I keep his last words. Between us we have most of him.

Ossifrage took longer.

The crow — last familiar of the royal aviary, self-appointed conscience of the realm, menace with feathers — sulked at me for a solid month after Vael returned. Perched on my windowsill at dawn and stared with one glittering eye, radiating the specific disappointment of a creature who told me *so*, repeatedly, at length, and was nearly traded away for his trouble.

"You gave it BACK," he said one morning, finally deigning to speak. "The queen's name. You gave it back. Do you know how long I waited? Seventy years I waited, and you give it back in a night, through a DOOR, with DRAMA—"

"That was rather the point."

"The point," Ossifrage said, hopping onto the sill's inner ledge, closer, "is that you were supposed to fail interestingly so I could narrate your downfall to posterity. Instead you succeeded. Do you know what success does to a bird's material?" But he said it while preening the hair at my temple with his awful clever beak, gently, the way he must have preened the queen's, and I let him, because some forgiveness doesn't use words. Crows remember everything. Apparently that includes how to love someone new-shaped.

He lives in the guildhall rafters now. Calls himself Senior Archivist. Takes payment in candied almonds and gossip. Has already unionized the mice.

* * *

But here's what I actually sat down to write about, before Wren and plums and crows crowded in: the flaws.

When you lose yourself and rebuild from testimony, you get a flattering portrait. People are kind to the amnesiac. They edit. Sef read me my own ledger nightly, and even his entries — honest man, stubbornly honest — softened at the edges, because love is a lens and lenses bend light.

So I went hunting. Deliberately. Asked the questions nobody volunteers answers to.

"What was I like?" I asked Maren, one evening, over the training yard. "Before. Honestly."

She considered me for a long moment. "You were cold," she said. "Efficient-cold. Papa said you could take a man's childhood and count the change before he finished crying." A pause. "But you never once kept anything you stole. Not ever. He used to say that was either virtue or a wound, and he could never decide which."

"And which do you think?"

"I think," Maren said, "that you're asking, which means the answer matters to you now, which means maybe it was a wound, and wounds can close." She tossed me a practice knife. "Also you telegraphed your left side terribly. That part hasn't changed."

I laughed until my ribs hurt. Some things return without any memory attached at all — laughter, apparently, is stored in the body, along with grief and plum-loving and the exact way Wren hits. I'm keeping a list. The healers say the list itself might help; the mind grows toward whatever you water.

Some flaws came back intact, uninvited. I still lie reflexively when frightened — small lies, structural lies, the architecture of a life built on not-being-found. Sef catches them now, gently, holds them up to the light: "That's not true, is it." And I stand there hating it and then, worse, admitting it, and the world does not end, which remains the single most disorienting discovery of my second life. Old Lena believed every truth confessed was a lockpick surrendered. New Lena is learning that some doors open from the other side if you just knock.

Some things didn't come back at all. I don't dream in color — the healers say that may mend, may not. I have no feeling whatsoever about honey, which Wren finds tragic and Ossifrage finds suspicious. And there's a stretch of road in the Thornfell pass that makes my chest ache so badly I have to stop walking, every time, though I've never been able to say why. Sef watches me stop there. He never explains. He just stops with me, and hums something low under his breath, half a melody, like a man carrying the other half very carefully and not ready yet to set it down.

* * *

Last night, Wren and I sat up late with the plums and a candle, and she asked me the question I'd been dreading and craving in equal measure.

"Do you want them back? Your memories? Truly? The court would move heaven and earth — the queen owes you —"

And I made myself sit with it before answering, because it deserved more than reflex.

Here's what I've come to understand, sitting in this rebuilt country full of people who remember me better than I do: a memory returned is not a life relived. It's a letter from a stranger. I could have every year of old Lena poured back into me tomorrow and she would still be someone I'd have to get to know — and I'd be doing it with her regrets pre-installed, her armor, her arithmetic of scarcity, her conviction that connection was a commodity and love a liability on the books.

Whereas the gaps? The gaps are where the new things grow. That's not comfort-talk; I've watched it happen. In the space where my sister's face used to be, I got to fall in love with her fresh — with the woman she became while I was gone, sharp and stubborn and three years brave. In the space where my guilt should be, I found purpose instead, which is guilt's better-behaved cousin. In the gap where old Lena kept her heart locked, somebody left a window open, and a warden climbed in through it, and stayed.

"I want what I earned," I told her. "Not what I lost."

Wren looked at me for a long moment, then snorted — our mother's snort, resurrected perfectly in a face I'm only beginning to map. "That's the most annoying wise thing anyone's ever said to me."

"It runs in the family."

"It really doesn't. I'm delightful." She blew out the candle. In the dark, her voice went quieter, younger. "Lena. Tomorrow — walk with me up the pass? There's a place I go. Where the wards sing. I think... I think you should hear it. I think you'll understand something there that you can't learn from ledgers."

The wards sing. Of course they do. Old Brannoc used to say the wards hummed louder every year, and I used to hear them sing when no one else could — that much survived, that strange inherited hearing, the one talent of mine that was never theft.

"Tomorrow," I agreed.

And in the dark, unbidden, half-asleep, I caught myself humming — four notes, rising, then a silence where the fifth should be. A melody with a missing line, like a sentence with its best word stolen.

I don't know the tune. But my body does. And tomorrow, on the pass, I intend to find out who taught it to me.
