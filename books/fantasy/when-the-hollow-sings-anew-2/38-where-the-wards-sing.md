## 38. Where the Wards Sing

The Thornfell pass looked smaller than I remembered, which was a strange thing to think, since I didn't remember it.

That's the trouble with a rebuilt life. You get the bones back — the guild ledgers, the testimony of friends, Sef's nightly readings from the book he kept of me — but bones aren't the same as flesh. I knew I had crossed this pass a hundred times. I knew I had bargained with Brannoc here, traded strangers' childhoods for safe road, learned which scree slopes sang underfoot and which ones stayed silent out of spite. I could recite all of it like a merchant reciting inventory. It sat in my skull with no weight to it at all.

So when we came up through the last switchback and the wind changed, and something in my chest lurched sideways like a boat hitting a swell, I didn't understand it. Not at first.

"Stop," I said, and stopped, mid-stride, one boot on a stone worn smooth by decades of smuggler feet.

Sef stopped too. He'd gotten good at that — stopping instantly, without questions, the way you learn to when you travel with someone whose mind is a house with half its doors bricked over. He'd also gotten good at watching me, which used to make me feel like a museum exhibit and now mostly just made me feel accompanied.

"What is it?" he asked.

"I don't know." The wind came down the pass again, threading through the standing stones along the ridge — the old border wards, cracked and patched and singing. They'd been humming since Vael returned. Everyone said so. The whole country hummed at its edges like a struck bell that wouldn't quite finish ringing, and folk had decided it was a good omen, the sound of a land remembering itself, and they built little shrines at the bases of the ward-stones and left offerings of bread and ribbon and, at one of them, inexplicably, a single very clean turnip.

I had heard the hum every day since the door opened. It had never once done to me what it just did.

It wasn't a sound, exactly. That was the thing about the wards — Ossifrage had tried to explain it years ago, preening on a broken wall, saying *crow-song isn't words, girl, it's the shape of meaning before words get their grubby hands on it* — and the wards were the same. The hum was a melody the way a name is a word: technically true, criminally incomplete. And this melody—

This melody had a hole in it.

I stood there on the frozen scree with my breath coming out in white flags of surrender and listened to a song that was missing a line, and I wanted it the way I'd once wanted coin, the way I'd once wanted other people's weddings and first kisses and lazy Sunday mornings. The wanting was old. Older than my new ledger. It rose up from somewhere below the brickwork, from whatever foundation the Hollow had left standing when it took its payment, and it knew things about me that I didn't.

"Lena." Sef's hand on my shoulder. "You've gone pale."

"The song," I said. "It's wrong."

He cocked his head. He was a warder; he could hear more of it than most, though not what I heard. "It sounds right to me. Whole, even. Better than before the—"

"Not whole." I turned to face him and grabbed the front of his coat, which is a habit I apparently developed sometime during the two years I can't account for, because he didn't even flinch. "There's a line missing. Listen. It goes—" I hummed what I could hear, four phrases rising, and then the gap, and my voice cracked open on the nothing where the fifth should have been, and closed awkwardly around the sixth.

Sef went very still.

Not warder-still, not danger-still. A different kind of still. The stillness of a man holding a full cup while walking on ice, who has just heard the first crack.

"You know it," I said. "Don't you. You know the line."

"Lena—"

"Don't you *dare* manage me right now, Sef. Don't use your careful voice. There's a hole in the song and there's a hole in me and I have spent two years learning which holes are mine and which ones were carved, and this one—" My breath fogged between us. "This one fits my shape."

* * *

We climbed to the shrine stone instead of arguing on the road, because some conversations need altitude. The ward rose above us, a finger of gray granite veined with faint silver light, humming its wounded melody into the wind. Someone had left ribbons tied around its base, dozens of them, whipping and snapping in the gusts like small bright prayers. Someone else had left the turnip. Vael was a strange place now, warm and half-familiar, a coat sewn for someone else's body.

I sat down on the cold stone and pulled my knees up like a girl much younger than I had any evidence of being. Sef sat beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and took off one glove, and I realized my own hands were shaking.

"You have to understand," he said slowly, "what I did at the door. What I told you I did."

"You kept a memory. One of ours. Your first meeting, you said. You swapped it in when the lock fed, so that some part of me survived somewhere, even if it lived in your head instead of mine." I'd made myself say it calmly, that night outside the vault when he finally confessed. Calmly, and then less calmly. There had been shouting. There might have been a thrown cup. I stand by the cup.

"That's what I told you," Sef said. "And it's true. But I didn't tell you everything about what it's like. Owning something that belongs to you."

The wind gusted. The ribbons snapped. The ward hummed its four rising phrases and fell silent, and waited, and began again, patient as grief.

"When you look at me," he said, "you see a man you've known for — what does your ledger say? Two years of travel, one year of aftermath. Three years, minus the gaps. When I look at you, I see that, and underneath it I see a girl lifting a nobleman's memories in a tavern in Lowmarket, and I know exactly how she held her shoulders so nobody would notice her hands moving. I know what she said to me the second time we met, when she thought she was being clever, and I know that she stole three hours of my doubts that night because she thought they were heavy and I'd be better off, and she was right, and I never told her." His jaw worked. "I have carried both versions of us, Lena. Every day. The ones I remember and the one you let me keep. Do you know what that's like? Being the only person in a country of millions who remembers the way your laugh changed?"

I did not answer, because there was a lump in my throat shaped exactly like an answer, and swallowing around it felt important.

"And now," Sef said, and his voice dropped, went almost shy, which on a man who once hunted thieves for the Gray Choir was a sight I'd have paid good coin for once, "now the wards sing, and there's a line missing, and you're looking at me like that, and I think — I think I know where the line went."

The world got very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"The lock didn't just take," he said. "It *traded*. That's how the First Door works — Ilvane told us, the queen built it that way, nothing given to it stays lost, it goes into the country. Every memory you fed it, it wove into Vael. Into the wards. Into the borders and the stones and the songs mothers sing. You didn't pay the door with your memories, love. You paid the country *with* them. They're everywhere now. That's why Vael came back so rich, so fast. That's why the stars reignite the way they do, why the wards sing louder every spring." He laughed, unsteadily. "You're not gone from this land. You're its *flooring*. You're under everything."

"Then why," I said, and my voice came out thin, "can I not remember any of it?"

"Because the trade goes both ways. What you gave up, you gave up. But—" He turned to face me fully, and took both my hands, and his palms were warm and one of them had the old scar across it, the one from the door, the one I'd kissed a hundred times without knowing why it mattered. "But the memory I kept. Our meeting. I've carried it alone for three years, and I've read it to you from the ledger, and you've nodded politely like I was reading someone else's letters. And the whole time, the melody has been sitting in my head, complete, and the wards have been singing it broken, and I never put the two together until you flinched on that road."

"Sef." My heart was doing something arrhythmic and embarrassing. "What are you telling me?"

"The missing line," he said. "The reason the song sounds wrong to you and right to everyone else. It's not missing from Vael. It's missing from *you*. Because the melody the wards sing — the border song, the oldest one, the one the queen's line used to sing at coronations — your version of it has always been different. You hummed it the day we met. In the tavern. Badly. While stealing from me." His eyes were wet and he was smiling and it was the worst and best thing I had ever seen. "The line the wards are missing is the line you always added. Your variation. The one you made up yourself, because you'd heard the song a hundred times crossing this pass and you thought it needed improving, and you were right, it did, and somehow the door knew, and somehow it wove your version into Vael's borders, and the wards have been waiting ever since for the person who wrote it to come home and claim it."

The pass spun, gently, like a coin settling.

"That's not possible," I said. "I'm a thief. I was a thief. I stole things, I didn't *make* them—"

"You made a line of a song," Sef said, "and it became part of the border of a kingdom. Lena. You of all people should know that's exactly what a thief's work looks like when it's done well."

* * *

We stayed on the mountain until the sun went low and long, turning the snowfields gold, and the ward above us hummed its incomplete circle over and over, and I learned the shape of the gap so thoroughly I could feel it in my teeth.

Because here's what I hadn't said yet, what I'd been circling like a crow over roadkill: I couldn't remember the line either. Not from the ledger — Sef's written record of our meeting mentioned my humming, one line, *she hummed something tuneless and infuriating* — but a written note isn't a melody. And the memory itself, the real one, lived locked in Sef's head, and he could no more hand it to me than I could hand him my sister's face.

Or so I thought.

"There's a way," he said, as the light died. "Ilvane described it once. A memory spoken aloud, in the place it happened, to the person it belongs to — it doesn't transfer, exactly. It *re-seeds*. Like planting. What grows won't be the same as what was lost. But it grows."

"You'd lose it," I said. "Yours. The only piece of me you kept."

He was quiet for a long moment. Below us the valley lights came on one by one, little villages waking into evening, each one humming faintly along with the wards whether they knew it or not, the whole country singing a song with a hole in it, waiting.

"Three years ago I chose to keep something of yours instead of my own," Sef said finally. "It was the easiest choice I ever made, and I've never regretted it, and I've regretted it every single day, and both of those things are true. But I didn't keep it to hoard it. I kept it the way you keep a seed. Not to look at. To plant."

The wind came down the pass one more time, and the ward sang its four rising phrases, and fell into the gap, and waited.

And I found — this is the part I'll put in the new ledger, the first page, ink still wet — that I wasn't afraid. A year ago the gaps in me had been wounds. Six months ago they'd been scars. Tonight, sitting on a frozen mountain inside the borders of a country I'd bought back with everything I had, the gap in the song sounded less like absence and more like a threshold. Like a door. And I have some professional history with doors.

"Hum it," I said. "The line. Sing me the missing piece, Sef, right here, under the ward, and we'll see what grows."

He took a breath. Above us the silver veins in the stone brightened, as if the mountain itself had been holding that breath for three years, waiting for exactly this.

He opened his mouth.

And the ward screamed.

Not hummed — *screamed*, a shriek of silver light and dissonance that sent the ribbons at its base whipping vertical and startled crows out of the pines in a black, cursing cloud, one of which wheeled overhead and shouted, in a voice I knew better than my own forgotten ones:

"GIRL! THE BORDER IN THE EAST IS *BLEEDING*!"
