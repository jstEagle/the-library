## 39. When the Hollow Sings Anew

*Lena*

The wards on Thornfell had always hummed. When I was young and stupid and light-fingered in ways that didn't involve magic, I thought the hum was just the mountains — old stone settling, cold air through the passes, the ordinary music of a hard country. It took me years to learn the truth: the wards were singing a warning. Every year the hum got louder, and every year I ignored it, because that's what thieves do with warnings. We file them under *somebody else's problem* and pocket what isn't nailed down.

Now I stood on the same pass where Brannoc used to bargain me across the border for stolen childhoods and watered ale, and the wards weren't humming anymore.

They were listening.

"It knows we're here," Sef said quietly beside me. He had his ward-sense open — I could tell by the way his eyes went distant, unfocused on the world in front of him and focused on something underneath it, the way a man looks at a river while reading its current. "It's not hunting. It's... waiting."

"Waiting is worse," I said.

"Is it? Hunting is worse. Waiting is polite."

"Sef. It ate an entire kingdom. Politeness is not its defining feature."

He almost smiled. He'd been doing that more lately — almost smiling, then catching himself like a man remembering he'd sworn off drink. The Choir had hollowed a lot out of him, but they'd missed the part of him that wanted to laugh at my jokes, which I considered proof that no silence is thorough enough to kill everything worth keeping.

Below us, in the valley where Vael had been a wound and then a country again, the land lay still in the last violet hour before true dark. The stars were back — had been back since the night at the First Door — but they hung wrong tonight. Not wrong like broken. Wrong like held breath. They didn't wheel. They didn't flicker. They stood in their places like an audience before a performance, and every one of them was looking down at us.

Or rather, down at *it*.

The Hollow came up the pass the way dusk comes: not walking, not flying, simply arriving everywhere at once from the direction where it wasn't yet. I felt it in my teeth first, then in the place behind my ribs where I keep my inventory of myself — the ledger no one else can read, the list of things that make me Lena Underhill. That list shivered. Some entries dimmed. Some brightened, as if the darkness below were turning pages, deciding what it wanted.

And I understood, standing there with my hand on Sef's sleeve, that it did want something. That was the horror and, somehow, the comfort of it. The Hollow had never been mindless. Grief isn't mindless. Grief is the most single-minded thing there is.

"It's going to speak," I said.

"It doesn't speak. It takes."

"Then why hasn't it taken?"

Sef had no answer for that. So we stood, thief and warder, heir and key, two people held together mostly by shared damage and a written ledger, and watched the end of the world walk politely toward us.

* * *

*Sef*

I should begin by admitting something, since honesty has become our household religion and I am a recent convert with the zeal of one.

When the door opened — when Lena poured every warm thing they owned into that hungry lock and spoke the queen's dying word into the wound where our country used to be — my first feeling was not joy. My first feeling was arithmetic. *Will it be enough. Will the price hold. Did I substitute the memory correctly.* Even in the moment the sky reignited and the wards sang themselves back into being, some gray corner of me trained by the Choir was checking the sums.

That is what duty does. It teaches you to count instead of feel.

Lena taught me to feel again the way a bucket of cold water teaches a sleeping man he's on fire. Slowly. Unpleasantly. Thoroughly.

Now, on the pass where I once hunted thieves for creatures made of eaten memory, I stood beside the greatest thief of them all and felt — counted, checked, verified — that I was afraid, and that under the fear, impossibly, I was glad.

Because I had finally understood what the Hollow was.

Ilvane told us, in her library that no longer existed, in words I resisted for months: *it is the country's unspoken grief*. A queen died nameless. A whole nation swallowed its mourning because there was nothing left to mourn *with* — no name, no shape, no story to hang the sorrow on. And grief with nowhere to go doesn't vanish. It densifies. It darkens. It learns to eat, because eating is the only appetite left when every other feeling has been forbidden.

We fed it wrongly for so long. We ran from it, so it chased. We hid memories from it, so it pried. We fought it, so it grew teeth. Everything we gave it, we gave in terror, and terror seasons everything bitter.

But at the First Door, something else happened. Lena gave. Freely. Enormously. Not out of fear — out of love, though Lena would rather eat glass than say the word aloud, so I am saying it here, privately, where no one can make me defend it. And the Hollow *drank* that offering, and for one instant — I felt it through my ward-sense, a shudder through every ward in Vael — for one instant it was not hungry.

It was fed. There is a difference. Hunger is a hole. Being fed is a whole.

I think it has come up this mountain not to take from us.

I think it has come up this mountain to learn how that felt.

* * *

*Lena*

Here's a thief's observation, offered free of charge, which should tell you how far gone I am:

Everything I know about taking, I learned by being taken from.

You learn the weight of a thing when you lift it. You learn what a memory costs by carrying other people's in your skull for coin. You learn that the worst thefts aren't the loud ones — nobody misses a jewel the way they miss their mother's voice. The best thefts are quiet, surgical, deniable. The victim walks around for years with a hole shaped like a wedding day or a sister's face and calls the wind through it *contentment*.

The Hollow is the best thief who ever lived. It took a whole country and most people never noticed the hole. It took my sister's face, the taste of honey, my own name, and I walked around calling the wind through those gaps *fine, I'm fine, everything's fine.*

So when it rose over the lip of the pass and stopped — actually stopped, gathering itself like a performer waiting for the hall to settle — I knew exactly what it was doing.

It was checking whether we'd notice.

"Say your name," Sef murmured. Not a question. A warder's instinct: anchor yourself before the tide comes in.

"Lena Underhill." The words tasted strange, like a coin too long in another man's pocket. "Thief. Sister — probably; I'm told so. Ledger-keeper. Idiot in love with a former enemy, allegedly, according to multiple witnesses."

"I've heard worse self-descriptions."

"You wrote half of them."

"Ledger's accurate," he said, and there it was, the almost-smile, breaking through all the way this time, brief as a struck match.

And then the Hollow spoke.

I want to be precise about this, because precision matters when you're describing the impossible. It didn't use sound. Sound requires air moving in patterns, and the Hollow has never cared for mediums — it goes straight to the source. What happened was that I suddenly *remembered* things it said, the way you remember a dream a heartbeat after waking. It deposited its voice directly into memory, past the ears entirely, which is either the most invasive thing imaginable or the most intimate, and I have decided, after much deliberation, that it's both.

What it remembered into me was this:

*You gave.*

Two words. The whole of its vocabulary, apparently. Two words, and underneath them a vast shifting pressure, like a sea learning to form syllables.

"We gave," Sef answered aloud, because he was braver than me about talking to eldritch silences. Or foolhardier. In my experience the two trade places depending on outcome. "Freely. It changes things. It changed you."

The Hollow turned its attention on him, whatever *turning* means for a creature that is mostly absence wearing faces. And I saw them — the faces. Everyone it had ever eaten, surfacing through the dark like drowned folk rising to a window. Brannoc's heavy brow, his beard frosted with pass-snow. Ilvane's scholar squint. The mother from Ashfall, Maren, whose wedding day Lena bought back at ruinous cost. Hundreds more, thousands, a congregation of the consumed, and every face wore the same expression, which was the expression of people waiting for a song they only half remember.

"It wants—" I started.

*I know,* the Hollow remembered into me, gently, which was somehow the worst part. *I know what I want. I did not always.*

"Grief doesn't," Sef said softly. "Wanting gets buried under surviving. Believe me. I served the Choir for years."

The faces rippled. Somewhere in that vast dark mass, something shifted its grip — the way a fiddler shifts fingers before a new tune.

*Sing for me,* the Hollow said. *No one has ever sung for me. They screamed. They fled. They forgot. You gave. Now sing.*

And I, Lena Underhill, who had faced down Gray Choir hunters, river-wights, a talking crow with delusions of majesty, and the entire concept of personal honesty, felt my knees go weak at the sheer naked need in that voice.

It wasn't asking to eat.

It was asking to be included.

* * *

*Sef*

My mother — the queen, the nameless dead woman whose last word Lena carried across a broken country in her skull — my mother died without a song. Ossifrage told us that. She died in a vault-dark room with her secret safe and her kingdom already forgetting her, and the only voice at her passing was the small dry click of a memory being hidden away for a stranger to steal decades later.

A queen should have a dirge. A country should have keeners at the door, women paid in bread and gin to wail your name into the hills until the hills learn it. Vael had customs for death that went back further than the wards. We abandoned them all for her, because we didn't know she was dying, because her name was already locked away, because grief unspoken is grief unmarked.

I think the Hollow is what became of her unmarked mourning. I think it has spent however many years being the loudest silence in creation, and what it has wanted, all along, beneath the hunger, beneath the terrible appetite, is what every mourner wants:

witness.

So when it asked us to sing, I did not reach for a weapon. I reached for a memory.

I have few enough to spare — I renounced the Choir, poured my blood into the First Door, and handed my hoard to the lock alongside Lena's, keeping back only one stolen substitution, one meeting-on-a-road that belongs to me now even though it happened to both of us. But the ledger habit dies hard. You keep records. You keep *some*thing. And among the things I kept was a fragment I never showed Lena: the melody our mother used to hum in the vault corridors, four notes, descending, the beginning of a lullaby whose ending she died before teaching anyone.

Four notes. That's all I have of her voice.

I gave them to the mountain.

I hummed them, thin and cracked, into the cold air of Thornfell pass, and the wards caught the notes and carried them — I felt them go, running the border-stones like fire along a fuse, north and south, coast to coast, every standing stone in Vael picking up my mother's four descending notes and passing them on.

And the Hollow began to shake.

Not the shaking of rage. I know its rage; I've stood inside it. This was the shaking of a held breath released. Of a knot working loose. Of something enormous trying to do something it had forgotten how to do, the way a man who hasn't wept in twenty years shakes the first time someone touches him kindly.

Beside me, Lena drew breath and joined in.

Of course they did. My thief, my empty-handed darling, who gave everything at the door and walked out blank and started over from nothing, who relearned their own name from a ledger written in my handwriting — of course they couldn't carry a tune in a bucket. They sang anyway. Flat, wavering, stubborn, four notes plus whatever the four notes reminded them of, which was apparently nothing, since they'd sold their musical memories to a lock, but they sang on pure spite and tenderness in roughly equal measure, and I fell in love for approximately the ninth time, which I mention only for completeness.

Then the mountain joined.

* * *

*Lena*

Here's what I cannot explain and will spend the rest of my life failing to explain:

Every ward in Vael began to sing, and every song it sang was a memory.

Not mine. Not Sef's. *Everyone's.* The wards had been drinking the country for generations — every blessing whispered at a threshold, every promise made under a lintel, every lullaby sung within sight of a warding-stone had soaked into them, layer under layer, a nation's whole domestic history pressed into stone like flowers in a book. The Hollow had been starving at the borders of all that richness, chewing on scraps, growing monstrous on famine.

Now the wards opened the book.

The pass filled with voices. Not ghosts — I know ghosts, I've been haunted by a professional — these were *memories*, freed from the stones the way light is freed from a struck match. A miller's wife singing to dough. Two boys swearing friendship over a knife's edge. An old man counting the names of his grandchildren, all forty-one, none forgotten. A girl at a window practicing a love confession and getting it wrong eleven times, and the eleventh wrong version was the sweetest thing I heard all night. Brannoc's laugh — his actual laugh, the great bark of it — rolling down from a high stone where he'd once sat trading passage for pilfered childhoods, and I stopped singing to listen to it, and Sef put his hand on my shoulder and we listened together, and it was grief, yes, and it was also joy, and I finally understood that those were never opposites. They're harmonies. One holds the note low so the other can climb.

And the Hollow — the vast, face-wearing, kingdom-eating silence that had chased us across a erased map — the Hollow *opened*.

I don't mean it dissolved. Nothing that big dissolves; it transforms, the way a storm transforms into rain, the way grief transforms — given witness, given voice, given time — into mourning, and mourning into memory, and memory into the strange sturdy warmth you carry instead of the person you lost. The faces in it didn't vanish. They settled. Brannoc's brow smoothed into rest. Ilvane's squint softened into something like scholarly satisfaction, which was very Ilvane. Thousands of faces, easing out of the dark one by one, each absorbed upward into the singing wards, each becoming part of the country's chorus instead of its swallowed silence.

The Hollow had been Vael's grief with nowhere to go.

Now Vael was giving it somewhere to go. Note by note. Voice by voice. Every keener at every door, every wail in every hill, all the mourning we'd failed to do for a nameless queen and a vanished country and a stolen century, done at last, done loudly, done *together*, rising off the mountains in waves.

The stars began to wheel.

All night they had stood frozen, holding their breath, and now, as the chorus swelled, the sky resumed its ancient rotation, slow and stately, the constellations of Vael — the Miller, the Knife, the Forty-One Grandchildren, I swear that one's real — turning overhead like a vault-door swinging open onto treasure.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. I laughed until I cried, which I'm told is the traditional method, and Sef held my hand through both, and above us the last of the Hollow poured itself into the sky like smoke finding a chimney, and what remained on the pass was just — air. Ordinary, cold, singing mountain air, smelling of pine and snowmelt and the faint copper tang of wards well-fed.

"It's gone," I said, when I could talk.

"No," Sef said. His ward-sense was wide open; I watched his eyes track something vast and gentle moving under the skin of the world. "It's *everywhere*. It's in the wards now. In the chorus. It'll always be there, under every song — the grief in the harmony. That's not a flaw in the music, Lena. That's what makes it music instead of noise."

"Were you always this wise, or is it the near-death experiences?"

"The Choir beat most of the wisdom out of me. You put it back crooked."

"That sounds like us."

Below us, in the valley, lights were kindling — real lights, lamplit windows, the whole reborn country waking to a sky that moved again. Somewhere down there Ossifrage was no doubt taking credit. Somewhere down there Maren was sleeping beside a husband she'd nearly sold, and a new ledger sat blank on a desk in a guildhouse that didn't officially exist yet.

Sef hummed the four notes. Descending. Our mother's fragment, the lullaby with no ending.

And because the wards were awake, and the chorus was listening, and the whole country's grief had finally learned to sing — the mountain answered.

New notes. Rising. Four of them, then eight, then a full phrase, unfolding out of the stones like something that had been folded away for safekeeping a very long time ago: the *ending* of the lullaby. The verse our mother died before teaching anyone, kept all these years in the wards she'd woven, waiting for someone to hum the question so it could give the answer.

Sef went absolutely still. I watched tears take him by surprise, a grown warder, heir of Vael, ambushed on his own border by a bedtime song.

"What is it?" I asked, though I knew.

"It's the rest of it," he said. "She finished it. She finished it and hid the ending in the wards." He laughed, wet and wondering. "She knew she was going to die nameless. She saved the ending anyway. For whoever asked."

Above us the stars wheeled. Below us the valley glittered. Inside us — in the gaps, in the ledger-pages torn out and the ones never written — something settled into place that I don't have a word for yet. I'm a thief. I'll find one. I steal words the same as anything.

"Come on," I said at last, tugging his sleeve toward the downward path, toward the lights, toward whatever a country owes the two idiots who rebuilt it. "If we're late for the homecoming feast, Ossifrage will give the toast, and none of us will emotionally recover."

But at the turn of the path, where the first warding-stone stood humming its new old song, Sef stopped, and looked back once at the wheeling sky, and said the thing I would write in the ledger the next morning, in the entry I still think of as the first true page:

"Listen. It's not silent anymore. It never has to be silent again."

And the mountain sang us home.
