## 40. A Ledger With Blank Pages

The ledger they give me in the restored court of Vael is bound in white calfskin, which is either very expensive or very optimistic. The pages are thick and cream-colored, ruled in faint silver ink, and there are — I count twice to be sure — three hundred of them. Three hundred blank pages.

"Symbolic," the court bookbinder says, with the pride of a man who has never had to fence stolen goods in his life. "A new beginning."

"It's a lot of paper," I say, because I have always been bad at accepting gifts gracefully, and because if I say anything truer I will start crying in front of a bookbinder, and I did enough crying last month to last a lifetime. Possibly several lifetimes. I wouldn't know. I wasn't there for most of mine.

He wraps it in oilcloth anyway, unoffended, and Sef carries it out of the city under his arm like a man carrying something far more dangerous than paper, which is accurate, because I intend to fill it with things.

* * *

Here is what I know about ledgers, and I knew it before I knew my own sister's face, before I knew the taste of honey or the sound of Brannoc's laugh or — for one long gray stretch — even my own name:

A thief's ledger is a list of debts. What was taken. From whom. For how much. It's an accounting of absence. You write down what's missing so you can pretend that writing it down is the same as keeping it. I kept ledgers for eleven years. Eleven years of columns: *one childhood, mint seller's son, paid in copper. One first kiss, Lord Aldery's heir, unpaid — kept too long, returned damaged.* I told myself the rules made me honest. Never keep what you steal. Always leave something behind in its place, even if it's only an empty hour. Rules are just theft with good posture.

But here is what I know now, standing on the Thornfell pass with wind coming off the mountains and the wards singing overhead like a choir that finally learned the words:

An accounting of absence is still an accounting. And I am done with absences.

The new ledger starts today. And the first entry will not be what was taken from me, or by me, or for me. It will be what comes next.

* * *

It takes me three days to work up the nerve to touch pen to page, which Sef finds hilarious.

"You stole the true name of a kingdom," he says, watching me hover over the first blank sheet like it might bite. "You walked into the Hollow wearing nothing but stubbornness. You're afraid of a notebook."

"The Hollow never asked me to be legible," I say. "Also, shut up."

We're in the little house on the pass — not the smuggler camp; Brannoc's old waystation, rebuilt, the stone chimney re-stacked by hand by two people who are terrible at masonry and worse at admitting it. The roof leaks in exactly one spot, over the chair Sef likes, which I consider the house's single act of good judgment. Outside, the wards hum their melody, and when the wind hits them right, there's a line missing from the song, and Sef fills it in without thinking, low under his breath, and every time he does it something in my chest turns over like a key in a lock I'd forgotten I owned.

That's entry material. That's the whole point of this stupid beautiful book.

I dip the pen. My hand shakes. I let it.

*Entry the First,* I write. *Today Sef hummed the missing line of the ward-song while fixing the roof. He doesn't know he does it. He thinks I don't notice. I notice everything. I'm a thief.*

Then, after a moment, underneath: *Note: he also sings badly. Off-key in a way that suggests the Choir took his sense of pitch along with his doubts. Keep this. It's important.*

"What does it say?" he asks, craning.

"State secrets."

"I'm the heir to the throne."

"Which is why they're state secrets."

He laughs — really laughs, the kind that took months to come back to him, that used to surface rarely and sideways, like a fish checking whether the water was safe — and goes back to arguing with the chimney, and I sit there looking at the first words in a book that owes no debts to anyone, and I think: *this. This is what we'll make.*

* * *

The guild hall in Cael Morath has a new sign now. It took some debate. The old guilds of Vael had names like *The Silver Hand* and *The Quiet Order*, all of which sounded, in retrospect, like exactly the kind of places that would end up robbing royal vaults and selling childhoods by the ounce. We wanted something different. Something that said what we actually were.

The sign reads: **THE LEDGERHOUSE — MEMORIES KEPT, GIVEN, AND RETURNED. NO COIN REQUIRED.**

"No coin required" nearly bankrupted us in the first week, until Ilvane — who haunts the library that exists again, solid shelves and living scholars and her ghost drifting between them like a bookmark nobody ordered — pointed out that people don't trust free things, they trust *fair* things. So now there's a barter board by the door. A memory given earns a memory taken, if you want one, or bread, or a bed, or nothing at all. A memory returned to you that you thought was gone forever costs whatever you think it's worth, and if your answer is *nothing, it's priceless*, then nothing it is.

The mother from Ashfall — Maren, her name is Maren, I wrote it down the day she got her wedding day back and wept into my shoulder hard enough to crack a rib, possibly literally — works the front desk now. She's better at this than I am. She remembers being robbed. She remembers what the wanting feels like from both sides.

"Lena," she said to me yesterday, in her patient desk-clerk voice, "there's a boy here who wants to sell his grief. His brother died in the Hollow years. He wants it cut out so he can sleep."

"And?"

"And I told him we don't do that anymore." She looked at me steadily. "But he'll be back tomorrow, and the day after, and someday he's going to beg, and I need to know — what's the rule? What do I say?"

I thought about it. I thought about the old rules, the ones with the good posture, and about Mother Vesper, who offered me everything back if only I'd hand over the name, and how seductive that offer was right up until I understood it came with an empty country attached. Grief taken out isn't grief gone. It's grief orphaned. It wanders. It finds other things to eat.

I thought about the Hollow itself — the vast silent thing wearing ten thousand faces — and how, in the end, at the border where the stars wheel overhead, it didn't die. It *changed*. Fed honestly, at last, on a kingdom's grief spoken aloud instead of swallowed, it opened its mouth and sang. All that devouring silence turned into chorus. Ilvane says grief is just love with nowhere to go. Maybe the Hollow was a country's love with nowhere to go, for sixty years, screaming in a register nobody could hear.

"We tell him grief isn't a debt," I said. "We tell him it's a payment already made. And we offer him a bed and someone to sit with him while he can't sleep, and if he still wants it cut out after that —" I exhaled. "Then we bring him to me. And I talk to him. Not take. Talk."

Maren wrote it down. In her own ledger. Everyone has one now; it's catching, like kindness.

*Entry the Ninth:* *Learned today that the hardest part of giving back what you steal is that some people want to stay robbed. You cannot return a gift to someone determined to refuse delivery. Sit with them instead. Note: sitting is harder than stealing. Practice.*

* * *

Sister found me at the market last week. My sister. Ilse. Alive — was alive all along, on the far side of the border when Vael vanished, walking around in a world that had forgotten her homeland, carrying memories of me that I couldn't answer because I didn't have them anymore.

She stood in front of me in the square, and I knew her — Sef had read me the ledger entries enough times that I knew her the way you know a place from maps, from descriptions, from other people's postcards. Brown hair going gray early. A scar on her chin from falling off a wall I dared her up. A habit of crossing her arms when she's about to say something unforgivable.

"You're smaller than I remember," she said.

"You're taller than I remember," I said. "Because I don't remember you."

And she laughed, this wet broken laugh, and grabbed me, and held on, and said into my hair: "You absolute idiot. You gave up *everything*, and the first thing you do is apologize for not remembering my birthday."

"It's in the ledger," I mumbled into her shoulder. "Fourth month, the ninth. Sef keeps a copy. There's also a note that you once set fire to a barn."

"That was *you*."

"According to the primary sources, it was both of us, and I took the blame as a professional courtesy."

Some things came back after the door opened — trickles, echoes, things the country remembered on my behalf and pressed into my hands like lost luggage. But most of it stayed gone. My dearest memories fed the lock, and locks keep what they're fed. Some days that's a wound. Most days, lately, it's just weather. Ilse is rebuilding me from the outside in, one anecdote at a time, and half of them are lies, and I've stopped checking, because the lies are *better*, and because a sister's job is apparently to curate you, and hers has excellent taste.

*Entry the Twelfth:* *Ilse says I once traded our neighbor's entire flock of geese for a "magic rock," age seven, and cried for a week when it turned out to be a rock. This is unverifiable. I am choosing to believe it. I was clearly magnificent.*

* * *

Brannoc's cairn stands on the high shoulder of the pass, under the singing stones, where he made his stand. The stone we carved is still blank. We talked about adding his name. We decided against it, and I want to explain why, because it matters, because it's the whole philosophy of the Ledgerhouse carved in granite:

Everyone the Hollow took, everyone whose names went with them, everyone erased — the blank stone stands for all of them, not none of them. Anyone who walks past can see their own losses in it. Brannoc would have hated being special. He'd have wanted to be *useful*. So the cairn stays anonymous, and travelers leave things on it — a button, a ribbon, once an entire roasted chicken, which Ossifrage handled with great dignity and zero remorse.

Ossifrage. Yes, the crow survived, wounded wing and all, and has appointed himself Auditor of the Ledgerhouse, a position nobody created and nobody can abolish. He sits on the barter board and evaluates transactions with one cold black eye.

"The widow's offering is sentimental but valid," he croaked this morning. "The merchant attempting to purchase his rival's courage is denied. Courage purchased secondhand curdles. I have seen it. I was there for the reign of three kings and I will be here for yours, and I remember *everything*, girl, including the exact quality of your hesitation outside the queen's vault, which was cowardly, and the fact that you went in anyway, which was not."

"Thank you, Ossifrage."

"Do not thank me. Thank me by not wasting it." A pause. The feathers on his bad wing ruffled in the wind. "The queen would be pleased. I am not pleased. I am a crow. But she would be pleased."

From him, that's a knighthood.

* * *

Tonight the house is quiet. Ilse went home to her own roof hours ago. Sef is asleep in the leaky chair, which he insists is comfortable, which it is not, which is why I let him have it. The wards sing outside, complete now — he taught the missing line back to them weeks ago, note by patient note, the way you'd teach a song to a child, and now the mountains carry it and won't lose it again. Somewhere below, in the dark, the Hollow-turned-chorus is singing too, far off and vast and no longer hungry. When the wind shifts you can hear it harmonize with the wards. Two songs that used to be enemies. Grief transmuted. Love with somewhere to go, finally, at last.

I light the lamp and open the white calfskin ledger to the next clean page.

Three hundred pages. I've used thirteen. I used to worry about running out of time. Now I worry about running out of *paper*, which is the correct order of things.

*Entry the Fourteenth,* I write. *Tonight the stars were out over Thornfell, all of them, in their old positions, wheeling like they never left. Sef fell asleep mid-sentence telling me about the coronation — he keeps trying to make me attend court functions and I keep explaining that I am constitutionally incapable of standing in a room full of nobles without lifting something, and that this is now a matter of professional reputation. Tomorrow: the Ledgerhouse opens the branch in Ashfall. Maren's idea. She says the refugees coming home deserve a place to trade their roads for their futures. I agreed before she finished asking, which she says is growth.*

My pen hovers. The lamp flickers. Down the mountain, faint, the chorus swells and settles like breathing.

And here is the truth I've been circling for fourteen entries, the one this whole book is secretly about, so I may as well write it plain:

I spent eleven years taking things from sleeping strangers because I couldn't bear the weight of my own empty hands. Every stolen kiss, every borrowed childhood — I told myself it was commerce, craft, survival. It was hunger. I stole moments because I had none worth keeping, and the rule against keeping them meant I never had to find out what I was missing.

Now the book in front of me isn't a record of loss. It's a plan. Entry by entry, page by page: a roof fixed badly, a sister recovered loudly, a mother's wedding day returned, a boy's grief sat-with rather than stolen, a crow's terrible blessing, a line of song taught back to a mountain. Nothing here was taken. Everything here was made. Chosen. Freely, clumsily, on purpose.

There's a craft to it, still. There's a knack. Slipping through a sleeping mind and finding the one warm thing worth carrying out — I know how to do that better than anyone alive. I've simply changed direction. Instead of lifting the light out of other people's lives, I've learned to carry it in.

Tomorrow I'll walk down into the valley, into the country I bought back with everything I had, and I'll knock on the first door with a light burning behind it. Someone inside will be asleep beside their own small hoard of days — ordinary, unguarded, precious. And I'll wake them gently, and show them my empty hands, and ask them the only question that matters now:

*What would you like to keep?*

They'll tell me. And I'll write it down.

And this time, I promise you — I swear it on the blank pages ahead —

I'm stealing nothing. I'm only making sure it stays.
