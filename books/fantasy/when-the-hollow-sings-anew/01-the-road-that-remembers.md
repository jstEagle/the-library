## 1. The Road That Remembers

The mountain lion had been following them since dawn, and Ansel Quintana was getting tired of pretending not to notice.

"Big cat on our left," she said casually, adjusting the strap of her pack as the caravan picked its way along the switchback above Ravenfall Gorge. "Maybe two hundred yards back. Nobody make sudden movements."

Brannic, who owned the caravan and most of its cargo and exactly none of its sense, went pale in a way that suggested he was about to do something sudden. He was a barrel of a man with a merchant's smile and a smuggler's ledger, and he had hired Ansel three days ago in Cinderhatch because she came cheap and came recommended, which in his experience never happened at the same time.

"How big?" he asked.

"Big enough."

"Should we — should I get the crossbows?"

"You should absolutely not get the crossbows." Ansel kept her voice light, bored even, the voice of a woman discussing weather. "It's a she, she's got two cubs denned up near the high pass, and she's not hunting us. She's curious. There's a difference, and it matters mostly to people who shoot first."

"And how," said Brannic slowly, "do you know it's a *she*?"

Ansel considered her options. The truth — *because I asked her* — had gotten her mother burned to ash in a market square when Ansel was nine years old, so that one stayed where it lived, folded small and buried deep. The lie was easier.

"Ears," she said. "Toms carry their ears different. Any tracker knows that."

Which was nonsense, and Brannic probably knew it was nonsense, but nonsense delivered confidently is the native language of commerce, so he grunted and let it go. Behind him, the other six members of the caravan exchanged the look they always exchanged when Ansel did something quietly impossible. She'd been collecting that look for fifteen years, from a dozen crews, and she had long ago stopped hoping anyone would stop giving it to her.

*Good tracker,* they called her. Best in the Thornfells, some said, and they meant it as praise.

It felt more like a cage with very polite bars.

* * *

The lion's name — the name she used for herself, anyway, in the way lions have names, all weight and territory — translated roughly to *Drought-Snow-That-Ends*, or possibly *The-Long-Hunger-Before-Green*. Beast-speech didn't sit neatly in human mouths, and Ansel had learned early that translation was less about words than about shape. You caught the shape of a thought and pressed it flat, and something was always lost, like pressing a flower in a book.

*You are loud,* Drought-Snow observed from the rocks above, her thought arriving in Ansel's mind the way sunlight arrives through water: warm, moving, slightly bent. *Your herd stinks of fear and salt-pork.*

*They're smugglers,* Ansel answered silently, keeping her eyes on the trail. Talking while walking took practice; she'd bitten through her own cheek twice as a girl before mastering it. *Fear is their perfume. They think it makes them mysterious.*

*And you? What do you smell of?*

*Lye soap and bad decisions.*

The lion made a sound that was almost a laugh — a huff of breath, a flicker of amber amusement — and Ansel felt it land somewhere behind her ribs, where all the good feelings went to be rationed carefully and never shown.

Here was the arithmetic of her life, worked out over years and paid for in vigilance: she could speak with beasts, and speaking with beasts was death by fire, and therefore she would speak with beasts only in the privacy of her own skull, only ever in whispers no one else could hear, only ever to accomplish small, deniable mercies. A wolf warned off a cliff edge. A mule soothed through a rockslide. A lion persuaded, gently, over the course of a morning, that this particular string of humans smelled like trouble and should be escorted rather than eaten.

*What will you give me,* Drought-Snow asked, *for my road?*

This was the part outsiders never understood about beast-bargains. They weren't commands. Ansel couldn't *make* a lion do anything any more than a lion could make her fly off a cliff by wanting it hard enough. It was trade, always trade, and the currency was stranger than coin.

"That depends," Ansel murmured aloud, too low for Brannic to hear, letting her lips barely move. "What's your price?"

*The white goat,* said the lion. *The one at the back of your herd who bites.*

"The goat bites because he's frightened."

*He will bite me if I eat him, and I intend to eat him. These are separate concerns.*

"He's Brannic's goat. He carries the good brandy."

*Then I will eat him last and savor the brandy fumes.* A pause, a ripple of something like genuine consideration. *Very well. Not the goat. The dried fish in the third pack from the left. Two hands' worth. And the tall one with the red beard — tell him to stop throwing stones at the pikas. They are children. He is frightening them.*

Ansel glanced back at Tallis, the tall one with the red beard, currently idly chuckling pebbles at a scree slope with the aimless cruelty of a bored man. "Tallis," she called. "Quit lobbing rocks. You'll bring down a slide."

Tallis shrugged and stopped, because Ansel had a way of being obeyed without quite being argued with, another small inheritance she tried not to examine too closely.

"Fish," she told the lion. "Two hands' worth, left at the split cedar tonight. And you take the eastern shelf instead of the gorge floor, so my clients don't see you and do something stupid."

*The eastern shelf is windy.*

"You're a quarter ton of muscle with claws like sickles. Walk it off."

Another huff, warmer this time. *Small speaker,* the lion said, using the name the beasts had given her mother, and then her mother's mother, and probably some great-great-aunt nobody had warned Ansel about — *you bargain like your kind always does. Too many words for too little meat.*

"And yet here we are, making deals." Ansel smiled at the trail, careful not to smile at anything with eyes. "Pleasure doing business."

*Until the next hunger,* the lion replied, which was either a farewell or a threat or, in lion, both.

* * *

They made the split cedar by late afternoon, and Ansel left the fish wrapped in oilcloth at its roots while the others set camp. It was a good campsite — she'd chosen it partly for the sightlines and partly because a badger she knew had confirmed the springs ran clean, which was the sort of advantage you didn't put on any résumé.

"You always know these camps," Brannic said that evening, tearing bread with the concentration of a man who trusted bread more than people. "Every trip. Never a bad spring, never a washed-out ford. Fifteen years I've run these roads, and I've never once seen you guess wrong."

"Guessing right is what you pay me for."

"I pay you to walk in front and look dangerous. The guessing's free." He chewed, watching her across the fire with eyes that were shrewder than his belly let on. "My grandfather said there were families in the Thornfells who could talk to animals. Before the purges. He said the mountains used to be full of them."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, an owl asked its question and got no answer.

"Folk say a lot of things," Ansel said.

"Mm," said Brannic, and let it go, which was the most frightening thing he could have done.

She slept badly, as she always did on the first night of a run, one ear cocked toward the dark where the lion's thoughts moved like a lantern through trees. This was the part nobody warned you about, having a gift: not the danger of it, but the loneliness. Every secret is a wall, and hers was load-bearing. She had built her whole life against it — the gruff manner, the deliberate ordinariness, the careful performance of a woman whose talents began and ended with reading trails. And she was *good* at it. Gods help her, she was so good at it that sometimes she fooled even herself, for whole hours at a stretch, into believing she was just a tracker, just a guide, just a woman with good boots and better instincts.

Then a lion would ask her opinion about goats, and the wall would remind her it was holding up a roof.

* * *

Morning brought fog, the thick grey kind that ate sound, and with it the day's real work: the Hollow Way, a smuggler's path that threaded between the ward-stones marking the Cinderhatch border. The wards were old — older than the free cities, older than the kingdoms that claimed them, older than any story Brannic told after his third cup. They stood every half-mile along the frontier like grey teeth, each one taller than a man and carved with script nobody living could read, humming with a magic so constant that nobody heard it anymore. Like a river's sound. Like home.

Ansel had been hearing them her entire life.

They hummed, and she walked beside them, and that was simply the texture of the world, the way stone was hard and winters were long. Her mother had once tried to explain what the wards actually *did* — protection, sealing, a pact written in a hand older than kings — but young Ansel had mostly remembered how her mother's face went careful when she said the word *pact*, the same carefulness she used around the word *daughter*.

Today, coming down through the fog toward the border stones, Ansel noticed the humming had changed.

Not gone. Changed. Thinner. The way a bell rings differently when it's cracked — the same note, technically, but with a hollowness underneath, a wobble that lived in the teeth rather than the ear. She stopped at the third ward-stone and laid her palm flat against it, a habit she usually avoided because touching wards made the hair on her arms stand up and attracted questions.

The stone was cold. Wards weren't supposed to be cold.

"Hold up," she called back. "Water break."

They broke, gratefully, badly — Tallis immediately started a debate about whether pika meat counted as food or punishment — and Ansel knelt by the ward-stone and pressed her hand to it and listened with the other listening, the deep one, the one she kept locked.

The hum stuttered.

Once. Twice. Like a heart skipping beats. Then it steadied, but wrong, thinner than yesterday, and Ansel sat back on her heels with her stomach doing something slow and cold that had nothing to do with the altitude.

Fifteen years on these roads. The wards had hummed steady every day of them, through storms and wars and one memorable incident involving a runaway hay cart. Whatever made a ward *fade* —

Her mother had known. Ansel carried the memory like a splinter: her mother, three nights before the pyre, standing at the window with her head tilted, listening, saying to no one, *"Something's drinking."*

Ansel had assumed she meant the wine.

"Quintana!" Brannic's voice. "We moving?"

She stood, brushed off her knees, and put the careful ordinariness back on like a coat. "Moving. Fog'll lift by midmorning, and we want the Hollow Way done before the sun hits the scree."

They moved. But as they wound down through the last folds of the mountain toward Cinderhatch's smoke-stained rooftops, Ansel found herself counting the ward-stones as they passed, and listening, and counting again.

Every one of them was thinner than the last.

*Something's drinking,* she thought, and told herself it was nothing, and knew she was lying, and hated being right in advance — a skill, like everything else about her life, that she'd taught herself alone.

Behind them, far up the slope, Drought-Snow watched the little caravan descend, and did not follow, and did not sing, and the mountain held its breath.
