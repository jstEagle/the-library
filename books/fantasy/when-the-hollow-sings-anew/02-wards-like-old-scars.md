## 2. Wards Like Old Scars

Cinderhatch smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and the particular despair of a border town that had never quite decided whether it was a town or an argument. Ansel had always found it comforting. At least here, everyone was honest about being miserable.

The caravan rolled in at dusk, six wagons heavy with salted fish and unmarked crates that Ansel made a point of not describing too precisely in her own thoughts. Good trackers kept their eyes open. Better ones kept their mouths shut. The best ones — like her — did both while looking bored.

"Ansel!" Bram Halloway waved from the lead wagon, his beard frosted with trail dust. "You see the gate?"

"I see it."

"What do you think?"

She thought it looked wrong. That was the trouble with Cinderhatch's old wards: they were supposed to be invisible. They were supposed to be *nothing*, a hum beneath the world so constant you stopped hearing it the way you stopped hearing your own heartbeat. Generations of ward-keepers had laid them down along the Thornfell passes, thread after thread of quiet power, and for two hundred years they'd held like scar tissue holds skin — ugly, unnoticed, doing its work.

But tonight, riding under the eastern arch, Ansel heard them. Not as light or color, the way she sometimes felt the mountain's larger moods. As sound. A bell, struck once, long ago, still ringing — but fading. Each pass through the ward-line felt thinner than the last. Like walking through a cobweb where there used to be a wall.

"Looks fine," she said.

"You're lying," said Pip, the youngest of the Halloway crew, hanging off the wagon's tailboard with the boneless ease of a boy who hadn't yet learned that the world could drop him. "Your face does a thing when you lie. Right side of your mouth goes up first, like you're about to tell a joke you're too polite to tell."

Ansel considered her options. She could deny it, which would only confirm it. She could ruffle his hair, which he hated now that he was fourteen and officially ancient.

"The gate looks fine," she said, "and the ale inside it will look even better. Go count your coin before the tavern keeper counts it for you."

Pip went, satisfied, because fourteen-year-olds were mostly satisfied by being dismissed with affection. Ansel watched him go and let herself look back at the arch once, properly.

The ward-stones set into it were old gray granite, worn smooth by weather and the shoulders of a thousand smuggled bales of wool. Nothing visible was wrong. But when she laid her palm flat against one — casually, the way a traveler steadies herself on a gate, nothing more — the stone answered her touch with a vibration she felt in her teeth. A hum, yes. But a dying one. The way a struck bell doesn't stop all at once; it thins and thins until your ear can't say exactly when it ended, only that the silence arrived without knocking.

*Something's drinking,* she thought, and then immediately: *don't think things like that, people get burned for thinking things like that.*

Her mother had gotten burned for considerably less than thinking.

* * *

The Drowned Ox was full, loud, and serving something it called beer, which Ansel drank anyway because standards were for people who weren't paid on delivery. The caravan took over three tables near the fire, and for an hour the world reduced itself to what worlds should be: hot food, bad wine, Pip losing dice to Bram's wife Sorrel, who cheated with such cheerful transparency that nobody minded.

Ansel sat near the window with her back to the wall — habits, habits — and tried not to listen to the wards.

It didn't work. It was like trying not to listen to a dripping tap. Every few minutes the hum stuttered, a skipped beat, and each skip landed somewhere behind her ribs. Twice she caught herself holding her breath until it resumed, and both times she made herself exhale slowly and drink her terrible beer and be a normal woman having a normal evening in a normal town.

"You're doing the thing again," Sorrel said, dropping into the seat across from her. Sorrel had the flat brown eyes of a woman who had raised four sons and outlived two husbands' business ventures, and missed nothing.

"What thing?"

"The listening thing. You do it in the mountains too. Stop mid-stride and go still, like a deer deciding whether to bolt." Sorrel tore a hunk of bread and pushed half across the table, which was her way of feeding people whether they wanted it or not. "Whatever you're listening for, if it shows up, I want to know before the horses do."

"It won't show up," Ansel said. "It's probably nothing. Old stones settle. Cold nights make things creak."

"Mm," said Sorrel, in the tone of a woman filing a statement away for later prosecution.

That was when the shouting started from the corner table nearest the hearth — a voice like a rusted hinge arguing with the whole room.

"—laugh! Go on, laugh, all of you, drink your swill and laugh at Odile Ferran, mad old Ferran—"

"Oh no," said the tavern keeper, appearing at their table with a fresh pitcher and the weary expression of a man whose establishment hosted a regular disaster. "Ignore her. She'll tire herself out."

The woman in question stood on a chair, which was ambitious, given that standing seemed to require strategic use of the chair back, the wall, and what appeared to be pure spite. She was perhaps fifty, gray-haired, wearing a ward-keeper's coat that had once been formal blue and was now mostly stains and memory. In her fist she brandished an iron rod tipped with a dull crystal — a keeper's probe, Ansel realized, though she'd never seen one carried like a club.

"The wards are *dying*," the woman announced to the room. "Not weakening. Not fraying. *Dying.* And none of you can hear it because none of you ever listened!"

"We listened," someone called back. "We heard 'buy me another drink.'"

"Buy yourself a grave, Toma, that's what's coming!" She jabbed the rod toward the ceiling, toward the mountain beyond it. "I've kept the Cinderhatch line for thirty years. Thirty! My father before me, his mother before him. We *know* these wards the way you know your own teeth. And this season—" her voice cracked, dropped, became almost private, almost frightened, and somehow that was worse than the shouting, "—this season they've gone thin. I walk the line and it's like touching an old wound. There's something *under* us, drinking. Something down in the deep places with its mouth on the roots of the world, pulling, pulling, patient as winter—"

"Veyra Marchen's people say the wards are fine," offered a merchant near the door, in the careful voice of a man hedging bets.

"Marchen's people," Odile Ferran spat, "would tell you the sun rises in the west if she paid them enough. Marchen's people are why the high keeps have been stripped of keepers — oh yes, don't look shocked, everyone knows it, half the ward-lines from Thornfell to the coast are tended by boys with six weeks of training and a manual. Because the old keepers wouldn't sign her conscription oaths. Wouldn't turn the wards into *leashes*." She laughed, a horrible bright sound. "A ward is meant to hold things *out*. You don't ask it to hold people *in*. That's not what it's for. That's not what any of it was for—"

Two large men — the tavern keeper's cousins, presumably, or hired patience — took her gently by the arms and steered her, still declaiming, out into the night. The door banged shut. The noise of the room rebuilt itself around the hole she'd left, conversations resuming like water closing over a stone.

But Ansel sat very still, her hand flat on the tabletop, feeling through the wood, through the floor, through forty feet of rock, that thinning bell-note hum.

*A ward is meant to hold things out.*

And something underneath was drinking it dry.

* * *

She told herself she was going upstairs to sleep. She even got as far as the stairs.

Then she turned around, told Sorrel she needed air, and slipped out into the cold, because apparently she was the kind of fool who went looking for drunk ward-keepers in dark streets. Her mother would have had opinions about that. Her mother had had opinions about everything, right up until the bonfire.

Odile Ferran wasn't hard to find. She was sitting on the steps of the ward-house at the end of the lane — a squat stone building with the old sigil of the Keeper's Guild carved above the door, half its crystal windows boarded over. The guild had been rich once. Now it was one woman, a building, and whatever was left in the cellar.

"Ferran," Ansel said.

"If you've come to tell me I'm drunk, I know." The woman didn't look up. "If you've come to tell me I'm wrong, save it. If you've come to tell me I'm right—" she finally raised her head, and her eyes were red-rimmed and startlingly clear, the way drunk eyes sometimes go clear at the exact center of the storm, "—then sit down, because nobody has come to tell me that in eleven years."

Ansel sat. The step was cold through her coat. Above them, the ward-line ran along the ridgeline east of town, marked by standing stones that caught the moonlight like broken teeth.

"Your probe," Ansel said. "The crystal. It reads resonance, doesn't it? Ward-strength?"

Odile squinted at her. "Trackers don't usually ask about probes."

"This tracker grew up in the foothills. Ward-keepers came through every spring, checking the shepherd lines. I watched them work." All true. None of it the truth. Ansel was getting very good at sentences like that, and she hated herself a little more each time.

"Hm." Odile held out the rod. "Feel it yourself, then. Thumb on the crystal. Tell me what you read."

Ansel took it. The crystal was warm from the woman's grip, and faintly — *faintly* — it sang. She closed her eyes and let the sound move through her the way she let the mountain's sounds move through her, the way she'd learned as a child to feel the foxes' dreams and the ravens' gossip and never, ever let it show on her face.

The ward-song was there. Still holding. But it was like a river in late summer — running, technically, but over exposed stones, a third of its proper depth, and getting shallower.

"How fast?" she asked quietly.

Odile went still. "Fast?"

"The decline. Is it years, or months?"

For a long moment the ward-keeper just stared at her. Then she laughed — softly this time, with no performance in it at all, and it sounded almost like relief.

"Months," she said. "Weeks, maybe. Nobody believes me. I brought my readings to the guild chapter at Highmoor and they sent back a letter saying my instruments needed recalibration. My instruments. I built those instruments. I wrote the appendix on instrument calibration in the *guild handbook*." She snatched the rod back and cradled it. "Something is drawing off the wards from below. Slowly. Carefully. Whatever it is, it doesn't want to break them — it wants to *empty* them. There's a difference. Breaking leaves wreckage. Emptying leaves..." She gestured vaguely at the dark mountainside. "A shell. A shape where the strength used to be. And then anything can walk in wearing the shape."

"What kind of anything?"

"That's the part that keeps me drinking." Odile tipped her flask back, then seemed to reconsider and passed it over instead. Ansel took a swallow. It tasted like a barn fire. "Old records. The sealed ones, the ones the guild says are 'contextual folklore.'" Her mouth twisted. "The wards weren't laid down against bandits or beasts, girl. Bandits don't need two hundred years of vigilance. The first keepers anchored these lines against what's *beneath* — the Hollow Court, the old stories call it. The court under Thornfell that the star-pact bound. Every ward in these mountains is a nail in that coffin. And every nail, per the old texts, was driven home with a pledge. A soul-debt. Signed names, generation on generation, binding the living to the sealing."

Ansel's mouth had gone dry. "Soul-debt."

"Poetic language for a legal mechanism, probably. The guild loved its paperwork." Odile shrugged, but her eyes stayed sharp on Ansel's face. "Why? Does the phrase mean something to you?"

"No," Ansel said, and her right side of her mouth went up first, like she was about to tell a joke she was too polite to tell.

Odile looked at her for a long moment. Drunk, disgraced, and eleven years unheard, she was still sharp enough to catch the lie — Ansel saw her catch it, saw her decide to pocket it rather than spend it.

"Well," Odile said instead, hauling herself upright with the help of the doorframe, "whoever's drinking the wards dry has either found a way to collect on those old debts, or has decided the debts are due. Either way, the bell's fading, and when it stops—" she rapped her knuckles on the ward-house door, twice, like a knock on a coffin lid, "—everything these walls have been keeping out walks in through the front door, smiling."

She went inside. The door shut. The night settled back over Cinderhatch, cold and ordinary, woodsmoke and dogs and someone's fiddle starting up badly in a distant tavern.

Ansel stayed on the step a while longer, her palm pressed flat to the frozen stone of the street, listening down through the mountain's bones.

Far below — deeper than mines, deeper than roots, deeper than anything that ought to have a mouth — she thought she felt something shift its weight. Patient. Attentive. Drinking.

*Don't think things like that,* she told herself again, feebly.

Above the ridge, the stars burned on, steady and countless, as they had every night of her life.

They had less than a week left, though she couldn't have known it. She only knew, walking back to the inn through the sleeping lanes, that for the first time since childhood she fell asleep counting the seconds between the ward-hum's beats — and woke before dawn to find the gaps between them growing wider.
