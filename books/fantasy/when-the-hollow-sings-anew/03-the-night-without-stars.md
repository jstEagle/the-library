## 3. The Night Without Stars

Ansel had learned long ago that the best way to survive a bad feeling was to keep her hands busy.

So while the wrongness in Cinderhatch's wards grew from a whisper to a wail — a sound only she seemed able to hear, a struck bell fading somewhere beneath the town's cobbles — she mended harness straps, counted bolts of dye-madder, and argued with Marta Voss about the price of mule shoes with more energy than the subject strictly deserved.

"Four coppers a shoe," Marta said, arms crossed in front of her forge like a small, soot-streaked fortress. "Iron's dear since the wards started acting strange. Half my apprentices won't go down to the ore-works anymore."

"The iron hasn't changed."

"The *men* have changed, and men dig the iron." Marta spat into her quenching barrel. "You can't shoe a horse with courage, Quintana."

"No," Ansel agreed, "but you can overcharge for it, apparently." She counted out the coppers anyway, because Marta was honest in the way of people who complained loudly about prices and quietly fed every stray dog in town, and because Ansel's caravan needed those shoes before they crossed the high passes come morning.

The caravan was nearly ready. Twelve wagons, forty-one mules, three nervous merchants from Rivermoor who kept asking whether the Thornfell roads were safe as though Ansel could hand them safety wrapped in brown paper. She'd told them what she always told them: nothing on the mountain wanted their cargo, and the things that might were mostly reasonable if you knew how to ask.

She did not tell them how she asked. She never told anyone how she asked.

That was the arrangement of her whole life: she gave the beasts wide berth and quiet respect, they gave her passage, and everyone else believed she was simply very, very good at reading weather and scat and the angle of broken branches. A good tracker. That was all. A good tracker could be admired. A beast-speaker got burned, or her mother had, twenty-three years ago, back when Ansel was small enough to still believe adults understood the world.

The fading-bell sound under Cinderhatch had been going on for two days now. Odile Ferran, the drunk ward-keeper, had been ranting about it for a month. Ansel had dismissed the woman as a cautionary tale in a ward-keeper's coat. Now, walking back from the forge with her arms full of horseshoes, she found herself listening to the ground the way she listened to birdsong — not with her ears, exactly, but with some older room in her chest.

*Fading*, the wards said. *Fading, fading, almost gone.*

She didn't like it. She especially didn't like that the beasts didn't like it. The ravens along the eaves of the Wayhouse had gone silent at dusk, which ravens never did; silence from a raven was like silence from Marta Voss, a sign of something genuinely wrong.

"You look like a woman chewing a wasp," said Bram, her second, leaning in the Wayhouse doorway. He was sixty, built like a rain barrel, and had smuggled everything through Thornfell except his own opinions, which he distributed free of charge.

"I'm fine."

"Mm. And I'm the Duchess of Rivermoor." He handed her a cup of cider. "Drink that and tell me what's actually eating you."

Ansel drank. "The wards are dying," she said, which was as close as she ever came to saying what she truly heard. "Odile Ferran's been shouting about it in the Drowned Rat for weeks. Everyone laughed. I'm starting to think she wasn't just drunk."

"Bram's First Rule of Mountains," Bram said, "is that when the mad old coots start agreeing with each other, check your rope knots." He glanced up at the sky, where the first stars were pricking through the blue-black over the peaks. "Wards have held since before my grandmother's grandmother. Whatever's drinking them dry will get bored eventually. Everything gets bored eventually."

"Everything except hunger," Ansel said, without quite knowing why she said it.

Bram looked at her a moment longer than was comfortable. "Get some sleep, Quintana. We cross at dawn."

* * *

She did try to sleep. She lay in the loft above the stable with the window cracked to the cold, listening to the mules shift below and the wards hum their dying note, and somewhere past midnight she drifted off the way you fall off a ledge — suddenly, and with a lurch.

What woke her was not a sound.

It was an absence of sound so total that her body registered it as violence. The wind stopped. The crickets stopped. The mules stopped shifting. Even the fading bell of the wards — that thin, suffering thread she'd been hearing for two days — simply *stopped*, cut clean, like a singer's throat closed mid-note.

And then the light went out of the sky.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out.* Every star above Thornfell, all at once, as if a great hand had pinched a thousand candle flames between one heartbeat and the next. Ansel sat up in the dark so complete it had texture, pressing on her eyes, and felt her breath go shallow and fast.

She had lived her whole life in mountains. She knew every kind of night — storm nights, moonless nights, nights when fog swallowed the world down to arm's length. She had never known a night with *nothing*. The stars weren't hidden. They were gone, and the darkness where they'd been had a quality she could only call *attentive*, as though something enormous had leaned close to look.

Below her, the mules began to scream.

Not bray — scream, the high panicked screaming of animals whose instincts had abandoned civilization entirely. Hooves hammered stall walls. Somewhere a wagon broke loose in the yard with a crack of splintering wood. Dogs all across Cinderhatch began howling at once, hundreds of voices, and then — this was the part that would live in Ansel's memory forever — the howling *stopped*, all together, all at once, as though the dogs had been told to stop by something they could not refuse.

Then the wards fell.

There was no flash, no thunder. What she heard — what she felt through the floorboards, through the bones of her feet, through the roots of her teeth — was glass breaking underground. A vast sheet of it, miles long, shattering somewhere deep beneath the mountain, the sound arriving muffled and wrong, the way sounds arrive in dreams. The humming that had faded for two days ended in a single crystalline shriek, and then there was nothing under Cinderhatch at all. No wards. No hum. Just stone, and whatever lived inside the stone, no longer separated from the world by anything but its own patience.

Ansel was moving before she decided to move. Boots, coat, knife, lantern unlit — no point, the dark outside was total — down the ladder and into the yard, where chaos had already bloomed.

"BRAM!"

"Here!" His voice came from the direction of the wagons, steadier than she'd expected. "Mules are half-loosed, we'll lose maybe six — Quintana, *look at the sky*—"

"I'm looking."

"What in the nine hells am I looking at?"

"Nothing," Ansel said. "That's the problem."

Torches were kindling all over town now, little islands of orange in an ocean of black, and by their light she saw Cinderhatch emptying into its streets. People stood in doorways in their nightclothes, faces tipped upward, mouths open. Old Mother Cass knelt in the middle of the square praying aloud to stars that were no longer there to receive it. Two boys from the ore-works ran past carrying a third between them, all three of them white-faced, and someone was ringing the chapel bell in arrhythmic, terrified clangs, as if the ringer had forgotten how bells worked and was improvising.

And on the eastern road — the low road, the valley road, the road that led away from the mountain rather than over it — movement. A lot of movement. Torches strung out for a mile in the dark like a lit fuse.

"Refugees," Bram said beside her. He'd come to stand at her shoulder, and she realized distantly that his hand was shaking where it gripped the fence rail. "Coming up from the valley towns. At night. In the dark. Nobody walks the valley road at night unless—" He stopped.

"Unless home is worse than the dark," Ansel finished.

The first of them reached the square within the hour: a miller with his family on a single exhausted horse, a cluster of quarry workers, an old man carrying nothing but a birdcage with a dead bird in it, which he refused to put down. Their stories arrived in fragments, overlapping, contradictory, all of them the same story underneath. The stars went out. The wards went with them. In Lowmarsh, the boundary stones had cracked down the middle. In Ferrow's Crossing, the river had run backward for the space of ten heartbeats and then resumed as though embarrassed. Everywhere, the same detail, repeated in a dozen shaking voices: *it wasn't like a storm, it wasn't like anything, it was like something looked at us.*

Ansel listened, and nodded, and handed out bread from the caravan stores, and did the competent things that needed doing, and underneath all of it she was listening to something else.

Because the beasts were leaving the mountain.

She felt it the way you feel a crowd going quiet behind you. All night, as the refugees flooded in, the wild things of Thornfell had been turning downslope — deer in streaming lines through the high meadows, foxes trotting the game trails two abreast like pilgrims, a bear family that should have denned for winter instead lumbering east with their cubs pressed close. And the wolves—

The wolves were screaming.

Not howling. Howling was language, howling was community, howling meant *we are here, where are you*. This was different. This was terror given a throat, pouring down the slopes from every direction, and only Ansel could hear what the screams *said*, because only Ansel had ever bothered to learn.

*Down,* the wolves screamed. *Down, down, the deep thing is awake, the deep thing is hungry, down, get low, get small, get GONE—*

She stood in the torch-lit square with bread in her hands and a lie on her face — *yes, friend, yes, we'll sort it out, the wardens will come* — while inside her skull a thousand voices she'd spent her life pretending not to hear begged her to run.

*They're right,* whispered the part of her that had inherited her mother's gift and her mother's fate in the same bundle. *Run. Take Bram, take the caravan, run east until the mountain is a memory.*

But the mountain cities were east too. Rivermoor. Ferrow's Crossing. Forty thousand souls living their small warm lives directly above whatever had just eaten the sky.

Ansel set down the bread. She walked to the edge of the square, to where the torchlight gave out, and stood alone in the starless dark, and let herself listen properly for the first time in twenty years.

The mountain was singing. That was the horror of it. Beneath the panic of the beasts, beneath the shattering of the wards, the deep places of Thornfell had begun to make a sound — vast, slow, patient, less a roar than a *breathing*, in and out, in and out, the rhythm of something that had waited a very long time and saw no reason to hurry now.

It sounded, she thought with a lurch she felt in her stomach, almost like hunger.

"Quintana." Bram's voice, soft, behind her. He'd followed her, of course he had. "You've gone grey as ash. Talk to me."

She thought about lying. It was her oldest skill, older than tracking, older than the gift itself.

Instead she said: "The animals are running away from the mountain. All of them. Everything with four legs and sense enough to use them is heading downhill, and the wolves are screaming that something under the rock has woken up hungry." She turned to face him. In the distant torchlight his face was carefully blank, the way men's faces go when they're deciding whether to believe something that will change everything. "I know how that sounds, Bram."

"It sounds like madness," he agreed. "It also sounds like the sky going black and the wards breaking and ten thousand people running from their homes, so frankly, madness has competition tonight." He blew out a long breath. "Your mother—"

"Don't."

"—was not a madwoman," he finished, gently stubborn. "I knew her. Whatever she had, you've got it, and I've watched you use it for fifteen years while pretending you hadn't. So." He squared himself like a man accepting a load onto his shoulders. "Tell me what the wolves say, and I'll decide what we do."

Ansel's throat was tight. "They say get out."

"And what do *you* say?"

She looked east, toward the valley, where the refugee fires made a false constellation on the ground to replace the true one overhead. She looked west, toward the mountain, blacker than the sky now, breathing its slow hungry breath.

Somewhere in the dark between those two directions was her brother's grave, if Tomas even had a grave, if he'd ever had anything at all after the fever took him and the debt collectors took his body and nobody, not once in twenty years, had told her sister-anything about where he'd gone. She'd stopped asking. Asking hurt. But tonight, with the stars snuffed out like a mother pinching candles at bedtime, the old question rose in her again with new teeth: *where do they take them?*

"I say," Ansel heard herself answer, "that running east puts us exactly where it wants everyone to be. Gathered. Counted. Easy." Her hand had found the wolf-tooth at her throat without her noticing — her mother's keepsake, the one thing she'd saved from the burning. "Something emptied the sky tonight, Bram. You don't do that to hide. You do it to announce."

Above them, the starless dark pressed down, attentive, patient.

And out of that dark, from the tree line at the square's edge, came a sound that made every hair on Ansel's arms stand upright: a single wolf, standing in full view of the torchlight — which no wolf ever did — looking directly at her, and singing one long, rising note.

She understood it perfectly. She'd have understood it even if she'd been born deaf.

*Come,* the wolf sang. *Come and see what took your brother.*

Ansel's blood went cold and then hot. Because she had never spoken of Tomas to any living soul in Cinderhatch. Not to Bram. Not to anyone. There was no way for a wolf to know that name —

— unless the thing beneath the mountain knew it, and had told the wolves, and wanted her to know that it knew.

"Well," she said, and her voice only shook a little. "That answers the question of whether we're leaving in the morning."

Bram stared at her. "Quintana, what did it say?"

She picked up her lantern, and her knife, and her mother's tooth, and turned toward the mountain.

"It said," Ansel Quintana replied, "that it knows my name."
