## 4. What the Wolves Carried Back

The wolf was dying when it found them, and it was courteous enough about the timing.

Ansel felt it first as a wrongness in the mountain's morning chorus — the thrum of roots and small hearts she had learned to read the way other people read weather. A gap in the song, high up on the eastern shoulder of Thornfell, where something that should have been singing was singing less and less and then not at all.

She stopped on Cinderhatch's muddy high street with a sack of oats over one shoulder and said, "Bram."

Bram Tuller, who had followed her out of the Wayhouse at dawn still arguing about whether they were *actually* going up the mountain or just standing around looking like people who might, glanced from her face to the ridgeline and back. "Don't tell me. The lion again."

"Not the lion." She set down the oats. "Something's coming down. Something that won't arrive."

It took the wolf a quarter hour to cover ground Ansel could have walked in ten minutes, because it was no longer walking so much as falling forward politely, one paw at a time, in the manner of creatures who have decided to die somewhere specific and intend to see the errand through. It was a big grey female, ribs like a washboard, one flank torn open in long parallel lines — claw, and claw, and something wider than either claw that Ansel did not want to think too hard about. Her fur was crusted black with old blood and new.

She came down the middle of the street, past the tannery and the shrine to some minor river saint, past Old Marta's stall of turnips nobody wanted, and every dog in Cinderhatch went silent at once, which was somehow worse than barking.

"She's coming to us," Bram said unnecessarily.

"Wolves don't come into towns," Ansel agreed. "Not living ones."

The wolf reached the well at the street's center, lay down with the deliberate care of someone folding away a possession they will not need again, and looked at Ansel with yellow eyes that were *looking*, not merely seeing, and Ansel's skin prickled from wrist to scalp because that was a person's look. That was the look of something with words in it.

*Tracker,* the wolf said. Her voice in Ansel's head was like wind moving through a hollow log — dry, resonant, older than her years. *I carried something down the mountain for you. I would give it to you before I go.*

"Give me what?" Ansel asked aloud, because speaking aloud kept her steady, and because Bram was watching her mouth move and she owed him some version of this conversation.

The wolf lowered her great head to one forepaw, worked at something tangled in the fur there with careful teeth, and nudged it across the mud.

A bundle of cloth, folded small, dark with road-dirt and worse.

Ansel did not pick it up right away. This was not cowardice; this was training. Twenty years of hiding a gift had taught her that between *seeing* a thing and *taking* a thing lived a margin where you could still pretend otherwise, and she stood in that margin for exactly four heartbeats before curiosity — the same cursed curiosity that had gotten her mother burned — put out her hand.

The cloth was heavy wool, dyed the deep blue-black of midnight, and even crusted as it was she knew it. Every Quintana had known it. Her grandmother had woven three bolts of it before the war, before the family money went wherever family money goes, and her mother had worn a cloak of it until the town took the cloak and burned it along with her.

Embroidered at the fold, half-hidden by blood, was their crest: a thorn branch crossed by a shepherd's crook. *We keep what is wild,* the crest said, in the way crests do. Ansel's whole childhood in a single image, and here it was, dropped in the mud of Cinderhatch by a dying wolf who had run — how far? how many days? — from somewhere under the mountain.

"Quintana," Bram said quietly. "That's your family's mark."

"I know."

"That's your family's mark coming out of a mine shaft that hasn't paid ore in eighty years, carried by a wolf that let itself get gutted rather than lose it."

"I *know*, Bram."

She unfolded it with fingers that were steadier than she deserved.

Inside the folds, written in a fine white hand — not ink; the strokes shimmered faintly, like frost given ambition — were two lines.

The first line was a name.

**TOMAS QUINTANA.**

The second line was a number, and the number was **XX**, and beneath both, small as an afterthought, a seal pressed into the cloth itself: a crown made of teeth.

Ansel had prepared herself, in the night, for many things. For the mountain to be angry. For whatever drank the wards to notice her gift the moment she used it within range. For Bram to ask questions she could not answer. She had not prepared for her brother's name, in his own letters, the ones he'd been so proud of at seven, rendered in frost on cloth from hell.

She sat down in the mud. It seemed wise.

* * *

Here is the thing about grief that is twenty years old: it does not wait politely in the ground where you buried it. It comes up through the soles of your boots.

Tomas Quintana had been taken — sold, taken, lost, the village never settled on a word — the autumn Ansel turned six. He was nine. There had been men in fine coats and one woman whose face Ansel had spent two decades deliberately misremembering. There had been coin counted into her father's shaking hands, though her father swore until his death that he never touched it, and perhaps he didn't; perhaps it simply sat on the table between them like a third party while the fine-coated men explained that debts were debts, and the family name was written somewhere it ought not be, and boys could settle such things as easily as men.

There had been a search. There had been dogs that refused to track. There had been her mother going up the mountain alone, twice, three times, coming back each time quieter, and then the fourth time, when she hadn't come back at all, only the smoke where the town had made its point about beast-speakers and the things they must surely traffic in.

*Tomas Quintana.* Written below the mountain, in a hand that gleamed. Carried out by a wolf who had torn herself open getting clear of something and had thought, with whatever wolves think with, *the tracker should know.*

Ansel became aware, gradually, of two presences: Bram crouched beside her saying her name in the careful tone of a man defusing something, and the wolf watching her with those yellow eyes, breathing in long rattling pulls.

"You knew him," Ansel said to the wolf, and her voice came out scraped raw. "You're one of hers — one of the mountain's. You knew my brother."

*I know the scent of the court on everything that leaves it,* the wolf said. *And I know that the court has begun, after twenty quiet years, to spend. To count its holdings. The cloth-bearers are being sent out. Yours is the first that got free of the deep galleries. The others—" she flicked one ear toward her ruined flank— "the others are being collected."

"Collected by what?"

The wolf's answer was a picture, not a word: something wide and pale unfolding in the dark of a tunnel, something with too many joints and a patience like cold water. Ansel's stomach performed a slow, thoughtful revolution.

"The wards fell two nights ago," Ansel said. "Every star gone. And now the mountain is counting its souls. Those are the same event, aren't they. Not cause and effect. The same thing, seen from above and below."

*You ask questions like a Quintana,* the wolf said, which was not agreement but was adjacent to it.

"Hey." Bram's hand hovered near her shoulder without landing, in accordance with the ancient treaty between them. "Quintana. Talk to me. What did the cloth say? Because I watched your face go through four different funerals just now and I only recognized two of them."

Ansel looked at the name in frost-white thread. At the seal shaped like a crown of teeth.

"It says my brother's alive," she heard herself say. "Or — something of him is. Down there. It says somebody under the mountain has been keeping a ledger, and my family's in it, and it wants me to know."

"Why?" Bram asked. "Why would a place like that want you to know? That's not how monsters work. Monsters don't send invitations."

"No," Ansel agreed softly. "Debtors' prisons do."

* * *

They made the wolf comfortable, or as comfortable as a dying wolf in a town square can be, which is to say Ansel sent Old Marta's grandson running for water and a hide blanket and stood guard while the animal rested her head on the well-stone. Children gathered at a respectful distance, the way children do, and were dispersed by parents who had opinions about wolves and remembered fires, and Ansel met those parents' eyes one by one until they found other places to be.

"What will you do with her?" Bram asked.

"Nothing. She's earned her rest wherever she wants it." Ansel crouched and laid her palm flat against the wolf's brow, which was hot as a hearthstone. *Thank you,* she said, in the open way, the way she had not used in front of another human being since she was six years old. It felt like undressing in the market square. *Thank you for the carrying.*

The wolf's ear twitched. *You speak like your mother.*

Ansel's breath left her in a thin line. "You knew my mother?"

*All the hill-kin knew the one who sang at the treeline. We mourned her badly, in our fashion. We have not sung well since.* The yellow eyes held hers. *Tracker. The court does not send messages out of generosity. Ask yourself what it buys, letting you see that name.*

"I've been asking nothing else since I picked it up."

*Good. Ask it once more before you answer the invitation.* The wolf struggled upright onto her sternum, an effort that visibly cost her, and fixed Ansel with a final look. *One more thing, freely given, because you thanked me like her: the name on the cloth is written in the hand of the one it names. Souls keep their letters, if nothing else. Whatever waits down there, part of him wrote to you.*

Then she lay back down, sighed once — a long sound, like wind leaving a house — and was done with the trouble of breathing.

Ansel stayed crouched there for a while. Bram, to his eternal credit, kept the children back and said nothing at all.

* * *

They buried her at dusk above the treeline, because Ansel refused to leave her in town soil and Bram, who had grown up fearing wolves like every good lowland boy, dug anyway without complaint, which told Ansel something about Bram Tuller she filed away next to all the other things about Bram Tuller she wasn't examining.

She sang over the grave. Not a spell — just a hill-song, the kind her mother used to hum at the treeline, four notes rising and falling like the land itself. And because the stars were still gone, because the sky overhead was a flat black lid and the wards on Cinderhatch's boundary stones were still humming their fading bell-note, the song went out into a world with nothing between it and the dark, and Ansel felt it travel farther than a song had any business traveling.

Down through root and stone. Down through old tunnels and older silences.

Down to where something with a ledger received it, and considered, and — she would have sworn this on any oath — *answered*. Four notes, returned from far below, slightly changed. Corrected. Like a teacher marking a student's work.

Like a brother remembering, for one note, that he had ever had a sister.

Ansel knelt there with her hand on fresh-turned earth and let herself cry for exactly as long as it takes a fire to catch, and then she wiped her face, because the Quintanas wept efficiently or not at all, and climbed back down to town.

* * *

Bram was waiting at her door with two cups of something alcoholic and the expression he wore when he was about to be brave.

"Twenty years," he said. "Twenty years I've tracked with you, and shared tents with you, and pulled you out of two rockslides and one regrettable marriage proposal, and tonight I find out you talk to animals, sing spells over graves, and have a dead brother the mountain's writing letters about." He handed her a cup. "So. The tent-sharing thing stands, obviously, but I want the rest."

Ansel looked into her cup, and then at the black sky where stars should have been, and understood that she stood at one of those margins again — the space between seeing and taking, where pretending still lived. She could give Bram a piece of truth sized to fit a smuggler's discretion. Or she could give him the whole of it, and lose the option of ever going back to her life as it was.

But her life as it was had ended two nights ago, when the stars went out, and definitively this morning, when a wolf died in the street to bring her a name.

"My mother could speak to beasts," she said. "So can I. They burned her for it. Tomas was sold to something under the mountain to pay a debt nobody would explain, and now that something is counting souls and the sky's gone dark, and it has invited me down." She drank. The liquor was terrible. It helped. "That's the shape of it. I'm going under the mountain, Bram."

"To do what?"

"Get him back."

Bram was quiet for a moment. Then, because he was Bram: "Alone?"

"Probably. Ideally. Quietly, certainly."

"Mm." He drank, staring at the starless dark. "You know the ward-keeper woman? Ferran. The drunk one who screams at the boundary stones."

"I've been trying very hard not to know her."

"Well, she sobered up this afternoon for the first time since the stars went, and she's been asking the tavern about you. Specifically. Says she knows how the wards were anchored, says it's written down somewhere, says the word 'pledge' a lot and then cries." Bram turned to her. "Whatever your family signed twenty years ago, Quintana, I don't think you're the first one to go looking for the paperwork."

From the direction of the boundary stones, faint across the evening air, came the sound of a woman's voice raised in argument with the universe — and this time it was not screaming. It was chanting. Rhythmic. Ritual.

And the stones, Ansel realized with a chill that started in her mother's tooth amulet and spread outward, were beginning — very faintly, very wrongly — to chant back.

She drained her cup, set it on the step, and went to find out what her family had promised, and to whom, and in what currency.

Because the frost-white hand on the cloth had written one name. But ledgers, her instincts whispered, always had two columns.

Somewhere beneath Thornfell, in a book made for counting souls, someone had written what the Quintanas *owed* — and Ansel meant to read every line of it before she paid a single thing.
