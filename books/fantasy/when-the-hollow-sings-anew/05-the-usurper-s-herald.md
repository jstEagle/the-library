## 5. The Usurper's Herald

The herald came to Cinderhatch on the ninth day after the stars went out, riding a white horse that had been dyed white — Ansel could see the gray roots coming in at the fetlocks, which struck her as the most honest thing about the entire procession.

She was standing in the market square pretending to buy turnips when the horns sounded from the east road. She had not bought turnips in years. She did not especially like turnips. But the market square was where you went when you wanted to see everything and be seen seeing nothing, and after nine days of starless dark and refugee columns and wards humming like a kettle about to boil over, everyone in Cinderhatch wanted to see something.

The something turned out to be Veyra Marchen's herald, and behind the herald, forty soldiers in charcoal-and-silver livery, marching in step through mud that swallowed boots to the ankle. They didn't seem to notice the mud. That was the first thing Ansel noticed about them: they moved like people who expected the world to cooperate.

"Turnips," she said again, to no one, because the farmer across the stall was staring at her with his mouth open and she needed him to stop.

"Did you hear?" he whispered. "That's the Marchen silver. That's the Silver Company. They took Harrowgate in three days."

"Harrowgate surrendered," said Ansel. "I heard it took them an afternoon."

"That's worse."

"It is," she agreed, and picked up a turnip so she'd have something to do with her hands.

* * *

The herald dismounted in front of the townhouse where Magistrate Corren Hale kept his office and, more importantly, his wine. He was a young man, the herald — younger than Ansel by a few years, with the kind of face that had been trained into pleasantness the way a hedge is trained into a wall. His voice, when he spoke, carried without effort, which meant it had been made to carry, probably by someone who charged by the syllable.

"People of Cinderhatch!" he called. "I bring greetings from Lady Veyra Marchen, Warden of the Northern Reaches, Protector of—"

He paused there. Ansel watched him pause. It was a small thing, a half-second hitch, but she'd spent her life reading animals and animals taught you to read pauses. The herald had been given a title list, and somewhere between *Protector of* and whatever came next, someone back at headquarters had realized the rest of the titles were now lies. There was no one left to protect. The sky was empty.

"—Protector of the free peoples," the herald finished smoothly, and smiled, and Ansel thought: *there it is.* The word *free* doing all the work of a confession.

"I come in a time of darkness," the herald continued. "You have lost your stars. Your wards are failing — yes, we know, we have seen it in every settlement from here to the coast. And in this hour of fear, Lady Marchen offers what fear needs most." He let the silence stretch, letting every frightened person in the square fill it themselves. "Protection."

Ansel set down the turnip.

"The Lady's armies will garrison your walls. The Lady's wardens will restore your borders — the old rites still function, friends, if you have hands willing to perform them and the authority to command them. In return, she asks only this: each household of fighting age shall offer one soul to the musters. One in ten for the towns. One in twenty for the villages." He smiled again, warmly, the way a man smiles when he believes warmth is a tool he owns. "Not conscription. Contribution. The difference matters. Conscription is what tyrants do. Contribution is what neighbors do."

"Or what sheep do," said a voice near the well, and a ripple of nervous laughter went through the crowd, and died fast, because forty soldiers had stopped looking bored at exactly the same moment.

* * *

Odile Ferran found Ansel within the hour, which was impressive considering Odile had been drunk since roughly the second day of the starless night and had only recently begun negotiating her way back toward sobriety, in the way of sailors approaching a hostile shore — slowly, in fits, with frequent retreats.

She materialized at Ansel's elbow outside the Drowned Bell tavern smelling of last night's decisions and gripping a cup of something that was, generously, tea.

"One in ten," Odile said without greeting. "Do you know what that means? Do the arithmetic?"

"Six hundred and some out of Cinderhatch alone."

"Six hundred and twelve," said Odile. "I counted while you were pretending to shop. Six hundred and twelve souls marched off to garrison towns that don't need garrisons, because the threat isn't at the border, girl, the threat is—" She stopped. Her eyes did something complicated. "The threat is where it's always been," she finished, in a different voice. "Underneath."

Ansel looked at her. Odile Ferran had been a ward-keeper for thirty years before drink unmade her, and even pickled she had more sense in her little finger than most of the magistrates had in their whole bodies. But right now her face was doing the thing faces did when they knew something and were deciding how much of it to say aloud in public.

"You know why the wards fell," Ansel said quietly.

"I know why the wards *were falling*, before the stars went out." Odile drained the tea, grimaced like it had betrayed her, and set the cup upside-down on a barrel with great finality, as if sealing it away forever. "And I know who signed the first pledge. But not here." Her gaze flicked to the soldiers drilling in the square — or rather, ostentatiously *not* drilling, just standing in formation where everyone could see them, which was its own kind of message. "Walk with me. Buy nothing. You're terrible at buying things anyway; I saw you threaten that turnip."

"I wasn't threatening it."

"You held it like a knife, dear."

* * *

They walked the long way, along the palisade, past the refugee fires where the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool hung thick enough to chew. Cinderhatch had doubled in population in nine days. The town hadn't doubled in anything else — not food, not beds, not patience. Tempers were the one resource in surplus.

At the third fire, Ansel stopped, because a family was kneeling.

Not praying. Kneeling. A mother, two grown sons, a grandmother with a cane laid across her lap — arranged in a row facing the townhouse, heads bowed, while one of the Silver Company officers stood over them holding a slate. Writing names.

"What are they doing?" Ansel asked a woman beside her, though she already knew. Knowing things and saying them were different economies.

"Volunteering early," the woman said. Her jaw was tight. "If your name's on the first slate, you get the good rations. First pick of the garrison bread. And your household gets marked *contributing*, so nobody comes knocking later." She spat, carefully, away from the fire. "Old Marta there — the grandmother — she knelt so her grandsons wouldn't have to draw lots. One of them goes either way. But this way she chooses which."

"And the other one?"

"Stays home and hates himself," the woman said, "and hates her too, eventually, and hates himself for that. That's how it works. I've seen it before." She looked at Ansel then, properly. "You're the Quintana tracker. The smuggler roads. My brother went over Thornfell with you two springs back — said you got a caravan through a whiteout that killed four guides the year prior."

"He exaggerates."

"He said you talked to a mountain cat."

Ansel held the woman's gaze and gave her the smile she used for exactly this — the flat, friendly, absolutely closed smile. "He exaggerates," she said again, and walked on, and felt Odile's eyes on her back the whole way.

* * *

By evening the kneelers had grown from one family to dozens, and Magistrate Corren Hale had accepted the Marchen charter with a speech about pragmatism that lasted eleven minutes and contained the phrase *"the responsible stewardship of necessary arrangements"* twice, which Ansel felt was eleven minutes too many and two phrases too many respectively.

She watched the signing from the back of the square, arms crossed against the cold, next to a rain barrel that smelled of itself.

"There it is," Odile murmured. She was soberer than she'd been that morning, which was to say barely, but her voice had edges again. "No battle. No siege. They'll write in the histories that Cinderhatch resisted for a whole day, maybe, because the magistrate hemmed. But look at them." She nodded at the crowd dispersing toward their homes, their fires, their lists. "They're not conquered. They're *managed*. There's a difference, and the difference is that managed people thank you for it."

"They're scared."

"Of course they're scared. Fear's the whole product. Marchen doesn't sell protection, girl, she sells the *absence of the alternative*. Look at the arithmetic of it — it's elegant, I'll give her that. Stars go out. Wards fail. People panic. Then the herald arrives with bread and walls and a neat little number — one in ten — and suddenly panic has a shape, and a shape you can sign. She didn't cause the dark." Odile's mouth twisted. "But she was ready for it. Ready before it happened. You want to lie awake tonight, ask yourself how."

Ansel was quiet for a long moment. Below them, in the square, the last kneelers rose and brushed off their knees. One of the young men — the spared grandson — stood a long time, looking at nothing, before walking home in the wrong direction and having to turn around.

"Her soldiers knew about the wards," Ansel said slowly. "The herald said it — *we've seen it in every settlement*. Before the stars even went out, they were surveying the failure. Like a buyer walking a house before the seller knows she's selling."

"Now you're thinking like a smuggler."

"I'm always thinking like a smuggler. It's the only honest trade left." Ansel pushed off the barrel. "One in ten, Odile. And when six hundred and twelve aren't enough — and they won't be — what then? One in five? Every household? Where does the ledger stop?"

Odile looked at her strangely. "Ledger," she repeated. "That's an odd word choice."

"Is it?"

For a moment neither of them said anything, and the cold came up off the mountainside and settled over the town like a lid. Somewhere beyond the palisade, far up the black slopes of Thornfell, something howled — long and low, and then cut short, as if remembering it shouldn't be heard.

Odile flinched. Ansel did not, because she had understood the howl, and it had said: *they are counting us too.*

"We need to talk," Odile said. "Properly. Tomorrow, sober, if such a thing can be arranged. About pledges. About who signed them. About—" She glanced at the soldiers, and lowered her voice until it was nearly lost in the wind. "About a name I saw today that I haven't seen in thirty years, written in a place names should never go."

Whose name. Whose place. Ansel asked, and Odile shook her head and walked away, steady enough, mostly, leaving Ansel alone with the rain barrel and the cold and the arithmetic running in her head like water through a cracked dam.

One in ten. Six hundred and twelve.

And beneath it all, faint as a struck bell fading, the wards hummed their wrong note, and Ansel found herself wondering — for the first time, but not, she suspected, for the last — what a soul cost on the open market, and who had ever paid one for hers.

Above the town, where nine days ago the stars had been, the sky sat empty and patient, like a page waiting to be written on.

Someone was going to fill it.

Ansel intended to find out who — and what they were writing — before the ink dried on Cinderhatch.
