## 6. Odile's Broken Compass

Odile Ferran had three rules for the morning after the world ended: drink before noon only if the world had ended twice, never apologize to anyone who hadn't earned it, and above all, do not think about the compass.

The compass sat on her workbench where it had sat for thirty years — a squat brass thing her master had pressed into her hands on the day of her oath-taking, along with a lecture she'd been too hungover to fully absorb. It didn't point north. It pointed at the wards. Every ward-keeper carried one; it was how you found the broken thread in a web that stretched across half a kingdom, how you knew which scar to tend before it tore.

For thirty years it had pointed, steady as scripture, at the Thornfell line.

Three days ago, it had begun to spin.

Not drift. Not waver. Spin, like a drunk trying to find his own front door, and then — last night, at the exact moment every star in the sky had gone out like candles pinched by an enormous and unhurried hand — it had simply stopped, needle quivering, pointing straight down.

Down was not a direction the compass knew.

Odile poured herself two fingers of plum brandy, considered the hour (well past dawn, so technically legal), and drank it looking out her window at Cinderhatch, which looked like a town that had been caught mid-sneeze. Refugees clogged the square. The inn had run out of beds, then floors, then the concept of privacy. Someone had tied a goat to the ward-stone at the crossroads, presumably for luck, and the goat was eating the luck.

And there, threading through the crowd with that particular walk of hers — like a woman crossing a frozen river who has done it a hundred times and trusts the ice exactly as far as it deserves — came Ansel Quintana.

Odile had known the tracker for perhaps six years, in the way one knows a fox that passes through your garden: with respect, from a distance, without any illusions about being told anything true. Ansel bought lamp oil and dried plums and once, memorably, forty feet of rope and no explanation. She was polite the way a knife is polite. And yesterday, when the sky went dark and the whole town had stood in the square making small animal noises, Odile had watched her look up — and then *down*, toward the mountain, with an expression Odile recognized because she'd seen it in her own mirror for thirty years.

The expression of someone who already suspected what had broken the sky.

That was the only reason Odile opened her door before the knock finished landing.

"You're awake," Ansel said. "I wasn't sure."

"I'm always awake now. It's a side effect." Odile stepped back to let her in. "Come in before the goat sees you. He's got opinions."

* * *

The interior of the ward-keeper's house was, in fairness, less a house than an argument between a library and an avalanche. Books stacked in towers that leaned at angles suggesting either deep knowledge or structural cowardice. Brass instruments hanging from hooks. A wall covered in maps of the Thornfell ward-line, each generation of keeper adding their own annotations until the mountains were more ink than paper.

Ansel stopped just inside the door and looked at the wall for a long moment.

"Your mother drew that one," Odile said, nodding at the oldest map, browned with age, its margins crowded with a fine, fierce handwriting. "Sera Quintana. She annotated my master's survey. He said she understood the Thornfell line better than anyone alive, and he said it grudgingly, which from him was practically a love letter."

Something crossed Ansel's face — quick, controlled, gone. "Everyone says my mother was clever."

"Everyone says she was burned."

"Yes."

"Well," Odile said, and fetched the brandy bottle and two cups instead of one, which for her was a ceremony roughly equivalent to kneeling, "everyone is wrong, or rather everyone is right in the way crowds are right, which is to say confidently and at the expense of accuracy. Sit down. I'm going to tell you something I haven't said aloud since the night the stars went out, and I'd prefer to do it sitting down, because my knees have opinions too."

Ansel sat. She held the brandy cup without drinking it, turning it slowly, and waited. It was, Odile thought, the most unnerving thing about her — that patience. Most people filled silence like a hole in a roof. This woman let silence fill itself.

"Thirty years ago I took the ward-keeper's oath," Odile began. "You know what we tell people the wards are? A net. Woven by the old kings, anchored in stone, humming away, keeping the bad things out. Nice story. Simple. Simple stories are the kingdom's favorite food." She turned her cup in a slow circle. "Here is what we tell each other, in this room, in the dark, after the third cup. The wards are not woven out of nothing. Nothing holds. You can't anchor a spell to air. You have to anchor it to *weight*. To something real enough to bear it."

"Stone," Ansel said.

"Stone, yes, and under the stone—" Odile exhaled. "Souls. Pledged souls. When the star-pact was signed — and don't ask me the details, the pact predates my order and most of its records were eaten by a fire that was probably not an accident — the courts below agreed to hold the weight of the world's seams. In exchange, they'd be fed. Fed with memory, with devotion, with souls freely pledged by families who wanted the borders strong. Generations of them. My order kept the ledger of pledges on this side. We called them *anchors*. Lovely word. Sounds architectural. Doesn't sound like what it is."

"And what is it?"

"A mortgage paid in people," Odile said flatly. "Paid in grandmothers and second sons and the occasional inconvenient daughter. Freely pledged — that part mattered, that was the law of it, the court couldn't take, only receive. So families signed. Whole villages signed. And the wards held, and the stars shone, and everyone agreed not to wonder too loudly what happened to Aunt Petronel after she 'went into service beneath the mountain.'"

Ansel set her cup down. It made no sound; she placed things the way she walked. "You're telling me the wards fell because the feeding stopped."

"I'm telling you the wards fell because the feeding *changed*." Odile got up, went to the locked drawer she'd been avoiding for three days, and took out the compass. She set it on the table between them. Its needle still pointed down, patient as guilt. "Look. For thirty years this pointed along the Thornfell line, wherever the weakest anchor was, so I could go mend it. Then, over maybe a year — I thought it was my eyes, I thought it was the drink, I thought it was everything except the truth — it started pulling *inward*. Toward the mountain's root. Something down there had started drawing harder than the line could bear. Drinking instead of sipping. And the anchors — the pledged ones — they thinned. You can't pull water from a well faster than rain fills it. Somebody down there got greedy, or desperate, and started taking what was never pledged, and the whole structure went hungry all at once, and three nights ago it snapped."

"The sound," Ansel said quietly. "Underground. Like glass."

"That was the Thornfell line breaking. I heard it in my teeth." Odile laughed, short and joyless. "I'd spent a decade telling my superiors the line was failing. Do you know what they wrote back? *Keeper Ferran is advised to moderate her intake.* Eleven letters. Eleven! I have the correspondence somewhere if you enjoy comedy." She rubbed her face. "They demoted me for alarmism. Then they retired me for drunkenness, which was fairer, since I'd taken up drinking specifically to survive knowing what I knew and being unable to make anyone listen. So when you ask yourself why the free cities' great ward-system collapsed with no warning — it had warning. It had me. I was just shaped like a warning nobody wanted."

Silence again. Ansel let it sit, and Odile found, to her irritation, that she kept talking anyway.

"There's more. There's the part I've been circling for three days like a moth around a lamp I know will kill me." She pulled the oldest map off the wall — her mother-master's survey, Sera Quintana's handwriting crowding the margins — and laid it flat, weighting the corners with cups and a fossilized biscuit. "When I took the oath, my master gave me the pledge-ledger to study. Every anchor family, every signature, going back four hundred years. I read them all. It was supposed to teach me reverence. Mostly it taught me that the gentry's handwriting deteriorates when they sign away their children."

She turned the map to face Ansel and put her finger on a margin note near the mountain's western root.

Ansel read it. Odile watched her read it — watched the stillness take her, the way it takes a deer that has decided the best response to the hunter is to become furniture.

The note read: *S.Q., of Cinderhatch, pledges herself. Sealed at the root. Witnessed: M. Ferran.*

"My master witnessed it," Odile said softly. "He wrote it in the margin because he couldn't bring himself to put it in the formal ledger. A living pledge. Not a family tithe — a whole soul, given whole, freely, by a beast-speaker the magistrates were already hunting. She didn't wait to be burned, Ansel. She *walked under*. She gave herself to the Hollow Court to seal a crack in the line — a crack, singular, one seam, patched with one soul — and the magistrates burned her effigy anyway because a martyr is easier to govern than a volunteer, and a story about wicked witches keeps children away from the mountain better than a story about sacrifice does."

"That's not true." Ansel's voice came out level, which was somehow worse than shaking. "I was seven. I remember—"

"You remember what you were shown. That's not an accusation. That's just what happens to children." Odile refilled both cups, mostly so her hands would have employment. "I didn't connect it until the compass started spinning inward. Your mother's pledge was the strongest single anchor on the entire Thornfell line — a whole soul, freely given, seals deeper than any tithe. Whatever's been drinking down there these past years, it drained *her* first. And when she thinned through, the line lost its deepest stitch, and everything unraveled from that hole outward. The night without stars wasn't the beginning. It was the end of a very long dinner."

Ansel was looking at the margin note. Her finger hovered over the initials without touching them, the way you hover over a burn you already know hurts.

"Tomas," she said, almost to herself. "My brother disappeared twenty years ago. Everyone said wolves."

Odile felt the floor tilt slightly, though she was fairly sure that was metaphor. "Wolves don't generally leave receipts."

"No," Ansel said. "But something else might have."

And there it was — the thing Odile had seen in the square, confirmed. Ansel Quintana hadn't come here about the sky. She'd come about a boy, and the sky was merely the shape her grief had chosen. Odile had spent thirty years among abstractions — lines, loads, load-bearing souls — and had nearly forgotten what it looked like when an abstraction put on a human face and knocked on your door.

"I can't give him back," she said, because someone had to say it. "Whatever the court took, the court keeps. That's the other half of the law. Freely received, never returned. It's older than the pact. It might be the reason the pact exists."

"I'm not asking you to give him back."

"What are you asking?"

Ansel finally drank her brandy. All of it, in one motion, like closing a ledger. "A way in. You said your order sealed pledges at the mountain's root. Sealing means a road. Roads have doors."

"They do." Odile looked at the compass, pointing faithfully, stupidly, down. "There's a flooded mine shaft below the old Cinderhatch workings — the Drowned Stair, the miners called it, because miners are poets with terrible lives. It descends further than any mine has business going. My order used it for the sealing rites. The last time anyone officially went down was the night your mother did. Officially, the stair collapsed behind them. Unofficially—" she tapped her temple "—my master drowned in brandy rather than draw the map, but he talked in his sleep for twenty years, and I listened, and I drew it anyway, and I've hated myself in a comfortable sort of way ever since."

"Why hate yourself?"

"Because keeping the map meant admitting the wards were built on people. Burning it would've been kinder to everyone, including me." She smiled crookedly. "Instead I kept it, and hid it, and drank, and every year the wards hummed a little thinner and I wrote another letter to superiors who filed me under *difficult*. So you see, when the sky went out, that wasn't just the court's doing. That was mine. Thirty years of a coward holding one piece of paper."

Ansel looked at her — really looked, for the first time, without the fox-distance — and said, "You wrote eleven letters."

"Twelve. They didn't answer the twelfth."

"Then you're the only coward in history who kept knocking." Ansel stood. "I'll need the map. And you."

"*Me?*" Odile's laugh cracked in the middle. "Child, I'm sixty-one. I get winded arguing. My qualifications for descending into a starving underworld consist of alcoholism, insubordination, and an excellent memory for things I wish I could forget."

"Those are exactly the qualifications I need. Nobody else alive remembers the rites, the roads, or the names." Ansel picked up the compass, studied the needle pointing down, and set it back with strange gentleness. "Besides. If my mother's down there — whatever's left of her — I'd rather be led by someone who carries her handwriting on their wall than by anyone who doesn't."

Odile turned to the window because her eyes had become unreliable and she refused to have it witnessed. Outside, the goat had moved on to eating a refugee's hat, and beyond the rooftops Thornfell stood black against a starless noon sky, beautiful and wrong, like a face you loved wearing an expression you'd never seen on it before.

"I'll want conditions," she said, when her voice was trustworthy again.

"Name them."

"One: we stop in the low country for proper supplies, because I refuse to meet my death in boots I haven't broken in. Two: you tell me what the beasts have been saying to you — and yes, I've watched you talk to the mountain cats for six years, I'm a ward-keeper, noticing things is the entire job. Three—" she hesitated, then reached out and turned the old map so Sera Quintana's fierce marginal hand faced upward, where they could both see it. "Three: whatever we find down there, whoever we find, we say her name. Sera. Out loud. Courts and queens and hungry gods can all learn to say it too."

For a moment Ansel said nothing. Then, quietly, like setting something heavy down after carrying it for twenty years: "Sera."

"Sera," Odile agreed, and felt thirty years of guilt shift its weight slightly, not gone, but shared, which was more than she'd hoped for and more than she deserved.

She was fetching the hidden map from its place inside the false bottom of the brandy cabinet — where else — when she heard it: hooves. Many hooves, moving fast and in formation, coming up the Cinderhatch road from the south. No refugees rode in formation. No pilgrims, either.

She went to the window. Below, in the square, the crowd was parting around riders in grey-and-crimson livery she hadn't seen since the last war — Marchen's colors, Veyra Marchen's colors, and the men wore white armbands stitched with a symbol that turned her stomach: a circle bisected by a line. The mark the new proclamations used for *gifted*.

"They're not here for the wards," she breathed. "Ansel. They're here for *people*. For anyone the sky's fall touched. They'll have lists—"

Ansel was already at the window beside her, and Odile saw the tracker's face change — saw the concealment she'd worn like a second skin for her whole life decide, in real time, that it had done its work.

Below, a soldier dismounted and seized the goat's owner, a boy of perhaps fifteen who'd been crying since the stars went out, and held him up by the collar while an officer consulted a list.

"He stutters when he's frightened," Odile said numbly. "Half the town will swear he speaks in tongues."

"Get the map," Ansel said. Her voice had gone very quiet and very even, the way it had when she spoke to the mountain cat. "And Odile — stay away from the window."

"Why? What are you going to do?"

Ansel was already opening the door, and what she said next, Odile would carry the rest of her life, however much of it there proved to be:

"What my mother should've been allowed to do in daylight. Something loud."
