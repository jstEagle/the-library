## 7. Beasts at the Border

The trouble with hiding a gift your whole life, Ansel had learned, was that you got very good at hiding and very bad at everything else that required honesty — including, apparently, lying to yourself about how long the hiding could last.

She stood in the treeline above Cinderhatch's eastern road with her hood up and her hands in her pockets, watching six of Veyra Marchen's soldiers turn a refugee family out of their cart. The morning was cold enough to see breath, and there was a great deal of breath to see: dozens of families camped along the road since the starless night, huddled under tarps and overturned wagons, waiting for a world that made sense again.

It wasn't coming back. Ansel was fairly sure of that now.

"Third patrol this week," Odile muttered beside her. The old ward-keeper smelled like juniper spirits and woodsmoke, which she claimed was medicinal. "They're not looking for smugglers anymore. They're sorting."

"Sorting what?"

"People." Odile spat into the bracken. "Useful and not useful. You can tell by how they do it. Watch."

Ansel watched. A soldier with a sergeant's knots on his shoulder held up a hand, and the family stopped. He asked questions Ansel couldn't hear, but she could read the shape of them in the way people answer: Where from. Where going. Any strange talents in the family, any gifts, any *affinities*. The mother shook her head too quickly. The father put his body slightly in front of his daughter, who was maybe nine and staring at the soldiers with the flat, careful expression of a child who has already learned that some adults are weather you survive rather than people you talk to.

"They're hunting gifted folk," Ansel said quietly.

"They're hunting everyone who might be useful when Marchen starts feeding things to whatever's under that mountain." Odile's jaw worked. "And they're hunting gifted folk *first*, because gifted folk are the only ones who might notice what she's doing before it's done."

One of the soldiers laughed at something. It carried up the slope, bright and casual, the laugh of men doing a job they'd decided didn't weigh anything.

Ansel's hands curled in her pockets.

For twenty years she had managed this feeling the way she managed everything dangerous: by giving it a wide berth and knowing its habits. Fear, anger, grief — all of them were animals, and animals could be routed around if you knew the trails. She had built her whole life as a network of detours. Don't sing where anyone can hear. Don't look too long at a hawk. When the wolves spoke, nod politely and keep walking.

But the stars were gone. Her brother's name was folded in a scrap of cloth against her ribs. And down on that road, a soldier was leaning toward a nine-year-old girl and asking, in the tone of men doing paperwork, whether she ever talked to animals.

*No,* the mother said — Ansel read it off her shoulders, off the way her spine went rigid as a fence post. *Never. She's just a child.*

The soldier smiled and wrote something anyway.

"Odile," Ansel said. "How many are there?"

"Six visible. Probably two more holding the horses back around the bend." Odile glanced at her sideways, and there was something in the glance that Ansel didn't like — something that had been there for days now, patient and appraising, like a woman watching a fuse burn toward powder. "Why? Thinking of doing something foolish?"

"I'm thinking about how fast I can get down that slope."

"Foolish, then. Good. I'll watch the road behind us."

* * *

Later — much later, when she had time to take the thing apart and examine it — Ansel would try to identify the exact moment she decided. She never could. There was no moment. There was just the slope under her boots, and the smell of pine resin, and the memory of her mother's face going white every time young Ansel so much as hummed at a bird, and underneath all of it, steady as bedrock, one fact: *they are asking a child questions in the tone you use on livestock.*

She came out of the trees forty yards behind the patrol, walking openly, which was itself a kind of declaration. Nobody walked openly toward Marchen's soldiers unless they wanted something or were very stupid, and Ansel had arranged her face into the particular blend of worry and deference that got smugglers waved through checkpoints.

"Ho there," called the nearest soldier, a young one with chapped cheeks. "Road's closed to through traffic."

"I'm not through traffic. I'm a guide. Freya Sterling's caravan lost a mule two miles back — spooked by something in the brush. They sent me to ask if your patrol's seen it."

"A mule," the sergeant repeated, without interest. He hadn't stopped writing in his ledger. That ledger bothered Ansel more than the swords did. Swords were honest; ledgers decided who ate this winter.

"A gray mule. Bad-tempered. Bites."

"We haven't—" The young soldier started, and then the brush on the far side of the road moved.

Not much. Not dramatically. Just a shifting of shadow, a suggestion of mass, the kind of movement your eye catches and your mind files under *bear, probably, keep still.* Every head on the road turned. The refugee family went silent. The little girl's eyes went very wide.

"Bear," someone whispered, uselessly.

It was indeed a bear — a big cinnamon-colored sow, and Ansel knew her, because Ansel had known her for years, the way you know a neighbor whose land borders yours. Her name, in the way bears have names, which are less names than reputations, was something like *Fish-Breaker* and something like *Two Winters' Fat* and something like *Do Not*. She had cubs denning up the valley, and she was hungry after a thin spring, and she had been following the smell of the refugee camp's cooking fires for two days with the patient ambition of her kind.

She had also, three minutes ago, heard Ansel's voice in her head saying *sister, I need you, and I will owe you* — which was, incidentally, the first time in her entire life Ansel had spoken to a beast in front of another human being on purpose.

The bear stepped onto the road.

"Sergeant—" the young soldier began.

"Steady." The sergeant finally looked up from his ledger. He had the calm of a man who had been told he was brave so often he believed it. "Bears spook easy. Nobody run. Halberds up, make yourselves big—"

The bear did not spook. The bear ambled forward with the unhurried confidence of eight hundred pounds of opinion, and Ansel felt the soldiers' fear bloom like heat off a forge, and felt — this was the part that frightened her, this was the part she would examine later and find no bottom to — she felt the bear's fear too, and beneath the fear, the question:

*Sister. These ones?*

And Ansel, standing in full view of seven human beings, answered aloud, because the words needed a body and hers was the only one available:

"These ones."

What happened next took perhaps ten seconds and would be described in Cinderhatch taverns for years afterward with increasing embellishment. The bear rose. The sergeant's calm evaporated with an audible squeak. The halberds went up and then, sensibly, down, then sideways, then dropped entirely as Two Winters' Fat took a swipe at the supply cart — not at any person, Ansel noted with professional pride; she'd asked for chaos, not corpses — and scattered a week's rations across the road like festival confetti. The soldiers broke. Six trained men of Veyra Marchen's army ran from a bear and a small woman in a gray hood, and the last thing the sergeant saw as he fled was the woman walking calmly to the refugee family's cart, helping the father hitch the mule — *their* mule, the real one, which had been hiding in plain sight the whole time — and slapping its rump toward the trees.

"Go," she told the family. "North fork, past the burned mill, there's a smuggler cache with food. Tell them Sterling sent you."

The mother stared at her. "You — who *are* you?"

"Nobody," Ansel said, and heard how the word had changed. For twenty years *nobody* had been armor. Now it sounded like a door closing on a room she no longer lived in.

Behind her, the bear dropped to all fours, licked spilled honey off a broken jar with tremendous satisfaction, and looked at Ansel with an expression that in a human would have been a raised eyebrow.

*I said I'd owe you,* Ansel told her silently. *I remember.*

*You always remember,* the bear replied, which was either a compliment or a warning, and ambled off into the trees with a jar of pickled eggs.

* * *

Odile was waiting at the treeline, and she was laughing. Actually laughing, bent double, wheezing, holding a tree for support.

"You should have *seen* the sergeant's face—"

"Yes, very funny."

"It *was* funny. Six armed men routed by a lady bear and a lie about a mule. Marchen's recruiting posters won't mention that part." She straightened, wiped her eyes, and then her expression settled into something more careful. "You spoke to it. Out loud. In front of witnesses."

"I did."

"In front of *Marchen's* witnesses."

"I did," Ansel said again, and found she had nothing to add, because the truth was she didn't regret it, and the absence of regret was the loudest thing in her chest.

They walked in silence for a while, up the game trail toward the high camps. The forest was doing its ordinary morning business around them — jays scolding, somewhere a woodpecker knocking like a impatient carpenter — and every sound of it passed through Ansel differently now. All her life the beasts' voices had come to her through a wall she'd built herself, muffled, half-heard, deliberately ignored. Walking with the wall down was like taking cotton out of her ears. It was unbearable. It was wonderful. It was like being deaf for twenty years and forgetting, day by day, that other people lived in a world of sound.

"The girl," Odile said at last. "The little one on the cart. She watched you the whole time. Did you notice?"

"She was scared."

"She was *memorizing*. There's a difference." Odile ducked under a low branch. "That child will grow up telling a story about a woman who talked to a bear and meant it kindly. Stories like that are how the next generation decides what's possible. My grandmother had one about a ward-keeper who sang storms apart. I became a ward-keeper because of a story." She paused. "Then I became a drunk because of what happened after. But that's the other half of stories, isn't it."

"Odile."

"Mm?"

"Why didn't you stop me?"

The old woman considered the question with the seriousness she usually reserved for ward-mathematics. "Because I've spent thirty years keeping walls standing and calling it protection, and last week every wall I ever kept fell down at once, and do you know what I learned?" She didn't wait for an answer. "I learned that the walls were never the point. The point was what we were protecting. And sometimes protecting the thing means breaking the wall." She shrugged. "Also, the sergeant had it coming. Man wrote my name in a ledger at Cinderhatch two days ago. 'Former ward-keeper, erratic, possibly gifted.' Possibly. As if I don't know exactly what I am."

Ahead, through the trees, the smuggler high camp came into view — tarps and cook-smoke and the familiar geometry of people living illegally and well. Ansel's people. Or they had been. She slowed.

"What is it?" Odile asked.

"When I walk in there, I'm going to have to tell them what I did. Word travels faster than I do — some scout will have seen the bear, seen me. And then every one of them has to decide whether knowing me makes them safer or dead." Ansel flexed her hands. "Twenty years I kept them safe by being nobody. Today I spent that."

Odile was quiet for a moment. Then: "You know what I noticed, back on the road? When you said *these ones* to the bear?" She looked at Ansel sidelong. "Your voice didn't shake. Not once. Twenty years of hiding, and when you finally opened the door, you walked through it like you'd been through it a thousand times." She snorted. "Terrifying woman. No wonder the bear likes you."

The camp saw them coming. Ansel saw the moment the news arrived ahead of them — a ripple through the tarps, heads turning, conversations stopping mid-word. Marta Voss, who'd run the Thornfell routes with her for eleven years, stood up slowly by the cook-fire, flour on her hands, and looked at Ansel with an expression Ansel had never seen directed at her before: hope and grief in equal measure, the look of someone watching a friend become a stranger and a legend in the same breath.

"Ansel," Marta said. "Tell me it's not true."

Ansel thought about lying. She was very good at it. She had been good at it her whole life, and look where the goodness had gotten everyone: here, at the edge of a starving dark, with the stars gone out and soldiers writing names in ledgers.

"It's true," she said. "I speak to beasts. I always have. And yesterday I found out my brother's soul was sold to the Hollow Court, and I'm going under the mountain to get him back, and Marchen's people are hunting anyone like me, which means every one of you gets to choose, right now, whether you know me." She took a breath. "I won't think worse of anyone who walks."

The silence stretched. Somewhere a kettle whistled and nobody moved to take it off the fire.

It was Marta who moved first — Marta, who dusted the flour off her hands, crossed the camp, and did the most smuggler thing imaginable: she hugged Ansel briefly, hard, and then immediately started negotiating.

"Under the mountain," she said. "Through the drowned stair. That's Rennick's route, and Rennick will want payment, and Rennick's payments are never money." Her eyes were wet and furious and fond. "You're going to hate what he asks for, Ansel. I need you to hear that now, so when he says it, you remember I warned you."

"Warned me about what?"

Marta glanced at Odile, at the camp, at the sky where stars should have been and weren't, and lowered her voice.

"He's already met with Marchen's people. Yesterday. And what he's selling isn't you." She swallowed. "It's the road."
