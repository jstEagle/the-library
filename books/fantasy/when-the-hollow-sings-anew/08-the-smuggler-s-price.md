## 8. The Smuggler's Price

The Saltcellar was not a cellar and had never contained salt, but it had been called that for so long that even Ansel had forgotten which lie came first. It sat beneath a tannery at the crooked edge of Cinderhatch, down a stair that smelled of oak gall and old smoke, and it was where her life — her real life, the one that fit like a coat cut for someone slightly braver — had been stitched together over twelve years.

She arrived after midnight, because the Saltcellar only existed after midnight. Before that it was a storage room full of hides and bad air.

Bren Halloway looked up from the map table when she came in, and his face did something complicated: relief first, then grief, then the careful blankness of a man deciding which of those he could afford to show. He was sixty if he was forty-five, gray to the collarbone, with hands like tree roots and a voice you had to lean toward to hear. He'd taught her how to read a ridge line, how to bribe a customs man with flattery instead of coin, how to know when silence in the forest meant danger and when it simply meant deer.

"Ansel Quintana," he said. "Alive."

"Barely. It's crowded up there."

"It's crowded everywhere." He gestured her over. Around the table sat six others, and every one of them was family in the way that mattered more than blood: Tam and Ilsa Vey, who ran the eastern switchbacks; old Corvo, who hadn't guided anything in a decade but still knew every cave mouth in Thornfell by its taste; Maret, young, sharp-faced, Bren's shadow; and the twins, Pim and Pella, who finished each other's silences.

They were all looking at her the way you look at weather that has turned.

"You've heard," she said.

"We heard the stars go out," Bren said. "We heard the wards fall. We heard Rivermoor opened its gates without a fight." He paused. "And we heard there was a woman on the Cinderhatch road who made a bear eat a soldier's shield. A woman who talked to it. In front of God and Marchen's patrol."

Ah. There it was.

"I was going to tell you myself," Ansel said.

"Were you?" Pim asked, and Pella added, "Or were you going to keep being a good tracker until the mountains wore out?"

It wasn't cruel, exactly. That was the worst part. It was tired.

Ansel pulled out a stool and sat down among them, deliberately, the way you sit down in front of a frightened animal. "Twelve years," she said. "Twelve years you've trusted me with your routes, your cargoes, your lives. And yes, I lied about one thing the whole time. One thing. The rest was true."

"The rest was true *because* of the one thing," Corvo said mildly. He was cleaning his nails with a fishhook, an affectation he maintained because it unsettled people. "A guide who can ask the mountain what's coming is a different trade than a guide who guesses. You set your prices as a guesser."

"I didn't charge extra for it, if that's what—"

"That is not what I said." Corvo looked up, and his eyes were kind in a face built entirely out of wrinkles and disapproval. "I said you let us believe we knew what we were buying. I don't blame you. My mother was hedge-touched. I know what happens to the touched when word gets out." He turned the fishhook over. "But you should have told us before the stars went out. After would have been forgivable. Before would have been trust."

Ansel felt that land somewhere under her ribs. "You're right," she said, because she was too tired to be proud. "I should have."

Silence settled. Bren pushed a cup toward her — cider, sour, the good batch they kept for funerals and homecomings — and she drank.

Then he said, "There's a reason we asked you here besides scolding. Though the scolding was overdue."

* * *

He unrolled a map across the table. Not a smuggler's map — those lived in Bren's head and died with him — but a surveyor's map, old, water-stained, marked with the official roads that no longer mattered. On top of it he laid a second sheet, thin as onion skin, and Ansel felt her stomach drop, because she recognized the hand-drawn lines. Switchbacks only she used. Springs only she knew. The drowned stair.

That last one stopped her cold. "Where did you get this?"

"Maret drew it," Bren said. "From watching you. Over years."

Maret wouldn't meet her eyes. She was twenty-three, quick and hungry, the best apprentice Ansel had ever trained, and she had spent those years mapping her teacher's secrets — not out of malice, Ansel understood suddenly, but out of the same hunger Ansel herself had once carried: *the roads are everything, and whoever knows the roads owns the future.*

"And who's it for?" Ansel asked quietly.

Bren exhaled through his nose. "Veyra Marchen's quartermaster sent an envoy. Ten days ago. Before the stars went out, mind — this was arranged back when we all thought the world would keep spinning." He tapped the onion-skin map. "They want passage through Thornfell. The high passes, the ways around Cinderhatch's tolls, and—" his finger landed on the drowned stair "—this. Whatever this is. They'll pay enough to retire three networks. And they want you to lead them. By name."

"They know my name?"

"Quartermasters know everyone's name. It's their whole job." Bren rubbed his eyes. "Here's the shape of it, Ansel, and I'll give it to you straight because you're family and family gets it straight. Half our people are scattered. The refugees have choked the low roads; nothing moves; we're eating savings. Marchen's army pays cash and doesn't ask what's in the wagons. If we take this contract, the network survives the winter. All of it. Every family on our rolls, fed and housed, through whatever's coming."

"And if we don't?"

"Then we hope the free cities win a war they're already losing." He said it without drama, which made it worse. "That's the offer. Passage under the mountain — and I won't pretend I know what's down there or why you want it, though I can guess, given the wolf — in exchange for you walking Marchen's vanguard through Thornfell safe and whole."

The table was very quiet. Somewhere above them, the tannery dogs barked at nothing, twice, and went silent.

"You want me," Ansel said slowly, "to guide the army that's conscripting refugees. That's hunting gifted children. That burned the granaries at Fennmark when they resisted." Her mother had been burned for less than what Ansel had done on the Cinderhatch road, and these people knew it. "You want me to lead them through my mother's mountains."

"We want you to choose," Bren said. "That's the whole point of putting it in front of you instead of behind your back. Maret could take the contract. Corvo could. But none of them know the passes like you, and none of them can promise the army arrives alive. If you say no, we give the map to someone else anyway, and they'll do it worse, and more people die on both ends. If you say yes—" he spread his hands "—the network eats this winter, and maybe the next one, long enough to matter."

"And the people Marchen feeds to whatever's under the mountain?"

Bren didn't flinch. That was the discipline in him; he'd carry a load past breaking and his face would never show the strain. "Are real," he said. "And I'm sorry for them. And I can't feed two hundred families on sorry."

* * *

They argued for an hour. It was, in its way, the most honest conversation the Saltcellar had ever hosted, because there was no longer any point pretending.

Tam wanted to take the contract. His reasoning was simple and terrible: the war was lost, the stars were gone, and survival wasn't a sin. "When the flood comes, you get in the boat," he said. "You don't stand on the roof arguing about whose fault the rain is."

Ilsa, his wife, said, "You get in the boat. You don't row it for the flood." She put her hand flat on the onion-skin map. "This isn't neutrality. Guides are weapons. We've always known that; it's why customs men hate us. Whoever holds these passes holds the throat of the north. Handing it to Marchen isn't staying out of the war. It's picking her side with extra steps."

"She's already picked a side," Tam shot back, nodding at Ansel. "Look at her. Bear-wrangler. Hollow-court pen pal. She's been fighting since before any of us agreed to it, and she never asked us either."

That one stung because it was half-true, and Ansel watched it land on the twins' faces too. Pim and Pella hadn't spoken since the beginning. Now Pella said, "My question is simpler. If she says no, do we still take the money?"

"Yes," said Tam.

"No," said Ilsa, at the same moment, and the twins looked at each other, and Ansel saw the network cracking along a seam she'd never noticed — not loyalty versus betrayal, but fear wearing two different coats.

Corvo spoke last, as usual. "I'll tell you what I told the ward-keepers' guild, back when they still listened to me." He folded his fishhook away. "Old pacts aren't decoration. The wards held because somebody paid for them, generations back, and we all lived on top of the payment without thinking about it. Everything comfortable is standing on somebody's bones. So the question's never *is there a price*. There's always a price. The question is whether you're the one paying it, or whether you've just found someone else to pay it for you and are calling that wisdom." He looked at Bren. "Marchen's coin is somebody else's bones. That's all I'll say."

Bren nodded slowly, and then did the thing that Ansel would remember for the rest of her life, in whatever form her memory still took by then. He rolled up the onion-skin map, and he put it on the table in front of her.

"Your call," he said. "Not because I'm washing my hands. Because you're the best of us, and because if we do this, I'd rather it break your heart than mine — yours will heal honest. Take the night. Say yes, and we march with you at dawn. Say no, and—" his voice caught, just barely, a knot in old rope "—and no hard feelings, Ansel. That's a promise. Whatever you choose, you'll always have a stool here."

"No hard feelings," Ansel repeated. She looked around the table at the faces she loved: Tam's stubbornness, Ilsa's fury, Maret's shame, the twins holding hands under the table the way they'd done since they were small, Corvo's ancient tired kindness, Bren's heart breaking politely behind his eyes.

"You should know something before I answer," she said. "The thing under the mountain. It's starving. It's been starving for two hundred years, and Marchen means to feed it — souls, thousands of them, the conscripts, maybe the cities entire. That's what the army's for. That's what your contract buys. You wouldn't be selling passage. You'd be selling the herd to the slaughterhouse, and taking a cut."

Nobody spoke. Maret had gone white. Tam opened his mouth, closed it, and for the first time all night looked uncertain.

"If that's true," Bren said carefully, "then the envoy lied to us."

"The envoy works for Marchen. Lying is her native language."

"Then we needed to hear it from you, not from rumor." He pressed his palms flat on the table, and the old man looked older than Ansel had ever seen him. "Go. Sleep on it. Dawn, at the tannery gate."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She walked.

Cinderhatch at three in the morning was a town pretending to be asleep the way a rabbit pretends: ears up, heart hammering, ready to bolt. Refugees camped in the churchyard and the wool market and the lee side of every wall, huddled under carts, and the smell of woodsmoke and unwashed humanity hung in the frost. Ward-less, starless dark pressed down like a lid. People had started lighting lamps at all hours just to have something overhead that glowed.

Ansel found Odile Ferran exactly where she'd left her: in the chapel annex, sober for eleven hours now and furious about it, surrounded by ward-scratchings and empty ink pots, trying to reconstruct a rite from fragments.

"Well?" Odile didn't look up. "Did your criminals murder you? I had money on the big one."

"They offered me a job instead."

"Marchen's?"

"How did you—"

"Because it's the only job going, girl, and because you have the face of a woman who's been handed a bag of gold with a hook inside it." Odile finally looked up. Ink stained her fingers to the second knuckle. "What was the price?"

"My help getting their army through the passes. In exchange, they take me under the mountain."

Odile was quiet for a moment. Then she laughed — short, bitter, genuine. "That's elegant. That's really elegant. They sell you your brother's grave and bill you for carrying the shovel." She stood, joints crackling, and came around the table. "So what did you tell them?"

"Nothing yet. Dawn."

"Hm." Odile studied her with the flat professional attention she usually reserved for failing wards. "You know what I notice? Everyone keeps offering you deals where other people pay and you walk. The smugglers want you to buy your brother with strangers' souls. Marchen wants to buy the mountain with yours." She tapped Ansel's sternum, once, with an inky finger. "Careful, beast-speaker. Get in the habit of letting other folk pay your debts and one day you'll look up and owe everybody."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I've watched good warders go bad the exact same way. Never all at once. Just one reasonable compromise, then another, each one defensible, until you're standing in the ruin wondering when you became the sort of person who signs things." She turned back to her papers. "For what it's worth — and I know it isn't much — I think the answer's already made. I think it was made the day you yelled at a bear in front of soldiers. You just haven't told your mouth yet."

Ansel almost smiled. Almost.

Outside, the east stayed black. No dawn blush. No birdsong — the birds had gone quiet days ago, all of them, and only she knew they hadn't fled. They were waiting, like her, like the beasts massing in the high country, like the hungry patience under the stone.

She walked to the tannery gate as the bells rang the hour that would have been dawn, back when dawns were a thing the sky did.

Bren was there. So were Tam and Ilsa, standing apart, arms crossed, not touching. Maret hovered behind them with the onion-skin map clutched to her chest like a shield. Corvo leaned against the wall, and the twins on either side of him.

Bren held out his hand, palm up. Waiting for her answer, or for the map — she couldn't tell which, and neither, she thought, could he.

Ansel took a breath of the cold, starless air, and reached into her coat.

She drew out not the map, but the wolf's scrap of cloth, crested, name-stained, and laid it gently in Bren's palm instead.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm not selling you the herd."
