## 9. Down the Drowned Stair

The mine shaft had a name, because everything in Thornfell had a name, and the name was the Drowned Stair.

"It's not actually drowned," Odile said, peering down into the black. "That's the joke. Miners have a terrible sense of humor."

"Then what's the water?"

"Knee-deep for the first mile. Then it gets ambitious." She said it the way she said most things — flatly, with her hands jammed in her coat pockets as if they might otherwise do something she'd regret. Three days sober now, and her hands hadn't stopped shaking since Cinderhatch fell out of sight behind them. Ansel pretended not to notice. It seemed the kindest lie available.

The shaft mouth gaped at the base of a scree slope like something that had been mid-yawn when the mountain died. Old timber bracing framed it, black with a century of drip-water, and someone — long ago, in a more optimistic era — had carved letters above the lintel: *QUINTANA & SONS, DEEP WORKS.*

Ansel stared at the carving until her eyes stung.

"Your family's?" Odile asked. She'd seen Ansel look at things before — the crest on the wolf-scrap, the burned foundation stone outside Cinderhatch — and she'd learned not to ask twice.

"My mother's family mined tin," Ansel said. "Before the wards needed keeping. Before—" She stopped. *Before anyone sold anything to anything.* "It was a long time ago."

"Mines are like families," Odile said. "The deeper you dig, the more you find you wish you hadn't." Then, because she was Odile and couldn't let a decent aphorism stand unbruised: "Also both full of dead canaries."

Ansel almost smiled. Almost. Her mouth wasn't sure how anymore; it had been three days of walking downhill through country where nothing sang, and the silence had gotten into her jaw.

Because that was the thing about the mountains since the stars went out. The beasts were gone from the high passes — fled downslope in a screaming tide only she had heard, a chorus of *down, down, away* that had woken her every night since. And what remained up here, in the thin cold air under a sky like a closed lid, was a hunger. Not hers. Something beneath her boots, patient as geology, breathing slow.

She could feel it through the soles of her feet if she stood still too long. Like standing on the chest of a sleeping thing very large and very empty.

"Well," she said. "After you?"

"After me? I'm the one who knows the way."

"You're the one who knows the way *and* has a lantern. I'm just the one who'll be eaten first if we're wrong about which end of the dark the hunger is on."

"That's a comforting division of labor," Odile said, but she lit the lantern anyway, and its small gold circle steadied them both more than either would admit.

* * *

The water was exactly as advertised: knee-deep, shockingly cold, and tasting of iron and old stone when it splashed past your lips no matter how tightly you kept them shut. The stair itself ran down along the left wall — steps cut into living rock, each one slick with a film of something that wasn't quite algae, because nothing grew down here anymore. Nothing grew anywhere near the Hollow. Things only endured.

Ansel counted steps to keep her mind busy. At four hundred she stopped counting, because four hundred was when the dark changed quality.

It wasn't that the lantern dimmed. It was that the dark got *thicker*, the way honey thickens in cold — as though light had to work harder to move through it, as though the air itself had opinions about being illuminated. Odile noticed too. Ansel saw her raise the lantern higher, then higher again, and finally stop pretending.

"The court's attention," Odile said quietly. "What my order used to call it. When the wards were strong, this was just a mine. Now—" She gestured vaguely with her free hand, an eloquent gesture meaning *all of this, all of this wrongness.*

"Now it knows we're here."

"Now it's curious. There's a difference. Curiosity you can survive. It's appetite you have to worry about, and we're not food yet." A pause. "Probably."

"Wonderful. I'll write that on my tombstone. *Not food yet. Probably.*"

"Make sure they spell 'probably' correctly. It's doing a lot of work in that sentence."

The humor helped. That was the strange discovery of the last three days: Odile Ferran, disgraced ward-keeper, drunk, exile, keeper of a compass that pointed at guilt instead of north, was funny in the specific way of people who had looked directly at the worst thing they could imagine and decided to be rude about it rather than afraid. Ansel had spent her whole life around smugglers, who joked too, but theirs was the humor of men hiding from consequences. Odile's was the humor of a woman who had already paid them.

Ansel trusted her more for it than she'd ever trusted anyone, which was inconvenient, because trusting people was how you ended up burned or sold or kneeling in town squares while heralds read conscription rolls.

At step six hundred — she'd started counting again, unable to help herself — the bats found them.

* * *

They came out of a side gallery in a soft rush of wings, and Ansel felt them before she heard them: a dozen small bodies threading the dark, and threaded through each body, a song.

Not sound-song. Not exactly. It was the same channel the beasts spoke on, the one she'd been listening to in secret since she was six years old and had never once answered aloud where anyone could see. But these voices were different from wolves and lions and the chattering crows of the low forests. They were *shaped* differently — narrow, precise, built for echo. Each bat sang a thread of sound into the black and listened to what came back, and what came back was the world: wall, water, stair, gap, ceiling, drop.

And because Ansel could hear the songs, she could hear the world they painted.

"Oh," she breathed, and stopped walking so fast that Odile nearly went over her back.

"What? What is it?"

"They're mapping. Listen—" but Odile couldn't listen, not really, and Ansel fumbled for words big enough. "Every squeak comes back different. Off the walls, off the water, off each other. They're singing the tunnel. I can hear it. It's like— it's like standing inside a drawing while someone draws it."

Odile looked at her for a long moment, lantern-light catching the deep lines around her eyes. "You can hear echolocation."

"I can hear *them*. The rest is — translation, I suppose. My whole life I've been translating without knowing there was an original text."

"Hm." Odile turned back to the dark ahead, but Ansel caught the corner of her mouth. "My grandmother used to say the gifted always describe their gift like weather. *It's like rain, it's like a drawing.* Never once 'I hear the thoughts of animals' out loud, oh no. Too honest. Might get you burned."

The joke landed wrong and they both knew it, and Odile had the grace to look briefly stricken. "I didn't mean—"

"No. You did mean. And you're right." Ansel watched the bats wheel overhead, little gray ghosts stitching the dark together. "But my mother's dead twenty years and the ones who burned her are mostly dead too, and the ones who aren't are kneeling in Cinderhatch signing Marchen's rolls. So I've decided to be honest in places where it can't hurt anyone. Here. With you. And apparently with bats."

One of the bats — bolder than the others, or perhaps simply the one whose song-thread had been braiding closest to her awareness — dropped out of the wheel and landed on the head of Odile's lantern-pole. Upside down, wings folded like a folded letter, face a marvel of wrinkled absurdity. Its ears were enormous. Its eyes were two beads of skin, sealed over, useless and smooth.

Blind. Born blind, generations blind, a whole lineage that had never once seen the sun and had never once missed it.

"Hello," Ansel said softly.

The bat sang at her — a short phrase, bright and interrogative — and the echo came back carrying *her*: the shape of a woman standing in cold water, arms at her sides, heart going too fast.

"She's asking what you are," Ansel translated, and then flushed, because that wasn't quite it. "No — sorry. She's asking what I am. To her I'm a loud thing that hears loud things. That doesn't happen often, apparently."

"And what did you tell her?"

Ansel considered. Then she did something she had never done in thirty-one years of careful, concealed, exhausting life: she sang back. Not words. Just a thread of hummed sound, shaped the way the bats shaped theirs, sent out into the dark to see what would return.

The echo came back carrying the tunnel, the water, the stair, the twelve small singers, the vast patient hunger far below —

— and something else. A note underneath, so deep she felt it in her teeth rather than heard it. The court, noticing. The court, turning its attention toward the small bright noise like a great eye turning toward a candle.

The bats scattered upward, shrieking. Even the bold one lifted off the lantern-pole, though it circled instead of fleeing, and its song when it returned was urgent, rapid, layered — and unmistakably a warning.

"She says we should follow," Ansel said quietly. "And be quick. And quiet. In that order." She swallowed. "Something down there heard us. Something old."

"The Queen?"

"I don't know. Maybe. Or maybe just the court's edges. Its — fingers." Ansel flexed her own fingers, which had gone numb in the cold water hours ago. "Odile. When it turned toward us, I felt it the way I feel a bear testing the wind. Except a bear wants meat, and this wanted—" She searched. "*More of itself.* It heard something alive and it wanted to hollow it. That's the appetite. That's what's been drinking the wards. It doesn't hate life. It just can't tell the difference between eating it and becoming it."

Odile was silent for a moment. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, keeping time like a patient metronome.

"My order had a hymn about that," she said at last. "We weren't allowed to sing it after the third schism. *The Hollow does not gnash, the Hollow does not roar / the Hollow opens like a door.*" She snorted. "Terrible meter. Accurate theology. Come on. Quick and quiet, in that order."

* * *

They followed the bats down.

It was hard, afterward, for Ansel to keep the descent in order in her memory — partly because memory was about to become currency and she was spending it faster than she knew, and partly because the tunnel refused to behave like a place. Stairs gave way to ladders rusted to lace; ladders gave way to slopes of rubble; once, for a hundred yards, the "stair" was simply a seam in the rock that the miners had widened just enough for a stooped man with a sack, and Ansel crawled it on her elbows with cold water in her ears and a bat's song painting the passage for her stroke by stroke, and thought, with a kind of hysterical clarity: *I am inside the mountain. I am inside the throat of the thing that ate my brother. And I am humming.*

But there was wonder in it too, and she held onto the wonder the way you hold onto a rope over a drop, because the alternative was terror and terror made noise.

There was a chamber where the ceiling opened into a cathedral of pale stone columns, and the bats' combined song filled it so completely that Ansel could see it — *hear*-see it, a vision laid over the darkness like breath on glass — every pillar and rib and hanging fold of rock rendered in silver threads of returning sound. She stood in the middle of it with her head tipped back and her mouth open and felt, for one suspended moment, that blindness might be the truest way to see a place like this. Eyes would only get in the way. Eyes expected light, and light had no business here.

There was a stretch where the water rose to their thighs and then their waists, and Odile held the lantern overhead with both arms shaking, cursing with the fluency of a woman who had sworn ward-oaths and therefore had a large vocabulary to fall back on, and Ansel laughed — actually laughed, a real one, startled out of her — and the sound came back off the walls smaller than it went in, as if the tunnel had taken a bite.

They didn't laugh after that.

There was a place where the old workings ended and the older dark began, and the boundary between them was marked by a door. Not a miner's door. A door of black wood bound in green metal gone to verdigris, set into the rock with no frame and no hinge visible, and carved across its face in a script that hurt to look at directly — not because it was ugly but because it was *hungry*, because the letters seemed to lean toward the reader like plants toward sun.

Odile stopped ten feet from it and would not come closer. Ansel understood. Some distances were professional.

"This is the threshold," Odile said. "Past here, the wards never reached. Past here, my order's authority ends and the Court's begins. Everything we've walked through tonight — that was the mountain's own dark, natural dark, honest dark." She nodded at the door. "That's the other thing."

"How do we open it?"

"We don't. It opens for payment." Odile's jaw worked. "I told you the tolls start early. This is the first one. Small. A trifle, the courtiers used to call it, back when my order still traded with them — before the trade curdled. A memory. Something small. You choose what, and the door takes it, and you won't miss it, that's the trick of it, you won't even know it's gone unless someone tells you."

"Someone like you."

"Someone like me." Odile's voice went rough. "I've stood at doors like this before, Ansel. During the last negotiations, forty years ago. I watched a senior warden pay a toll and walk through smiling, and three days later she couldn't remember her daughter's name, and everyone told her she'd never had a daughter, and she believed them, because why wouldn't she? The door doesn't leave a wound. It leaves a smooth place." She looked at Ansel, and in the lantern light her eyes were wet and furious. "So think carefully. Choose something you can afford to lose. And let me watch your face when you pay, so that later, if you go strange, I can tell you what you lost."

The bats had gone quiet. All twelve of them hung from the ceiling above the door in a row, upside down, wings folded, like a jury that had already deliberated.

Ansel stood in the waist-deep water and took inventory of herself.

Her mother's face — no. Not yet. Not that. She'd need it. Whatever waited below, she'd need the reason she was doing this, and the reason had a face, and the face had taught her a lullaby.

Tomas — he wasn't a memory, he was a wound, and wounds don't fit through keyholes.

The mountain lion from the pass, the bargain they'd struck, the great golden weight of her leaning against Ansel's shoulder in the dark — no. That was armor.

Smaller, then. Smaller and dearer and survivable.

"I know what I'll pay," she said.

"Don't tell me what. Tell me *that* you know, and then pay it, and let me remember for you."

Ansel nodded. She stepped forward, up onto the first dry stone in a mile, and laid her palm flat against the black wood.

The door was warm. That was the worst part. Everything down here was cold except the things that wanted something, and those were warm as living flesh.

*Take,* she thought, not knowing if the door heard thoughts or only memories, and not caring, and choosing: *the afternoon. The nameless one. The good day.*

Because there had been one — one perfect, ordinary afternoon, years ago, before any of this, when she'd sat on a rooftop in some border town with someone whose name she suddenly, deliberately, could not hold onto, drinking something, laughing at something, the sun warm and the world simple, and it had been the single most contented hour of her life and it belonged to no story, led nowhere, meant nothing except that she had once been happy without earning it.

She gave it away gladly. That was the payment's cruelty and also its mercy: the door took the afternoon entire, the rooftop, the drink, the laughter, the unnamed someone, and left behind only the knowledge that a happiness had been removed, a smooth place, a socket where a pearl had been.

The door sighed open inward, revealing stairs — descending, wide, worn concave by feet long dead, lit from below by a light that was not fire and not moonlight but the color of lanterns seen through fog.

Ansel stepped back, blinking, her cheeks wet without her permission.

"Gone?" Odile asked gently.

"Gone." Ansel wiped her face with a hand that shook only a little. "And I know what it was, which is worse somehow. I know I had a perfect afternoon once. I just — can't go there anymore. The room's empty and swept."

"That's the toll," Odile said. "That's all of them, forever, all the way down. Every payment leaves the room clean." She picked up the lantern. "Ready?"

Above them, softly, the twelve bats began to sing again — and their song had changed. It was slower now, deeper, woven through with harmonics Ansel hadn't heard before, and when she listened with the other sense, the beast-sense, the gift-sense, the sense that had gotten her mother burned, she understood what they were singing.

They were singing her name. Or the shape of it. The echo of her, returned from the dark below, so that whatever lived down there would hear her coming and know she was *announced* — vouched for, escorted, claimed by the small blind things of the mountain as one of their own loud-hearing kind.

"Ansel," she said, translating, and her voice cracked on it. "They're telling the court I'm under their protection."

"Do you believe that will hold?"

She watched the stair descend into the fog-colored glow, and felt through her boot soles the vast slow breathing of the starving thing below, and thought of Tomas's soul burning in a ledger, a flame among thousands, and of a queen who had waited so long that waiting had become her whole nature.

"No," she said honestly. "But it's the first time in my life anyone's sung for me instead of against me. So I'm going to walk down there anyway."

She put her foot on the first step.

Far below — miles below, or inches, distance meant nothing down here — something vast turned over in its sleep, and the water in the flooded stair trembled in rings, and every bat in the mountain began, very softly, to sing.
