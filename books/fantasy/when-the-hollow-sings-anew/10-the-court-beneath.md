## 10. The Court Beneath

The bats stopped singing all at once.

Ansel had grown so used to their song over the drowned stair — that strange clicking chorus that mapped the dark for her the way Odile's broken compass used to map the roads — that its absence hit her like a hand clapped over her ears. She froze on the last ledge above the flooded gallery, water to her knees, and listened.

Below them, something was singing back.

"It's not a song," Odile whispered. The old ward-keeper stood beside her, gray as wet ash, her one working lantern shuttered down to a coin of light. "It's counting."

"What could possibly be counting down here?"

"Souls." Odile said it the way you'd say *landslide* or *plague*. "The court keeps its books. It has always kept its books. My order just preferred not to think about what the arithmetic was *for*."

The bats wheeled overhead once — a farewell, Ansel thought, or a warning — and poured themselves back up the flooded shaft they'd descended, a river of soft bodies flowing into the dark. She felt oddly abandoned. Then she felt the singing again, and stopped feeling anything much at all.

Because it wasn't coming from below. It was coming from everywhere. From the stone itself. A sound like ten thousand voices humming in unison, except none of them were voices exactly — they were more like the memory of voices, the way a bell remembers being struck long after the ringing stops.

"Odile." Her own voice came out small. "What is that?"

The ward-keeper didn't answer right away. She lifted her lantern, unshuttered it a finger's width, and the light fell across the cavern ahead — and Ansel understood why the bats had gone quiet.

They had reached the Hollow Court.

* * *

She had imagined a cave. Of course she had. Twenty years of smuggler stories had painted it as a hole in the world: dripping dark, bones in puddles, something old and hungry coiled at the bottom like a worm in an apple. The smugglers who claimed to have seen it were liars, and the ones who claimed to have heard it were worse than liars, because they'd heard only fragments and filled in the rest with nightmare.

None of them had told her it was beautiful.

The cavern opened beneath them like the inside of a struck bell, vast beyond any sense of size, and it was built — no, *grown*, she corrected herself; there wasn't a joint or seam anywhere, the whole architecture flowed like frozen music — it was grown out of bone. Not heaped bone. Not the bone of slaughter. Ribs arched into vaulted ceilings a hundred feet overhead, pale and translucent, and behind them burned lights. Lanterns, or what served for lanterns here: glass globes of every shape, hanging from rib-cables and root-lines and threads too fine to see, thousands upon thousands of them, each holding a flame that did not flicker.

And the flames were colored. Warm gold, deep amber, a green like spring water, a blue like the hour before dawn. They lit the bone cathedral in shifting constellations, so that looking up felt like looking at a sky someone had loved very carefully.

"Oh," Ansel said. It was not a word she meant to say aloud. "Oh, that's not fair."

"What isn't?"

"Something this cruel shouldn't be *lovely*." Her throat was tight. "It should be ugly. It should look like what it does."

Odile's jaw worked. "My grandmother kept a lamp lit for my grandfather for forty years after the mountain took him," she said quietly. "Beautiful things make better traps. That's half of what wards are — pretty lies that hold the door shut." She shuttered her lantern fully now; there was no need. "Come on. Stay close to the wall. And whatever burns down there — do not reach for it."

They climbed down through galleries of rib-stone, along paths worn smooth by feet that had come before them — some human-shaped, some not, some with toes that curved like hooks. And as they descended, the flames drew closer, until Ansel could see what the lanterns actually were.

Glass. Yes. But inside the glass, where a wick should be, burned a small figure of light. Some were shaped like people mid-stride. Some curled like sleeping children. Some flared and guttered restlessly, pressing against the curve of their prisons the way a moth presses at a window.

Each one was a soul.

There were thousands. Tens of thousands. They hung in ranks along the walls, clustered in chandeliers over open courts of polished bone, strung like festival lights down corridors whose ends were lost in glow. And among the galleries moved figures — tall, slender, wrong in ways Ansel's eye kept sliding off of — carrying ladders and tongs and ledgers bound in something that was not leather. The hollow courtiers went from flame to flame with the unhurried attention of lamplighters, trimming, adjusting, and occasionally lifting a globe down, holding it up to the bone-light, and making a note.

Every time a globe came down, the humming faltered — one voice dropping out of the vast chord — then resumed, thinner.

"They're *spending* them," Ansel breathed. "That's the currency. That's what the pledges buy. They burn souls for light."

"For everything," Odile murmured. "Light. Warmth. The wards, I'd wager, though I never knew it. Every favor the mountain ever did us." She was gripping her lantern so hard the shutter bit into her fingers. "Gods. We paid our debts in other people and called it civilization."

Ansel couldn't answer. She was watching a courtier trim a flame — reaching into the globe with tongs that looked like nothing so much as a mother's fingers combing hair — and the little light-figure shrank away from the touch, and the courtier adjusted it anyway, gentle and precise and utterly indifferent, the way you'd straighten a picture frame.

Somewhere under her ribs, older than thought, something began to sing back at the great chord. Low. Angry. Her gift, rising without permission, answering hunger with hunger.

*Not yet,* she told it. *Not yet. We're guests.*

The singing under her ribs subsided to a growl. But it didn't stop. And she noticed, with a chill, that several of the nearest flames had turned — the little figures inside the glass orienting toward her, faces she hadn't known they had pressed to the inner curve of their prisons.

They could feel her. The beast-speaker blood, the thing that got her mother burned — down here it rang like a struck bell in a room full of the deaf, suddenly given hearing.

"Walk," Odile said through her teeth. "Don't run. Running means something to hunt."

* * *

They moved through the courts of light for hours, it seemed. Time behaved strangely; the humming made it stretch. Ansel catalogued what she saw the way she catalogued mountain passes — exits, patrols, weight-bearing arches, places where the bone grew thin enough that a wolf's claws might find purchase. Old habits. If this went badly, she wanted to know how to run out of it.

But part of her — the larger part, if she was honest — wasn't cataloguing at all. It was searching.

Because Tomas was here. Somewhere among these tens of thousands of small burning prisoners, her brother's soul hung in a glass globe, and had hung there for twenty years while she learned to track and bargain and keep her head down, while she ate birthday dinners and slept warm and never once, not once, felt the pull of him through the stone. He had been under her feet the whole time. All those years guiding caravans through Thornfell, she had walked on top of her brother.

The thought arrived like cold water and stayed like ice.

"Odile." She stopped in the shadow of a rib-arch the size of a cathedral door. "How does the Ledger work? The book of sales. Sef— the courtier Odile, the one who wrote to me—" she caught herself; they hadn't spoken of Sef Andrade since the mine shaft, and saying his name down here felt like shouting in a library — "the letter said names live in a Ledger. If I find the Ledger, I find him."

"If you find the Ledger, you find the heart of the court, and the heart of the court finds *you* right back." Odile leaned against the bone, breathing hard. She was sixty years old, half-drunk when they'd met, and three days underground had not improved her. Still her eyes were sharp. "But yes. Everything sold is written. Entry, seal, price, term. If your brother was sold, there's a line with his name on it, and lines point to shelves, and shelves point to globes."

"And where's the Ledger kept?"

"At the center. Where else?" Odile nodded down the gallery, toward the deepest bowl of the cavern, where the lantern-light gathered so thickly it seemed almost solid. "The Court Proper. The Queen's hall. Where the pact was signed, if half my training was true."

"The Queen," Ansel said. "You've seen her."

"No one's seen her. That's rather the point of queens." Odile rubbed her face. "I've seen what she leaves. Wards drained to lace. Wardens found hollowed, sitting peaceful as saints. Once — I was young, I was stupid, I went looking — I found a chamber where the air was so *hungry* it pulled the warmth out of my hands. I ran. I've been running ever since, mostly downhill and into bottles." She glanced sideways at Ansel. "You're going to ask me anyway."

"I'm going to ask you anyway."

"She'll know we're here the moment we mean anything. Until then we're two mice in a granary. So don't mean anything yet." Odile pushed off the wall. "Ledger first. Brother second. Queen never, ideally."

They went on.

* * *

It was a child's laughter that undid her.

Not real laughter — the memory of it, trapped in a globe near the gallery path, a small blue flame shaped like a boy mid-leap, and as they passed it played its moment over and over: joy, joy, joy, sealed in glass, burning as somebody else's lamplight. Ansel stopped without meaning to. The little flame leapt toward the glass toward her, once, twice, and the motion had the unmistakable rhythm of a child reaching for a parent's hand.

Her eyes were burning. She wiped them impatiently. There was no time for every grief in this place; there would be time for none of them, if she spent herself on strangers' sorrows.

But the globe's light dimmed as she turned away, and she felt it — felt it *ask* — and hated the court a degree hotter for having taught her that souls could beg.

"That one's recent," Odile said softly. "A decade, maybe less. The old ones stop asking."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I helped write the pledges, girl. I told you. My order anchored the border wards on court-debt. We signed families over, generation after generation, and told ourselves it was tribute, not theft." Her voice cracked on the last word and she mastered it. "I've been drinking since the wards fell because sober, I can count. Do you know how many entries a hundred and forty years of ward-keeping makes? I can count."

Ansel put her hand on the old woman's shoulder — briefly, because neither of them was built for comfort — and they walked on.

The galleries narrowed as they descended toward the Court Proper. The globes hung closer together here, and the flames burned brighter — newer stock, Ansel realized, souls fresher from the surface, Marchen's conscriptions already arriving. Some still wore the shapes of soldiers. One, horribly, wore the shape of a woman holding a bundle, and the bundle was light too, and Ansel made herself look away because looking was a wound that didn't close.

Then the gallery opened onto a balcony of rib-stone overlooking the Court Proper itself —

— and the humming swelled, and Ansel forgot how to breathe.

The central hall was a hollow sphere of bone a quarter mile wide, and every surface of it was shelved, and every shelf was full. Globes rose in spirals toward the distant crown of the dome, tier upon tier upon tier, a galaxy of imprisoned light, hundreds of thousands of flames arranged with terrible tenderness, the way a miser arranges coins: lovingly, and to be spent.

At the center of the sphere, suspended on cables of root and gristle, hung the Ledger. It was the size of a fishing boat. Its pages turned by themselves, slowly, endlessly, and with every turn a voice dropped out of the great chord somewhere and another small light dimmed.

Courtiers drifted between the shelves on ladders and on wings she refused to examine closely, tending the stock. And above it all — around it all — through it all, so vast that Ansel's mind kept refusing the fact of it, something *waited*. She couldn't see it. She could feel it the way you feel weather coming in a bad tooth. A patience. A hunger so old it had forgotten to be urgent.

The Queen, not moving, filling the hall like pressure fills the deep sea.

"Don't look up," Odile whispered. "Don't look up, don't listen, don't—"

But Ansel wasn't looking up. She was looking at the third shelf tier, east face, where the light ran blue-gold in long family rows — old stock, well-tended — and where, forty globes along, a flame burned that made her gift scream in her chest like a hawk on a wrist.

It was small. It was dimmer than its neighbors, trimmed close by decades of careful spending. But its shape — its shape was a boy of eleven standing very straight the way their mother had taught him to stand, chin up, fists at his sides, stubborn even as light. And as Ansel stared, the little figure turned. Pressed both palms flat against the inside of the glass. Leaned there.

Twenty years. Twenty years under her feet, and the first thing her brother's soul did on seeing her was press its hands against the glass the way children do at windows.

"Tomas," she said.

The name left her like blood leaving a wound, and the great chord stuttered — thousands of flames turning at once toward the balcony, toward the voice that had spoken a true name aloud in the one hall where names were currency — and below them, in the dark above the Ledger, the waiting thing shifted its attention like a glacier deciding to notice an ant.

Odile grabbed her arm. "*Don't*—"

Too late. The humming had changed key. Something in the Court Proper had begun, very slowly, to descend.

And out of the shadow of the shelves — unhurried, alone, wearing the polite smile of a man about to do his job — came a courtier who was not entirely hollow, whose eyes still remembered how to be sorry.

"Well," he said. "A speaker. The Queen will want to hear this."
