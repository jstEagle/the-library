## 11. Courtier of Ash

Sef Andrade had been counting the lanterns again.

It was a habit he'd developed in his first decade below — a way of proving to himself that his mind still worked, that the hollowing hadn't eaten the part of him that noticed things. There were four hundred and twelve lanterns in the Long Gallery of the Hollow Court, and every one of them burned on a soul. He knew this because he had once been assigned to trim their wicks, back when he was new and obedient and still flinched at the light.

He no longer trimmed wicks. These days he kept the ledgers' outer shelves dusted and said very little, which was the safest occupation in the court. Dusting required no opinions. Opinions, in the Hollow Court, were currency too — and like all currency here, they could be taken from you.

He was dusting shelf nine hundred and six when he heard them coming.

Not footsteps. The court's servants made no sound; they glided like thoughts you'd rather not have. No — what Sef heard was *breathing*. Two sets of it, ragged and human and trying very hard to be quiet, which meant they were failing at both. One breath belonged to someone older, wheezing slightly on the exhale, muttering under her breath in the cadence of a woman reciting ward-geometry to keep herself calm. The other breath was younger, steadier, but with a hitch in it — the hitch of someone who had been crying recently and had decided, firmly, to stop.

Living people. In the third gallery. Past the toll-gates, past the whisper-bridges, past the corridor where the walls remembered your name whether you told them or not.

Sef set down his cloth.

He should call the sentinels. That was the correct action. The court rewarded correct actions with small mercies — an extra hour of warmth, a memory left unexamined for another season. He had been correct for sixty years. It had kept him mostly intact, mostly functional, mostly able to look at his reflection in the black water of the cistern without flinching overmuch.

But he had also been sold, once. And there was a part of him — the part the court had never managed to price — that had been waiting sixty years for living people to come down here, so that he could do something other than be correct.

He stepped out of the alcove and stood in the middle of the gallery, directly in their path, arms folded, wearing his most official expression.

They came around the corner of the bone arch and stopped dead.

The older one was short and square as a strongbox, gray-haired, with the ink-stained fingers of a lifelong clerk and the posture of someone who had been drunk within the last day and regretted it within the last hour. She had a broken compass hanging from her neck on a cord, and she was holding a lantern that gave off the wrong kind of light — soul-light, stolen from a waystation above, and it would cost her for that later, though she didn't know it yet.

The younger one was harder to read. Dark-haired, travel-worn, with a scar across one knuckle and eyes that moved the way a tracker's eyes move — not darting, but *sorting*, cataloguing exits and weights and threats. And something else. Something Sef felt before he understood it: a faint warmth in the air around her, like a hearth in a cold house. Beasts had passed near her recently. Beasts had *answered* her.

*Oh*, thought Sef, with a sensation he hadn't felt in decades and took a moment to identify as delight. *Oh, this is going to be trouble.*

"You're standing in our way," the younger one said.

"I'm standing in a gallery," Sef said. "You're the ones in my way, technically. I was here first."

"Who are you?" the older one demanded. Her hand went to the compass — useless down here, its needle spinning in slow, drunken circles, but the gesture was habit, and habits keep people upright.

"Sef Andrade," he said. "Courtier of ash, third rank, custodian of the outer ledgers." He paused. "Formerly of Rivermoor. Formerly of a great many things."

"Courtier," the tracker repeated flatly. "So you're one of them."

"That depends," Sef said, "on how much time you have, and whether you'd like the long answer or the true one."

* * *

They did not, at first, trust him. This was reasonable. Sef respected it the way he respected good masonry — as evidence of sound construction.

The older one — Odile Ferran, formerly First Warden of the Cinderhatch line, currently a fugitive with a head full of borrowed rites — kept a knife half-drawn the entire time and asked questions like a woman auditing a tax return. The younger one — Ansel Quintana, though she only gave her first name — said almost nothing and watched everything, and Sef found himself increasingly aware that she was watching him the way one watches a bridge before trusting it with weight.

They sat in a maintenance alcove behind shelf nine hundred and six, because Sef knew which alcoves the sentinels ignored. Sentinels ignored places that smelled of dust and tedium. He had spent sixty years cultivating an atmosphere of profound tedium, and it was finally paying dividends.

"The long answer," Ansel said at last. "And the true one."

"They're the same answer," Sef said. "I was born in Rivermoor. Fisherman's son. When I was nineteen, my father fell ill, and there was a man who came through the docks offering loans against — well, he didn't say *souls*. He said 'future consideration.' Very elegant language. My father signed. I was the collateral." He turned his hands over, palms up. They looked ordinary. They were, mostly. "The court collected twenty years later. I came down the Drowned Stair myself, actually, walking, because by then the debt had grown roots. And the court took—" he paused, doing arithmetic he hated, "—most of me. Not all. Some souls are worth more whole, so they take pieces. A memory here, a want there. They leave enough to work. You're more useful as a courtier if you remember being robbed. Bitterness is excellent for attention to detail."

Odile's knife hand had gone slack. "Sixty years," she said quietly. "You've been down here sixty years."

"Fifty-eight," Sef corrected. "I don't round up. Rounding up is optimism, and optimism gets audited."

"And yet you're helping us," Ansel said. "Why?"

There it was. The question he'd rehearsed for six decades and never once answered aloud.

He considered lying — telling them about rebellion, about a network of sympathizers, about some grand plan. But lies down here had a texture the court could taste, and besides, these two deserved better than a story. The warden had come down knowing the wards were built on pledged souls. The tracker had come down — Sef watched her hands, which kept drifting toward a scrap of cloth at her belt, a crest he recognized, because he dusted the ledger shelf where that crest appeared — the tracker had come down for someone specific.

"I hate what the court has become," he said simply. "That's the whole of it. You'll hear people say the court is necessary — the pact, the balance, the mountain held up by old promises. Maybe. I've had fifty-eight years to think about it, and I've concluded that somewhere along the way, necessity became appetite. The Queen starves, yes. But the *courtiers* — we learned to eat. We learned to enjoy the ledger. We stopped being jailers and became gourmets." His voice stayed level, which took effort. "I remember being sold. That's the joke of it — they left me the memory of my own sale because it makes me diligent. So every day I file entries, and every entry I file, I remember exactly what it felt like to be a number in a book. And I have decided, in the privacy of my own remaining self, that I am finished filing."

Silence. Somewhere far off, a lantern guttered and re-steadied, and Sef felt its soul shift in its sleep.

"You could be lying," Odile said.

"Wonderfully paranoid. You'll do well here." Sef smiled, and was aware that his smile probably wasn't reassuring — half-hollow faces did strange things in lantern-light. "Yes, I could be lying. Here's how you test me: ask me something a loyal courtier would never admit. Go on. Anything."

Ansel leaned forward. For the first time since entering the alcove, she spoke with her whole voice. "Where is Tomas Quintana?"

Sef had known the question was coming — had seen it building in her like weather. Even so, hearing the name landed hard, because he *knew* the entry. Shelf four hundred and eleven. Sold twenty years ago, age seven, sealed under Marchen writ. One of the oldest intact flames in the collection, prized precisely because it had never been spent down.

"Gallery of the Small Flames," he said. "Third tier, eastern wall. He's—" he caught himself, gentled it. "He's preserved. Whole. Which down here means untouched, not happy. Souls in the ledger lose their names over time. The flame will know warmth when it sees you. It may not know why."

Ansel's breath went out of her slowly, controlled, the way people breathe when they are holding themselves together with both hands. Odile put a hand on her shoulder, and Ansel let it stay there for exactly three seconds before straightening.

"Then we go now."

"No," Sef said.

"We didn't come this far—"

"You go *now*, you die *now*, and worse, he burns as contraband." Sef held up a finger. "Please. Fifty-eight years. Let me finish a sentence; it happens so rarely. The galleries between here and the Small Flames pass the Toll of Names — a gate that takes one true name from everyone who passes. Give it yours, and you'll forget your own before you reach him. What good is finding your brother if you arrive as a stranger?"

Odile swore softly, creatively, in three dialects.

"But," Sef continued, "there are ways. Service corridors. The courtiers use them; the sentinels are forbidden to, which tells you everything about how much the court trusts itself. I can walk you to the Small Flames without passing a single gate — if we move during the Changeover, when half the court sleeps and the other half pretends to." He paused. "In exchange, I want one thing."

"Name it," Ansel said instantly.

"Careful," Sef said, and for a moment his voice lost all its dryness. "Down here, 'name it' is how people end up owing things. Listen first. All I want — all — is that when you leave, whatever you do, however this ends, you take the ledger entries with you. Copies. Every sale. Every seal. Especially the Marchen seals." He saw Odile's head snap up. "Yes. *Those.* The court thinks paper is safe because paper can't run. Paper just needs someone willing to carry it. I've been willing for fifty-eight years. I needed hands that weren't already owned."

* * *

They spent the next hours — hours being approximate below, where time pooled rather than flowed — preparing, and Sef discovered something alarming: he was enjoying himself.

This was not supposed to happen. Enjoyment attracted attention. But Odile Ferran turned out to have the particular humor of ward-keepers, who spend their lives maintaining invisible structures and therefore find everything funny in a tired, structural way. When Sef explained that the sentinels navigated by scent-memory and that the service corridors therefore smelled of lye and boredom, she snorted tea through her nose — real tea, hoarded from the surface, which she shared with the generosity of the perpetually hungover — and declared that she'd worked with worse security.

"I once guarded a vault," she said, wiping her eyes, "where the lock was a riddle and the riddle was written on the *outside of the door*. Forty years. Nobody broke in. Do you know why? Because nobody believed it."

"People believe what frightens them," Sef agreed. "The court runs entirely on that principle. We call it governance."

Ansel, meanwhile, asked questions — precise ones, in the manner of someone mapping a route in her head and discarding nothing. How many sentinels on the night rotation. Whether the blind bats came down this deep (they did; Sef fed them, or rather left scraps they pretended not to need). Whether the flames could feel being watched. He answered honestly, and each honest answer seemed to firm something in her, as though honesty were a currency she'd expected to find debased down here and was relieved to find circulating.

At one point she paused, studying him across the alcove's dim glow.

"You said they left you the memory of your own sale," she said. "Do you remember before? Rivermoor? Your father?"

"Some," Sef said. "A boat. The smell of tar. My father singing off-key on purpose to make me laugh, and me laughing on purpose to make him think he could sing." He smiled at the middle distance. "They took the big things. The grief, mostly. Grief is heavy and heavy things burn bright. But they couldn't take the shape of it. I know something enormous used to live in me and was removed, the way you know a tooth is missing. I built my life in the gap."

Ansel was quiet a moment. "My mother was burned for beast-speaking," she said. "Or that's what I was told. I came down here because my brother was sold, and I'm starting to suspect I don't know anything true about either of them."

"Then you're ahead of most people," Sef said. "Most people never find out. They just carry the false story until it wears smooth as river stone and hand it to their children." He tapped the shelf beside him — shelf nine hundred and six, dusted daily for decades. "Everything true is written down somewhere in this court. That's its one virtue and its entire horror. If you want the truth about your mother, it's here. It might cost you. Truth always does, down here — the tolls aren't only at the gates."

Odile and Ansel exchanged a look — the look of two people silently renegotiating a plan — and Sef, who had spent fifty-eight years reading the small politics of the powerless, pretended not to see it, and began folding copies of ledger pages into oilcloth with the contentment of a man doing, at last, the only work that had ever mattered to him.

* * *

The Changeover came the way it always did: a shiver through the lantern-light, a collective settling, the court exhaling into its shallow sleep. Sef led them out through the maintenance corridors — low passages ribbed with root and old stone, smelling of lye and mineral dark — moving with the unhurried certainty of a man walking through his own house.

Twice they froze as sentinels drifted past the corridor mouths, tall and wrong, faces like unfinished sketches. Both times, Sef stood calmly in the open, dustcloth over his arm, radiating tedium, while Ansel and Odile pressed into shadow. Both times the sentinels slid past without pausing, and Sef allowed himself a private, ancient satisfaction.

Near the last junction, Ansel touched his sleeve.

"The bats," she murmured. "You feed them."

"I leave scraps. They don't eat. I think they just like knowing where I am." He glanced at her sideways. "You speak to beasts. Don't deny it — I've seen the way the dark leans toward you. That gift got people burned, once upon a time."

"It got my mother burned," Ansel said. "Allegedly."

"Mm." Sef faced forward again. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much, I'm a dusty little clerk in a cathedral of thieves — I hope it's true. Whatever your mother did. Because this place has been waiting a long time for someone who can talk to things that aren't people. The court only understands debts. Somebody down here ought to understand songs."

Ahead, through a grate of black iron, the Gallery of the Small Flames opened up: thousands of lights, tier on tier, burning in their niches like a city seen from a mountainside at night. Somewhere on the eastern wall, third tier, a flame that had once been a boy named Tomas Quintana waited without knowing it was waiting.

Sef checked the corridor, found it empty, and lifted the grate.

"There," he whispered. "Go and get him back. And Quintana—" he pressed the oilcloth packet of copied pages into her hands, warm from his keeping, "—when you learn what your mother really did, try to remember that some of us down here have been reading those pages for years, hoping someone would come who'd know what to do with them."

Ansel looked at him — really looked, the way she'd looked at bridges — and nodded once.

Then she slipped through the grate into the gallery of small fires, and Sef Andrade settled down in the dark to keep watch, dusting nothing, guarding everything, happier than he had been in fifty-eight years.
