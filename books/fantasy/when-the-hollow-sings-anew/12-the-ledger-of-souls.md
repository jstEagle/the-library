## 12. The Ledger of Souls

Sef Andrade had been a courtier for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned exactly three things worth knowing.

First: never let the hollow things see you flinch. They could smell fear the way moths smelled lamplight, and once they caught the scent they would worry at you like children worrying a loose tooth.

Second: the lantern-light in the Court was not light at all but memory burning slow, and if you stared too long at any single flame you would begin to remember things that had never happened to you — someone else's wedding, someone else's drowning — and you would spend the rest of your shift trying to shake them loose.

Third, and most important: there was a way to walk through the galleries that made no sound on the bone floor, and he had spent nearly a decade perfecting it, and it was ruined forever now, because he had gone and acquired *companions*.

"Stop dragging your feet," he hissed over his shoulder.

"I'm not dragging my feet," Ansel whispered back. "I'm walking quietly. This is my quiet walk."

"It sounds like a sack of turnips falling down a stairwell."

"That's insulting to my walking."

Behind them both, Odile Ferran snorted — a rich, unguarded sound that echoed off the ribbed ceiling and made Sef's heart stop twice in the space of one breath. He turned around fully, which was against every rule he'd set himself, and pressed one finger to his lips with the desperate emphasis of a man mime-acting his own funeral.

The gallery gave them a moment of silence, as galleries went. It was built of vertebrae the size of doors, arched into vaults that some long-dead mason had carved with vines and birds and other jokes about the world above. Lanterns hung in their hundreds from hooks of yellowed ivory, each one a sold soul burning at its own private pace. Some flickered fast, like nervous candles. One near the far arch burned so slowly it seemed barely alive at all, and Sef made a point of never looking at it, because he was fairly sure — though he had never confirmed it, and never would — that it was burning at the pace of a child.

Odile had stopped snorting. She was staring up at the lanterns with her mouth slightly open, and her hand had found Ansel's sleeve without either of them appearing to notice.

"You said souls," Odile murmured. "You said it, and I believed you, the way you believe a map. But this isn't a map of it. This is the country."

"Yes," Sef said. "Now do you understand why we whisper?"

"I understand why you're rude," Odile said. "The whispering I'm still deciding about."

He should have left them in the flooded stair. That was the honest accounting, and Sef was a great believer in honest accounting; it was practically all he had left. The woman with the tracker's eyes and the woman who smelled of spilled brandy and old oaths were a liability stacked on top of a catastrophe, and every hour they remained in his care was an hour closer to the moment when something vast and patient noticed them and asked, in its voice like a bell heard through a mountain, *whose are these?*

But the tracker had said her brother's name, back at the edge of the water — Tomas, she'd said, Tomas Quintana, sold twenty years ago — and she had said it the way people say names they've carried so long the name has worn smooth as river stone. And Sef, who had once been sold under a name too, and who had spent eleven years being called only *courtier*, or *you there*, or nothing at all — Sef had looked at her and felt the oldest thing in him, the part the Hollow had never managed to eat, sit up and pay attention.

So here they were, sneaking through the Gallery of Small Debts toward the Vault of Records, where the Ledger sat open on its stand of fused spines, waiting, as it always waited, for the next name.

"This way," he said. "And if anyone speaks to you, let me answer."

"Who's going to speak to us?" Ansel asked. "It's a cathedral made of bones in the middle of a mountain under a starless sky."

"You'd be surprised," Sef said grimly. "The court keeps clerks."

* * *

The clerks were the worst of it, honestly. Worse than the sentries, worse than the Queen's distant breathing that sometimes came up through the floor like tide. The sentries were hollowed soldiers, empty as winter branches, and you could avoid empty things. But the clerks had been left just enough of themselves to hold pens, and pens required hands, and hands required arms, and arms came attached to faces, and the faces still remembered how to smile.

They passed two of them in the antechamber before the Vault — robed figures bent over side-ledgers, updating interest, Sef supposed, adjusting the weight of this debt against that one, moving small flames between shelves with tongs of black glass. One of them glanced up as they crossed the room, and its face did the thing Sef dreaded most: it smiled with genuine warmth.

"Sef," it said pleasantly. "Carrying guests?"

"Inventory," Sef said smoothly, without breaking stride. "Two lots, pending appraisal. The Queen wants them weighed before the next tithe."

"Mm. Unusual route." The clerk's pen scratched on. Its eyes stayed on them a half-second too long, mild as milk. "Mind the third step. It remembers falling."

They walked on. Only when the Vault door had swallowed them and Sef had thrown both bolts — real iron, a relic from the days when the court had still bothered pretending to be a place with locks — only then did he allow himself to lean against the stone and breathe.

"'It remembers falling,'" Ansel repeated. "What does that mean?"

"It means the third step remembers falling, and occasionally shares the memory with whoever steps on it. You'll dream about a miner named Brannock for a week. Don't fight it. He was lonely."

Ansel stared at him. In the grey light of the Vault — the only light here came from the Ledger itself, a pale glow like moonlight through ice — her face had the look of someone recalculating everything they'd agreed to.

"How long have you been down here?" she asked quietly.

"Eleven years." Sef pushed off the door and straightened his courtier's robe, which was woven from moth-grey silk that never quite settled. "I was sold at nineteen. Debt of my father's, four generations deep. The collectors came in autumn. I remember the apples were in." He said it lightly, because he had practiced saying it lightly until the lightness held. "The first year is the worst. They take your name early — it's collateral, you see. Everything else can wait, but a name is liquid. Spends anywhere."

"And after?"

"After you're a courtier. You serve the court's accounts. You learn that hunger has excellent manners." He gestured ahead of them, into the Vault's heart. "And you learn where everything is written. Come on. You wanted proof. Proof is the one thing this place keeps in surplus."

* * *

The Ledger lay open on its stand in the center of the Vault, and it was smaller than Odile had expected — Sef saw her disappointment arrive and rearrange itself into awe, the way weather changes. Everyone expected a tome the size of a table, something dramatic. Instead it was a book scarcely bigger than a hymnal, bound in leather gone soft as cloth, its pages thin and faintly warm. But it was thick the way a well is deep. Pages kept surfacing that had not been there a moment before, rising gently through the book like bubbles through dark water, and each new page arrived already written.

"It writes itself," Odile breathed. She had drifted forward without touching the floor, Sef noticed — the old ward-keeper's training, the instinct to approach power on the balls of the feet. Her hand hovered above the page without landing. "Every sale. Every pledge."

"Every sale, every pledge, every inheritance of debt, every forgiveness — of which there have been four in three hundred years, all of them mistakes." Sef came to stand beside her, close enough to feel the Ledger's warmth on his knuckles. "This is the court's heart. Not the Queen. Her. The Queen is only the hunger. This is the appetite's *bookkeeping*."

Ansel hadn't moved from the doorway. She stood with her shoulders square and her hands loose at her sides — a fighter's posture, or a walker's, someone used to ground under her that might at any moment decide not to be ground. "His name is Tomas," she said. "Tomas Quintana. Sold twenty years ago, give or take. Our mother signed something — I don't know what. I was seven. I remember men in grey coats and I remember the smell of the ink."

"Ink," Sef said softly. "Yes. The old contracts used blood, but blood degrades. Ink holds. Show me your palm."

She crossed the room and held out her hand without hesitation, which told him more about her than anything else she'd done. He turned it over, traced one finger along the lines of it — not reading fortune, whatever Odile's raised eyebrows suggested, but reading *lineage*. The Ledger knew its signatories. Blood remembered blood. It was the one mercy in the whole rotten architecture.

"Now ask it," he said. "Out loud. It's vain. It likes to be asked."

Ansel looked at the little warm book for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had dropped into something older than the smuggler's drawl she wore like a coat.

"Tomas Quintana," she said. "Sold beneath Thornfell. Show me."

The pages turned themselves.

Not quickly — the Ledger was never quick, it was patient the way debt is patient — but steadily, page after surface-page lifting and sliding past, entries flashing by in a script that shifted between alphabets the way a stream shifts between ripples. Sef watched the tracker's face and saw her try to read as they went, and fail, and stop trying, and simply *wait*, the way you wait out a storm in a doorway.

Then the turning slowed. Slowed further. Stopped.

The entry was short. They were all short. That was the obscenity of it — that a whole human life, nineteen years of it by then, could be compressed into six lines and a seal. Sef read it upside down, from long habit:

*SOLD INTO THE HOLLOW COURT: one (1) soul entire, male child, age nine years, name TOMAS QUINTANA, of Cinderhatch. Consideration rendered: discharge of ancestral debt, principal and interest, per schedule annexed. Condition of title: absolute. Term: perpetual.*

Beneath it, in the place where the sealing wax went, was a stamp pressed in silver-grey — not wax at all but metal leaf laid into the vellum, a crest Sef had seen ten thousand times on ten thousand documents, because it appeared on every contract of consequence the court had brokered in the last thirty years.

A mountain split by a road. Above the mountain, a star with eight points. Below it, a root shaped like a grasping hand.

The Marchen seal.

"No," Odile said. She had gone very still. "No, that's — Marchen was nobody, twenty years ago. A landholder's daughter with more ambition than acreage. She wasn't brokering court contracts. She couldn't have been."

"She was," Sef said, "if she inherited the brokerage." He touched the crest, carefully, the way you touch a burn to confirm it's real. "Look at the date. Look at the consideration clause. This isn't a family selling a child. This is a *creditor collecting*. The Quintanas owed someone. Someone held their paper. And when the debt came due and there was nothing else to take—" he tapped the entry, once, gently, "—the collector took what was left. The child. And discharged the rest as consideration."

Ansel's voice came out flat and careful, a voice walking on ice. "Whose paper? Who did we owe?"

"That," Sef said, "is the interesting question." He reached past her and laid his palm flat on the page. "Show the debtor of record," he told the Ledger. "Original instrument. Quintana line."

The pages stirred again — reluctantly, he thought, though books don't think, though God knew he had spent eleven years telling himself that — and produced a document older than any of them, older than the town of Cinderhatch by a generation, written in the brown ink that meant blood after all. A pledge. A standard one, the kind three hundred families in the free cities had signed in the fat confident years before anyone understood what the wards were actually eating: *we pledge to the Hollow Court such portion of our issue as the Court shall require, in satisfaction of protection rendered, ward maintained, star-pact observed.*

Signed at the bottom in a strong slanted hand.

Sef watched Ansel read the signature. He watched her read it twice. He watched her lips move around the shape of it without sound, and he thought, with the peculiar double vision of the long-hollowed — half in the room, half always somewhere colder — *there it is. There's the wound opening. Now we find out what she does with the knife.*

"Marchen," Ansel said finally. The name came out of her like a stone coming up from a well. "Veyra Marchen's grandmother held our family's debt. My mother didn't sell my brother. Your usurper *collected* him. Twenty years ago. Before anyone knew her name."

"The rise of houses is written in ledgers like this one," Sef said. "Nobody climbs to an army on charm. She took over the family brokerage, consolidated debts across four valleys, and discovered that the court paid commissions. Handsomely. In influence, in ward-tithes forgiven, in information." He smiled, and hated the feeling of his own smile, which these days moved a half-beat behind his intention like a shadow lagging its owner. "Your Veyra Marchen did not inherit her ambition, keeper Ferran. She inherited a *client list*. Every conscription she's announced since the stars went out — every 'protection levy' — those aren't new ideas. They're the same old contract with the numbers enlarged. She has been feeding this court her whole career. She simply used to feed it one child at a time, quietly, and call it debt service."

The silence in the Vault had a texture. Odile broke it, because Odile always would — Sef was beginning to suspect she'd break silence the way other people broke bread, generously, at every opportunity.

"So the woman who's going to save the free cities by feeding them to the Queen," Odile said, "built her house on selling children to it. And the ledger proves it. In her own seal. In *blood-brown ink*."

"In her own seal," Sef agreed. "Which brings us to the part I've been walking you down here to show you, and which I will now ask you to receive calmly, because I have found that 'calmly' is not the natural register of either of you—"

"Give it to us anyway," Ansel said.

He liked her enormously, in the way he permitted himself to like very few things, which was to say: briefly, dangerously, and against his better judgment.

"The Ledger cannot be argued with," he said. "That's its horror and its holiness — it doesn't lie, it doesn't exaggerate, it doesn't forget. Which means that everything in it is *evidence*. Evidence of every debt ever forged, every collection ever dressed up as justice, every soul taken under false pretenses and billed as fair trade. Including—" he turned back to the first entry, the little six-line obituary of a nine-year-old boy, and rested his fingertip beside the Marchen seal, not quite touching it, "—including this one. Do you understand what you're holding, tracker? You walked in here wanting to steal a soul out of grief. You're walking out holding a weapon that could unmake a queen. Grief is coal. This is a forge."

Ansel looked at the entry for a long time. Then she looked at her brother's name — *TOMAS QUINTANA*, in the Ledger's patient script, the letters as fresh as if they'd been written that morning, because for the Ledger they had been; it re-wrote every debt every day, the way a wound re-opens.

"He's in here," she said quietly. "All this time, I thought he was gone. Dead, or worse than dead. But he's *filed*. He's an entry. Six lines." Her jaw worked. "My mother's signature bought ward-protection for a valley that let her burn anyway."

"The valley didn't know," Odile said, and there was something raw in it, the rawness of a guild-woman watching her profession indicted. "The ward-keepers didn't know what the pledges fed. We were taught the pacts were *holy*. We rang the bells and called it covenant." She laughed, short and terrible. "We were the court's sales force and we sang while we worked."

"Yes," Sef said. "Welcome to the honest version of history. It's colder up here on the high ground, isn't it."

* * *

They stayed in the Vault until the Ledger's warmth had seeped into all their hands, copying what could be copied — Odile sketching the Marchen instruments from memory with a charcoal stub, her ward-keeper's shorthand flowing fast, twenty years of guild training finally pointed at the right target. Ansel mostly stood over her brother's entry and read it, and read it again, the way you press a bruise to be sure it's yours.

It was Ansel who noticed, in the end.

"Sef." Her voice had changed. He turned, and found her pointing at the bottom of Tomas's entry, at a line of fine print he had skipped past a hundred times in eleven years because fine print at the bottom of court documents was always boilerplate, always the same: *subject to adjustment per schedule, per discretion, per tithe.*

Except this line wasn't boilerplate.

*Note:* it read, in a different hand, older, hurried, *note: subject recalled for renegotiation upon renewal of star-pact. Holder instructed: keep flame distinct. — V.M.*

"Keep flame distinct," Ansel read aloud. "What does that mean? Why would she want his flame kept separate?"

Sef felt the cold come up through the bone floor then, the true cold, the one that had nothing to do with temperature. He looked at the note again. He looked at the hurried hand — he knew that hand, he filed that hand's correspondence weekly, he knew every abbreviation of it, every impatient crossbar.

And he understood, with the particular clarity available only to people standing inside the machine looking out, exactly what Veyra Marchen intended to do with a soul-flame she had kept distinct for twenty years.

"She's not going to feed him to the Queen with the rest," he said slowly. "A soul kept distinct. Held off-market for two decades. Appreciated." He looked up and met Ansel's eyes, and wished, briefly and uselessly, that he were still the kind of man who could lie well enough to soften this. "She's not bringing him as tribute, tracker. She's bringing him as *collateral*. The one soul in this mountain with a claim on the pact's renewal — the sibling of a beast-speaker, the son of a self-burned saint. When she makes her offer to the Queen, she won't offer the cities' souls alone. She'll offer the court a *bargaining chip it can't refuse.* Your brother is the keystone of her whole architecture. And she has just written down, in her own hand, in the court's own book, that she intends to spend him."

Above them — far above, up through miles of stone and root and the drowned stair — something in the mountain shifted its weight, the way a sleeper turns, and every lantern in the Gallery of Small Debts guttered at once, and steadied, and burned on.

Odile closed her sketchbook with a snap like a musket.

"Then," she said, "we had better get to him first."
