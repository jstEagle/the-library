## 13. Memory as Coin

The tollbooth was not what Ansel expected.

She had imagined something like the customs houses at Cinderhatch — a squat stone arch, a bored official, a ledger of goods and duties. What she got instead was a doorway made of lantern-light that had forgotten to be attached to a wall, standing alone in a corridor of bone-white stone, and behind it a small desk, and behind the desk a creature that might once have been a clerk.

It looked up at them with eyes like two coins held against a candle flame.

"One to pass," it said. Its voice was dry paper folding. "One to return. Tolls are collected in remembrance. The Court thanks you for your patronage."

"That's it?" Odile whispered. "That's the whole booth? Where's the wall? Where's the *rest* of the wall?"

"The Court does not believe in walls," said Sef Andrade, half-hollow courtier, traitor to his own kind, and — as of three hours ago — their reluctant guide. He said it the way a man describes weather he has long since stopped arguing with. "Walls imply boundaries. The Court prefers *arrangements*."

"It's a doorway floating in a hallway," Odile muttered. "In my day, architecture had opinions about load-bearing."

"Your day," said Sef gently, "was several centuries ago by this clock's reckoning, and I promise you the masons were no happier about it."

Ansel barely heard them. She was looking at the desk, at the little brass bell sitting beside an inkwell full of what she sincerely hoped was ink, and at the scale.

The scale was the problem. It was a merchant's balance, brass pans swaying on fine chains, except one pan held nothing at all and still hung lower than the other, as if weighed down by absence. As if nothing, here, could be very heavy indeed.

"How does it work?" she asked.

The clerk-creature turned its coin-flame eyes to her. "You think of a memory. You hold it out. The Court takes what it judges fair." A pause, dry as dust settling. "The Court is generous in its judgment."

"She means it isn't," Sef translated.

"I gathered," Ansel said.

Odile put a hand on her arm. Her grip was stronger than a drunk ward-keeper's should have been; three days underground had burned whatever was left of the drink out of her, and what remained was all iron and guilt. "Girl. We can find another way through. There's always another way through the undermountain — the old miners' maps—"

"The old miners' maps lead past the galleries where they keep the souls," Ansel said. "Sef told us. Past the *Ledger*. We need to go through the heart of this place, Odile, and there's exactly one door into the heart, and it's asking for a memory."

"And it will take more than it says."

"Yes," Ansel agreed. She rolled her shoulders, the way she did before a hard climb, before stepping onto scree that might slide, before any of the hundred small wagers with gravity that had kept her alive in Thornfell for twenty years. "So I'll pay attention to what it takes."

She stepped forward.

The clerk slid a sheet of pale paper across the desk — not white, exactly; the color of paper that had been thinking about becoming bone. On it, instructions appeared as she watched, letters rising like frost:

*Name the memory. Do not describe it. Description invites appraisal.*

"Handy," Ansel murmured. "Even the tollbooth knows I'd haggle."

She thought about which memory to give.

This was the arithmetic she'd been doing since the drowned stair, in the dark stretches where the bats' song couldn't reach: what could she afford to lose? Not the mountain passes — she needed those, every ridge and switchback, or she was no guide at all. Not the smugglers' faces, not the wolves who had carried her brother's name to her, not the sound of Tomas laughing before he was sold. Not the lullaby. Whatever happened, however hollowed-out she came out the far side of this place, she would walk into that cathedral of lanterns knowing the lullaby, because if some scrap of her brother still listened, the song was the only key she had.

Which left, among everything else, the thing she had guarded hardest and used least.

Her mother's face.

She hadn't seen it in twenty years — not truly, not since the burning, when grief had done what grief does and blurred the edges. When she tried to picture Maren Quintana now, she got the shape of her: tall, wind-burned, hands that were never still. She got the voice, clear as a bell rung in cold air. But the face itself, the particular face — the set of the mouth when she was worried, the exact brown of the eyes, whether there had been a scar over one brow or whether Ansel had invented that — the face had gone soft in her keeping, like a sketch left out in the rain.

*I'm only losing what I've already lost,* she told herself, and knew even as she thought it that this was the kind of lie you tell yourself right before doing the unforgivable thing, and that knowing didn't stop her.

"Ansel." Odile again, low. "Whatever you're choosing — don't choose your mother."

Ansel looked at her. "Why not?"

"Because I know how you're justifying it. 'I've already lost her.' That's the bargain everyone makes with this place, and it's the worst one, because—" Odile stopped. Her jaw worked. "Because I paid a toll myself, once. Long ago. To learn a rite I shouldn't have needed to buy." Her hand drifted toward her own temple, an old habit, touching a scar that wasn't visible. "And I can't remember anymore *why* I shouldn't have needed it. That's the trick, girl. The Court doesn't take the whole memory. It takes the *edges*. And then the middle sags, and then one day you reach for it and there's a hole shaped like a person where your whole reason used to be."

The clerk waited. The scale swayed. Somewhere far off in the corridors of bone, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the lantern-light flickered like a held breath.

"I know," Ansel said quietly. "But we're not here for me, Odile. We're here for Tomas. And I'd rather lose her face than lose him twice."

She leaned toward the paper and spoke the name of the memory — not aloud, quite; more inside-out, the way the bats sang their maps, the way the beasts spoke when they spoke true. *My mother's face. All of it. Every angle.*

The paper drank the words. The clerk nodded, once, with the terrible satisfaction of a bureaucrat whose form has been filled out correctly.

"Hold out your hand."

Ansel did.

What came was gentle. That was the horror of it. She braced for tearing, for theft, for the wolf-snap of something ripped away — but it arrived like sleep arriving, like warmth after cold, like her mother's hand smoothing hair back from her forehead on a fever night, *there now, there now,* and—

The scale tipped.

The empty pan sank, heavy with what it had been given, and somewhere in Ansel a door closed that she had not known was open, and she stood in the corridor of bones trying to remember why her cheeks were wet.

"Why am I crying?" she asked. Her voice sounded strange to her. Lighter. Unburdened. *Unburdened* — she caught the word as it went past and hated it.

"You paid a toll," said the clerk.

"For what?"

The coin-flame eyes regarded her without malice, which was somehow worse than malice would have been. Malice you could fight. This was simply process. "Passage," it said.

* * *

They walked.

The Hollow Court did not have hallways so much as it had *inclinations* — corridors that curved because they felt like curving, stairs that descended in directions Ansel's inner ear filed formal complaints about. The walls were ribbed white stone, and every few paces a lantern burned without flame, its light the color of the moment just before dawn. Sef moved through it easily, a man walking through his own house with the furniture rearranged by someone else's taste.

"You get used to the geometry," he offered, catching her expression after the fourth staircase that went down-and-sideways.

"I don't want to get used to it."

"No," Sef said. "I suppose you don't. That's rather the point of you." He glanced back at her, and in the pre-dawn light his half-hollow face looked almost kind — the human half, anyway. The other half was smooth and faintly luminous, like moonstone worn by water. "How much do you remember of the last hour?"

Ansel opened her mouth. Closed it. Ran backward through the last hour and found it... shorter than an hour should be. There was the tollbooth. There was the crying. There was — walking. A long gray stretch of walking with nothing in it, no thoughts, no observations, no snatches of the bats' song. Just movement, like a page with the middle paragraphs cut out and the margins sewn shut.

"It took more than the toll," she said.

"It took the walk home," Sef confirmed. He said it without drama, the way you'd note the price of bread going up. "Tolls here aren't transactions, they're *excisions*. The Court takes the memory, and then it tidies. Smooths the edges around the wound so you don't limp. Which sounds merciful." His jaw tightened. "It isn't. Because you don't know what's gone. You just feel lighter, and lightness feels like relief, and relief feels like proof you made the right choice. That's how it keeps people paying. Everyone down here is chasing the feeling of having lost less than they did."

Odile had stopped walking. "How many times did you pay?"

Sef smiled. It was not a happy smile, but it was an honest one, and Ansel was beginning to understand those were different things down here. "I stopped counting when I realized I'd counted wrong. Let's say: enough times to forget I was ever sold, and just enough scraps left to be angry about it. Anger keeps, apparently. It's gristle. The Court doesn't like the taste."

Ahead, the corridor opened into a gallery, and the gallery was *occupied*.

Lanterns, hundreds of them, hanging in tiers from a vaulted ceiling of fused vertebrae — and each lantern held a light that was not firelight, that moved when it moved the way a face moves in sleep. Soul-flames. Ansel had seen the cathedral from a distance, on their first terrible approach; she had seen the thousands. But this gallery was close work. Here she could see individual flames lean toward each other, drift apart, press against the glass of themselves.

She found herself slowing, searching the tiers despite herself, though Sef had warned her: *don't look for him here, this is storage, not the vault.* Looking anyway. Some part of her would look until she found him or died looking.

"Don't," Odile said softly, and put herself bodily between Ansel and the nearest tier. "Not here. Not yet. You'll spend yourself staring at strangers' souls and have nothing left for the finding."

"She's right," Sef said. "Also, heads up — tollgate ahead. Second ring. The Court charges per precinct, like a city with very committed tax collectors."

The second tollbooth stood where the gallery narrowed, and this one was staffed differently — taller clerk, robes like poured shadow, and beside its desk a second scale, larger, its empty pan already sagging.

"Two travelers and one guide," it intoned. "Precinct of Whispering Glass. Tolls due."

"We just paid," Odile said flatly.

"The previous toll admitted you to the Gallery of Kept Lights. This toll admits you onward." The tall clerk tilted its head. "Unless you wish to remain among the lanterns indefinitely. Many find them restful."

"Many find them restful because they've been *tithed to idiocy*," Odile snapped, and Ansel heard the ward-keeper coming up through the grief in her voice, the woman who had once held the border wards humming true with nothing but stubbornness and oaths. "Fine. Fine! What's the rate?"

"Remembrance," said the clerk, pleasantly.

"What *kind* of remembrance?"

"The kind you can spare."

Ansel stepped in before Odile could start something they couldn't finish. "How do we make sure you don't overcharge us?"

For the first time, a clerk hesitated. The tall one exchanged a glance with the smaller one — or rather, its coin-flame eyes did something flickering and complicated — and then it said, in a tone that had the ghost of embarrassment in it:

"You cannot. But you may *witness*. Name your payment aloud before offering it. Speak precisely. The Court honors precision, because precision is rare, and the Court collects rare things." A pause. "Most travelers do not speak precisely. Most travelers say *take what you need*, and oh, we do. Oh, we do."

"Mother above," Odile breathed. "It's a customs house. It's a customs house run by *lawyers*."

"Now you understand the Court," said Sef.

* * *

They learned fast, the way people do when the tuition is themselves.

Ansel went first again — someone had to test the water, and she had decided, in the gray missing hour after the first toll, that it would be her. She stood at the second booth and spoke with a smuggler's care, the same care she used naming terms on a dangerous contract:

"I offer the memory of the winter I was nine, the one with the deep snow and the quarrel with Denna Marsh about the stolen apples. The whole quarrel. Nothing adjacent. Not the winter, not the friendship, not the apples. The quarrel only. One quarrel, complete, bounded on all sides."

The clerk's eyes flickered — that flicker again, that hungry appraisal — and the scale tipped only slightly, and the loss, when it came, was clean-edged. She knew a quarrel had happened. She could name its participants. But it was like knowing a room existed behind a locked door: factual, distant, hers only by title.

"Ha," said Odile, watching. "*Bounded on all sides.* It didn't like that."

"It honored it, though," Sef said, with something like approval. "You're learning the grammar. Most people feed themselves to these booths in great unguarded slabs — *take my childhood, take my grief, take anything but this one thing* — and the Court hears *anything but* as a menu. You're speaking in parcels now. Good. Keep your parcels small and labeled."

Odile paid next: a memory of a tavern song, precisely bounded, though Ansel noticed she chose one she'd clearly already half-lost, singing it earlier with only half the verses and filling the gaps with *hm-hm-hm*. Efficient. Heartbreaking, if you let yourself notice. Ansel decided not to notice. There would be time for noticing later, or there wouldn't, and either way the road forward was the same.

Sef paid nothing.

"They don't charge me," he explained, catching their looks. "I'm Court property. Charging me would be double-entry bookkeeping." He smiled his honest-unhappy smile. "There are advantages to being owned. Fewer than advertised."

Three precincts they crossed that day — or what Ansel's body insisted was a day, though the Court kept no sky and its lantern-dawn neither rose nor set. Three booths, three payments, each parcel named and bounded and handed over with a steady voice.

A summer storm seen from a cave mouth, the whole of it, lightning included.

The taste of her grandmother's honey-bread — *the taste only,* she specified, *not the baking, not the kitchen, not the woman* — and afterward she found she could remember her grandmother perfectly except that remembering her involved no pleasure at all, a portrait with the warmth scraped out, and she understood with cold clarity what Sef had meant about edges and middles.

The name of a river she'd camped on eleven years running. Just the name. She could still picture the water. It simply had no word anymore, and when she tried to give it one, her mind slid off like rain off oilcloth.

Each time, the scale took a little extra. Never much. A grain. A breath's worth of something adjacent that she only noticed by its absence later — a half-hour gone missing here, a color slightly dimmer there. The Court's skimming was so gentle it was almost polite.

"That's the overcharge," Sef said, as they rested in an alcove of ribbed stone, sharing water that tasted of minerals and patience. "It never takes the parcel you named. It takes the parcel *plus the string*. A bit of the wrapping. A corner of the shelf it sat on. You won't miss any single piece. You'll miss the *shape*, eventually. You'll wake up one morning lighter and lighter until there isn't enough of you left to hold a grudge, and then, friend, you are ready for employment in the Court's civil service."

"You stayed angry," Ansel said.

"Barely. I ration it." He looked at his moonstone-smooth hand, flexing fingers that cast too little shadow. "Anger's the last thing they digest. Everything else goes down sweet. So I hoard mine like a miser. One day I'll spend it all at once, and it will be magnificent, and possibly fatal, and worth it."

Odile snorted. "You're a romantic."

"I'm an accountant with feelings," Sef said. "It's worse."

Ansel laughed — surprised by it, the laugh coming out cracked and real, the first one since the stars went out. It echoed strangely down the bone corridor and came back sounding like several people laughing, none of them quite her.

Then she stopped, because the echo had reminded her.

"Sef. The vault where they keep the Ledger entries. The soul-flames with names still attached. How many more tolls between here and there?"

He considered. "Four precincts. Four booths. Maybe five — the Court rearranges."

"Five more payments." Ansel looked at her hands. They were steady. They were always steady; it was the rest of her that was developing gaps. "I can do five. I've been inventorying. I have the smuggling routes — no, wait, I need most of those to get us out. I have the beasts. I have—" She ran the list, parcel by parcel, labeling each one, weighing each one on the internal scale she trusted more than any brass pan in this place. "I have enough. Barely. If I'm precise."

"You have enough for the vault," Odile said. It wasn't a question. She was watching Ansel with the expression of a woman doing her own arithmetic and not liking the total. "But child — getting to him isn't the same as getting him *out*. You've read what Sef told us. A soul leaves that Ledger over somebody's dead weight or nobody leaves at all. What do you think you'll have left to pay *that* toll?"

The question sat in the alcove with them, patient as the Court itself.

Ansel didn't answer it. She stood, rolled her shoulders the way she did before bad scree, and said, "One problem at a time. That's how you cross Thornfell. That's how you cross anything."

They walked on, down corridors that curved because they felt like it, past lanterns that dreamed, toward the fifth booth and the sixth, toward the vault where her brother burned patient and nameless.

At the next tollgate, the clerk was new — small, quick, its coin-flame eyes bright with something that might have been eagerness. It slid the pale paper across the desk, and the frost-letters rose, and Ansel read them and went very still.

The instructions were the same. The rate was the same.

But beneath them, in smaller script, in handwriting disturbingly like her own, one additional line had appeared:

*Toll for the Precinct of Names: one memory freely given, of the person you are seeking. The Court finds it fitting.*

Sef read it over her shoulder and said, very quietly, "That's not standard."

Down the corridor behind them, faint and far, something vast turned over in its sleep again — and this time, Ansel could have sworn, it was smiling.
