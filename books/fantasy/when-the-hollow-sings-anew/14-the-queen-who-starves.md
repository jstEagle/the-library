## 14. The Queen Who Starves

Odile Ferran had spent thirty years learning to read wards the way other people read weather — a flicker here, a hum there, the faint sour note that meant trouble three valleys over. She had thought herself prepared for anything the Hollow could show her. She had been wrong about that, and she would spend the rest of her life being wrong about it in increasingly creative ways.

But first, there was the matter of her boots.

They had been walking down through the drowned stair for what felt like a season and might have been two days. The bats had led them true, their song painting the dark in shapes Odile could almost see if she squinted her mind sideways. And now the passage opened into a gallery so vast that her lantern-light simply gave up, pooling around her feet like a coward.

"Your boots," Ansel said, not looking back. She was crouched at the edge of the light, one hand flat against the stone floor, listening to it the way Odile listened to bells. "They're squeaking."

"They're *walking*," Odile said. "That's what boots do. It's the whole arrangement."

"Every sound carries down here. For miles."

"Then I'll walk squeakily and proudly."

Ansel looked up at her then, and there was something in her face that stopped Odile's argument before it properly got going. Not fear exactly. Fear she could have worked with; fear was familiar territory, fear was Tuesday. This was closer to awe wearing fear's coat.

"You need to see this," Ansel said quietly. "And you need to be quiet seeing it."

Odile grumbled — quietly, she wasn't a monster — and followed.

* * *

The gallery opened like a throat.

That was Odile's first thought, and she hated how accurate it felt. The passage they'd descended through narrowed, then flared, then simply *ended*, spilling them onto a ledge high on the wall of a cavern that had no business existing beneath a mountain that had no business holding itself up. The ceiling was lost somewhere above them; when Sef — who had joined them two days past, half-hollow and half-ashamed and entirely useful — tossed a pebble upward, they heard it strike nothing for a count of nine before its echo came back changed.

Below them, the floor of the cavern glowed.

Not with fire. Not with any light Odile had a name for, though she'd catalogued every variety the ward-keepers' manuals described and several they didn't. It was lantern-light, Sef said later, or had been once — ten thousand lanterns burning in a pattern too regular to be natural and too vast to be built. They lined avenues. They ringed a central space like seats at some endless assembly. And in the middle of it all, rising out of the glow like a mountain that had decided mountains were thinking too small—

"There," Sef said. "The Court."

Odile had expected a throne room. What she saw was a cathedral the size of a weather system, its pillars carved from bone — real bone, old bone, bone worn smooth by centuries of air moving through it — and hung with lanterns that burned without flame. The light they gave off was the color of memory. That was the only way she could describe it, and she resented having to: warm gold at the edges, gray at the center, shifting when you weren't looking directly at it, the way your mother's kitchen shifts when you try to picture it twenty years on.

And among the lanterns, between the pillars, along the avenues — *people*. Or the shapes of people. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, standing in ranks that did not move, each one wrapped in a small bright flame that burned somewhere behind their ribs.

"Soul-flames," Sef said. His voice had gone flat, the way voices go when a man is holding himself together with both hands. "Sold souls. Each flame is a life someone gave up — or had taken. The court burns them as currency. Light, heat, power. Everything down here runs on them."

Ansel made a sound low in her throat, and Odile, watching her rather than the vista, saw her friend's eyes move across the thousands of flames with terrible purpose. Searching. Odile knew what for. A brother sold twenty years ago, a name written in a ledger they hadn't found yet, a grief that had reopened like an old wound and refused to close clean.

"Not tonight," Odile murmured. "You can't search ten thousand flames tonight. You'd walk off this ledge trying."

"I know." Ansel's voice was steady. Her hands were not. "I know."

* * *

It was Sef who told them to wait for the changing of the watch.

"The court keeps hours," he explained, settling his thin frame against the cavern wall with the ease of long habit. He moved like a man who had learned to be furniture. "Even starving things keep rituals. It's how they convince themselves they're still something other than hunger." He nodded toward the far side of the cavern, where a procession of courtiers — tall, gray, beautiful in the way that frost on a window is beautiful — was assembling. "In an hour, the night-court yields to the deep-court, and the Queen is brought her accounting."

"Accounting of what?" Odile asked.

"What she's owed. What she's been paid. How much longer the arithmetic holds." Sef's mouth twisted. "She has been doing the same sum for a hundred years, and every year the answer is worse."

So they waited. Odile dozed against her pack, because she was fifty-three years old and had learned that sleep was a resource like any other and hoarded accordingly. Ansel did not sleep. Ansel sat at the ledge's edge with her legs dangling over a drop that would kill a wyvern, humming under her breath — the lullaby, Odile realized. The one she'd mentioned once, drunk on plum brandy in Cinderhatch, the one her mother taught her. Down here, in a cathedral of bone above ten thousand stolen souls, she hummed it like a woman leaving bread crumbs in a forest.

When the hour came, the whole cavern changed.

The lanterns dimmed in unison — all of them, ten thousand points of light bowing at once — and the courtiers processed inward along avenues that lit ahead of them and darkened behind, and at the center of everything, where the greatest concentration of soul-flames burned in a ring, the Queen was.

Odile had braced herself for a monster.

What she saw instead was worse, and it took her years of professional composure not to say so out loud.

The Hollow Queen was vast — that part the stories had gotten right. She rose out of the ring of flames like a figure carved from the dark between stars, taller than the pillars, her outline less a body than a suggestion of one, the way smoke suggests the shape of the fire it came from. But she was not writhing. She was not raging. She was not devouring anything, though the flames burned close enough to touch her.

She was *sitting*. Perfectly still. Patient as geology.

And she was thin.

That was the detail that lodged in Odile like a splinter. The Queen was starving — visibly, unmistakably starving, the way a winter-starved wolf is starving, all architecture and no flesh, every line of her drawn tight over the hollow underneath. The star-pact had broken — Odile understood that now, had understood it since the night the sky went black — and whatever had once fed up from the world above, freely given or fairly traded, had stopped. So the Queen had begun eating her own reserves. Then her own court's. Then anything the Ledger put in front of her.

And still she waited.

"She doesn't rage," Sef said softly. He had gone very gray, even for him. "Everyone expects her to rage. Marchen expects it. The cities expect it. They build armies against a thing that attacks." He shook his head slowly. "She waits. She has been waiting longer than your city has existed. She can wait longer than your language does. And every year she waits, she gets more patient and more hungry, and patience plus hunger equals—" he gestured at the ten thousand flames, at the fallen wards, at the starless sky they'd left behind them, "—all of this."

Odile watched the Queen receive her accounting. The courtiers knelt — or did whatever passed for kneeling in a body that had never needed knees — and presented their figures, and the Queen inclined her vast head by perhaps a degree, and the ring of soul-flames guttered, just slightly, just for a moment, the way a candle gutters when something breathes near it.

Ten thousand small lights, flinching at once.

"That's the feeding," Sef said. "A sip. She rations herself now. Do you understand? She could take all of it — every flame, every soul, drain the court dry in a single night. Instead she takes a sip. Because she knows—" his voice caught, "—she knows that when the reserve is gone, there's only one direction left to drink from. Up."

The word landed in Odile's chest like a stone dropped down a well.

Up. The free cities. Rivermoor, Cinderhatch, the towns clinging to Thornfell's skirts like children to a mother too poor to feed them. Every soul above ground that Veyra Marchen was even now conscripting, cataloguing, promising protection to. Odile thought of the herald in Cinderhatch square, with his silver tongue and his ledger of names, and understood suddenly, completely, with the awful clarity that only comes at the worst possible moments:

Marchen wasn't building an army. She was building a *pantry*.

"Oh," Odile said. It was not a large word, but it came out of her very small.

* * *

They withdrew from the ledge before the watch changed back, moving through passages Sef knew the way Odile knew the inside of a bottle — intimately, regretfully, with a certain hard-won expertise. They made camp in a side gallery where the stone sang a low, constant note that Sef claimed masked conversation from the court's listeners. Odile got a fire going from their hoarded coal, mostly out of stubbornness. If the dark wanted to be infinite, fine. Her little orange defiance would sit in the middle of it regardless.

Ansel sat across the coals, turning something over in her hands. A strip of cloth, wolf-carried, crest-embroidered. The name folded inside it.

"She's not what I expected either," Ansel said, after a while.

"No?"

"I expected teeth. Hunger with teeth." Ansel looked into the fire. "She looked like my grandmother at the end. Sitting by the window. Waiting for everyone to come home." A pause. "Except my grandmother was waiting for something good."

Odile fed the fire another lump of coal and considered the strange shape her life had recently taken. Two years ago her biggest problem was whether the Cinderhatch ward-budget would cover re-tuning the north bell. Now she was sitting in the roots of the world, conspiring with a smuggler, a ghost of a man, and an army of animals yet to be recruited, against a starving god and the woman who meant to sell her neighbors to it.

Her mother would have had opinions. Her mother had opinions about everything, most of them delivered at volume. Odile found, to her surprise, that she missed them.

"We should go back up," she said. "Soon. Whatever we're going to do, we need to know what's happening above. If Marchen moves first—"

"If Marchen feeds her even one city," Ansel said, "the Queen stops rationing. And then it won't matter what we do down here. There'll be nothing left up there worth saving." She folded the cloth away, carefully, the way you fold a letter you intend to answer. "We find the Ledger. We find Tomas. And then we decide what kind of fire we're going to be."

"Spoken like a woman whose family has a history with fires," Odile said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because Ansel's face did something complicated and closed, and Odile was many things but cruel was not supposed to be among them.

Before she could apologize — and she was composing the apology, it was going to be excellent, she had a gift for apologies honed over decades of needing them — the stone began to sing.

Not the low masking note. Something else. A vibration through the floor, through the walls, through the soles of Odile's boots and the bones of her shins, rising like a struck bell that would not finish fading. The coals shivered. Somewhere far above, loose rock ticked down a shaft like slow rain.

Ansel was on her feet instantly, hand pressed flat to the wall, listening with her whole body. Odile saw her expression change — saw the blood leave it in stages, like a tide going out.

"What?" Odile demanded. "What is it? Ansel. *What?*"

"It's the beasts." Ansel's voice came out strange, stretched thin. "Something's wrong on the surface. The wolves are singing an alarm — the old alarm, the one for—"

She stopped. The stone kept singing.

"For what?" Odile asked. "For what, girl?"

Ansel turned to look at her, and Odile saw in her face the reflection of every disaster she'd ever failed to prevent, arriving all at once.

"For war," Ansel said. "Something's coming down the mountain roads. A lot of somethings. With banners."

Sef rose slowly from his corner, and for the first time since Odile had met him, the half-hollow courtier looked afraid — not of the Queen, not of the dark, but of the news.

"Marchen," he said. "She's not waiting for the cities anymore. She's bringing them to the Queen herself."

Above them, through a mile of stone, the mountain groaned — long and low and weary, like an old dog hearing footsteps it knows too well.

The Queen, somewhere in her cathedral of bone, went on waiting. Patiently. She had always been good at that.

But now, Odile understood, the waiting was nearly over. Something was finally coming down to her.

And it was bringing dinner.
