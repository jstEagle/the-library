## 15. Above: The First City Falls

The thing about being a disgraced ward-keeper, Odile Ferran had learned over thirty years, was that everyone assumed you were dead. Not metaphorically — actually, literally dead. Wardens who fell from grace were struck from the rolls, their names scraped off the guild stones, their pension stopped, their boots confiscated. (The boots, she'd always felt, was the truly unforgivable part. They'd been good boots.)

So when she surfaced at the Cinderhatch waystation with Ansel Quintana's beasts at her heels and a head full of ward-rites she no longer fully trusted herself to remember, the first person to see her — a round-faced apprentice named Pimm, hauling buckets of lamp oil up the stair — dropped both buckets and sat down hard on the third step.

"Keeper Ferran," he said. "You're alive."

"Last I checked." She held out her hand. "Help me up, boy, the mountain's been eating my knees."

He helped her up. Then he hugged her, which was less expected, and smelled strongly of lamp oil, which was entirely expected. Odile patted his back with one arm and let him have his moment. It had been a long time since anyone in the guild had been pleased to see her, and she intended to savor it even if it only lasted until he remembered she was technically a heretic.

"You're supposed to be dead," Pimm said into her shoulder.

"That's the general consensus, yes."

"The rolls say—"

"I know what the rolls say. I wrote half of them before they scraped me off." She peeled him away gently. "Now. Tell me something useful. What's happened while I've been under the mountain?"

Pimm's face did a complicated thing — the particular collapse of expression that Odile had spent the last month learning to read as *the world has gotten worse and I don't know how to say how much*.

"Rivermoor," he said.

* * *

They heard the rest of it in the waystation kitchen, which was the way all important news traveled through Cinderhatch, because the kitchen had the only fire big enough to make sitting through bad news bearable. Odile sat with a bowl of barley soup she wasn't tasting. Across from her, an old carter named Brannic — sixty if he was a day, hands like tree roots, a man who had driven the Thornfell roads for forty years and lied about the weather for all of them — told it plain.

"It didn't fall," Brannic said. "That's the thing nobody wants to hear. A city can fall. Walls breached, gates burned, blood in the streets. That's falling. That's a fight somebody lost." He turned his cup slowly on the table, around and around. "Rivermoor opened its gates."

"Opened," Odile repeated.

"Wide. Marchen's army comes up the river road — twenty thousand by some counts, thirty by others, doesn't matter — and the city council meets for one day, and then the mayor walks out with the keys on a velvet cushion like he's presenting them at a coronation. Gates open. Bells ring. *Ring*, mind you. Welcoming bells." His mouth twisted. "Half the garrison joined her by sundown. The other half took the pay she offered and went home."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone, the mountain made a sound like a settling house.

"There was a faction," Brannic went on, quieter now. "Wanted to fight. Younger council members, some of the river captains. They got shouted down. And here's the argument that won — I heard it from a cousin of mine who was there, so it's secondhand but he doesn't lie about this sort of thing—" Odile privately doubted that; everyone in the trade lied about everything; but she let him continue. "—the argument was that Marchen wasn't asking for blood. Just fealty. Taxes, levies, obedience. And that resisting would mean burning the granaries and starving through winter for nothing."

"And people believed her?" Odile asked. "That it would just be taxes?"

Brannic looked at her then, and his eyes were old and tired and terribly clear. "People believe what lets them sleep," he said. "You'd know something about that, Keeper."

* * *

The refugees started coming down the smuggler roads three days later.

Odile watched them arrive from the waystation porch — a thin, strung-out procession of families with too many bundles and not enough cart, moving at the pace of the smallest child. There weren't many of them, at first. A trickle. But Odile had been a warden long enough to know that a trickle meant a dam somewhere upstream, holding back something enormous, and dams always broke.

Ansel came out to stand beside her, arms crossed against the cold. The beast-speaker had been quiet since the news came up from below — quiet in the way of someone doing arithmetic they don't like. Beside her hip, a gray wolf leaned against her leg like a dog twice its size, which in Odile's professional opinion it very nearly was.

"They're not running from soldiers," Ansel said.

"No."

"They're running from *nothing*. From quiet. From a city that decided to kneel politely." Ansel shook her head slowly. "I've guided people fleeing wars, Odile. Fires, floods, bandit lords, a plague year once. This is different. Look at them. Nobody's hurt. Nobody's crying. They're leaving because staying felt like—" She searched for the word.

"Drowning," Odile said. "Staying felt like drowning in slow water. You don't scream at slow water. You just leave, if you're smart enough to notice."

One of the refugee children — a girl of six or seven with a chicken tucked under one arm like a loaf of bread — noticed the wolf and stopped dead in the road. Odile braced herself for screaming. Instead the girl studied the animal with the frank appraisal of the very young, and the wolf studied her back, and after a long moment the girl gave a single solemn nod, as though some treaty had been ratified between nations, and walked on.

"What was that?" Odile asked.

"Permission," Ansel said. "She asked if it was safe. The wolf said yes." A pause. "It was right. Mostly. Don't tell it I said 'mostly,' it gets smug."

Despite everything — despite the starless sky overhead and the mile of stone beneath her feet and the arithmetic she could see Ansel refusing to finish — Odile laughed. It came out rusty, like a gate hinge in winter, but it was real. "Your wolves get smug."

"All of them. It's a pack-wide condition. The bears are worse."

* * *

The detailed accounts arrived with a wool merchant named Hetta Voss, who had left Rivermoor nine days after the gates opened and had the specific, terrible energy of a woman who had seen something unbelievable and intended to make everyone believe it whether they wanted to or not.

Hetta held court by the kitchen fire, as was tradition, and Odile squeezed in among the carters and the waystation staff to listen, a bowl of soup forgotten at her elbow.

"The first week, honestly, it was almost funny," Hetta said. "Marchen's officers walk around in their nice uniforms telling everyone the war is over and the border is safe. Posters everywhere. *PROSPERITY THROUGH ORDER.* My nephew stole four of them; says they'll be worth money someday, one way or another." She waved a hand. "And then the lists started."

"Lists?" Pimm asked.

"Levy lists. Names of every soul in the city between fifteen and fifty. Not conscription — oh no, nothing so crude — 'voluntary service for the protection of the realm.' Except your name goes on the list whether you volunteer or not, and if you're not on the list you can't hold property, and if you can't hold property you can't eat, and so." She spread her hands. "Voluntary."

Odile felt something cold slide down her spine and settle, coiled, in her stomach. Because she knew — she alone in this room knew, or nearly alone — where those names were going. Not onto muster rolls. Into ledgers. Into a cathedral of bone a mile beneath her feet, where souls burned as currency and something vast and patient was waiting for dinner to be served.

"They're not feeding an army," she said quietly.

Every face turned toward her.

"What?" Hetta said sharply.

"The levy. The lists. It's not mustering soldiers." Odile stared into the fire, choosing each word like a woman walking on river ice. "Veyra Marchen isn't building an army. She's building an *offering*. Every name on those lists is pledged — sold, the same way my order sold souls to anchor the wards, except she's not anchoring anything, she's *paying*—"

She stopped herself. Too much. Too fast. Half the room was staring at her like she'd gone mad, and the other half — Brannic, Hetta, Pimm — were staring at her like she might be right, which was worse.

"Sold to what?" Hetta demanded.

"To something under Thornfell that's been hungry for two hundred years," Odile said. "And I need a drink, and then I need to go back down the mountain, and none of you should ask me any more questions tonight because the answers are worse."

Brannic, without a word, poured her one.

* * *

Later — much later, when the kitchen had emptied and the fire had burned down to a red heart of coals — Odile sat alone with a map of the Thornfell passes spread across her knees, drinking the last of Brannic's excellent and illegal plum brandy, and tried to do sums.

She had always been good at sums. It was why they'd made her a keeper in the first place, before the drinking, before the disgrace, before the night she'd signed her own name to a compromise she'd spent twenty years pretending hadn't gutted her. Sums were honest. Sums didn't care about your feelings.

Sum the first: Rivermoor held — had held — perhaps forty thousand souls. If Marchen levied even half of them, and if the pattern held for the next city, and the next—

She turned the brandy glass in her fingers and refused to write the total anywhere, even in her head. Some numbers were better left as shapes rather than values.

Sum the second: how long? Rivermoor had fallen — had *opened* — without a single arrow fired. Grentsmoor was next on the river road, smaller, prouder, and full of people who would also very much prefer to sleep. Three weeks? Four? How long before Marchen's offering was large enough, rich enough, to buy whatever it was she wanted from the thing below?

And sum the third — the one that made her set down the glass:

How long before Ansel Quintana, who was currently sleeping in the loft with wolves curled around her like a living blanket, went back down into the Hollow Court carrying a grief the size of a brother, and found out exactly how little time Odile's broken compass had bought them?

"We're late," Odile said aloud, to the empty kitchen. "We are already late, and we're sitting here drinking brandy."

The kitchen, having no opinion, declined to argue.

* * *

She went up to the loft to wake her. That was the plan, anyway. In practice she climbed the ladder, stuck her head through the hatch, and found herself looking at Ansel already awake, sitting cross-legged in the straw with a wolf's chin heavy on her knee, staring out the little window at a sky with no stars in it.

"You have the look," Odile said.

"What look?"

"The look of a woman composing a speech she thinks she's going to have to give me. I've been on guild councils, Quintana. I know a preamble when I see one."

Ansel huffed — almost a laugh. The wolf sighed and resettled. For a while neither of them spoke, and the silence was the comfortable kind, which Odile had come to treasure the way other people treasured gold.

"Hetta's leaving tomorrow," Ansel finally said. "Back over the pass, toward Grentsmoor. She asked me — quietly, the way people ask when they already know the answer will cost them — whether the high road was safe to travel." Ansel's jaw tightened. "Safe. As if safety is still a thing I can promise anyone. As if I'm still just a guide."

"What did you tell her?"

"That the road is safe. That the things on it mostly aren't. And that I'd take her as far as the fork myself." Ansel looked down at the wolf. "And then I stood there like an idiot trying to work out how to say the next part."

"Which is?"

"Which is that every day we spend up here, more names go on more lists. And every name on a list is—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Tomas has been down there twenty years, Odile. Twenty years, and he can wait, because he doesn't know he's waiting. But the ones Marchen takes next week *will* know. They'll feel it. And I keep thinking — every hour I hesitate, I'm choosing. I'm spending other people's waiting time to protect my own courage."

Odile climbed the rest of the way into the loft and sat down across from her, joints protesting, and said the true thing, because somebody had to and it was apparently going to be her:

"Yes. You are. And so am I. And here's the thing about guilt, girl — it makes a terrible compass. It points wherever you hurt the most, not where you need to go. We go down when we're *ready*, not when we're guilty, because if we go down guilty we'll pay any price they ask, and the court always—" she caught herself echoing Sef, "—the court always overcharges."

Ansel looked at her for a long moment. "When did you get wise?"

"About ten minutes ago. It'll wear off by morning."

This time Ansel did laugh, short and startled, and the wolf thumped its tail once against the straw, and for a moment the loft was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the season.

Then hooves came up the valley road.

Not many — two horses, maybe three, at a hard gallop, in the middle of the night, which on the Cinderhatch roads meant exactly one thing. Odile was on her feet before her knees finished voting. Below, the waystation dogs started up. Doors banged open along the yard. And through the loft's small window, Odile saw riders reining in hard, saw torchlight catch on torn cloaks and faces gray with road dust and something worse than dust, and heard the first rider shout up at the gathered crowd in a voice cracked raw:

"Grentsmoor! Grentsmoor's gates — they never even reached the walls! The council surrendered by messenger — Marchen's men haven't arrived yet and the gates are already *open*—"

The words tumbled down into the yard like stones into a well. Odile heard the sound the crowd made — not a gasp, nothing so dramatic, just a long collective exhale, the sound of a whole settlement losing air.

Beside her, Ansel had gone very still. When she spoke, her voice was flat and calm in the way Odile had learned to fear more than shouting.

"Two cities," she said. "In eleven days. And the second one didn't even wait to be asked." She turned from the window, and her eyes in the dark had the banked shine of coals. "Get your compass, Odile. We're done doing sums."

Below in the yard, the rider was still shouting, and now Odile caught the rest of it, the detail that would matter most, the hook that sank in deep and pulled:

"—and she's posted criers on every road! Marchen's heralds, saying the army moves south within the week — saying the Queen beneath the mountain has promised the cities' freedom to whoever delivers the free folk's pledge — saying—" the rider swallowed, steadied himself, "—saying there's a place at court for anyone who brings them a *gifted* one. Alive."
