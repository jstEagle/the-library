## 16. Tomas, Almost

The lanterns of the Hollow Court did not flicker. That was the first thing Ansel had learned down here, and the thing she hated most.

Fire was supposed to be alive — supposed to lean and breathe and argue with the wind. But these flames burned in glass vessels shaped like teardrops, each one hung from a bone-white branch of the great chandelier that arched overhead like a frozen willow, and they burned perfectly still. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. A sky of small imprisoned suns, and every single one of them had been a person.

"They call them *tithes*," Sef Andrade said softly, walking half a step behind her. His voice always sounded like it was coming through a wall, even when he stood close. Half-hollow men spoke that way, she'd learned — as if part of them were always in another room. "Not souls. Never souls. The courtiers get very particular about vocabulary. A soul is a tragedy. A tithe is an entry."

"Then show me the entry," Ansel said.

Her voice came out steadier than she felt. She had been rehearsing it for three days, ever since they'd come down the drowned stair with bats singing the dark around her like a map made of sound. Three days of paying tolls in memories to move through the court's galleries — her mother's face first, gone now, a smooth blank place in her mind where a woman's smile used to live; then the name of the village where she'd been born; then, horribly, the taste of honey bread, which she hadn't known she loved until it left her. The court always overcharged. Sef had warned her. The court always overcharged, and smiled while doing it, if courts could be said to smile.

Odile Ferran walked on her other side, one hand pressed flat against the wall as though she needed to convince herself the stone was real. The old ward-keeper had been quiet since the gallery of tithes began — quiet in the way of someone counting something enormous.

"There are more than I thought," Odile murmured. "Gods below. When I kept the Cinderhatch wards, we were taught there were nine hundred pledges. Nine hundred, across all the border towns. This is—" She stopped counting. Ansel watched her decide not to know the number.

"The Ledger doesn't waste," Sef said. "That's the court's whole genius. Everything is kept. Nothing is spent except what must be." He gestured upward with a hand whose fingers were faintly translucent at the tips, like candle wax held too near a flame. "Your brother is up there somewhere. Or the flame that was him."

*Was him.* Ansel refused the phrasing. She took it out of the air and broke it over her knee, privately, where no one could see.

"Take me to him," she said.

* * *

Sef led them up through the galleries by ways that weren't stairs — ramps of polished rib-bone, bridges strung between chandelier-branches so narrow that Ansel had to walk with her arms out like a girl on a fence rail. Below them the court spread in its terrible cathedral silence: courtiers drifting like moths with too much dignity, hollow servitors carrying trays of nothing, and everywhere the low hum of hunger, felt rather than heard, pressing on the ears the way deep water presses on a diver's chest.

She had expected to feel triumphant this close. She had imagined finding Tomas's flame the way you imagine reaching the summit of a mountain you've climbed your whole life — breathless, victorious, whole.

Instead she felt small. She felt like a smuggler caught at her own border.

"Here." Sef stopped at a branch thick as a ship's mast. He ran his translucent fingers along its surface, reading something in it — grain, or temperature, or grief. "Third vessel from the fork. Sold twenty-three years ago, entered under the Marchen seal. It burns... steady. Some of them gutter, when what's left of the person fights the vessel. His doesn't fight anymore."

"That's not comforting, Sef."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true. You seem to prefer that, from what I've seen."

Ansel reached up.

The vessel was cool glass, teardrop-shaped, no larger than her two fists curled together. Inside it burned a small gold light — warmer than the others, or perhaps that was hope lying to her. She cupped it in both hands the way she'd once cupped a sparrow with a broken wing, when she was eight and didn't yet know that some things you cannot fix by holding them.

"Tomas," she said.

Nothing. The flame burned on, indifferent as any fire.

"Tomas. It's Ansel. It's— " Her voice cracked on the second syllable of her own name, which seemed unfair. She'd said that name ten thousand times in her head over twenty years. She'd practiced arguments with him, apologies to him, the whole long imaginary conversation of siblings separated too young. *You idiot, why did you wander off. I'm sorry I told Mother it was you who let the goats out. I found a wolf den once and thought of you because you always wanted to see one.*

But the flame only burned. And slowly, horribly, she understood what Sef had been trying to tell her in his careful wall-of-a voice.

Souls in the Ledger lose their names.

Not their shapes, maybe. Not entirely their selves — something in there knew warmth when it felt it, knew *nearness*, the way a root knows rain without knowing the word. But names were the handles. Names were how a self got held onto. The Ledger took the handle off and set the soul on its shelf, and after twenty-three years, her brother did not know his own sister standing in front of him with her heart in her hands.

"He can't hear me," she said.

"He hears," Sef said. "Hearing is cheap. Remembering costs the court something, so the court doesn't pay it." He paused, and when he spoke again there was something raw under the wall in his voice. "When I was first brought here, I used to talk to my own flame. Did I tell you that? I'd stand under it and recite my name for hours. Sef Andrade. Sef Andrade. Like calling across a lake. The flame never answered. But I liked to think it leaned. Toward the sound. Toward its own name."

Ansel looked at him then — really looked — and saw for the first time that the courtier of ash had once been somebody's little brother too.

"What happened?" Odile asked quietly. "To your flame."

"I don't remember." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Ansel had ever seen. "That's rather the point."

* * *

She tried everything.

She talked. She told him about the mountain lion she'd bargained with last spring, about the pass called the Widow's Tooth and how she'd gotten a caravan of silk and stolen prayer-books over it in a fog so thick the mules walked by faith alone. She told him about the night the stars went out. She described their mother — and hit the blank place where her mother's face should have been, and had to stand there for a moment breathing through the loss before she could go on, describing a woman she could no longer picture: tall, sharp-tongued, smelled of pine resin, sang badly on purpose.

The flame burned. Steady. Patient. Gold.

"She can't keep doing this," Odile said at last, from the bridge behind them. The old woman had lowered herself to sit cross-legged on the bone, which for Odile constituted surrender to circumstance. "Girl. Ansel. Come sit before you fall off that branch and break your neck in a place with no healers."

"I'm not leaving."

"I didn't say leave. I said sit. There's a difference, and it matters." Odile's eyes, rheumy and shrewd, held hers. "I watched you bargain with that cat on the Thornfell road like you were haggling over eggs. What did you do? You didn't shout at it. You didn't plead. You gave it something it already wanted and let it think it was its own idea."

Ansel opened her mouth to say *he isn't a cat* — and shut it again.

Because Odile was right, and the rightness of it hurt like a splinter going in. She had been talking at the flame. Pouring words into it like water into a locked cistern. But beasts didn't work that way, and neither, apparently, did souls. With beasts she never led with language. She led with—

With song.

"Oh," she said.

"Oh?" said Odile.

"Mother's lullaby." Her throat tightened around the words. "She used to sing it when the storms came over the pass. Tomas was terrified of thunder — our Tomas, who later bragged he'd wrestled a wolverine, which was a lie, it was a large housecat — but when he was small, thunder undid him. So she'd sing, and he'd go asleep against her ribs like the storm wasn't even happening." She swallowed. "It's the oldest thing I own. Older than my name, almost."

"Then sing it," Odile said. "And stop performing, child. Lullabies aren't performances. They're invitations."

Ansel closed her eyes.

The court hummed its hunger around her. Somewhere far below, a servitor's bell tolled the hour in a language of clicks. The glass vessel sat warm in her cupped hands like a bird that had forgotten flight.

She began to sing.

It was a small song. That was the first surprise — she'd remembered it as grander, somehow, a hymn, but it came out of her mouth humble as bread. Six lines, maybe seven. About a fox going home at evening, about the den being warm, about the dark being only the mountain closing its hand gently around everything it loved. Their mother had sung it in the old dialect, the one with too many vowels, and Ansel's accent had gone rough with disuse, and none of that mattered. Lullabies aren't performances.

Halfway through the second line, the flame moved.

Not much. Anyone else would have missed it — a tremor in the burn, a lean, the smallest possible tilt toward the sound, like a flower finding a window. But Ansel had spent her whole life reading the small languages of things that couldn't speak. She knew leaning when she saw it.

Her knees nearly went. She locked them and kept singing, and her tears ran freely down her face and dripped off her chin onto the bone floor, and she did not care, because her brother was leaning toward her voice.

*Toward the sound,* Sef had said. *Toward its own name.*

No. Toward *her*. Toward the girl who used to fall asleep in the same bed during storms because she was scared too and would die before admitting it, and who had listened to that lullaby every bit as hungrily as Tomas had, pretending she was staying awake to guard him.

She sang it through. Then again. On the third pass, her voice steadied, and she added the harmony — the second part, the one their mother had taught them both, the one that only worked if someone sang the melody beneath you. She sang the harmony alone, into the empty places where the melody should have been, and it was like setting a table for someone who might still come home.

The flame leaned. Held. Burned gold and warm against the glass, and for one impossible moment Ansel would have sworn — would have staked her remaining memories on it — that the light pulsed in rhythm. In time. In *answer*.

"Tomas," she whispered between verses. "You don't have to know me. Just know the song. The song knows me. The song knows both of us. Meet me there."

He couldn't answer. Of course he couldn't. But the flame stayed tilted toward her, stubbornly, the way a sleeper turns toward a door they expect someone to come through, and it was almost — it was *almost* —

It was devastating. That was the word. Not tragic, not even heartbreaking, though it was both. Devastating, in the old sense: laid waste, and yet still standing, the way a tree stands after lightning takes its crown.

* * *

She lost track of time. Down here there was no time worth tracking; the court kept hours the way a miser keeps coins, and lent none of them out. She sang until her voice wore down to a thread, and then Odile — gruff, tone-deaf Odile, who claimed she hadn't sung since her husband died — hummed the melody under her, badly, generously, and Ansel laughed wetly in the middle of a verse and kept going.

It was Sef who finally touched her shoulder.

"You need to eat," he said. "What passes for eating here. Your body is spending itself faster than your memories, and the court has noticed. Things notice hunger. It draws them."

"Five more minutes."

"You said that four songs ago."

"Then five more real ones."

"Ansel." He said her name carefully, the way he handled everything — like a man who had learned that objects break. "Look at the vessel."

She looked.

The flame had changed. Subtly — anyone else would have missed it — but she was fluent now in this small dialect of light. It burned brighter than its neighbors. Warmer. And at its base, where the gold met the glass, there was a thin thread of color that hadn't been there before: a pulse of deep red, rhythmic, steady, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Or like a harmony holding on.

"What is that?" Odile breathed.

"I don't know." Sef had gone very still. His translucent fingers hovered near the glass without touching it, and his face — what remained of it under the ash-pale hollowing — wore an expression Ansel had never seen on him. Fear, yes. But underneath the fear, something that looked painfully like hope, worn thin from years of disuse. "Flames don't do that. Flames *can't*. They're stored, not alive. Stored light doesn't learn new colors."

"Unless it's not stored light," Ansel said. "Unless it's him. Unless he's—" She couldn't finish it. Saying it aloud would give the universe a chance to take it back.

*He's remembering the song,* she didn't say. *He can't remember his name, but he can remember the fox going home. He's keeping it. He's keeping it the way I kept his memory for twenty years — badly, faithfully, against all sense.*

"The Ledger will notice," Sef said quietly. "That's what frightens me. The court tolerates a great deal, but it does not tolerate change it didn't sell. A tithe that begins to... respond..." He shook his head. "There are protocols. There are courtiers whose entire office is quieting flames that grow restless. If they mark his vessel—"

"Then we move faster." Ansel straightened. Exhaustion fell away from her like a coat she no longer needed; she could pick it up later. "Whatever it takes. Whatever it costs. Sef — you know this place. You know the Ledger, the offices, the locks. Can a soul be taken out of here? Truly taken, not just dreamed about?"

The silence stretched.

Odile looked at Sef. Ansel looked at Sef. The red thread in the glass beat on, patient as a heart, waiting for an answer it couldn't understand and somehow trusted anyway.

"Yes," Sef said at last.

The word landed strangely — too heavy, too careful, like a coin flipped across a counter that lands on its edge.

"And the price?"

"There is always a price. The court taught me arithmetic before it taught me despair." He met her eyes, and whatever he was about to say, she watched him decide — visibly, the way a man decides to step off a ledge — to say a different part of it first. "Come. Both of you. There's a gallery where we can speak without being overheard. And Ansel—" He glanced back once at the flame, tilted forever toward its sister's voice, burning a color it had no right to know. "Sing to him again tomorrow. Same hour, if the court lets us keep one. Songs, apparently, are the one thing this place can't tax."

They went, down the bone bridges, back toward the humming dark. And behind them, in its teardrop of glass, a small gold flame held its new color like a secret, and waited — the way the whole court waited, the way the hungry always wait — but for the first time in twenty-three years, it waited *toward* something.
