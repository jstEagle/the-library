## 17. The Courtier's Bargain

Sef Andrade had died once, in the ordinary way, at nineteen, and had spent the forty years since trying not to make a habit of it.

He thought about this as he watched Ansel Quintana sleep in the alcove behind the Lantern Gallery — or rather, he thought about it while pretending to inventory the lanterns, which was what passed for work among the courtiers of ash. The girl was curled against the wall with her back to the room and her knife hand free, which told him she'd grown up sleeping somewhere dangerous, and her brow was furrowed even in sleep, which told him the rest.

Forty years of being half-hollow gave a man a great deal of time for noticing things. It also took away most of the things worth noticing, so you learned to ration your attention like bread in a siege.

The lantern-light did its usual trick on his hands when he held them up: flesh-colored where the light hit directly, translucent gray where it passed through. In the court's glow he looked almost alive. He'd gotten good at standing in exactly the right places.

"You're staring at me," Ansel said, without opening her eyes. "It's very rude."

"I'm counting lanterns."

"You're staring at me *while* counting lanterns."

"Multitasking," Sef said. "It's how I got promoted." He turned a lantern on its hook, watching the soul-flame inside gutter and steady. Each one held a sold soul, burning quietly, giving off that particular honeyed light the court ran on. Somewhere down the long gallery were thousands more. Somewhere among them, one flame answered to the name Tomas — if it still remembered having a name, which was precisely the problem they'd been chewing on for two days now.

Ansel sat up. She did everything quickly, he'd noticed, like a person who distrusted pauses. Even her grief moved fast, arriving all at once and then getting shoved into a pack and carried uphill.

"Say it," she said. "You've been circling it since yesterday. You count things when you're nervous. Lanterns today, stairs yesterday, the number of teeth in the Queen's choir the day before. Say whatever it is."

"I don't have teeth in the choir," Sef said. "I counted those out of professional interest."

"Sef."

Her voice did something to his name that made it feel less like an inventory label. He set the lantern down and sat across from her, folding his hands — the right over the left, so the more transparent one hid underneath. Old habit. Pointless here. He did it anyway.

"Stealing a soul from the Ledger isn't stealing," he said. "That's the first thing you need to understand. The Ledger doesn't lose entries. It trades them."

"Odile said the same thing about wards. Everything down here is a trade."

"Everything everywhere is a trade. Down here we're just honest about the arithmetic." He drew a shape in the dust between them — a rectangle, then a line through it. "Every entry has weight. Not metaphorical weight. Actual weight, the way a stone has weight. Your brother's flame weighs what it weighs, and the Ledger will not release it unless something of equal weight is put in its place. That's not cruelty. That's the whole architecture. The pact, the Queen, the hunger — all of it balances because nothing leaves without something entering. The court is a set of scales the size of a mountain, and the mountain is sitting on top of them."

Ansel was quiet. He could see her doing sums, because of course she was; she'd been a smuggler, and smugglers understood tolls better than priests ever would.

"So I pay," she said. "Memories. I've been paying memories."

"Yes, and the court is robbing you blind, but that's a different lecture. Memories are small coin. A memory might weigh as much as a candle-flame. A soul weighs—" he searched for the comparison and found it immediately, because he'd carried it for forty years, "—a soul weighs as much as a whole life. Every morning you ever woke up. Every name you ever learned. All of it, pressed together into one bright thing."

"Then I pay in something bigger. Years. Odile says the court takes years too."

"It does." He kept his voice level, which was the hardest thing he'd done in a decade, and he had once sung a eulogy for a man whose face he no longer remembered. "And you could pay for years and years and never balance a soul. You'd hollow yourself out to a whisper and Tomas's flame would still be sitting in its niche, because you can't get there from here. There's only one currency heavy enough."

The lantern nearest them flickered. Down the gallery, the flames burned in their thousands, patient as stars.

"No," Ansel said.

"You haven't heard the offer yet."

"There's only one thing down here that weighs a whole life, Sef, and I've been looking at it for two days." She pointed down the gallery — not at Tomas's flame but along the whole burning length of it, and then, with a turn of her wrist, at him. At the gray translucence under his skin. At the place where a man used to be.

"Ah," Sef said. "You *have* been paying attention. And here I thought you just liked my company."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Make jokes so I'll stop looking at you. It works on courtiers. It doesn't work on smugglers. We invented that trick."

He laughed — a real laugh, rusty, surprising them both. Then he stopped laughing, because the moment had arrived that he had been walking toward for two days, and rust or no rust, he meant to say it plainly.

"All right," he said. "Plainly. I was sold to this court when I was nineteen. My father gambled away the farm, and then he gambled away me, which I'm told is a very old story, though knowing that has never once helped. I've been a courtier of ash for forty years. Do you know what that means? It means I remember my name and almost nothing else. Sef Andrade. I know I loved someone — there's a shape in me shaped like loving someone — but I don't know their face, their voice, whether they were my mother or my wife or my friend. I know I had a dog. I know this because I keep reaching down to pet a dog who isn't there, and my hand knows exactly how high to reach. That's what's left. A name, a reach, and forty years of carrying lanterns for the thing that ate the rest."

Ansel's face had gone carefully still. Smuggler-still. He appreciated the courtesy.

"The court overcharges everyone," he went on. "But it underpays some of us, and I was one of the lucky underpaid ones — I kept enough of myself to hate it, and hating things keeps a man sharp. So. Here is my offer, plainly, and then you can refuse it, and I'll pretend to be surprised." He spread his hands, both of them, letting the light shine straight through the left one so she could see exactly what she'd be trading for. "A soul already pledged counts as payment. The Ledger doesn't care whose. Mine weighs a full life — nineteen years of it before the hollow ate the interest. Put me in the scale in your brother's place, and Tomas walks out with his name, his memories, his whole self. And I finally stop reaching down for a dog that isn't there."

The silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere far above, water dripped through stone with the patience of geology.

"No," Ansel said again.

"You said that already. I'd hoped repetition would wear you down. It's how the court does everything."

"My family has been paid for twice already." Her voice stayed low, but it had an edge on it now, the kind of edge he imagined she showed to people who tried to cheat her on mountain roads. "My mother paid with her life — whatever the Ledger says that actually bought. My brother was taken as the price of somebody else's ambition. I am not going to stand in this cathedral of bones and let a third stranger buy my family's freedom with himself. That's not rescue, Sef. That's just moving the debt around and calling it love."

"It's called arithmetic," Sef said gently. "And arithmetic doesn't care about our feelings. Someone pays. That's the design. The only question the universe is asking is *who*, and I am volunteering, which makes me the cheapest answer anyone has proposed all week."

"Then the design is wrong."

"Oh, certainly. The design is monstrous. I've had forty years to form an opinion." He smiled, and was aware of how much effort the smile cost, and did it anyway. "But you can't argue with scales, Ansel. You can only put things on them."

"Watch me."

* * *

She didn't speak to him for four hours, which in the Hollow Court was roughly a geological era of interpersonal conflict. She inventoried her pack instead. He watched her check the same three vials of ward-ash eleven times, and on the twelfth check she caught him watching and threw a dried apricot at his head, which he caught, because forty years of courtiership had left his reflexes intact and nearly nothing else.

"They'll notice," she said finally. "If you go into the scale. The other courtiers. Whoever runs the Ledger day to day. Won't they ask questions?"

"Courtiers go into the scale sometimes anyway. When we forget ourselves too far. There's a courtier down the west gallery who reached for his dog one too many times and simply... stepped into his own lantern. The Ledger accepted him as a donation. Nobody asked questions then either." He shrugged. "Grief looks like routine, down here, if you perform it correctly. I've had practice."

"That's the bleakest sentence anyone's ever said to me, and I grew up with Odile Ferran."

"Is she always like that?"

"She once described a beautiful sunrise as 'the sky showing off before it bills us.' So yes. But she'd also die before letting a debt land on someone innocent, which is why she drinks, probably, in advance." Ansel sat back against the wall. "Sef. Tell me honestly. If I said yes — if I let you do it — what happens to you? Actually happens?"

He considered lying, mostly out of politeness, and then decided he'd spent forty years being polite to monsters and wasn't going to extend the courtesy to friends.

"I stop," he said. "Not painfully. Not dramatically. The flame that's me gets weighed against the flame that's him, and mine goes into the Ledger's keeping, and whatever's left of Sef Andrade burns in a niche until the end of the pact. Maybe someone frees me someday. Maybe someone reaches for a dog beside my lantern and remembers why. It's not a bad ending, as endings go. Better than the one I was headed for, which was slowly becoming furniture."

"And you want that."

"What I want," Sef said, "stopped being available in 1198. What I have instead is a chance for one useful act before the end, and I find that I want that very much indeed. You've been down here a week and you've already given me more of myself than the court left — you use my name. Do you know how long it's been since anyone used my name? The other courtiers call each other by function. Lantern-bearer. Stair-warden. Ash-and-file. You walked in and said *Sef* like it was a word worth saying, and I have been insufferable about it internally ever since."

Ansel looked at him for a long moment. Then, horribly, her eyes went bright, and she scrubbed a hand across her face like she was angry at it.

"Stop that," she muttered. "I'm negotiating."

"Negotiators are allowed one tear per session. Court custom."

"Whose court?"

"Mine. I just founded it. It's very small and meets exclusively to annoy you."

The laugh broke out of her despite everything, wet and unwilling, and he counted it as a victory, though he suspected — correctly, as it turned out — that it changed nothing.

* * *

They argued for the rest of the day. It was, Sef reflected later, the best day he'd had in forty years.

She attacked the problem like a smuggler, which meant she attacked the toll itself. Could the scale be cheated? No — he'd seen men try; the Ledger weighed intention along with substance, which was deeply unfair. Could they substitute something else that weighed a life? She proposed it seriously: a beast's life, one of the great mountain lions, equal in years. He hated telling her no, and hated more the way her jaw set when he explained that the Ledger knew the difference between a soul pledged freely and a soul taken by force, and that the second weighed nothing at all — the scales rejected stolen goods. The court, he said, was greedy about everything except consent.

"Of course it is," Ansel said bitterly. "Consent is the expensive part. Consent is the whole price of the thing."

"Now you understand the architecture better than Marchen does, and she's been studying it for years."

At dusk-court — which was no different from dawn-court, since the Hollow had neither, but the courtiers lit fewer lanterns and spoke more quietly, so it felt like dusk — she tried the last approach he'd expected. She offered herself. Half of herself. Half a life of memories, half her remaining years, anything the scale would take in installments.

"The Ledger doesn't do installments," Sef said. "It's not a moneylender. It's a door, and doors are all-or-nothing."

"Everything down here is a bargain except the one bargain I need."

"Yes." He crouched in front of her, so they were eye to eye, so she couldn't look anywhere but at the gray in his skin and understand what half-measures led to. "Which is why I'm offering you the whole answer, and you're refusing it out of pride."

"It's not pride." Her voice cracked, just barely, on the last word, and she hated it, and he pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of kindness. "It's — my mother burned so that no one else in my family would ever be the price of anything. That was the entire point of her. And I will not unmake her sacrifice by feeding her another child to the dark. You're someone's child, Sef. Whoever reached for that dog — someone taught you to reach."

He hadn't expected that. Forty years, and the girl with the knife-hands found the one argument he had no armor for.

"I don't know who taught me," he said quietly.

"Then we'll find out." She stood, sudden and certain, the way she did everything. "When Tomas is free and the Ledger's open, we read your entry. We find out who sold you, and where you came from, and who's still up there missing a boy named Sef Andrade. And then you walk out of this mountain and reach for that dog and find out how tall he was. That's the deal. That's my counteroffer. Nobody goes into the scale. Everybody walks out."

"It won't work."

"Most of my plans don't. I've survived on the ones that do." She stuck out her hand — the callused one, the rope-burned one, a living hand in a cathedral of ash. "Do we have a bargain, courtier?"

Looking at that hand, Sef Andrade felt something move in the hollowed-out places, something small and stubborn, the way grass moves in a crack of pavement. It was not hope exactly. Hope was too large a word for what remained after forty years. But it was hope's smallest surviving relative, and it was enough to make him lie.

"We have a bargain," he said, and shook her hand, and felt the touch all the way through, both directions.

* * *

He waited until she slept — really slept this time, furrow smoothing out, knife hand slack — before he went to check on the third member of their expedition, because a bargain refused was not a problem solved, and Sef had lived long enough among predators to know that plans had a way of acting on their own.

He found Odile Ferran in the side gallery where she'd been spending her hours alone, supposedly mapping the route to the Ledger vault. She was sitting cross-legged on the floor with her broken compass in her lap and her lips moving, and the air around her had that particular shimmer he'd learned to dread — the shimmer of a transaction in progress. Of payment being taken.

"Odile," he said softly. "What did you give them?"

Her eyes came up. They were clear. They were terribly, terribly clear, the way a wiped slate is clear, and she smiled at him with the pleasant blankness of a woman greeting a stranger, and said:

"I'm sorry — do I know you?"

Down the gallery, unnoticed by either of them, the lantern holding Tomas Quintana's soul flared once, bright as a struck match, and went dim.
