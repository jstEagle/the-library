## 18. Hollowed Friends

Odile Ferran had been a ward-keeper for eleven years before the drinking started and a drunk ward-keeper for nine years after, and in all that time she had never once lost anything she could not find again at the bottom of her own coat pockets. Keys, chalk, the little brass compass her grandmother had pressed into her hands along with the oaths. Even her dignity — lost thoroughly, lost repeatedly — always turned up again by morning, bruised but present.

So it was a particular humiliation to discover that she had lost an entire night, and that the thing she'd traded it for was knowledge.

She woke on a cold bench in one of the Hollow Court's side galleries, cheek stuck to the stone with what she sincerely hoped was lantern-wax, and found the ward-restoring rite written out inside her head in her own handwriting, every sigil and cadence of it, as clean as if she'd copied it from her grandmother's lost primer. She knew how to re-anchor a soul-debt. She knew the sevenfold knot that bound a pact to stone. She knew things no living ward-keeper had known since the star-pact cracked.

She did not know why there was a woman sitting across from her, watching her with an expression Odile could only describe as *carefully neutral*, the way people look at strangers on long ferry rides.

"Good morning," the woman said. "Or whatever passes for it down here. Good dark."

"Good dark," Odile said automatically, because that was Cinderhatch talk, and then frowned. The woman was travel-worn, dark-haired, with a scar through one eyebrow and the kind of competent hands Odile associated with people who fixed things or stole them. Familiar hands, somehow. "Have we met?"

The woman's face did something very small and very controlled. "You could say that. I'm Ansel."

"Ansel," Odile repeated, tasting it. It sat strangely on her tongue, like a word in a language she used to speak. "Ansel Quintana?"

Something flickered across the stranger's face — relief and grief in the same instant, which was a lot to fit into one eyebrow. "Yes. You know me?"

"I know *of* you." Odile sat up too fast and regretted it; the gallery tilted politely and righted itself. "The smuggler. The tracker. Half of Cinderhatch swears you can walk Thornfell blindfolded, and the other half swears you're a witch. I'm the drunk who let the wards fall, so between us we've got the whole town's gossip covered." She stuck out her hand. "Odile Ferran."

Ansel looked at the hand for a moment too long. Then she took it, and her grip was steady, and her voice, when it came out, was only slightly wrong. "Pleased to meet you, Odile Ferran."

*Again,* said some small voice in the back of Odile's skull, in her grandmother's disapproving tone. But her grandmother had been dead twenty years, and dead women didn't know anything about the Hollow Court, so Odile told the voice to hush and went looking for her boots.

* * *

They were three galleries from the Ledger when Odile started noticing the holes.

It wasn't like forgetting. Forgetting was soft-edged, forgivable — where did I put the chalk, what was that fellow's name, the one with the beard. These holes had walls. She reached for the memory of leaving Cinderhatch and hit something smooth and blank, like touching a tooth socket with your tongue. She remembered deciding to come under the mountain. She remembered the flooded stair, distantly, the way you remember a story someone told you. She did not remember walking it.

"You paid for the rite," Ansel said quietly, when Odile asked — carefully, casually asked — how long they'd been traveling together. They'd stopped in an alcove where a lantern of pale bone-light burned without flame. "Last night. While I slept. You slipped off and found one of the court's toll-takers."

"Well, obviously I paid *something*." Odile waved this off. "That's how it works here, isn't it? Memory as coin. Sef told us—"

"You told him not to take memories. We agreed. Both of us. After—" Ansel stopped. Her jaw worked. "After what happened to me, with my mother's face. You held my hand while I couldn't remember her face anymore, Odile. You swore you'd never pay that way yourself. You called it 'letting the bastards pickpocket your soul.'"

"That doesn't sound like me," Odile said. "I don't swear things. Swearing's for people with better follow-through."

"Odile—"

"And anyway." She heard her own voice going light and breezy, the voice she used on customs officers. "Anyway, it worked, didn't it? I've got the rite. Whole thing, top to bottom. Seven knots, four refrains, the bit where you have to insult the mountain in old court-tongue — which, frankly, is the first sensible liturgical practice I've ever encountered. One night of sleep traded for the only spell that might save two cities and put the stars back. That's not being pickpocketed. That's *shopping*."

"What did you give them?" Ansel asked. "For the rite. What exactly did they take?"

And here was the thing Odile could not answer, because the question had edges too, and when she reached past them all she found was a receipt written in her own mind: *Paid in full.*

"I'm sure it was nothing important," she said.

* * *

Sef Andrade found them an hour later, materializing out of a side passage with the unhurried air of a man who had been half-hollow so long that urgency had worn off him like paint. He was carrying three cups of something steaming, which he distributed with the gravity of a sacrament.

"Nettle tea," he said. "Grown in the queen's gardens, under the roots, in the dark. Tastes like regret and calcium. Drink it anyway; it keeps your thoughts from fraying down here."

Odile drank. It tasted like regret and calcium. "Sef," she said, "a professional question. When the court takes a memory — when it takes several — does the person always know what they've lost?"

The cup paused halfway to his mouth. He looked at her over its rim, and then at Ansel, and something passed between them that Odile was not invited to read.

"No," Sef said gently. "That's rather the point. A stolen memory announces itself. A sold one tucks itself in and leaves a note that says *gone fishing*. You spend years wondering why certain rooms in your house feel cold." He settled onto the bench beside her, folding up like a heron. "What did you sell, Odile?"

"The price of a ward-restoring rite."

"Mm. And what do you remember of last night?"

"I remember deciding to do it. I remember feeling quite pleased with myself. I remember—" She stopped. Reached. Hit the smooth wall. Behind the wall, faintly, like music through stone: her grandmother's kitchen. Chalk dust. The smell of lamp oil. Someone laughing.

Someone laughing. Whose laugh?

"Sef," Odile said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended, "I can't remember my grandmother's voice."

"Ah," said Sef, and set down his cup, and took both her hands in his cold ones, and did not say *I warned you*, though Odile could see the sentence standing behind his teeth like a man locked out of his own house.

"They took her voice," Odile said. The holes were opening now, one after another, the way they say you see all the water at once when the dam finally goes. "They took her voice and they took — the winter of the great frost, when the wards sang all night — they took my ordination, Sef, I think they took my *ordination*, I can't remember the oaths, I said the oaths out loud every year for twenty years and now it's just — it's just words on a page I apparently once read—"

"Odile." Ansel crouched in front of her. Close enough that Odile could see her eyes properly for the first time, and think, absurdly, *I have seen these eyes cry before.* "Listen to me. The rite is real. What you bought is real. We are going to use it. But you don't get to decide what the court charges after the fact, and neither do I, and if you sneak off to trade again without telling us, I will — I'll—" She faltered. Whatever threat she'd been reaching for required a history Odile didn't have, and they both felt the shape of it, the missing weight of it, like a coat hook with no coat.

"You'll what?" Odile asked, because she genuinely wanted to know.

"I'll be very disappointed in you," Ansel said at last, "in a way you won't understand, and it will be worse than shouting."

"That does sound dreadful," Odile admitted.

* * *

They went back toward the Ledger because there was nowhere else to go, and because Tomas's soul-flame still burned somewhere in those thousands of lights, and because Ansel walked faster whenever the conversation drifted anywhere near feelings.

It was on the Long Gallery — bone pillars, lanterns like drowned moons, the floor humming faintly underfoot with the court's slow digestion — that Odile finally understood the full inventory of her purchase. Not through any dramatic revelation. Through a joke.

Because Ansel said something — dry, quick, half-smiling, the kind of thing you say to someone who knows the story — about the time Odile had bribed a border inspector with a wheel of bad cheese and a forged hymn license, and Odile laughed, and then realized she was laughing at a stranger's anecdote. There was no warmth under it. No *oh, that was the week the wards flickered, oh, that was when we first trusted each other*. Just a funny story about a cheese, told by a woman she'd known for a day.

"How long," Odile said, stopping in the middle of the gallery, "have we been traveling together?"

Ansel stopped too. "Since Cinderhatch. Before that. Since you fell off the wall of the ward-house trying to relight the beacon and I caught you, and you offered me a job, and I said no, and then you followed me for two days until I said yes just to make you stop."

"That sounds like me," Odile said. It sounded exactly like her. And she had absolutely nothing — no ache, no echo, no cold room. The memory hadn't been taken whole; it had been taken *clean*, with all its meaning scraped off and kept, and the shell handed back to her like an empty purse.

"They didn't take one night," Odile said slowly. "They took everything that made one night matter. Ansel, what else did I buy? What else did I pay while you were sleeping?"

"I don't know." Ansel's voice was flat and careful, the voice of a person holding something heavy at arm's length. "I woke up and you were back on your bedroll, smiling in your sleep. I thought — I hoped you'd just gone to look at the lanterns. Then you woke up and asked me who I was."

The gallery hummed. Somewhere far off, the Queen turned over in her vast sleep, and the lanterns all leaned the same direction at once, like grass under wind.

"You know what the worst part is?" Odile said. "I'd do it again. That's the worst part. Because I can't remember why I shouldn't. The court didn't just take my memories, it took my *reasons*. I sold the rite's price, and the price included the part of me that would have objected." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "That's not shopping. That's the shop eating the shopper."

"Yes," said Sef softly, from behind them. "Now you understand what we are."

* * *

They made camp — as much as one makes camp in a cathedral of bone — in a gallery above the Ledger hall, close enough that the soul-flames threw their light up the stairs like the glow of a city seen from hills. Odile sat apart, ostensibly keeping watch, actually writing.

Chalk. Thank the absent stars for chalk. On the flagstones, in her careful ward-keeper's hand, she wrote down everything that remained:

*Rite of Re-anchoring: intact. Grandmother: name (Marigold), face (approximate), voice (gone), kitchen (walls only). Ordination: gone. Oaths: gone. Cheese incident: shell only. Ansel Quintana: name, face, current purpose, zero prior history.*

Then, underneath, because she was still a keeper of records even if she was no longer anything else:

*Note to self, whoever that turns out to be: the court always overcharges. Do not go back. Whatever you're missing, it isn't worth more than what's left.*

She stared at that last line for a long time. Then she heard footsteps on the stair — Ansel's, she knew them already, that was something, she'd learned them in a day, maybe she could learn a lifetime in a week — and covered the writing with her coat, out of a modesty she couldn't account for.

"You should sleep," Ansel said.

"So should you."

"I don't sleep much down here." Ansel hesitated, then came and sat, uninvited, at the edge of the chalk lines. "Can I ask you something? As — as a new acquaintance."

"Ask."

"When you look at me," Ansel said, studying her own hands, "do you feel anything? Anything at all? Like you almost remember?"

Odile considered lying. Considered kindness. Settled on honesty, because it was quicker. "No. Should I?"

Ansel was quiet for a while. Down below, ten thousand souls burned their small patient flames, and among them, somewhere, a brother who didn't know his own name.

"We were friends," Ansel said finally. "Real ones. You used to call me 'tracker' like it was an insult and a compliment at the same time. You taught me to play cards badly on purpose so the smugglers would underestimate me. You—" Her voice caught, and she smoothed it flat. "You were the first person who ever saw my gift and wasn't afraid of me. So. That's what you sold. In case you wanted to know what it cost. It cost you me."

Odile sat with that. The lanterns leaned. Far beneath them, the Queen breathed.

"Well," Odile said at last, and her voice was rough, but it was hers, "then I suppose I'd better earn it back. I've done harder jobs." She picked up her chalk. "Tell me everything. From the wall of the ward-house onward. Every detail. Twice, if you've got it in you. If the court's going to keep my memories in its vaults, then fine — we'll rebuild them out of yours. I'll be a copy of a copy, but I'll be a *good* copy. Best-forged document in Thornfell. You'd all vouch for me."

And Ansel laughed — wetly, unexpectedly, helplessly — and wiped her eyes with her sleeve, and began at the beginning: *"You fell off the wall because you were drunk, and you were drunk because the wards were dying, and you blamed yourself, and you were right to, and you got up anyway..."*

Odile wrote it all down. It was like filling a house with borrowed furniture. Nothing fit quite right. But a house, she thought, is a house.

She was halfway through the second telling — *and then you followed me for two days, complaining the entire time, eating all my dried plums* — when Sef came up the stairs two steps at a time, which he never did, because urgency had been worn off him like paint, and his face was the color of the lanterns.

"There's smoke in the upper galleries," he said. "Torch-smoke. Boot-mud. Ward-salt." He looked at each of them in turn. "Someone from the surface has come down the Drowned Stair. Someone with an army behind them."

Down in the Ledger hall, ten thousand flames flickered at once — and among them, Odile would later swear, one small flame flared bright, as if it had heard its name called from a very long way off.
