## 19. The Beasts Descend

The dark beneath the mountain had a texture, Ansel was learning. Not the honest dark of a cellar or a moonless road, which simply hid things. This dark *leaned*. It pressed against her ears like deep water, and somewhere far below it made a sound she had no name for — not breathing, exactly, though it rose and fell like breath. More like the mountain itself, chewing slowly on something that never filled it.

She sat with her back against a gallery wall of veined black stone, knees drawn up, and tried to remember her mother's face.

That was the trouble. She reached for it the way you reach for a coin in your pocket, and found only the shape of where it should be. She knew she had loved that face. She knew it had laughed at something Tomas once said about goats. But the features themselves were gone, paid out at some toll-gate in the court's winding halls, spent like a smuggler's last silver to buy them passage past a thing made of lantern-light and teeth.

"Stop doing that," Odile said from across the gallery. The old ward-keeper sat cross-legged over her kit, re-inking a warding circle for the fourth time. Her hands shook. They had been shaking since the rite, since she'd come back from the Ledger's antechamber not knowing Ansel's name and had to be told it twice, and had wept — briefly, furiously, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand as if grief were an inconvenience — before getting back to work. "You'll wear a groove in yourself, reaching for it like that."

"You told me my name twice," Ansel said. "You don't even remember telling me."

"I remember being annoyed at how long your name is." Odile blew on the ink. "Ansel Quintana. Four syllables of stubbornness. Now hush. I'm trying to hold this circle together and you're radiating gloom like a wet dog by a fire."

Ansel almost smiled. Almost. Sef stood at the gallery's edge, half in shadow, and his stillness reminded her that smiling was a currency too, down here, and she wasn't sure what she had left to pay with.

*They're coming,* he had said, an hour ago, drifting back from the lower galleries with his ash-grey face unreadable. *Marchen's vanguard entered the Hollow Stair. A hundred soldiers, maybe more behind them. And she's coming with them.*

A hundred soldiers. Against one disgraced ward-keeper whose memory leaked like a cracked jug, one half-hollow courtier who might turn at any moment out of loyalty or terror, and one smuggler who had spent twenty years pretending she couldn't do the one thing she did best.

Ansel looked at her hands. Callused rope-hands, map-hands, hands that had lied their whole life by being ordinary.

"Odile," she said. "When I was small — before — my mother told me the gift wasn't a door you open. She said it was a well. You don't call the beasts. You go quiet enough, deep enough, that they hear the water in you."

"Poetic," Odile said. "Is there a version with instructions?"

"She died before the instructions." Ansel stood. Her legs ached from days of walking the court's impossible geometry, but underneath the ache something else had been gathering, low and patient, the way weather gathers. "I've spent my whole life keeping the well covered. Stones on top. Lies on top of the stones." She flexed her fingers. "I'm going to take the stones off."

Sef turned. In the lantern-less gloom his eyes caught light that wasn't there, two pale coins. "You can't mean to call them *here*. Beneath the mountain. Beast-speakers don't work underground — everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows wrong." Ansel thought of the wolves who had carried the dying messenger to her door. Thought of the bear she'd turned against Marchen's patrol, the hot astonishing joy of it, the first time she'd spoken aloud without flinching. Thought of the blind bats in the drowned stair, singing the dark into maps. "Stone carries sound better than air. Root carries it better than stone. And everything that lives in Thornfell has been listening to the Hollow's hunger for years without knowing what it was hearing. They're *afraid*, Sef. Fear travels through rock like a drumbeat. I just have to give them something else to listen to."

Odile set down her brush very carefully, the way she did when she was about to say something important and didn't want her hands to betray her. "If you open that well all the way, girl, you may not be able to close it again. That's why they burned people like your mother. Not because the gift is evil. Because it doesn't have edges."

"I know." Ansel swallowed. "But neither does the Queen."

* * *

She went alone to the gallery's heart, where the black stone opened into a natural hollow like the cupped palm of something enormous. Roots had found their way down here — Thornfell pine, white birch, the deep ironwood that grew only on the mountain's shoulders — threading through cracks in the court's architecture, drinking from channels of meltwater that had no business existing this far under. Where root touched stone, the stone was warmer. Alive, in the slow mineral way mountains are alive.

Ansel knelt. Put both palms flat against the root of an ironwood, older than Cinderhatch, older maybe than the wards.

And began, very quietly, to stop being loud.

It was harder than it sounds. Twenty years of concealment is twenty years of noise — the constant hum of *ordinary, ordinary, just a tracker, nothing to see* — and all of it lived in the body, in the set of the shoulders, the careful blankness of the eyes. She peeled it off layer by layer. The smuggler's swagger. The orphan's armor. The careful, practiced dullness she'd worn like a hood so no priest or neighbor would ever look at her twice.

Underneath all of it, at the bottom of the well, there was water.

She let herself feel it: the gift rising, cool and enormous, filling her the way meltwater fills a spring channel. Not power, exactly. More like *attention* — a vast, gentle, total attention, turned outward through root and stone and the wet dark veins of the mountain.

*Hello,* she said, without words, in the language that had gotten her mother burned. *Hello, hello, hello.*

For a long moment, nothing. The mountain chewed its slow sound below. Odile's ward-circle hummed faintly at her back. Ansel knelt with her hands on the root and poured herself outward and felt like a fool, like a child shouting into a canyon, like—

Then the root shivered.

Not wind-shiver. *Answer*-shiver. A pulse came up through the ironwood, through her palms, up her arms and into her chest, and it was old and rough and patient, and it said — not in words, words were for creatures with throats shaped like hers — it said:

*We know you. Little-voice. We have heard you all your life, whispering to lions, lying to wolves with kindness. Why do you uncover yourself now?*

Ansel's breath caught. She hadn't known they'd been listening. All those years of careful bargains, the lion at the pass, the ravens she'd paid in roadkill, the foxes she'd warned away from snares — she'd thought she was hiding. She'd been *audible* the whole time, a small clear voice under the noise of the world, and every creature on the mountain had known exactly what she was, and none of them had ever told.

Her eyes stung. There was no time for that feeling, but she let it sit in her chest anyway, warm as a coal.

*Because the hunger below is going to eat everything,* she answered. *The souls above. The cities. And when the free folk are gone, the court will get hungrier, and it will drink the roots next, and the streams, and then there will be nothing left on Thornfell but stone and teeth. You've felt it. Don't tell me you haven't.*

Silence. Long silence. Somewhere far above, through a hundred fathoms of rock, she felt — distantly, like weather on the edge of hearing — the movement of many feet. Soldiers on the Hollow Stair. And beneath them, slower, vaster, a presence like a cold star descending: Veyra Marchen, coming to sell the world.

*We have felt it,* the ironwood-root said at last. *The caves by the western ice are empty. The bats have gone silent in the high galleries. Even the worms have moved downslope. The hunger grows, little-voice, and hunger does not stop at the last mouth it filled.* A pause, and then something that was almost humor, dry as bark: *You ask much. Wolves do not fight in the dark. Bears do not fight for men.*

"Not for men," Ansel said aloud, and her voice cracked and she didn't care. "For the mountain. For the roots and the streams and the dens. And not for me — *with* me. I'm not asking you to be soldiers. I'm asking you to help me protect our home. That's all any of us are doing."

Another silence. Then, from deeper in the stone, another voice joined the first — lower, heavier, with the slow gravity of a creature that sleeps four months of the year: a bear, somewhere in the middle galleries, awake when it should have been denned, restless and angry at a winter that wouldn't end.

*The small flying ones already sing of you,* the bear said. *They mapped your road down. They say the water-in-you is deep.*

"The bats came to me first," Ansel whispered. "In the drowned stair. They sang me the way."

*They sing of many things,* said a third voice, high and quick and layered, a chorus rather than a single throat — the bats themselves, thousands of them, hanging in the galleries between here and the surface, and now all of them speaking at once through the stone like rain on a roof. *We sing of the starless night. We sing of the flame-that-was-your-brother. We sing of the tolls you paid. Little-voice, we have been waiting for you to speak loudly enough to answer.*

Ansel pressed her forehead against the root and laughed, or sobbed, or both — some ragged animal sound that echoed strangely in the hollow. All her life she'd thought the gift was a secret. It had been a *conversation*, and she'd been the only one refusing to talk.

*What do you need?* asked the wolf-voice, and there were more wolf-voices under it now, a pack's worth, a hillside's worth, coming down through the stone like cold running water.

"Marchen's soldiers are on the Hollow Stair," Ansel said. "A hundred or more, and worse behind them. They're coming to feed the Queen the souls of the free cities. I need time. I need chaos. I need the dark to belong to *us* tonight."

*The dark always belonged to us,* said the bear, and there was something in its voice that was very nearly a grin. *Men only borrow it.*

* * *

They came down through the mountain the way water comes down through a hillside: everywhere at once, inevitable, finding every crack.

Ansel felt them arrive before she saw them — a pressure of living warmth flooding the cold galleries, dozens of minds touching hers through the gift, each one distinct as a fingerprint. The wolves came first, pouring out of fissures Ansel had never known existed, grey shapes moving through the bone-lit corridors with the unhurried confidence of creatures in their own house. Their leader was a big scarred female whom Ansel recognized with a jolt — the same wolf who had led her caravan safely past the ice-falls two winters ago, in exchange for three elk carcasses and a promise. The wolf looked at her now with pale yellow eyes, and there was recognition in them, and something that might have been reproach.

*You could have called louder,* she seemed to say. *All these years. You could have called louder.*

"I'm sorry," Ansel whispered, and meant it more than she had ever meant anything she'd said to a human being.

The bears came slower, a dozen of them, shaggy avalanches of muscle filling the wide galleries shoulder to shoulder. One of them — the huge cinnamon male from the border patrol, the one she'd turned against Marchen's soldiers — huffed at her in greeting, and the huff carried a question: *Where?*

Ansel closed her eyes and let the gift spread outward, mapping. Through bat-song relayed from gallery to gallery she could see the Hollow Stair now, could feel the soldiers' boots on it, a hundred small hard rhythms descending. Ahead of them, court sentries made of lantern-light and malice were falling back, confused — the court had never been invaded from *above* by anything that wasn't human, and it didn't know what to make of the mountain turning itself inside out.

"There," she said, pointing down the main gallery toward the great junction where the Stair met the court's outer ring. "The vanguard reaches the junction within the hour. Sef — the ceiling there. How is it held?"

Sef drifted to the gallery's edge, and for a moment his hollow eyes went distant, reading the architecture the way Odile read wards. "Old bone-work. The courtiers call it the Ribs. It hasn't been maintained since the star-pact broke — the court doesn't build anymore, it only consumes." He looked at her, and something flickered across his ruined face that was almost hope, and it hurt to see, hope on a face that had forgotten how to wear it. "You're thinking of bringing it down on them."

"I'm thinking of giving them a very bad evening," Ansel said. "And if a few tons of ancient rib-bone happens to fall on the ones carrying soul-cages... well. The mountain giveth."

Odile snorted from her circle. "The mountain giveth. I'm putting that on your memorial stone." She tied off the last knot of the warding and stood, joints crackling like green wood in a fire. "Circle's ready. As long as we stay inside it, the court's little horrors can't unmake us directly — they'll have to do it the old-fashioned way, with teeth. Which, I note, we now have several hundred of."

As if summoned, a wolf trotted to the circle's edge and sat, tail curled, watching Ansel with an expression of profound patience. Behind it, the galleries were filling — wolves on the ridges of bone-architecture, bears wedged improbably into corridors built for courtiers, and overhead, in the high vaults, a soft rustling thunder as the bats gathered, thousands upon thousands, their blind song weaving a living map of every approaching soldier, every soul-cage, every flicker of lantern-fire.

Ansel stood in the middle of it and felt the well-water rise in her until she was dizzy with it. This was the thing they'd burned her mother for. This was the thing she'd hidden under twenty years of lies and ordinariness. And here it was, unfolded at last, vast and warm and *alive*: not a curse, not a trick, but a conversation the whole mountain had been waiting to finish.

"They're close," Sef said quietly. His pale eyes had gone fixed on the far dark. "The vanguard. And—" he paused, and his voice dropped to something raw, "—she's with them. Marchen. I can smell the Ledger on her. She's carrying pages."

Ansel's hand found the knife at her belt, then left it. Knives weren't the point anymore. She knelt, put her palm to the warm stone, and let the gift pour out of her one final time — not a whisper now, not a bargain, but a voice like a landslide given gentleness.

*To every den and hollow and root-run on Thornfell:* she sang through the stone. *Tonight the dark is ours. Tonight we are the mountain's answer. Hold nothing back — and harm no soul-flame, for some of those lights are prisoners, not jailers. We are coming to free them.*

From a thousand throats, through a thousand feet of living rock, the answer came back, and it sounded like an avalanche deciding to be kind:

*We hear you, little-voice. We descend.*

Far below, at the junction of the Hollow Stair, the first torchlight rounded the bend — and above it, silent as snowfall beginning, the bats started to move.
