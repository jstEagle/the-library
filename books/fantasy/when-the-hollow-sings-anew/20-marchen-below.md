## 20. Marchen Below

Veyra Marchen had been descending for six hours, and she had already decided she hated the Hollow Court.

It was not the dark. Veyra had grown up in the salt-cellars of Rivermoor's merchant quarter, where a girl learned early that darkness was merely an inventory problem — something you solved with lamps, ledgers, and sufficient ruthlessness. Nor was it the smell, though the smell was remarkable: wet stone and old smoke and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going bad in a sealed room. She had smelled worse in the conscription camps. She had *built* worse.

No, what Veyra hated was the acoustics.

Every footstep her army took echoed back at them from below, from ahead, from places footsteps should not come from. Ten thousand soldiers marching sounded like twenty thousand, and the extra ten thousand were laughing.

"General," said Captain Ivo Reyes, falling into step beside her chair. They had brought her down the Stair in a litter because her leg had never forgiven the winter at Kestrel Pass, and because a general who limps invites questions. "Scouts report movement on the galleries above us. Animals. Large numbers."

"Bears?"

"Wolves, mostly. Bats." He paused. "One bear. Very large."

"One bear is a scouting error, not an army."

"The wolves are moving in formation, General."

Veyra considered this. In her experience, formation was a human invention, one of their better ones, up there with currency and the siege ladder. If the beasts of Thornfell were drilling, then someone was teaching them, and there was only one person on this mountain foolish enough or gifted enough to teach them anything.

The beast-speaker. Quintana's daughter, if the reports could be believed — and Veyra's reports could always be believed, because she paid the people who wrote them enough that lying became economically irrational.

"Good," she said.

Ivo blinked. "General?"

"If they're organized, they can be negotiated with. If they're a mob, they can only be survived. I prefer negotiating." She adjusted the blanket over her knees and looked out at the torches strung along the column like a necklace being pulled slowly into a throat of stone. "Send word: no one fires on the animals unless fired upon. And Ivo — if any man so much as *points* at a soul-flame, I'll have his hands. Are we understood?"

"Loudly, General."

*Good*, she thought again, and did not say the rest aloud, which was: *because every flame down here is collateral, and collateral is only useful while it is intact.* The free cities' souls — eleven thousand conscripts, each one signed over with a thumbprint and a lie about protection — were the entire weight of her offer. You did not smash the scales before the weighing.

* *

They reached the outer galleries near midnight by Veyra's pocket-watch, which had stopped agreeing with anyone else's watch two days ago and which she kept consulting purely out of spite.

The Hollow opened before them like a held breath.

Her engineers had described it to her, badly, the way men describe cathedrals — all vaulting and reverence and no load-bearing detail. The reality was simpler and worse: a cavern the size of a city, ribbed with bone-white pillars that might have been stone and might have been something older, hung with lanterns that burned without oil. And filling the lanterns — no. Not filling. *Being* the fuel.

Souls. Thousands of them, drifting in their glass like moths that had forgotten how to want out.

Veyra stood very still and let herself feel what she felt, which was nothing, which was the point. A woman who felt things in front of a room full of souls-for-sale would soon be selling her own.

"There," said Ivo quietly.

At the far end of the gallery, where the bone pillars gathered into something like a dais, stood the Ledger.

She had imagined a book. Everyone imagined a book; books were comforting, books had edges. This was a slab of black stone tall as three men, and its surface moved — writing crawling across it like frost forming in fast-forward, entries inscribing themselves as new debts came due somewhere in the world above. Even at this distance Veyra could hear it, a whisper like sand poured onto sand.

And standing before it, small against all that dark, was a woman with grey eyes and her mother's stubborn jaw, flanked by a drunk-looking old ward-keeper and a courtier whose left half kept trying to drift out of focus.

"Quintana," Veyra said, mostly to herself. "You're early."

* *

They met across the Ledger because both parties, independently, had decided the Ledger was the only furniture in this entire cathedral worth standing near. It gave them a table. Negotiators needed tables; tables told everyone present that this was business, not slaughter.

Up close, the beast-speaker looked younger than her reputation and tireder than her years, which was the usual arithmetic of people who cared about things. Her companion — Ferran, the disgraced ward-keeper, smelling of gin and old oaths — gripped a crowbar like a rosary. The courtier, Sef-something, watched Veyra with an expression she recognized from mirrors: a man doing sums he didn't like.

"Veyra Marchen," Ansel said. Not *General*. Interesting.

"You have the advantage of me, Mistress Quintana, though I suspect we're even — you know my seal, and I know your mother's name is on page four hundred and six."

Something flickered behind those grey eyes. Good. Veyra collected reactions the way other women collected lace.

"You've brought an army into my mountain," Ansel said.

"*Your* mountain. How quickly ownership transfers downward." Veyra gestured, and Ivo stepped forward bearing the strongbox — iron-banded, sealed with wax, heavy as guilt. Inside were the contracts. Eleven thousand of them. Each one a life, signed away by a frightened hand in exchange for bread and a promise. "You've noticed the wards fell. You've noticed the stars went out. You may even have worked out why — the Court starves, and starving courts eat whatever isn't anchored. I am here to anchor it."

"With people."

"With *contracts*," Veyra said pleasantly. "Which is a different thing entirely, though I understand the confusion. Look at them." She nodded upward, at the flames in their lanterns. "Sold souls. Sold by whom? By families who couldn't feed them. By creditors. By kings, when kings ran short. The Court has always eaten; the only question ever asked was who got paid. My arrangement simply makes the payment honest. No more snatching travelers off the roads. No more burning beast-speakers — ah, forgive me. Poor choice of reference."

Ansel's jaw tightened. "Eleven thousand conscripts."

"Eleven thousand souls currently eating my army's rations and producing nothing but fear," Veyra corrected. "I'm offering them to the Queen in exchange for dominion over the surface — real dominion, written into the pact, witnessed by the Court itself. The cities keep their walls, their markets, their little councils and their terrible poetry. I keep order. The Court keeps fed. Everyone keeps breathing." She smiled the smile she used for closing deals over bodies. "Compare that to your alternative, which appears to be wolves."

"The wolves," Ansel said, "are better company than you'd expect."

"I don't doubt it. Wolves have never read one of my contracts."

For a moment — a long moment, long enough that the Ledger's whisper seemed loud — the beast-speaker just looked at her. Not with hatred. That was the unsettling part. With *assessment*, the way Veyra herself looked at a bridge before deciding whether to burn it or tax it.

"Why?" Ansel asked finally. "You had the cities already. Rivermoor opened its gates. You could have ruled from above like every tyrant in history and never smelled this place. What does a woman like you need with a starving god?"

And there it was: the question Veyra had answered a hundred times in the privacy of her own skull, in the voice of her father counting salt-barrels, in the voice of the famine winter when she'd learned that mercy was a currency that inflated to worthless the moment everyone spent it at once.

"Because I watched the last pact fail," she said. "Not collapse — *fail*, slowly, the way all pacts fail when they depend on people keeping promises they never understood. The old warders pledged souls they didn't own. Your mother burned herself for a crack she didn't make. Generations of good intentions, and the stars still went out, because good intentions are not collateral." She laid one gloved hand flat on the Ledger's cold stone. "I am offering the first contract in this Court's history where everyone knows the price going in. That is not cruelty, Mistress Quintana. That is the only kindness left that doesn't lie about itself."

Sef made a sound — half laugh, half cough. "You rehearse that."

"Every sentence worth saying is rehearsed," Veyra said. "That's what separates negotiation from screaming."

Above them, faint through miles of rock, something howled — many somethings, in unison, and the sound came down the galleries like weather. Ivo's hand went to his sword. Veyra did not move.

"Your army," she said mildly, "is arriving."

"They're not mine," Ansel said. "That's rather the point of them."

* *

Later — after the terms had been stated, countersigned in wax, and set aside; after Ansel had asked for the thing Veyra had known she would ask for, time, a night, the universal stalling tactic of the cornered — Veyra sat alone in the tent her quartermasters had pitched in a cathedral of bones, because she had decided long ago that a general must sleep in the shape of her authority even when the authority was absurd.

She could not sleep. Instead she read.

The contracts, all eleven thousand, in triplicate. Names, ages, thumbprints. She read them the way she read everything: looking for the flaw, the clause, the crack where the whole edifice would eventually split. There was always a crack. Her father taught her that too, in his way, by dying of one.

Near the bottom of the third box she found the Quintana boy's entry — Tomas, aged nine, sold twenty-two years past, seller's mark worn nearly smooth. She had pulled it deliberately. Leverage was not cruelty; leverage was *preparation*, and she liked to know precisely what she held.

A child's thumbprint. Small as a seed.

Veyra sat with the paper for a long time, and felt the nothing again, and noted, with a bookkeeper's precision, that the nothing was costing her slightly more effort than usual tonight.

Outside the tent, the howling came again, closer now, threaded under it with a sound she hadn't heard yet in all her forty-four years of listening:

Singing.

Someone — something — was singing to her army in the dark, and by the sound of the silence that followed each verse, her soldiers were listening.

Veyra closed the box on the child's thumbprint and reached for the lamp.

"A night," she murmured to the empty air, to the Court, to the grey-eyed woman across the Ledger. "Take your night. But dawn comes down here too, Mistress Quintana. And at dawn, everyone signs something."

The singing outside grew louder — and now, unmistakably, beneath the wolf-harmonies, a human voice was leading it, and it knew all the words.
