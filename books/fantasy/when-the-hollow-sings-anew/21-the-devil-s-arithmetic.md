## 21. The Devil's Arithmetic

Veyra Marchen poured the tea herself.

That was the first thing wrong with it. Ansel had spent three days in the usurper's camp below the mountain — a guest, technically, though the word sat in her mouth like a pebble — and she had watched Marchen's soldiers do everything at a march. They ate at a march. They slept, presumably, at a march. But their general knelt at a dented copper kettle with the unhurried ease of a grandmother and warmed the pot before she filled it.

"You take it plain," Marchen said. It wasn't a question. "You've been drinking smuggler tea for twenty years. Burnt leaves, boiled twice, whatever the couriers could hide in a saddlebag. Your tongue has forgotten what patience tastes like."

"My tongue remembers plenty," Ansel said.

"I imagine it does." Marchen set the cup in front of her. The tent was large enough to hold a war council and furnished like a scholar's study instead: maps weighted with river stones, a shelf of ledgers, one small window flap through which the dark of the Hollow's upper galleries leaked in like smoke. Somewhere beyond the canvas, the court hummed its low, hungry note, the sound Ansel had begun to think of as the mountain breathing.

She did not touch the tea. Marchen noticed her not touching it and smiled, which was somehow worse than if she'd been insulted.

"Poison would be wasteful," Marchen said. "I've read your file."

"People have files on me?"

"Everyone has a file. Mine is eleven pages. Yours is four." She sat down across the folding table, and up close, without the army and the banners and the forty paces of stone between them, she was smaller than Ansel expected. Grey at the temples. Tired around the eyes in a way that looked earned rather than painted on. "Four pages is impressive, actually. Most people who matter have six."

"What's in my four?"

"That you're the best guide Thornfell has ever produced. That you vanished into the Hollow with a disgraced ward-keeper and no backup plan worth the name. That you turned a bear against one of my patrols." Marchen sipped her own tea. "And that your brother was sold to this place twenty years ago, under a seal I'm told you've now seen."

The tent felt suddenly colder. Or maybe that was just the truth arriving, the way it always did — not gradually, but all at once, like stepping off a ledge you'd been standing on for days without noticing.

"You know about Tomas."

"I know about every soul in the Ledger. That's rather the point of a ledger." Marchen set down her cup. "Which brings me to why you're here, drinking tea you don't trust, in a camp you can't leave, listening to a woman you've decided to hate."

"I haven't decided to hate you," Ansel said. "I'm still gathering evidence."

Something flickered across Marchen's face — surprise, quickly folded away, but not quickly enough. Ansel filed it. Whatever else the usurper was, she was not used to being made to wait for other people's verdicts.

* * *

Here was what Ansel had learned in three days as Marchen's guest, and she had learned it the way she learned everything — by watching, by keeping her mouth shut, and by letting other people talk until they told her more than they meant to:

Marchen's soldiers were afraid of her, but they were *proud* to be afraid of her, the way children are proud of a strict teacher. They believed she was hard because the world was hard, and that hardness was a kind of honesty.

Her officers called the Hollow Court "the partner." Never the Queen. Never the hunger. The partner, as though it were a merchant house in Rivermoor with whom terms had been struck.

And once, passing the command tent at night, Ansel had heard Marchen alone inside, speaking very quietly to someone — or something — in a voice Ansel recognized from her own worst moments. The voice of a person negotiating with something enormous and trying very hard to sound calm.

So when Marchen folded her hands and said, "Let's talk about your brother," Ansel was almost ready.

Almost.

"Tomas Quintana," Marchen said. "Sold in the winter of his ninth year. Entry four thousand and eleven in the Ledger. Purchased against a debt incurred by—" she paused, delicately, "—a third party. He has been a flame in the court's treasury for two decades. He does not age. He does not suffer, precisely. He simply burns, slowly, the way all the pledged souls burn, to keep the star-pact stitched together and the mountain standing and the cities above it from sliding into the sea."

"You say that like it's a comfort."

"It's context. I deal in context." Marchen leaned forward. "Here is what I offer. Tomorrow morning, I will sign an order releasing soul four thousand and eleven from the Ledger. Freely given. No replacement required — I'll cover the weight myself, out of my own pledged accounts, and yes, before you ask, I have accounts, everyone who deals with the court does, that's how the arithmetic works. By tomorrow evening, your brother walks out of this mountain with a name, a body, and the rest of his life ahead of him."

Ansel's heart did something complicated and painful. She kept her face still. Twenty years of hiding a gift taught you stillness the way rivers teach stones roundness.

"And in exchange," Marchen continued, "you give me the beasts."

"The beasts."

"The beasts of Thornfell. Your gift — the one you've hidden since you were a child, the one that got your mother burned, although we'll come back to that story, because I suspect you only know half of it." Marchen's eyes didn't waver. "Every wolf, every bear, every bat and hawk and mountain lion that answers your voice. I want them bound to my service. And I want the smuggler roads — the real maps, the passes, the ways through and under Thornfell that your network has guarded for generations. My armies move through the mountains unopposed. Your beasts patrol them. In return, the free cities keep their souls where they belong, in their own chests, and I keep the pact fed by other means."

"Other means," Ansel repeated. "Meaning conscripts."

"Meaning volunteers," Marchen said, with the serenity of a woman who had practiced the sentence in a mirror. "There are always volunteers. The desperate, the indebted, the grieving. The court takes memory freely given now — you'd be surprised how much people will give. A widow will trade a decade of grief for her children's safety. Is that so different from what your mother—"

"Don't," Ansel said quietly, "say my mother's name."

The tent went very still. Outside, a soldier coughed. The mountain breathed its long hungry breath.

Marchen studied her for a moment, then inclined her head — a small concession, offered like a coin to a beggar. "Very well. But understand what you're refusing to hear. I am not the villain of this story, Quintana. I'm the accountant. The old ward-keepers let the pact rot for two hundred years because maintaining it was unpleasant and nobody wanted to look at the bill. The stars went out on their watch, not mine. Someone has to pay the court, and I would rather it be paid in memories the owners choose to sell than in cities full of souls dragged here screaming." She spread her hands. "I'm offering you mercy. I'm even offering it dressed as mercy, which is more than most rulers bother with."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I take the roads anyway, eventually, and the beasts stay wild and get hunted, and your brother stays a flame in a vault until the court grows hungry enough to burn him fast." Marchen picked up her tea again. "But you won't say no. You've already done the arithmetic. Everyone does. One boy against two cities. One woman's freedom against ten thousand souls. Even you can make that sum come out."

Ansel looked at the tea. Steam curled off it, patient and ordinary, absurdly domestic in the mouth of hell.

"One night," she said.

Marchen raised an eyebrow.

"I decide at dawn. Not before. And I decide alone — no heralds, no officers, no helpful reminders." She stood. "If you're half the accountant you claim to be, you'll grant me that. Bad decisions get made in a hurry. Good ones need a night to steep. Like tea."

For a heartbeat — just one — something moved behind Marchen's eyes that looked almost like respect. Almost like loneliness.

"Dawn," she agreed. "The tent at the end of the row is yours. Sef Andrade will bring you supper."

"Sef," Ansel said carefully, "is currently holding a collapsing gallery together with his bare hands and his remaining conscience."

"He was. He's been relieved. Rotating shifts; even martyrs need sleep." Marchen returned to her tea. "One night, Quintana. Don't waste it reading the Ledger. I know that's what you're planning. The answer isn't in the books."

But she was wrong about that. Ansel was almost certain she was wrong about that. And there was one page — one old, brittle, water-stained page — that she had been circling for two days like a moth around a lantern, afraid of what it would show her and more afraid of never knowing.

* * *

Sef brought the supper himself, balancing a tray with the careful grace of a man who had learned that bodies in the Hollow were mostly habit. Half-hollow, he looked like a man seen through smoked glass: familiar shapes, wrong edges. His eyes were kind, which in this place was either courage or a wound that hadn't finished opening.

"She offered you the brother," he said, setting down bread, cheese, dried fruit that had no business existing underground.

"She offered me everything," Ansel said. "That's how you know it's a trap. Cheap things are sold cheaply."

"Mm." He lowered himself onto the cot across from hers, joints crackling like green wood. "Can I offer you some unsolicited advice from a man who signed his own soul away and regrets the paperwork most of all?"

"Is any advice in this place unsolicited? Or unpaid?"

He laughed — a dry, rusted sound, but real. "Fair. Here it is anyway, free of charge, which should make you suspicious. Veyra Marchen believes her own arithmetic. That's what makes her dangerous. A liar you can catch. A true believer just keeps counting." He looked at his hands, at the faint grey translucence of the fingertips. "She genuinely thinks she's saving everyone. She genuinely thinks one soul weighed against ten thousand is a sum, not a person. And she genuinely thinks you'll take the deal, because in her experience everyone does, eventually, when the numbers are big enough."

"Do you think I will?"

Sef considered her for a long moment. "No," he said. "But I think you're going to do something worse than either option, and I think you already know what it is, and I think that's why you asked for the night." He rose, stiff as an old door. "Read your page, Quintana. Then come find me before dawn. There's a version of tomorrow where nobody pays for anybody — but it needs three people to build, and one of them has to be brave in a way that doesn't photograph well."

He paused at the tent flap.

"For what it's worth — the lullaby. When you sang it to the flame in the treasury. The whole court heard it. Things down here that haven't remembered anything in centuries remembered it." His voice went quieter. "It's why half the courtiers are helping you now, whether they know it or not. Songs are the only currency the Queen can't mint herself."

Then he was gone, and Ansel was alone with the bread she couldn't eat and the decision she couldn't make and the page she couldn't stop thinking about.

* * *

She waited until the camp settled. Until the watch changed and the fires burned low and the mountain's breathing faded into the background the way thunder fades if you stand in it long enough. Then she took the lamp Odile had charmed — poor hollowed Odile, who had looked at her that morning with polite blankness and asked if they'd met — and she went down into the archive where the Ledger lay open on its lectern of black stone, thousands upon thousands of entries burning softly in the dark like a field of captive fireflies.

Entry four thousand and eleven glowed near the middle. *Tomas Quintana.* She had read it so many times she could recite it. She made herself look away.

Because past the recent entries, past Marchen's neat columns and the older clerks' crabbed script, the Ledger went back further than anyone above ground remembered. Back past the founding of the free cities. Back to the first pages, where the ink wasn't ink at all but something pressed into the vellum like scars.

And there — page after page of pledges and debts and names she didn't know — she found the entry she had been circling for two days.

Not a sale. Not a pledge.

A *sealing.*

The date was twenty-three years before Ansel's birth. The hand was old-fashioned, formal, and the entry read:

*Paid in full, freely given, by Mirabel Quintana of Cinderhatch: one life, surrendered willingly at the crack beneath the Widow's Tooth, to close the breach in the star-pact. Witnessed by the Court. Entered not as debt but as gift. Let no clerk call it otherwise.*

Ansel read it four times. Her mother's name. Her mother's *choice.*

Not dragged to a pyre by a mob for the crime of speaking to beasts. Not burned in shame and fear, the story Ansel had carried like a stone in her chest since she was seven years old. Her mother had walked to the crack beneath the Widow's Tooth — the same crack, Ansel realized with a cold spreading certainty, that ran somewhere beneath her feet right now — and had given herself to seal it, and the mob and the pyre and the shame were stories other people had built afterward because the truth was too large to hold.

*The family legacy is sacrifice,* whispered something in her blood, in the beast-gift, in the mountain itself. *It always was.*

The lamp guttered. Far off in the dark of the court, something vast shifted in its sleep, and the Ledger's flames all leaned toward the sound like grass toward wind.

Dawn was coming. Marchen expected an answer: the beasts and the roads, or nothing.

Ansel closed her eyes, and her mother's face — the one memory she hadn't yet paid away, the one she'd guarded like a coal cupped in both hands — finally came to her whole, and she understood, at last, what she was going to say.

It was not the answer Marchen had priced.

It was not, quite, the answer Sef had predicted either.

But first, before dawn, she had a courtier to find — and a third door to build.
