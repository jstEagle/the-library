## 22. What Mother Actually Did

Sef Andrade kept his private alcove behind a curtain of woven lantern-chains, each link holding a flame no bigger than a thumbnail. When Ansel pushed through them — they parted like warm water — she found him sitting cross-legged on the floor with the Ledger's oldest volume balanced on his knees, and a teapot.

"You have tea down here," she said.

"We have *pretend* tea." He poured a second cup without looking up. "Ash and hot water and a memory of chamomile that one of us sold to the court about sixty years ago. The court brews it back out for special occasions. Drink slowly. It tastes like someone else's summer."

She drank. It tasted like hay dust and honey and a porch she had never sat on, and her eyes stung for no reason she could name. "That's horrible."

"That's the Hollow Court in a cup," Sef said. "Now. You wanted the old pages."

The alcove was barely big enough for two people and a book the size of a tomb door. Up close, the Ledger was not paper at all but something between vellum and skin and slate, cool to the touch, and the writing did not sit on its surface so much as *swim* just beneath it, like fish under ice. The newest entries — Tomas's among them — burned bright as coals. These old ones were banked low, embers under ash, legible only when you tilted your head and let your eyes go soft.

"Entries don't die when they're paid out," Sef said, tracing a line with one grey finger. "They just stop burning so loud. Which is fortunate, because what you're looking for happened before either of us was born, and nobody keeps records down here except the Ledger itself."

"Then how do I find her?"

"Her name will know you." He said it simply, the way he said most terrible things. "Souls leave fingerprints. Sit with the page and think of her until your head aches. The page will do the rest."

Ansel set down the cup of other people's summer, put both hands flat on the cold old leaves, and thought of her mother.

It should have been easy. It was not. Twenty years of careful not-thinking had grown over the memory like moss over a stone, and every time she reached for Mara Quintana's face she found instead the smoke, the pyre, the sound the village made — which wasn't a sound at all, really, just a held breath that never quite got let out. She'd been nine. She had stood between Odile Ferran's legs, small enough that the ward-keeper's coat hem brushed her ears, and she had watched, and she had learned the first lesson of her life: *what you are gets people killed.*

"Stop that," Sef said gently.

"Stop what?"

"Bracing. You're bracing like the page is going to bite you." He turned his lantern-chain curtain down to a whisper of light. "I've watched souls read this book for three hundred years. The ones who brace get smoke. The ones who open get answers. Choose."

"I'm choosing," Ansel muttered, "you're just impatient," and closed her eyes, and unclenched, and let her mother come.

Not the pyre. She refused the pyre. She went further back, back past the burnings, to the kitchen with the crooked door, to flour on dark hands, to a voice pitched low in the shape of a lullaby — *hush now, hollow's far below, hush now, starlight's long ago* — and under her palms the Ledger warmed.

Then it moved.

The swimming letters began to rearrange themselves, drifting together like iron filings finding a magnet, and line by line a passage assembled itself out of the deep pages and rose toward the surface. Ansel felt it coming the way you feel weather in an old scar.

"*Mara Quintana,*" she read aloud, because reading aloud made it real, and she needed it desperately to be real or desperately to be a lie, and she couldn't yet tell which. "*Of Cinderhatch. Beast-speaker, third generation. Sold to the Hollow Court by her own hand—*"

"No." Her voice cracked on it. "No, that's wrong, she didn't sell herself, she was *taken*, there were men with torches, I *saw*—"

"Keep reading," Sef said quietly.

"*—by her own hand, on the night the stars first thinned, as payment in full for the sealing of the Sundered Gate. Witnessed: the Wardens of Cinderhatch. Witnessed: the Court entire. Terms: one soul, freely given, freely spent — to close what greed had opened.*"

The alcove was very quiet. Somewhere far off, the vast sleeping thing shifted again, and the lantern-flames leaned.

"There were men with torches," Ansel said. Her hands had gone numb on the cold page. "There was a pyre. There was — the whole village came, I remember the coats, I remember the smell—"

"And who lit it?" Sef asked.

And there it was, the question she had spent twenty years not asking, lying in wait at the bottom of her memory like a stone at the bottom of a well, and now she dropped the bucket all the way down and brought it up and looked at it.

Her mother had walked to the pyre. Nobody had dragged her. Ansel could see it now, could see it *finally*, the way you can only see a thing once you've been given permission: Mara Quintana climbing the pyre steps with her chin up and her daughter's face turned away into Odile Ferran's coat, and the torches — the torches weren't being carried *toward* her. They were being handed to her. And the men who'd brought them weren't executioners.

They were witnesses. They were making it official. Because that was how the old pacts worked — Sef had told her this, three nights ago, in this same alcove, over worse tea: *a soul given freely outweighs a soul taken ten to one.* A sacrifice had to be seen to be free. So the village came, and the village watched, and the village was told a story it could live with, because the truth — that the quiet Quintana woman had cut a deal with the hungry dark under the mountain, and paid it in the only coin that mattered — would have broken something in Cinderhatch that the town could not afford to break.

So they called it a burning. And a nine-year-old girl stood in the crowd and learned to hate the gift in her own blood.

"They let me believe it," Ansel whispered. "Odile let me believe it. All those years — she took me in, she taught me wards, she never once—" Her jaw tightened. "Twenty years. She watched me flinch from my own gift for twenty years and said nothing."

"Or," Sef said, with the mildness of a man who has had three centuries to learn that first conclusions are usually the cheap ones, "she was sworn. Look at the witnesses, Ansel. Wardens of Cinderhatch. Your Odile signed her silence into the pact itself. She may not have been *able* to tell you."

Ansel stared at him. Then down at the page. The witness lines swam, and beneath the formal script, smaller, almost shy, a hand-noted addendum had surfaced — the kind of marginalia a clerk adds when the truth won't fit the form:

*Note: subject's daughter present at sealing. Subject's final request: that the child be raised ignorant of the debt, and free of it. Request granted. Debt passes to no one.*

Free of it.

Ansel laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, mostly disbelief, and Sef had the grace to pretend he hadn't heard. Free of it. She had spent her whole life paying interest on a debt her mother had explicitly, formally, with her dying breath, cancelled. Every smuggler run, every flinch, every year of pretending to be merely a good tracker — she had done it all in service of a shame that had never belonged to her.

"She sealed the Sundered Gate," Ansel said. "What is — what's the Sundered Gate?"

Sef was quiet long enough that she looked up. His lantern-light caught the ash-grey planes of his face, and for once he looked less like a courtier and more like what he'd been before: a man, tired, carrying something heavy.

"It's the crack in the star-pact," he said. "The first one. Before Marchen, before the Ledger grew fat, there was a warden — a greedy one, generations back — who skimmed off the top. Took pledged souls and sold them twice, pocketed the difference. The court noticed. Courts always notice. And when it demanded restitution, the warden panicked and tried to renegotiate the pact in its favor, and the whole agreement tore along one seam." He gestured upward, vaguely, at everything. "That tear is why the court started starving. That tear is why the stars have been thinning for a hundred years instead of burning like they should. Your mother closed it — patched it with a freely-given soul, which is the only patch that holds. Bought the world another generation."

"One soul. One woman. For a whole generation of stars."

"For a whole generation of everything." Sef shrugged. "You're surprised? You crossed a drowned stair and bargained with bats to get here. Your family doesn't do anything small."

The absurd little laugh escaped her again, and this time it was mostly real. But underneath it, the new knowledge was settling into place with the weight of load-bearing stone, and she could feel the architecture of her life shifting around it.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she turned over and over as the old page dimmed and the alcove light steadied:

Marchen had offered her a deal. Tomas's soul, freely given, in exchange for the beasts and the smuggler roads — survival dressed up as mercy, the devil's own arithmetic. And Ansel had asked for a night, and the court had granted it, and all night she had turned the offer like a coin, looking for the trick in it, and the trick had seemed to be simply *everything*: that any answer cost too much, that there was no clean way out, that she must choose between her brother and the world.

But that arithmetic assumed the only currencies on the table were the ones Marchen priced.

Her mother had looked at an impossible ledger once — a torn pact, a starving dark, a world with one seam split open — and had not chosen between the options presented to her. She had written a new entry. *One soul, freely given, freely spent.* Not taken, not traded, not extorted. Given. And the giving had been worth more than the taking ever could be, because that was the deep law under all the court's ugly bookkeeping, the law Marchen had built her whole usurpation on violating:

Freely given outweighs. Ten to one.

"Sef," Ansel said slowly. "When the Queen wakes — when the pact gets rewritten — does it have to be rewritten the way Marchen wants? Stolen souls, conscript armies, cities fed to the dark?"

"The terms are set by whoever holds the pen at the root of the mountain." His eyes narrowed, ash-grey gone sharp. "Which is why Marchen is coming down with an army instead of a petition."

"And if the pen were held by someone who understood that a gift outweighs a theft? Someone whose family had already — proven the method?" She was thinking aloud now, fast, the pieces clicking together with an almost audible snap. "The court is starving. Not for souls. For *trust*. For payment that doesn't scream. What if the court could be fed honestly? Memories given, not taken. Pledges volunteered. What if the beast-speaker — the third generation, the one they tried to burn twice and failed both times — what if she stood at the root and offered terms nobody down here has heard in five hundred years?"

Sef had gone very still. In the hush, the lantern-flames straightened, all of them at once, like soldiers hearing their general's step.

"Do you know," he said at last, "in three hundred years of courtiering, I have watched humans come down that drowned stair full of exactly two ambitions: steal something, or kill something. You are the first to arrive proposing *bookkeeping reform*."

"Somebody has to. The current management is terrible."

"It's worse than terrible, it's *Marchen*," Sef said, and then, quieter: "You understand what you're describing. You understand what standing at the root means. The Queen will ask you — she'll ask you directly, it's the one question she still remembers how to ask — bind yourself or bury the cities. There is no third door, Ansel. There has never been a third door."

"There's never been a third door *that anyone asked for*." Ansel stood, joints stiff, the old page cooling under her hands. Above them, muffled through fathoms of stone, the court's outer galleries murmured with the sounds of Marchen's army making camp — boots and harness and the particular silence of thousands of stolen souls waiting to be spent. Dawn was close. Marchen expected an answer: beasts and roads, or nothing.

But Ansel Quintana had spent the night learning that her inheritance was not the pyre. It was the pact. Not the shame — the seal. Her mother had walked into fire with her chin up because the world needed patching and she was the only one holding thread; and if the world was splitting again, then the honest thing, the Quintana thing, was not to choose the least-bad price some usurper had written down.

It was to write better terms.

"Find Odile," Ansel said. "Wake her gently — she's missing memories and it makes her mean. Tell her I need everything she remembers about the rite of renewal. Every fragment she bought back with her own mind, every scrap. And Sef—" she paused at the lantern-curtain, "—when Marchen comes to hear my answer, I want the whole court listening. Can you manage that?"

Sef rose, and bowed — the first full, unironic bow he had given anyone in Ansel's presence, deep and deliberate, a courtier of the old school honoring something he recognized.

"Lady Quintana," he said, using a title that had never existed and sounded, somehow, exactly right, "the court has been listening to you since the night the wolves carried your name. Managing the acoustics will be my pleasure."

He swept the curtain aside. Beyond it, the cathedral dark waited, bone arches and drifting lanterns and the distant sea-sound of burning souls, and somewhere in it, her brother's small steady flame, and somewhere deeper than that, the vast patient hunger that had once accepted a freely given soul and kept faith with it for twenty years.

Ansel stepped out into the dark with her mother's true legacy settled on her shoulders like a mantle, heavier than armor and warmer than wool.

Dawn was coming. Veyra Marchen expected an answer.

She was going to give the court one instead.
