## 23. Refusal

Sef Andrade had died once already, in a manner of speaking, and so he had developed strong opinions about what people did with the time they were given.

Most of them wasted it. That was his conclusion after two hundred years in the Hollow Court. They hoarded their hours like misers, spent them on grudges and small comforts, and then — when the court's collectors came with their ledgers and their polite smiles — they sold the whole lot for a promise of safety that never arrived. He had watched it happen thousands of times. He had done it himself. That was the part that made him an expert rather than merely a critic.

But Ansel Quintana, standing at the edge of the Ledger Hall with ash on her boots and her brother's name held carefully behind her teeth, was not wasting her time.

"You want my answer tonight," she said to Veyra Marchen.

"I want your answer now," Marchen corrected. She stood across the great open book with the ease of a woman who had never once been told no by anything larger than a horse. "The Queen is patient, Quintana. I am not. Tomas's soul, returned to you whole. In exchange: the beasts of Thornfell bend to you and stay out of my war, and the smuggler roads carry my grain and my soldiers instead of my enemies'. No one else has to burn. Isn't that what you wanted? A bargain where only one person pays?"

*Only one person pays.* As if that made it clean. Sef drifted along the gallery above, half-hidden in the lantern-glow, and marveled again at how the living could dress a knife in kindness and call it mercy. He'd have admired it, honestly, if he hadn't spent two centuries watching the trick from the wrong end.

Ansel was quiet for a long moment. Beside her, Odile Ferran stood swaying slightly, her eyes glassy, mouthing fragments of ward-rites under her breath like a woman counting coins she wasn't sure were still in her purse. The beasts waited beyond the outer galleries — Sef could feel them the way he felt weather, a pressure of teeth and patience pressed against the court's skin. Wolves in the dark places. Bears in the deep roots. The blind bats hanging in their thousands from the drowned stair, listening with their whole bodies.

And below everything, vast and slow as a tide, the Hollow Queen waited too. Not for Marchen's answer. For something else. Sef didn't pretend to understand her anymore; perhaps he never had.

"Here's my problem," Ansel said at last, conversationally, as though they were two smugglers haggling over a toll. "You're offering me a deal where I get my brother back and everyone else stays safe *as long as nothing ever changes*. The cities keep kneeling. The conscripts keep marching down here in chains. The Queen keeps starving, quietly, forever, and you keep feeding her just enough to keep your armies walking." She shook her head. "My mother taught me a song about that kind of arithmetic. It doesn't come out even."

Marchen's smile thinned by exactly one degree. "Sentiment. How very like your mother."

"You knew my mother?"

"I knew *of* her." Something moved behind Marchen's eyes — quick, cold, gone. "A woman who burned for less than you're contemplating. Learn from her, Quintana."

"Oh, I have," Ansel said softly. "That's rather the point."

Sef saw it before Marchen did. He'd spent two hundred years reading faces in lantern-light, and he saw the moment Ansel Quintana stopped negotiating and started deciding. Her weight shifted onto her back foot. Her left hand curled, very slightly, the way it did when she was reaching through stone toward something with fur and opinions.

He should have felt afraid. Instead he felt — and this surprised him so much he nearly laughed aloud — *hungry*. Not the hollow kind. The other kind. The kind he hadn't felt since before they took his name.

"No," Ansel said.

"No?" Marchen's voice stayed pleasant. Pleasant the way a frozen lake is pleasant.

"My answer is no. To the deal, to the grain, to the roads, to all of it." She lifted her chin. "And since we're doing this in front of witnesses — Your Grace, I'm going to need you to step away from the Ledger."

For a heartbeat, the Hall was silent except for the soft burning of ten thousand souls in their lantern-cages, each flame a person, each person a debt, the whole cathedral of bone humming with borrowed light.

Then Veyra Marchen laughed, and the sound was genuinely warm, which somehow made it worse.

"And who will make me? Your wolf-friends can't fit through the galleries. Your drunkard can barely stand. And your courtier—" Marchen's gaze lifted, found Sef in the shadows without effort, because of course it did; usurpers got that way or died trying, "—your courtier has been mine since the day he signed. Haven't you, Andrade?"

Sef stepped out of the glow.

It cost him more than it used to. Two centuries of half-hollowed existence meant his edges blurred when he moved quickly, ash flaking from him like snow off a branch. But he came forward anyway, down the gallery stairs, until he stood between the Ledger and the woman who owned his signature, and he said:

"That's the thing about being sold, Your Grace. You spend a long time thinking the signature was the worst part. Then one day you realize the worst part was believing you deserved it." He smiled, and felt his own face do it properly for the first time in decades. "I've stopped believing."

Marchen looked at him the way one looks at furniture that has begun to talk. "Guards."

"They're rather far away," Sef said. "Sound travels strangely down here. Didn't you notice? All that shouting in the galleries earlier — nobody came. Almost as if someone had asked the bats to eat the echoes."

Ansel glanced at him sidelong. "*Eat* the echoes?"

"Bats are thorough," Sef said. "I'll explain later, if there is a later. Which — forgive me — is very much in question."

Because Marchen had stopped smiling.

She didn't draw steel. That was the frightening thing. She simply raised two fingers, and from the shadowed colonnades at the Hall's edge came her own people — not soldiers but *bound* ones, conscripted souls wearing flesh like ill-fitting coats, their eyes lit from within with the same pale fire as the soul-lanterns. The court's currency, weaponized. Marchen had been spending her army's souls for months, Sef realized. No wonder they followed her. You don't desert a woman who holds your receipt.

"Last chance, Quintana," Marchen said. "Tomas. Whole. Free. One word."

Ansel closed her eyes.

Sef understood later that she wasn't hesitating. She was *singing* — silently, through the bones of the mountain, through the root-threads and the old waterways, in the language her mother had burned to keep alive. He couldn't hear it. But everything else in the Hollow could.

The answering roar came up through the floor.

* * *

The first thing through the wall was a bear, because of course it was. Why go around architecture when you could be architecture?

Stone burst inward in a bloom of dust and screaming masonry, and out of the ruin came something the size of a granary barn with opinions, and behind it — flooding through the breach like night given joints — wolves. Dozens. Then more than dozens. They poured down the gallery walls upside down, flowed between the colonnades, and hit Marchen's bound guards with the specific joy of animals who have been promised something good if they work hard.

The Hall erupted into chaos, and Sef — courtier of ash, traitor of long deliberation and short execution — did the thing he had rehearsed in his head for two hundred years without ever quite believing he'd get the chance.

He grabbed Odile Ferran around the waist and threw both of them behind the Ledger's iron lectern.

"Don't take this personally," he said.

"I take everything personally," Odile slurred, "it's how I know I'm still me." Then her eyes cleared, abruptly, the way a drunk's do when the world gets interesting enough. "Is that a bear?"

"That's a bear."

"In the *court*."

"Yes."

"Hah." She gripped his sleeve. "Good bear."

Above them, the battle unfolded with terrible grace. The wolves fought the way water fights — everywhere at once, never where the blade fell, wearing their enemies down by refusal. The bound guards were stronger, faster, lit with stolen fire, but every time one of them fell, its flame guttered and rose out of the body like smoke seeking a chimney, drifting toward the soul-lanterns, home at last. Sef watched a guard die and be *freed* in the same instant and thought: *that*, that right there, is the whole argument. Marchen spends souls. The mountain returns them.

Marchen herself cut a path toward the Ledger with two blades and no expression at all. Ansel met her in the open floor, and the duel began — steel against a smuggler's hooked staff, footwork echoing off the bone vaults. But Sef saw what Marchen's guards didn't: the fight was a distraction, hers and Ansel's both. Because while they crossed blades, Odile was already climbing up the lectern with a ward-keeper's chalk stub in her fist and murder in her bloodshot eyes, drawing lines on the Ledger's iron spine.

"What are you doing?" Sef hissed.

"The rite," she said. "Or most of it. I paid for most of it." Her face flickered — grief crossing it fast, a face she'd forgotten losing. "There's a seam. Every binding has a seam. If I mark the spine and someone burns the page-roots—"

"Someone?"

"You're flammable, aren't you? Half-hollow?" She grinned at him, terrible and bright. "Consider it a promotion."

"I *hate* this plan," Sef said, and went to find a torch.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about betraying your own kind: it feels exactly like coming home, and that is the shameful part.

Sef moved through the side galleries where the junior courtiers kept their accounts, and they turned to look at him — pale faces, lantern eyes, his colleagues, his fellow sold — and some of them reached for alarms and some of them reached for blades and one of them, a girl named Merrin who'd been sold out of Rivermoor sixty years ago, simply looked at him and whispered, "Is it happening? Is it finally happening?"

"It's happening," he said. "Choose fast."

Merrin chose. She snatched the alarm-bell rope and cut it with a letter-opener. Two others followed her. Three more raised the cry anyway, and those three Sef fought — badly, gracelessly, with a candelabrum — and won, mostly because they had forgotten how to want anything, and wanting is nine-tenths of fighting. His last opponent went down clutching Sef's coat and said, with the last of his voice, "You think she'll thank you? The beast-speaker? We're all just fuel to them. Sooner or later. Fuel."

"Probably," Sef agreed, panting. "But she'll feel bad about it. That's new. That's *everything*."

He caught fire easily, being half-hollow — that was the grim joke of it, the courtiers burned like paper and always had, and the court had always known, and had never once mentioned it. He carried flame from the lantern-niche to the Ledger's page-roots, those thick black cables of script that ran down into the floor like tree roots, and where the fire touched, the writing *woke up*. Names screamed silently off the pages. Debts blazed gold. Somewhere beneath them all, the mountain felt its oldest scars begin to loosen, and groaned — a long, tectonic complaint that rattled dust from the bone vaults and made even the wolves pause mid-leap.

"ODILE!" Sef bellowed. "IT'S BURNING!"

"GOOD!" she bellowed back. "KEEP IT THAT WAY!"

Then the Queen woke.

Not gradually. Not the slow tidal attention Sef had known for two centuries, that vast ambient hunger he'd learned to live inside of. This was sudden, total, a lamp flaring in a room that had been dark so long everyone forgot it was a room. The temperature dropped. Every soul-flame in the Hall bent sideways as if in wind. And into every mind present — wolf, bear, soldier, courtier, drunkard, traitor — poured a voice like the first cold hour of winter:

***Who.***

Just that. One word, and it carried a question a thousand years wide. *Who disturbs me. Who burns my debts. Who dares.* And underneath it, worse than any threat, a naked and endless wanting, so large it made Sef's knees buckle. He had served her all this time and never understood that she wasn't cruel. Cruelty would have been simpler. She was *empty*, and emptiness this size mistakes everything for food.

Half of Marchen's bound guards dropped their weapons where they stood. Some knelt. One began to laugh, high and helpless, tears freezing on his cheeks. Whatever the Queen offered each of them — and Sef felt his own offer arrive, personal, tailored, *your name back, boy, your name, your mother's kitchen, the smell of bread* — it undid them more thoroughly than the wolves had.

Odile staggered upright, chalk-white, gripping the lectern. "Sef." Her voice was small. "Tell me that worked. Tell me the fire worked, because I'm looking at the Queen and I'm hearing her in my *head*, and I would very much like this to have been worth it."

The Ledger blazed. The debts unraveled. Ten thousand flames guttered, steadied, and began — one by one — to rise free of their cages, drifting upward through the bone vaults like sparks returning to a fire that had gone out a lifetime ago.

And Ansel Quintana, bleeding, breathing hard, staff broken in her hands, looked up at the dark above the vaults where the Queen's attention gathered like a storm finding its eye, and did the bravest and stupidest thing Sef had ever witnessed.

She bowed. And answered.

"*Me,*" she said. "It was me."

The dark leaned closer. The whole mountain held its breath — beasts, armies, ashes, and all — waiting to hear what the starving dark would say to the woman who'd just set its ledger alight.
