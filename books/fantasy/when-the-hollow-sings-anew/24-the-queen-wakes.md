## 24. The Queen Wakes

Odile Ferran had spent forty years learning the sound a ward made when it was happy.

A contented ward hummed — low, steady, like a well-fed cat. A strained one buzzed. A dying one wept. She had heard all three from the inside of Cinderhatch's border-stones, drunk and sober both, though mostly drunk if she was honest with herself, which she tried to be at least once a week now that sobriety had been forced upon her by circumstance and by a stubborn beast-speaker who kept confiscating her flask with an apologetic smile that made it worse somehow.

But she had never heard anything like what she heard when the battle reached the outer galleries of the Hollow Court.

It began as a wrongness in the stone under her boots. Not a hum, not a buzz, not a weep. A *breath*. The mountain drew one — vast, slow, dragging — like an old woman pulled out of a nap she had earned over ten thousand years and did not appreciate being denied.

"Oh," said Odile, very quietly, to no one at all. "Oh, you fools. You absolute, magnificent fools."

The battle above — Ansel's beasts pouring down through the root-galleries, Sef Andrade turning on his fellow courtiers with lantern-fire in his hands, Marchen's soldiers firing crossbow bolts into the dark at things that moved like water and hit like avalanches — all of it, the screaming and the snarling and the ringing of steel against hollow bone, was noise. Wonderful, terrible noise. Noise was exactly what you did *not* bring to a sickbed. And the Hollow Court, Odile understood suddenly and completely, was a sickbed. It had been starving for centuries, curled around its hunger like a fist around its last coin, and Ansel Quintana had just kicked open the door and started ringing bells.

The second breath came. Then a third.

Then the Queen woke up.

* * *

Odile felt it first in her teeth.

Every soul in the caverns felt it, she would learn later — human, hollow, and beast alike. It was not a sound. It was an attention. Imagine being a candle flame and having someone lean close to study you. Imagine being a single note and feeling the whole orchestra turn toward you, waiting for your cue.

The battle did not stop so much as it forgot itself. Somewhere above, a wolf's howl cut off mid-note. A crossbow clattered to the floor, dropped rather than set down. Even the lanterns seemed to dim politely, the way a household dims its lights when word comes that the matriarch is stirring.

And then the voice arrived, and it arrived everywhere at once, and Odile would spend the rest of her life failing to describe it properly.

It did not come through the ears. It came through the *wanting*. Every person in the Hollow Court had carried something into those depths — a grief, a debt, a hunger of their own — and the Queen's voice found each of those things individually, by name, in the same instant, and spoke to them.

To Odile it said:

*You want them back.*

Her hands went cold. Her knees nearly followed.

Because there it was, laid out plain as a ledger line, the thing she had not let herself think about in twenty years: her ward-circle. Six keepers, sworn on the Cinderhatch stones, sharing watch-fires and terrible stew and a joke about the mayor so old it had grown its own folklore. Five names on the memorial wall after the wards failed at Grayfen. Five funerals she had attended already drunk, because drunk was the only way she knew how to stand upright in front of a grave.

*I can give them back,* said the voice, and it was gentle, and that was the horror of it. Gentle as a mother's hand. *They are only memories now. I hold memories the way the sea holds rain. Say yes, keeper, and tomorrow morning you will wake in Cinderhatch and hear Tam arguing with the baker about the price of rye, and it will be real, it will be warm, it will be yours.*

"Tam's dead," Odile said aloud, to a cavern full of fighting people who were no longer listening to her. "Tam died because I was too far gone to hear the stones singing wrong. You're offering me the corpse of a memory dressed up as breakfast conversation."

*I am offering you mercy.*

"You're offering me a lie with good posture." Her voice cracked, and she hated that, and she kept talking anyway because that was the whole of her talent, really — she talked. "I've read the old oaths, Your Vastness. I've got maybe a quarter of my own mind left, and even that quarter knows: nothing given by the starving comes free. You'd take the payment out of whatever's left of me. And then who'd be left to fix what's broken? Who'd remember how?"

Silence. Not absence — *consideration*. Being thought about by the Hollow Queen felt, Odile decided, like being weighed by a very patient fishmonger.

*Clever,* the voice said at last, and there might have been amusement in it, or might have been appetite. Those two things sounded remarkably similar. *Keep your scraps, then, little keeper. There are others here less attached to their wholeness.*

And across the galleries, Odile watched the Queen go fishing.

* * *

It worked on the soldiers first.

Marchen's conscripts — farm boys and dockhands and second sons pressed into grey coats, marched underground on promises of glory that everyone involved knew was a lie — they had wants so simple and so starved that the Queen barely had to reach for them. Odile stood on a ledge overlooking the great gallery and watched it happen like watching frost climb a window.

A young soldier near the eastern stair stopped mid-reload. His eyes went soft and distant, and his mouth moved, and although the words were for the Queen alone, Odile caught fragments as the voice echoed through him, using his want as a bell:

*Mother. Warm kitchen. Bread.*

He sat down. Right there, in the middle of a battle, he sat down on the cold stone and smiled, and when his sergeant screamed at him to get up and fight, he looked at her with mild, shining eyes and said, "Why? We're going home," and forty other soldiers around him began nodding.

"They're defecting," breathed a smuggler beside Odile — Marta Voss, one of Ansel's road-family, crossbow still smoking. "Half the line's just — sitting down."

"Not defecting." Odile gripped the ledge until her knuckles went white. "Converting. There's a difference. Defection means you choose the other side. This means the other side chose you, and you haven't got the sense left to argue."

It spread like spilled wine through cloth. Whole squads laid down their arms. A lieutenant — a hard-faced woman Odile had seen put down a mutiny with her bare hands two days ago — walked calmly to the center of the gallery, knelt, and began reciting her childhood address. Street, town, province. Over and over. The Queen drinking it in, or perhaps giving it back; from this distance Odile couldn't tell, and suspected there was no longer any difference.

One soldier in three stayed standing. That was the arithmetic of it, and later, when the survivors told it, they would say "half," but Odile counted, because counting was the last discipline she had left and she held onto it the way a drowning man holds a plank.

One soldier in three stayed standing because their deepest want was too tangled, or too guarded, or — in a few cases Odile marked with grim professional respect — because their deepest want was *this*. To be here. To finish the fight. The Queen could offer a man his mother's kitchen, but she couldn't offer him his own self-respect back, and some of Marchen's soldiers wanted that more.

And one in three — the hollow ones, the courtiers, Sef's former colleagues drifting through the smoke on their bone-white limbs — the Queen didn't need to tempt at all. They simply turned, all together, like a field of flowers finding the sun, and bowed toward the deep places where their Queen's attention pooled, and waited to be told what came next.

Which left roughly a third of the cavern still fighting, scattered across the galleries: Ansel's beasts, roaring confusion at soldiers who had stopped being enemies and started being *sad*; Sef Andrade holding a chokepoint at the western arch with fire in one hand and, Odile noticed, his own name clenched in the other like a talisman; and somewhere in the middle of it all, small and stubborn and glowing like a struck flint, Ansel Quintana, still moving, still shouting to her wolves, still — Odile would swear to this on any stack of holy books you liked — still *humming*.

That was the thing about the beast-speaker that Odile had never managed to explain to anyone who hadn't seen it. Everyone expected the gift to be loud. Commands, domination, the roar of the wild bent to a human will. But Ansel's gift ran quiet. She didn't command the beasts; she conversed. And right now, with the Queen's voice pressing down on every mind in the mountain like deep water, Ansel was standing in the middle of the great gallery singing — actually singing, some old Thornfell herding-song, tuneless and off-key — and her beasts were singing back, and the two sounds braided together into something the Queen's voice couldn't easily grip, because it wasn't a want. It was a *we*.

The Queen noticed. Odile felt the whole vast attention shift, like a searchlight swinging.

*Little speaker,* the voice said, and now it was not gentle at all. *You do not want. How tiresome you are.*

* * *

Odile found Ansel by following the wolves, which was either very brave or very stupid, and she had long since stopped pretending there was a difference.

The girl was backed against a pillar, blood on her temple, a wolf the size of a pony bristling at her shoulder, and her face was pale as tallow — but her feet were planted, and her jaw was set, and when Odile skidded to a halt beside her, Ansel said, without looking away from the dark, "Tell me you have a plan."

"I have half a plan and a quarter of my memory."

"So a normal Tuesday."

"The Queen's awake, girl. Fully. Do you understand what that means? Everything in the old texts says the pact-starved court sleeps light. Half-waking. Dreaming its hungers instead of acting on them. But your beasts woke it proper, and now it's—" Odile gestured helplessly at the kneeling soldiers, the bowing courtiers, the lieutenant still reciting her childhood street name in a voice like a music box winding down. "Now it's *shopping*."

Ansel flinched. Just slightly. Just enough that Odile knew the voice had found her too, had gone looking in her for a want and found — what? A brother. Obviously a brother. A soul-flame burning in a ledger somewhere below them, wearing a face Ansel hadn't seen in twenty years.

"It offered me Tomas," Ansel said quietly.

"There it is."

"It offered me Tomas *alive*. Warm. Remembering me. And it would be real, wouldn't it? For a while. It holds enough souls to build him a whole life."

"Aye. And it'd collect on the loan the way the sea collects on shipwrecks. Slow, then all at once." Odile took the girl's arm — steadying herself as much as Ansel, if she was honest. "What did you tell it?"

"That it doesn't get to sell me my brother twice." Ansel's voice didn't shake, which told Odile exactly how much it cost her. "It went quiet after that. Thinking-quiet."

"Thinking-quiet from a thing that size is worse than shouting." Odile peered out across the gallery. The battle had become something stranger than a battle: pockets of resistance moving through a landscape of the surrendered. Wolves threading carefully between kneeling soldiers, hackles up, confused by prey that wouldn't run. Sef still holding his arch, alone now, most of his fellow defected courtiers having simply drifted past him toward the deep, ignoring him the way you ignore furniture. And beneath it all, under everything, the mountain breathing that slow, satisfied breath.

"We can't fight it," Odile said. "Not with teeth. You don't beat a famine with swords, girl. You beat it with *food*." She heard herself say it and stopped, and stared at nothing, and the half-plan and the quarter-memory collided in her skull and struck a spark.

"What?" said Ansel. "Your face just did something."

"The Ledger." Odile grabbed her sleeve. "The Ledger of Souls. Every debt, every sale, every stolen memory written down and bound. That book isn't the court's *record*, Ansel, it's the court's *stomach lining*. It's how the Queen digests what she takes. It's the mechanism. And mechanisms—" her voice climbed, giddy and terrified in equal measure, the way it used to get when she was three days sober and finally saw the shape of a failing ward, "—mechanisms can *break*."

"You want to burn it."

"I want to burn it *correctly*, which is harder and more important. There's a rite. Ward-keeper's rite, third circle, severance of false bonds — I learned it at nineteen and forgot it at thirty-five along with my mother's maiden name and how to whistle. But the Queen's been picking through my head, and picking cuts both ways, and while she's rummaging in there for my wants, I can rummage right back for my oaths." Odile grinned, and knew it was probably a frightening grin, and did not care. "She's showing me my dead friends right now, the sentimental fool. Tam's kitchen argument. All of it. And tucked behind the grief like a coin behind a loose brick — there. There's the rite. Oh, I *hate* that she's helping. I'm going to hate it forever."

"And if it works?"

"If it works, every soul-debt burns at once. Every stolen memory goes home. Every sold name comes free." Odile's grin faded into something more careful. "And if it works *wrong*, we unbind everything the pact holds together, and this mountain comes down on two cities, and we'll wish the Queen had eaten us. So. No pressure."

Ansel looked at her for a long moment. Around them the Queen's voice rolled on, offering a bat its sightless sky, offering a wolf the pack it had lost, offering Sef Andrade — Odile saw him stagger at the western arch, saw the fire gutter — offering him his humanity back, his name, his before.

"She'll offer you anything to stop us," Ansel said.

"Course she will. I intend to say yes to everything and do none of it. Lying to gods is a ward-keeper's traditional final examination." Odile squared her shoulders, closed her eyes, and opened herself — deliberately, horribly, gratefully — to the vast and patient attention below. *Come on, then, Your Vastness,* she thought, as loudly as a woman with a quarter of her mind could think. *You want to know what I want? I want you to look over here.*

The Queen looked.

And in the sudden, blinding warmth of that attention — wrapped in resurrected kitchens and resurrected friends, drowned in mercy with excellent posture — Odile Ferran reached past all of it, into the rubble of her own mind, and closed her fingers around the third-circle rite like a thief in her own house.

Behind her, Ansel began to sing louder, and the wolves with her, and the whole wounded mountain rang with two songs at once: the Queen's sweet arithmetic of wanting, and a tuneless herding-ballad that refused, on principle, to want anything but *forward*.

The Queen noticed the theft almost immediately. Odile felt the warmth curdle, felt the attention sharpen from fishmonger to butcher.

*LITTLE KEEPER,* said the voice, no longer gentle at all, shaking dust from the ceiling of the world, *GIVE THAT BACK.*

"No," said Odile Ferran, and ran for the Ledger.
