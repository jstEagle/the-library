## 25. Ledger Fire

Odile Ferran had been drunk for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned exactly one useful thing: that forgetting was a skill, and like any skill it could be unlearned.

She was remembering now. That was the problem.

It started small — the way bad things always did. A fragment of the ward-keeper's oath surfacing while she watched Ansel sing to her brother's soul-flame: *I am the bell that does not break.* Then another, later, when Sef pressed his ash-grey hand against a gallery wall and whispered the court's architecture aloud: *the seal is the singer; the singer is the seal.*

And then, three hours ago, huddled behind a pillar of ribbed bone while Marchen's soldiers marched past on their way down to the root, Odile had remembered the whole of it — every word of an oath she'd spoken at nineteen with wine still on her breath and her father's badge pinned crooked over her heart.

*The wards are debts made visible,* she had recited. *Every light above is a promise below. And promises, keeper, can be forgiven.*

Forgiven. Not renewed. Not renegotiated. Forgiven.

She sat in the shadow of the Ledger's alcove with her knees drawn up and her hands shaking, and she thought: *Oh, you absolute fool. You've known for two days. You've known since the Queen woke and half an army knelt to her voice, and you've been sitting here trying to drink your way back into not-knowing because knowing means doing something about it.*

Her flask was empty. Of course it was. It had been empty since Cinderhatch, technically — she'd been drinking memories of drinking out of it, which was either very sad or very efficient, depending on how honest she was feeling, and she had never once felt honest in her entire life except possibly right now.

"Odile."

Ansel's voice, low, from the dark of the gallery mouth. The girl moved like smoke these days — more of her living in the court than in her own body, hollowed out one memory at a time. She crouched beside Odile, and up close you could see what the tolls had taken. There were gaps behind her eyes where whole seasons used to be. But her spine was straight as a ward-pole, and her jaw had that set to it that meant someone was about to have a very bad evening, and it would probably be Veyra Marchen.

"The beasts are in position," Ansel said. "Wolves along the western galleries. The bats are mapping the soldier movements — there are four hundred of them between us and the root, give or take the ones who defected to the Queen." She almost smiled. "Give or take a lot, actually. Half of Marchen's people took the Queen's offer the moment she spoke. Turns out when you offer soldiers their deepest want, most of them just want to go home."

"And Marchen?"

"Still descending. Still carrying her little book of conscription contracts like it's scripture." Ansel's voice hardened. "Sef says we have until dawn-cycle before she reaches the root. After that—"

"After that she feeds two cities' worth of souls to a starving god and calls it peace. Yes. I heard the plan. Beasts attack the outer galleries, chaos, confusion, heroic last stands." Odile waved a hand. "Very stirring. I've read better."

Ansel blinked at her. "You've been reading?"

"I've been *remembering*, which is worse." Odile pulled herself upright by a rib of the pillar — everything down here was ribs, the whole court was built like something enormous had lain down and died politely — and stood, swaying only slightly, which for her counted as grace. "Ansel. When I told you I knew a way under the mountain. When I've been muttering about ward-restoring rites for the past week like a madwoman with a head cold. Did you ever wonder why I never actually performed one?"

"You said the rite needed the original anchors."

"It did. And the original anchors are ash, because the Ledger holds them." Odile tapped her own temple. "But here's the thing about ward-keepers, girl. We're taught the oaths backward. First we learn the words, then we learn what they mean, and by the time we understand what we promised, we're too old and too tired to care. I swore an oath at nineteen. *Promises can be forgiven.* Do you know what forgiveness looks like, in the old tongue? In the binding-tongue the courts and the wards both speak?"

Ansel went very still. She was good at that. Odile suspected it came from years of standing next to mountain lions pretending to be furniture.

"No," Ansel said carefully. "Tell me."

"*Solvare.* To unbind. To release. The same word they use for forgiving a debt and for breaking a seal." Odile's laugh came out cracked. "The wards above aren't separate from this place, Ansel. They never were. They're the *interest payments*. Every generation, the free cities paid a little soul-debt to keep the Hollow Court fed enough to hold up the sky, and the ward-keeps collected it, and nobody — not one keeper in six hundred years — asked what would happen if the debt were simply *forgiven*. All of it. At once."

Understanding moved across Ansel's face like weather. "The Ledger."

"The Ledger." Odile turned to look at it.

Even after days down here, it stopped her breath. It filled the alcove like a fallen angel fills a cathedral — a book the size of a granary, bound in hide no beast would claim, its pages turning themselves in a slow wind that came from nowhere. Each page burned with names. Thousands of souls, written in flame that never consumed, each entry a little lantern in the dark. Somewhere in those pages Tomas Quintana glowed, freshly named again, singing lullabies with his sister in the galleries. Somewhere in those pages Odile Ferran's own father had signed away a decade of his life to patch a failing ward, and had come home grey-eyed and gentle and unable to remember why he cried at harvest festivals.

"Burn it," she said, "and every debt dies. Every sold soul walks free. The wards don't need restoring, Ansel — they need *releasing*. The whole rotten arrangement, forgiven in one act. Six hundred years of quiet theft, ended with a spark."

Ansel stared at her. "Then why are you telling me instead of doing it?"

"Because," Odile said, and here her voice finally failed her, so she said the rest plainly, the way you say things when you've run out of clever, "because I'm the one who let the wards fall. I drank through the humming. I told myself the old woman in Cinderhatch was mad and the bell-sound was nothing and it wasn't my problem anymore, because I had resigned, hadn't I, formally, with paperwork. Eleven years of hiding in a bottle while my oaths starved." She held up her hands. They were steady. That was the strange thing. For the first time in eleven years, her hands were perfectly steady. "This isn't a job for heroes, girl. It's a job for a disgraced keeper paying off a disgrace. Let me do this one thing sober."

*

They argued about it for an hour, which under the circumstances Odile considered restrained.

Sef arrived midway through — he had a way of arriving that suggested he'd been there longer than anyone realized, which Odile supposed was a professional habit among courtiers — and he argued hardest of all.

"You don't know what fire does in this place," he said. He stood with his back to the Ledger, arms crossed, and in the lantern-light his half-hollow face looked carved from candle-drippings. "I've served this court ninety years, keeper. The Ledger isn't a book. It's a *keystone*. Every gallery, every bone-pillar, every lantern hangs from those debts like fruit from a branch. Burn it and yes, the souls go free — but the architecture goes with them."

"Then the architecture can fall," Odile said.

"The *mountain* goes with them!" Sef's voice cracked, and beneath the crack was something older and more frightened. "Two cities sit on roots held by this pact. I have spent ninety years watching this place eat people, and I hate it, I hate it down to whatever's left of my name — but I also know that when the keystone breaks, everything the arch holds comes down. You'd trade stolen souls for buried cities. That's not forgiveness. That's arithmetic with bodies."

"That," said a new voice, "is exactly what Veyra Marchen would say."

Tomas stepped out of the gallery shadows with Ansel's hand in his, and even now, freed barely a day, he moved like a man still learning that his limbs belonged to him. His soul-flame flickered under his skin where his chest should be, warm gold instead of flesh, and when he smiled at Odile it was his mother's smile, and it hurt to look at, and she looked anyway.

"I was in the Ledger forty years," Tomas said. "Do you know what I remember of it? Not pain. *Hunger* — but not mine. The court's hunger, pouring through every page, and underneath it, older than that—" he paused, searching, "—a promise. Something the first signers wrote into the foundation. I couldn't read it then. But since Ansel sang me back, I can hear it. The pact was never meant to be permanent. That's what none of you understood — not the keepers, not the courts, not Marchen. The star-pact was a *loan*. And loans end."

"Or default," Sef said quietly.

"Or get forgiven," said Odile.

They stood there in the bone-lit dark, four people and the biggest decision in six hundred years, and Odile thought, absurdly, of the morning she'd been sworn in. Her father adjusting her badge. The old High Keeper saying, *welcome to the long arithmetic, child,* and everyone laughing, because it was a joke, because it was always a joke right up until it wasn't.

"Well," she said. "Someone has to strike the spark. And per tradition, it should be a keeper. Preferably a disgraced one." She raised her hand before anyone could object. "I've already heard all the arguments. I wrote half of them myself, in the margin of my oath-book, at nineteen, in ink my father pretended not to see. *What if we just stopped?* That's what I wrote. What if we just stopped. And he confiscated the book and told me idealism was a young woman's disease." Her grin felt feral on her face. "He was wrong. It's chronic."

*

The problem, it turned out, was getting close enough.

The Ledger's alcove sat at the heart of the Court of Accounts, and the Court of Accounts was crawling. Marchen's remaining loyalists held the approaches — maybe two hundred soldiers, bayonets burning with hollow-light — and around them drifted the courtiers, ash-faced and patient, and above everything coiled the attention of the Queen, vast and slow, like weather deciding whether to happen.

"We can't fight two hundred," Ansel said. They crouched in the gallery called the Throat, a hundred yards from the alcove, close enough to feel the Ledger's heat on their faces. "We don't need to fight two hundred. We need thirty seconds and nobody looking."

"Nobody looking," Odile repeated. "In the middle of a war. In a cathedral full of creatures whose entire culture is looking."

"So we make them look somewhere else." Ansel's eyes had gone distant — the look Odile had learned meant she was listening to things with more legs than sense. "The wolves can hit the eastern galleries. Loud. Ugly. Sef's people — the courtiers who'll turn — start a fire in the record-halls, real fire, nothing precious, just smoke. Every eye goes east. And we walk up the center like we own the place."

"And if the Queen notices us?"

Ansel was quiet a moment. "Then I'll ask her for thirty seconds," she said. "Politely. I'm told I'm good with large dangerous creatures."

"You would flirt with an apocalypse," Odile said, with genuine admiration.

"I wouldn't call it flirting."

"You absolutely would. I've watched you negotiate with a bear."

*

It went wrong immediately, and then it went right, and Odile would spend the rest of her life unsure whether those were different things.

The wolves hit the east galleries like a landslide with opinions. The smoke went up in the record-halls. The courtiers turned — more of them than Sef had dared hope, dozens of ash-faces suddenly remembering their names and screaming them as battle-cries. Chaos, beautiful and total.

And then a sergeant of Marchen's — a big woman with a scarred jaw and hollow-light in her eyes — saw three figures walking calmly up the central nave toward the most important object in the underworld, and did the math faster than Odile liked.

"HOLD THE BOOK!" the sergeant bellowed, and fifty soldiers turned.

What happened next, Odile processed in pieces. Ansel stepping forward and making a sound that wasn't a word — a sound like stone remembering it was once a mountain, like every wolf and bat and bear in Thornfell answering at once through one girl's throat. The soldiers faltering, some dropping weapons, some kneeling. The sergeant charging anyway, brave and doomed. Sef moving — quick, ash-grey, ninety years of courtier grace — putting himself between blade and keeper without hesitation, taking the blow across the shoulder that would otherwise have reached Odile's spine.

And Odie Ferran, sixty-one years old, hungover on memories, walking the last twenty yards alone.

The Ledger rose before her, vast and burning. Up close she could hear it — every page a voice, every name a note, thousands of stolen lives humming their captivity. Her father's handwriting was in there. Her mother's debts. Half the free cities' grandmothers. Six centuries of quiet, polite theft, dressed up as necessity.

*I am the bell that does not break,* she thought. *Well. Perhaps it's time somebody rang me.*

She had no torch. She didn't need one. Ward-keepers carried fire differently — not in flasks and lamps but in oaths, and hers had been starving eleven years, and starving things burn hottest when you finally feed them.

She laid both palms flat against the first page.

"Solvare," she said.

Nothing happened, for one horrible heartbeat.

Then she remembered the rest. Not the formal oath — the private part, the part she'd scrawled in margins at nineteen, the question her father confiscated. *What if we just stopped?* She fed it everything: the wards falling like glass, the stars going out, the wolf dying on her doorstep with a family crest in its teeth, Ansel's face the first time she paid a memory and forgot her mother's laugh, Sef bleeding on the stones behind her, Tomas's voice singing in the dark. Eleven years of shame and one night of clarity, poured into two words in a dead language:

"*Solvare. Promissa dimittimus.* Unbound. We release you. All of you. Go home."

The page caught like dawn.

Not red fire — nothing so small. Light spread outward from her hands the way morning spreads over a valley, page to page, name to name, and every soul-flame it touched flared bright and *lifted*, peeling off the vellum like sparks off a bonfire, rising through the bone-vaulted dark in rivers of gold. She could hear them going — not screams, not wails, but the sound a crowd makes stepping out of a cold church into sunlight, that collective exhale, that astonished laughter.

"Oh," Odile said, laughing herself now, tears cutting through the grime on her face, "oh, you *beautiful* old fraud of a book. Run. All of you. *Run.*"

The fire reached the bindings. The bindings, which had held for six hundred years, sighed — actually sighed, a sound like a great door opening after a long illness — and came apart. And the whole Hollow Court lit up like a struck bell: every gallery, every lantern, every corridor blazing with released light, dawn in a place that had never once had a morning.

Behind her, Sef was laughing and crying at once, pressing his grey hand to his chest where a heart used to hesitate. "Ninety years," he kept saying. "Ninety years and it ends with a *librarian*."

Ahead of her, Ansel stood in the flood of golden souls with her arms open and her brother's hand in hers, and for one moment — one perfect, unrepeatable moment — everything was forgiven, everything was free, everything was light.

Which was, Odile realized approximately four seconds later, exactly the moment the mountain noticed its keystone was gone.

The first tremor rolled up from the root like a cough from something enormous turning over in its sleep. Dust sifted from the bone vaults. Far away, faint but unmistakable, came the groan of stone under strain — and threaded through it, thin and high, the sound of the Hollow Queen waking fully for the second time in six hundred years.

Above them — miles above, through rock and root and the sleeping bones of two cities — Odile felt the wards flicker, gutter, and begin, slowly, terribly, to *fall*.

"Well," she said, into the ringing light and the rising roar. "Anyone else remember the bit in the oath about what happens after forgiveness?"

No one did.

That, she thought grimly, grabbing Ansel's arm as the second tremor hit, was going to be a problem.
