## 26. The Cost of Burning

The fire in the Ledger did not behave like fire.

Ansel had expected paper to burn the way paper always burned — fast and greedy, curling at the edges, gone. But Odile's ward-flame moved through the great book like dawn moves through a valley: slowly, inevitably, from page to page, from name to name, and everywhere it touched, something *rose*.

Souls came up out of the vellum like steam off a summer road.

They were not ghosts, exactly. They were more like the heat-shimmer above a forge, if the shimmer could weep. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, spiraling upward through the cathedral of bone, and each one made a sound as it lifted free — a small, unmistakable sound that Ansel felt in her teeth more than her ears.

A sigh. A long, held breath, released after years or decades or a lifetime of being held.

"Oh," said Odile beside her, her voice cracking clean down the middle. "Oh, it's *working*."

Ansel couldn't answer. She was watching a flame detach itself from a page near where Tomas's name had been written — watching it waver in the lantern-light, watching it turn, slow as a leaf falling, toward the sound of her breathing.

*Tomas.*

She reached out. The soul-flame drifted into her palm and settled there, warm as a bird, weightless as morning. It didn't speak. Souls freshly freed rarely could; Sef had warned them of that. But it pressed against her hand the way a cat presses against a familiar knee, and Ansel pressed back, and for three heartbeats — three whole heartbeats in a burning cathedral beneath a mountain that had stolen twenty years from her — everything was right with the world.

Then the first crack came.

It wasn't loud. That was the terrible thing about it. It was a *small* sound, deep below them, like a knuckle popping in a quiet room. Ansel felt it through the soles of her boots before she heard it at all.

The soul-flame in her palm flickered.

"Tomas?" she whispered.

The crack came again. Closer. Or rather, *deeper*, which was somehow worse, because Ansel knew this mountain the way other people knew their mothers' voices, and she knew that the sounds beneath her feet were supposed to be water sounds, and stone-settling sounds, and the patient creak of roots drinking. This was none of those. This was the sound of something *letting go*.

"Odile." Her voice came out flat and strange. "What did you do?"

"What I *had* to do," Odile snapped, but she'd gone pale around the mouth, and her hands were already moving through the diagnostic gestures every ward-keeper learned before they learned their own names — thumb to sternum, two fingers to the temple, palm flat against the air as though pressing it for a fever. Her eyes went wide. "Ansel."

"Tell me."

"The wards aren't empty." Odile's voice dropped to a whisper, as if the stone might be embarrassed to hear it. "I thought they were empty. I thought the court had been drinking them dry for centuries, and when I saw the Ledger — every pledge, every soul-debt, all of it feeding down into the root — I thought burning it would sever the theft. Cut the leech off the body." She swallowed. "It isn't a leech, Ansel. It never was. It's an *anchor*. The pact doesn't just bind the souls to the court. It binds the court to the mountain. The court holds up the mountain the way—"

"—the way a keel holds up a ship," Ansel finished. She'd heard smugglers use the phrase a hundred times about ballast, about balance, about the things you couldn't cut loose no matter how much they weighed you down.

Above them, somewhere impossibly far overhead, Cinderhatch heard its first tremor.

They knew because the bats told her. Ansel's connection to the beasts of Thornfell had been growing stranger and stronger since the descent — since she'd stopped hiding it, since she'd let the gift spread through her like warmth through cold hands — and now the blind cave-bats' song poured into her mind in a cascade of terror. Their echolocation painted the dark for her in pulses of sound, and what it showed her was the rock itself beginning to *sing wrong*: fractures propagating through the deep galleries like cracks racing across ice, hairline at first, then not.

"The cities," Ansel said. "Cinderhatch is built on the mountain's shoulder. Rivermoor sits on the root-ridge. If the pact anchored the stone—"

"Then unbinding the souls unbinds the stone." Odile had begun to laugh, a thin, horrible, exhausted laugh. "I burned the Ledger. I freed every soul in the Hollow Court. And I may have just signed death warrants for everyone who ever complained about the price of bread in Cinderhatch." She dragged both hands down her face. "My grandmother trained forty years to keep those wards humming. I got drunk and burned the family business to the ground."

Ansel wanted to comfort her. There was no time, and also — she found, searching herself honestly — there wasn't much comfort *in* her. Because the soul still resting in her palm was trembling now, and around them the freed dead were rising faster, streaming up out of the dying pages in rivers of light, and they weren't sighing anymore. They were confused. Frightened. A few of the oldest flames — souls sold so long ago they remembered nothing else — seemed to be trying to climb back *into* the burning book.

"You're free," Ansel told them helplessly. "You're free. You can go."

Go where? The dark asked her back. Go *where*, little speaker?

* * *

The main gallery was chaos by the time they reached it, and the beautiful kind of chaos first, before it turned ugly.

Sef Andrade stood on the steps of the bone-dais directing traffic with the crisp efficiency of a man who had spent decades managing courtiers and had discovered that managing panicking refugees used exactly the same skills. "Single file! Single file, please, yes, you are dead, congratulations, it happens to everyone, you may still form an orderly line!" He caught sight of Ansel and his ash-grey face split with something between relief and accusation. "Your friend," he said, pointing at Odile, "is either the greatest hero in the history of the Hollow Court or its final murderer, and I genuinely cannot tell which, and neither, I think, can the architecture."

As if to answer him, the ceiling shed a fine rain of mineral dust.

The freed souls filled the gallery like snow filling a bowl — drifting, swirling, thousands upon thousands of them, some weeping with joy, some clutching at one another, some simply hovering and staring at their own light with the wonder of prisoners seeing sky. Ansel searched the streams for Tomas's flame and found it near her shoulder, keeping pace with her like a dog that had decided whose household it belonged to.

"Where do they go?" she asked Sef. "When they're free. Where *can* they go?"

"Nowhere, yet. That's the cruelty of it." Sef's voice gentled. "A soul needs a door. Flesh, or rest, or renewal into the pact — there must be somewhere for it to *be*. The Ledger provided the 'where' for ten thousand years. Burn the Ledger and you don't just free them. You unhoused them." He looked up at the groaning dark. "And the mountain noticed. Oh, how the mountain has noticed."

Another crack — louder this time, a real crack, the sound of a pillar-sized thing surrendering — and the whole gallery swayed like a boat in weather. A section of bone-lattice far overhead broke loose and fell, and would have crushed a knot of newly freed souls if a shape hadn't lunged into its path: one of the great blind bats, wings wide as sails, catching the debris and hauling it aside with a shriek that Ansel felt translate directly into her bones as *MINE TO GUARD, DOWNLING*.

The beasts were holding the roof. She realized it gradually, then all at once — wolves braced against leaning columns, bears shoulder-deep in rubble clearing paths, bats wheeling through the dark singing warnings of every fresh fracture seconds before it spread. The Hollow Court's hunger had threatened them too; that was why they'd come. But this — this crumbling, this collapse — this they understood in their marrow. Den animals knew what it meant when the den shook.

"They're buying time," Ansel breathed. "The beasts are holding it together."

"For now." Sef's jaw tightened. "But stone is patient, and beasts are not masons. Listen."

She listened. Past the groaning, past the weeping dead, past the wolves' concerted effort, she heard it: the deep, rhythmic, enormous sound beneath everything. Not cracking anymore.

Breathing.

Something vast, far below, drawing slow tidal breaths in the dark at the mountain's root. And with every exhale, the tremors eased slightly. With every inhale, they worsened.

"The Queen," Odile whispered. She'd gone grey as Sef. "The Queen is awake, and she's *holding the mountain up*. With her own—" She stopped, recalculated, started again. "The pact ran through her. Through the throne. When the Ledger burned, every other anchor point went with it. What's left is *her*, and her strength, and however long she chooses to keep spending it."

"Why would she?" Ansel asked. "She's starving. She's been starving for centuries. Why spend what little she has left holding up the roof for the people who fed her stolen souls?"

Sef and Odile exchanged a look — the particular look of two people who have each glimpsed half of an awful truth and are waiting for the other to say it aloud.

"Because she's the only one who remembers what the pact was *for*," Sef said quietly. "Not the thefts. Not Marchen's arithmetic. The original covenant, the one signed at the root before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. The court was made to hold, Ansel. Holding is the deepest habit it has. Even starving. Even betrayed." His ash-grey hands folded together. "Even now, I think, part of her hopes someone will come and make holding possible again. Properly. Freely."

"And until then?"

"Until then she breathes," Sef said, "and we count."

* * *

They counted in dust and cracks.

Word came up from the smuggler runners within the hour — living couriers, gasping, soot-streaked, men and women Ansel had known half her life who had followed her down on promises she was no longer sure she could keep. The news they carried arrived in pieces, each worse than the last:

Cinderhatch's bell tower had shed its capstone. The old wards along the mountain road had guttered out entirely — not drunk dry this time, simply *unanchored*, fading like footprints in wind. The thermal springs that heated the terraced farms had gone cold. And in Rivermoor, two hundred leagues of root-ridge away, the great aqueduct had cracked down its length, and people were standing in the streets looking up at a mountain that was visibly, undeniably, *shifting*.

"We won," said Maret, one of Ansel's oldest runners, standing in the flickering gallery with ash in her hair and her voice shaking. "Didn't we? You told us we'd win. The Ledger's ash, the debts are dead, the souls are free. So why does my mother's house have a crack in it wide enough to put a fist through?"

Ansel had no answer that fit in a sentence. She tried anyway, because leaders who couldn't speak plainly might as well be usurpers. "We broke the thing that was hurting everyone," she said. "It turns out the thing that was hurting everyone was also the thing holding everything up. The people who built it that way — the ones who signed the first pact — they made sure there'd be no clean way out. No door without a price."

"So pay it," Maret said flatly. "You've been paying prices since Cinderhatch. Everyone says so. Your memories, your mother's face—" Ansel flinched, and Maret had the grace to look sorry. "I'm not saying it's nothing. I'm saying you've never once stopped at the toll. Don't stop now."

"It's not a toll this time, Mare." Ansel looked up, at the dark, at the breathing beneath the dark. "Tolls end. This one might not."

The runner stared at her. Around them, the freed souls drifted like luminous snowfall, and the wolves sang to the stone, and somewhere below a starved queen exhaled and bought them another hour.

* * *

Later — she would never remember how much later, time going strange under pressure the way it always did — Ansel climbed alone to the edge of the broken dais where the Ledger had been, and sat with her legs dangling over the drop, and let herself feel it.

Victory curdled. That was the only word for it. Milk left in the sun, sweet turned sharp. Every soul they'd freed was a debt paid; every debt paid was an anchor lost; every anchor lost was a home shaken above. Somewhere up there in the ordinary evening world, a baker in Cinderhatch was pulling loaves while her shopfront cracked. A child in Rivermoor was learning to whistle while the aqueduct split. None of them had voted on the Ledger. None of them had known it existed. And she had burned it, gladly, gloriously, and the gladness was already turning to ash in her mouth to match the ash on the floor.

Tomas's flame settled onto her knee.

"I know," she told it softly. "I know you didn't ask for any of this either. Sold at nine years old. Held for twenty years. Freed into a falling world." She laughed, wet and unsteady. "The Quintanas really do leave a trail."

The flame pulsed — and this time it wasn't just warmth. Something pushed through, faint as a memory of a memory, and for half a heartbeat Ansel heard it: not words, not yet, but the ghost of a rhythm. Four notes, rising. The opening phrase of the lullaby she'd sung him in the dark, days ago, when he'd been a flame that knew her voice but not her name.

He was learning to speak again. Freed souls regained themselves slowly, Sef had said — name first, then voice, then memory, like ice thawing from the edges in. He was thawing. He was *here*. And he was trying to sing to *her*, to comfort *her*, which was so absurdly, unbearably like the brother she remembered that she had to press her sleeve against her eyes for a moment before she could breathe properly.

"All right," she said finally. "All right, Tom. Here's what we know."

She counted on her fingers, the way their mother used to when laying out impossible choices at the kitchen table.

"One: the Queen is holding the mountain up, and she cannot do it forever, and when she stops, two cities die. Two: the pact can be remade — Sef says so, the old pages say so, the fact that she's still *breathing* instead of letting go says so. Three:" — and here her finger hung in the air, and the truth of it settled over her with the heavy inevitability of snow sliding off a roof — "three: a new pact needs a voice. Someone who speaks beast and hollow and human alike. Someone the court will accept. Someone willing to be bound."

The flame on her knee went very still.

"Yeah," Ansel agreed quietly. "I see it too."

Below her, in the deep, the Queen drew another vast, straining breath. Above her, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, she felt the mountainside shudder — felt, with the gift she'd spent her whole life hiding, the fear of ten thousand people in two cities who did not know why the ground had grown restless, only that it had.

And beneath everything, threading through stone and root and bone and flame, the Hollow began — very softly, very patiently — to sing.

It was not a threat. That was the worst part. It sounded almost like an invitation.

Ansel stood, wiped her face, and went to find Odile and Sef, because whatever came next, it would need all of them — and because the song rising through the mountain had just spoken her name.
