## 27. Sef's Choice

The mountain groaned like an old man asked to rise from a chair he'd been sitting in for three thousand years.

Sef Andrade had heard the court make many sounds in his long half-life beneath the Thornfell — the wet whisper of souls burning in their lanterns, the Queen's patience settling over everything like snowfall, the courtiers' silk feet on bone floors. But he had never heard it *hurt* before. Now every gallery rang with it, a low grinding note that lived in the teeth rather than the ears.

"Right," he said to the crowd of trembling civilians huddled beneath the Arch of Debts. "Listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once, and I have a very distracting voice."

They looked up at him. Perhaps forty of them — kitchen-souls and lamplighters, freed ledger-clerks still clutching the ash of their own entries, a grandmother who had been sold to the court sixty years ago and had spent the last hour of freedom asking everyone if they were eating enough.

"I was hollow for two hundred years," Sef said. "Which means I know every corridor in this place better than I know my own face. Which is fortunate, because I no longer entirely remember my own face." A few of them laughed — the thin, startled laughter of people discovering they could still do that. Good. He'd learned long ago that fear and laughter used the same door; whichever got there first owned the room. "There are three ways out of the Hollow Court. The Drowned Stair is flooded waist-deep and full of eels with opinions. The Chimney is narrow, vertical, and requires you to not panic, which I realize is asking a great deal tonight. And the Processional — which is wide, dry, and currently collapsing."

"Why are we trusting you?" someone called from the back. A man with a merchant's squint, the kind who haggled even with salvation.

"You shouldn't trust anyone tonight," Sef agreed pleasantly. "But I am the only one offering directions instead of a knife, so let's begin there."

* * *

He divided them as he spoke, moving through the crowd with his hands, sorting by age and steadiness the way he'd once sorted souls by weight. That was the ugly joke of his existence: two centuries of handling other people's essences had made him very good at exactly one thing, and now that thing was herding frightened humans through a dying cathedral of bone.

"The elderly and the small take the Processional first," he said. "It holds — mostly. It held during the Ledger fire, didn't it?" He paused, listening to the groan shift pitch. "It held during the Ledger fire. We're going to pretend that's reassuring."

The grandmother raised her hand like a schoolgirl. "Young man—"

"I'm four hundred years old."

"Young man, where are the others? The ones fighting. The wolf-girl and her friends."

"Wolf-girl," Sef repeated, savoring it. Ansel Quintana would have hated that and he intended to tell her so if either of them survived. "They're deeper in. Buying us time." He did not say *paying for our lives*, because the word *paying* had gone out of fashion down here tonight, thank all the dark little gods. "And we will honor that by being quick, obedient, and alive. In that order."

He put the grandmother at the head of the Processional column on the grounds that she was the only person present unafraid enough to set a pace. She took the assignment seriously. Within minutes she had organized the group behind her into something between a funeral procession and a market queue, and was scolding a freed clerk for stepping on her hem.

"She reminds me of the court before it starved," Sef murmured to Odile Ferran, who had appeared at his elbow carrying a lantern in one hand and a ward-keeper's broken oath in the other. The old woman moved like a ship in bad weather — listing, but determined to arrive somewhere.

"Don't you dare get sentimental," Odile said. "Sentimental men walk slowly."

"I'm walking at a perfectly brisk pace."

"You're standing still and narrating."

"That's called leadership."

"It's called stalling, boy, and I've been watching you do it since the Drowned Stair." Her eyes, red-rimmed and missing whole decades of memory since her last payment, fixed on him with terrible accuracy. "You're planning to stay behind. At whatever pinch point comes first. I can smell a self-sacrificing idiot the way I used to smell a cracked ward."

Sef considered lying to her. He was, after all, a courtier; lying was his native language, the tongue of his cradle, if courtiers could be said to have cradles. But Odile had paid away her mother's face and her wedding day and, apparently, her capacity to be fooled.

"The Processional narrows at the Gallery of Names," he admitted. "When the mountain finishes groaning, that gallery goes. Someone has to hold the brace-spell at the far pillar until everyone's through."

"And then?"

"And then the pillar and I will have a great deal in common."

Odile hit him. Not hard — she was seventy, exhausted, and possibly couldn't remember how many children she'd had — but with the flat, sincere palm of a woman who had earned the right. "You don't get to do that," she said. "You don't get to spend yourself like coin. I've seen what this place does with spending."

"Someone has to hold it."

"Then hold it and *walk*. Braces don't need heroes, they need hands, and hands can leave when the work is done."

The mountain answered her with a crack that ran the length of the ceiling, and a drift of white dust fell through the lantern light like the world's saddest snow. Somewhere behind them, deeper in the court, came the sound of beasts roaring and stone answering — Ansel's war, doing its loud best to bury them all.

"Walk," Sef told the column gently. "Grandmother, you're our pace-setter. Everyone else, her hem is holy ground. Go."

* * *

The Gallery of Names was the oldest room in the Hollow Court, older than the Ledger, older than the Queen's hunger. Before the court learned to burn souls as currency, it had simply written them here — carved into pillars of black basalt, name upon name upon name, the record of everyone the pact had ever touched. Sef's own name was on the fourth pillar from the entrance. ANDRADE, SEFRIEL. SOLD, AGE NINETEEN. PRICE: FORGIVEN.

His father had gotten forgiveness for him. Forgiveness of debts, though at nineteen Sef had understood it as forgiveness of himself, which was worse, because it meant someone had decided he was a mistake worth erasing.

He ran his thumb along the groove of the letters as the column filed past. Two hundred years, and the carving hadn't weathered a day. Basalt remembered. That was the trouble with this entire mountain — everything down here remembered except the things that most needed to.

"Uncle Sef!" A girl — Tam, eight years old, sold with her whole village in a border raid Marchen had never bothered to explain — grabbed his sleeve. "You're coming too, right?"

"Absolutely," he said. "I'm bringing up the rear. Rear-guard. Very prestigious position."

"Is that true?"

"Tam." He crouched to her height, which took some doing, because his knees were basalt-adjacent these days. "Do you know what happens to a hollow thing that chooses? Nothing. Nothing has ever happened to one. No hollow thing has ever been given a choice before tonight." He tapped her nose. "So I'm going to go stand next to a pillar, and choose, and then I'm going to come find you above ground and complain about the sun. You'll want to be there for that. I'm told my complaints are excellent."

She studied him with the flat skepticism of childhood, found him wanting, and hugged him anyway, which was the most merciful thing anyone had done in the Hollow Court in a century. Then she ran to catch the column, and Sef stood alone in the Gallery of Names with a cracking ceiling and a job to do.

* * *

The brace-spell was simple, as the old magics went — a ward-keeper's trick, really, the kind Odile could have done drunk and blindfolded, which was possibly her literal historical condition. You gave the stone your weight and your attention and a piece of your intent, and the stone agreed, for a while, to keep holding what it had always held.

For a human, the cost was fatigue. For a half-hollow creature made mostly of borrowed architecture and regret, the cost was somewhat different.

Sef pressed both palms against the far pillar and felt the gallery lean into him like a tired horse. Above, the vault of bone ribs groaned and settled and groaned again, dust sifting down in golden sheets through the light of the burning Ledger far below — dawn-colored light rising through the floor cracks, which was such a beautiful wrongness that he almost laughed.

"All right," he told the pillar conversationally. "Let's you and me have an understanding."

The pillar said nothing. Pillars were famously tight-lipped.

"I know how this ends for me," Sef went on. "I'd like to be clear about that up front. I've read enough ledgers to know when the arithmetic doesn't balance. Forty people through that door, one half-hollow courtier left holding a roof — any auditor would call that a bargain." He shifted his grip as a fissure walked up the wall beside him. "The question isn't whether. It's *who* does the leaving."

Because that was the thing nobody had said aloud yet, the thing coiled under his ribs like a second heart: when the gallery finally came down, he would not simply die. Half-hollow things didn't die neatly. They came apart into what they were made of, and Sef had spent two hundred years being made of other people's payments — scraps of sold memory, fragments of intent, the residue of every soul that had passed through the court's hands. When he broke, all of that would spill back out. Some of it might reach the surface. Some of it might reach *her* — Ansel, who had refused to let anyone pay for her family twice, who had shouted at him about it with tears running into her furious mouth.

He didn't want her to carry him. She had enough ledgers.

"So," he said to the pillar, to the gallery, to the listening dark. "New arrangement."

* * *

The column took eleven minutes to pass through the Gallery of Names. He counted every head, because counting was the last honest work he knew. Thirty-eight, thirty-nine — the merchant with the squint, who paused at the far door and gave Sef a single nod, courtier to customer, acknowledgment of a transaction fairly conducted. Sef laughed out loud at that. Forty.

Forty through, plus one grandmother who stopped at the threshold, looked back at him across the shuddering hall, and said, in the tone she probably used on grandchildren and livestock alike: "You come find me, young man. I mean it. I'll feed you whether you deserve it or not."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And eat more. You're all bones."

"I'm *mostly* bones, actually, it's structural—"

The mountain interrupted with a roar, and the gallery began, in earnest, to come down.

Sef poured himself into the spell. There was no other way to describe it — he opened every door inside himself and let the stone draw through what it needed. He felt the borrowed memories go first, two centuries of other people's Tuesdays, other people's weddings, other people's griefs he'd carried without permission or complaint. They streamed out of him like water finding its level, and each one, departing, showed him its face one last time. Thank you, he told them. Thank you, all of you. I kept you as well as I could.

Then his own things started to go, and that was different, that was *him*: the memory of sunlight on a harbor he hadn't seen since he was nineteen. His mother's voice saying his full name in the tone that meant *trouble*. The exact color of the sky the day he was sold, which he had hated and hoarded both, because it was his, proof he had once stood under open air.

He let them go. All of them. Because here was the secret he had finally, in the last hour of his long unlived life, understood:

The court had taught him that a soul was a weight on a scale, a price, an entry in a book. But a soul wasn't the *memories*. A soul was the one who chose what to do with them. And the memories were burning off him now like fog in morning light, and underneath, stripped clean, there was still someone standing at a pillar, holding a roof, choosing.

*Sefriel,* said the fourth pillar, in a voice like deep water, like the mountain itself reading its oldest book. *ANDRADE. SOLD.*

"Not anymore," Sef said, and his voice came out strange and light, with nothing left in it but himself. "Strike the entry. I'm taking my name back."

The gallery fell.

He held the pillar while it fell, and the falling took a long time, long enough for him to hear forty pairs of feet reaching stone stairs beyond the door, long enough to feel the mountain's groan change key — grief, maybe, or gratitude, or just geology — long enough to notice that the light rising through the cracks was warm, genuinely warm, the color of a thing forgiven rather than sold.

The last thought he had was not grand. It was small and specific and entirely his own: *I hope Tam tells the grandmother I complained about the sun. I had a really good bit prepared.*

Then Sef Andrade, courtier of ash, holder of roofs, keeper of one recovered name, let go of everything except the choosing — and the choosing, as it turned out, was heavier than the sky and lighter than a feather, and it carried him all the way down like a hand.

* * *

Above, in the shaking dark of the mountain's throat, the collapse echoed and faded, and the road to the root lay open — the deep way, the old way, the way Veyra Marchen had taken with her army an hour before, toward the place where the first star-pact had been signed in blood and starlight.

Ansel Quintana heard the gallery fall from three corridors away. She stopped mid-stride, one hand pressed to the stone, and Odile watched her go very still, the way people do when they feel a debt settle that they never agreed to owe.

"He held it," Odile said quietly. Not a question. Ward-keepers always knew when something ended.

"He held it," Ansel said. Her voice was rough. Then she lifted her head, and there was nothing soft left in it at all — only direction, only the long slope down toward the mountain's root, toward the woman who had built all of this on other people's names. "Marchen's ahead of us. Move."

They moved. Behind them, far above, the stars were waiting to be lit.
