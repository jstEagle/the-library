## 28. Confrontation at the Root

The way down to the root of the mountain was not a road so much as a grudge.

Ansel had walked smuggler paths her whole life — goat tracks, drowned stairs, ledges that existed only on Tuesdays and during certain phases of the moon — but this passage was older than any of them. The walls were not stone, exactly. They were stone that had *agreed* to be stone, and you could feel the agreement straining, like a handshake held too long between people who'd rather be fighting.

Behind her came the sound of Odile Ferran breathing in the particular rhythm of a woman reciting ward-keeper oaths under her breath, trying to keep the fragments of them stitched to her skull.

"Say it louder," Ansel said over her shoulder. "It helps."

"Does it?"

"No idea. But I like hearing it."

"You're a cruel woman, Ansel Quintana."

"I'm a tired one. It's the same thing after midnight."

They descended. Above them, faint as a headache, the battle still rang through the outer galleries — wolves howling in harmonies no wolf should know, bats screaming maps, Sef Andrade somewhere in the middle of it all shouting his half-hollow heart out. Below them, through the stone itself, came the other sound: the Queen waking, vast and patient, a hunger the size of a season.

And below *that* — at the very root, where the first pact had been signed by people whose names the Ledger had already eaten — Veyra Marchen was waiting.

Odile felt it before Ansel did. She stopped on the last stair, one hand pressed flat against the wall like a physician taking a pulse. "She's down there. And she's brought the Ledger."

"The Ledger burned."

"Ledgers don't burn all the way," Odile said grimly. "I've tried. Ask me about my twenties sometime." Then her face did something complicated. "Actually don't. I might not remember my twenties."

* * *

The root chamber opened around them like a held breath.

It was not carved and it was not grown; it was simply *there*, the way the inside of a bell is there — curved, resonant, made for a single enormous note. Overhead, where the ceiling should have been, the rock thinned to something like skin, and through it filtered a light that had no source: pale gold, old, the color of the stars that had gone out. The original starlight, Ansel realized. Pact-light. Twenty generations of an agreement, glowing faintly through the mountain's hide like a bruise going yellow.

At the center stood a table of black glass — the signing table, or what remained of it — and beside it, a brazier in which something that was not quite fire burned low and sullen. On the table lay a book. Half its pages were ash. The other half lay open, patient, waiting for new names.

Veyra Marchen stood behind the table with her hands folded, and she looked — this was the thing Ansel would remember later, the thing that would keep her up nights — she looked *reasonable*. A tall woman in travel-worn grays, silver at her temples, ink stains on two fingers. A clerk who had climbed. Not a monster. Monsters were easy.

"Quintana." Marchen's voice carried in the bell-chamber without effort, the way a whisper carries in a church. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other army."

"Half of mine defected to a starving god an hour ago. I've seen everything today." Marchen gestured at the bench across from her, an invitation so mundane it was almost funny. "Sit. You've been running since Cinderhatch. Running makes people stupid, and I'd rather negotiate with someone intelligent."

Ansel didn't sit. She came around the table slowly instead, putting herself between Marchen and the stair, and noted with a tracker's satisfaction the small shift in Marchen's weight — the usurper had wanted the high ground of the doorway herself and hadn't gotten it. Small victories. You banked them the way you banked dry kindling.

"Where's your escort?" Ansel asked.

"Gone. Defected, dead, or listening to the Queen tell them their deepest want." Marchen smiled thinly. "Do you know what she offered me? Not dominion. That's what I promised *myself*. Her offer was quieter. She offered me a night where nobody needed anything from me. No petitions, no armies, no debts." Her smile flickered, just once. "It took me eleven full seconds to refuse. Eleven seconds is a long time, Quintana. Remember yours when it comes."

"I'll set a candle to it," Ansel said. "Why are we here? You lost. The Ledger's burning, your heralds are running, and the beasts have the galleries. This isn't a negotiation. It's a surrender with better lighting."

Marchen turned her head and looked at the half-burned book on the table, and her expression did something Ansel had never seen a powerful person do in front of witnesses: it went honest.

"Do you know what I found when I was nineteen?" Marchen said. "Not power. Paperwork. I was a junior clerk in Rivermoor, copying tax rolls, dying of boredom, when I found the old pledge-rolls — the ones your mother's name is in. Every city in the free marches, every family of standing, sold a soul to the Hollow Court generations ago. That's what holds the wards. That's what holds the *mountain*. Do you understand what I learned at nineteen, Quintana? Our entire civilization is a payment plan. We are all living on credit, and nobody told us, and nobody ever intended to pay."

"So you decided to pay with other people."

"I decided to pay with the people who were *going to be eaten anyway*." Marchen's voice sharpened. "Conscripts. Criminals. The desperate. Souls the cities would never miss — souls the cities throw away every day, into mines and wars and ditches. I did the arithmetic your ancestors refused to do. Someone always pays. I chose who, and I chose few, and I built roads and granaries with the time I bought. Call me a monster if it helps you sleep. But tell me your alternative. Tell me what happens now that your wolf-friend has burned the Ledger and unbound the debt."

And there it was. Underneath the floor of the root chamber — beneath the stone, beneath the stone's agreement to be stone — Ansel felt the answer moving. A groan, long and geological, the sound a house makes when its foundation remembers it is standing on nothing.

The mountain cities were held up by the pact. The pact was held up by the court. The court was starving because the star-pact broke. Break any link, and everything above came down.

"That's why you're here," Ansel said slowly. "You're not negotiating a surrender. You're negotiating a rescue."

"I'm negotiating *leverage*," Marchen corrected, but gently, which was worse. "Same paperwork. Better margins."

* * *

The duel, when it finally came, began — as duels in the Hollow did — with words.

"Your beasts are tearing apart galleries full of hollowed civilians," Marchen said. "People who were sold before they could walk. Your friend Sef is trying to save them, and he can't, because your wolves can't tell a courtier from a corpse. Every minute you spend posturing, more of them die. So: terms. I still command perhaps four hundred soldiers above, and every gate-code in Thornfell. Give me safe passage out with my officers, and I'll order them to stand down, open the passes, feed the refugees. Refuse, and I burn the remaining pledge-pages myself — all of them, right now — and we find out together how fast a mountain falls."

Ansel heard the trap in it, all of it, every tooth of it. Marchen would never actually burn the pages — her whole life was leverage, and ash had no margin — but she didn't need to mean it. She needed Ansel to *believe* it, because belief moved faster than thought.

"You're bluffing," Ansel said.

"Probably. Care to bet two cities?"

There it was. Ansel exhaled. Behind her, on the stair, Odile muttered something that was either an oath or a curse, possibly both — the ward-keepers' liturgy did tend to blur at the edges.

"You know what I noticed about you," Ansel said, drifting along the black glass table as she spoke, tracker-casual, buying her feet time to learn the ground the way they'd learned a hundred smuggling floors. "In Cinderhatch. Your herald read your proclamation off a scroll, and the seal on it was fresh wax, but the *stamp* was old. Cracked. Heirloom work. You didn't make a new sigil for yourself. You used your family's. Because you believe the blood matters more than the deed."

"My grandmother's stamp. What of it?"

"Nothing. Except that the Ledger says otherwise." Ansel stopped walking. "I read your entry, Marchen. Page after page. Do you know what your first sale was? Before the conscripts, before the clever arithmetic? It was *your own name*. Nineteen years old, junior clerk, and you sold your name to the court to buy the influence to find the pledge-rolls in the first place. That's why you use your grandmother's stamp. You literally cannot sign your own name anymore. The court ate it."

Silence. In it, the mountain groaned again, closer, like a giant turning over in bed.

Marchen's face did not change, which was itself an answer. "Names are currency," she said quietly. "I spent mine on something worthwhile."

"On *paperwork*."

"On the only honest transaction in three hundred years! Everyone else signed pledges and pretended they hadn't. Everyone else let the debt ride and called it tradition. I at least *read the contract*, Quintana. Can you say the same? Have you read yours?" And here Marchen leaned forward across the black glass, and her voice dropped into something almost kind, which was the most dangerous register she owned. "Because you have an entry too. Did you know that? Your mother pledged something before she burned. Something large. The Ledger doesn't say what — the entry's smudged, damaged, the only one in the whole book. But the weight is recorded. And weight, in this place, is destiny. You didn't come down here for your brother by accident, beast-speaker. The court has been *expecting* you."

The words went in like a splinter — small, deep, working. Ansel thought of her mother's face, or the place where her mother's face had been, that smooth blank patch in her memory where the toll had been paid. She thought of the smudged page. She thought: *later.* Grief was a luxury with a queue, and she wasn't at the front of it.

"Here are my terms," Ansel said. "You write a full confession. Names, dates, every sale. You sign it — with whatever name you have left. You give it to Odile Ferran, who is a sworn keeper of wards and the most stubborn person in either world, and she takes it up to Cinderhatch. Then you surrender yourself to the free cities' judgment. In exchange, I call off the beasts from your soldiers, and you get trial instead of teeth."

"And my officers?"

"Trial too. Most of them, probably. Some mercy. I'm told the cities are capable of it when fed."

Marchen laughed — a short, real laugh, quickly caged. "Judgment. From the cities I fed and clothed for ten years. They'll tear me apart in a square and then quietly adopt my grain schedules."

"Probably," Ansel agreed. "That's what winning looks like. It's not dignified."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I do the thing you're afraid of."

"Which is?"

Ansel put her hand on the black glass table, and reached — carefully, the way you'd reach toward a strange dog — with the gift. Down through the table, down through the signing-stone, down to the root itself, to where the oldest pact sat coiled in the dark. And she sang to it. One note, quiet, the lullaby note, the one her mother had taught her and that she'd sung to a soul-flame that couldn't remember why it loved her.

The whole chamber answered. Not the Queen — the *pact*. The pale gold light overhead brightened, steadied, and every hair on Ansel's arms rose as the mountain's oldest agreement stirred and *listened*, the way beasts listened, the way the lion in the pass had listened twenty chapters of her life ago. The stone under their feet hummed a chord.

Marchen went white. "You're bound already. When did — how far along are you?"

"Not yet bound. Just fluent." Ansel lifted her hand. The chord faded, reluctant. "I can't destroy the court without burying your cities. But I can do this: I can renegotiate. New terms, freely given — memory as gift, not theft, beasts as wardens, wardens as partners. The pact survives. The cities survive. The court eats honestly or starves politely. And there is no line in that arrangement for a woman who sold souls by the consignment." She met Marchen's eyes. "So it's judgment, or it's the root. Choose fast. The building is coming down either way, and I'd rather dig you out alive than apologize later."

For a long moment, Veyra Marchen — clerk, usurper, the woman who had done the arithmetic — looked at Ansel Quintana, who had never done arithmetic in her life and had instead taught a mountain lion to share a trail.

"You really think mercy scales," Marchen said. It wasn't a question so much as a wound being probed.

"I think it's the only thing worth scaling."

"Sentiment." But Marchen's hand was already moving — to her belt, to the pen she carried the way soldiers carry knives, ink-stained fingers uncapping it with a clerk's economy. "Fine. Judgment. But hear me, Quintana, and mark it well: when the cities learn what holds them up — when they learn the price of their own rooftops — they will come looking for someone to blame, and they will not thank the woman who saved them. They will want the old arrangement back within a generation. Debt is comfortable. Mercy is *work*." She pulled a fresh page from the ruined Ledger's remains, laid it on the black glass, and began to write. "I'm confessing because you'll do it anyway, with or without my cooperation, and I'd rather be the author of my downfall than a footnote in yours."

"That," said Ansel, "is the most honest thing anyone's said to me all week."

"It's the only kind worth saying." Marchen wrote on, quick and neat, columns of names — the same tidy hand that had once copied tax rolls in Rivermoor, that had signed away Tomas Quintana with a flick of the wrist. She paused once, mid-column, and something crossed her face that in a better woman might have been grief. "Your brother," she said, not looking up. "The sale was efficient. I remember being pleased with myself about it. I want you to know that I remember, and that I am writing it down in my own hand, and that I will say it aloud at my trial if they allow me to speak. Efficiency is not innocence. I understood that about nine seconds too late, and nine seconds, as I said, is a long time."

Ansel found, to her surprise, that she had no clever reply. Sometimes the best you got from a monster was the truth, delivered late and cold, and you had to decide whether late-and-cold fed anyone. She looked at the stair, where Odile stood watching Marchen write with an expression like a woman watching a storm decide to be polite.

"She finishes the confession," Ansel called to him, "you take it up. Both of you. Now."

* * *

It was Odile who saw it first, because Odile had spent thirty years reading wards and wards were just promises written in light, and she knew a broken promise on sight.

"Quintana." Her voice cracked on the second syllable. "The ceiling."

Ansel looked up. Through the thin skin of rock overhead, the old gold light was guttering — flaring, failing, flaring — like a lamp with a torn wick. The pact-song she'd woken was still humming in the stones, but underneath it now came the other sound, the true sound, the one she'd been refusing to name since the Ledger burned:

The Queen was awake. Fully. And she was rising.

The floor tilted — gently, terribly, the way a ship tilts. Ash lifted off the ruined Ledger and hung in the air, directionless. Somewhere far above, iron groaned; somewhere farther above than that, she knew, two cities full of people were feeling their floors tilt the same way, and looking at each other, and beginning to run.

Marchen stood very still, pen hovering. "Terms," she said, and for the first time her voice had no leverage in it at all. "New terms. Quickly. Whatever you're going to give that thing — give it now."

Ansel closed her eyes. She felt it all at once, the whole shape of it: the starving vastness below, the cities swaying above, the beasts in the galleries falling silent and turning their blind faces downward, the freed souls drifting like embers, the confession half-written on the table, her brother somewhere in the dark with his name newly returned to him, calling hers.

Every eye in the mountain — hollow and human and in-between — was about to turn to the only creature present who could speak to all of them at once.

"Oh," Ansel said quietly, to no one, to the dark, to the lullaby. "*Oh, no.*"

The Queen spoke, and the word was her name.
