## 29. The Empty Throne

The ash was still falling when the mountain stopped screaming.

Not all at once. It came down in stages, the way a frightened animal settles — a shudder through the deep galleries, then a long groan from the root-chambers where Marchen had died, then a silence so total that Ansel could hear her own heart tripping over itself. She stood in what had been the Ledger Hall, up to her ankles in burnt paper, and listened to a cathedral hold its breath.

"Is it dead?" Odile asked. She was leaning on Ansel's arm more than she'd ever admit to, her ward-keeper's staff reduced to a charcoal stub she refused to put down. "The court, I mean. Not Marchen. I watched you handle Marchen."

"I didn't *handle* her," Ansel said. "She fell into the crack her own great-grandfather signed open. That's not handling. That's bookkeeping."

"Well. Someone had to balance it." Odile peered around at the drifting grey flakes, catching one on her palm and rubbing it between finger and thumb like a merchant testing flour. Two hundred years of soul-debt, and it felt like nothing at all. She frowned. "That's the trouble with burning ledgers, you know. Very satisfying. Very final. And then everybody looks at you and says, *wonderful, now what?*"

The lanterns were guttering. They'd been lit by sold souls once — little flames of borrowed life pinned to bone sconces — and with the Ledger gone they were going out politely, one by one, like guests slipping away from a party that had turned awkward. In their place, something else was beginning to glow. Faint, greenish, coming up out of the floor itself. The hollow-light that lived in the stone.

Ansel could feel it better than see it. That was new. Or rather, it wasn't new — it had been growing since the beasts descended, since she'd called them down through stone and root and they had answered — but here, in the heart of the Hollow Court, with the old pact cracked wide open, it roared. Every hollow thing in the mountain was listening to her the way a room listens to someone about to say something important.

She hated it immediately.

* * *

They found the throne by following the light.

It sat at the far end of the hall, past the dais where Marchen's army had stacked its offerings, past the scorched outline where the Ledger's reading-table had stood. It was not a grand thing. That surprised her. After everything — the bone arches, the galleries carved like throats, the vast dark where the Queen coiled — she had expected a seat made of teeth or gold or grief.

Instead it was a chair. Old wood, worn smooth, sized for an ordinary person. A human person. Someone had sat in it a very long time ago, before the court grew its cathedral bones, back when the star-pact was young and the first bargain was struck face to face. There were initials scratched into the armrest. Ansel knelt and brushed away the ash.

*T.M.*

"Tomas," she said aloud, and then corrected herself, because the name didn't fit. Her brother was twenty miles above ground by now, walking down out of Thornfell with the other freed souls, learning again how to carry his own weight. This was an older Tomas. Or no Tomas at all. Just some man who had come down into the dark with a knife and a promise and left his mark on a chair because that's what people did when they were afraid of being forgotten.

"He was the first voice," said a courtier.

Ansel came up fast, hand on her blade, but it was only one of the half-hollow ones — a small figure, mostly ash-colored, with a face like a candle burned low. One of Sef's people. Sef's survivors, rather. The ones he'd pulled toward the exits while the mountain groaned, before he stayed behind in Gallery Nine and held the ceiling up with nothing but his stubbornness and his own remembered name.

"The first what?"

"Voice." The courtier's mouth moved oddly, as if speech were a skill it kept losing and finding again. "When the pact was made, the Queen could not speak to the world above. Too vast. Too hungry. So there was a voice — a person bound to the court, who spoke for it upward and for the world downward. Who carried both songs." It gestured at the chair with a hand that ended in smoke instead of fingers. "Veyra Marchen wanted to be queen. She never understood. There is no queen. There is only the Queen, and the Voice, and the space between them where the pact lives."

"And when the last Voice died?"

"It was not replaced. The court grew... crooked." The courtier's ember-eyes dimmed. "Hunger without manners. We began to take instead of trade. You have seen what we became."

Ansel looked at the chair. Small, worn, patient. Waiting two hundred years for someone to sit down and start being reasonable again.

"Oh no," she said. "No, no, no. I know what that look means. All of you — every one of you — stop looking at me like that."

But the hall was filling. The freed souls were drifting back down — she didn't understand how, only that the mountain's roads had become strange since the Ledger burned, that distance below meant something different now. They came in ones and twos, blinking, holding each other's hands, and behind them came the beasts: wolves sliding out of side-galleries with their ears forward, blind bats hanging in clusters from the bone arches, turning their ruined faces toward her. Even the mountain lion from the high passes — the first creature she'd ever bargained with openly — was there, sitting at the edge of the light with its tail curled around its feet like a cat waiting for dinner.

Everyone looking at the chair. Everyone looking at her.

"No," Ansel told them all. "I burned my mother's name clean, I got my brother back, I watched a tyrant fall into her own arithmetic. I am *done*. I want a bath, and bread, and to sleep somewhere that isn't actively hungry."

The lion yawned.

"Don't you start."

* * *

Odile found her an hour later, sitting on the dais steps with her knees drawn up, watching the hollow-light pulse in the floor like a slow heartbeat.

"You've been elected," Odile said.

"I haven't accepted anything."

"That has never once stopped an election." The old ward-keeper lowered herself onto the step beside her with a grunt. Up close, in this light, Ansel could see what the quest had cost her — the soft gaps in her memory, the way she sometimes paused mid-sentence like a woman crossing a bridge missing planks. But her eyes were still sharp. Whatever else the court had eaten, it hadn't touched that. "There's been a council. Of sorts. The souls won't leave while the mountain shakes, the beasts won't leave while you're here, and the courtiers keep asking me questions in Latin nobody remembers teaching them. Somebody has to speak for all of them. And apparently—"

"Apparently the only person who talks to both wolves and wraiths is the smuggler."

"Apparently the only person who talks to both wolves and wraiths," Odile agreed, "is the smuggler who is currently refusing to look at a perfectly good chair."

Ansel laughed, which surprised her, and which hurt, which surprised her more. "You don't understand what they're asking. My mother heard voices her whole life and they burned her for it. Well—" she caught herself, because the Ledger's older pages had rewritten that story too, had shown her a woman walking into fire on purpose to seal a crack in the sky. "*She chose it.* That's the part that frightens me. Everyone keeps choosing this. And I can feel it already, Odile. The pull of it. When the wolf speaks, I hear it plain. When the court hums, I hum back. Sit in that chair, bind myself to this place, and I would never be just Ansel Quintana again. I'd be a door. Doors don't get to walk away."

Odile was quiet for a moment. Then she tapped her charred staff against the step, twice, the way she used to tap her compass before giving bad news.

"Do you know why the wards fell?" she said. "Really fell? Not because something drank them dry — though something did. They fell because they were anchored to pledges nobody living understood. Souls sold in secret, debts signed in the dark, and the whole structure held up by people who never got a say. That's what happens when the voice goes silent, girl. The strong write the terms, and everyone else gets conscripted." She nodded at the chair. "That thing isn't a crown. It's a mouth. And right now the mountain is holding its breath because it wants to hear whether anyone will use it honestly."

"And if I say no?"

"Then somebody else sits down eventually. And I'd like to be alive to see whether it's someone who asks the beasts' permission first, or someone who sells them."

Which was, Ansel reflected bitterly, exactly the sort of argument that worked on her. Odile had known her three weeks and already fought dirtier than the smugglers she'd run with for fifteen years.

* * *

They came for her at midnight, if midnight still existed underground — the hour when the hollow-light ran lowest and the bats sang their maps into the dark.

The courtiers came first, a procession of ash and ember, carrying nothing, wanting nothing except to stand near the chair and look hopeful in the way that only the desperate can. Then the freed souls, hundreds of them, the ones who couldn't yet face the climb to daylight, pressing close with faces she almost knew — neighbors from Cinderhatch, cousins from Rivermoor, people whose names had been scraped off them by the Ledger and were growing back like skin. Then the beasts, padding in silent, filling the galleries until the whole court smelled like fur and cold stone and, underneath it, that greenish hum.

And last, down through the arches that had been carved like throats, came the Queen.

Ansel had seen her before — glimpsed her, rather, vast and patient in the deepest dark, starving since the star-pact broke. Seeing her properly was different. She was less a shape than a weather. She filled the cavern the way night fills a valley, and her attention settled on Ansel like snowfall: soft, total, inescapable.

Every soul in the hall went to its knees. The wolves lay flat. The bats stopped singing.

Ansel stayed standing, mostly because her legs had locked.

*You are the one who sings,* said the Queen, and her voice did not arrive through the air. It arrived the way memory arrives, from inside, wearing Ansel's own cadence. *We have heard you since you were small. Calling the lions. Bargaining with crows. You thought you were hiding. Nothing hides from us. We are under everything.*

"Lucky me," Ansel managed.

*The pact is broken. The Ledger is ash. The debt-slaves are free and the thief is dead and we are glad of it — do not mistake us, little singer, we are glad. But the mountain stands because the pact holds it, and the stars shine because the pact feeds them, and the pact is a sentence that must be spoken anew. Spoken by someone who belongs to both sides of it.* The attention tightened, gently, terribly. *Who speaks beast and hollow alike. Who has paid this court in her own memory and walked out still owing nothing. Who burned the lie off her mother's name and refused a tyrant's mercy. There is one such person in all the world, and she is standing in front of our throne, telling it no.*

"I'm telling it *not yet*," Ansel said. Her voice echoed strangely, off stone and fur and hollow things, and she realized distantly that every creature in the hall could hear her as clearly as she heard the Queen. Both directions at once. Already a door. "There's a difference. I've spent my whole life watching people get handed terrible choices dressed up as the only choices. My mother got one. Sef got one. Half these poor souls got theirs written down in a ledger they never saw. So no. I'm not saying no. I'm saying that before anybody binds anything to anything, we're going to talk about terms. All of us. Beasts and courtiers and souls and cities above. Nobody signs anything in the dark tonight."

A ripple went through the hall — surprise, she thought, and something that might have been hope.

The Queen was silent a long moment. When she spoke again, something in the vastness of her had shifted, almost imperceptibly, the way a glacier shifts.

*Nobody has negotiated with us in two hundred years,* she said. *They only begged. Or sold.* A pause, long enough for Ansel's heart to beat four times. *Come to the deep chamber tomorrow, when you have slept. Bring your wolf and your broken keeper and your brother, if he will come. And bring your terms, little singer. We find ourselves curious what you will ask for.*

The presence lifted like fog at dawn. Around the hall, souls and beasts and courtiers slowly rose, blinking at each other, suddenly ordinary again — or as ordinary as anyone gets, standing in a bone cathedral full of green light.

Ansel exhaled for what felt like the first time in an hour.

"You realize," Odile said at her elbow, "that you just scheduled negotiations with an ancient hunger-god. While covered in ash. Without any terms prepared."

"I bought myself a day. That's how smuggling works. You never negotiate tired, and you never show them your price list."

"Mm. And what will you ask for, oh great Voice-elect?"

Ansel looked at the chair. Small, worn, waiting. T.M., whoever he'd been, had sat down there afraid of being forgotten. She was afraid of exactly the opposite.

"I don't know yet," she admitted. "But I know the first rule of it. Whatever I agree to — it doesn't cost anybody else anything. Not one memory, not one soul, not one wolf. Not even mine, unless I give it freely." She grimaced. "I have a feeling the Queen's going to drive a very hard bargain about that last part."

Above them — impossibly, through a mile of stone — something flickered. A single point of light, showing faintly through a crack in the vaulted dark, like a star deciding whether to come back.

The whole hall looked up. And Ansel, half-hollow already and not yet knowing it, felt the court lean toward her like a listener, and understood that tomorrow she wouldn't be bargaining for herself at all.

She'd be bargaining for every light in the sky.
