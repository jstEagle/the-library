## 30. The Queen's Question

The throne room of the Hollow Court had been a cathedral once, Ansel thought, or something that wanted to be one. Bone pillars arched overhead like the ribs of some patient leviathan, and between them hung lanterns that burned without flame — soul-light, Sef had called it, back when he'd still been walking beside them instead of holding shut a gallery full of screaming stone. The light was soft. That was the horror of it. It fell on everything the way candlelight falls on a family supper.

And at the far end of it all, coiled around a throne of black glass and older bone, the Hollow Queen waited.

Ansel walked toward her because there was nowhere else to walk. Behind her, Odile limped along, one hand pressed to her temple where the missing name of Ansel Quintana lived now — a smooth blank place, an absence she kept touching like a tongue finding a pulled tooth. Ahead, sprawled at the foot of the dais, Veyra Marchen lay where the mountain's last convulsion had thrown her, breathing hard, her sword skittered away across the floor, her face for the first time stripped of its beautiful arithmetic.

The Queen did not rise. The Queen did not need to rise. She was vast in the way weather is vast, in the way winter is vast — not a shape so much as a presence that occasionally consented to have edges. Her eyes were two lanterns of their own, dim and deep, like stars seen through very dirty water.

*Little singer,* said a voice that was not a voice. It arrived inside Ansel's skull the way cold arrives through a door left ajar. *You have made a great deal of noise in my house.*

"It's not your house," Ansel said. Her own voice sounded small and human and stubborn, which was, she decided, about right. "You just live here. There's a difference."

A pause. Then, impossibly, something that might have been amusement rippled through the dark.

*Your mother said something similar.*

The words went through Ansel like a blade finding an old scar. She thought of the Ledger's older pages, the ink gone brown, the entry written in her mother's own hand: *Given freely, to seal what was cracking.* She thought of fire, and of the stories Cinderhatch told about witches, and of how wrong every one of those stories had turned out to be.

"Then you knew her," Ansel said. "Good. Then you'll know I don't scare easy."

*I know,* the Queen said, *that she bargained better than anyone who ever stood where you stand. Come closer, beast-speaker. I would look at you properly.*

Odile's hand found Ansel's sleeve. "Ansel—" she started, then stopped, frowning, because the name wouldn't come to her lips right; she'd reached for it and found the shelf empty. She covered the stumble with gruffness. "Don't get eaten. I've lost enough today."

"You haven't lost me yet."

"Don't jinx it." Odile let go and lowered herself onto a broken step of the dais with the sigh of a woman whose knees had opinions. From somewhere in her coat she produced a flask, looked at it, and put it away again. Even now, even here. Discipline was a kind of prayer, Ansel supposed.

Marchen laughed from the floor. It was not a pleasant sound. It was the laugh of someone who has done the sums and found them still, against all evidence, in her favor.

"Listen to it," Marchen said, propping herself up on one elbow. Blood ran from her hairline and she wiped it away as though it were rain. "It will offer you the same thing it offered me. It will make it sound noble. It is not noble. It is hunger wearing a crown."

"And what were you offering it?" Ansel asked. "The free cities' souls, poured down this hole so you could keep your armies marching? You dressed up theft as salvation to half a border's worth of desperate people. You're hardly in a position to lecture anyone on nobility."

"I was going to save the cities." Marchen's voice cracked on the word, and Ansel heard, underneath the pragmatism, something almost like belief. That was the worst part of Veyra Marchen — she had never lied to herself. "One city fed to the dark so five could live. Every ruler in history has made that trade and slept well after. I simply wasn't a coward about writing it down."

"You weren't the one being written down," Odile said flatly.

"No," Marchen agreed. "That's rather the point of ruling."

The Queen moved then — a slow shifting, tectonic, and the whole cathedral groaned around her like a ship in a storm. Above them, faintly, through miles of stone, Ansel felt the mountain tremble. Felt the cities trembling too. Cinderhatch. Rivermoor. Ten thousand people going about their evening, banking their fires, kissing their children, entirely unaware that the ground beneath them was held together by a bargain three hundred years old and a Ledger that was currently ash.

She had done that. Odile had lit the match, but Ansel had stood guard over the burning. Victory curdling into catastrophe — Sef had warned her the court always overcharged.

*Now,* said the Queen, and the word filled the room the way water fills a lung. *Let us speak plainly, little singer, as your mother did.*

"I'd prefer that to everything else that's happened lately."

*You burned my Ledger.* Not an accusation. A statement, the way one notes the weather. *In doing so you unbound every soul-debt ever written — including the debts that bind me. Including the debt that binds the mountain. Do you understand what holds up Thornfell?*

"Stories say the court anchors it."

*Stories are wrong in the details and right in the bones. The first pact was struck when the star-pact above cracked and the sky began to forget us. The hollow places needed feeding or they would collapse inward, and everything built upon them — every city, every road, every grave — would follow. So the old kings gave souls. Willingly, at first. Then less willingly. Then not at all, and men like Marchen's grandfathers learned that stolen souls burn brighter.* A ripple of something that might have been disgust moved through the dark. *I did not ask for thieves. Hunger does not choose its cooks.*

"But you ate what they brought you."

*I did. And I am not proud, and pride is a luxury of the fed.* The great head lowered, and those drowned-star eyes came level with Ansel's own. *So. Here is your question, and I will ask it only once, and I will not dress it up as your mother would have, because dressing things up is how your kind ends up burning each other.*

The silence stretched. Somewhere far off, stone settled. Odile had gone very still. Even Marchen had stopped smirking.

*The Ledger is ash. The debts are unbound. Which means the pact that holds this mountain — and the two cities sleeping upon it — is unbound with them. Within days, perhaps hours, Thornfell begins to fold. Rivermoor goes first. Then Cinderhatch. Everyone you have ever smuggled, everyone who ever bought your silence or sold you bread, everyone who knelt to Marchen and everyone who spat at her — they go down into the dark, all of them, and the dark closes over, and no song brings any of it back.*

"There has to be another way," Ansel said.

*There is not.* Flat. Final. *Believe me. Your mother searched for one. She was cleverer than you and she searched for years before she chose the fire. There are exactly two doors out of this room, beast-speaker, and I will show you both.*

The darkness shifted, and Ansel felt rather than saw the first door open: a cold wind rising from cracks in the floor, carrying the smell of deep stone and ending. *Door the first: you walk away. Take your wolf-ways and your smuggler roads and whatever scraps of your memory I haven't eaten, climb back into the starlight, and let the mountain do what unbound mountains do. Perhaps some few will flee in time. Most will not. But your hands will be clean, and clean hands are famously comfortable in the coffin.*

"That's not fair," Ansel whispered.

*Nothing is fair. Fair is a story mothers tell. I had a mother once too, if you can believe it.* For a moment — just a moment — the vast voice thinned, and underneath it Ansel heard something old and tired and terribly lonely. Then it hardened again. *Door the second: you stay. Bind yourself to this court as its voice, as its warden, as the hand that renegotiates the pact your mother died patching. Give me a speaker who can talk to beasts and hollow things both, and we write a new covenant — not stolen souls, not burned witches, but something honest, if your kind remembers how to be honest. The mountain holds. The cities hold. The stars may even return, if the pact is strong enough to reach them.*

"And the price?"

The Queen was silent for a long moment.

*Part of you lives here forever. Part of you hears this song always, in waking and sleeping, until you die. You will never again be wholly in the sunlit world. You will know the hunger of the hollow places from the inside, and you will spend the rest of your life deciding, day after day, what gets fed and what gets spared — and some days you will decide wrong, and you will carry that too.* The eyes dimmed, gentled. *I will not pretend it is a small thing. Your mother refused this door. She said no voice should belong only to one side. She may have been right. But she is ash, and the pact needs a voice, and you sing.*

Ansel stood in the lantern-light and felt the weight of it settle onto her shoulders like a yoke measured precisely to her.

Two doors. No third.

Except —

She thought of her mother's face in the Ledger pages. Thought of the lullaby she'd sung to a soul-flame that couldn't remember her. Thought of wolves carrying cloth through the dark because something in them understood grief. Thought of blind bats mapping tunnels with song, of a bear turning against armed men because she'd asked, of every beast in Thornfell coming down through stone and root when she called — not commanded, *asked*. Asked, and answered.

Her mother hadn't found a third door because her mother had been looking for a door a single person could walk through alone.

Ansel Quintana had never done anything alone in her life. She'd just spent twenty years pretending otherwise.

"I have a counteroffer," she said.

Marchen barked a laugh from the floor. "Oh, this is marvelous. It's going to negotiate with the starving god. Truly, the free cities deserve their fate—"

"Shut up, Veyra." It came out automatically, the way you'd hush a dog, and to Ansel's mild surprise, Marchen actually shut up.

*Speak,* said the Queen.

"Not tonight. Not standing in your wreckage with the mountain groaning and half my mind still reeking of smoke. If I bind myself to you — if I give you my voice, my song, the rest of my life — then I do it awake and unhurried, with terms written down where both sides can read them, the way any decent smuggler seals a contract. And I want one night first." Her throat tightened. "There's someone freed from your Ledger who used to know a lullaby. I'd like to remind him of the second verse."

The Queen regarded her for a long, long moment. The lanterns flickered. Deep beneath them, the mountain groaned and held, groaned and held — a patience matched against a patience.

*A night,* the Queen said at last. *You drive a harder bargain than your mother, little singer. She only ever offered herself. You offer yourself and then ask for time to think about it.*

"Family trait."

Something moved through the dark then that took Ansel a heartbeat to recognize, because nothing in the stories had ever suggested the Hollow Court was capable of it.

The Queen laughed. It was low and vast and rusty as an unused gate, and it made the bone pillars hum.

*Go, then. One night. He waits by the cold pool in the eastern gallery — he has been asking for you since the fire set him free, though he didn't know your name until the flames gave it back.* The great eyes dimmed, and the voice softened to something almost tender. *Sing him the second verse, beast-speaker. It has been three hundred years since anyone sang anything down here that wasn't a plea or a ledger-entry. I had forgotten verses existed.*

Ansel turned. Odile was watching her with an expression caught between awe and exasperation — the expression, Ansel realized, of a woman trying very hard to remember why the stranger in front of her mattered, and failing, and trusting her anyway.

"Well?" Odile said. "Do I follow you, whoever-you-are? Because my feet seem to have decided already, and I hate when they do that without consulting me."

"Yeah," Ansel said, and her voice cracked, and she let it. "Follow me."

Behind them, in the dark, the mountain groaned. Above them, through miles of stone, ten thousand people slept unknowing under a sky still black of stars. One night. One song. And then the hardest bargain of her life — if she survived to strike it.

Ansel took her sister-of-convenience's arm and walked toward the eastern galleries, toward the cold pool, toward a brother who was learning her name for the second time.

* * *

The eastern gallery smelled of mineral water and old quiet. The cold pool lay in a basin of veined marble, reflecting lantern-light that had no source, and beside it sat a young man with his knees drawn up, turning a smooth stone over in his hands as though checking whether it was real.

He looked up when her footsteps echoed. His face did the thing faces do when recognition arrives ahead of memory — a brightening, a confusion, a reaching.

"You," he said slowly. "You're — the songs. There were songs. Someone sang them and I dreamed of a kitchen, and bread burning, and a woman laughing at us both—"

"Tomas," Ansel said.

And just like that, the name landed, and twenty years fell off him all at once, and he was on his feet with his arms open before either of them could think better of it.

They held each other over the cold pool while the mountain groaned above and the stars waited, somewhere far away, to see what the morning would bring.

"Ansel," he said into her hair, testing it, keeping it. "Your name is Ansel. I'm never losing it again."

"No," she said. "You're not. Nobody is. Not anymore."

Above them, faint as breath, the first crack of dawn began working its way down through the stone — and with it, fainter still, something that might have been the sky remembering how to shine.
