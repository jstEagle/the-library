## 31. Tomas Remembers

The first thing I remembered was the smell of rain on slate.

Not my name — that came later, arriving like a guest who had been knocking for twenty years while I'd forgotten the house had a door. Not my mother's face, which came after that, in pieces, like a mosaic reassembling itself by candlelight. First it was rain on slate, the roof of our house in Cinderhatch, and a small girl's voice singing badly and with tremendous conviction two rooms away.

Ansel.

The name arrived, and everything else came crashing in behind it like water through a broken weir: the orchard behind our house, the crooked apple tree that bore fruit only every other year and made up for it with apples the size of fists; the game we played counting stars from the rooftop because our mother claimed you could tell fortunes by which ones blinked; the debt-collectors who came when I was nine, and the ledger they carried, and the way our mother's hands went still in her lap when she saw the family seal at the bottom of the page she was asked to sign.

And then — the Hollow Court. The sale. The flame.

I opened eyes I had not used in two decades, because souls in the Ledger do not need eyes, and looked at my hands.

They were not hands, exactly. They were the suggestion of hands, drawn in lantern-light and held there by will. But they were mine, and they were shaking.

"Careful," said a voice nearby. "It comes back all at once. Like falling down stairs in reverse."

I turned. A woman sat cross-legged on the cathedral floor beside me — Odile Ferran, though I didn't know her name yet. She had the look of someone who had been drinking for years as a professional commitment rather than a pleasure, and the further look of someone who had recently lost something enormous and was deciding whether to grieve or burn something down. Behind her stood another woman, half-lit, half-shadowed, with ash where parts of her should have been. He — Sef, though again, I knew nothing yet — watched me with an expression I would come to understand: the look of a man seeing someone walk out of a fire he'd lived in so long he'd forgotten it was hot.

But none of them mattered. None of them could have mattered less.

Because standing over me, lantern raised, face wet, was my little sister, and she was singing.

*Hush now, hush, the mountain's deep,*
*but deeper still's the dark we keep—*

"You're flat," I said.

Ansel stopped mid-verse. Her mouth did something complicated — the beginning of a laugh, the middle of a sob, and an ending she gave up trying to control entirely.

"You're *talking,*" she said.

"You're flat," I repeated, because some things are sacred and must be attended to first. "You were always flat. Mother said the goats sang better, and the goats were being polite about it."

"Oh — oh, you *ass*—" And then she dropped the lantern, which rolled, thankfully unbroken, throwing mad shadows across the bone-white pillars of the Hollow Court, and fell to her knees and grabbed me, and her arms went through me once, twice, and then found their grip on the third try, and my sister who had spent her whole life learning how to hold herself perfectly still held on to me hard enough to hurt if either of us had been entirely real.

We stayed like that for a long time. Somewhere above us, the court murmured and rustled — thousands of soul-flames burning in their niches, the courtiers drifting through the galleries, the vast patient hunger of the Queen pressing down on everything like deep water. It is difficult to explain what it is like to weep in that place. The Hollow drinks sorrow; it is currency here. But this sorrow it could not touch, because it wasn't being offered. It was just mine.

* * *

Here is what I remembered, in the order it returned:

I remembered being nine, and proud of it, because nine seemed terribly grown-up at the time. I remembered the collectors coming in their grey coats, and Mother signing the pledge with a steady hand, and afterward taking me aside and explaining, in the calm voice she used for important things, that the family owed a debt and that debts to the Hollow Court were paid in one coin only, and that she had arranged matters so that the coin would be hers.

Except she hadn't. That was the thing I understood only now, whole, with the Ledger's truth laid open in front of me. She had signed a pledge intending to pay it herself. But the Court had taken me instead. There had been a clause — there is always a clause — about collateral, about the youngest bearer of the bloodline, and Mother had not read far enough because she was grieving and hurried and human. Or perhaps she had read it, and believed love could out-argue ink. I would never know which. Both possibilities broke my heart in different shapes.

I remembered the descent. Cold stone under bare feet. A voice asking me my name, and me giving it, because I was nine and had been taught that lying was wrong, and the sensation of the name being lifted out of me like a splinter drawn whole.

After that — nothing, and then everything, and no way to tell the difference. Souls in the Ledger burn without burning down. You are fuel and fire both, forever, and you forget you ever were anything else. Twenty years. My sister had been eleven when I last saw her. She was a woman now, weathered and wary, with a scar through one eyebrow and a way of scanning every room for exits that I recognized with a sick lurch as our mother's trick, learned in a harder school than Mother ever attended.

"I looked for you," Ansel said. We were sitting against a pillar now, shoulder to shoulder, watching the soul-flames gutter and sway. "Twenty years. Every hollow, every den, every place the old stories said sold souls go. I thought — I don't know what I thought. That you'd be somewhere. Waiting."

"I was waiting," I said. "That was the whole problem. That's all a soul in a ledger does."

She laughed, and then pressed the back of her hand to her mouth as if the laugh had betrayed her.

"I found your wolf," she said, quieter. "Or it found me. It died getting your cloth to me. The crest. Your name."

"Brannan," I said.

She stared at me.

"The wolf. His name was Brannan. He ran messages between the upper galleries before the court grew too hungry to feed its runners." I smiled, and felt my borrowed face ache with it. "He bit a courtier once. A terrible one. I've missed him for six years."

Something in Ansel's face cracked open, and I realized she had been carrying that wolf's death like a debt of her own. "He knew you," she whispered. "At the end. He carried your name like it was — like it mattered."

"It did matter. Names are the only wealth down here, Ansel. Everything else is ash and appetite." I looked at my translucent hands. "Which brings us to the part I'm going to say once, clearly, and then never mention again, so listen well."

"Don't." She already knew. Of course she already knew; she'd spent twenty years preparing for this and I'd spent twenty minutes remembering it, but sisters have a way of arriving at the same door from opposite directions.

"They told me," I said. "Sef and the ward-keeper. What it costs to take a soul out of the Ledger. Equal weight, freely given." I took her hand — or put mine near hers, which is the closest approximation this place allows — and my voice, which had been steady through everything else, wobbled like a bad roof in wind. "Ansel. I was nine. I got to be nine. I got apples off the crooked tree and star-fortunes and a mother who sang worse than you, which nobody thought possible. I got nineteen more minutes of childhood than most souls in this place. You don't get to spend whatever you're planning to spend on twenty more years of me. You don't get to pay for me *again.* Do you hear me? Once was already too many times for one family."

"No," Ansel said.

"It wasn't even your debt!"

"No," she said again, with the particular mildness I remembered from every argument she had ever won, which was all of them. "It wasn't. That's exactly why it's mine to settle. Debts you owe can chain you. Debts other people paid for you — those you carry. I've been carrying you for twenty years, Tomas. Let me put you down or let me carry you properly, but don't ask me to set you down in *here.*"

"That's manipulation," I said. "That's emotional manipulation and you learned it from Mother."

"I learned it from you."

"I was nine!"

"And apparently very effective." But she was smiling, wetly, and I was smiling, and for a moment neither of us pushed, because we both understood that this argument would not be won tonight by either of us, and that some arguments you agree to lose slowly together.

* * *

Odile Ferran brought us food, which surprised me until I remembered that smugglers' guides pack for contingencies, and "rescue my brother's soul" had presumably been filed somewhere between "rockslide" and "bad weather." Dried apricots. Hard cheese. A heel of bread that tasted, to a tongue barely more than a rumor, like the entire concept of home.

"Your sister," Odile said, jerking her chin at Ansel, "talks in her sleep. Did you know that? Whole conversations. Arguing with somebody named Marta about toll rates."

"Marta Oves ran the eastern roads," I said. "Ansel, you argued with *Marta?* She once threw a man off a bridge for shorting her three coppers."

"He survived," Ansel said.

"He *swam away,*" Odile corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is why I'm telling this story."

Later Sef Andrade sat with us — at a careful distance, the way a man sits when he has forgotten how to be included and doesn't want to presume. He was mostly shadow and courtesy. He asked me questions about the upper galleries, about the courtiers' rotations, about which wardens still took bribes and which took offense, and I answered what I could, and it gradually dawned on me, watching him watch Ansel, that this ash-colored stranger had decided my sister was worth dying for and had not mentioned it to anyone, least of all her.

"She gets that from Mother," I told him quietly, when Ansel had gone to speak with Odile. "The stubbornness. The walking into fires."

"I know," Sef said. "I mean — I've seen enough of her to guess." He turned his incomplete hands over. "Your mother. Was she really burned for beast-speaking?"

"That's the story down here too?"

"It's the story everywhere."

"Then it's probably not true," I said. "In my experience the stories everyone tells are always covering for the ones nobody does."

He laughed — a rusty sound, like a gate that hadn't swung in years. "You're going to be trouble."

"I'm nine," I said. "Trouble is my entire portfolio."

"You're twenty-nine, Tomas."

I considered this. "I'm nine," I said firmly, "with twenty years of interest."

* * *

When the court's long dim cycle turned toward what passed for night — the lanterns banking low, the galleries going quiet, the Queen's vast attention settling into something like slumber at the root of everything — Ansel and I climbed to a ledge above the great hall, the way we used to climb to the roof in Cinderhatch.

"We used to count stars up there," she said.

"There are no stars here."

"I know." She was quiet a moment. "I counted anyway. Every night since I got here. Out of spite."

So we counted nothing, together, pointing at the empty dark overhead and assigning each invisible point of light its fortune. *That one means money. That one means travel. That one means you'll finally admit the goats sang better.*

Then we sang.

All of them. The lullaby, obviously, twice, once straight and once in the deliberately awful harmony we invented at ages seven and five that made Mother threaten to move to a different house. The road songs the caravans sang, which Ansel knew in versions with substantially more swearing than the ones I remembered. The song about the miller's wife and the talking cat that we had both independently forgotten the ending to, and resolved, as we had resolved at ages eight and six, to simply make up a new ending and pretend it was always thus.

My voice was thin. Hers was flat. We sounded, together, like two buckets being dragged over gravel, and it was the finest music ever performed in the Hollow Court, and several drifting courtiers paused in the shadows to listen, and one of them — I am almost certain — wept, if ash can weep, which I now believe it can.

Halfway through the third round of the lullaby, Ansel stopped.

"Tomas. When the Queen spoke to you — when the battle woke her, and she offered everyone their deepest want. What did she offer you?"

I had been waiting for this question, and dreading it, because the answer was shameful and true.

"Nothing," I said.

"What?"

"She offered me nothing. Everyone else heard their heart's desire — Odile said she heard her oaths renewed, half the soldiers heard forgiveness. Me, she just..." I searched for it. "She looked at me. However you look at a soul-flame. And she moved on. Like I was already spoken for."

Ansel went very still. "Because you were in the Ledger. Because you were already — property."

"Maybe. Or maybe she knows something I don't about what happens next." I took her hand again, more successfully this time. "Listen to me. Whatever you're turning over in that stubborn head of yours — the deal Marchen offered, the choice the Queen will put to you, whatever Sef and Odile haven't told me about yet but absolutely have told each other within earshot, because neither of them can whisper — I want you to hear this from me plainly."

"Tomas—"

"*Plainly,* Ansel." And the strangest thing happened then: my borrowed voice steadied, and I felt, for the first time in twenty years, like the older brother. Nine years old and dead and translucent, and still somehow the older brother, because somebody had to be. "You have spent your whole life paying for other people. For Mother's secret, for the smugglers' secrets, for every beast you helped and every friend you covered for. You are the best payer-of-debts I have ever met, and it has cost you everything, and tonight, sitting here with you, I finally remember enough to know that Mother would be furious about it. Not proud. *Furious.* She signed that pledge thinking she could keep it from touching either of us. If she could see what it cost instead, she'd climb down here herself and give the Queen a piece of her mind, and then she'd take you aside and tell you that love that keeps score isn't love, it's accounting."

"That's very moving," Ansel said thickly, "for a nine-year-old."

"I told you. Interest." I bumped my insubstantial shoulder against hers. "Whatever you choose — and I know, I *know* you're going to choose something enormous and self-sacrificing because you are physically incapable of choosing anything else — choose it because it's right, not because it pays. Promise me that much. Not that you'll be safe. Not that you'll spare yourself. Just that the ledger stays closed between us. No more debts. From tonight, everything between us is a gift."

For a long moment she didn't answer. Above us the empty dark hung heavy with everything the court wanted from her and everything she was willing to become.

"One more song," she said at last. "And then I have to go talk to the Queen."

"What are you going to tell her?"

"I don't know yet." She began the lullaby, softly, and for once — for the very first time in recorded history — she was not flat. Perhaps the dead sing truer. Perhaps it was the company. "But I think," she said between verses, "I've just thought of a third door."

Far below us, at the root of the world, the Hollow Queen opened one vast eye — and for the first time in a thousand years, she looked *afraid.*
