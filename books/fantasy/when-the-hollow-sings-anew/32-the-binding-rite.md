## 32. The Binding Rite

The Hollow Queen's question hung in the dark like a held breath.

*Destroy us and bury two cities, or bind yourself to us. There is no third door.*

Ansel had spent her whole life looking for third doors. Smugglers learned early that every wall had a crack in it, every locked gate a tired guard, every "impossible" passage a toll that somebody simply hadn't thought to pay yet. Her mother had died finding one such door — had walked into fire to seal it, which was its own kind of answer, though Ansel hadn't understood that until three days ago, reading the old pages of the Ledger with ash on her fingers.

So she looked up at the Queen — vast as weather, patient as stone, starving since before Ansel's grandmother's grandmother drew breath — and said, "You're right. There is no third door."

The Queen's attention settled on her like snowfall.

"There's only the question of who builds the next one," Ansel finished, "and what we make it out of."

A sound moved through the court then, through the bone pillars and lantern-light and the thousand small flames of freed souls drifting like dandelion seeds. It might have been laughter. In the Hollow Court, it was hard to tell laughter from hunger, though Ansel suspected that distinction was exactly what she'd just volunteered to fix.

*Speak,* the Queen said, into every mind at once, gently this time, the way Odile described deep water pressing rather than drowning. *I have waited four hundred years. I can wait while a small creature arranges her terms.*

"Small creature," Ansel muttered. "Right." She turned to look at the others.

Odile stood near the ruined plinth where the Ledger had burned, still in her scorched ward-keeper's coat, her hands trembling with the aftershock of the rite she'd barely survived. Half her memories were scattered somewhere in the court's ledgers-of-old, paid out to buy knowledge she'd already used. She didn't know Ansel's name anymore. But she'd insisted on being here anyway, because — as she'd put it, gripping Ansel's arm with the fierce familiarity of someone who trusted her body's memory even when her mind couldn't follow — *"I don't know who you are, girl, but my bones say I'd burn for you, so let's get this over with."*

Tomas stood beside her. Her brother. Twenty years sold, one night restored, his soul wearing borrowed light in place of flesh until the freed souls' gratitude could knit him something more permanent. He was watching her with an expression she remembered from childhood — the face he made when she climbed somewhere too high. Fear and pride, fighting for the same window.

And Sef — no. Sef was gone. He'd stayed behind under the collapsing gallery with two hundred civilians above him and held it long enough for every last one to walk out. They'd found his name scratched into the stone afterward: not *Courtier of Ash*, not a ledger number. *Sef Andrade.* He'd chosen it at the end. Ansel carried that name now the way she carried her mother's lullaby — as fuel.

She would build the next door out of that, too.

"Here are my terms," she said, and her voice rang strangely in the cathedral of bone, and she realized distantly that it wasn't entirely her voice anymore. Part of it belonged to the stones. That was going to take some getting used to. "One. The court will never again take a memory that isn't freely given. Not bought with lies, not extracted by desperation dressed up as choice. Freely given, or not at all."

*And when the free cities decide giving is easier than fighting?*

"Then that's their failure, and yours to refuse. You're allowed to be hungry, Your Majesty. You're not allowed to be a thief."

Something vast shifted in the dark. The Queen's amusement or her anger — again, hard to tell.

"Two," Ansel pressed on. "The wardens of the pact will not be hollow things alone. The beasts of Thornfell will stand watch alongside the ward-keepers. Wolves at the thresholds. Bears at the gates. The bats—" she paused, and permitted herself a small, exhausted smile, "—the bats keep the maps."

Above them, faint through miles of stone, something howled in acknowledgment. Ansel felt it land in her chest like a hand on a shoulder. The wolves had come down for her. The bears had bled for her. The blind bats had sung her through the drowned stair when her eyes were useless. If the court was going to be fed, it would be guarded by creatures who understood hunger without being consumed by it.

*They are wild,* the Queen said. *They do not kneel.*

"No," Ansel agreed. "Neither do I. That's rather the point."

* * *

Negotiating with the Hollow Queen, it turned out, was less like diplomacy and more like arguing with a glacier that occasionally asked very good questions.

Ansel laid out the rest in the hours that followed, pacing the length of the great hall while Tomas sat on the steps of the empty throne — the empty throne! — swinging his legs like he was nine years old again, which Ansel chose to find endearing rather than deeply alarming.

Memory, freely given, would sustain the court. Not enough to make it fat — she was firm on that — but enough to end the starvation that had driven four centuries of theft. In exchange, the court kept the star-pact intact, held the mountain's root steady, anchored the cities above. The ward-keepers would be restored to their oaths, their order rebuilt, their compasses unbroken. Odile, hearing this, cried briefly and then claimed it was dust from the Ledger fire, which everyone politely pretended to believe.

"And the memories people give," Tomas said quietly, when the terms were nearly settled. "What happens to them?"

"They're kept," Ansel said. "Not eaten. Kept." She'd thought about this during the long walk from the root of the mountain back to the throne hall, one hand trailing the wall, listening to the stones hum. "Grief someone wants to set down. Joy someone wants kept safe forever. The court becomes..." She groped for the word. "A vault. A library. A place where the worst day of your life can be put down and picked up again later, lighter."

"A reliquary," Odile said.

"A pantry," said the Queen, at the same moment.

Everyone looked at each other.

"We'll workshop it," Ansel said.

But the hardest term came last, and she saved it deliberately, because she knew the Queen would try to slip past it the way hunger slips past any fence.

"I set the price," Ansel said. "Me. Whatever the court takes from me — my memories, my years, whatever binding costs — I decide what's given. No one else pays for my family. Not again." Her voice caught on the last word, and she let it. There was no shame left in grief; her mother had taught her that, posthumously, through ash and old paper. "That's not negotiable."

*You would hold power,* the Queen observed, *and immediately leash it.*

"I watched Veyra Marchen do the opposite," Ansel said. "It went badly for everyone. Also—" and here she smiled, thin and real, "—my mother bound herself once, alone, in secret, and it cost her everything because nobody knew. I'm doing it in front of witnesses, with lawyers." She gestured at Odile. "Sort of. Ward-keepers count as lawyers. It's in the oath."

"It is not," said Odile.

"It should be."

* * *

The rite itself took place at dawn, if dawn could be said to exist four hundred feet underground. The court measured time differently — by the pulse of the lantern-souls, by the slow breathing of the mountain — but Ansel insisted on dawn, because her mother had sealed her crack at dawn, and symmetry mattered. Odile agreed. "All the good magic happens at dawn or dusk," she said. "Midnight is for amateurs and poets."

They gathered in the throne hall. The Queen coiled herself into something almost conversational in scale — a darkness the size of a chapel instead of a cathedral, which was apparently her version of dressing down. The beasts had come down in force: wolves lining the gallery edges with their eyes like banked coals, the great bear from the border sitting at the hall's entrance with the patience of a landslip, bats hanging from the bone rafters in soft gray clusters, singing their clicking map-song low and constant, blessing the space in their own language.

Tomas stood at her left. Odile at her right, holding the reconstructed fragment of the original star-pact, copied out in her own careful hand because the original was ash now and somebody had to remember the wording exactly.

"You don't have to memorize anything," Odile had told her. "The binding doesn't care if you understand it. It cares if you mean it."

"I mean it," Ansel said.

"I know," Odile said. "That's the terrifying part."

The rite was simpler than Ansel expected, and harder. Words, spoken in both tongues — human speech and the stone-song that lived half in her throat now. Blood, one drop, on the root-stone. And then the giving: the court reaching into her, gently, so much more gently than the toll-gates had ever been, and asking.

*What will you give?*

Ansel had prepared a list. She'd sat up half the night with Tomas making it, and he'd argued with her about several entries, and they'd laughed until they cried about the time he'd pushed her into the millpond and she'd gotten a leech stuck to her ear, and eventually she'd struck some items and added others, and the list was hers, chosen, priced, fair.

"The afternoon I was seven," she said aloud. "When nothing happened. The whole nameless golden afternoon by the river." She felt it lift out of her like a leaf off current — and land somewhere safe, shelved, kept. It hurt. It also didn't. Both things were true, and she was beginning to understand that both-true was the whole grammar of the Hollow.

"My grandmother's laugh. I never recorded it right anyway."

"The smell of the first tavern I ever smuggled through. Honestly, the court's welcome to it."

Each gift settled into the dark like a coin into a well, and each one came back changed — not gone, but *held*. She could feel them down there, warm, part of the architecture now. When she was an old woman, if she was allowed to be one, she could visit her own seventh summer. Walk through it. Sit by the river again.

It wasn't losing everything. It was putting things somewhere safer than a skull.

*And the last?* the Queen asked, when the list was done. *There is always a last. The binding requires one thing you would rather keep.*

Ansel had known this was coming. She'd argued with Tomas about this entry longest of all.

"The lullaby," she said. "Our mother's song. The one that woke the stars." She sang the first line — just the first line — and the whole court stilled, bats and wolves and Queen alike, because the melody was woven through the star-pact itself, older than the Ledger, older than the hunger.

"I give you the song," she said. "But I give it as a *duet*. Every time it's sung — by me, by anyone — the court sings the harmony. So neither of us ever does it alone again."

Silence. Long silence, of the kind only very large beings can produce.

Then the Queen laughed — actually laughed, a sound like a mountain deciding spring has come — and Ansel felt the pact close around her like a coat being put onto her shoulders from the inside. Not a cage. A coat. Warm in places she hadn't known were cold.

*Accepted,* the Queen said. *All terms. Witnessed by fang and flame and the keeper's hand.*

"Witnessed," said Odile, and her voice cracked on the word, and she pressed her stamped seal — the Ferran mark, disgraced no longer — against the root-stone.

"Witnessed," said Tomas, and his voice was whole, and his name was his own.

The lanterns flared. Somewhere far above, Ansel felt the first star catch light — felt it the way you feel a note struck on a piano in another room, vibration more than sound — and the mountain sighed beneath her feet, settling, held.

* * *

Afterward, there was the extremely undignified business of Ansel trying to stand up and discovering that binding yourself to an ancient subterranean court leaves your legs about as reliable as wet rope.

"I've got you." Tomas caught her elbow. Up close, his borrowed light had softened into something almost like skin. "Twenty years of watching you from inside a ledger, and the first thing I get to do is catch you falling over. Worth the wait."

"Shut up," she said fondly.

"You're glowing, you know. Literally. There's a faint gold — all along your—"

"If you say 'aura,' I'm revoking your sibling privileges."

"Your *sclera*, Ansel. It's very dramatic. Mother would have loved it."

At the name — *Mother*, spoken freely, in the open, by someone who remembered her face — Odile turned away quickly and became very interested in repacking her satchel. Ansel squeezed her hand as they passed. Odile didn't know why this stranger kept doing that. But she squeezed back, because her bones told her to.

The walk out took two days. Wolves escorted them to the drowned stair; the bats sang them up through the flooded dark, and this time Ansel understood every note of the map-song, heard the harmony underneath it, heard — faintly, always now, forever — the court's own slow song beneath the stone, like a heart in the floor of the world.

They emerged near Cinderhatch on the second evening, into air so clean it made her dizzy.

And the sky —

Odile stopped dead on the scree slope, satchel slipping from her shoulder, and made a sound Ansel had never heard from her before, small and undone. Above the Thornfell peaks, where blackness had lived for weeks, the stars were returning. Not all at once. One, then another, then a scatter of them, like embers breathed back to life, like a city lighting its windows after a long war.

Tomas sat down right where he stood, in the dirt, and tipped his head back, and didn't say anything at all.

Ansel stood between her brother and her friend and her wolves, half-lit by stars she could feel humming in her new hollow places, and listened. Below, in Cinderhatch, someone was shouting. Then more voices. Then bells — actual bells, ragged and joyful, the alarm-bells repurposed on the spot into celebration. Refugees pouring out of shelters, pointing up, weeping at lights they'd forgotten existed.

Then the shouting changed.

Because down the smuggler road, dust rising behind it, came a column of soldiers under no banner anyone trusted — Marchen's army, surrendered or splintered or simply lost, filtering toward the free towns like water finding low ground. And riding ahead of them, alone, carrying a white cloth and a face like a woman arriving at her own trial, came a herald bearing a message addressed, by name and seal, to *the Voice of the Hollow Court*.

Odile looked at Ansel. Tomas looked at Ansel. Even the wolf looked at Ansel.

"Well," Ansel said, straightening her coat, feeling the stars hum and the mountain hold and the whole enormous weight of what came next settle onto her shoulders like the pact itself. "I suppose we'd better go tell them the news."
