## 33. Stars Return

Odile Ferran had spent thirty-one years as a ward-keeper, and in all that time she had never once been asked to simply *wait*. Waiting was not a skill the oaths covered. There were rites for sealing, rites for mending, rites for the solemn and ceremonial drinking of terrible tea with other ward-keepers while pretending your colleague's new apprentice wasn't insufferable. But there was no rite for standing on a mountainside with your hands empty, watching a crack in the world, and hoping someone else was doing the work.

So she waited badly.

She stood at the mouth of the drowned stair with perhaps four hundred souls crowded around her — smugglers and shepherds, Marchen's surrendered soldiers still looking dazed about it, a woman holding a goat that had refused all reasonable suggestions of leaving — and every one of them was looking up at her as if she knew something.

She didn't. She knew exactly one thing: somewhere beneath their feet, Ansel Quintana was negotiating with a starving god, and Odile had promised not to follow her down.

The promise had been the hardest sentence she'd ever said aloud, and she'd once read the full terms of a ward-renewal contract at a funeral without flinching.

"Keeper Ferran." The speaker was a broad-shouldered woman in the gray of Cinderhatch's militia, one of the ones who'd knelt for Marchen's herald and then, when the truth came out, knelt again — this time to help evacuate the children. Her name was Petra. She had been asking Odile questions for six hours with the tireless patience of a sheepdog. "The ground's stopped shaking. Does that mean—"

"It means," Odile said, "that either it's working, or we're about to find out what happens when a mountain finishes deciding."

"That's not reassuring."

"I'm a ward-keeper, not a comfort. The two professions diverged centuries ago."

Petra almost smiled. That was the trick of it, Odile had found — you couldn't give people hope directly, hope being a thing that curdled when handled, but you could give them dry remarks and they'd make hope out of them themselves, the way birds made nests out of string and spite.

Above them the sky was still wrong. It had been wrong since the night without stars — not black, exactly, but *unwritten*, a blankness that made the eye slide off it the way a tongue slides along a missing tooth. The moon was gone too. People had stopped talking about it out loud because saying it made it worse, but everyone looked up anyway, over and over, the way you check a wound.

Odile looked up too. She'd earned the right; she'd paid for it in pieces. That was the arithmetic nobody warned you about — that memory spent in the dark did not come back with the dawn, even when the dawn came back. Somewhere below her, in a cathedral of bone and lantern-light, there was a shelf with her name on it, and on that shelf sat her mother's laugh, the smell of the first ward-house she'd served in, and — this was the one that stung — the entire plot of a novel she'd been reading the night the stars went out. She would never know how it ended. Some losses were small enough to be unbearable.

But she remembered her oaths. She remembered the rite she'd bled for. And she remembered Ansel Quintana's face in the last hour before the binding — hollow-cheeked, lit by soul-flame, more certain than any person had a right to be.

"She'll do it," Odile said, to no one in particular.

"You keep saying that," Petra said.

"Because repetition is the mother of belief, and belief is the only material I have left to build with."

* * *

The waiting had texture. That was the strange thing. Odile catalogued it the way she'd once catalogued ward-signatures, because cataloguing kept the fear in labeled boxes where it belonged.

There was the sound: a deep, slow groaning from the mountain's bones that came and went like breathing, and between breaths a silence so complete you could hear the goat chewing. There was the light: lanterns and cookfires and one absurd, defiant bonfire the smugglers had built from crates of brandy they'd decided, in the end, they could live without. And there was the feeling — the pressure everyone had described since descending began, that deep-water weight of the court's hunger — thinning. Easing. Like a hand unclenching finger by finger around the whole valley.

At some point the beasts arrived.

They came down through the treeline without ceremony: wolves first, moving in that loose, purposeful way wolves have when they're on an errand rather than a hunt, then bears, then a scatter of deer and foxes and one enormous lynx that sat down at the edge of the firelight like a magistrate attending a trial. The crowd flinched, collectively, and then did not flinch, because word had come up from below weeks ago — carried in a wolf's jaws, wrapped in cloth, in Ansel's own handwriting — that the beasts were coming, and that they were not to be feared, and that anyone who harmed one would answer personally to Odile Ferran, who had nothing left to lose and a great deal of pent-up energy regarding threats.

"They're listening," Odile said softly.

"To what?"

"To her." She crouched, slowly, and extended a hand toward the lynx — an old keeper's gesture, respectful, palm-down, *I am not reaching for anything of yours*. The lynx regarded her with yellow eyes that held, unmistakably, intelligence, and after a long moment dipped its head a fraction. "She told me once, before we went under, that she'd always heard them. That hiding it was like humming your whole life and pretending you couldn't sing." Odile's throat tightened. "I told her that was a foolish way to live. I'd like the record to show I was wrong."

"The record shows it," Petra said gravely. "I'll write it up."

* * *

It happened at dusk, because of course it did. Odile would later learn that the binding rite had concluded near midday, but the pact had insisted on dusk — the hinge of the day, the moment the old star-pact had originally been signed, some clerk of cosmic affairs having apparently insisted on symmetry.

The first star returned over the western peak of Thornfell.

It did not fade in. It did not flicker uncertainly, gathering itself. It simply *was*, a point of white fire punched through the unwritten sky, so sudden that several hundred people made the same involuntary sound — a gasp with a sob inside it — and the goat screamed.

Then a second star. Then three in a cluster, low in the south, and Odile recognized them — the Shepherd's Crook, the first thing every ward-keeper learned to orient by — and her knees nearly went. Then more, a scattering, a constellation, a *sky*: stars arriving one by one across the blackness like a city lighting its lamps at evening, each one a small detonation of light, and the crowd was crying now, openly, unashamed, grown men and hardened smugglers and the goat's owner, who had sunk to sitting in the dirt with her arms around the animal's neck, both of them staring upward.

"Oh," Odile said. Just that. Thirty-one years of professional composure, reduced to a single syllable.

She had forgotten — genuinely forgotten, though whether the forgetting was the court's theft or merely the ordinary erosion of years, she couldn't say — how much the stars had mattered. Not practically; navigation was the least of it. They had mattered the way a heartbeat matters: unnoticed until absent, and then the only thing in the world. Every ward she'd ever kept had been tuned against starlight. Every oath she'd sworn had begun with the old formula, *under the watching lights*. For weeks the whole sky had been a held breath, and now—

Now the world exhaled.

She felt it happen. That was the part no one expected, and she'd spend the rest of her life failing to describe it properly: a shudder running up through the soles of her boots and out the top of her head, a vast subterranean *release*, like a knot the size of a mountain range finally slipping loose. The wards — what remained of them, scarred and threadbare and patched by her own desperate hand — flared once, gently, all along their length, the way a sleeping dog stretches. And beneath her feet, faint but unmistakable, something sang.

Not words. A chord, maybe. Or many chords braided together — stone-deep and flame-bright and one voice, clear as a winter stream, threading through all of it. Ansel's voice. Odile knew it instantly, would have known it anywhere, because she'd spent months listening to that voice argue with bats.

"She's singing," Odile whispered. "Listen. She's — that's the pact-song. My grandmother hummed it. I thought it was just a lullaby." She laughed, wetly. "Everything important in this country is disguised as a lullaby. No wonder we never notice it going wrong."

The lynx tipped its head back and answered — a sound that was not quite a yowl and not quite music, rising to meet the song from below. Then a wolf, further up the slope. Then the bats, pouring from some high crag in a river of leather wings, their ultrasonic chatter catching the melody somehow, translating it into a frequency the bones could hear. The whole mountain became an instrument, and every creature on it a string.

Around the fires, people had gone quiet — the good quiet, the held-breath-before-the-vow quiet. And then, hesitantly, a shepherd's voice picked up the lullaby's refrain, the bit every Thornfell child knew without knowing where it came from, and others joined, raggedly, off-key, weeping and singing at the same time, which is the traditional human manner of it.

Petra was singing. The goat was making noise that was arguably singing. Odile found her own mouth moving around words she hadn't spoken in decades and discovered she still knew every one.

*Under the watching lights, the mountain keeps its word.*

*Under the watching lights, the hungry thing is heard.*

*Sing it down and sing it through, sing it soft and sing it true —*

*what was sold shall be returned; what was burned shall be relearned.*

By the last line the eastern horizon had caught up: stars over the low hills, stars above Cinderhatch's dark streets, and — Odile's breath caught — the moon. Thin, new, barely a fingernail of silver, but *there*, hanging in the repaired dark like a signature on a renewed contract.

She put her hands over her face and let herself shake for exactly as long as it took the song to end. Ward-keepers were permitted this, once per crisis. It was in the bylaws. Probably.

* * *

Word came up the stair before dawn, borne by Sef Andrade's young successor — a hollow-eyed, hollow-cheeked boy named Merrick who had volunteered for messenger duty on the grounds that he was "mostly see-through anyway, so the trip doesn't cost me much." He emerged blinking into true starlight for what Odile suspected was the first time in his altered life, and stood there swaying until Petra steadied him.

"Well?" Odile demanded. "Report. All of it. In order. Ward-keepers are very fond of order."

Merrick blinked at the sky a while longer, visibly filing it away to process later. "The Queen accepted the terms," he said. "Memory freely given, never stolen. The beasts stand ward alongside the keepers. The Ledger stays ash. The debt-books are — well." He smiled, and it was a strange smile, sweet and slightly terrible, the way all the half-hollow smiled. "There's rather a lot of paperwork. The Queen has opinions about proper documentation. She's very excited about the gift-gates."

"The Queen," Odile said, "is excited."

"You'd have to meet her."

"I'd rather not, thank you. What about Quintana?"

"She's climbing." Merrick pointed up-slope, toward the pass road. "She wouldn't take the stair. Said she wanted to walk out the front door for once. She asked us to tell you—" He frowned, concentrating. "She asked us to tell you that you owe her a drink, and that she remembers which tavern, and that if you try to claim you don't remember promising, she knows for a fact you still have that memory because it was too stubborn to sell."

Odile snorted. "That girl haggles like a fishwife even with gods. Fine. One drink." Then, quieter: "And her brother? Tomas?"

"Resting. The freed souls are — tending him. Building him back. It may take seasons; flesh is complicated." Merrick's gaze drifted to the stars again, helpless. "But he sang with her, Keeper. All the way up the stair. He has his name back. He said it out loud like he was tasting it." His voice wobbled. "I'd forgotten names could sound like that."

Odile clapped him on the shoulder — gently; the half-hollow bruised like overripe fruit — and sent him to the fire for soup, and stood alone for a moment at the stair's mouth, looking up at a sky that worked again.

Somewhere below, in the warm dark, a starving queen was learning what it was to be trusted instead of fed by force. Somewhere above, cities that had spent weeks braced for collapse were sleeping soundlessly, their foundations held by a contract rewritten in mercy. And somewhere on a mountain road between the two, a woman who had hidden her whole life was walking toward daylight, singing, half of her forever elsewhere.

Odile Ferran took out her flask — real brandy, hoarded for emergencies, and this qualified — poured a measure onto the stones for the dead who'd bought this morning, and drank the rest herself, to the living.

Then she shouldered her pack and started up the pass road to meet her friend, rehearsing under her breath the first thing she intended to say when Ansel crested the ridge:

*"You're late, and the tavern's closed, and I've decided you owe me two drinks now — one for the wait, and one for making me learn the words to a lullaby at my age."*

Behind her, unnoticed by anyone, the first pale edge of true dawn touched the peaks of Thornfell — and in the stones under her boots, faint as a heartbeat, something patient and vast and newly hopeful began, very quietly, to hum.
