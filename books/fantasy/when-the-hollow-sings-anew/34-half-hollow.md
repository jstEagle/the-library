## 34. Half-Hollow

The last stair out of the Hollow was the one nobody had ever bothered to name.

Ansel had climbed a thousand stairs in her life — smugglers' stairs cut into cliff faces, watch-stairs in Cinderhatch that groaned like old men complaining about weather, even the spiral stair inside the mountain's root where she had faced Veyra Marchen and spoken words that still tasted of iron and starlight. But this final flight, rising from the flooded gallery toward the mine shaft where it had all begun, had no name at all. It simply went up, and she followed it, because going up was what stairs were for, and because somewhere above it there was sky.

Her legs knew how to climb. That was something. After everything, her legs still worked.

What surprised her was the singing.

It wasn't coming from anywhere. Or rather, it was coming from everywhere — from the stone under her palms, from the water dripping somewhere behind her, from the marrow of her own bones. The Hollow Court's song, the vast slow chord that had once pressed on her like deep water, now lived *in* her. She heard it the way you hear your own heartbeat: always, whether you wanted to or not.

*Hello,* said the song, in its wordless way.

*Hello,* Ansel answered, and felt absurd doing it, and did it anyway.

Halfway up, she stopped on a landing barely wide enough for two people and leaned against the wall to catch her breath. The stone was warm. It had been warm since the binding rite — the whole mountain ran warmer now, like a body that had finally gotten enough sleep.

"You're talking to rocks," she told herself. "You're a woman who talks to rocks."

Well. She'd been that woman since she was six years old, whispering to marmots while her mother pretended not to notice. The difference was that the rocks talked back now, and they were very chatty, and one of them was technically her co-regent.

She laughed at that — actually laughed, alone on a dark staircase, which would have worried her a month ago but now just felt like proof she was still herself. Mostly.

Mostly was the important word.

---

She took inventory as she climbed, because taking inventory was what smugglers did, and whatever else she was now — voice of the Hollow Court, warden-adjacent, half-hollow, the woman who had renegotiated a pact older than the free cities — she had been a smuggler first, and smugglers counted their goods.

Item: her mother's face. Gone. She knew this the way you know an empty pocket; she reached for it and found only the shape of the reaching. She remembered that her mother had had a face, obviously, and that people had loved looking at it, and that it had crinkled at the corners when she smiled — but the face itself lived below now, in the court's keeping, one coin among thousands. Some nights, she suspected, some hollow thing was looking at it, learning what love looked like.

That didn't hurt the way she'd expected it to. That frightened her more than the grief would have.

Item: the summer she was nine. Gone entirely. There was a year-shaped gap in her childhood, and when she probed it, the edges were smooth, like a river stone. Tomas had told her what happened that summer — he'd been there too, before the selling, before everything — and his telling had been so vivid that she'd almost been able to borrow it back. Almost.

Item: a nameless afternoon. Just that. A single afternoon, unmarked, ordinary, that she had paid without ever knowing what it held. Sef had warned her the court always overcharged. He hadn't mentioned that the worst part wouldn't be losing what she lost — it would be not knowing what she'd lost. Somewhere below, in the lantern-light of the bone cathedral, her ninth summer and her mother's smile and one perfectly good afternoon sat in the court's treasury, and she would never be able to visit them without paying again.

Except.

Except that was the old economy. The stolen one. The rite had changed the terms, and terms mattered — Odile had drilled that into her often enough, drunk or sober, usually both.

"Freely given," Odile had rasped, gripping Ansel's arm with fingers surprisingly strong for a woman who smelled permanently of cheap plum brandy. "You made them promise. Freely given, freely kept, freely *returned*. You understand what you wrote, girl? Memories given can be given back."

Ansel had understood. She understood now, climbing, hearing the court hum against her ribs. The door swung both ways. Someday — when the treasury was full enough, when the new courtiers had learned their work — she could stand at those gates and ask for her mother's face home. And the court, bound by her own terms, would have to open its hand.

It wasn't freedom. But it was a door, and doors were what Ansel Quintana had spent her whole life finding where other people saw walls.

---

The light came gradually — first a gray thread above, then a pale wash, then the green smell of pine and wet moss and morning, real morning, the kind with weather in it.

She pulled herself out of the mine shaft into a world so loud with small sounds that she stopped dead and simply listened. Birds. Wind through the pines. A stream arguing with some stones downhill. Somewhere far off, the complaint of a goat who had strong opinions about breakfast.

And underneath it all, woven through it like a bass note under birdsong: the court's chord, steady and patient. The mountain's heartbeat. Her second heartbeat now.

A wolf was waiting for her at the treeline.

Not just any wolf — she recognized the gray muzzle, the torn ear, the particular way he sat like a judge who had already decided against everyone in the room. He had carried a dying kinsman down a mountain to find her, weeks ago, and changed the shape of her life with a scrap of cloth and a name.

"Tomas is well," she said. It came out as greeting, apology, and report all at once.

The wolf huffed. In the language of wolves-with-whom-Ansel-shared-a-pact, this meant approximately *I know, I can hear him singing from here, he has no sense of pitch whatsoever.*

"He gets that from Mother," Ansel said, then caught herself. *Mother.* Whose face she couldn't see. Whose lullaby she still remembered — that had survived, thank every star in the sky, though she'd paid enough other things that she checked on it nightly, like a farmer counting sheep after wolves.

She crouched and scratched the wolf behind his good ear. He permitted it, the way royalty permits commoners to breathe nearby.

"The cities held," she told him. "Cinderhatch, Rivermoor, the rest. The trembling stopped." She paused. "Marchen's army surrendered at the gates. Half of them wept. I'm told the other half are asking for jobs."

The wolf made a sound that might have been a laugh, if wolves laughed, which they didn't officially, but Ansel had spent a lifetime around beasts and knew better than official accounts.

They walked down together, wolf and half-hollow woman, through pines still standing, past wards — Odile's wards, rebuilt, humming *right* now, like bells that had finally been tuned — and toward the thin smoke of cookfires in the valley below.

---

Odile met her at the edge of Cinderhatch, arms crossed, wearing the closest thing to formal dress anyone had ever seen on her: a clean shirt, buttoned to the collar.

"Well," Odile said. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other usurper."

"Oh, I have. There's a painting going up in the town square. Marchen with little horns drawn on. Very tasteful. The artist charged double for the horns." Odile's eyes narrowed, and beneath the bluster Ansel saw the real question, the one the old ward-keeper couldn't ask straight because she'd forgotten how, in more ways than one. "Do you remember me?"

Ansel smiled. "Grumpy. Plum brandy. Oaths you keep reciting in your sleep. You taught me the ward-restoring rite twice because the first time I fell asleep, and you've never let me forget it."

Something in Odile's shoulders let go. "Huh. So the mountain didn't eat *all* of you."

"Just the good parts."

"Those were never in you to begin with, girl." But Odile's voice cracked on it, and she turned away quickly, pretending great interest in the town gate. When she spoke again, her voice was steady. "Tomas is at my house. Hasn't left it in three days except to argue with the baker about bread prices. Says the baker's a thief. Says it *loudly*."

"That sounds like my brother."

"It sounds like a man who got his name back three weeks ago and intends to use it on everybody." Odile glanced at her sideways. "He waited for you. Every day, he asked if you'd come up yet. Every day I told him souls don't climb fast. He didn't find it funny."

"He never does. It's his worst quality." Ansel's chest did something complicated. "Is he—"

"He's whole. Whatever else you're wondering — yes. Whole, and angry about it in all the right ways, and *waiting*, so stop stalling on my doorstep and go."

Ansel went.

---

Tomas was sitting on Odile's step, and when he saw her, he stood up so fast he knocked over a cup, and then neither of them moved for a long moment, two people across ten feet of dirt road and twenty years of sold and burned and buried things.

Then he crossed the distance and hugged her hard enough to creak ribs, and she hugged him back, and over his shoulder she noticed the neighbors pretending not to watch, and noticed that she didn't care, and noticed the court's song swell gently beneath everything, like a hand laid warm between her shoulder blades, and thought: *oh. It's happy for me.*

Her co-regent, the ancient starving dark beneath the world, was happy for her.

"I remember the lullaby," Tomas said into her hair, voice wrecked. "I remember you singing it to me when I was sick, the winter before—" He stopped. Started again. "I remember everything now. They gave it back when the Ledger burned. All of it. And I've been sitting here for three days knowing that you paid for me with pieces of yourself, and I have *so many things to say about that*, Ansel, so many, and I practiced speeches, I want you to know I practiced—"

"Tomas."

"—and every speech ends with me being furious at you, which is ridiculous, because I also missed you so much I thought I'd come apart, and I don't know how to hold both of those at once, nobody teaches you how to hold both—"

"*Tomas.*"

He pulled back and looked at her. His eyes were their mother's eyes. She couldn't see her mother's face anymore, but she could see it in his, refracted, and she decided that would do. It would have to do, and it did.

"Come inside," he said finally. "I saved you dinner. It's terrible. Odile cooked."

Behind them, Odile shouted something unrepeatable, and Tomas grinned — their mother's grin too — and Ansel laughed until her sides hurt, sitting on a doorstep in Cinderhatch with her brother and the smell of burnt stew and the stars coming out overhead one by one, steady as promises kept.

---

But that night, lying awake in Odile's spare room with the court's song humming its low contented chord beneath her thoughts, Ansel got up and went outside, because she needed to test something.

She walked to the edge of town, past the last lamplight, to where the Thornfell slopes rose black against the starred sky. Then she closed her eyes and listened — really listened, the way she'd learned to listen below.

There. Faint but unmistakable, threaded through the mountain's chord: her mother's face. Not seen, but *kept* — held safe, tended, like a candle someone watched over. And beside it, her ninth summer, bright as a jar of fireflies. And the nameless afternoon, whatever it had been, resting in the dark among treasures.

Not lost. *Deposited.*

She opened her eyes. Stars everywhere — the pact renewed, burning honest and steady, and somewhere among them the ones that had gone out and come back, which she liked to think of as hers.

She could leave. That was the miracle nobody had quite believed, least of all her. The binding didn't chain her to the deep places. She could walk the high passes, sail to cities she'd never seen, grow old in some sunny valley full of goats. The court would hum in her bones the whole time, patient as tide, but it would not pull. It had asked her to stay of her own will and she had said *yes, but on my terms*, and the terms held, because she had written them well and the Queen — starving, vast, and lonelier than anything alive — had signed them like a drowning woman grabbing a rope.

So Ansel Quintana stood at the edge of Cinderhatch under returning stars, half-hollow and wholly herself, and realized the strangest truth of all:

For the first time in her life, she wasn't hiding anything.

No gift concealed. No self split into the woman above and the beast-speaker beneath. Every secret she'd ever carried was either spoken aloud in front of armies or traded away to the dark, and what remained was just — her. A woman who talked to rocks. Who missed a face she couldn't picture. Who had a brother cooking terrible stew and a mountain singing in her chest and, come morning, a valley full of people who needed to hear exactly what had been done in their names, by whom, and why.

Tomorrow she would testify. Odile had the list of names ready — friends who'd nearly sold her, councilmen who'd knelt too fast, the baker (for separate crimes). It would be ugly. Truth usually was, before it started being useful.

Tonight, though, Ansel tipped her head back and sang four notes of her mother's lullaby into the dark — quietly, just to check — and felt the whole mountain answer, from the peak to the Hollow Court itself, a chord like a held breath released.

Far below, something vast and ancient and no longer starving sang back.

She smiled, and turned toward the fires of Cinderhatch, where her brother was probably reorganizing Odile's kitchen out of pure spite, and where tomorrow — with testimony, and justice, and a great deal of burnt stew — the rest of her life was waiting.
