## 35. Reckonings in Cinderhatch

Odile Ferran had spent thirty years keeping wards and drinking in roughly equal measure, and she could say without fear of contradiction that the tribunal was worse than either.

The Council of the Free Cities had taken over Cinderhatch's wool hall for the proceedings, which meant the town's largest building now smelled of ink, wet wool, and the particular desperation of clerks who had been awake for four days. Benches that had once held bolts of cloth now held delegates from six cities, each of them convinced — Odile had counted — that every other delegate was an idiot. They were mostly right.

She sat at the witnesses' table with a mug of tea she had no intention of drinking. Three months sober now. Nobody had asked her to stop; there simply hadn't been time, between the mountain nearly falling and the stars coming back, to visit the tavern. Grief and miracle made a poor pair of drinking companions anyway. They talked too much.

"Keeper Ferran." The senior delegate, a Rivermoor merchant named Halvard with a face like a folded ledger, peered down at her. "You were present when the Hollow Court renegotiated its pact. You witnessed the binding rite."

"I did."

"Can you confirm that Veyra Marchen is dead?"

"She is." Odile set down the untouched tea. "I watched Ansel Quintana defeat her at the mountain's root. I won't describe it. Some things you carry better unwrapped."

Halvard made a note anyway, because clerks would clerk. "And the army she brought below? Four thousand soldiers of the conscripted companies?"

"Most of them walked out," Odile said. "Half defected while the Queen was still speaking to them. The rest surrendered when the Ledger burned and they understood what they'd been carrying. They weren't soldiers by then, Delegate. They were men and women who'd discovered their paymaster intended to sell their souls by weight. Surrendering was the bravest thing most of them had ever done."

A murmur ran the length of the benches. Somewhere near the back a woman said, too loudly, "Then hang them all and be done," and somewhere nearer the front someone else said, "With what army, exactly?" and Odile settled in. This was the fourth day of that argument. It would outlast them all.

* * *

The problem, as far as Odile could tell, was that everyone wanted a simple story, and nobody had gotten one.

The simple story went like this: Veyra Marchen, wicked, was dead; her army should hang; the soul-ledgers were an abomination and should burn again for good measure; and the Hollow Court beneath the mountain was either a monster to be sealed away forever or a convenient fiction invented by traumatized survivors, depending on which delegate had eaten breakfast.

The true story was worse. The true story was that the free cities' founders had sold souls to feed the star-pact three hundred years ago and then written it out of every history book, that Marchen had merely found the oldest crime in the valley and turned it into an army, and that the court they all wanted to condemn was now — through no fault of its own, Odile would maintain — bound to a beast-speaker from Thornfell who had negotiated better terms than any diplomat in living memory.

"You're telling this council," Halvard said, on the fifth day, "that the solution to the soul-trade is *more* soul-trade. Freely given memories. Voluntary deposits." His tone suggested she had proposed solving famine by eating the seed corn.

"I'm telling you it's already happening," Odile said. "There's a line at the mountain gate. Have been since midwinter. People bring griefs they want set down and joys they want kept. The court feeds on memory; it always has. The only question anyone gets to answer is whether it steals or receives. Your founders answered 'steal.' Ansel answered otherwise. She's alive and your cities are standing, so I'd think twice before telling her she negotiated wrong."

"And the ledgers' contents? The record of every sale? The council has petitioned for those documents."

"The ledgers are ash. That's rather the point of burning them."

"Then we cannot know who sold whom!"

"No," Odile agreed pleasantly. "You can't. Isn't that inconvenient for everyone who slept well for three centuries?"

That earned her a gavel and a recess, which she considered a fair trade.

* * *

It was Ansel who found her on the wool hall steps afterward, shoulders up against the cold, watching Cinderhatch go about the business of not being doomed. The market stalls had crept back into the square like grass after fire. Children chased each other around the fountain, where somebody had hung a garland of tin stars — badly made, all different sizes, because every smith and tinker in town had contributed one.

"They're going to ask me tomorrow," Ansel said, sitting down without ceremony. "To testify."

"I heard. Halvard sent a courier with a summons so formal it could have been used as a weapon."

"He also sent one to Tomas."

Odile glanced at her. Tomas Quintana had been flesh for barely two seasons — restored by what the scholars insisted on calling "anomalous gratitude resonance" and what everyone else called the freed souls saying thank you — and he was still learning how to be a person who cast a shadow. He'd sat through three days of hearings already, hands folded, saying nothing, listening to men debate whether his twenty years in a ledger constituted property, theft, or an administrative matter.

"He shouldn't have to speak," Ansel said.

"No," Odile said. "But he might want to. That's the trick of it. Everyone keeps deciding for him. Marchen decided he was currency. The court decided he was flame. Now the council wants to decide he's evidence." She bumped Ansel's shoulder with her own. "Let him choose. He's had precious little practice."

Ansel was quiet for a while. Below them, a child dropped a tin star in the fountain and fished it out again, delighted by the whole arrangement of events.

"They'll ask me about the smugglers," Ansel said finally. "About Kestrel's people. About the night before I went under."

Odile didn't answer. She knew that night. Everyone who'd helped organize the descent knew it, though nobody had said it aloud: how the smuggling network had offered Ansel safe passage into the Hollow on one condition — that she first guide Marchen's vanguard through the Thornfell passes, buying the network's own survival with four thousand conscripts. How Ansel had refused. How Kestrel herself had locked the door behind her and said, gently, *nothing personal, Quintana, but the roads stay open longer than you will.*

How Ansel had gone anyway, through the flooded shaft, because blind bats don't use doors.

"Halvard will want names," Ansel said. "He wants to make the smugglers the whole story. One bad network, a few traitors, justice done, everyone goes home. And if I give him Kestrel—" She stopped. Started again. "Kestrel taught me the passes. Fed me through two winters when my mother's name was still burning on people's tongues. And she would have fed me to Marchen's army just as quick. Both of those are true and I don't know what a tribunal does with both."

"It does what tribunals do," Odile said. "It takes the truth and cuts it into pieces small enough to file. Your job is just to keep handing it the whole cloth." She stood, knees cracking like green wood. "Come on. If you're going to be hanged for honesty tomorrow, you'll eat honestly tonight. I'm cooking."

"You're a terrible cook."

"I'm a *sober* cook. Entirely different catastrophe."

* * *

Ansel testified on the sixth day, and Odile, from her seat at the witness table, watched the council discover that they had invited a landslide into their nice tidy hall.

Ansel told it plainly, which was somehow worse than any drama could have been. The starless night. The wolf with the scrap of her family's crest. The descent, the tolls paid in memory — she named what she'd lost without flinching, her mother's face first, and Odile saw several delegates look away, because everyone had a mother and nobody liked doing arithmetic with them. The Queen. Marchen at the root. The binding.

Then Halvard leaned forward. "During your approach to the Hollow, you sought passage through existing smuggling routes. Is that correct?"

"Yes."

"And that passage was denied on condition you commit treason against the free cities."

"It wasn't treason against the cities," Ansel said. "It was treason against four thousand specific human beings, which is worse, and I'd thank the council not to launder it. Yes. Kestrel Maren offered me my brother's soul in exchange for leading Marchen's vanguard through the high passes. When I refused, her people barred the door and left me the flooded shaft as my only road down."

"And you name her. Here. Now."

Ansel looked at him for a long moment, and Odile recognized the look — the same one she'd worn turning a bear on a patrol, the same one she wore whenever the court's song rose too loud in her skull. The look of someone stepping off a ledge on purpose.

"I name her. Kestrel Maren, of the Thornfell roads. And Bren Ostrow, who forged the offer's terms. And the ferryman Doss, who carried the message to Marchen's camp knowing what it bought. I name them because hiding their names wouldn't unmake what they did, and because half this council has already bought something off those roads and knows it." A stir along the benches. Ansel didn't raise her voice at all. "I'm not asking for their heads. I'm asking that you write down what happened. All of it. Even the parts that make my friends ugly. *Especially* those."

"Why not ask for punishment?" Halvard asked. Something in his face had shifted — not softness, but recognition. A man discovering the witness was more dangerous honest than defiant.

"Because I've seen what happens when debts get collected instead of settled," Ansel said. "I lived under a mountain full of the results. Punish them if they've broken laws — they broke laws. But don't pretend hanging Kestrel makes the cities clean. The cities were dirty three hundred years before she was born. Start there, if you want reckonings. We'll all be here a while."

Silence, of the heavy kind. Then, from the back benches, a voice Odile knew — Tomas Quintana, standing for the first time in six days of hearings.

"I was sold," he said. His voice was careful, a man laying stones across a stream. "Twenty years ago. My name was written in a ledger and I became a flame in a jar, and I remember all of it now, and I have thought every day since I got my name back about what should happen to the people responsible. Most of them are dead. Marchen is dead. The ones who signed the old pledges are dust. What remains is a room full of living people deciding what to do about the living." He looked down the length of the benches at Kestrel Maren, who sat in the accused's row with her wrists bound in wool cord, chin up, eyes fixed on Ansel with an expression Odile couldn't read and suspected Ansel couldn't either. "Kestrel Maren offered my sister a bargain that would have cost thousands of souls to save one. My sister refused. And then — this is the part I want in your record — my sister came for me anyway. Through the water. Without the roads. Nobody had to sell anybody." He sat down.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. Then a delegate from Grayharbor, an old woman with a fisherwoman's forearms, said: "Motion. The conscripted companies to be paroled to their home cities, labor oaths, three years. The named smugglers to face civil trial, not the rope. And a permanent commission on the old pledges — everything the founders buried, dug up and read aloud in every market square."

"That last part will destabilize half the valley," Halvard protested.

"Yes," the old woman said. "That's generally what truth costs. We've seen the installment plan. I recommend the lump sum."

They voted late in the afternoon, and it passed — not cleanly, nothing passed cleanly, but it passed. Parole. Civil trials. The commission. Odile signed her testimony with a hand that only shook a little and walked out into the square feeling lighter than she had in thirty years, which she distrusted on principle until she realized it was simply the absence of a secret she'd been carrying since the day the wards fell.

* * *

That evening, the town put out lanterns for the returning parolees — the first companies marching home through the pass, gaunt and quiet and unsure of their welcome. Cinderhatch, to its credit, lined the road and said nothing cruel. To its further credit, somebody's grandmother started handing out bread, and after that it stopped being a political question entirely.

Odile found Kestrel Maren in the wool hall's holding room, because apparently she was the sort of person now who visited jails voluntarily. Time changed people.

"You came to gloat," Kestrel said. She looked smaller without her gear, her hands loose in the wool cord, but her eyes were as sharp as ever. "Or to lecture. Everyone's been coming to lecture. The lectures are worse than the hanging would be."

"I came to bring you dinner and tell you what she paid," Odile said, setting down the basket. "Ansel. At the tolls. Her mother's face first, then years off her own life, then a nameless afternoon she can't even miss because she doesn't remember having it. That's what your door cost. That's what the flooded shaft cost. I thought you ought to know the price tag on the goods you were trading."

Kestrel was silent for a long moment. Then: "Is she going to testify against me at trial?"

"She already did. Honestly. Completely. Named you and everything."

Kestrel laughed — a short, cracked sound with nothing much fun in it. "Of course she did. Honest to the bone. Worst trait in a smuggler. Best trait in just about anything else." She looked up. "Will she come to the trial?"

"She'll walk to Grayharbor barefoot if you ask her to. That's the thing you got wrong, Kestrel. You thought loyalty was a road you controlled access to. It isn't. It walks through flooded mines if it has to. It'll probably testify at your sentencing, too, and ask for leniency, and embarrass everyone." Odile stood, joints announcing themselves. "Eat your dinner. Trials go better fed."

At the door, Kestrel called after her. "Ferran. The roads. When this is over. Someone has to hold the passes — the court's gates are open now, and every fool in the valley will want to leave a memory or steal one. Someone honest, someone who knows the stone." A pause. "Tell her the network will follow her into daylight. Those that want to. Tell her... tell her Kestrel says the high passes were always hers. We just held them while she was busy."

"I'll pass it along," Odile said, "after the trial, when it can't be construed as bribery. I have a reputation. Well. I'm building one. It's never too late."

Outside, the lanterns burned down the whole length of the pass road, and above them — new and still slightly unbelievable — the stars. Odile stood a moment counting constellations she'd forgotten she knew, and was turning toward her lodgings when a boy in council livery came running, out of breath, waving paper.

"Keeper Ferran! Delegate Halvard — the commission — they've opened the first of the founder archives under Rivermoor, and there's— sir, there's a second ledger. Older. Pre-pact. And the seal on the cover is the Quintana mark, and Delegate Halvard says to tell Miss Ansel—" The boy gulped air. "He says to tell her the first page has her mother's handwriting, and it's addressed to whoever comes looking, and it begins '*If you are reading this, I failed, and you must go deeper than the court.*'"

Odile looked at the stars a moment longer. Then she sighed, took the boy's arm, and started toward the lamplight where Ansel was teaching Tomas to dance, badly, to music only the beasts could hear.

"Well," she said. "It's never too late for anything, apparently."
