## 36. The Smuggler's New Road

The first thing Ansel did with her new authority was hang a sign.

It was a modest sign, hand-painted by Odile, who had insisted on doing the lettering herself despite hands that still shook on cold mornings and letters that leaned like tired travelers. It read, in Odile's careful script:

**QUINTANA GUIDES — THORNFELL PASSES. ALL ROADS OPEN. TOLLS FAIR. BEASTS FRIENDLY (MOSTLY).**

"You can't put *mostly*," Ansel said, holding it up to the light of Cinderhatch's rebuilt waystation. "That's terrible for business."

"That's honest for business," Odile said, dipping her brush again. "People trust honesty more than they trust friendliness. Learned that from watching you lie for twenty years."

Ansel opened her mouth to object, found no argument waiting there, and closed it again.

The truth was that everything had changed and almost nothing looked changed. The Thornfell roads were the same switchbacks she'd been walking since she was nine years old, the same scree slopes and goat-trails and the drowned stair's dry sister passages winding down toward the Hollow. What had changed was that she could walk them in daylight now. Could stand at the trailhead at dawn and say her own name to anyone who asked. Could let a mountain lion cross her path without pretending not to speak to it.

*Mostly friendly,* the lion had said when she'd told him about the sign, offended. *I am entirely friendly.*

*You ate a mule in autumn.*

*I was hungry,* said the lion, with dignity, *and he was rude.*

She hung the sign herself, on the first morning of spring thaw, hammering nails into the post while a small crowd of Cinderhatch locals watched from a respectful distance — the distance people kept from her now, half reverence and half unease. Word had come down from the mountain cities about what she was. Half-hollow. The court's ambassador. The woman who sang with beasts and bargained with things under the earth.

Children, though. Children didn't keep their distance. A girl of maybe seven broke from the crowd, marched up to the post, and studied the sign with the gravity of a magistrate.

"Which beasts aren't friendly?" she demanded.

"The wolverines," Ansel said. "But they're rude to everyone, so don't take it personally."

The girl nodded solemnly, as though this confirmed everything she'd suspected about wolverines, and ran back to her mother.

Ansel hammered in the last nail and stepped back. The morning light came up over Cinderhatch's rooftops, catching the new wards along the town's edge — wards renewed, humming clean and bright, fed now not by stolen souls but by memories freely left at the court's gates. She could feel them the way she felt everything below now: a low warm chord running through stone and root, the Hollow's song rising through her bones.

It never stopped. That was the bargain. She could leave the mountain, but the mountain never quite left her.

She found, lately, that she didn't mind as much as she'd expected to.

* * *

The old network came to her one week later, in ones and twos, the way smugglers did everything — carefully, at odd hours, through intermediaries who weren't really necessary anymore but old habits died hard.

Marta Kess arrived first, at dusk, walking up the guide road openly, which told Ansel everything before Marta said a word. Marta Kess had run contraband salt and fugitive families through the northern passes for fifteen years, and in all that time she had never once walked anywhere openly. Openness, in Marta's philosophy, was for gravestones.

"You hung a sign," Marta said, settling onto the waystation bench like a woman preparing for a long siege. "A literal sign. With your name on it."

"I did."

"Your actual name."

"It's a good name. I've grown attached to it."

Marta stared at her the way she might stare at a colleague who'd decided to swim the sea to the free cities. "Ansel. We spent two decades building something. Routes nobody knows. Signals nobody reads. Trust you can't buy or steal, only earn, drop by drop, over years. And you've hung it on a post next to a road where any tax collector with legs can see it."

"Any tax collector can also walk it," Ansel said. "That's rather the point."

"And what happens to the routes? The blind drops? The hollow trees with messages in them?" Marta's voice cracked slightly on the last part, and Ansel understood, finally, that this wasn't really about trade routes. "What happens to us? The people who only ever mattered because we were secret?"

Ah. There it was.

Ansel sat down beside her on the bench. Below them, Cinderhatch's evening fires were lighting, one by one, and somewhere a fiddler was playing badly, which meant enthusiastically.

"Marta, do you remember the Corren family?"

"The refugees, three winters back. The ones we took through the high pass in a snowstorm." Marta's jaw tightened. "I carried the little one myself."

"They're in Rivermoor now. The father works the ferry. The little one — Ilsa — sends me a letter every month. She wants to be a guide when she grows up." Ansel paused. "She wants to do it legally. She wants to wear a badge."

Marta was quiet for a long moment. "And if she'd asked to be a smuggler?"

"Then I'd have taught her the blind drops myself. But she didn't ask." Ansel turned to look at her old friend. "The roads don't stop being ours because other people know about them. The snowstorms don't get easier. The passes don't get shorter. All that changes is that nobody has to hide anymore — unless they choose to. And some will choose to, Marta. Some always will. Fugitives, debt-runners, people fleeing men worse than tax collectors. The network doesn't die. It just stops being the whole story."

Marta picked at the bench's splintered edge for a while. Then, gruffly: "The badge. Does it come with pay?"

"A fair wage. Court-sanctioned."

"Court." She spat, ceremonially, into the dirt. "Fine. But I want my section of the northern passes, and I want it known that I answer to you and no one else, and I want—" she hesitated— "I want the hollow tree on the Widow's Trail left standing. Empty. Just... left standing."

"For old times' sake."

"For old times' sake," Marta agreed, and shook her hand, and it was done.

Some came easy like that. Most did not.

* * *

Dario Voss did not come to her at all. She had to go to him, up a switchback trail to a camp hidden in the pines above the old salt road — hidden still, though hiding meant nothing now, which Dario clearly knew and clearly couldn't help.

He was sitting by his fire alone when she walked in, and he didn't stand, and he didn't smile.

"So it's true," he said. "You're working for them now. For the thing under the mountain."

"I'm working *with* it. There's a difference."

"There's always a difference," Dario said. "That's how it starts. That's how it always starts. First it's *with*, then it's *alongside*, then one morning you wake up and you're its voice in the daylight and everyone who remembers the old ways is a criminal." He poked the fire hard enough to scatter sparks. "My grandfather ran these roads when beast-speakers burned. My father ran them when ward-keepers bought souls and called it faith. We survived because we trusted nothing with power, Ansel. Nothing. And here you are, handing it your name."

"Dario." She crouched across the fire from him. "I held my brother's soul in my hands. I paid for it with pieces of my own mind. I watched Sef Andrade die holding up a ceiling so strangers could live. Don't tell me what the Hollow is. I know better than you ever will."

"Then why—"

"Because someone has to hold it to its promises. Because the pact holds up two cities and forty thousand souls and if the wrong person speaks for it, everything falls again." She spread her hands. "You think I wanted this throne? It's a job, Dario. It's the worst job in the world, and I got it because nobody else could do it, which is the oldest reason there is."

Dario looked at her across the fire for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its anger and found something sadder underneath.

"My brother's in Marchen's surrendered army," he said quietly. "Conscripted. He'll stand trial in Cinderhatch come summer, with all the rest. You're testifying, they say. Naming names."

"I am."

"If you name him—" His jaw worked. "He drove supply wagons. He never hurt anyone. But he wore the uniform, and you'll name him, because you name everyone now, because that's the new way — everything in the open, everything written down."

"Yes," Ansel said gently. "I'll name him. And then I'll testify that wagon drivers who surrendered without bloodshed should go home, and Odile will back me, and the council will listen, because that's the other thing about everything being written down — the mercy gets recorded too. That's the trade, Dario. Truth for mercy. You can't have one without offering the other."

He was silent for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, an owl called, and Ansel felt the call settle into the great chord beneath the world, one note among thousands.

"I won't take a badge," Dario said at last. "Not yet. Maybe not ever. But my routes are good ones, and my people are loyal, and..." he exhaled, "...if a fugitive needs moving quiet, off the books, the old way — send word to the hollow tree on the Widow's Trail."

Ansel smiled. "Marta just asked to preserve that same tree."

"Marta always had sense." Dario almost smiled back. Almost. "Go home, Ambassador. Tell your beasts the high camps stay mine."

"They mostly respect that already."

"*Mostly,*" he muttered, and waved her away, and that was as much reconciliation as either of them could manage that night.

Incomplete. But real.

* * *

By midsummer, the new order had taken shape the way paths do — not designed but worn, one traveler at a time.

Licensed guides walked the passes in daylight, wearing tin badges stamped with a mountain and a star. Pilgrims climbed to leave their griefs at the court's gates, and came back lighter, and told everyone who'd listen. Caravans moved openly, paying fair tolls, and the toll-houses were staffed half by humans and half by beasts, because the wolverines, it turned out, were magnificent negotiators once you accepted that rudeness was simply their manner of speech.

Not everyone followed. Old Renna closed her safehouse in Cinderhatch and moved to the coast, sending word that she was done with mountains, courts, and "everything with a song in it." Two of the Ferro cousins took badges; the third went north and wasn't spoken of, which was its own kind of answer. The network didn't shatter — it shed, like a snake shedding skin, keeping what fit and leaving the rest behind in the grass.

And on an evening in late summer, Ansel stood at the Widow's Trail head and watched a young guide — Ilsa Corren, sixteen now, badge polished to a mirror-shine — lead her first solo caravan down out of the high country, singing a walking song to keep the mules steady.

The mountain lion appeared at Ansel's shoulder, as he often did at dusk.

*You're pleased,* he observed.

*I'm content. It's different.*

*Is it.*

"She's singing the route-calls," Ansel said softly. "The old ones. The smuggler signals. Except now they're just... songs. Everyone knows them. Nobody's hiding." She shook her head, wondering at it. "Twenty years I kept those calls secret, and it turns out they sound better in the open air."

The lion considered this. *The hollow tree on the Widow's Trail,* he said. *There is a message in it. Left last night. I smelled ink and fear.*

Ansel went still. "From whom?"

*A stranger. Not of your network.* The lion's ears flattened. *The message asks for the Ambassador. By that title. And it says—* he recited it in the careful way he recited human words, each one handled like a hot coal— *"They are selling memories at the gates who have no right to buy. Check your ledgers. The theft has started again."*

Below them, Ilsa's song drifted down the valley, bright and clear. Above, the first stars were coming out, steady and true, each one anchored by the pact Ansel had bound herself to.

And somewhere between those two lights, in the dark of the old tree, a stranger's warning waited with her name on it.

Ansel looked at the trail climbing up into the shadows, and sighed, and reached for her pack.

"Stay here," she told the lion. "Or come. Either way — I think the new road just got longer."
