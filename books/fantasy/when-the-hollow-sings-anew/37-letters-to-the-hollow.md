## 37. Letters to the Hollow

The first memory ever left at the gates of the Hollow Court was a jar of plum preserves.

Not metaphorically. An actual jar. Wren found it on the third morning of her stewardship, sitting primly on the lowest step of the eastern gate as if it had been invited to tea and had arrived embarrassingly early. A note was tied to the lid in careful, looping handwriting:

*For the Court. It is the summer my daughter learned to walk. I do not want to lose it, but I cannot carry it anymore. Please keep it safe.*

Wren stood there for a long while, holding the jar, feeling the memory inside it like warmth through glass — sticky feet on sun-warmed stone, a small hand gripping a thumb, laughter that tasted like plums.

Then she did what any sensible successor of Sef Andrade would have done.

She panicked.

"Sef never taught me this," she said to the empty gate. The gate, being a gate, said nothing back, though the stones beneath it hummed their low court-song, which Wren had learned to interpret as either *all is well* or *we are thinking about eating something*, depending on the pitch.

She carried the jar inside, past the galleries where the great bone lanterns now burned steady instead of ravenous, past the Ledger's empty plinth where the ash had been swept into a respectful urn that nobody quite knew what to do with, all the way down to the Queen's hall.

The Hollow Queen had grown smaller since the binding. Not smaller in the way of things diminishing — smaller the way a storm becomes rain, contained now, shaped, given somewhere to go. She lay coiled around the roots of the star-pact like a cat around a warm hearth, vast and patient still, but patient the way a grandmother is patient rather than the way a famine is patient.

"Someone," Wren announced, holding up the jar, "has left us fruit."

The Queen opened one eye. It was the size of a cartwheel and dark as deep water, and Wren had long ago stopped flinching at it, mostly.

"I can smell it," the Queen said. Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way it always did, though since the rite it had gained something new underneath — a kind of tired gentleness. "Summer. A child. A woman who loved both enough to set them down so she could keep walking."

"That's exactly what it is," Wren said. "What do I *do* with it?"

"You keep it safe," said the Queen, as though this were obvious. "That is what we are for now. Did you think the work would be more complicated?"

"I thought the work would involve fewer preserves."

"Give it time," said the Queen, and closed her eye, and Wren could have sworn the old monster was smiling.

* * *

In those first months after the stars came back, the court had been a place of triage. Freed souls drifting home or choosing to stay; ward-keepers arriving in twos and threes to learn the restored rites; Odile Ferran stomping through the lower galleries twice a season with her broken-then-mended compass, barking at everyone about resonance loads as though the mountain were a badly run warehouse, which, in fairness, it had been for three hundred years.

And Ansel Quintana, when she came down — which was not often, because half of her lived here always and she guarded her visits above like a woman rationing bread — walked the halls touching the walls and listening, and wherever she touched, the hollow places grew quieter. Calmer. She was the court's voice now, its bound ambassador, half-hollow herself, and the strange thing — the thing Wren turned over in her mind during slow watches — was how much gentler the court had become under a voice that knew what it was like to be robbed.

But triage ended. Triage always ends, and then you find out what a thing actually *is*.

By midwinter, the jars became a trickle. By spring, a stream.

They came at night, mostly. Farmers and widows and soldiers and one extremely apologetic baker, leaving memories at the gates wrapped in cloth, sealed in wax, folded into paper boats and floated down the flooded stair itself, which Wren spent an entire week fishing out before she rigged a net. Griefs people wanted to set down: the last words spoken to a son before the conscription carts took him, guilt over a brother sold twenty years ago by a desperate father, the memory of a house burning that someone had carried so long it had worn a groove into them. And joys, too, which surprised Wren more — joys people wanted kept *forever*, unspoilable, proof against forgetting: a wedding dance in a kitchen, the smell of a grandmother's bread, one man's entire recollection of a dog named Captain who had once defended a turnip cart from a goose.

"The goose won," the note explained. "Captain never forgave it. I want that remembered even after I'm gone. Some things are too good to be lost."

Wren read every single one. It wasn't policy. There was no policy; she was inventing policy weekly and mostly by accident. But she read them because Sef had told her, in his last lucid teaching days before he went down into the mountain's bones for good, that the old court's crime was never hunger. Hunger was only weather. The crime was that no one had ever *asked*. That souls were taken instead of given, hoarded instead of held, and that a thing held without consent curdles even when it shines.

"So ask," he'd said. He'd been half-gone already, more gallery than man, his edges soft as smoke. "Ask everything. Ask permission until asking is the whole law. If they say no, thank them anyway. Gratitude costs nothing and it's the only currency the court never learned to counterfeit."

So Wren asked. She built shelves — actual carpentered shelves, hauled down piece by piece through the drowned stair, an enterprise that had required negotiating with three bats, one bear, and a ward-keeper with strong opinions about load-bearing stone. Each memory got its own niche, labeled, dated, catalogued in a ledger that was very deliberately *not* the Ledger: no debts, no weights, no entries that could be called due. Just names, if names were offered. Just *kept*.

She called it the Archive of Given Things, mostly because "the Memory Pantry" had been voted down by a committee consisting of herself, two bats, and the Queen, with the bats voting yes out of contrariness.

* * *

Not everything went smoothly. Wren would be the first to admit that institutionalizing hope involved a truly humiliating number of mistakes.

There was the incident with the merchant from Cinderhatch who tried to leave a memory of his rival's business secrets, having grasped roughly none of the concept. ("This is not grief or joy, this is *espionage*," Wren had told him, marching him back up the stair personally. "Also your rival bribed me last month to leave a memory of *your* secrets. You're both banned. Go be honest with each other, it's faster.")

There was the winter a grieving mother asked whether the court could give a memory back — her own, sold long ago to pay a debt she'd never chosen — and Wren had sat with her for hours in the lantern-light, explaining gently that what was taken freely in the old days could sometimes be traced among the freed souls still wandering the galleries, and then spending four nights doing exactly that, and finding it, and handing it back, and standing there awkwardly while the woman wept and thanked her and Wren realized, with a jolt, that this was the job. This was the whole job. Not guarding treasure. Handing treasure back.

And there was the afternoon the beasts came down en masse.

Ansel's wardens — wolves and bears and the blind bats whose songs mapped the dark — had always patrolled the upper galleries, per the terms of the binding. But one gray afternoon in early spring, a wolf trotted into the archive hall carrying a memory in its jaws as carefully as a pup: a scrap of hide-wrapped bundle containing the memory of a den in high snow, of cubs grown and gone, of a mate's song fading. The wolf laid it on the shelf, looked at Wren with pale gold eyes, and waited.

"You want us to keep it," Wren said slowly. "You want the court to hold it. So you don't have to."

The wolf made a sound low in its chest that Wren had learned meant *yes, obviously, are you new*.

Within a month, the beasts were the archive's most devoted clients. Bears left memories of salmon runs. Bats, who remembered in song rather than image, contributed entire choruses of the oldest cave-songs, some predating the court itself, and these Wren stored in a special alcove where they hummed faintly forever, so that walking past it sounded like walking through the throat of the mountain singing to itself.

The Hollow Queen pretended not to notice the animals. Then Wren caught her, one quiet watch, with her vast head lowered to the beast-alcove, listening to a bat-song older than her starving, and the expression on that ancient face was so nakedly homesick that Wren withdrew very quietly and never mentioned it, except to the bats, who mentioned it to everyone.

* * *

The hardest letters were the ones addressed to specific dead people.

A widow wrote to a husband taken by Marchen's conscription, and the letter was just a memory of an argument — their last one, about money, about nothing — and a wish that he could know she hadn't meant the last thing she'd said. Wren read it four times before she understood what was being asked: not keeping. *Delivery.*

"We don't do that," she told the Queen that night. "We're not — there's no sending things onward. The freed souls go where they go. We can't forward mail to the afterlife, assuming there is one, which frankly the job description doesn't cover—"

"No," the Queen agreed. "We cannot send."

"But she needs him to know."

"Does she?" The Queen's voice curled thoughtfully through the hall. "Or does she need to have *said* it? Say it aloud, in a place that listens? Little keeper, I starved three hundred years beside thousands of souls who died with unsaid words in their mouths. Do you know what I learned?" A pause, long as a season. "The saying matters even when no one receives it. Perhaps especially then. The words change the sayer. We can be the place where the words are finally said. That is not nothing. That may be everything."

So Wren built the Saying Place — a small round chamber off the western gallery, one lantern, one bench, acoustics tuned by bats until a whisper there sounded like a voice heard across years. People came and spoke to their dead. Some screamed. One old smuggler sang, terribly, for nearly an hour, a drinking song his partner had loved, and laughed at the end and cried at the end, possibly simultaneously.

And here was the thing Wren never expected, the thing she reported to Ansel on one of her rare descents, haltingly, watching the half-hollow woman's face for mockery and finding none: sometimes the words *were* received. Not always. Not provably. But the freed souls who lingered in the galleries — the ones who'd chosen to stay near the court that had once eaten them, drawn perhaps by the new warmth of the place — began gathering near the Saying Place on busy nights. Listening. And twice — twice that Wren witnessed, and she'd stopped doubting after the second time — a lingering soul heard a name it recognized and drifted toward the sound like a moth toward the first star after a starless year.

The widow with the argument met something in the dark of the western gallery on her way out. Wren didn't see it clearly. Nobody does. But the woman came up the drowned stair at dawn looking like someone who had set down a weight and picked up a feather, and she pressed a coin into Wren's hand and said, "He always was a stubborn listener," and Wren decided that whatever had or hadn't happened was none of her business and all of her joy.

* * *

On a bright evening in late spring, Wren climbed the long stair to the eastern gate to collect the day's offerings and found Ansel Quintana sitting on the top step, boots dusty from the high passes, sharing dried apricots out of a paper bag with a wolf the size of a pony.

"You're supposed to announce yourself," Wren said. "There are protocols. I wrote protocols."

"I'm half the institution," Ansel said. "I feel like that exempts me." She held out the bag. Wren took an apricot and sat down on the step below her, because there were rules about sitting next to the Voice of the Hollow Court, and the rule was you didn't unless invited, and the other rule, unwritten, was that Ansel hated ceremony so thoroughly that the fastest way to make her comfortable was to ignore every protocol she'd just complained about.

They ate apricots. Below them, the drowned stair breathed its slow tide-sound. Somewhere in the deep, the Queen hummed along with the bat-songs, quietly, the way she thought nobody heard.

"Odile tells me the archive holds eleven thousand entries now," Ansel said.

"Eleven thousand four hundred and six."

"And not one of them stolen."

"Not one."

Ansel nodded slowly. Twenty years ago — well, less than two years ago, though it felt geological — this place had been a furnace where souls burned as currency. Now a young courtier with ink-stained fingers sat on its doorstep sorting through jars of jam and memories of geese, and the mountain hummed, and the wolf had fallen asleep against Ansel's knee like a farm dog.

"My mother used to say the Thornfell takes what it's owed," Ansel said at last. "Everyone said it. It was just true, the way weather is true." She turned an apricot over in her fingers. "Nobody ever asked what the mountain would do if you gave it something it wasn't owed."

"What does it do?"

Ansel smiled — a small, crooked, tired smile, the smile of a woman carrying a whole court's worth of memory behind her eyes. "It says thank you," she said. "Every single time. Listen."

Wren listened. Under the tide-sound, under the bat-songs, under everything, the court's low hum shifted — warmed — and if you didn't know better you'd swear it was a chord resolving, a bell struck and ringing true.

"It's learning," Wren said.

"It's remembering," Ansel corrected. "It knew how once. Before the pact cracked. Before the starving." She stood, brushing off her coat, and the wolf woke and stretched dramatically. "Keep the shelves full, keeper. Keep the asking. And Wren—" She paused at the head of the stairs, silhouetted against the last light leaking down from above. "The anniversary's coming. The starless night. They'll want to mark it up there, in Cinderhatch and Rivermoor and all the cities. Candles, probably. Speeches, definitely, knowing Odile."

"There will absolutely be speeches. Odile has been drafting one since midwinter."

"When they light the candles," Ansel said, "listen for us. All of us. Down here." Something moved through her expression, brief and private — a memory of a lullaby sung to a soul-flame in the dark, maybe, or the shape of a promise made at the mountain's root. "We'll be singing back."

* * *

The eve of the anniversary arrived warm and clear, and Wren spent it doing inventory, because ritual comforted her and also because someone had to make sure the goose-and-Captain entry survived another year.

At dusk, the offerings began coming faster than usual. Whole families climbing the drowned stair together, hand in hand, each carrying something to leave. A blacksmith brought the memory of his first forge-fire. Three sisters brought one shared memory between them, arguing cheerfully the whole way down about whose version was accurate. An old ward-keeper — one of Odile's students — brought the sound of the wards humming *right*, from before, because she wanted the mountain to remember that it had once been sung to properly and could be again.

And at the very end of the line, as the last light failed, a child. Six years old, maybe seven, born after the starless night, who had never known a sky without stars and couldn't imagine one, and who solemnly presented Wren with a memory wrapped in a handkerchief: the taste of honey on bread, her grandmother's hands, a morning that had felt endless.

"For keeping," the child said, with the gravity of a queen bestowing a duchy. "Forever. So it doesn't get lost even when I'm big."

"Forever," Wren promised, and meant it, and watched the child skip back up the stairs toward the starlight.

Wren carried the little bundle down into the archive, found it a niche in the newest shelf — front row, eye level, the shelf she privately called the Shelf of Small Foreverness — and set it in among eleven thousand neighbors. The lantern-light caught the handkerchief's edge. The court hummed its low, contented chord. Far above, muffled through miles of stone, she could hear the cities beginning to sing — thin voices threading down through rock and root, gathering strength, the opening notes of the anniversary song.

And the mountain answered.

Not just the hum this time. The whole deep of it — the Queen's vast voice beneath, the bats' choruses, the beasts' howls rolling down the high passes, the freed souls in the galleries lifting whatever remained of their human throats — rising through the stone in harmonies no surface choir had ever attempted, meeting the cities' song halfway, note for note, verse for verse.

Wren stood in the middle of it with honey-bread memory in her hands and tears running freely down her face, laughing at the same time, because it was beautiful and also slightly off-key and also the most important thing that had ever happened in this mountain, and she was the only one in the archive to witness it.

Then, cutting through the music from far above, came a voice she knew — a voice half-hollow and wholly itself, climbing the high passes toward Thornfell's peak, singing the lead line as though the pact had been written for her throat specifically. And beneath her voice, joining on the harmony, a second voice Wren didn't know, young and clear and achingly familiar in a way she couldn't place —

— until she could, because she'd read his entry in the old Ledger a hundred times, and because freed souls sometimes came back to flesh when gratitude ran strong enough, and because there was only one person alive who could harmonize with Ansel Quintana like they'd learned the song as children.

"Tomas," Wren whispered, grinning through her tears at the empty archive. "Well. Somebody get me more shelves."
