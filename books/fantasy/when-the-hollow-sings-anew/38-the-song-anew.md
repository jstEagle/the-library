## 38. The Song Anew

One year, and the stars still surprised her.

Ansel had climbed Thornfell's peak since before dawn, following a path that no longer needed hiding — the smuggler trails had been widened, mapped, and marked with little tin signs that Odile had insisted upon, because "a road without signage is an invitation to litigation." The sign at the last switchback read PEAK — MIND THE WARD-STONES — DO NOT FEED THE BATS. Someone had scratched out *do not* and written *please do*, which told Ansel everything about who had climbed this way before her.

The morning was cold and clean, the kind of cold that rang like a struck coin. Below her, Cinderhatch spilled down the mountainside in its crooked rows, chimneys smoking, laundry lines strung between buildings like bunting for a holiday no one had officially declared but everyone had silently agreed to observe. Beyond it, the valley roads were dark with people moving uphill — hundreds of them, thousands maybe, a slow river of lanterns flowing toward the mountain on the anniversary of the night the sky went empty.

Ansel pulled her coat tighter and tried to remember how to be nervous.

She'd sung in front of people before. She'd sung to smugglers to keep their nerve up on bad passes, sung to horses, sung once — memorably, disastrously — at a wedding in Rivermoor until the groom's mother asked her, very politely, to stop. But she had never sung *this*. The pact-song was old, older than the wards, older than the Ledger, older than the family that had kept fragments of it alive in lullabies without knowing why. And today she would sing it as the Hollow's voice, with every listening thing on and under the mountain answering her in turn.

If she got it wrong, the worst case was embarrassment. That was what she kept telling herself. The slightly worse case was a minor geological event.

"You're doing the face," said a voice behind her.

Tomas came up over the last rise with a rucksack over one shoulder and snow in his dark hair, walking with the easy, deliberate stride of a man who had spent twenty years as a flame and was still, some days, astonished by the weight of his own boots. He'd been flesh for eight months now. He still ate like each meal might be his last, and he still stopped sometimes in doorways to feel a draft on his skin.

"What face?" Ansel said.

"The one where you're negotiating with your own stomach."

"That's my thinking face."

"Your thinking face is when you look like you smell something unfortunate. This is the other one." He dropped the rucksack, dug out a thermos, and poured two cups of something that steamed and smelled aggressively of cinnamon. "Drink. You can't hold up half the world on an empty stomach. I've done the research."

Ansel took the cup. It was too hot and too sweet and exactly right. "Where's Odile?"

"Arguing with a marmot."

"On purpose?"

"I believe the marmot started it."

* * *

The marmot dispute, when Ansel descended fifty feet to arbitrate, turned out to be a territorial disagreement over a specific sunny rock near the upper ward-stone, and it was resolved in the traditional manner: Odile got the rock, the marmot got a biscuit, and both parties went away feeling they had won, which is the mark of any lasting settlement.

Odile Ferran looked better than Ansel had ever seen her. A year of steady work, steady meals, and a ward-keeper's pension reinstated with back pay had filled her out; her hands, once unsteady as water, were sure on her staff now. Some of her memories had never come back — the court had taken its tolls from her too, and memory given to the Hollow did not return just because the war ended. She kept a notebook for the gaps, and she read from it every morning, and sometimes she laughed at herself in it, which Ansel considered the bravest thing she had ever watched anyone do.

"The lower wards are humming," Odile reported, squinting up the slope. "Properly humming, mind. Like a well-tuned bell, not a dying one. Your court's in good voice today."

"*Our* court."

"My court pays me a salary. That makes it my employer." She tapped her staff against a ward-stone, and the stone answered with a low, pleased tone that traveled down through the rock like warmth through wine. "They've been rehearsing all week, you know. Underneath. I can hear them through my boots when I inspect the deep galleries. The whole mountain's tuned up like a choir before a festival."

"Any nerves?"

Odile gave her a look over the top of her spectacles. "I held a lit match to the most dangerous book ever written while an army screamed at me from three directions. No, girl, I don't get nervous anymore. I get *scheduled*." She consulted the notebook. "Song starts at dusk. Cities answer in turn — Cinderhatch first, then the pass towns, then Rivermoor if the wind's kind. Beasts on the second verse. Court beneath on the third. And you don't stop between verses, no matter what happens, because a pact-song with a gap in it is just shouting."

"And if something happens?"

"Something always happens," Odile said comfortably. "That's why we rehearsed."

* * *

The beasts began arriving at midday.

They came without ceremony, which is to say they came the way animals come anywhere — singly, in twos and threes, appearing at the edges of things as though they had merely happened to be passing by. A gray wolf lay down ten yards from Odile and proceeded to ignore her with tremendous dignity. Two bears — the same two, Ansel was nearly certain, from that terrible day at the border, though bears are hard to tell apart and even harder to apologize to — took up positions flanking the peak like a pair of enormous, skeptical honor guards.

The bats came at sundown, as bats do. They poured out of the high caves in a ribbon of leather and echo, wheeling above the peak in patterns that Ansel had learned to read as maps and now could read as music. Their song was the oldest part of the pact-song, the part the court itself had learned from them: a weaving, clicking counter-melody that mapped the dark the way light mapped the day.

And threading through all of it, faint as a pulse through stone, she felt the Hollow — the great slow attention beneath her feet, the cathedral of bone and lantern-light that had starved for centuries and was learning, slowly, awkwardly, the strange new discipline of being *fed* rather than feeding. There were more voices down there now than there used to be. Sef's successor — a young courtier named Mirabel who had been sold at nine and remembered her own name thanks to the freed souls' gratitude, and who ran the memory-gates below with terrifying efficiency and a habit of thanking every donor twice — sent up reports weekly. The gates received forty memories last month. Then sixty. People came with griefs they wanted set down and joys they wanted kept forever, and the court, which had once taken, was learning to receive. It turned out those were different muscles entirely.

"They're early," Tomas observed as the wolf shifted closer to the fire and pretended not to be warming itself.

"Everyone's early," Ansel said. "Look downhill."

The lantern-river had reached the mid-slopes. People had brought blankets and bread and children who should have been asleep, and someone had built a bonfire on the switchback below the peak that was, in Ansel's professional assessment, far too large, and in everyone else's professional assessment, not remotely large enough.

Her old network was there too — she could pick them out by their posture even at distance. Renna the fox, who had offered her betrayal on a cold night and had spent a year trying to earn the offer back. Half the Thornfell runners had followed her into daylight when she reopened the roads; the other half still moved freight in the dark, and Ansel did not hate them for it. Roads take time. So do people.

Tomas sat down beside her at the peak's edge, boots dangling over a drop that would have terrified him a year ago, when he'd had no body to break. For a while neither of them spoke. Below, the crowd murmured like surf. Above, the first stars were coming out — cautious at first, then confident, then everywhere, the whole spilled treasury of the sky that had been locked away for one long night and returned for every night since.

"Do you remember any of it?" Ansel asked quietly. "Being a flame."

"Some." He turned his cup in his hands. "Warmth. Being *held*, in a way — the court held all of us together, like coins in one purse. I remember hearing you sing the lullaby and not knowing why it hurt." He smiled sideways at her. "I know why now. It hurt because it was mine."

"She'd be proud of you," Ansel said. "Of both of us."

"She'd be appalled at our eating habits and proud of everything else."

Ansel laughed, and the laugh felt like the first warm day after winter — surprising, slightly cracked, real.

* * *

Dusk came down the mountain like a held breath finally let go.

Odile struck her staff on the ward-stone thrice, and the sound rolled down every switchback, and the crowd below went quiet in rings, silence spreading outward like the rings of the sound itself. The wolves stood. The bears settled onto their haunches. The bats wheeled into their high pattern and stilled into a living constellation. Somewhere beneath Ansel's boots, a mile of dark air and ancient bone drew itself up to listen.

Ansel stood. Her heart was going like a drummer boy, and she let it, because Odile had told her once that fear and readiness were the same animal wearing different coats.

She sang the first note alone.

It was not a beautiful note — she was a guide, not a cathedral singer — but it was *true*, it sat square in the middle of itself, and it went down into the stone the way a key goes into a lock. The pact-song unfolded from there, verse by verse, the words half Old Tongue and half plain speech, about debts and gifts and the difference between them, about a sky that gives its light freely and an earth that holds it faithfully, about the bargain underneath all bargains: *we will remember you if you will carry us.*

On the second verse, the beasts answered.

The wolves began it — a low rising harmony that was not quite howling and not quite singing, and had never, in Ansel's whole life of listening, been aimed *with* a human voice instead of against it. The bears added a depth beneath it like distant thunder deciding to be friendly. The bats stitched the whole of it together with their bright needle-work of clicks and echoes, and Ansel felt the song become a fabric with the mountain for its loom.

On the third verse, the cities answered.

Cinderhatch first — a thousand ordinary voices, shopkeepers and smelters and the too-loud child from the laundress's line, none of them in tune and all of them in time, because Odile had spent three weeks drilling the melody into anyone who would stand still, and apparently even the marmots had attended. Then the pass towns, fainter, carried on the wind. Then Rivermoor, so far away it arrived less as sound than as a shiver in the ground, a low tidal hum, the city that had opened its gates without a fight singing now with everything it had.

And beneath it all, patient and vast and glad — Ansel had no other word for it, *glad* — the Hollow Court rose into the song. The stone itself seemed to open like a throat. Every lantern in the deep galleries, every freed soul-flame that had chosen to stay and serve the new covenant, every hollow thing that had once only hungered: they answered her in a chord that she felt in her teeth, in her ribs, in the half-of-her that lived below and always would.

Tomas harmonized.

His voice slid in beside hers on the fourth verse — the verse about siblings, about debts forgiven between kin, the verse their mother had taught them both before one of them burned and the other was sold — and his voice cracked on the third word and he kept going anyway, and Ansel reached over and gripped his hand hard enough to hurt, and he gripped back, and they sang the rest of it into the turning sky.

The last note went on longer than any of them planned. It hung over the peak, over the beasts and the bat-constellation and the burning bonfire and the ten thousand upturned faces below, and then it came apart gently, the way light comes apart at dawn, into ordinary evening.

For three full seconds, nobody anywhere made a sound.

Then Cinderhatch cheered, and the cheering rolled uphill like weather, and the wolf nearest the fire howled in what was either participation or complaint, and Odile Ferran wiped her eyes with her sleeve and wrote in her notebook, in letters Ansel could see from ten feet away: *Went fine.*

* * *

Later — much later, after the bonfire had shrunk to a civilized size and the crowds had begun their slow lantern-lit descent — Ansel walked the peak's edge alone, or as alone as one ever is with a wolf pretending to follow coincidentally.

The stars were all out. All of them. She counted a few constellations the way her mother had taught her, naming them in the old fashion — the Shepherdess, the Long Road, the Open Hand — and thought about how for one entire night these had all gone dark, and how the world had learned in that darkness exactly what it was standing on.

Her head hummed with the court's residual chord. It always did, after singing. Part of her memory lived below now, had lived below since the binding — faces, years, a nameless afternoon she'd paid without knowing what it held — and in exchange she carried the Hollow's song everywhere, a second heartbeat under her own. She could leave the mountain. She simply could never be entirely away from it. Most days, lately, that felt less like a cage and more like being known.

Tomas found her at the eastern edge, where the drop fell away toward the deep passes.

"There's a delegation at first light," he said. "Rivermoor wants to talk about trade tariffs on memory-gifts. Odile says you'll want coffee and moral support."

"Odile says correctly."

"And after?" He looked east, where the sky would lighten first. "There's somewhere you go every year around now, isn't there? I've noticed. You think I don't, but I do."

Ansel was quiet a moment. The wind moved. Far below, the last of the crowd's lanterns threaded down toward town like embers returning to a hearth.

"There's a memorial on the south face," she said at last. "Mother's. Nobody maintained it for twenty years because everybody thought she was a criminal and I thought I couldn't bear it." She turned the cup in her hands — he'd refilled it at some point, cinnamon and all, without asking, which was the kind of thing brothers learned. "I go now. I've been going. And every year I understand a little more about what she actually paid, and whether it was worth it, and what she'd want me to count up on her behalf."

"And this year?"

"This year I'm ready to do the arithmetic honestly." Ansel smiled, small and true, and drained the cup. "Come with me tomorrow. Both of us owe her a visit — you most of all. And I want to tell her about the song. She started it, you know. Everything we sang tonight — she sang the first broken piece of it twenty years ago, alone, in the dark, so the rest of us could learn the tune."

Tomas put his arm briefly around his sister's shoulders, careful as a man handling something rescued from a fire.

"Then we'll go at dawn," he said. "And Ansel — whatever you have to tell her, tell her the ending too. She'd want to know how the song goes *now*."

Below them, deep in the stone, the Hollow hummed the melody softly to itself in the dark, already practicing for next year.
