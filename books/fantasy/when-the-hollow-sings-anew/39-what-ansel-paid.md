## 39. What Ansel Paid

The road up to the memorial had been a smuggler's path once, back when everything worth carrying went by night and nothing worth saying was said aloud. Now it was just a road — wide enough for two carts, marked with honest signposts, and traveled in daylight by people who waved at her as they passed.

Ansel still wasn't used to being waved at.

She climbed on foot, as she always did, leaving her mule hitched at the bottom where old Denna kept a pasture and charged an outrageous fee for oats and gossip in equal measure. The morning was cool and clear, the kind of autumn morning that made the Thornfell look almost gentle, all gold larch and blue shadow, the peaks wearing their first dusting of snow like respectable people trying on hats.

Halfway up, a fox fell into step beside her.

"You're not subtle," Ansel told it.

The fox looked offended, which was its default expression, and continued walking. It was one of hers now — one of the court's wardens, the beasts who kept watch over the hollow places so that no other child would ever learn what lived beneath the mountain by accident. It had opinions about her pace, her route, and her habit of talking to herself, and it expressed all of them by walking exactly half a length ahead of her, tail high, radiating the smugness of a creature that had never once paid for anything with a memory.

"Enjoy it while it lasts," she muttered.

The fox's ears flicked. Somewhere below them, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the Hollow sang its low morning note. *Good morning,* it seemed to say. *We kept your Tuesday.*

That was the thing about the bargain nobody warned her about. She could leave the mountain. She left it often — Cinderhatch, Rivermoor, even the coast once, though the sea had made her feel like a coin taken out of its purse. But part of her lived down there now, in the lantern-lit dark, and the court was punctual about reminding her. Every memory she'd paid came home to roost on a schedule. Her mother's face arrived on the first of each month. The nameless afternoon came whenever it pleased, which was usually at inconvenient moments, like during important negotiations or while eating soup.

Today was the last day of the month. Tomorrow her mother would come back to her.

But today, she walked to the memorial with no face in her head at all — only the shape of one, the way you know a room is warm without remembering why.

* * *

The memorial stood on a shoulder of the mountain where the larches opened onto sky. It wasn't grand. Her mother would have hated grand. It was a cairn of grey stone, waist-high, with a wooden post beside it where travelers tied ribbons — red for grief, white for gratitude, and lately, since the story had gotten around, a third color: yellow, for the ones who'd burned themselves to hold something shut.

There were a great many yellow ribbons now. More every season. Ansel tried not to find that bitter. Mostly she succeeded.

She knelt and did what she always did first: checked the stones. Old habit from smuggling days — checking whether a cache had been disturbed. Some habits outlive their reasons. The cairn was undisturbed except for fresh wildflowers tucked into a crevice, stems still damp, which meant someone had come this morning. Probably the Halloway children from the farm below, who had decided some years ago that the Burned Woman of Thornfell was the most interesting person in regional history and treated her memorial like a shrine to a particularly dramatic aunt.

Ansel approved. Her mother would have pretended not to be pleased, and then been pleased.

She sat down on the flat stone beside the cairn, pulled her knees up, and got out the little book.

It was a ledger, actually — a real one, bought from a stationer in Cinderhatch who had gone very pale when he realized whose hand would be writing in it, and then charged her double anyway, which Ansel had respected enormously. She'd been keeping it for three years now. Not a journal. She'd tried journals; they turned into either self-pity or propaganda, depending on the week. This was something more honest.

It was an account book. Debits and credits. What she'd paid, what she'd gained, and whether the arithmetic held.

She licked her pencil and opened to the page she'd been avoiding all month.

* * *

**DEBITS,** she wrote, because starting with the losses was the only way she trusted herself to keep going.

*One mother's face.* The oldest entry, the first toll, paid at the court's gate before she understood that the court always overcharged and that "first" memories were priced like rare wine. She had known, going in, that she might lose it. She had done it anyway, because the gate-warden had asked, and because somewhere under the fear she'd thought: *she'd have paid it for me.* Later she learned her mother had paid far more, and voluntarily, and that the woman in the Ledger's oldest pages was braver than any story the burnings told. But knowing didn't refill the well. Once a month, the court lent her mother's face back to her, and once a month she gave it back, and every time it felt like handing over a sleeping child.

*Four years.* Scattered ones, mostly — the court took years the way magpies took buttons, wherever they glittered. She'd lost the year she learned to swim, which meant she now technically couldn't swim and refused to test it. She'd lost most of a winter in Cinderhatch, including, apparently, a romance, because Odile kept bringing it up at dinners with the merciless delight of a woman who remembered everything and forgave almost as much. *"You were terrible for each other,"* Odile would say, *"and you cried for a month, and I held your hair back, and you don't remember any of it, and honestly that's the cruelest thing the Hollow ever did — to me."*

*A nameless afternoon.* This was the one that haunted her precisely because it wouldn't haunt her. One whole day, gone entire — sun, weather, company, conversation, all of it — and the court hadn't even needed it for anything. Sef's successor had explained once, apologetically, that the court sometimes took whole days because days were easier to store than pieces, the way you'd carry water in a cup rather than cupped hands. Somewhere in the deep galleries there was a shelf — she'd seen it, once, rows upon rows of small glass vessels glowing softly — and on that shelf was an afternoon with her name on the stopper, and she would never know if it had been a good one. That was the trick of it. The court didn't just take your past. It took your certainty about your past. She could no longer swear that any given day of her life had happened, or hadn't. She'd stopped swearing. She'd started writing things down instead, which was probably why the stationer's ledger had become what it was.

*Odile's name.* For eleven days, once. A toll demanded at the worst possible moment, deep in the court, and Ansel had paid it rather than turn back, and had spent nearly two weeks walking beside a stranger who was kind to her and called her "the guide" and shared her rations anyway. When the memory came home — the court returned that one early, unasked, which Sef's successor swore was unprecedented and Odile swore was guilt — Ansel had hugged her so hard the old woman's ribs creaked.

*"Never do that again,"* Odile had said into her shoulder.

*"I can't promise that."*

*"Then promise me you'll write my name somewhere. In case."*

So: page one of the ledger, top line, in letters too large to miss. **ODILE FERRAN. WARD-KEEPER. MY FRIEND. SHE DRINKS BUT SHE IS ALWAYS RIGHT.**

*Tomas's childhood.* Not his — hers of him. The court had taken her memories *of* him as part of the price of his freedom, reasoning — with the flawless, horrible logic of ledgers everywhere — that a soul restored should not arrive pre-weighted with someone else's grief. He had come back to flesh whole, and she had met him nearly as a stranger, and they had rebuilt it from scratch: songs first, because songs survived in the body even when the mind lost them, then stories, then the slow accumulation of new years to replace the old ones. It worked. It was working. But some nights he'd laugh at something and she'd feel the shape of a missing echo, a joke they'd surely made before, in a year she no longer owned.

She sat with the list a long while. The wind moved through the larches. The fox had curled up against her boot, radiating patience, or possibly waiting for lunch.

* * *

**CREDITS,** she wrote next, and here her hand steadied, because this column was easier and she refused to pretend otherwise.

*The stars.* All of them. Every night, over every city, for everyone, forever — or as close to forever as pacts got. She had stood on the walls of Rivermoor the first night they returned and watched grown soldiers weep at lights they'd forgotten existed. There was no honest way to shrink that.

*Tomas.* Alive, named, stubborn, currently arguing with the Cinderhatch town council about flood defenses as though he'd been doing it his whole life, which, in fairness, he sort of had — souls remembered duty even when they forgot birthdays. He was better at politics than she was. He was better at most things than she was, and told her so weekly, and she let him, because watching him be alive was a pleasure she intended to hoard greedily for the rest of her life.

*The beasts.* Not hers — nothing was ever really theirs — but theirs, hers to walk among. The lion from her first caravan still greeted her on the high passes. The bats had taught her three new verses of the pact-song. Even the bear from the border patrol — the one she'd turned against Marchen's soldiers in the first wild act of her life — had apparently forgiven her, or at least stopped holding grudges, which among bears was the same thing.

*Her mother, understood.* This was the newest entry and the hardest to write, so she wrote it slowly. For twenty years she had carried her mother's death like a brand: proof that the gift destroyed, that hiding was wisdom, that love meant silence. And it had all been wrong. Her mother hadn't burned because she was caught. She had burned because she chose to — because a crack in the star-pact needed sealing and the sealing wanted a willing fire, and she had walked into it the way you'd walk into a river to save a drowning child, without hesitation, without drama, without even telling her daughter why. Not shame. Sacrifice. Not a warning. An inheritance.

Ansel looked up at the cairn, at the yellow ribbons stirring in the wind, and felt the old knot in her chest loosen another degree. Not gone. Some things didn't fully untie. But looser.

*And,* she added, after a moment, *one very good mule.*

Gentle humor was also an inheritance, though she suspected she'd gotten hers from her father, whoever he'd been. The ledger didn't say. The court hadn't taken him; he'd simply never been there to take.

* * *

She totaled the columns, because she was her mother's daughter and her mother's daughter totaled things.

The debits were heavy. She didn't lie to herself about that — lying to yourself was how the Hollow got its hooks in people in the first place, Marchen had proven that much. The faces and years were genuinely gone-gone, not sad-now-but-fine-later gone. She would stand at this cairn every month for the rest of her life and hand her mother's face back across the counter, and it would cost something every single time, and anyone who told her the sacrifice had made her stronger could go negotiate with the bear.

But the credits column ran off the page.

She sat back and looked at the two lists side by side and asked herself the question she asked every month, the one the whole ledger existed to answer: *was it worth it?*

And gave the answer she always gave, the honest one, the one she'd carved into the back cover in small letters the first winter:

***Mostly.***

Mostly was not a small word. Mostly was the biggest word she knew. Mostly meant: yes, and I'm not pretending the cost away. Mostly meant: ask me again next month. Mostly meant that a woman who had spent her whole life concealing things — her gift, her grief, her brother, herself — had finally found one thing she could say plainly, and it was this: she had paid a terrible price, and she would pay it again, and both of those sentences were true at once, and she was tired of pretending truths had to travel alone.

The fox yawned enormously.

"Yes, fine," Ansel said, and dug the last strip of dried fish out of her pack. "You've earned it. You sat through the accounting."

The fox accepted tribute with dignity.

* * *

She stayed until the light went long and gold. She retied two of the yellow ribbons that had frayed. She told her mother about the flood council argument, in detail, doing voices, because if there was one thing she'd learned from losing and regaining a family, it was that you told the stories while you had the face to tell them with.

On her way down, she paused at the treeline and looked back once. The cairn stood grey and plain against the burning larches, and above it the peak rose white into a sky already deepening toward evening, and in that sky — she checked, she always checked, she would check until she died — the first stars were coming out. One, then three, then a scatter of them, faithful as ever, lit by a pact renewed and a voice bound willing.

Tomorrow was the anniversary. The starless night, they called it now, though fewer people remembered to shudder when they said it. Tomorrow she would climb to the top of Thornfell with the whole mountain listening — beasts in the passes, courtiers at the gates, cities in the valleys below — and sing the pact-song, and everything with ears would answer.

Including, if the rumors reaching Cinderhatch were true, some things that didn't have ears yet. Something new was moving in the deep galleries, something the young courtier described in her letters with a mixture of terror and delight as *"a sound learning to be a voice."* The Queen had been cagey about it. The Queen was always cagey about things that surprised her, which was itself surprising.

Ansel shouldered her pack and started down through the dusk, the fox trotting ahead, the Hollow humming its evening note beneath her boots like a house settling, like a choir warming up.

Whatever was learning to sing down there, tomorrow it would meet the whole mountain's voice at once.

She hoped it had been practicing.
