## 40. When the Hollow Sings

The singing started three years after the stars came back, though nobody in Cinderhatch agreed on when, exactly.

Old Marta at the Waystone Inn swore she'd heard it first — a low thread of melody rising out of the mountain on a spring evening while she hung washing, so faint she'd assumed it was her late husband humming from wherever late husbands go. Then the drovers heard it on the high pass above the Drowned Stair. Then a wool merchant with no imagination whatsoever reported that the stones near the third switchback had *sung back* to him when he'd whistled, and he'd been so unnerved he'd dropped his lunch down a ravine.

By the time Ansel Quintana heard about all of this, she was the one they came to ask.

"It's wind," said the young clerk from the Reckoning Commission, who had come up from Rivermoor with a satchel of documents and an expression like a man hoping to be told there was nothing under his bed.

"Is it?" Ansel said.

He shifted. "Well. The Commission's position is that—"

"The Commission's position," Ansel said, "is that the mountain is full of hollow things bound by a pact renewed by a woman who is now half-hollow herself, and that those hollow things have *voices*, and that voices sometimes sing. You can write 'wind' if it helps you sleep."

The clerk wrote *wind*.

Ansel didn't blame him. There was a version of the truth that was easy to carry and a version that was not, and she had spent most of her life learning which truths people could hold without dropping them down a ravine. The truth was that the Thornfell stones sang because she sang, or perhaps she sang because the stones did; after three years of binding she had stopped being able to find the seam between the two, and mostly she had stopped looking.

She was not, whatever the ballads claimed, trapped. That was the part everyone got wrong. She could leave. She left often — walked down to Cinderhatch for Odile's terrible stew, rode as far as Rivermoor to testify or argue or drink wine that was better than anything within forty miles of the peak. The court lived in her now the way a second language lives in you: always present, sometimes surfacing unbidden, occasionally making her say something strange in company. But a language you carry does not carry you. She went where she pleased.

It was only that she was never entirely *away*.

* * *

On the evening the story properly begins — though "begins" is generous, since endings that become beginnings are rather the whole point of the thing — Ansel was walking the high passes at dusk with a mule named Duchess who was, in Ansel's professional opinion, the finest animal in the Thornfells and would hear no arguments on the subject.

Duchess had opinions too. Most of them concerned oats.

"Not yet," Ansel told her. "You've had supper."

*Supper was insufficient,* said Duchess, in the wordless way mules say everything, which is to say with her ears pinned and her whole body radiating grievance.

"You're talking to the wrong woman about insufficiency," Ansel said. "I once traded my mother's face for passage through a flooded mine. You got shortchanged on oats. We've all suffered."

That was the kind of joke she made these days. It landed differently depending on the audience. With strangers it landed badly — a silence, a careful change of subject, someone suddenly very interested in their boots. With Odile it landed as a flat look and the words *"that's not funny, Ansel."* With Tomas it landed as a laugh, because Tomas had decided early on that if his sister was going to make jokes about the worst year of their lives, someone ought to laugh at them, and he had appointed himself to the post permanently.

With the court, the joke landed as something else entirely. The deep things below heard *mother* and stirred, and somewhere beneath six hundred feet of stone, a hunger older than the cities turned its attention upward like a cat hearing its name, and Ansel felt it against the inside of her ribs — not painful, never painful anymore, just *present*. A hand resting lightly between her shoulder blades.

*Careful,* the mountain said, in the voice it used for everything now: patient, vast, faintly amused.

"I'm being careful," Ansel muttered. "I'm always careful."

*You are telling oat jokes to a mule.*

"Somebody has to keep morale up."

Duchess snorted, satisfied to be included in any conversation, and plodded on.

* * *

The pass they were climbing that evening was the old smuggler road above Cinderhatch, the one Ansel had walked a thousand times in the years when her gift was a secret and her cargo was untaxed brandy. It looked different now. Everything did, but this road especially, because this was the road where she'd bargained with a mountain lion over safe passage and called it tracking, and every stone here remembered her lie even if no person did.

She paused at the switchback where it had happened. Habit, more than sentiment. Below her, Cinderhatch was lighting its lamps — a scatter of gold in the blue shadow of the valley, smoke rising straight in the still air. Somewhere down there Odile Ferran was arguing with the Reckoning Commission by letter, which was how Odile did most things these days: loudly, in writing, with footnotes. *Re: your inquiry regarding ward-restoration costs, please see attached list of everything I know, which is more than you deserve.* Three pages. Odile sent them weekly. The Commission had stopped replying months ago, which Odile interpreted as victory.

Higher up, a light moved along the far ridge — one of the night guides, licensed now, carrying a lantern instead of hiding one. Ansel knew every one of them by silhouette. Half of them had once threatened to gut her for choosing the mountain over the network. The other half worked for her. The overlap was considerable, and everyone pretended not to find it funny except Ansel, who found it extremely funny and said so at every guild meeting.

And above all of it — she tipped her head back — the stars.

They still caught her sometimes, the way they'd caught everyone in the first weeks after the return: mid-stride, mid-sentence, head thrown back like a woman struck. Three years and the whole free cities still flinched skyward at dusk. Children born after the starless night were taught the constellations as history lessons. *This one went out. All of them went out. And then a woman walked into a mountain, and this is what she paid, and this is what we owe her,* and Ansel wished they'd stop teaching that last part, because what people owed her was nothing, and what she owed the mountain was everything, and debts that ran in both directions tended to confuse the sort of people who wrote ballads.

There was a new star, actually. Small, stubborn, low on the southern horizon, visible only from the high passes. The court had noticed it the winter before last and could not explain it. Odile theorized it was the pact itself, made visible — the binding burning like a wick. Ansel privately thought it might be Sef.

She had never told anyone that. It was the kind of thing you kept, the way you kept a pressed flower: proof that a moment happened, even if the moment was only a guess. Sef Andrade, courtier of ash, who had chosen his own name at the end and held a gallery of screaming stone together long enough for four hundred souls to walk out of it. If anyone deserved to burn forever in a sky that had forgotten him, it was the man who had spent fifty years forgetting himself.

*He would hate the sentimentality,* the mountain observed.

"He would hate it enormously," Ansel agreed. "That's why I do it."

*You honor him by refusing to grieve him properly.*

"I honor him by remembering him wrong on purpose. He'd prefer that. He lied about his own death twice before he managed a real one."

The mountain considered this, the way it considered everything: slowly, thoroughly, with the weight of six centuries behind each thought. *True,* it conceded finally, which from the Hollow Queen was practically applause.

* * *

They made camp at the high shelf where the lion had once granted her passage, because Duchess liked the grass there and because Ansel liked the echo. The court's song was strongest in this place — the deep chord the bound things hummed day and night, the sound the travelers heard and mistook for wind. To Ansel it was less a sound than a temperature. Warmth in the bones. A hearth-fire lit in a room she couldn't see but always knew was there.

Some nights she sang with it. Not the pact-song — that was for the anniversary, for the peak, for the formal business of keeping two cities standing. This was the other singing, the private kind. Her mother's lullaby, mostly. The one she'd sung to a soul-flame that had almost forgotten her, the one that had reached through the Ledger's erasure and found her brother waiting on the other side of it.

Tomas was coming up the mountain tomorrow. He'd written ahead, in his terrible handwriting, on paper that smelled of the tannery: *Arriving Thursday if the ferry behaves. Bringing cheese. Do not let Odile cook it into whatever she cooks things into. Also I dreamed about Mother again — the good dream, where she's singing. Tell you the rest. Bring the good tea, not the bark water you pretend is tea.*

Ansel had read the letter four times. She would read it another four tonight, and tomorrow she would complain about the handwriting, and he would complain about her tea, and neither of them would ever quite get over the miracle of complaining at each other, because for twenty years there had been nothing to complain about — a name in a scrap of cloth, a flame in a cathedral of bone, a boy sold so completely that even his own soul had lost the reasons it loved anything.

Freed souls had rebuilt him. That was the strangest grace note of all, the one the ballads handled worst: gratitude made flesh. When the Ledger burned and the sold souls poured free, enough of them owed their release to the Quintana family — mother, daughter, the whole stubborn line of sacrifice — that the court's freed dead had pooled what remained of themselves and given it shape. Not resurrection exactly. Something more like a *return*, the way a river returns to a lake. Tomas woke in the ash-gardens with his name in his mouth and twenty years to catch up on, and he had spent those years catching up loudly, voraciously, asking questions until strangers fled, eating everything, learning every song.

He remembered their mother now. That was the part that undid Ansel every time she thought about it. She had paid her mother's face into the dark and never gotten it back — that toll was spent, gone, one of the holes in her that the binding filled with song instead of memory. But Tomas remembered. He remembered the exact tune, the way she'd held the high notes a little sharp, the words she'd changed to make them nonsense because nonsense made the children laugh. And when he sang it, Ansel could stand inside the memory like warmth from a fire she hadn't built, borrowing a past she'd pawned, and it was almost — *almost* — the same as having it.

Almost was a word she'd made peace with. Mostly.

* * *

Dusk came down the valley like slow water. Duchess dozed. Ansel sat with her back against a stone that was warm when it should have been cold, and watched the lamps of Cinderhatch multiply, and felt the court breathing beneath her, and listened.

A party of travelers was coming up the lower road — she heard them before she saw them, boots and pack-creaks and a child's voice asking a question children always asked on this stretch. Ansel smiled. She knew the question.

"Papa. Papa, listen. Is that wind?"

The father's answer drifted up, half-lost: "...just wind, love. Mountains make all kinds of..."

But the child wasn't satisfied. Children never were, on this road. Ansel watched them come around the bend below — five of them, a family moving house by the look of the overloaded mule, heading for the new settlements in the high valleys where land was cheap and the wards were honest. The child had stopped walking. She stood with her head cocked, listening to the stones sing, and Ansel saw the exact moment the girl decided that adults were wrong about this, the way children decide such things a dozen times a day and are usually corrected and occasionally, very occasionally, are not.

Ansel leaned toward the edge of the shelf and sang.

Just a phrase. Two bars of the lullaby, pitched soft, sent down through the rock the way her voice traveled now — not through air, but through stone and root and the deep grain of the mountain, arriving everywhere at once like sun through fog. The stones carried it. They always carried it.

Below, the child went absolutely rigid with delight.

"*Papa!*" she shrieked. "*Papa, it IS singing, it answered me, it — Mama, the MOUNTAIN—*"

The family stopped. Stared. The father laughed nervously and said something about echoes, and the mother squinted up at the dark hillside, and for a moment Ansel met her eyes across thirty yards of dusk and did nothing to hide — no tracker's blankness, no smuggler's shrug. Just a woman on a rock, smiling, singing to a child.

The mother's breath caught. Ansel saw her decide — saw the arithmetic run behind her eyes, the stories she'd heard weighed against the woman she was looking at — and then the mother lifted a hand. Not a wave, exactly. Something older than a wave. The gesture people had begun making on the mountain roads, palm open, fingers spread: *I see you. I'm not afraid.*

Ansel raised her hand the same way.

The child, meanwhile, was singing back — tuneless, ecstatic, inventing words — and the stones carried her small voice up the mountain too, because the binding went both directions now, that had been the whole point, that had been Ansel's one non-negotiable term written into the heart of the pact: *the court will feed on memory freely given, never stolen, and every voice in it counts, and every voice can leave.* The Queen had accepted because the Queen had been starving for trust longer than she'd been starving for anything else. Six hundred years of stolen meals and what she'd wanted, all along, was someone to sit down at the table on purpose.

The family moved on. The child kept turning around to wave until the road bent her out of sight, and her thin glad noise lingered in the stones for a long time afterward, and Ansel sat with it the way you sit with anything precious: carefully, and without needing to explain it to anyone.

"That'll be another legend," she told Duchess, who had one ear open out of professional obligation. "Singing witch on the high road. By midsummer they'll have me ten feet tall."

*Ten feet tall would be inefficient,* said the mountain.

"You're ten feet tall."

*I am a mountain. Efficiency was never among my vices.*

* * *

Full dark came, and the stars thickened, and Ansel banked the little fire and lay back against the warm stone with her cloak pulled up and Duchess's nose draped across her boots like a furry accusation.

She took inventory. She did this some nights — the honest accounting her mother's memorial had taught her, the listing of losses without flinching from them. Faces: her mother's, gone, replaced by borrowed warmth. Years: a scattered handful, an afternoon here, a season there, paid out at tolls in the deep dark. Certain names. The taste of a specific bread from a market she could no longer picture. A whole nameless afternoon that bothered her precisely because she couldn't miss what she couldn't remember — a hole shaped like nothing, which is the strangest kind of hole there is.

Against the ledger of loss: the stars. The cities standing. Four hundred souls walking free into the ash-light with their names restored. Odile, who had lost her memories of Ansel and gained, by stubborn incremental rebuilding, a friendship with her that was somehow better the second time — newer, chosen, unencumbered by old debts. A brother asleep in the valley below with cheese in his pack. A child on the road below, singing back.

A stone that was warm when it should have been cold.

Worth it, she decided. Again. As she decided most nights.

Mostly, she amended, because honesty mattered, and because somewhere in the dark the mountain chuckled at the amendment, and because the chuckle was worth more than perfect peace anyway. Peace without texture would have been just another kind of hollow.

Sleep came easy, the way it did now. In it, or in the place that wasn't quite sleep, the court sang its deep chord, and she sang with it, and the boundary between her voice and its voice was a line on water — visible if you stared, real if you believed in it, and passing, always passing, in both directions.

* * *

She rose before dawn, out of habit older than the binding, and climbed.

The peak was an hour's walk from the high shelf, and she made it as the east went from charcoal to ember, and she stood on the summit of the Thornfells with the world laid out below her like a promise kept: Cinderhatch waking, Rivermoor beyond it with its cranes and its quarrels, the high valleys filling with new roofs, the roads — open roads now, licensed and lamplit — threading the passes like stitches closing a wound.

Tomorrow was the anniversary. Tomorrow she would stand here with Odile and Tomas and the guild guides and however many pilgrims made the climb, and she would sing the pact-song, and the beasts would answer from the ridgelines — wolves and bears and the blind bats wheeling out of the Drowned Stair at dusk — and the court would answer from below, and the cities from their bells, and the whole layered world would hold its harmony for one hour, one song, one held breath of everything she'd bled for.

But today was today, and today the peak belonged to her alone.

The wind came up from the south, off the new star's patch of sky, and it smelled like snowmelt and pine and the mineral breath of deep places. Ansel closed her eyes and let it move through her — the half of her that was here, the half that was below, the whole that was neither and both and simply, at last, at the bottom of everything, herself.

Then she opened her eyes, turned her face to the brightening east, and began to sing.

Not the pact-song. Not yet. Just the lullaby, soft, for practice, for pleasure, for the child on yesterday's road and the brother in tomorrow's valley and the mother whose tune she kept alive in a throat that was half stone now and glad of it.

Far below, the mountain sang back. Farther down, something older and gentler than hunger harmonized. And on the roads and ridges of the Thornfells, in the ears of travelers pausing mid-step to wonder at the wind, the song went out into the morning —

— and the morning, for once, sang first.
