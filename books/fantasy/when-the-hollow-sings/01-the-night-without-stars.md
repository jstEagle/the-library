## 1. The Night Without Stars

The mule's name was Buttercup, which told Dominic everything he needed to know about the sense of humor of whoever had named her, and she was, at this precise moment, refusing to die on a washed-out bridge.

"Come on," Dominic murmured, keeping his voice low enough that only she — and possibly a very attentive beetle — could hear him. "It holds. I checked it myself. Twice."

*You checked it with your eyes,* Buttercup said, in that flat, patient way mules had, like a grandmother explaining for the third time why you couldn't have sweets before dinner. *Eyes are liars. Feet are honest.*

"It held when I put my weight on it."

*You weigh about as much as a sad thought.* She flicked one long ear toward the gap in the road ahead, where the Toll Road's stone had crumbled into the gorge maybe twenty years ago and someone had thrown planks across it in a fit of optimism. The river below ran loud and white in the dark. *I weigh more than a sad thought. I weigh eleven sad thoughts and a wagon.*

Behind her, six wagons of smuggled goods — salt, iron nails, and, if Renna Kade's manifests were to be believed, absolutely nothing else worth hanging anyone over — waited in the black throat of Thornfell Pass. Twelve men and women sat wrapped in blankets, pretending not to watch their guide argue with livestock.

Dominic sighed and pressed his palm against the mule's warm neck. "If I cross first, will you follow?"

*Will you fall?*

"I won't fall."

*Liar,* said Buttercup, but it was an affectionate liar, and she stepped forward anyway, hooves testing each plank with the grave deliberation of a judge weighing evidence. Dominic walked backward ahead of her, hands out, murmuring nonsense — *good girl, steady, yes, that's it* — while the wagon drivers behind them muttered that Pemberton had some kind of way with animals, didn't he, uncanny almost, and the others hushed them because everyone knew you didn't talk about that. Not in Thornfell. Not since the Wardens started lighting their pyres again.

He'd been guiding caravans through these mountains for nine years, and in nine years no one had ever once looked too closely at how he did what he did. People were happy to believe in luck. Luck didn't burn.

Buttercup gained the far side, turned, and gave him a look of profound vindication, as though she had personally engineered the crossing as a lesson in humility.

"You're a saint," Dominic told her.

*I'm hungry,* said Buttercup. *These are compatible.*

He was still smiling at her — at the small, private comedy of his life, the secret he kept folded up inside himself like a letter he could never send — when the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Went out*, all at once, above the eastern peaks, the way a candle goes out when a hand closes over it: a clean absence, total and wrong. Dominic's head came up. The sky over Thornfell's high ridgeline had been thick with stars all evening, the cold hard glitter of autumn, and now there was nothing there at all, just a slab of perfect darkness shaped exactly like where the stars should be, edged in the last few stubborn lights near the horizon.

The caravan felt it before they understood it. Horses screamed. One of the drovers began swearing in a shaking voice, and another began praying, and a third dropped something metal that rang against the stones like a small bell at a funeral.

"Hold!" Dominic called, pitching his voice into the calm register he used for spooked animals, which tonight included every human being present. "Hold positions! It could be weather—"

It was not weather. Weather did not come with a sound.

The sound arrived a heartbeat later — a high, crystalline ringing from the east, from the border, so pure and so vast that Dominic felt it in his back teeth, in the roots of his hair, in the water standing in the horses' buckets, where little rings spread outward from the center as if the whole world were a struck bell.

And then the wards broke.

He heard it happen. Everyone within fifty miles must have heard it: a shattering, enormous and delicate at once, like the world's largest sheet of glass giving way somewhere beyond the peaks. A pulse of pale light rolled along the entire line of the Thornfell border — he saw it reflected on the undersides of the clouds, a sick green-white wash, brightening and guttering — and then the light went out too, and the night came down close and complete, darker than any night Dominic had known in thirty years of living in and around mountains.

In the sudden blackness, Buttercup pressed her nose against his chest.

*Little brother,* she said — she had called him that since the day they met, when he was nineteen and crying behind a stable and she was four and unimpressed — *the mountain is screaming.*

"I know," Dominic whispered.

Because he did know. That was the thing no one else in the caravan could understand, the thing that made his hands go cold on her bridle. He had spent his whole life half-hearing the low conversation of the land — the contentment of good pasture, the grumble of stones settling, the old, slow dreams of things that lived under hills. His mother had called it the hollow-speech and made him swear never, ever to let the Wardens hear him use it. And beneath all its many quiet voices there had always been one note underneath, constant as a heartbeat: the hum of the wards, the great ancient working that ringed Thornfell's borders, fed by something deep in the mountains, keeping out — or keeping in — whatever the pact had been made to hold apart.

That note had just stopped.

The silence it left was worse than the screaming. It was the silence of a heart that has stopped beating, and every beast within earshot knew it. Down the column, horses fought their pickets; somewhere a dog began to howl and would not stop; even the insects had gone quiet, as if the whole living world were holding its breath and listening to the shape of the hole where safety used to be.

"Pemberton." Renna Kade appeared out of the dark at his elbow, silent as she always was, a compact woman with grey in her black braid and eyes like coins left in a well. Her voice was level, but he'd known her ten years and heard the thin wire of fear under it. "What happened?"

"The border wards fell," Dominic said. "All of them. At once."

"That's impossible. Those wards have stood since before the Conclave. They stood through the last war."

"Yes," Dominic said. "They did."

She studied him for a moment too long, the way she sometimes did when she suspected there was more in him than salt-route competence, then turned and started snapping orders — torches doused, wagons tight, watches doubled, no fires until we're off the high road. The caravan moved with the grim efficiency of people who had all, in their various careers, done illegal things during emergencies before.

Dominic stayed by Buttercup, one hand buried in her mane, and listened to the mountain.

The hollow-voices were in chaos. He caught fragments as they tumbled past him like debris in a flood: the terror of burrow-things, the grief of an old oak feeling its roots go strange, and beneath it all, rising from the deep places under the peaks, something vast and wounded trying to speak and failing, its voice fraying like rope pulled over a sharp edge. A spirit-voice. He'd felt the hollow spirits his whole life the way you feel the sun through a wall — warmth without shape, presence without words. Now one of them was *dying*, or calling, or both, and its voice was full of a word it kept repeating that his gift translated, imperfectly, terribly, as:

*Told. Told. Someone must be told.*

"Dominic." Renna again, low. "You've gone white as milk. What aren't you telling me?"

"That the bridge held," he said, "and I think we're going to want to be a lot more worried about other things."

She snorted — Renna Kade did not scare easily and disliked being reminded that other people might — and moved off down the column. Above them, the starless blot over the eastern peaks remained, absolute, and as Dominic watched, it seemed to him that it spread, very slightly, the darkness creeping westward like ink through wet cloth.

Buttercup leaned into him, heavy and warm.

*We should go home,* she said.

"We don't get to go home," Dominic said. "Home is on the wrong side of whatever that is."

*Then we should go toward it,* said Buttercup, who was, despite everything, the bravest soul in Thornfell, *because the screaming place is that way, and someone is asking for help.*

And far below the mountains, in the deep hollow places where the oldest pacts slept, something broken began, with the last of its strength, to reach for anyone in the dark who might hear it.

Dominic heard it.

God help him, he had always been able to hear it.
