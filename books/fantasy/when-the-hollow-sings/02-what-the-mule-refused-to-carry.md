## 2. What the Mule Refused to Carry

The mule's name was Buttercup, which she resented deeply, and she made her opinion of it known every morning by refusing to answer to anything but a low, grudging flick of one ear.

"Come on, sweetheart," Dominic murmured, keeping his voice under the wind. The caravan was still a half mile back, lanterns doused, wheels wrapped in wool so the smugglers could move quiet through Thornfell's eastern passes. He'd walked ahead as he always did — not because Renna Kade asked him to, exactly, but because nobody else in her crew could hear what he heard, and what he heard right now was Buttercup saying, in the flat, stubborn way mules had, *No.*

Not *no, I'm tired.* Not *no, my hoof hurts.* This was a *no* that came up from somewhere deep and old, the kind of no that lived in the bones.

"Sweetheart," he tried again. "It's just the pass."

*It is not just the pass,* said Buttercup, and stopped dead in the middle of the trail, all four hooves planted like fence posts.

The pass in question was called the Kettle by anyone with a map and the Screaming Place by anyone who'd listened to animals for longer than five minutes. Dominic had taken caravans through it a dozen times without incident — narrow walls of gray stone, a stream running cold over slick rock, nothing worse than the usual complaints from the horses about echoes. But that was before last night. Before every star above the peaks had gone out at once, like candles pinched by an enormous hand, and the border wards had shattered with a sound he still felt in his teeth.

He crouched, pretending to check Buttercup's near-side hoof, and spoke softly into the space between them where words didn't need air. "Tell me what you smell."

The mule's thoughts came slow, the way they always did, heavy as millstones turning. *Stone. Water. Old blood gone to dust. And screaming. The stones remember screaming.*

"The stones remember," Dominic repeated aloud, barely a breath. Behind him, one of the caravan men — Petch, who snored like a landslide — called up the trail asking what was holding them.

"Mule's being difficult," he called back. "Give me a minute."

He gave himself considerably more than a minute. He walked the last fifty yards alone, one hand trailing the cold rock of the pass wall, listening. And there it was, underneath the water-sound and the wind: a thin, high note, so far below hearing that his ears dismissed it and something older in him did not. It was the sound of a voice stretched too thin. A voice that had been screaming for a very long time and had nearly forgotten how to stop.

Dominic stood in the middle of the Kettle Pass with his heart going like a rabbit's and understood two things. First: whatever was making that sound had been trapped here since long before last night, and last night — when the wards fell, when the stars went dark — had only made it worse. Second: if he walked away now, he would hear it every night for the rest of his life, the way you hear a dripping tap through three closed doors.

He turned back down the trail. "Petch. Tell Renna I'm scouting a detour. Take the caravan around by the low road; we'll meet at the shepherd's hut past the saddle."

"It'll cost you," Petch said cheerfully. Renna charged for everything, including detours, including opinions.

"Put it on my account."

"You don't have an account."

"I do now."

* * *

Buttercup followed him willingly enough once he turned toward the screaming — which told him everything. Mules did not follow danger. Mules followed the thing that frightened other things. She picked her way off the main trail onto a goat track barely wider than her shoulders, climbing until the stream became a thread and the thread became ice, and all the while the thin note grew louder, and all the while she kept saying, grimly, *Yes. Yes. There. Hurry. There is not much left.*

"Not much left of what?" he asked, and got no answer, because mules considered some questions beneath answering.

The track ended at a shelf of stone overlooking a hollow — a bowl in the mountain's shoulder where snow lay unbroken except by one long scar, as if something enormous had been dragged down into the center. At the hollow's heart stood a ward-stone.

Dominic knew ward-stones the way every border child knew them: tall, finger-thick with old carvings, humming faintly if you pressed your ear against them. Except this one wasn't standing anymore. It had come down — cracked clean through its middle, the upper half toppled into the hollow — and where it had fallen, the ground around it was wrong. The snow hadn't melted so much as *given up*, lying gray and brittle like ash. And beneath the fallen half of the stone, in the gap where the crack had opened, something was moving.

Or rather, something was trying to move and mostly failing.

Dominic slid down the scar on his heels, breath ragged, and reached the stone as the sun finally cleared the eastern ridge and threw light into the crack. What he saw made him sit down hard in the dead snow.

There was a face in the stone.

Not carved — *grown*, the way frost grows on glass, lines and hollows and two closed eyes rendered in the granite itself, as if the rock had decided, ages ago, to dream of a person and never quite woken. As he watched, the eyes opened. They were the same gray as the stone, and they were looking at him.

"Oh," said Dominic, to no one, feelingly.

*You hear,* said the face. The voice arrived the way the screaming had — under the ears, straight into the marrow — but where the screaming had been thin, this voice was vast and slow, a bell rung underwater. *Little speaker. You hear me. How long has it been since one of your kind heard me?*

"I— I don't know." His mouth was dry. "What are you?"

*A hollow spirit. Keeper of the threshold. Warden of the wards, if you will forgive the irony.* Something that might have been amusement moved the stone face, and flakes of granite sifted down. Then the vastness guttered, and what came next was small, and that was somehow far more terrible. *I am dying, little speaker. The pact that fed me has been broken from the other side. I have been screaming into the mountain for nine days, and the mountain has grown deaf, and now even my scream is ending.*

Nine days. Nine days since the stars went dark, since the wards shattered, since the world's hidden machinery broke open on the same awful night. Dominic's hands were shaking. He pressed one flat against the warm-cracked stone because it seemed rude, somehow, to touch a dying creature with a fist.

"Who broke it?" he asked, though some part of him already knew, some part that had spent thirty years hiding his gift from Wardens whose bonfires burned beast-speakers, some part that had always suspected the machinery ran on something.

And the spirit looked at him — really looked, with those gray stone eyes that held weather older than any kingdom — and said, *See.*

* * *

Later, Dominic would try to describe what happened next and fail. The closest he ever managed was: *it was like being read aloud.*

The memory pressed into him without kindness or cruelty — simply urgency, the way a drowning man grips whatever floats. He was suddenly standing in another time entirely, in this same hollow when it was green, when the ward-stone stood whole and sang instead of screamed, and the mountains were full — *full*, thronged, the slopes alive with spirits like standing storms, hundreds of them, thousands, their voices braided together into the hum that kept the border wards alight and the war-machines of two kingdoms from crossing the passes.

He saw the first war, the one no history book admitted to: armies of iron and fire marching on the Conclave, and the beast-speakers of that age walking unarmed into the mountain councils, offering the pact — *our voices for your vigilance; our memories for your strength* — and the hollow accepting, because the alternative was ash.

He saw centuries of the bargain kept. Quiet years. Singing years. Beast-speakers born generation after generation, each one able to carry the voices, each one honored — *honored*, and the word landed in Dominic's chest like a blow, because he had spent his whole life hiding the very thing these people had once been loved for.

Then he saw the council chamber in Warden High Command, recent — terribly recent — and the maps spread across the table, and the generals' voices, smooth with justification: *the machines require more. The war requires more. The pacts are sentimental antiquities. Divert the feed.* He saw the order signed. He saw engineers arrive at the hollows with drills and siphons and no ceremony at all, tapping the ward-stones like wells, draining the spirits' strength into engines of war while the spirits screamed into deaf stone.

And he saw the last part, the part the spirit saved for the end, the part that made him come back to himself on his knees in the dead snow with tears freezing on his cheeks: a woman, generations ago, the first beast-speaker to renew the pact after it frayed — standing before the hollow, offering herself, and the price named plainly, the price that had always been named plainly: *your memories of everyone you love. All of them. That is the coin. That is the only coin.*

The vision released him.

For a long while Dominic simply knelt there, breathing, while Buttercup stood over him like a gray umbrella, her nose nearly touching his hair.

"That's proof," he said at last, and his voice came out cracked. "That's testimony. The Wardens broke the pact. They're killing you, all of you, to feed their machines, and I'm — I'm the only one who heard it."

*I am the last,* said the spirit. The gray eyes were dimming, the stone face beginning, horribly, to smooth over, features dissolving back into ordinary geology. *When I end, the others' voices end with me, and no one will be able to say what was done. Unless someone carries it. Unless a speaker carries the truth to those who can act — the Conclave, little speaker. The neutral ground. Carry my testimony, and let it be spoken before the pact dies entirely and the mountains rise to settle their own accounts.*

"And if I don't?"

*Then the vengeance comes, and it will not distinguish the guilty from the children of the guilty.* A pause, vast and tired. *I am sorry. I know what I ask. I know what it costs to carry us. I can feel your fear of it from here, and I am ashamed to ask anyway. But I have no one else. You are the last who hears.*

Dominic sat in the ash-colored snow and thought about his little house behind Renna's warehouse, his kettle, his routines, his careful, careful life built on never being noticed. He thought about the bonfires. He thought about the coin.

"I'm not brave," he told the spirit honestly. "I want you to know that up front. Whatever stories get told afterward, I was scared the entire time."

*I know,* said the spirit, gently, the way you'd speak to a frightened animal — and Dominic almost laughed, because he recognized the technique. *Bravery is only fear that keeps walking. Come here, little speaker. Let me give you what must be carried, and teach your bones to hold it.*

He crawled forward and pressed both hands and, after a moment, his forehead against the stone.

The fragment settled into him like a coal dropped into straw — small, contained, unmistakably burning. Proof, folded tight: the council chamber, the signatures, the drills biting into living song. He would carry it whether he wanted to or not now. It lived in him.

Behind him, distantly, down in the valley, a horn sounded — twice, then a third time. Renna's recall signal, sharp and urgent, the one she used when something had gone wrong.

The spirit's eyes were closing. *Go,* it whispered, and the stone was only stone. *They are coming for you already. Go quickly, little speaker. And whatever you do — trust the mule. She has better sense than either of us.*

Dominic scrambled up the scar with his heart hammering, Buttercup trotting grimly beside him, and below in the valley he could see them now — riders on the toll road, too many of them, wearing colors that weren't Renna's.

Warden green.
