## 3. Ashes on the Toll Road

Captain Ilse Varga had been a Warden for nineteen years, and in all that time she had never once seen the border wards go dark. Nobody had. The wards were as constant as the mountains themselves — older than the Warden Corps, older than Thornfell's crown, older, the old-timers said, than the idea of borders at all. Wardens did not ask what kept them burning. Wardens simply patrolled their light, the way lighthouse keepers do not interrogate the sea.

So when the sky over the peaks had gone starless two nights ago — not clouded, *emptied*, as if someone had reached up and picked the heavens clean — and every ward-stone from Greywater to the Sunder Pass had shattered like dropped crockery, High Command's response had been swift, decisive, and, in Varga's professional opinion, completely insane.

*Smugglers*, the dispatch said. *Sabotage by smuggling syndicates along the toll road. Investigate, root out, restore order.*

Varga read the dispatch again as her wagon rattled up the toll road, and frowned at it the way one frowns at a dog that has just claimed responsibility for the harvest.

"With respect, sir," she'd said to Major Ostrand via the speaking-glass, "smugglers are many things, but they are not stupid enough to break the wards they hide behind. That's like an arsonist burning down his own hiding spot."

"Orders are orders, Captain."

"Of course, sir." They were not, in her experience, usually orders. Usually they were instructions that had been arrived at through reasoning. But she saluted the glass anyway, because nineteen years.

Her sergeant, Brennick, rode beside the wagon with his helmet pushed back and his usual air of a man perpetually disappointed by weather. He was reading the same dispatch upside-down in her hands, which was a skill of his.

"Still nonsense?" he asked.

"Still nonsense."

"Good. I hate when you're right about things. It makes the week longer."

Varga almost smiled. Brennick had served with her for eleven years and had perfected the art of being her conscience without ever once using a serious tone of voice. It was, she suspected, the only reason he was still alive; serious men burned out in the Wardens, but Brennick simply eroded, like a cheerful boulder.

The smell reached them before the village did.

Brennick's humor drained out of his face so quickly it was like watching a lamp gutter. "That's not a cookfire."

"No," Varga said. "It isn't."

* * *

Harrowmere had been a toll village — forty houses, a waystation, a shrine to some river god whose name Varga had never learned, and a population that paid its border taxes on time, which in her private ledger made them model citizens. Now it was forty smears of black and ash, the waystation a collapsed ribcage of timber, and the shrine's little stone god lay face-down in a ditch as if it too had tried to flee.

The garrison troops who'd done it were still there. That was the part that turned her stomach. A squad under a Lieutenant Corr — young, sweating, eyes too wide — had occupied the ruins, ostensibly "maintaining order."

"Maintaining order," Varga repeated flatly, standing in what had been a bakery. There was a child's wooden horse in the ashes. She made herself look at it, because looking away felt like a kind of permission.

"The villagers attacked us," Corr said. His voice had the brittle brightness of a man reciting something he'd been told. "When the wards fell, they — they rioted. We were fired upon."

"With what?"

"...What?"

"With what were they firing upon you, Lieutenant? This is a toll village. Their arsenal consists of tax ledgers and turnips."

Corr's mouth worked. Behind him, one of his soldiers — a girl who couldn't have been more than eighteen — turned away and was quietly, thoroughly sick into the ditch.

"There was panic," Corr admitted finally. "The sky went dark. People came out of their houses screaming questions. Someone threw a stone. Sergeant Dulac gave the order to clear the square, and then..." He trailed off, and his hand made a small, helpless gesture that encompassed everything: the ashes, the horse, the dead god in the ditch.

"And then the fires started," Brennick said softly, "and nobody gave any order to stop them."

"They were *harboring smugglers*—" one of the other soldiers began, and Varga rounded on him with such speed that he stepped back into a fence post.

"Were they," she said. "Show me one piece of evidence. One ledger, one hidden crate, one brand-marked doorpost. You've had two days to loot this village for proof, and I notice the only things missing from it are anything worth carrying."

Silence. Somewhere a crow called, once, and got no answer.

"Captain," Corr started, "Major Ostrand's dispatch said—"

"I know exactly what the dispatch said, Lieutenant. I can read. What I cannot do is make it agree with what I'm standing in." She looked back down the ruined high street. Forty houses. Perhaps two hundred people, most of whom she could name from the tax rolls, because she was the sort of captain who read tax rolls. "Get your people out of Harrowmere by nightfall. If I find a single one of them here at dawn, I will arrest them myself, and I will enjoy it more than is seemly."

"You can't—"

"I can, actually. Senior investigating officer, active inquiry. Look it up. And Lieutenant?" She waited until he met her eyes. "Whatever they told you at the garrison — whatever story makes this bearable — do not tell it to me again. I've heard it. It doesn't fit. Go home while you still have one."

* * *

They found the survivor in the shrine's cellar, which was less a cellar than a hole where a determined woman had decided not to die. Her name was Maret Ostrow; she was somewhere between fifty and ancient, built like a fence post, and she emerged from the dark with a hay knife in her hand and an expression that dared them to try anything.

"At ease," Varga said, showing her palms. "Warden Corps. Captain Ilse Varga. I'm investigating what happened here."

"Investigating." Maret spat, with impressive accuracy, into the ash. "Your boys did this, and now your boys are investigating it. That's rich. That's a festival joke."

"It is," Varga agreed quietly. "And it will stop being funny when I find out who gave the order. Tell me what happened. Everything."

Maret studied her for a long moment — the kind of appraisal farmers give livestock at market, honest and unsentimental. Then she sat down on a burnt beam as if her legs had only just now remembered they were angry instead of tired.

"It started before the sky went dark, if you can believe it. Three weeks back, strangers in the pass. Wardens' colors, but wrong — new uniforms, new officers, asking questions about the old shrines. About the hollow places in the mountain." She shrugged. "We told them nothing because we knew nothing. Grandmother used to leave cream out for the mountain at midwinter, but everyone does that. It's manners, not treason."

Varga felt something cold walk down her spine. "Wardens asking about hollow places. Before the collapse."

"Mm. Then two nights past, the whole sky goes empty, and the stones on the ridge — the ones that hum, you've heard them hum — they scream. All together, like something being killed. And then your garrison arrives at a dead run, already frightened, and frightened men with torches are just fire waiting for an excuse." Maret's voice never rose. That was somehow worse. "I survived because I'm too stubborn and too far underground. My sister was not stubborn."

Brennick crouched nearby, hat in his hands, and for once had absolutely nothing funny to say.

"The dispatch says smugglers sabotaged the wards," Varga said.

"Then the dispatch is a lie wearing a uniform, Captain, and I'd know. I've been lied to by professionals." Maret looked up at her, eyes steady as well-water. "You want my advice? Stop reading what they send you and start listening to what the mountain's saying. Because since the stones went quiet, it's not saying nothing. It's holding its breath. And things only hold their breath when something bigger is coming."

* * *

They made camp that night on the ridge above Harrowmere, where the first broken ward-stone stood. Or rather, where half of it stood; the rest was shards, and the shards were wrong. Varga turned a fragment in her gloved fingers. Ward-stone was granite-grey and dull as old bread. This was veined through with silver light — or had been — and the light wasn't extinguished. It was *spent*. Drained, the way a candle gutters when someone drinks the wax.

"Brennick."

"I see it." He crouched beside her, turning his own shard. "Smugglers don't drain ward-stones, Captain. Smugglers don't know how. Half the Corps doesn't know how."

"Somebody does."

"Somebody with new uniforms," Brennick said, "asking about hollow places three weeks before the sky broke." He paused. "I want to state for the record that I hate where this conversation is going."

"Noted for the record."

"Because if High Command broke the wards—"

"—then High Command burned Harrowmere to give the fire somewhere else to point." Varga closed her fist around the shard until the edges bit her palm. Nineteen years. Nineteen years of patrols and ledgers and cold camps and the quiet pride of keeping a border no one thanked her for keeping. It had never once occurred to her to ask what fed the light. She had been a lighthouse keeper after all, content not to interrogate the sea — and someone upstairs had been draining the ocean the entire time.

She thought of Corr's too-wide eyes, of the girl sick in the ditch, of Maret's sister who had not been stubborn enough. She thought of the word *orders*, and how it had always been a floor beneath her feet, and how tonight it felt like a trapdoor.

"Captain," Brennick said carefully. "What are we doing?"

Varga looked east, toward the Sunder Pass and the shattered border, where the official story said villains were running loose in the starless dark. She looked at the shard in her hand, spent silver fading to grey.

"We're going to find whoever's out there in those mountains tonight," she said slowly, "because either they're the criminals Command says they are — or they're the only witnesses to the crime Command committed. Either way, I want to hear them speak before anyone else gets to silence them."

"That's treason, Captain."

"That's investigation, Sergeant. It only becomes treason depending on what we find." She stood, pocketing the shard like evidence, like a promise. "Get some sleep. We ride for the pass at first light."

Above them the sky sat empty and wrong, and far off in the direction of the Sunder Pass, faint as a held breath, Varga would have sworn she heard something singing in the stone — thin, and ragged, and dying.

It sounded, she thought, like testimony.

And it sounded like it didn't have much time left.
