## 4. The Smuggler's Price

Renna Kade kept her court in a defunct tollhouse at the mouth of the Graywater Pass, and she held court the way she did everything — loudly, generously, and with a knife within reach of her left hand. Dominic had known her for eleven years. She'd found him half-starved at fifteen, lying about his age to haul cargo for people who asked no questions, and she'd never once asked him one that mattered.

Until tonight.

He stood in the tollhouse's main room while her people settled around him like crows on a fence — Brannick with his crossbow he polished more than he shot, old Maret who could smell a Warden patrol three miles off through rain, the twins Ferro and Fenn who finished each other's sentences and other people's purses. The fire cracked. Outside, the sky was still wrong, still starless, and everyone pretended not to look at it the way you pretend not to look at a wound.

"So," Renna said. She was a broad woman with silver-shot black hair and a laugh that had gotten men killed out of sheer willingness to hear it again. "Caravan's late. Bridge is gone. And my beast-whisperer comes back down the mountain looking like he swallowed a ghost."

"Just a mule," Dominic said. "Opinionated."

"Dominic." She said his name softly, which from Renna Kade was worse than shouting. "The stars went out. Every ward-stone between here and the coast cracked like dropped crockery. I've got three crews stranded and every smuggler from here to Vell asking if the world's ending." She leaned forward. "You were *up there*. On the mountain. What did you see?"

He thought about lying. He was good at lying; eleven years of practice. But the memory the hollow spirit had pressed into him sat behind his eyes like a coal that wouldn't cool, and when he opened his mouth what came out was true.

"The Wardens broke the pact," he said. "The border wards were fed by the mountain spirits. Have been since before Thornfell had a name. High Command tore them open to fuel their war machines, and the spirit under the Broken Tooth is dying because of it." His voice steadied in a way that surprised him. "I carry its testimony. To the Conclave. That's where I'm going."

The silence afterward had weight. Somewhere outside, the mule — whose name, as far as Dominic knew, was whatever you called her — made a low sound that might have been commentary.

Renna laughed. It was her real laugh, warm as bread, and it should have relieved him. It didn't.

"Testimony," she repeated, delighted. "Oh, Dom. Do you know what you're carrying? Not the truth — nobody pays for truth. You're carrying the only proof alive that the Wardens' entire war is built on theft. The Conclave would pay a kingdom for it. The Wardens would pay two to burn it. And every faction in between—" she spread her hands, rings flashing "—would pay just to keep it from their rivals."

"I'm not selling it."

"Of course you are. You just don't know your own price yet." She stood, came around the table, put her hands on his shoulders. Her hands were small and strong and he'd seen them snap a man's fingers for cheating her porters. "Dom. Sweet boy. I found you starving in a ditch. Everything you have, you have because this family gave it to you. So let me protect you properly. We sell it together. You'll never haul cargo again. You'll have a house. A name that isn't whispered. You'll be safe."

"That's not what the spirit asked of me."

"Spirits ask. Queens decide." Her thumbs pressed in, affectionate and immovable. "Who even knows? Just us?"

"A Warden deserter," Dominic said, and immediately regretted it.

Her eyebrows rose. "A *what*?"

* * *

The deserter in question was unconscious in the stable loft, which Dominic considered the best possible place for him given the circumstances. Tam — nineteen, maybe twenty, Warden-grey coat dyed a bad brown, a gash along his ribs from something with too many teeth that lived above the snowline — had stumbled into the caravan's camp two nights ago babbling about orders he wouldn't follow. Dominic had stitched him by lantern light while the mule watched with an expression of profound judgment.

Now Renna stood at the foot of the ladder, and Tam hung back in the hay shadows like a cat deciding whether the dog was serious.

"He's a child," Dominic said.

"He's a *Warden*."

"He burned his coat. He walked away from them. In this weather, that's a death sentence either way."

"It's a bargaining chip either way," Renna said. "Do you understand what you've done, walking into my house with a dying spirit's secrets in your skull and a runaway soldier in my loft? Do you understand what happens to me if I *don't* sell you?" For the first time, her voice cracked around something that wasn't laughter. "There are people who will come here, Dom. People who make me look like a fishwife. If I don't hand you over, they take my family apart piece by piece to get to you. I love you. I am telling you exactly how much trouble you are, and you're standing there worried about a boy with a hole in him."

"Then come with me," Dominic said. "To the Conclave. All of you. Let the truth—"

"The Conclave doesn't want us. The Conclave wants *clean hands*, and ours haven't been clean since before you were born." She turned away. When she spoke again, her voice was flat and businesslike, and that was the worst sound he'd heard all night, worse than the wards shattering. "Here's what happens now. You leave tonight, alone or otherwise. By dawn, I tell Brannick where you went."

"Renna—"

"Because if I don't send someone after you, they'll send someone worse, and at least Brannick will bring you back *alive*. Probably." She looked at him over her shoulder, and her eyes were wet and furious. "Get out of my house, Dominic Pemberton. Before I change my mind and hug you, and we both do something stupid."

He went. At the door he paused. "The mule stays with me."

A ragged almost-laugh. "Take the mule. Take the whole barn. Just go."

* * *

They left through the goat track behind the stables, because the front gate would have eyes on it by moonrise — except there was no moonrise, no stars at all, just a flat black ceiling pressing down on the world like a lid. The mule picked her way sure-footed through dark that would have broken a horse's leg, and Tam clung to her pack frame with grey lips and gritted teeth, and Dominic led them up into the high passes with Renna's voice ringing in his ears: *I love you. Get out of my house.*

"She's sending someone after us," he told the mule quietly, an hour in. "Brannick, probably."

The mule flicked one ear backward. In eleven years he had learned to read her ears the way sailors read weather. This particular angle meant *obviously, and also you're an idiot, but go on.*

"He won't want to do it. Brannick's soft. He cries at weddings."

Both ears swiveled forward. *And yet he owns a crossbow.*

"Yes. Thank you. I'd forgotten the crossbow."

Tam stirred against the pack frame. "Is he talking to the mule?" he mumbled to no one.

"He talks to the mule," the mule's opinion seemed to be, "and the mule answers, and somehow the mule is the only sane one on this mountain."

They made the first shelter cave well past midnight — a smuggler's cache Renna herself had shown him years ago, which felt like a knife twist now, her generosity reaching out from the past to keep him alive on the night she'd sold him. Dominic got a fire small enough to hide and forced broth into Tam, who drank it and then said, in a small careful voice:

"You're the one they're hunting. The beast-speaker."

Dominic's blood went cold. "I don't know what—"

"I stood watch outside that tollhouse door for an hour before you came out. Walls are thin. Wardens train to listen." Tam stared into the fire. His face was very young in its light, and very tired. "My whole life they told me people like you were abominations. That hearing beasts talk was like hearing demons." He was quiet a moment. "Then the stars went out, and my captain ordered us to sweep a village that had done nothing, and I thought — the demons seem to be on the other side of this."

"You could still report me. When they catch up."

"They being the crossbow man?" Tam managed something that was almost a smile. "Captain, if I wanted to die slower I'd have stayed in the army." He lay back against the mule's warm flank — she permitted this with the weary tolerance of a creature who had decided, provisionally, that humans were a project — and was asleep in minutes.

Dominic stayed awake. Behind his eyes, the spirit's memory pulsed: stone and song and a pact older than kingdoms, and underneath it, faint as a heartbeat, the shape of the price still waiting to be paid. He didn't fully understand it yet. He understood enough to know that Renna hadn't betrayed him so much as arithmetic had — that everything he loved was now a liability to itself.

That was the thing about family, he thought, feeding the fire another stick. It wasn't the loving that hurt. Anyone could survive being loved.

It was finding out what the loving cost the ones who did it.

Far below, down the black throat of the pass, a single lantern kindled. Then a second.

The mule's ears went back.

"I see them," Dominic said, and reached for the reins.
