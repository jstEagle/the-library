## 5. Voices in the Stone

Tam had learned three things about Dominic Pemberton in the four days since they'd fled Renna Kade's camp together, and none of them made him any easier to trust.

First: the man could sleep anywhere, in any weather, curled against his mule like a boy against a dog. Tam had watched him do it on scree, in sleet, once with his boots still on and one hand tangled in the mule's mane as if he were afraid she'd wander off without him. The mule, for her part, seemed to tolerate this with the weary patience of a grandmother whose grandchild keeps grabbing her apron.

Second: he talked to animals. Not the way lonely soldiers talk to horses — that, Tam understood, that was just what people did when the road was long and the silence was longer. Dominic *talked* to them, and worse, they answered. Tam hadn't caught it directly yet, not clean, but he'd seen the edges of it. The mule stopping dead before a bridge no one else could see was rotten. A crow perching on Dominic's saddle horn and croaking at him for a full minute while Dominic nodded along like a man taking directions.

Third: Tam was a deserter, and deserters got shot, and beast-speakers got burned, and if the Warden patrols sweeping the high passes found either of them, both of those things would happen at considerable speed.

So Tam kept his hand near his knife and told himself the third thing mattered more than the first two.

They were three days out of Kade's territory now, climbing into country where the starless dark pressed close even at noon — because there was no noon anymore, just a gray dimness like the inside of a closed fist. The stars had been gone nine days. Tam had grown up on a farm below Thornfell's peaks and had never once considered that the stars might be something you could lose, the way you lost a goat or a harvest. Now their absence sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

"You're limping," Dominic said that evening, without looking up from the fire he was building.

"I'm walking."

"You're favoring your left leg and you've been doing it since the scree field. Sit down before I make the mule sit on you."

"She wouldn't."

"No," Dominic agreed mildly. "But she'd stand very close to you until you felt guilty." He fed a branch to the flames. "Sit. Your knee's swollen. I've got willow bark."

Tam sat, mostly out of spite, and let the man wrap his knee with hands that were surprisingly gentle for someone who'd spent his life among smugglers. The mule watched this operation with an air of professional assessment.

"Where did you learn battlefield medicine?" Tam asked.

"Nowhere. Animals get hurt all the time and they can't tell you where it hurts, so you learn to read them." He tied off the bandage. "You're easier. You complain."

"I do not complain."

"You've complained about the cold, my cooking, my cooking again, and the fact that I hum while I walk. That's four complaints in four days. Very helpful."

Tam opened his mouth to object and realized every item was accurate, which was somehow more infuriating than being wrong. "Your humming *is* terrible."

"It's not humming, it's an old drover's song, and the mule likes it."

"The mule has terrible taste."

The mule turned her head and looked at Tam with an expression of profound offense, and Dominic laughed — really laughed, head back, and for one unguarded moment he looked young, younger than Tam, maybe, though he had to be past thirty. Then the moment folded itself away and Dominic was all business again, checking the fire, checking the dark.

Tam lay awake long after, watching the man sleep against the mule, and thought: *deserters get shot.* And then, treacherously: *he fixed your knee.*

* * *

It happened on the sixth day, in a hollow of black pines where they'd stopped to water the mule at a frozen stream.

Tam came back from scouting the ridgeline and stopped dead behind a screen of brush, because Dominic was standing in the open with three ravens on his shoulders.

Not near him. *On* him. Two on each shoulder would have been remarkable enough; there were three, plus one sitting on his bowed head like a small feathered hat, and Dominic was talking to them in a low voice, and the ravens were talking back — that harsh, rattling raven-speech that Tam knew from every winter of his childhood, except it wasn't random. It had rhythm. It had pauses. It had the unmistakable cadence of conversation.

And Dominic was answering.

"—no, not the pass, the snow bridge is gone, I know, I *know*, but we go around and—" He broke off, listening, head tilted. His face did something complicated. "How many? ... All of them? Where?" Another pause, longer. When he spoke again his voice had gone quiet and rough. "Thank you. Tell the others. And tell whoever finds them—tell them I'm sorry I couldn't be faster."

The raven on his head croaked softly and pressed its beak, briefly, to his hair. Then all four birds lifted off him at once and wheeled up through the black pines and were gone.

Tam's hand was on his knife before he'd decided to move.

Beast-speaker. The word arrived whole and cold, wearing its uniform: the pyres at the garrison towns, the proclamations nailed to church doors, the sermon he'd sat through at fourteen about how communion with beasts was the sin of Babel repeated, man crawling down instead of up. He'd believed it. He'd *enlisted* believing it. He'd stood honor guard at two burnings and told himself the condemned looked peaceful because they were mad, not because—

Dominic turned around.

He didn't startle. He looked at Tam standing there with his knife half-drawn and his face surely saying everything, and he went very still, the way a man goes still when he's decided running isn't worth it.

"How much did you hear?" Dominic asked.

"Enough." Tam's voice came out steadier than he felt. "I heard enough to burn you for it."

"Yes," Dominic said simply. "You did."

He didn't argue. That was the worst part. Tam had expected pleading, or lies, or some smuggler's silver tongue, and instead Dominic just stood there with his empty hands loose at his sides and waited, as if Tam's decision were his own to make and always had been.

"The ravens," Tam said, because his mouth kept going without permission. "What were they—what did they want?"

"There are bodies in the north valley. Refugees who took the wrong pass after the wards fell. Cold took most, a rockslide the rest." Dominic's eyes didn't leave his. "They asked me if anything could be done. I said no. They thanked me anyway, because apparently that's what ravens do." A muscle moved in his jaw. "Well? I won't run, Tam. I'm too tired, and the mule won't leave me, and you're faster than both of us. So decide."

The knife was warm in Tam's grip. Six years in the Wardens. Six years of drills and oaths and the smell of pitch-wood smoke, and every one of those years said: *draw it, shout it, be the good soldier.* The oath lived in his arm like a second bone.

But so did the memory of Captain Varga's face the last time he'd seen her, the night he deserted — no, not deserted, that wasn't the word for a man who walked away because his orders had started smelling like ash — and the memory of the burned village outside Toll Road, and the way the official report called it smuggler work when everyone with eyes knew garrison torches when they saw them.

The Wardens' truth had a crack in it. Tam had fallen through the crack himself.

And this man had wrapped his knee. And talked to ravens about the dead. And apologized to birds for not being fast enough.

Slowly, feeling like a man dismantling his own spine vertebra by vertebra, Tam put the knife back in its sheath.

"I'm not deciding," he said. "I'm deferring. There's a difference."

"There is?"

"Deserter's privilege. We defer everything." He turned away before Dominic could see his face properly. "Water the mule. We're losing light that doesn't exist."

Behind him, he heard Dominic exhale — long and shaky, the breath of a man setting down something heavier than a pack.

* * *

Two nights later, in a ruined roadside shrine with a leaking roof and a stone saint whose face the weather had rubbed smooth, Tam woke to singing.

Raven-song, high and strange, coming from the shrine's broken doorway. He reached for his knife out of pure habit and then remembered where he was, and who, and crept forward instead through the blue-gray pre-dawn murk.

Dominic sat on the shrine steps with the mule's nose resting on his shoulder — when had she gotten up? — and a single raven perched on the saint's worn head above him. And the raven was singing.

That was the only word for it. Ravens croak and rattle and mock; they don't sing like this, in long rising lines that sounded almost like a human voice, almost like a woman's voice, thin and ancient and full of something Tam had no name for. And beneath the song, woven into it, came other things: images, feelings, arriving in Tam's mind like weather.

A mountain with its heart open and full of light. Hands — thousands of hands, human hands — laid on stone in oath. A war so old it had no names in it, only fire. A woman standing alone on a peak, giving something away, giving *everything* away, her face going smooth and empty as the saint's, while the mountain sealed itself around her gift. The wards rising like dawn. The stars burning steady because something below held them up.

And then the vision turned, and showed the breaking: men in Warden grey with Warden sigils, drilling into the ward-stones, drinking the pact dry to feed engines of war, and the hollow spirit — the woman, what remained of her — screaming soundlessly as the light was siphoned out of her, year after year, until the wards shattered like glass and the stars went out.

Tam realized he was on his knees. He didn't remember kneeling.

The song ended. The raven shook its feathers, hopped down onto Dominic's knee, and regarded Tam with one bright eye.

"That's what you carry," Tam said. His voice came out wrecked. "That memory. That's the testimony."

"That's part of it," Dominic said quietly. "The spirit gave it to me. She's dying, Tam. When she dies, the mountains—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to.

"And the price. In the memory. The woman on the peak—" Tam's throat closed. "She forgot everything. Everyone she loved. That was the cost of carrying it."

"Yes," Dominic said. Just that.

Tam looked at this man — this tired, humming, mule-worshipping man who had known since the shrine of his own memory what his burden would someday take from him, and carried it anyway — and felt the last of the soldier in him quietly put down its weapon.

"I nearly reported you," Tam admitted. "Six days ago. At the stream."

"I know," Dominic said. "The ravens told me you were behind the brush. They also said your hand was shaking."

"It was not shaking."

"They're very judgmental about grip strength."

Despite everything — despite the starless sky and the dead in the north valley and the price waiting somewhere ahead like a toll neither of them wanted to count — Tam laughed. It came out of him cracked and rusty, but it was real, and Dominic grinned, and overhead the raven made a sound that was unmistakably, contemptuously, a laugh too.

"All right," Tam said. He sat down on the steps beside Dominic, shoulder to shoulder, the way soldiers sit who have decided to share a fate. "Conclave road's five days east. Varga will be hunting us, and half of Kade's knives with her. So here's my deferral, final version: I'm with you. To the Conclave. Whatever it costs."

Whatever it costs. Even as he said it, Tam thought of the empty-eyed woman in the memory and hoped — hard, selfishly — that the bill wouldn't come due the way he feared.

Dominic squeezed his shoulder once, wordless. The mule sighed and leaned her weight gently against them both, knocking Tam sideways into Dominic, which Tam suspected was deliberate.

Above them, the raven lifted from the saint's head and flew up into the starless dark — and then stopped, mid-air, wings wide, and began to croak urgently toward the eastern ridge.

Dominic went still. "Something's coming up the valley," he said. "Fast. A lot of them." He turned to Tam, and the warmth in his face had gone hard and bright as drawn steel. "Get your kit. We're moving now."
