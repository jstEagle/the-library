## 6. The Hollow Remembers

The shrine found them the way shelter always found Dominic: by accident, at dusk, when the mule had opinions.

"She's done," Tam said, leaning against the trailside post as if it were the only thing holding her upright. The bandage on her shoulder had gone from white to a tired rust color sometime around noon, and she'd stopped pretending not to limp an hour after that. "Whatever she says."

The mule said nothing, being a mule, but she turned her great gray head and looked at Dominic with one liquid eye, and what she said with that eye was: *There is a roof two switchbacks up, and it smells of old cedar and older sorrow, and you will want to see it before you sleep in it.*

"Two switchbacks up," Dominic translated. "There's a roof."

"I understood 'roof,'" Tam said. "That's the word I needed." She pushed off the post, swayed, and let him take her elbow without comment, which for Tam was roughly equivalent to weeping with gratitude.

The shrine sat in a cup of the mountain like a stone boat run aground. It had been built to something — the carvings above the door had been chiseled away by hands that hated whatever they depicted — but whoever the god or spirit had been, its worshippers had left in a hurry and its enemies had left satisfied. The roof held. That was the whole of the shrine's virtues, and after three days of starless sky and cold rations and Tam's fever climbing by degrees, it felt like a palace.

Dominic got Tam onto the low stone bench near the hearth, got a fire going with the last of the dry wood, and got the mule settled in the broken portico where the wind couldn't needle her. Then he sat down across from Tam with the intention of staying awake all night, because her breathing had started doing something he didn't like, small and shallow, like someone reading aloud in a library.

He did not intend to fall asleep.

But the fire was warm, and the shrine was quiet, and the fragment of memory coiled behind his eyes — the fragment the dying spirit had pressed into him beneath the fallen ward-stone, the one that showed Warden sappers cutting the pact-roots and laughing about fuel quotas — had begun, over the days of travel, to *unfold*. He'd felt it happening the way you feel a letter opening in your pocket. And somewhere between the third hour and the fourth, the hollow reached up through his exhaustion and took him the rest of the way down.

* * *

He was standing in the mountains, except the mountains were young.

There was no other word for it. The peaks he knew as scarred and weathered rose around him green-shouldered and unbroken, and the valleys ran silver with water that hadn't yet learned to carry ash. The sky was crowded with stars — so many stars that the dark between them looked like the rare thing, like lace rather than cloth.

And the mountain breathed.

Not wind. Not weather. It *breathed*, a slow tide of warmth rising out of the stone, and Dominic understood with a lurch that this was the ward — not the brittle lattice of runes the Wardens maintained with tallow and tax money, but something alive, something the mountain itself exhaled every night to keep the hungry dark beyond the passes from leaning in.

A woman stood at the mouth of a cave that would someday be a collapsed quarry. She was small and brown-handed and dressed in the undyed wool of people who owned exactly one outfit, and she was arguing with a bear.

"You cannot eat the offering," she was saying, with the exhausted patience of a schoolteacher. "The offering is symbolic."

The bear, an enormous cinnamon-colored fellow with frost on his back, huffed something long-sounding and resigned.

"Yes," she agreed. "I know they're stingy this year. Everyone's stingy this year. Eat the blackberries and stop complaining."

Dominic's heart did something complicated. He knew her. Not her name, not her face — but the shape of her, the way you know the shape of a keyhole your whole life before you ever see the key. A beast-speaker. The last beast-speaker before him, four hundred years dead, alive here in the memory and mid-argument with a bear.

She turned, then, as if she'd heard him thinking, which — he supposed, in here — she had.

"Oh," she said. "You're the one carrying it. Oh, you poor lamb. Come here. You should see all of it before you decide anything."

"I haven't decided anything," Dominic said.

"No," she said, kindly. "You haven't. That's the problem."

She took his hand like a grandmother hauling a grandson across a market square, and the world folded.

* * *

The first thing she showed him was the making of the promise.

It was winter, the first winter, the one the histories didn't bother recording because nobody had invented writing in these hills yet. The dark beyond the passes had come down the slopes like a tide of cold iron — the *hollow-dark*, the old people called it later, the thing that unmade names — and the mountain spirits had fought it alone and lost, and the humans in the valleys had hidden in their cellars and listened to their gods die one by one.

Then a girl who talked to goats had walked up the mountain, because someone had to, and everyone else was busy hiding.

"She wasn't brave," the brown-handed woman said, watching beside him as the girl stood in the snow before a presence like a crack in the world, glowing gold and terrible. "That's the part everyone gets wrong in the songs. She was terrified. She just couldn't stand listening to frightened animals and doing nothing, which is a different thing than bravery. Bravery is a virtue. That was just a bad habit."

The spirit in the crack had asked — not in words; in weight, in the way a debt asks — *what will you give?*

And the girl had answered, "What do you need?" Which Dominic recognized, with a sick tilt of the world, as the exact phrasing he himself had used under the fallen ward-stone, kneeling in the screaming place, when the dying spirit had pressed its first fragment into his mind.

The bargain was struck. The mountain would breathe its living ward each night, and the hollow spirits beneath would feed it, and the dark beyond the passes would stay beyond them. In exchange: offerings, respect, and one speaker in every generation to keep the conversation going. Beast-speakers weren't accidents. They were *correspondents*. The gift was a post held open, a door propped with a foot.

"We were the treaty," Dominic said slowly. "All of us. Every speaker since."

"You were the pen," the woman corrected. "The treaty was written into the mountain. But treaties need readers. Someone has to hear both sides." Her mouth went wry. "It was good work, honestly. Mostly complaints about bears."

"And the price?"

Her face changed. The memory changed with it — the light went long and red, and they were standing somewhere new.

* * *

The second thing she showed him was the first war.

Not the border wars the Wardens fought now, over tolls and pride. This was worse. This was men with iron cutting into the mountain's roots because they'd discovered the hollow spirits could be *harvested* — that the warm golden stuff of them, rendered down through ward-stone and suffering, could power engines. Lanterns that never died. Forges that burned without coal. Weapons that drank starlight.

The spirits could not fight. They were made of agreement, of keeping promises; violence wasn't in their nature any more than drowning is in a flame's. So they did the only thing they could.

They called in the debt.

The last time, Dominic watched through the woman's eyes — she was there, she had been there, dragged up the mountain by desperate elders while the harvest-forges glowed on the horizon like a second, evil dawn. The mountain's breath was failing. The dark leaned close. Someone had to go beneath the ward-stone and speak the renewal directly, spirit to mortal, and bind it fresh.

"That's me," the woman said quietly. "This part I remember twice. Once living, once telling."

"What did it cost?"

"You already know," she said. "You've known since the screaming place. That's why you haven't slept properly. That's why you flinch when Tam laughs, because you're memorizing it."

Dominic's mouth was dry. Below them, in the memory, the woman knelt before the great golden crack, and the spirit asked its question — *what will you give?* — and she answered, steady as bedrock:

"What do you need?"

*Everyone*, said the mountain. *Everyone you love. Every voice, every face, every hand you have ever held. Love is the thread the pact is woven on, and a fresh weave needs fresh thread. Give us your loves, and we will hold the dark.*

"Not my life?" the woman below asked, and laughed shakily. "I half expected my life."

"Life is cheap," the spirit said, and it sounded, horribly, like an apology. "Memory is the only currency we have."

And she gave it. Dominic watched her do it — watched her walk down the mountain afterward a stranger to her own mother, watched her mother call her name and watch the name land on her like rain on glass. Watched her rebuild herself out of habit and kindness and the animals, who still knew her, who had never needed names to love her in the first place. Watched her grow old among beasts and strangers, beloved without knowing why, mourned by a bear who came down from the high country and sat by her grave for a winter.

"The pact held," the woman beside him said. Her voice had gone thin. "Four hundred years, it held. And I forgot my sister's laugh, and my mother's hands, and the boy I meant to marry by the river, and it *held*." She turned to look at him, and her eyes were full of everything she'd given away and couldn't feel the loss of anymore — which was somehow the worst part, that even grief had been taken with the rest. "That's the price, Dominic. Not death. Death leaves you your loves intact for others to bury. This leaves you standing in your own life like a lodger."

The firelight of the ruined shrine flickered at the edge of the memory, and he felt himself beginning to surface, and he grabbed at her sleeve.

"The Wardens broke it," he said. "They're harvesting again. The testimony — if I bring it to the Conclave, if the pact is renewed—"

"Then someone pays," she said gently. "Someone always pays. That's what no one writes in the songs, lamb. Peace isn't free. It's just paid on terms most people never see."

"It'll be me, won't it," he said. Not a question. He could feel it settling into his bones like snow into a boot. "If anyone renews it. It has to be a speaker."

"It doesn't have to be tonight," she said. "Decide nothing tonight. Walk tomorrow. Let the road argue with you." And she smiled — warm, crooked, the smile of someone who remembered being young even if she couldn't remember whose daughter she'd been — and the shrine came up around him like water.

* * *

Dominic woke with his cheeks wet and the fire down to embers.

Tam was asleep, truly asleep now, her fever finally breaking in a fine sweat, one hand curled loosely near the coals. The mule stood in the doorway of the portico, silhouetted against a sky that was still wrong — still starless, still bruise-dark — and she was looking at him with both ears forward, the way she did when he was upset and thought he was hiding it well.

"I'm all right," he lied.

She blew air through her lips, eloquently.

He sat up and fed a stick to the embers and tried to take inventory of himself, the way you'd inventory cargo before a dangerous crossing. Tam, asleep and healing. Renna's mark on him, somewhere behind. Varga's patrols, somewhere ahead. The Conclave, five days' hard walking. And inside him, unfolding like a letter he could no longer refold: the pact, the war, the price, the woman with brown hands and no memories, sitting patient in his skull like a tenant who paid rent in horror.

*Everyone you love.*

He looked at Tam's sleeping face and caught himself doing it — cataloguing. The exact angle of her grin. The way she said *shut up* when she meant *thank you.* The little scar on her chin from a knife she swore she'd won fair.

Stop, he told himself. Stop memorizing.

But his heart kept on anyway, quietly, treacherously, filing everything away — and somewhere far below the shrine, deep in the warm dark of the mountain, something ancient stirred and sighed, almost tenderly, like a lender who knows the borrower has already signed.

At dawn, a raven dropped through the broken roof, landed on the altar stone, and croaked three notes that meant, unmistakably: *Riders on the toll road. Coming fast.*
