## 7. Varga's Choice

Captain Ilse Varga had burned a great many things in her nineteen years of Warden service. Signal posts. A granary full of contraband dreamleaf. Once, memorably and entirely by accident, her own tent. But she had never before burned orders.

She sat in the command tent at Hollowmere garrison with the dispatch unrolled on the table before her, its wax seal already cracked, and read it a third time to be certain the lamplight was not playing tricks.

*Subject: Pemberton, Dominic. Status: suspected beast-speaker. Directive: capture alive. Intact. High Command requires the gift functional for weaponization trials. Lethal force authorized against any who shelter him.*

Weaponization trials.

Varga set the paper down and rubbed her eyes. Outside, the night was still starless — it had been six days now, and the men were starting to mutter about it the way soldiers mutter about lice or bad bread, which is to say constantly and without any actual expectation of improvement. Sergeant Brakke stuck his head through the tent flap.

"You wanted me, Captain?"

"Brakke. What would you say if I told you High Command wants us to capture a man alive so they can strap him to an engine and use him as a part?"

"I'd say that's above my pay grade, Captain."

"Everything is above your pay grade. You're paid almost nothing."

"That's why I say it so often, Captain." Brakke was forty, built like a door, and possessed of the particular calm of a man who had decided long ago that most problems were temporary but supper was eternal. He looked at the dispatch, then at Varga's face, and his expression did something careful. "That's from High Command?"

"Directly."

"And we're going to follow it?"

Varga looked out through the gap in the tent flap. Beyond the picket lines, past the burnt fields, the mountains stood black against a blacker sky. Three days ago she had ridden through Ashen Toll village, where the garrison troops — panicked, half-starved, following orders nobody could quite trace to their origin — had put the whole place to the torch because a miller's wife claimed she'd seen lights moving in the hills. Eleven dead. The official report said smugglers did it. Varga had signed the report herself, because the alternative was signing something worse, and because she had been tired, and because that was how it always went: you signed, and then one morning you woke up and discovered you were the kind of person who signed.

She was tired of being that kind of person.

"No," said Varga. "We're not."

Brakke nodded slowly, as though she'd just confirmed the weather. "I'll get my kit."

"You're not even going to argue? Ask if I've lost my mind? This is desertion, Brakke. It's the rope, if they catch us."

"They'd have to catch us first," he said. "And I've seen you ride. Also—" he paused, and for once the humor dropped away, "—my sister lives near Ashen Toll. She wasn't home when it burned. I'd like to know what kind of army burns villages and calls it justice, and I don't think I'll learn that standing in this tent."

Varga held out her hand. He shook it.

"Burn the orders," she said. "All of them. And Brakke — burn mine too. If anyone finds anything with my seal on it after tonight, I want nothing left but ash and confusion."

"What will we leave instead?"

"A note saying we've gone south after smugglers. It'll be half true. Half true is more honesty than High Command's had all year."

* * *

They rode out under a sky like a closed lid, two riders among the hundreds moving on the roads, and by dawn they were well clear of Hollowmere and into the hill country where the old shrine paths wound up toward the high passes. Varga knew these trails from her childhood, before the Wardens, when her grandmother had still made offerings at the hollow-stones and told her the mountain remembered everything. She had thought that was superstition. She was no longer entirely sure.

It was Brakke who spotted them first.

"Smoke," he said, pointing east. "Thin. Cooking fire, maybe. Or hunters drying out."

"Hunters." Varga reined in. "Kade's people?"

"If Kade marked Pemberton, she'll have sent her best. Which means they'll be good in the lowlands and miserable up here. Smugglers live by roads. Take away roads, take away their manners."

Varga studied the smoke. It rose from the direction of the Shepherd's Rest trail — the fastest route toward the Conclave road, and therefore exactly where she would position hunters if she were Renna Kade, and exactly where she would tell a fugitive *not* to go if she were trying to keep him alive. Which meant either the smugglers had guessed wrong about his route, or they hadn't guessed at all, and were simply waiting along every path at once.

"We intercept," she said. "Now, before they find him. If Kade's people reach him first, he's dead, and whatever he knows dies with him."

"And what does he know, Captain? Truly?"

Varga thought about the fragment of testimony that had reached her through channels she didn't fully trust — a dying spirit, a broken pact, the wards fed on something older than the Warden oath itself. She thought about the vanished stars, and the ward-stones gone cold as riverbed pebbles, and the way the horses had begun refusing to enter the high passes at all, ears flat, eyes white.

"He knows why the stars went out," she said. "And I think the rest of us are going to need to hear it."

They cut uphill through the pines, leading the horses where the ground steepened, and near midday Brakke — who moved through brush with a silence that should have been illegal for a man his size — raised a fist and pulled her down beside him.

Below, in a fold of the valley where the Shepherd's Rest trail narrowed between rock walls, four riders waited. Not camped. Waiting. Horses saddled, crossbows racked, one man posted on the rocks above with a clear view down the trail. They had the patience of professionals and the stillness of people being paid very well to have it.

"Four," breathed Brakke. "There'll be more behind. That's the trap, not the trappers."

Varga counted again, cataloguing without really deciding to — the angle of the crossbows, the loose rein on the lead horse, the way the watcher on the rocks kept shifting his weight off his left foot. A sprain, maybe. An old injury. A man favoring one side watches half the world.

"Pemberton isn't here yet," she murmured. "So we make sure when he comes, there's nothing here to meet him but bad memories."

"With four of them and two of us?"

"Five of them. And yes. But they're expecting smuggler fights and Warden patrols, Brakke. They are not expecting a defecting captain with a grudge and a sergeant shaped like a landslide." She was already loosening her sword in its scabbard. "Besides. I've been wanting to hit something since Ashen Toll. This is practically medicine."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, relied on three things: surprise, terrain, and the universal truth that no one expects the law to attack the criminals.

Varga rode down the trail openly, Warden grey and steel visible, hands empty, calling out in her best parade-ground voice that she had orders concerning toll enforcement on the high routes. The watcher on the rocks turned to look — turned *away* from Brakke's approach — and that was the last mistake any of them made cleanly.

What followed was short, ugly, and deeply unsatisfying to everyone except Varga. Brakke came down the slope like an apology from the mountain itself, took the watcher's legs out from under him, and sat on him with the serene finality of a drawbridge closing. Two of the riders wheeled toward Varga and learned, rather quickly, that twenty years of drill against border raiders translates poorly into fighting a woman who has stopped caring whether she lives to fill out the paperwork. One went into the rocks; the other threw down his crossbow when her sword point arrived at his throat with a small apologetic tap, like a polite knock.

The fourth rider — the leader, a lean woman with a Kade brand worked into her collar and eyes that did arithmetic faster than her mouth — kicked her horse sideways and ran. Varga let her go. A runner carried a message back to Renna Kade, and the message was simple: the passes were no longer empty, and the law had changed sides.

Then it was over, and Varga sat on a boulder catching her breath while Brakke trussed the prisoners with the gentle competence of a man who had once worked a sheep farm and never fully recovered.

"You're bleeding, Captain," he observed.

"It's not mine."

"That's usually the good outcome, yes."

One of the captured smugglers — young, freckled, terrified — stared up at her from the dirt. "You're Varga," he said. "The Iron Captain of Hollowmere. They say you hanged a man for smiling at a search."

"They say a lot of things," said Varga. "Mostly I hanged him for smuggling plague-ward charms to a fever village at triple price. The smiling was incidental." She crouched to his level. "Here's what happens now. You're going to tell me everything Kade promised you for Pemberton's head, and then you're going to sit very quietly, and in a day or two someone will cut you loose with a story to tell. Cooperate, and you'll live to spend whatever she paid you. Don't, and—" she glanced at Brakke, "—what happens if they don't cooperate, Brakke?"

"Nothing good," said Brakke comfortably. "But mostly nothing. Mostly I just sit nearby and eat loudly. It wears on a man."

The freckled smuggler talked for a quarter hour without stopping once.

By the time he finished, Varga had learned that Kade's hunters numbered eleven in these hills, that the remaining seven were sweeping the western trails, and that Pemberton had been seen — seen, gods, actually *seen* — two nights ago near the ruined shrine at Cold Hollow, traveling with a mule and a young man wearing a torn Warden coat.

A deserter. Traveling with a beast-speaker. Toward the Conclave.

"Well," said Brakke, when she'd relayed it all. "That's a pair of fools and a mule against eleven professionals and the entire weight of High Command."

"Yes," said Varga, swinging back into the saddle. Her ribs ached. Her hands were steady for the first time in days. Somewhere above them, in the starless dark of the high country, a frightened man was carrying the truth of a broken world toward the only court that might still listen — and every instinct she had, the ones she'd spent nineteen years drowning under regulation and report-signing, told her to ride.

"So we're doing it anyway," said Brakke.

"We're doing it anyway."

They spurred upslope into the black pines, toward Cold Hollow — and Varga did not see, until the ridge crest hid it forever, the thin thread of smoke rising from the shrine below, or the three figures moving quickly out of it into the trees, heading straight for the pass where seven of Kade's best were already waiting.
