## 8. The Conclave Road

The mountain had opinions about the weather, and tonight it was sharing them freely.

"Snow," Dominic said to the mule, who was named — insofar as a mule could be named — Buttercup, a joke Renna had made seven years ago that he'd never had the heart to retire. "Horizontal snow. In a pass. Marvelous."

Buttercup flicked one ear backward, which in mule meant *I heard you, and I remain unimpressed by both the snow and you.*

"You're not the one carrying—" He stopped himself. He was never sure anymore whether the spirit's fragment counted as something he carried or something that carried him. It sat behind his eyes like a coal banked in ash, warm and patient and waiting.

Behind him, Tam picked her way over the scree with the careful steps of someone whose left arm still wasn't right. She'd stopped saluting things around day three of their flight, which Dominic took as progress. "How far?"

"The Conclave sits above the cloud line. Another six hours, if the mountain's kind."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we'll have a conversation about it afterward."

Tam almost smiled. That was another thing she was learning: that Dominic joked when he was afraid, and that the worse the joke, the worse the fear. She'd gotten very good at reading his jokes like barometers.

They climbed. The starless dark pressed close — three weeks now without stars, and Dominic still caught himself looking up at the black where the Wheel should have been, feeling the absence like a missing tooth. The hollow spirits had told him once, through the memory-fragment, that the stars were not gone but *held* — kept somewhere behind the broken wards like coins in a closed fist. He tried to believe they could be given back.

The raven found them an hour past midnight.

It came down out of the blackness sideways, wings cracking against the wind, and landed on Buttercup's pack-frame with the urgency of a bird delivering bad news to a house on fire.

"*Road,*" it said, in the flattened way ravens spoke human words. "*Road below. Lanterns many. Climbing.*"

Dominic went cold in a way the snow couldn't account for. "Which road?"

"*Your road. Behind.*"

"How far?"

The raven did the thing ravens did instead of shrugging, a full-body ruffle that somehow conveyed *farther than I'd like, closer than you'd hope.*

"Renna's people?" Tam asked quietly.

"Lanterns many." Dominic sighed. "Could be smugglers. Could be Warden loyalists following Varga's trail. Could be both." He looked up the pass, then back the way they'd come, and did the arithmetic every smuggler learned young: distance, terrain, time, and how much of each he could afford to lose. "We can't outrun them to the Conclave. Not with this wind, not with your arm."

"So we don't run."

"So we hold something." He pointed ahead, where the trail narrowed between two shoulders of rock before opening onto the final switchbacks. A fallen boulder the size of a cottage half-blocked the path there; the gap beside it was barely wide enough for Buttercup, let alone two men abreast. "There. One at a time, anyone coming up has to come to us politely."

"Politely," Tam repeated.

"With weapons. But politely."

* * *

They built their little kingdom of rocks and waiting.

Dominic sent the raven out as a picket — *watch the road, count the lanterns, come back screaming* — and settled Tam behind the boulder where she could brace her good arm. Buttercup he stationed further up the trail with instructions to be a mule about things, which Buttercup assured him required no acting whatsoever.

Then they waited, and the snow fell sideways, and Dominic found himself talking, because silence in a place like this had teeth.

"When this is done," he said, "I'm going to find a farm."

"A farm."

"Somewhere south. No mountains. Mountains are terrible conversationalists — all attitude, no listening skills. Goats, maybe. Goats gossip terribly but they're honest about it."

"What will you grow?"

"I haven't got that far. Something that doesn't die when I look at it wrong." He pulled his coat tighter. "And you? After?"

Tam was quiet long enough that he thought she hadn't heard. Then: "I don't know what 'after' looks like for me. Deserters don't get farms. They get a rope, usually, if the Wardens catch them." She turned her knife over in her fingers. "Maybe I'll just keep walking until walking feels like a plan."

"It will," Dominic said. "It did for me."

Below them, faint through the wind, the raven began to scream.

* * *

They came up the gap four at a time, because the gap wouldn't allow more, and that was the whole of Dominic's strategy: make a big fight into a narrow one.

The first four were smugglers — he knew the walk, the rolled shoulders, the mismatched armor. Renna's colors under their cloaks. He felt that recognition like a bruise being pressed.

"Dom!" the lead one called up, cheerful as a market day. "Renna says come home! Says you've got something worth more than you know, and she's willing to forgive the running!"

"She always did have a way with apologies!"

"She also says Tam can walk away! Warden business isn't hers!"

"That's generous!" Dominic called back, and to Tam, low: "Don't believe a word of it. Renna doesn't leave witnesses. It's practically a religious practice."

The lead smuggler sighed, and the cheerfulness dropped off him like a coat, and they came up the gap with blades out.

What followed was ugly, short, and nothing like the songs. Fighting in a mountain gap at night in falling snow is mostly shoving, swearing, and hoping the other fellow slips first. Dominic held the gap's mouth with a quarterstaff and ten years of knowing exactly where feet go on wet stone. Tam struck from behind the boulder whenever a head presented itself, economical as a butcher. Twice, men who might have flanked them simply fell — and Dominic heard, high above, the small vindictive laughter of ravens dropping pebbles, which was either loyalty or entertainment, possibly both.

Three times the gap changed hands and three times they took it back. Dominic's arms burned. His breath came in knives. And through it all the coal behind his eyes stayed warm and steady, whispering nothing, only *upward, upward*, like a hand on his spine.

Then the smugglers withdrew — not routed, just withdrawn, falling back down the trail with the discipline of professionals — and Dominic understood why a heartbeat later.

Horns. Deep ones, brass-and-bone, the sound Wardens used instead of politeness.

"Oh," said Tam. "Oh, that's bad."

Lantern-light bloomed far below, in orderly ranks. Renna's people and the loyalists had found each other on the toll road, and whatever argument followed would end one of two ways, and both ways ended with climbing men coming up this gap soon, and more of them.

"We move," Dominic said. "Now, while they're busy deciding who hates whom more."

* * *

They nearly made it.

The final switchbacks rose above them, white and blind, and the Conclave's towers — when the snow thinned for a breath — showed as a suggestion of light above the cloud line. Warm light. Lamplight. Someone up there still lit lamps against a starless dark, and Dominic wanted that light the way a drowning man wants air.

He was twenty steps from the top of the last rise when the crossbow bolt took Tam.

No warning. No duel, no speech, just a single loyalist sharpshooter who'd climbed higher than everyone else and been patient. She made a small sound — surprise more than pain — and sat down against the rocks as though she'd simply decided walking was finished.

Dominic killed the sharpshooter. He would remember doing it only as motion and noise, a red curtain drawn across the night. What he remembered clearly was kneeling in the snow afterward, pressing his hands against the wound below Tam's collarbone while her blood came up between his fingers, too fast, too warm, impossibly bright in the starless black.

"No," he said. Foolish, useless word. "No, stay with me. Stay—"

Tam smiled up at him. Her face was already going gray around the smile. "Told you," she whispered. "Walking until it feels like a plan."

"Don't. Don't do the brave dying speech, I hate those, I've read forty of them—"

"Worth it?" She coughed. Her hand found his wrist, gripped with what strength remained. "The truth. Worth carrying?"

"Yes," Dominic said, and meant it so hard it broke something in him. "Yes. It has to be."

"Good." Her grip loosened. "Then it wasn't... wasn't a rope after all. Wasn't wasted." Her eyes drifted upward, to the black where stars should have been. "Put some back, Dom. Put some back and I'll..." 

The sentence never found its ending. The snow kept falling on her open eyes, gentle as anything, and Dominic knelt there holding a hand gone quiet, and the mountain held its breath around him.

Above, the Conclave's lamps burned on, warm and indifferent and near.

Below, horns sounded again — closer now, both armies done arguing, both coming.

Dominic laid Tam straight, folded her hands on her breast, and took her knife because she would have wanted it used rather than mourned. He stood. His legs shook. His eyes were dry, which surprised him; grief had gone somewhere deeper than water, down into the marrow where resolve is smelted.

The coal behind his eyes flared, warm as a hearth, and for the first time it spoke in something like words:

*Bring it home, beast-speaker. Bring it home, and none of this was loss. All of it was seed.*

"Buttercup," Dominic said, and his voice came out level, which felt like someone else's voice. "Last stretch."

Buttercup pressed her broad forehead briefly against his shoulder — mule for *grief acknowledged, walking anyway* — and turned up toward the light.

Together they climbed out of the dark, toward the Conclave, toward testimony, while behind them the horns sang their hunger into the starless night — and ahead, at the gates, figures in white and gold were already descending to meet them, and one of them, impossibly, wore a Warden captain's burned-off insignia and bloodied knuckles and the expression of a woman who had decided, finally and fully, whose side she was on.

"Varga," Dominic breathed.

"You're late," said Captain Ilse Varga, planting herself between him and the mountain's hunters. "Get inside. I'll hold the door."
