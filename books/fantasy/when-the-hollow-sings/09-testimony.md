## 9. Testimony

The Conclave hall had been carved out of the mountain itself, which struck Dominic as either very wise or very rude — inviting people to argue diplomacy inside the body of a creature that might wake up annoyed.

He stood at the center of the amphitheater, on a disc of pale stone worn smooth by three thousand years of difficult conversations. Above him, tier upon tier of seats rose into shadow. Below him — he could feel it through his boots, a slow pulse like a second heartbeat — the Hollow ran beneath the floor, threading through the roots of the mountain.

The factions filled the tiers in wary clusters. Wardens in slate-grey, their high command seated front and center with the rigid posture of people who had rehearsed looking innocent. Smugglers in road-leathers and stolen finery, Renna Kade among them, her arms crossed and her face arranged into something carefully unreadable, which for Renna was practically a confession. Conclave arbiters in undyed wool. Border folk who had walked days to be here. And scattered through them all, the beasts — hawks on perches, wolves lying with unnerving patience beside people they'd have eaten last month, and one extremely smug goat who kept trying to eat an arbiter's sash.

And near the western arch, where the light fell thinnest, Tam's body lay on a bier of pine boughs. They had carried him down from the pass. Varga had carried him most of the way herself, and refused all offers otherwise, and no one had been foolish enough to insist.

*Don't look at him yet,* Dominic told himself. *If you look now you'll come apart before you start.*

The mule stood beside him. Of course she did. She had forced her way past three arbiter wards and one very startled door-warden to do it, and now she stood with her ears forward and her opinion of everyone in the room clearly formed and available upon request.

"All right," Dominic murmured to her. "Any advice?"

*Don't die,* she said.

"Working on it."

High Arbiter Ondrel raised a hand, and the murmur of three hundred souls went quiet. "Dominic Pemberton. You claim to carry testimony from the last living spirit of the Hollow. You claim the border wards did not fail — they were broken. Speak, or be spoken over."

No pressure, then.

Dominic looked up at the tiers. At Varga, standing apart from her fellow Wardens like a splinter working loose. At Renna, whose jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. At the empty seat where Warden High Command's honesty should have been sitting.

"I'm not going to give a speech," he said. "I'm a terrible public speaker. I once gave a eulogy for a chicken and cried so hard nobody understood a word." A ripple of uneasy laughter. Good. Keep them breathing. "The spirit will speak instead. Through me. It's dying — it's been dying since your war machines drank its blood, and what it has left, it wants said plainly, in this room, where every faction can hear it at once, so that no one can say afterward they didn't know."

He closed his eyes and reached inward, to the place behind his heart where the memory-fragment had lived for weeks like a coal wrapped in cloth.

*Are you ready?* the Hollow asked. Its voice was thinner than when he'd found it under the ward-stone. Thinner than last night. Much thinner than this morning.

"No," Dominic said honestly. "But I'm here."

*Good answer. The brave ones always lie.*

The spirit poured into him.

It did not hurt, exactly. It was more like being remembered by something vast — being picked up gently in an enormous mouth, the way a mother cat carries a kitten. His spine straightened. His eyes opened, and they were not entirely his own; there was a depth in them now, a green-dark shimmer like light through deep water.

When he spoke, his voice came out layered — his own rough baritone underneath, and above it something older, resonant, like wind moving through a canyon.

"Hear me."

The mountain heard. Everyone in the hall felt the floor hum.

"I am the last of the Hollow. I fed your wards for nine hundred years, freely, as the pact demanded, because the pact was made in love and sealed in grief. Your ancestors came to my kind after the first war between the lowlands and the heights — a war you have all forgotten, because we paid to let you forget it."

Images moved through the hall like weather. Dominic's mouth spoke; his mind rode along, helpless and willing at once. He showed them: two armies on a frozen field, and a young woman with frostbitten hands walking between the lines, carrying memories like stones — a mother's face, a sister's laugh, a lover's name — laying each one down into the earth until the hatred drained out of ten thousand hearts like water from a pierced barrel. He showed them the price. He showed them the woman walking away from the battlefield whole in body and hollowed in soul, unable to remember why she was crying.

"The pact required a keeper," the Hollow said through him. "A beast-speaker, one in each generation, to walk the wards and carry my voice. And it required, once, long ago or soon — always once — a payment. Not blood. Not life. Memory. The old price. For a pact built on forgetting must be renewed by forgetting."

The Warden High Commander rose. "This is fable," she said. "The wards failed of age—"

"They failed of axes."

The image turned on her like a searchlight: Warden engineers in the deep vaults beneath High Command, drills biting into a ward-root, the Hollow screaming somewhere far off as its blood was piped into engines of war. The look on the engineers' faces — not malice. Worse. Efficiency.

The hall erupted. Wardens shouting at Wardens. Smugglers on their feet. The goat ate the sash.

"SILENCE," said the mountain, through Dominic's borrowed throat, and silence happened immediately, even to the goat.

"The testimony is given," the Hollow said. Its voice was failing now, fraying at the edges like a rope. "The crime is known. But knowledge does not feed a ward, little ones. Knowledge never fed anything. The pact must be renewed tonight, while I still have breath to seal it, or the mountains rise within the season — not in vengeance. In grief. Grief is heavier than vengeance. Ask anyone who has carried both."

Silence, deep as a well.

"And renewal," the Hollow continued softly, "requires the old price. One speaker. Willing. Who gives up every memory bound to love — every face, every name, every warmth ever held. This is the term. It has always been the term. Is there one here who—"

"Yes," said Dominic.

The word came out in his own voice, unlayered, and it came out *fast*, because he had learned weeks ago, in a ruined shrine, exactly what was coming, and he had spent every mile since deciding, and re-deciding, and deciding again.

He stepped forward off the pale stone before anyone could move.

*Dominic.* The Hollow's voice cracked with alarm. *You don't have—*

"You were going to ask for a volunteer and then hope nobody qualified," Dominic said. "I've met you. That's absolutely what you were going to do. So let's skip it."

"Dominic, *no*—" Varga was already moving down the tiers.

"There's nothing to discuss—" Renna, from the other side, and God help him, she sounded almost human about it.

"Both of you sit *down*," Dominic said pleasantly, "or I'll tell this entire room about the thing you did in the Toll Road inn, Renna, and the thing you keep in that lockbox, Varga, because the mule told me everything, the mule tells me *everything*, she is the worst secret-keeper in Thornfell—"

*I am standing right here,* the mule said, wounded.

"I know, dear. That was the point."

Tam. Don't think of Tam. He thought of Tam anyway — freckles and terrible jokes and the way he'd held the pass with a broken sword and a grin, buying minutes with his life because he believed what Dominic carried mattered. *Someone has to pay for the world to keep its softness,* Tam had said once around a mouthful of stolen cheese. *Might as well be someone who's bad at keeping secrets anyway.*

The Hollow gathered itself in Dominic's chest, and he felt it hesitate, felt its grief — a spirit that had watched nine hundred years of keepers and loved every clumsy one of them.

*You understand what you lose,* it said. *Him. Her. All of them. Yourself.*

"Yeah," Dominic said, and his voice broke only a little, and he decided that counted as dignity. "I understand. But here's the thing nobody says about memory. You keep thinking it's *yours*. It isn't. It's a loan. Every laugh Renna ever shared with me, every kindness Tam ever did — those live in other places too, in other people, in stories. If I forget them, they're not gone. They're just... not mine anymore. Which is sad." He wiped his eyes with his sleeve, roughly, like the gesture was an inconvenience. "But the alternative is everyone loses everything. And I've carried worse than sad. I carried your truth through a starless country for six weeks with one mule for company, and she snores."

*I do not snore.*

"You absolutely snore."

*Take the deal, idiot,* the mule said gently, *before you talk us all to death.*

Dominic laughed — actually laughed, wet and cracked, in front of three hundred strangers and one smug goat — and put his hand flat against the pale stone.

"Do it," he told the Hollow. "Freely given. Witnessed by all. Renew the pact."

The Hollow sang.

That was the only word for it — a sound that wasn't sound, rising through the floor, through the tiers, through the soot-and-grief crust everyone had grown over their hearts these black months. Dominic felt the memory begin to lift out of him, gently, one thread at a time, and he grabbed at them uselessly, the way you grab at steam.

Renna's face going soft and uncertain. Gone.

Varga burning her orders in a garrison stove. Gone.

Tam, laughing, bleeding, brave, *his*, gone, gone—

Wait, he wanted to say. Wait, not yet, I'm not—

The last thing to go was small and stubborn and entirely typical: he tried to hold onto the mule, and the mule held onto him, and for one impossible half-second neither of them would let go, and then the pact sealed with a sound like a door closing in a very large house, and the stars — though he could not yet see them — came back on above the peaks like lamps relit in windows.

The Hollow's presence withdrew, leaving only warmth, and quiet, and a man standing alone on smooth pale stone who could not remember why everyone was staring at him, or why he was crying, or why a mule was pressing her forehead against his chest as if her heart would break.

"Hello," said Dominic Pemberton, to no one he knew. "Sorry — do I know you?"
