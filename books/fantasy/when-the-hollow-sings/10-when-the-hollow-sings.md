## 10. When the Hollow Sings

The stars came back at dawn.

Varga had spent the night on the Conclave steps with a sword across her knees and her old Warden coat folded beside her like something she'd shot and was waiting to bury. Around her the great hall of the neutral orders filled and emptied, filled and emptied — delegates from three crowns, smuggler envoys in their gaudy road silks, hollow-order archivists clutching their ledgers as if paper could hold what had just been spoken through one exhausted man's mouth. And above it all, through the tall windows, the sky had been empty for eleven days, and now —

Now there were stars. Faint, washed pale by morning light, but there, stitched back into the dark where they'd been torn out. The mountains around the Conclave had gone quiet too, that deep grinding unease beneath the stone finally stilling, like a dog settling after a long thunderstorm.

Someone in the hall began to cry. Someone else began to laugh. Varga did neither. She kept looking at the doors through which they'd carried Dominic Pemberton an hour ago, half-dead, still speaking in a voice that wasn't entirely his own, and she thought: *I burned my orders for this. I'd better make sure it was worth it.*

A small raven landed on the balustrade beside her and looked at her with an expression she could only describe as expectant.

"Don't start," she said. "I'm going."

The raven hopped once along the stone, keeping pace with her, which was either coincidence or escort. She had learned, these past weeks, that it was rarely coincidence.

* * *

They had put him in the infirmary wing, a long room of whitewashed stone and herb-smell, all its beds empty except one. Two Conclave healers hovered at the foot of it, murmuring to each other the way healers did when the news was strange but not fatal — the particular murmur that meant *we don't understand it, but he'll live.*

He looked smaller than he had on the toll road, when she'd first put a blade to his throat and demanded to know why smugglers smelled of ward-oil and starlight. Thin. Younger, somehow, without the weight he'd carried. His eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling beams, and they were — this was the part that made her chest go tight — they were *empty*. Not dead-empty. Cleared-out. A house after the family has moved away, furniture gone, floors swept, waiting for someone new.

"Dominic Pemberton," she said, because you started with names. That was how you knew.

He turned his head toward her with polite, mild attention. "Is that me?"

Varga had faced down border raiders, a wyvern in the Kettle Pass, and her own commanding officer's fury, and none of it had prepared her for four words said so gently. She pulled a chair to the bedside and sat down heavily, the way she hadn't let herself sit down in eleven days.

"Yes," she said. "That's you."

"It sounds like a name people used to say." He considered this. "Like a word in a song you know the tune of but not the words." He looked at his own hands, turning them over. "There was a mule," he offered suddenly, with the air of a man producing his last coin. "I think I remember a mule. Or I remember that I should remember a mule."

"You had a mule," Varga said. "Stubborn creature. Bit me twice. I respected it enormously."

That got the first flicker of something — not memory, maybe, but warmth pooling in the shape memory had left. "Good," he said seriously. "Mules should be respected. I think I believed that very strongly."

*You did,* she thought. *You negotiated with that mule about a bridge like it was a guild council. You told me it had opinions about load distribution.* She didn't say it. It seemed cruel, somehow — handing him coins for a purse that no longer existed.

"The spirit," he said instead, and his voice changed, went quieter and steadier, and for a moment the emptiness filled with reflected light, like moonlight off snow. "It's finished now? What it needed?"

"It spoke. The whole Conclave heard. Every faction, every crown, at the same time, in the same words. No one can bury it now." She paused. "You did that."

"Did I?" He frowned, puzzled, not modest. "It doesn't feel like mine. Like a coat someone else wore home."

"That's roughly what happened," Varga said, "yes."

The healers had explained it to her while he slept: the pact renewed required the old price, freely paid. The first pact-keeper, centuries ago, had given up every memory of everyone she loved — the price of holding the mountain's truth inside a mortal mind without breaking. Dominic had stepped forward before anyone else could move, before anyone even understood the question, and the mountain had accepted. Everything he loved had been poured out of him into the hollow, and the hollow had taken it gently, and given back only this: that the beasts still knew him, and the stones still sang for him.

The debt collectors of heaven had come, and they'd taken everything except the kindness.

"I keep almost-remembering," he said now. He held up a hand and ticked off invisible fingers, and Varga realized with a lurch that this was a habit, some ghost of himself that survived the burning. "There was a boy. I don't have him, but I have — the shape of worrying about him. Feeding him. Being annoyed by him." His hand closed slowly. "Was there a boy?"

Varga looked out the window at the returned stars fading into blue morning.

"There was a boy named Tam," she said. "Nineteen. Warden deserter, terrible cook, brave past all sense. He held the pass so your testimony could arrive. He didn't make it down the mountain." She made herself meet those cleared eyes. "He chose it. I want you to know that, whoever you are now. He chose it laughing."

Dominic was quiet a long moment. Then tears slid down his face, unhurried, unaccompanied by any expression at all, as if his body remembered grief his mind had been emptied of.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I don't know why I'm crying."

"He was yours," Varga said. "Even if you can't find him. Grief like that keeps the address."

He laughed then — wet and startled, and something in the laugh was *him*, unmistakably, the same warm crooked sound she'd heard around smuggler campfires when he thought no one important was listening. "You're very strange," he told her. "Were we friends?"

"No," Varga said. "You hated me for most of it. Rightly. Then I burned my career to save your life, so we've agreed to call it even." She leaned back in the chair. "Ask me again in a year."

* * *

The beasts came over the following days, and this was how Varga learned the shape of what he'd lost and what remained.

The mule arrived first — Renna Kade's caravan mule, escaped or released or simply done with smuggling, standing at the infirmary gate at dawn with the affronted patience of its kind. When they brought it to the courtyard, Dominic went still, then walked straight to it and pressed his forehead against its long grey nose.

"Hello," he whispered. "Hello, I know you."

The mule blew a breath into his hair and did not bite anyone, which Varga took as a formal declaration of peace.

Then came the others. Ravens first, naturally — a dozen of them lining the roof ridge every morning like a jury that had reached its verdict and liked it. A fox that appeared at the garden wall each dusk and watched him with frank interest. On the fourth day a wildcat strolled through the infirmary door past two scandalized healers, jumped onto his bed, and went to sleep on his feet, and Dominic sat perfectly still for six hours rather than disturb it, radiating joy.

"They remember me," he told Varga, wonder-struck, the wildcat purring like a millstone. "I don't remember them, but they remember me. Is that — is that allowed? It feels like cheating."

"Everything about you is cheating," Varga said. "You speak to animals. It's the entire reason my former employers wanted you in irons."

"Oh." He digested this. "That seems rude of them."

"They also wanted to turn you into a weapon."

"*Ruder,*" he said, and scratched the wildcat under the chin, and Varga felt something in her chest unknot that had been tied since the night the stars went out.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. That was the whole arithmetic of it. The Wardens would have taken his gift and made it a leash; the hollow had taken everything else and left the gift free. He'd paid with every face he loved and woken up owning the one thing no army could conscript. The mountain had not robbed him. The mountain had *edited* him — cut the chapters that hurt, kept the spine.

She wasn't sure whether to bless it or burn something. Both, probably. She was learning to hold both.

* * *

Renna Kade came on the sixth day, escorted, disarmed, and furious about all of it.

Varga met her in the courtyard, partly to keep her away from Dominic until tempers settled, partly because she'd promised herself the pleasure. The smuggler-queen looked smaller outside her war-camp — a compact woman with road-dust in her braids and murder banked behind her eyes.

"He lives, then," Renna said.

"He lives."

"And remembers nothing." Her jaw worked. "Not me. Not what I—" She stopped. Whatever she'd been about to confess, she swallowed it, and Varga let her. There was no verdict to deliver here. The Conclave had already heard the spirit's testimony; Renna's hunters had already scattered; justice had already been served in bulk.

"No," Varga said. "Nothing."

Renna stood there a long moment, looking at the infirmary windows, where a man she'd condemned to death was teaching a fox to sit. "Good," she said at last, roughly, and Varga understood it was the truest mercy she had to offer. Then, quieter, almost to herself: "He used to talk to the pack animals on the long hauls. Told them stories. They worked harder for him than for any whip." She turned to go, then paused. "Captain Varga. When they ask who broke the wards' secret open — tell them a smuggler carried it. We're owed one good reputation."

"I'll tell them a mule carried it," Varga said. "Also true, and better for morale."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Renna's face, and then she was gone down the mountain road, and the fox trotted a little way after her before thinking better of it.

* * *

On the ninth evening, Varga found him on the shrine terrace above the Conclave, wrapped in a blanket, watching the stars come out one by one — real stars now, full dark, the sky restored like a repaired window.

"They sing," he said when she sat beside him. "Did you know that? The stones. Since I woke up, everywhere I walk, there's — a hum. Under the ground. The healers say it's not real. But the ravens hear it too. They keep time with it." He tapped the flagstone between them. "Listen."

Varga listened. She was a soldier, not a mystic, and she expected nothing.

But there it was, faint as a pulse through a floor: a low sweet resonance rising out of the mountain itself, threading under their feet, and the ravens along the balustrade shifted and cooed in rhythm with it, and somewhere far below in the dark of the hollow, something vast and old was singing — not a dirge anymore. A work-song. A welcome-home.

"It does that when you pass," Varga said. "Every time. The whole mountain's taken a liking to you."

"Why?" He sounded genuinely baffled. "I'm nobody. I don't even know my own favorite color."

"Amber," Varga said. "You told Tam once. He wouldn't shut up about it." She watched the boy's name land softly in him, a coin dropped in a deep well, rippling somewhere he couldn't see. "And you're not nobody. You're the man who paid the price everyone else flinched from. The hollow doesn't forget its debts. Neither do I."

He was quiet, watching the stars thicken overhead, and the hum rose gentle around them, and the ravens sang.

"What happens to me now?" he asked at last. "Does someone give me a new name? A new life?"

Varga looked out at the mountains — quiet, whole, starlit — and at the man the mountains had decided to keep, and she smiled for the first time in longer than she could measure.

"No need," she said. "The old one fits fine. You just have to grow into it again. Come on — supper's at seven, the mule will sulk if you're late, and I'm told you owe me a story about a bridge."

"A bridge?" he said, delighted, getting up.

"The best one you never remember telling," said Varga, and together they went down singing stairs into the singing dark.
