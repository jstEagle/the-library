## 1. The Girl Who Knocked Twice

The storm came down the Thornfell passes like something with a grudge.

Rosalind Sterling felt it building hours before it arrived — a pressure behind her teeth, a low hum in the copper wire she kept coiled around the cabin's ridgebeam, a restlessness in her hands that no amount of whittling or tea-brewing would settle. Ten years of exile had not dulled that sense. If anything, the quiet had sharpened it, the way a blind man's hearing sharpens: she had starved the storm-calling out of her daily life until all that remained was the knowing.

She knew this storm. It had been born over the Serrated Sea two days ago, had eaten three smaller fronts on its way inland, and now it meant to bury the north passes under six feet of snow and keep them buried until spring thaw.

Good, she thought, banking the fire and pulling the shutters closed. The fewer smugglers willing to run the high roads in weather like this, the fewer reasons anyone had to climb to her door.

Her cabin sat above the tree line where the Thornfell range folded into a shallow granite bowl, half a day's hard climb from the nearest waystation and deliberately unmarked on every map Dain Ferro's people carried. From the southern window, when the clouds allowed, she could see the switchback trail the smugglers called the Gutter — a ribbon of scree and frozen mud threading between two cliffs — and in ten years she had watched perhaps four hundred travelers pick their way along it with contraband salt, untaxed iron, letters that official couriers were not permitted to carry. She had guided some of them herself, before her knees and her caution agreed that the mountains should be walked alone.

She had built a life the size of a cupped hand. Small enough to hold. Small enough to hide.

The storm hit at dusk.

It did not arrive so much as descend — the whole sky dropping at once, wind finding every gap in the stone the way water finds cracks in a hull. Rosalind wedged the last shutter, checked the iron latch twice, and stood for a moment in the sudden blue-dark of her kitchen listening to the world outside try to tear itself apart.

That was when someone knocked.

Two knocks. Soft. Spaced wrong — too long between them, as if whoever knocked had to think about each one separately.

She went still, one hand drifting toward the iron poker by the hearth. Nobody climbed to her cabin in a whiteout. Not smugglers, who knew better; not soldiers, who couldn't find her; not wolves, though once — and she did not let herself think about that night, not ever — something had scratched at her door that had been neither wolf nor man.

The knock came again. Two more. Weaker.

"Who's there?" she called, and heard her own voice come out flat and hard, the voice she used on men who tried to short-change her for passage. "This is private ground. Turn back."

No answer but the wind. Then, faintly, a sound that was not the wind at all — a small, wet, effortful sound, like breathing through cloth.

Rosalind told herself she would open the door a crack, see nothing, and close it again. That is what a sensible woman does. That is what a woman does when she has spent a decade teaching herself that every knock is a hook and every hook is baited.

She opened the door wide, because the wind ripped it out of her hand, and because something in her chest had already decided.

* * *

The child lay crumpled against her doorstep like a bundle of laundry someone had dropped.

Small. Seven years old, maybe eight, though frost makes age impossible to read. Wrapped in a wool blanket that had gone stiff with ice, wearing boots meant for a child twice her size, both packed solid with snow. Her lips had the gray-blue color Rosalind associated with things pulled out of rivers too late. And her skin — where the hood of the blanket had slipped back — was marked with fine dark lines, inked or scarred, running from her temples down along her jaw in patterns too regular to be accident.

Frostbite, hypothermia, and lost. In a whiteout. On a mountain where the nearest village was two days' walk and the child could not possibly have walked one hour of it without dying.

Rosalind did the arithmetic in the space of a single heartbeat, and every answer came back *impossible*, and none of it mattered, because the girl's eyes opened — dull, glassy, but open — and found hers, and held them with an urgency that needed no language at all.

"Inside," Rosalind said, scooping her up. The child weighed almost nothing. "You're inside now."

* * *

She worked the way she'd been trained to work, hands remembering what her pride preferred to forget. Cut away the frozen clothes — careful, careful, never tear frozen fabric from frozen flesh. Warm water, not hot. Blankets, then skin contact, her own body heat against the girl's back because the fire alone would kill faster than the cold. Rub the fingers, the toes, the ears. Speak constantly, even nonsense, even curses, because a voice gives the drowning something to swim toward.

The girl did not shiver. That frightened Rosalind more than anything else. Shivering was the body arguing for itself, and this small body had stopped arguing somewhere on the mountain above her home.

"Come on," Rosalind muttered into the girl's frozen hair, chafing her arms, her own heart hammering in a way she resented deeply. "You climbed to my door. You don't get to quit on my threshold. Come on."

For a quarter of an hour there was nothing but the storm screaming against the shutters and Rosalind's own breath, ragged, counting. Then, beneath her palms, the smallest tremor. A shiver. Another.

"Good girl." Her voice cracked on it. She ignored that. "There you are. Argue with me. Good."

By midnight the girl's color had come back — first an alarming flush across the cheeks, then a steadier warmth. Rosalind got sweetened broth into her a spoonful at a time, most of it running down her chin, some of it staying down. The dark markings on her face were ink, Rosalind confirmed, drawn in a pigment that caught the firelight with a faint metallic gleam. Old ink, worn smooth at the edges. Someone had put these lines on a very young face a long time ago.

The girl slept in Rosalind's bed, in Rosalind's spare shift, looking absurd and breakable among the grey wool blankets. Rosalind dragged a chair to the bedside and told herself she was keeping watch for practical reasons — fever, relapse, the possibility that the child was a lure and her handlers were already climbing through the storm — and not because she found she could not stop looking at those inked lines, or at the girl's hands, which even in sleep stayed half-curled around something invisible, guarding it.

Outside, the whiteout howled. Inside, the fire ticked and settled, and Rosalind Sterling sat awake in her small, carefully hidden life and felt it straining at its seams.

* * *

Morning came grey and windless. The storm had spent itself in the night, and when Rosalind cracked the south shutter she saw a world remade — drifts to the eaves, the Gutter trail erased entirely, the pines bowed under white. No tracks anywhere. Whatever path the child had followed up the mountain had been wiped clean, as if the mountain itself had conspired to leave no evidence.

Which raised the question she had been avoiding all night: *what path?*

The girl woke slowly, surfacing in stages — a flutter of lashes, a frown, then a bolt upright and a sharp look around the room that was far too composed for a child. She took inventory the way a soldier would: the door, the window, the poker by the hearth, the woman watching her. Her gaze came back to Rosalind and stopped there, steady and assessing, and something about the quality of it made the hairs rise on Rosalind's arms.

"You gave me a bad scare," Rosalind said. "Can you tell me your name?"

The girl's mouth opened. What came out was nothing Rosalind had ever heard.

It was not the trade-tongue of the lowlands, nor the clipped mountain dialect of the Thornfell villages, nor the liquid merchant-speech Dain's caravan used, nor the formal High Cant of the storm-callers that Rosalind could recite in her sleep. It was older than all of those — or it sounded older, its vowels round and deep, its consonants soft as falling ash. Three syllables, maybe four. The girl said them slowly, watching Rosalind's face with desperate attention, and said them again when they landed nowhere.

"I'm sorry," Rosalind said. "Again. Slower."

The girl tried. She pointed at herself, then spoke — a word like a river-stone turning over. She pointed at the fire and produced another sound, lower, warmer, unmistakably meaning *fire*. Pointed at the window, at the snow, and made a third word that carried, impossibly, the sound of wind in it.

Rosalind sat very still.

She understood him. Not the words — she did not know the words — but underneath the words, in some cellar of her mind she had never had reason to visit, meanings were stirring. When the girl said the fire-word, Rosalind's mind supplied not a translation but a recognition, the way you recognize a melody heard once in childhood. When she named herself, that river-stone word, Rosalind felt a shape of meaning settle into place: not *name*, exactly. Something closer to *small bird*. Or — no. Closer than that.

"*Wren*," Rosalind said aloud, testing it, and the girl's whole face changed — relief flooding it so fast and so completely that she began to cry, silently, tears cutting channels down the inked lines on her cheeks.

Wren nodded. Wren pressed her small fists against her mouth. Wren looked at Rosalind as if Rosalind were the first person in the entire world to have understood her, which, Rosalind thought with a slow cold turn of her stomach, might be precisely true.

"How do I know that?" Rosalind asked the empty room. "How do I know any of this?"

The girl — Wren — reached across the blanket and took Rosalind's hand. Her grip was fever-warm and fierce. She spoke again, a longer phrase this time, urgent, her eyes searching Rosalind's face, and although the individual words slid past like fish through current, the *shape* of the sentence arrived whole, the way a blow arrives before you see the fist:

*They are coming. You have to hide me. Please.*

Rosalind withdrew her hand slowly. She rose, crossed to the hearth, and busied herself with the kettle because her hands wanted occupation and her face wanted somewhere else to be.

Ten years, she reminded herself. Ten years of silence, of smallness, of a life so carefully concealed that even her reflection seemed to apologize for taking up space. And within a single night, a child had fallen out of an impossible storm speaking a tongue that did not exist, carrying ink-marks Rosalind almost recognized and warnings Rosalind understood completely, and now the word *they* hung in the warm air of her kitchen like smoke that would not clear.

"They," Rosalind said quietly, in the trade-tongue, knowing the girl wouldn't follow the words. "Who's *they*, little bird?"

Wren answered anyway, as if the question had been heard perfectly. She lifted one small hand and drew, on the blanket with a deliberate finger, a circle. Then, inside the circle, a hollow — pressing down at its center until the wool dimpled, until the shape was not a circle anymore but an absence, a ring with a void at its heart.

Then Wren looked up, and Rosalind saw that the girl was not afraid of the thing she had drawn.

She was afraid *for* it.

* * *

Rosalind spent the morning telling herself stories.

The girl was a refugee from some eastern clan whose dialect had drifted beyond recognition — except no eastern clans sent children alone over the Thornfells in whiteout season. The girl had been separated from a caravan — except there were no caravans, the passes had been empty for weeks, Dain's last runner had confirmed it. The girl was mad, or cursed, or a ghost — except ghosts did not eat three bowls of porridge and fall asleep sitting up with a spoon still in their fist.

In the afternoon, while Wren slept, Rosalind unlocked the third trunk from the bottom of her wardrobe — the one she had sworn, on the day she'd carried it up this mountain, never to open again — and took out the lexicon.

It had belonged to her old life: a slim volume bound in grey leather, unmarked, its pages thin as onion skin. She had stolen it from the citadel archives the night she fled, or rather she had taken it without deciding to, the way a drowning woman takes hold of driftwood. For ten years it had sat in the dark, and now her hands shook opening it, and she could not have said whether from fear or hunger.

She turned to the appendix of dead tongues. Vessic. The word was there, waiting, the way certain words wait — patient, inevitable. *Vessic: liturgical and common speech of the Hollowlands. Extinct since the closing of the war, c. 300 years prior. Surviving fragments: ritual texts only.*

Below the entry, in faded ink, someone had copied out a fragment of the old script — a line of prayer, the archivist's note said, recovered from the ruins of a sanctuary in the Hollowlands.

Rosalind read it. And the cellar of her mind, that locked and lightless room, swung open.

She did not translate the prayer. She simply *knew* it — felt the words assemble themselves in her mouth like birds returning to a roost, felt their meaning bloom warm and terrible: *Keep what was given. Guard what was planted. Until the hollow turns, we hold.*

She sat down on the floor, the lexicon open in her lap, and laughed once — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it.

Three hundred years dead, the archivists said. Extinct. Fragments only.

Then why did the language sit inside her like a second heartbeat?

Behind her, small bare feet padded across the floorboards. Wren stood in the doorway wrapped in a grey blanket, hair sleep-tangled, watching Rosalind with those too-old eyes. She looked at the open book. She looked at Rosalind's face. And slowly, solemnly, she touched her own temple — right where the inked lines began — and then pointed at Rosalind.

At Rosalind's temple, where nothing was written at all.

"No," Rosalind said, and her voice came out strange, distant, belonging to someone standing just behind her shoulder. "Whatever you think I am, I'm not that."

But Wren only shook her head, patient, certain, and said something soft in Vessic — one sentence, four words, that Rosalind understood without wanting to, without being able to stop:

*I know you. You were there.*
