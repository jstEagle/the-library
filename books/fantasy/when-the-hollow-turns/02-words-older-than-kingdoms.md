## 2. Words Older Than Kingdoms

The girl slept like something drowned.

Rosalind had laid her on the cot beneath the south window, wrapped in the good wool blanket she kept for wounded smugglers, and for three hours the child had not moved except to breathe — slow, shallow breaths that fogged the cold glass in little pulses. Frost had climbed the panes from the outside. Inside, the fire cracked and settled, and Rosalind sat across the room with a cup of tea gone cold in her hands, watching.

She had not slept. She doubted she would.

The girl's boots stood by the hearth, steaming. They were fine boots — too fine, stitched with a pattern Rosalind didn't recognize, dyed a deep river-blue that no dye-house in Thornfell produced. The child's coat was the same. Someone had dressed her well before sending her up a mountain in the worst whiteout of the season. That meant money. Money meant purpose. Purpose meant that whatever had walked through Rosalind's door last night had been sent, deliberately, to *her* door specifically, or at least to this stretch of the high roads, and Rosalind did not believe in coincidence the way other people believed in weather.

And then there was the language.

The girl had spoken only a handful of words since collapsing — *thank you* shaped into something else, *water*, *cold*, rendered in syllables that slid past the ear like water over stone. Rosalind had understood them anyway. Not translated them; *understood* them, the way you understand a smell from childhood, the way your body knows a stair's height in the dark. That was impossible. Rosalind spoke four languages and could curse competently in six more, and none of them had ever touched her like that, from the inside, as if the words had been waiting in her all along for someone to come along and knock them loose.

She set down the tea and crossed to the eastern wall of the cabin, where the shelves stood. Most of what she owned was practical: rope, tallow, maps of passes that appeared on no surveyor's chart. But behind the false back of the third shelf, wedged between a ledger of Dain Ferro's debts and a tin of storm-glass shavings, lay the thing she had sworn ten years ago never to open again.

The lexicon was small — a hand-span square, bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth with age. No title. No author. When she pulled it out, dust rose off it in a faint grey ghost, and her hands remembered its weight the way her mouth remembered the words the girl had spoken.

She had stolen it from the citadel archives the night she left. Or rather, she had taken it openly, in front of witnesses, which was stranger — carried it down the great stair while the cantors watched her go and said nothing, as if the book were part of her exile, as if someone had decided she should have it. She'd never understood that. Ten years, and she'd never opened it past the first page, because the first page was written in a hand she recognized, and recognizing it had felt like pressing on a bruise she couldn't locate.

Now she sat by the fire with it open on her knees, and pressed anyway.

*Vessic*, said the first page, in the old cantor's script. *The tongue of the low valleys. Dead with the Hollow War. Do not teach it. Do not write it anew. What is named in Vessic remembers being named.*

Below that, in fresher ink, someone else had added a single line: *Too late.*

Rosalind turned pages with fingers that had gone carefully steady, the way they went steady before a storm-call. The lexicon was arranged not alphabetically but by association — word flowing into word like tributaries, each entry linked to the next by some logic older than kingdoms. And there, half-buried among the entries for *river* and *ice* and *door*, she found the shape of what the girl had said on her doorstep.

*Aeth-venn.* Small one. Little wanderer.

*Sessa.* Warm. But the root ran deeper — *sessari*, hearth-fire; *sessarim*, the keeping of fire for another; and beneath that, in smaller script, a note: *the highest courtesy. To keep fire for a stranger is to declare them kin.*

Rosalind read the entry three times. On the doorstep, frostbitten and half-blind, the girl hadn't begged. She had declared Rosalind kin. Offered the highest courtesy her dead language possessed to a woman whose name she could not have known.

The fire popped. Upstairs — no, across the room, there was no upstairs here, the cabin was one room and a loft — across the room the girl breathed on, small and even.

Rosalind worked until the candle guttered. She mapped every sound the child had made onto the lexicon's grammar, and the grammar held. It held *too well*. Vessic wasn't a pidgin or a border-slurry; it was intricate, inflected, built on principles no living tongue used anymore — verbs that conjugated by distance rather than time, so that how far away a thing was mattered more than when it happened. A language for people who thought in geography. A language for mountains.

Which raised the question that had been sitting in Rosalind's chest since midnight, heavy as wet snow: who taught a child of seven or eight years a language that had been dead for three centuries? Languages didn't survive like that. They leaked into lullabies, place-names, curses — but this girl spoke it whole, natively, the way Rosalind spoke common. You could not learn a drowned language without a teacher, and you could not have a teacher without a community, and the Vessic had no community. The war had seen to that. Every account agreed, Order-written though the accounts were: the Vessic people had ended in a single night of fire, and the world had moved on, and the world did not move backward.

Except, apparently, uphill, through a whiteout, to Rosalind Sterling's door.

She closed the lexicon. Her thumb had left a print in the dust on its cover, and she wiped it away without deciding to — an archivist's instinct, a cantor's instinct, an instinct she was supposed to have burned out of herself a decade ago.

Outside, the wind had dropped. The whiteout had spent itself against the peaks around midnight, and now the silence after storm lay over Thornfell like a held breath. In that silence Rosalind heard, very clearly, the thing she had been refusing to hear all evening: the girl murmuring in her sleep.

Not crying. Not dreaming aloud in the ordinary way. Speaking — measured, deliberate, the cadence of recitation — in that sliding stone-water tongue.

Rosalind crossed the room. Knelt by the cot. The girl's eyes were shut, her lashes crusted at the corners, her small face slack with the deep sleep of the truly spent. But her lips moved. Words came out of her in a whisper pitched exactly for one listener, and Rosalind leaned close because she couldn't help it, because the language reached into her again, that awful familiarity, that sense of doors opening in rooms of herself she'd never entered.

She caught fragments. *Ash.* Something about ash, and about *below* — the verb conjugated for very near, closer than touching. Then a string of words she lost entirely. Then, distinct, repeated twice with the solemnity of a bell:

*"Sereth-vale. Sereth-vale im-korra."*

Rosalind went cold from the scalp down.

She knew the name. Not from the lexicon — from elsewhere. From a room she had walked through exactly once, nine years ago, in another life: the sealed archive beneath the citadel of Karavel, where the Order kept the documents too dangerous for its own cantors to cite. She had been there under escort, a rising storm-caller granted a glimpse of the deep shelves as a mark of favor, and among the ledgers of troop movements and grain requisitions from the Hollow War she had seen a map. Just a map, glimpsed sideways, half a heartbeat. A valley in the heart of what was now Court territory, marked not in the cartographer's ink but in red, overprinted, with a word stamped across it in the Order's formal seal-script:

*SERETH-VALE — SITE OF THE ATROCITY — SEALED BY CONCORD OF THE NINE — DO NOT APPROACH.*

She had asked her escort what atrocity. The escort — old Cantor Brell, dry as paper — had looked at her with something she'd mistaken then for weariness and understood only later, much later, might have been fear.

"The one we do not name," Brell had said. "That is what the seal means, child."

And now a dead language's last living speaker was saying the name in her sleep, twice, like a tolling, in Rosalind's cabin above the smuggler roads, three hundred miles and three hundred years from anywhere it should exist.

The girl stirred. Her brow creased. Her whisper climbed, still asleep, toward something urgent — *im-korra, im-korra* — and Rosalind's hands hovered over her, useless, because the lexicon was right there on the floor by her knee and she already knew what *korra* meant. She'd read it an hour ago. *Korra*: debt. Obligation. The thing owed that cannot be refused.

*Sereth-vale, the debt unpayable.* Or perhaps: *the debt unpaid.* The grammar wouldn't settle for her, and the not-knowing sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

The girl subsided. Her breathing eased. Whatever current had carried her recitation broke apart, and she slept on, ordinary now, a child in a blanket, one fist curled under her chin.

Rosalind stayed kneeling a long time.

She thought about the boots. About the fine blue stitching. About whoever had sent this child up a mountain in a killing storm, toward — or past, or through — the one woman in the Thornfells who could half-hear a murdered tongue. She thought about her own exile, ten years ago, and the crime she'd been convicted of, the betrayal she had confessed to because everyone insisted she must remember it and she could not, and how the confession had felt like signing her name to someone else's life. She thought about the lexicon given freely to a traitor by cantors who watched her carry it away and said nothing.

*What is named in Vessic remembers being named.*

She got up. Fed the fire. Took down the storm-glass tin and set a reading, mostly to give her hands work: the glass showed clear air for two days, then a pressure-fall building in the northeast like a bruise coming up under skin. Weather, or something that rode the weather. She'd know which by tomorrow.

Dawn came grey and clean over the pass. The girl woke slowly, blinked at the ceiling beams, at the fire, at Rosalind — and did not scream, did not cry, did not do any of the things a lost child does on waking in a stranger's house. She looked at Rosalind with a steadiness far too old for her face, and said, in that sliding stone-water voice, one word.

*Aeth-venn,* she said. *Little wanderer.* Then, pointing one small finger at Rosalind's chest, she added a second word Rosalind hadn't heard yet, and had to look up.

It took her most of the morning, cross-referencing roots, muttering sounds until the grammar clicked. The word was a title. It had only one entry, and the entry was short, and the note beneath it was in that same second hand, the fresher ink, the one that had written *too late* on the first page.

The word meant *keeper*. As in: one who keeps. As in: the one who keeps the fire, the one who keeps the gate, the one who keeps the memory.

As in: *you.*

Rosalind set down the book very carefully, because if she didn't she was going to throw it into the fire.

Outside, far down the north road, where no honest traveler should be, she saw smoke rising — thin, deliberate, signal-smoke, three columns in a row. And beneath her feet, so faint she might have imagined it, the mountain hummed the way it always hummed before storms. The way it hummed, ten years ago, the day they took her name away.

The girl watched her across the cabin, calm as deep water, and waited.
