## 3. Smoke on the North Pass

Dain Ferro smelled the smoke before he saw it, and he had smelled enough burning waystations in his life to know the difference between a hearth left untended and a message written in fire.

The first one was at Kettle Gap, a squat stone shelter that had stood since before the Thornfell passes had names. He'd slept under its roof forty times in twenty years of running the high roads, back when he was a boy carrying bolts of grey wool for his father, and later carrying things no manifest would ever name. The smoke rose straight up in the still morning air, black and greasy with pitch — someone had fed the roof beams deliberately, someone who wanted it seen from three ridges away.

He reined up at the head of the column and held up a fist. Behind him, eleven mules shifted and stamped, and six men went quiet the way his people knew how to go quiet.

"Burned last night," said Tam, riding up on his left. Tam was the youngest, quick-eyed, and the best judge of cold ash in the company. "No more'n ten hours. Nobody home when it caught."

"Anybody home now?"

Tam spat over the side of his saddle. "If there were, we'd have seen them by now. Or they'd have seen us."

That was the trouble with the north passes. You could see a long way and be seen from just as far, and everything between was rock and scree and wind that carried sound like a courier carries letters — faithfully, and to whoever paid for the delivery.

Dain studied the ruin a moment longer. Waystations didn't burn by accident. Lightning struck them maybe once in a generation, and lightning didn't pile kindling against the door first, which is what somebody had done here; you could see the pale scar where the flame had licked hottest around the lintel, hungry inward. Somebody had wanted the place dead and had wanted everyone walking this road to know it.

"Move on," he said. "We're behind schedule as it is."

They moved on, and Dain kept looking back, which was not like him. A good smuggler looked forward or not at all. But something about the shape of that smoke had lodged under his ribs like a splinter, and it worked deeper at the second station too — Hollowford, half a day on, where they found the same story repeated: beams burned, walls standing, ash raked neat around the foundation as though the arsonists had taken pride in their work. And at Hollowford there was something new.

Boot prints. Dozens of them, pressed into the frozen mud of the approach road, uniform in size, uniform in spacing. Soldiers' prints. And beside them, dragged through the mud in long deliberate strokes, a symbol Dain hadn't seen in fifteen years and had hoped never to see again: a circle with a vertical line driven through it, the mark of a Crown writ of search and seizure.

"Regulars?" asked Brannic, his second, a slab-faced man who'd been with him since the Ferro company was four mules and a prayer.

"Crown regulars don't come north of the Saddle Rocks," Dain said. "Not in winter, not in force. It costs too much to feed them." He crouched by the prints and touched one edge, where the mud had frozen into a crisp ridge. "These are fresh. Two days old, maybe less."

"Then what are they?"

Dain stood and brushed the mud from his fingers and did not answer, because the honest answer was a thing he didn't want to say aloud on an open pass with six men listening. Regulars came north when they were paid to, and the only purses deep enough to pay for a winter march into Thornfell belonged to either the Citadel orders or the merchant houses of the lowlands, and both of those paid for one reason only: something up here was worth more than the cost of finding it.

He thought of Rosalind Sterling then, the way you think of a tooth that's been aching quietly for weeks. He thought of her cabin above the Greywater fork, of the girl he'd heard about secondhand from the wool-traders — *a child, Dain, she's taken in a child out of the whiteout, can you credit it* — and he felt the splinter under his ribs slide another inch.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen pillar-stone, a ring of canvas and mule-blankets and the small permitted luxury of a peat fire burned low in a pit lined with slate so no light showed upward. Supper was barley and salt pork and the dried bilberries Tam had hoarded against all sense. The men ate in silence, because silence was cheap and talk could be expensive.

It was Tam, naturally, who broke it.

"Captain. The stations. Someone said down at Ironhollow that soldiers were asking after passengers. Small ones. Children."

Dain kept chewing. "Who said?"

"A drover. Came through a fortnight back, wouldn't take the north road himself, went round by the lake. Said there were men at the tollhouse below Ravenmere asking every caravan about small passengers. Asking had anyone carried a girl northward. Alone. On foot."

"On foot," Dain repeated. "In winter."

"That's what he said." Tam turned his tin cup in his hands. "Captain, the way I hear it, these soldiers aren't asking like tax-men ask. They're asking like priests ask. Quiet-like. And where they've asked and gotten nothing, the next station along burns."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, on the dark shoulder of the mountain, wind moved through stone with a sound like slow breathing. Dain looked at his men — Brannic solid as the pillar-stone itself, Tam sharp and frightened and trying not to show it, the others watching him the way crews watch a captain when the weather turns: not for comfort, but for the truth.

"You want to know if we're carrying anything worth a burnin'," Dain said.

Nobody denied it.

"We're carrying dye-mordant and untaxed tin," Dain said, "and that's all, and if any man says different I'll want to know where he's been keeping his imagination. But." He held up a hand before relief could settle. "But there's a woman above the Greywater who has been the best client this company ever had, and there's word of a child at her door, and there are soldiers on the north passes asking questions in priest voices. I don't believe in coincidences that line up that neat."

Brannic set down his cup. "What do you want to do?"

"I want to finish this run," Dain said. "We deliver at Ironhollow, we take our pay, and then—" he paused, hating the words even as he built them, because every word was a plank knocked out of the wall he'd spent ten years building between his business and his conscience— "then we go up to the Greywater. Quiet. Off the roads."

"And after that?"

"After that I have a conversation with an old friend about whether it's time for her and her guest to be somewhere else."

* * *

Ironhollow sat in its bowl of slag-heaps and mine-shafts like a dog in a ditch, all of it built crooked and none of it built clean. The town existed because the mountain bled iron, and the mountain had been bleeding a long time; half the shafts were worked out, and the town had grown fat and strange on the empty spaces beneath it. Smugglers loved it. So did the kind of people who bought what smugglers carried.

The delivery went smooth. The buyer counted the ingots twice, frowned at the mordant once, paid without argument, and Dain walked out of the counting-house with a purse that would keep the company fed till spring and a mind that wouldn't sit still.

He found Maren Kestrel's shop on the lower terrace, wedged between a chandler's and a shuttered assay office, the window full of old maps and brass instruments gone green with age. She was the closest thing Ironhollow had to a scholar, which was to say she'd been thrown out of somewhere better and had landed here, selling charts to miners and answers to anyone who asked careful questions. He'd used her twice for route-work through the deep fells. She owed him for a debt he never named and she never acknowledged.

The bell over her door had been muffled with wax, which was new.

She looked up from a table buried under folios, and her eyes — sharp, grey, older than her face suggested — went past him to the street before coming back. "Dain Ferro. You look like weather coming on."

"There is weather coming on." He shut the door and dropped the bar, which she allowed, which told him she already knew why he'd come. "Soldiers on the north passes. Burning waystations. Asking after a child."

Maren's hands went still on the folio she'd been holding.

"Tell me exactly," she said.

So he told her — the fires, the boot-prints, the Crown writ-mark dragged in the mud, the drover's tale from Ravenmere. He watched her face while he talked, and Maren Kestrel, who had a face like a locked drawer, let two things slip across it: first a flicker of recognition when he mentioned the writ-mark, and then something worse, something that looked almost like grief.

"The circle-and-line isn't Crown anymore," she said when he finished. "It hasn't been for years. Certain houses use it still because it frightens people, and certain other hands use it because it hides whose work it really is." She closed the folio. "Dain, who is the child?"

"I don't know. That's the truth. I know what the wool-traders said — a girl, frostbitten, alone, at Rosalind's door during the whiteout. I know Rosalind took her in, because whatever else Rosalind Sterling is, she isn't the kind of woman who leaves a child in the snow. Beyond that, nothing."

"And the soldiers ask for a small passenger. Not a fugitive. Not a thief." Maren came around the table, moving stiffly, and pulled a chart from a shelf — an old survey of the north fells, annotated in a cramped hand. Her finger came down on the Greywater fork, then tracked east, past the Saddle Rocks, past the dead country beyond, and stopped at a blank space where most maps put nothing at all but where her annotation read, in tiny letters, *the Gate*. "There are perhaps four living reasons anyone would cross the whole of Thornfell in winter hunting a child," she said. "And none of them are kind."

"Then tell me plain, archivist: is my friend in danger?"

"She is in the middle of it," Maren said. "Which for Rosalind Sterling may amount to the same thing, given what she used to be." She rolled the chart and pushed it across the table to him. "Take this. Don't ask what the marking at the end means, because I'm not sure I could survive telling you tonight. Go to her. Go quickly, and go quietly, and when you get there, look at the child's eyes before you look at anything else. If they're wrong — if there's a depth in them that doesn't match a body that size — you send for me. However far. Whatever it costs."

"That bad?"

"Worse." For a moment Maren looked not like a disgraced archivist in a cluttered shop but like a woman carrying something heavy for a very long distance. "I helped bury some of this history, Dain. I'd hoped it would stay buried. Smoke on the north pass tells me my hope was wasted."

* * *

They went up the Greywater trail two days later, fast and light — Dain, Brannic, Tam, and two others; the rest of the company he'd sent south with the purse and instructions to winter at Lark's Ford and touch no main road. The trail climbed through pine shadow and then bare rock, following the frozen river up toward the notch where Rosalind's cabin crouched above the water like a stone that had decided to grow a chimney.

He'd made this climb a hundred times, always with cargo, always with a price agreed in advance. This was the first time he'd come empty-handed and uneasy, scanning the treeline for woodsmoke and finding it — one thin thread of it, rising from the cabin's chimney, ordinary as bread — and feeling relief and dread arrive together, hand in hand.

Rosalind met them in the yard. She'd heard them coming, of course; she always heard everything within two miles, a habit of a former life she'd never managed to shed. She stood with her arms folded against the cold and her eyes doing their usual work of cataloguing everything about him — the men, the absence of mules, the set of his shoulders — and finishing the sum before he'd crossed the gate.

"Dain. You're off your road."

"Ferro company delivers anywhere," he said. "Even nowhere."

Her mouth twitched, but her eyes didn't. They went past him to Brannic, to Tam, to the chart-case slung at his back, and narrowed. "You brought few men and no goods and a face like a funeral. Say it plain."

"Waystations on the north pass are burning. Kettle Gap, Hollowford, maybe more by now. Burned deliberate, burned for show. There are soldiers — not Crown regulars, whatever their marks claim — working the high roads, and at Ravenmere they're asking every caravan after a small passenger. A girl. Alone. On foot." He let that sit. "I came to see with my own eyes how big your problem is."

Something moved through Rosalind's expression, too quick to catch — fear, maybe, or the memory of it — and was gone, packed away behind the flat grey calm she wore like armor. "And how big does it look from the road?"

"Big enough that Maren Kestrel gave me a chart and told me to look at your guest's eyes."

At that, Rosalind went very still. Wind came down the valley and stirred the frost-fur on the pines, and for three heartbeats neither of them spoke, and Dain understood that he had just stepped over a line he hadn't known was drawn — that the child was not a matter Rosalind had intended to discuss in the yard, in front of witnesses, in the open air where the mountains themselves might be listening.

"Inside," she said finally. "Your men can stable themselves; you remember the shed. And Dain—" she paused at the door, her hand on the latch, and looked back at him with something rawer than she'd shown in ten years of business, "—before I decide what to tell you, I need to know one thing. When it comes to a choice between your company and your conscience, which one have you been feeding all these years?"

He thought of the burning stations. Of the boot-prints. Of Tam's voice saying *they ask like priests ask*. Of a purse of iron-money warm against his ribs, and of a promise he'd made nobody, out loud, ever, that he would not stand in a doorway and watch.

"Conscience," he said. "Bleeding, underfed, but alive."

Rosalind nodded slowly, opened the door, and the warmth of the cabin spilled out around them — woodsmoke and stew and something else underneath, faint and strange, like the smell of the air before lightning.

From the corner by the hearth, a small figure rose to her feet. A girl, thin and dark-eyed, watching Dain with an expression far too old for her face — and when she looked at him, Dain felt the bottom drop out of his stomach, because Maren was right, gods help them all, Maren was right: there was a depth in those eyes that didn't match the body holding them, a stillness, an age, something looking out through a child's face like a lamp lit behind frosted glass.

And the girl tilted her head, and spoke — soft, clear, in a tongue Dain had never heard and somehow, horribly, half-understood:

*"You bring the smell of smoke,"* she said. *"Smoke follows you. It will follow all of us."*

Rosalind's face went white as the snow outside.

"They're burning the waystations," Dain said quietly. "Rosalind — they're not searching for her anymore. They're herding her. Everything that burns sits along the only roads south."
