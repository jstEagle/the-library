## 4. What the Order Buried

The girl slept through the second night, and Rosalind did not.

She sat at the table with the lexicon open between her elbows, a candle guttering low, and read the same passage four times without it settling into sense. *Vessic. Tongue of the drowned coast. Extinct since the Hollow War.* The letters swam, and beneath them the ink seemed to darken, as though the page itself were reluctant to be understood.

Behind her, Wren breathed in the small, even rhythm of true sleep. Twice in the night the child had murmured — soft syllables that curled through the cabin like smoke — and twice Rosalind had caught herself understanding fragments before she could stop herself. *Cold. Door. The door was blue once.*

She had never learned Vessic. No one alive had. And yet the words landed in her like stones dropped down a well, striking something deep before the splash of meaning rose to meet them.

That was the thought that finally drove her out of the chair. Not curiosity. Fear.

There was a place where answers might live, if any did. Three days' hard walk north, past the smuggler roads, up where the Thornfell peaks went grey and toothed against the sky: the vault at Kestrelmoor. Abandoned — officially decommissioned after the Purges, when the Order of the Storm recalled its archives to the citadels and burned what it could not carry. But Rosalind had guided men who'd stripped copper wire from its doors, and she knew better than most how much the Order left behind when leaving behind was safer than explaining.

She packed by candlelight. Dried meat, flint, rope, the lexicon wrapped in oilcloth against the snowmelt. At the last moment she took her father's old storm-iron pendant from the box beneath the floorboards — not to wear it, never to wear it, but because going into an Order vault without iron felt like walking into a temple with your hands empty.

At the door she paused. Wren lay curled on the pallet, frost-pale still but breathing strong, one fist tucked beneath her chin. Rosalind studied her the way she'd study an unfamiliar mountain pass: looking for the route through, the danger, the way down.

"I'll be back before you wake twice," she told the sleeping girl, knowing the child couldn't hear her, or perhaps hoping she couldn't. "Lock it behind me."

The bolt slid home. The wind had died overnight, leaving the world white and silent and enormous, and Rosalind set out across it alone.

* * *

Kestrelmoor announced itself by absence. The pass narrowed, the trees gave up, and then there was simply nothing — no birdsong, no wind, a hush so complete it pressed against the ears. Storm-caller wards, dead for decades, still holding their shape in the air like footprints in ash. The Order built things to outlast their purpose.

The vault's entrance was cut into the mountainside beneath an overhang furred with ice, hidden behind a curtain of rock that looked natural until you knew to look. Rosalind had known for ten years and never come. She told herself that had been caution. Standing there now, breath smoking, hand flat against stone cold enough to burn, she admitted the truth: it had been fear. The Order had taken everything from her once — name, calling, the storm itself — and some stubborn animal part of her had refused to go looking for more to lose.

But the Order had put its name in a dead language inside a living child. That changed the arithmetic.

The seal was a storm-sigil, six feet tall, carved deep and filled with lead that had gone black with age. It should have been inert. When Rosalind pressed her palm to it, it woke.

Not fully. Not the screaming white fury of a live ward. Just a shiver under her skin, a recognition — *one of ours* — and then the lead softened like tallow and ran, and the rock ground aside on hinges no one had oiled in thirty years, and the dark breathed out at her. Cold, dry, smelling of dust and lightning-struck metal and something older underneath, something like a room that had been wept in.

Her blood sang. She hated it. Ten years of silence, and the vault called to whatever still lived in her veins like a mother's voice, and her body answered before her mind could forbid it.

"Ten years," she said aloud, to the dark, to herself. "You don't get to want this."

She went in anyway.

* * *

Inside, Kestrelmoor was a throat of descending corridors, each gate sealed with lesser sigils that woke and yielded to her touch with that same horrible eagerness. Whoever had keyed these locks had keyed them to bloodline, and whatever bloodline ran in Rosalind Sterling's veins opened every one. By the third gate she had stopped being surprised. By the fifth she had stopped pretending she wasn't cataloguing it, filing each yielding seal away like evidence.

The archive halls spread out below in long galleries lined with niches — thousands of them, clay cylinders and bound folios and lead boxes, the sediment of centuries. Most had been emptied in the recall. What remained was what the Order had chosen not to burn and not to keep: the embarrassing, the inconvenient, the half-finished.

Rosalind walked the aisles running her lantern along the spines, reading labels in the old cant: *Weatherworks, Third Cycle. Rite of Stillwater. Correspondence, Northern Citadels, sealed by order of—*

And then, in a niche set apart from the others, deeper than the rest, behind a gate that bore not the storm-sigil but a symbol she didn't recognize — a circle broken by seven lines radiating inward, like light falling backward into a point:

*Seedwork. Restricted. Access: First Cantors only.*

She stood before it a long time. Her breath made ghosts on the frozen air. Somewhere above her, snow began sliding off the overhang in soft, muffled slumps, the only sound in the world.

"You shouldn't," she said to herself.

She touched the gate. It opened like all the others — eagerly, gladly, as if it had been waiting thirty years for exactly her hand.

* * *

The chamber beyond was small, round, and utterly unlike the rest of the vault. Where the galleries were austere, functional, this room had been made beautiful once. Murals covered the walls, faded to browns and ghost-blues: figures robed in storm-grey standing in a ring around a single kneeling figure, and above them, rendered in gold leaf that still caught the lantern-light, a shape like a seed — a teardrop of light with roots growing downward and branches upward, both made of tiny script.

Vessic script. Rosalind recognized it now; three days with the lexicon had taught her the letterforms if not the language. The seed was written all the way through, dense as pith, every root and branch a line of text.

On a lectern at the room's center lay a folio bound in storm-grey leather, the Order's color, stamped with the First Cantor's seal. Its title plate read, in the old cant: *On the Cultivation and Keeping of Living Memory.*

Rosalind opened it with hands that were not quite steady.

The early pages were theory — dense, elegant argument about the nature of memory and matter, about how a recollection could be rooted in a living mind the way a cutting is rooted in soil. The handwriting shifted between authors, generations of them, marginalia arguing with marginalia. She skimmed, hunting, until she reached a section headed *Practical Application: The Vessic Matter,* and there the tone changed. The elegance fell away. What remained was procedural, clinical, and monstrous.

*The subject must be young enough that the seed may root without resistance, yet old enough to hold speech.* 

*Seven cantors are required for the working. Each must contribute a portion of their own remembered truth to serve as anchor-lattice, for a seed planted in falsehood will bloom false.*

*A portion of their own remembered truth.* Rosalind read the sentence again. Something cold moved through her chest, unrelated to the vault's chill.

*Upon completion, the anchors' contributing memories will be severed and dispersed. This loss is permanent and necessary: a carrier must never be traceable to its makers.*

She turned the page, and there was the roll of names.

It was formatted as a rite-record, formal as a marriage contract, each cantor listed with their station and citadel. Six names meant nothing to her — titles and houses extinct since the Purges, men and women three hundred years dead.

The seventh name was hers.

*Rosalynde Sterlynge, Cantor-Initiate, Third Circle, Citadel Veyra.*

She stared at it. The lantern hissed. Her own heartbeat sounded loud and wrong, like someone else's pulse heard through a wall.

*Cantor-Initiate, Third Circle.* She had been an initiate once — everyone had, at twelve, before testing sorted them. But she'd tested into the Fourth Circle, the field-callers, the weather-workers. Never the Third. Never the inner disciplines. And the spelling — *Rosalynde Sterlynge* — that was the archaic orthography, the form used only in records of the Old Rites.

Three hundred years old, the record said. The ink was brown with age. The vellum was cracked at the fold.

And there, in the seventh line, in a dead scribe's careful hand, was her name.

"No," she said. Her voice came out flat and small in the round room. "No, that's not—" 

But it was. However impossible, however wrong, it was unmistakably, precisely her. Not similar. Hers. The way a mirror is yours.

She sat down on the cold floor without deciding to. The folio lay open in her lap. Above her, the mural figures stood in their ring around their kneeling sacrifice, and now she saw what she hadn't seen on entering: the kneeling figure's face had been deliberately effaced, scraped smooth by some later hand, but the posture — head bowed, hands open at the thighs, unresisting — was the posture of the seventh name. Of an initiate. Of someone who had knelt because they trusted the ring of robed figures around them.

Of someone who had been lied to well enough to kneel.

* * *

She did not know how long she sat there. The candle in the lantern burned down twice while she worked through the folio page by page, forcing herself to read like an archivist instead of a woman drowning.

The record continued past the rite. There were maintenance notes — the seed required periodic tending, "renewals" performed by the anchor-cantors at intervals of years, feeding it fresh anchored truth to keep the lattice whole. There were warnings: *the carrier will dream in the buried tongue; this is expected and harmless.* There were contingencies, chilling in their calm: *should the seed approach premature bloom, the anchors must convene to suppress it, by whatever means the situation admits.*

By whatever means the situation admits. She read that phrase five times and each time it meant something worse.

And near the end, in different ink, added later, a final entry in a hand that trembled:

*The dispersal failed. Anchor Seven retains fragmentary recall. Recommend immediate severance of Anchor Seven from all records and relocation under cover of disciplinary exile. She must not know what she is. If she learns, she becomes a thread, and threads can be pulled — by us, or by the ones who wait in the Hollow.*

Disciplinary exile.

Ten years ago they had dragged her from Citadel Veyra in chains of dampening-iron, before the assembled cantors, and named her crime: conspiracy with smugglers, misuse of the calling, betrayal of her oath. She remembered the trial — or she remembered pieces of it, and the pieces had never fit together right. There were gaps. She'd always assumed grief, shock, the mind protecting itself. Everyone had nodded knowingly when she asked. *Trauma does that, Rosalind. Be grateful you forget.*

Be grateful you forget.

The trembling hand had recommended exactly that. Had engineered exactly that. Her exile — the wound she had carried for a decade, the thing that had shaped every mile of every smuggler road, every winter alone, every night she woke reaching for storms she was forbidden to call — her exile was not punishment. It was concealment. She had not been cast out for what she did.

She had been buried for what she was.

Anchor Seven. A lock on a thing older than kingdoms, and no one had told her she was a lock.

Rosalind laughed — one short, cracked sound that the round room swallowed instantly — and then pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw stars. Because here was the vertigo, arriving exactly as promised: if they could plant an exile in her, what else had they planted? If her memories had gaps shaped like a trial, what else lived in those gaps? She had spent ten years trusting the one self that remained to her — the woman of the mountains, sharp and solitary and honest because honesty was all she had left. And that woman might be the forgery. The clean surface painted over the vault.

Wren's face rose behind her closed eyes. Frostbitten, silent, speaking a dead tongue that Rosalind somehow half-knew. *Half-knew.* Of course she half-knew. She was an anchor. The seed had been rooted partly in her own surrendered truth, and some residue of it had stayed in her like scent staying in a coat, waiting three hundred years and ten for the seed to pass close enough to answer.

The child hadn't knocked on just any door. The child had knocked on hers.

*The ones who wait in the Hollow.* She turned back to that line. The Court. The resurrected Court, if the smugglers' whispers were true — revenants moving in the high passes, asking questions in towns that had no answers. They wanted the seed. And the Order wanted the seed. And Rosalind was beginning to understand that neither of them wanted it for what it held. They wanted it for what it could do to her — to the lattice, to the anchors, to the woman whose name sat in a dead scribe's careful hand at the bottom of a list.

She closed the folio. Stood. Her knees ached from the stone; she'd been on the floor for hours.

Take it. The thought arrived simple and absolute. Take the record, take every scrap in this room, burn the rest. But her hands paused on the lectern's edge, because taking the folio meant carrying proof, and carrying proof meant showing it to someone, and showing it to someone meant—

Meant what? Maren Kestrel, the disgraced archivist in Ironhollow, the one client-friend who still wrote to her twice a year in a cipher of book titles? Meant Dain, who would look at her like she'd grown a second shadow?

Meant admitting the exile was real and also not real, which was a thing a person could only say aloud once, and after that they were either believed or pitied forever?

No. Not yet. First she needed to know what she was carrying. First she needed to stand in front of a mirror and find out how much of the reflection was hers.

Rosalind copied the roll of names onto the flyleaf of her father's storm-primer, pressing hard enough to score the paper. She took two loose pages concerning the renewal rites, folded them small against her breast. Then, on an impulse she didn't examine, she broke off a fleck of the gold leaf from the mural's seed-shape and sealed it in wax.

At the chamber door she stopped, and looked back at the scraped-smooth face of the kneeling figure.

"They told me I betrayed my oath," she said quietly. "They never told me I was the oath."

The gate sealed behind her, eager and glad, and the dark took the room back.

* * *

She climbed out of the earth into afternoon light so bright it hurt, snow-blind white, and stood blinking at the mouth of the vault with her heart hammering and her mind refusing to settle on any single thought for longer than a breath. The descent had taken half a day. The ascent took twenty minutes, because terror is lighter than dread and climbs faster.

Halfway down the mountain, moving fast along the ridge trail with the vault already shrinking behind her, she saw it.

Smoke. Thin and black and deliberate, rising from the notch of the north pass — the pass Dain Ferro's caravans used, the pass that led, eventually, to her cabin.

Waystation smoke. Someone was burning the smuggler roads, methodically, the way an army burns a country it intends to walk through.

And beneath the smoke, faint but unmistakable, riding the wind down the valley to meet her, Rosalind felt a pressure she hadn't felt in ten years: the static hum of storm-calling that was not her own. Ordered. Disciplined. The Order's cadence, or something wearing it.

Someone was asking the mountains about a small passenger. And the mountains, Rosalind realized with a lurch, had only one person left to ask.
