## 5. Hollow Bells

The dead do not sleep, but they wait, and Sereth had learned the difference across two hundred years of waiting.

He crouched at the edge of the tree line above the Saddle Rocks, snow gathering on his shoulders in a crust that never melted, because nothing about him melted or warmed or changed. Below him, the pass wound down toward a waystation with a smoking chimney — one of Dain Ferro's, if the Court's maps still held true, and the Court's maps always held true; they were drawn by men who no longer knew how to lie. Smoke meant warmth. Warmth meant travelers. Travelers meant news, and news was a kind of food he could almost taste.

Behind him, in the hollow of a fallen pine, his companion waited as she always waited: silent, folded up like an unposted letter.

"Patience," Sereth said, though she needed no instruction. The word was for himself. It was always for himself.

He had been alive once — that was the Court's courtesy to its agents, the half-life they called *returning* — and in that life he had been a courier on these same mountains, carrying sealed dispatches between fortresses whose names had been scraped from every stone. He remembered the names. That was his curse within his blessing: the Hollow Court raised its servants but did not always finish emptying them, and so Sereth walked the world with gaps in him shaped like a wife, a son, a small stone house in a valley that was now a fen. He had gone looking for the edges of those gaps for a century. He had stopped looking when he understood that the Court had taken them deliberately, that a servant who remembered what love weighed would not carry what the Court asked him to carry.

And now they had asked him to carry this: find the seed before the storm-callers found it, follow it, and above all — *above all* — do not touch the child until the Gate was ready to receive her.

The bells told him why.

They rang at dusk and dawn, wherever the Court held ground, and only he and his kind heard them: a sound like bronze struck underwater, rolling out of the ruined chapels of the dead lands and up into the living mountains where no chapel stood anymore. Three centuries since the Vessic had been broken, their sanctuaries burned, their tongue forbidden under pain of the hollowing — and still the bells rang, because the Court had never accepted that it had lost. It had only accepted that it must wait.

Sereth rose, brushed snow from his knees out of habit rather than need, and descended toward the waystation.

* * *

The keeper was a woman past sixty with a smuggler's squint and a soldier's old scar, and she fed him stew because refusing food to a man who looked like Sereth required a courage she did not have. He wore flesh well enough to pass. Cold skin, gray-veined hands, eyes the color of a frozen pond — but passing was all his kind had ever managed among the living, and most days it was enough.

"Roads are bad," the keeper said, ladling him a second helping unasked, the way one feeds a dog one hopes will leave. "Badger's Rest burned three nights back. Ashford's Crossing too. Soldiers, not bandits — white cloaks, silver thread. Asking after passengers."

"What manner of passenger?"

"A child." She said it carefully, watching him. "Small girl. Alone on the roads, which is itself a wrongness. They say she doesn't speak any tongue a man can name."

Sereth tasted the stew. Salt, root, a ghost of smoked goat. Once, flavors had mattered to him. He missed mattering.

"And you?" he said. "Have you seen such a child?"

"No one comes through here without me seeing them." She wiped the ladle on her apron, slow, deliberate. "But I'll tell you what I told the white-cloaks. Whoever's hunting that girl — there's more than one hunt on, and I don't aim to be caught between them."

"More than one," Sereth echoed pleasantly.

"The soldiers ask one way. Then there was another, before them. A woman. Tall. Voice like a hymn sung through a keyhole. She didn't burn anything." The keeper's eyes came up and fixed on him, and for a moment Sereth saw the old soldier underneath the innkeeper, the one who had held a line somewhere long ago and never fully come home. "She just asked. And when she left, my dogs wouldn't stop howling till noon."

Ilvane. Or one of hers. The Order had scented it too, then. Sereth set down his spoon with care, as though the small courtesy of it might keep the evening civil.

"You've been helpful," he said.

"I'd like to keep being unhelpful to everyone equally." She took the bowl from him. "That's my price. Whatever you people want from each other, take it over the pass, not in my house."

"Wisely bought," Sereth said, and left coin on the table — real coin, minted in a kingdom that had forgotten why it minted it — and stepped out into the blue dark.

The bells were ringing. He let them fill him for a moment, that drowned-bronze tolling, and beneath it he felt the other thing, the thing the Court had put in him along with the returning: a thin, taut line of hunger running south-southwest, through stone and snow and sleeping roots, toward something warm and small and terrified.

The seed. He could feel it the way a blind man feels a hearth across a room.

It had woken further since the last bell. Two weeks ago it had been an ember behind his sternum, a heat he had to strain to read. Now it was a lamp behind a curtain — and lamps moved, and this one was moving north and east, up out of the lowlands, following the only roads a hunted thing could follow: the smuggler roads. Ferro's roads.

Which led, in the end, where all roads in Thornfell led if you climbed high enough and refused to turn around.

The Hollow Gate.

Sereth smiled, and was glad no one lived close enough to see it.

* * *

His companion's name had been Yeva, once, before the hollowing took even the memory of the name. The Court called her Six-of-the-Ninth-Choir now, and she traveled wrapped in gray wool with her face shadowed, and she did not speak because speech had been the first thing they took — they always took speech first from the choir-agents, so that nothing they learned could be sung away from them.

She sat up when he entered the hollow beneath the fallen pine, and her eyes caught what little light the snow reflected and gave none back.

"She passed through Badger's Rest," Sereth said. "Or will. The trail is four days cold, but cold trails warm again when you know where they're going."

Yeva tilted her head. In the dark her stillness was total, more total than any living person's could be, and Sereth found it restful the way other men found music restful.

"The Order knows too," he went on. "High Cantor Ilvane herself, if the keeper's description holds. Which means the seed's protectors will soon have two hunts behind them and a mountain ahead." He settled against the pine's root-mass, folding his hands. "Do you remember the Gate, Six? Before?"

Her head turned a fraction. Of course she did not remember before. None of the Ninth Choir remembered before; they had been made from children of the purges, emptied and refilled with service. He sometimes spoke to her of before precisely because she could not be wounded by it. It was a cruelty he permitted himself, the way starving men permit themselves to smell bread.

"There was a door there," Sereth said, "in the years when the Gate still had priests to tend it. A door of black basalt with no hinge and no handle, and the Vessic claimed it opened only for consent — that no force in the world could push it, that it swung inward only when someone on both sides wished it open." He laughed softly, a sound like gravel settling. "The kings thought that was superstition. They brought rams. They brought fire. They brought the Order's storm-callers, in the end, and the storms did what armies couldn't — cracked the mountain itself — and when the dust cleared the Gate stood open, and everyone who had wanted it open was already inside, and everyone who hadn't was ash. Consent," he said. "The Vessic were right, and being right saved no one."

Yeva reached out and touched his sleeve. Not comfort. Signal. Her finger tapped twice — the choir's sign for *movement* — and pointed southeast, toward the Saddle Rocks.

Sereth listened. Under the wind, under the far-off bells, he felt it again: the lamp behind the curtain, flickering. Moving. Faster than it should move, for a child on foot in winter.

And with it, faintly, something new. A second warmth, trailing the first like a hound on a leash — larger, older, ragged-edged. A storm-caller's signature, dimmed and muffled, wrapped in ten years of deliberate silence.

Rosalind Sterling.

Sereth closed his eyes and let the Court's knowledge unroll behind them, the way it did when he was still: the exile of the storm-caller Rosalind Sterling, ten years past, for a betrayal recorded in the citadel archives in seven careful hands. The details were sealed even from the Court's deep readers, which was itself interesting — the Order sealed only the things that shamed it, and shame, in Sereth's long experience, was the strongest mortar in any fortress.

A traitor guiding the seed-bearer over her own mountains. Or a woman who did not know what she was carrying, being carried by it in turn.

Either way, the Court had waited three hundred years, and patience was the one virtue the dead perfected.

"We don't touch them," Sereth said aloud, rising. "Not yet. We watch, and we learn what she intends, and we let the Order spend itself against the mountain. And when they reach the Gate — *if* they reach the Gate — we will be standing in the doorway, and we will ask nicely."

Yeva rose beside him, unfolding like smoke given bones.

Above the Saddle Rocks, the dusk bell tolled its last, and far to the southeast, faint as a pulse, the seed's light flared once — startled, perhaps, or dreaming — and Sereth turned toward it and began to walk.

* * *

They followed for six days without closing the distance, because distance was not the point. Understanding was.

From ridgelines and burned waystations, from the mouths of frightened carriers and one smuggler whom Yeva questioned in ways Sereth chose not to witness, the shape of it assembled itself: a girl called Wren, mute in every tongue but one; the exiled storm-caller who had taken her in; the smuggler captain Ferro, moving supplies south and lies north; and a fourth figure, newer, older — a woman calling herself Maren Kestrel, disgraced archivist, met in Ironhollow, whose name Sereth pulled from records three centuries deep and found attached to documents no archivist should have touched.

Kestrel's name appeared in the Gate archives. In the pre-war archives.

Sereth sat with that fact for a full night, letting it turn over like a river stone. People did not appear in three-hundred-year-old documents unless the documents were wrong, or the woman was impossible, or the Court's own records — kept by the dead, who had little talent for forgery — were lying. He wrote the name in his ledger, underlined it twice, and felt, for the first time in decades, the itch of genuine curiosity. The Court discouraged curiosity. Curiosity was a door, and doors, in the Court's theology, opened from both sides.

On the seventh day, the trail led them to the Saddle Rocks, and the Saddle Rocks taught Sereth everything he needed to know.

He arrived after it was over — arrived deliberately after, per his instructions, for the Court did not send its best hand to die in another's ambush — and stood amid the aftermath while Yeva circled wide, reading the ground. Scorch-fans radiated outward from a single point where the storm-caller had stood: perfect, concentric, disciplined beyond anything the current Order produced. Ten years of exile had not rusted her. If anything, the silence had concentrated her, the way water forced through a narrow gap cuts deeper than a broad river.

Three wraiths of the Court's forward choir had come apart under that storm. Their residue still hung in the air, threads of gray dissolving, and Sereth knelt and gathered a thread of it onto his gloved fingertip and read it the way his kind read such things: the ambush had been ordered from the Gate itself. Not by the Court's war-council. From *below* the Gate. From whatever slept in the foundations that even the resurrected Court did not enter willingly.

Something down there wanted the seed. Something older than the Court's return.

And the storm-caller had killed its messengers without knowing whom she served by killing them.

"Ah," Sereth said quietly, to the scorch marks, to the mountain, to the bells ringing somewhere below the world. "You're taking them straight to it. All of you. Every hunt, every hope, every lie — converging on one door."

Yeva returned and signed her report: tracks heading northeast, five travelers now, one wounded lightly, pace good, morale better than expected. Then, hesitating in a way she almost never hesitated, she signed one more sign — an old one, from the choir's formal liturgy, rarely used in the field.

*Why does the Court want consent?*

Sereth looked at her for a long moment. It was the question he was not permitted to ask, which meant it was the question worth asking, which meant asking it was the closest thing to freedom his condition allowed.

"Because the Gate opens from both sides," he said at last. "And I think whatever the Court is — whatever it truly is, under the bells and the banners and the beautiful grievance — I think it has forgotten how to want things honestly. You cannot force a truth into the world. It has to be *carried*. Willingly. By someone with something to lose." He began walking, northeast, following the warmth of the seed through the falling snow. "The kings never understood that. The Order doesn't either. Perhaps that's why they lost."

Yeva fell into step beside him, and the snow thickened, and ahead of them — days away yet, but closing — the lamp behind the curtain burned steadily toward the Gate, dragging behind it a storm-caller who did not know what she was, an archivist who should not exist, a smuggler in over his depth, and a child full of a nation's worst night.

Sereth walked, and did not hurry, and the bells followed him like a tide.

There was one more thing he carried, the thing he had not written in any ledger: on the night the seed had first flared, visible across half a mountain range, every revenant within a hundred miles had heard — beneath the Court's bells, beneath everything — a single clear note that belonged to neither side. A note like a door remembering it was a door.

And Sereth, keeper of his own hollow places, had known instantly what it meant.

The Gate was waking too.

Whatever happened at that threshold — whichever army arrived first, whichever lie triumphed — the door itself had begun, very slowly, very patiently, after three hundred years of standing shut, to think about opening.

And Sereth intended to be standing there when it did.
