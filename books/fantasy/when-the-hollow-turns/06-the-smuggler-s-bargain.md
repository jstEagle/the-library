## 6. The Smuggler's Bargain

Dain Ferro arrived at dusk, the way he always did when trouble was on his heels — too late to be polite and too early to be safe. Rosalind heard his mules before she saw him, their shoes striking stone in that uneven rhythm of a caravan climbing the last switchback below her cabin. She had known him for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once come up the mountain unannounced.

She put a knife in her belt and opened the door with the firelight at her back.

He stood in the blue snow-light, frost in his grey beard, and behind him the road fell away into a whiteout so thick it looked like the sky had come down to smother the pass. He was alone. That was wrong too. Dain Ferro traveled with six men minimum and a crossbow he named after his first wife.

"Inside," Rosalind said. "Before the mountain decides it wants you."

He came in slowly, stamping the snow from his boots like a man who wanted very much not to be where he was. His eyes went past her shoulder, to the corner by the hearth where Wren sat wrapped in Rosalind's second-best blanket, watching him with those dark, unblinking eyes. The girl had learned, in the two weeks since the whiteout brought her to the door, to say nothing in front of strangers. She had also learned to watch everything.

"She's still here," Dain said quietly.

"She's still here."

"You've taught her to hold a spoon. I can see it from here." He pulled off his gloves finger by finger. "Rosalind. We need to talk."

"We're talking."

"Not in front of the girl."

Wren's chin lifted. It was a small motion, but Rosalind caught it — the same stubborn tilt she'd seen in the mirror every morning for ten years. The girl said something then, soft, in that dead language that lived in her mouth like a bird that had outlived its flock. Rosalind didn't need the forbidden lexicon anymore to catch the shape of it. *I understand more than you think.*

"I'll say what I have to say in front of her," Rosalind said. "Whatever it is, she's part of it. She's been part of it since the night someone burned her people and sent her up my mountain."

Something flickered across Dain's face — not surprise. Confirmation. He lowered himself into the chair across from the hearth, groaning like an old man, though he wasn't one yet, and held his hands toward the fire.

"The north passes are closed," he said. "Not snowed shut. Closed. Every waystation from Grey Saddle to the Cold Larder is ash. Somebody's burning them in order, working south, and asking questions at every homestead between. Questions about a small passenger. A child traveling alone or near-alone, headed south through Thornfell." He looked up. "You know how I know? Because they asked me. Three days ago, at the Waymeet. Six soldiers in no livery I recognized, paying in old coin, and a captain with eyes like a fish. They had a drawing of her, Rosalind. A good one. Down to the scar on her left hand."

Wren's hand curled closed over that scar, as if she could hide it now, after all these years of wearing it.

"They showed you a drawing," Rosalind said. Her voice came out flat and cold, the voice she used on smugglers who tried to renegotiate mid-pass. "And you told them—"

"I told them I don't carry children. I told them small passengers freeze in the high country and I bury what I find in spring. I told them nothing." He met her eyes. "But they didn't believe me, Roz. And they let me go anyway. You understand what that means? Men like that don't let you go because they believe you. They let you go because following you is cheaper than stopping you."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind found the gap under the eaves and moaned through it, low and rising, the sound the mountain made when it was thinking about storms. Rosalind felt it in her teeth the way she always did — the old pull, the pressure behind the breastbone, the sky leaning on her shoulders asking to be answered. Ten years she had refused to answer it.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

"If they're working the stations in order? A week. Maybe less. Maybe they skip ahead because they already know exactly where she is and everything until now has been theater." He rubbed his face. "I don't know. That's the honest answer, and I hate it."

"So we run."

"So we run," he agreed. "South. Through the Undercut, down the Vessel Road, out at Ironhollow before anyone's the wiser. It's the safest route I know, and I know them all. No tolls, no garrisons, no eyes. I can move you both inside three days if the weather holds."

"And if the weather doesn't hold?"

Dain was quiet for a moment. He turned his hands over, studying the palms as though the answer might be written there.

"That's the other thing," he said. "That's why I came up the mountain instead of sending word. Because there's a price on this run, Roz, and it isn't coin."

* * *

She should have seen it coming. Later, sitting awake while Wren slept, she would turn the conversation over and over and wonder how she hadn't. But in the moment, with the fire warm and the storm gathering its breath outside, she only felt the floor tilt beneath her, slow and inevitable, like a slope giving way.

"Name it," she said.

"My caravan goes south through the Undercut in four days' time. Thirty head of mule, nine men, silk and iron and salt — the richest load I've moved in two years. And the Undercut in winter is a knife fight with the sky. Last year I lost two men and half a cargo to a squall that came off the ridge with no warning at all. The year before, worse." He leaned forward. "You're a storm-caller, Rosalind. I've known it since the third year I ran this road, when you cleared my path through the Greymaw with a word and thought I didn't notice. I've never asked you for anything. Never sold it, never whispered it, never once put your name and that word in the same sentence. Eleven years of silence. That's what I'm spending now."

"Dain—"

"Shield my caravan," he said. "Openly. Where my men can see it. Call the weather aside, hold the passes clear, walk us through the worst country on this mountain under a sky that behaves. Not a favor done in secret. Not a trick hidden in fog. They see you do it, they know what you are, and they trust the road because of it." He spread his hands. "That's the price. That's all of it."

"All of it," Rosalind repeated. "You want me to stand in front of nine armed men and call a storm. You want me to break ten years of hiding in a single afternoon, in front of witnesses who drink and gamble and talk in their sleep."

"I want my men to live through the Undercut," Dain said. "And yes. I want them to see it, because a shield nobody knows about protects nobody. If the sky turns on us in the deep cut, they need to know to get behind you, to trust you, to move when you say move. A secret storm-caller is a story. A visible one is armor." He paused. "And I won't pretend I don't know what else it buys. Word spreads. Word travels faster than any soldier. If the Order hears Rosalind Sterling called lightning over the Thornfell road—" he shrugged, heavy, unhappy, "—then maybe they stop hunting a ghost and start negotiating with a name. Or maybe it paints a mark on you a mile wide. I'd be lying if I said I haven't turned that over. I'd be lying if I said I don't want it either way."

"Why?" Rosalind stood. The chair scraped back. "Eleven years, Dain. You watched me build this life out of silence. You knew what it cost me. Why ask for this now?"

"Because the world stopped letting you hide!" For the first time his voice cracked upward, and Wren stirred in her blanket at the sound. Dain dropped it low again, urgent. "They're burning waystations, Roz. Burning them in *order*, like a man reading a ledger. Someone with resources and patience is erasing the north country looking for that girl, and when they reach this cabin — and they will reach this cabin — do you imagine your silence saves you? Your secrecy is a door with no lock. It only worked because nobody kicked it. Well, somebody's kicking now. So you can stay up here and be found, or you can come down my road and be *seen*, and I know which one frightens you more, but only one of them keeps you breathing."

From the corner, small and clear, Wren spoke.

"*Sella veth.*"

Both of them turned. The girl sat up in her blanket, dark eyes moving between them, and said it again, firmer, and then — haltingly, in the broken common tongue Rosalind had been teaching her — "*Go. I go. You go.*" She pressed her small scarred hand flat against her own chest, then pointed south, down the mountain, into the dark. Then she pointed at Rosalind, and at Dain, and made a shape in the air with both hands: a roof, a shelter, wide enough for all of them.

Then she looked at Rosalind, and did the thing that undid her. She touched her own chest again, and waited. *What about me?*

Rosalind crossed the room and knelt by the blanket. Up close the girl smelled of woodsmoke and the particular clean cold of high snow. "No one is leaving anyone," she said. "Do you hear me? Whatever we decide, we decide it together."

Wren studied her face with that terrible gravity children sometimes have, weighing words against the truth behind them. Then she nodded once, satisfied, and lay back down — but her hand stayed open on the blanket, palm up, an inch from Rosalind's knee. An anchor. Rosalind covered it with her own.

When she looked up, Dain was watching them both with an expression she'd rarely seen on him: something raw, quickly buried.

"Four days," he said. "Caravan musters at the Greymaw fork. I'll leave you my spare mule and a pack frame tonight. Think on it. But think fast, Roz, because the men burning the north aren't going to wait for you to feel ready."

* * *

He slept in the byre with the mule and left before dawn, and Rosalind spent the day doing the thing she hated most: waiting, and thinking, and climbing.

She climbed above the tree line in the late morning, up the scree to the shelf of granite she'd claimed years ago as the one place on the mountain where she allowed herself to look at the sky honestly. Below her the cabin sat small and smoke-stained in its fold of the valley. South, the peaks marched away in ranks — the Greymaw, the Saddle Rocks, the black seam of the Undercut barely visible as a shadow between shoulders of stone. Somewhere down there, soldiers were burning their way toward her, patient as rot.

She raised her hand.

It was a small thing. A test. She reached into the wind the way she'd been trained to, the way she hadn't permitted herself in a decade — not gripping it, just touching, the way you touch a sleeping animal you once loved and fear in equal measure.

The wind noticed.

Of course it noticed. It had always noticed. It gathered around her like a dog hearing its name after years of neglect, pressing close, eager, *hungry*, and Rosalind snatched her hand back with her heart slamming. Above her, a cloud that had been drifting east all morning hesitated, bulged, and began — slowly, unmistakably — to turn toward her.

"No," she said aloud. "No. Not yet."

She walked back down fast, and the cloud followed her for a mile before it gave up and went back about its business, and she stood in her doorway shaking, understanding suddenly the full shape of what Dain was asking. Not just exposure. Exposure was the least of it. He was asking her to open a door she had nailed shut from the inside, and she no longer trusted herself to control what came through it. The exile, the empty decade, the careful small life — all of it built on the belief that what she carried was too dangerous to loose. And the sky, just now, had agreed with her.

But Wren came out to stand beside her in the cold, and looked up at the clouds with no fear at all, and said, in Vessic, a word Rosalind had found in the lexicon and never forgotten: *storm*. The girl said it the way other children said *home*.

* * *

On the third night, Rosalind packed.

She was methodical about it, the way she was methodical about everything — the lexicon wrapped in oilcloth and sewn into her coat lining, the few true valuables, the food that kept, the blankets. She left the furniture. She left the garden under its snow. She stood in the middle of the room that had been her whole world for ten years and found that it weighed almost nothing, and that frightened her more than the soldiers did.

Wren watched from the doorway, dressed in layers, the good boots Rosalind had bought her in Ironhollow two winters ago laced tight. The girl had grown in these months, or seemed to; she stood straighter, watched more, spoke a little more each week — Vessic mostly, common in fragments, and once, memorably, a smuggler's curse she had absolutely learned from Dain's men.

"We're going south," Rosalind told her. "With the caravan. There may be storms. Real ones." She crouched to the girl's level. "If I tell you to get down, you get down. If I tell you to run, you run to Dain and you don't look back. Can you do that?"

Wren considered this with great seriousness. Then she reached out and took Rosalind's hand, and turned it over, and traced one deliberate finger across the old burn scar on her palm — the mark every storm-caller carries, the brand of the citadel, cut into her at seven years old in a ceremony she remembered only in flashes. The girl looked up.

"*Vessa tor,*" she said softly. And then, in her careful, broken common: "Storm-mother. Strong."

Rosalind laughed — a short, startled sound, the first real laugh in weeks. "Storm-mother," she agreed. "Let's hope so."

They went down the mountain in the dark, by the smuggler paths only she knew, and reached the Greymaw fork an hour before dawn on the fourth day. The caravan was already there: thirty mules breathing steam in the freezing air, nine men checking harnesses by lantern light, and Dain Ferro standing at the head of it all like a man trying to look calm and failing at it generously.

His eyes found her across the frozen stream. Then they went to Wren, bundled on her back, watching everything. Then back to Rosalind.

"Well?" he said.

Rosalind looked at the sky. It was a hard grey ceiling, low and heavy, the kind of sky that sat on the mountains all winter and ground them down grain by grain. Beyond the fork, the road climbed toward the Saddle Rocks and the mouth of the Undercut, and somewhere past that, invisible, the weather was already turning — she could feel it in her teeth, in the scar on her palm, in the pressure building behind the world like water behind a dam. A bad sky. A killing sky, for a caravan on exposed switchbacks.

Ten years of silence. One afternoon of noise.

She stepped forward into the open ground before all nine of Dain's men, pulled off her gloves, and raised her right hand to the west, where the worst of it was gathering. She felt every eye on her. She felt the wind turn its great attention toward her like a beast hearing its name.

"Everyone stop working," she said, and her voice carried strangely, the way it always did when the sky listened through it. "In a few hours, this valley is going to try to kill us. I'm going to teach it better manners."

The men stared. One of them — the young one, Pell, who'd been with Dain three years — actually laughed, until he saw his captain's face.

Rosalind closed her eyes, reached into the wind, and this time did not let go.

The storm came south off the ridge to meet her, eager as a hound, and she caught it by the throat and *held* it, feeling its weight settle onto her shoulders like a yoke she'd never truly taken off, only set down. Around her the air changed pressure; the mules went quiet; even the stream seemed to hush.

When she opened her eyes, the clouds overhead had parted into a clean corridor of darkening blue, running south along the road like a promise.

Behind her, far to the north, down the pass they had just abandoned, a thin column of smoke rose against the dawn — straight and deliberate, too orderly to be an accident.

Someone had reached her cabin.

And they had burned it.
