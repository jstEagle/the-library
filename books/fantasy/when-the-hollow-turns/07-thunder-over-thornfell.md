## 7. Thunder Over Thornfell

The Saddle Rocks announced themselves an hour before we reached them, the way bad places do.

Rosalind felt it first in her teeth—a low pressure that had nothing to do with weather and everything to do with the land remembering violence. The pass narrowed there between two shoulders of granite, a saddle of broken stone where centuries ago the Vessic kings had once watered their herds and, later still, someone had done something worse. She'd crossed it four hundred times in ten years of guiding smugglers. She knew every handhold, every blind approach, every place where a body could hide and wait.

Which was exactly why she hated how quiet it was.

"Stay close," she murmured to Wren, who walked at her left with the particular stubborn silence she'd maintained since Ironhollow. The girl hadn't spoken since the nightmare three nights past—hadn't screamed again, hadn't sleep-talked—but Rosalind could feel the seed inside her like heat off a banked fire. It changed the air around Wren. Animals noticed it. Rosalind noticed it most of all: a faint wrongness, a sweetness, like flowers growing over a grave.

Behind them, Dain Ferro's mules picked their way over scree, bells silenced with waxed wool, cargo wrapped against the cold. He caught her glance and gave her the smallest nod. *I see it too,* the nod said. *I don't like it either.*

They were halfway through the saddle when the light died.

Not cloud-dark—she knew cloud-dark the way she knew her own hands. This was darker, a shadow moving against the sun, and it came from below rather than above, rising out of the cracks in the rock like smoke poured from a tipped jar.

"Wraiths," she said, already turning. "Dain—the middle of the saddle. Get to the high ground on my mark."

He didn't argue. That was the thing about Dain Ferro; ten years of buying her silence and her maps had taught him when orders mattered. His whistle cut sharp through the pass, and his people moved—six of them, hauling the mules toward the shelf of stone on the northern side while the first of the wraiths uncoiled from the ground thirty paces ahead.

She'd seen Court wraiths twice before, in another life, before the exile. They weren't ghosts, whatever smuggler gossip said. Ghosts were sad things. These were *hungry* things—shaped like people who had been stretched too long and folded wrong, their outlines bleeding shadow, faces like wet clay someone had pressed thumbprints into. The Hollow Court made them from what it hollowed out, and it sent them where patience alone couldn't reach.

Three of them now, then five, rising between the boulders with the terrible unhurried grace of things that had never once needed to hurry.

Wren made a sound—not a word, not a scream, just a small cracked note—and something in Rosalind's chest went tight and cold and very, very clear.

*Get behind me.*

She stepped forward onto open stone, spread her arms, and did the thing she had sworn on her exile's name never to do again.

She called a storm.

---

It had been ten years. Ten years of walking past thunderheads with her jaw set, ten years of dampening the static in her own skin by force of habit, ten years of being nothing but a woman with good boots and better maps. She had expected rust. She had expected the power to come grudgingly, like an old joint aching back into motion, requiring everything she had just to summon a squall enough to scatter shadows.

Instead, the sky *answered*.

Not responded. Not stirred. *Answered*—the way a hound answers its name, the way a door answers a key, instantly and completely and with what she could only describe as joy. The clouds overhead, gray and sullen all morning, went black in the space between one heartbeat and the next. Pressure slammed down through the pass so hard the mules screamed. And beneath it all, threading up through the granite under her feet, came a current of raw charge that rose into her body like water finding its level, filling her to the brim and past it, asking nothing, demanding only that she *use* it.

"Oh," she heard herself say, and the word came out lit.

The first bolt took the lead wraith mid-lunge. There was no aiming to speak of; she simply thought *away from us*, and the storm obliged, a white-hot column of light that turned the creature to drifting ash before its shadow finished reaching for her throat. The thunder arrived a half-second later, so close it wasn't sound anymore but impact, a fist through her ribs, through the stone, through everything.

The exhilaration hit harder than the thunder.

Gods help her, it was *beautiful*. Every nerve alight, every fear burned away in the sheer electric enormity of it—she stood in the mouth of a storm that loved her, that had been waiting ten years for her to call, and it poured itself into her palms without reservation, without limit, without *asking whether she deserved it*. She flung lightning at the second wraith and felt the storm laugh through her, if storms could laugh, if laughter could be made of falling pressure and ozone and the deep tectonic satisfaction of finally, *finally* letting go.

The third wraith broke and ran—or did whatever wraiths did instead of running, folding itself down into a crack in the earth. The fourth and fifth circled wide along the southern rim, and she caught them both with a forked strike that split a boulder the size of a house and rang the whole pass like a bell.

Silence after. Real silence, ringing, absolute.

The storm hung above her, coiled and quivering, *waiting*. Not dispersing. Waiting for the next command, the next target, hungry to be used again. She could feel it pressing against the inside of her skull like a dog leaning on a door, and underneath the joy of it, underneath the blazing animal delight of being whole and powerful and *herself* for the first time in a decade—

—underneath all of that was the question she'd spent ten years refusing to ask:

*Why does it come this easily?*

Storm-calling was supposed to cost. Her instructors at the citadel had built their whole doctrine around the price: years of discipline to shape the summons, exhaustion afterward, nosebleeds, tremors, the slow erosion of a caller's nerves. You paid for every bolt. That was the covenant, the balance, the reason the Order kept its callers chaste of ambition—you couldn't spend the sky cheaply and stay human.

This storm had cost her nothing. Less than nothing. It had come like it had been *stored* inside her, waiting, patient as a seed.

A seed.

The word arrived uninvited and settled into her mind with a weight that made her stagger. Maren Kestrel's voice, three nights ago over a guttering lamp: *The ritual doesn't only hide memories, Rosalind. It moves them. It can put a memory somewhere it will grow. And sometimes, when the vessel is right, it puts other things in besides.*

Her own name, listed among the priests of the vault records. Her own blank decade-lost night. And now a sky that answered her like a lover, like a weapon that recognized its wielder.

"What did they put in me?" she whispered.

The storm, hearing even that, surged hopefully against her control.

She let go.

That was harder than calling it—letting go meant trusting the release, trusting herself not to clutch, and for one long vertiginous moment she held the whole charged dark of it cupped in her will like water in her palms before she opened her fingers and let it drain away into the stone. The clouds shredded. The pressure lifted. The pass went ordinary again, gray and cold and smelling of ozone and burnt shadow-ash.

---

She was shaking when she turned around. Not from the calling—from the stopping.

Wren stood three paces away, staring at her with those enormous dark eyes, and Rosalind realized with a lurch that the girl had watched the whole thing. Had watched her step into lightning like stepping into a bath. Something passed across the child's face—recognition, maybe, or grief, or both—and she raised one small frost-chapped hand and pointed, not at Rosalind but at the sky above her head, and said a single soft word in Vessic.

Rosalind didn't know the word. But she half-understood it anyway, the way she half-understood everything the girl said, and what she understood made her stomach drop: it was a title. An old one. *Storm-mother.* Or perhaps *storm-binder.* The distinction, in Vessic, was apparently a matter of some importance.

"Don't," Rosalind said hoarsely. "Don't call me anything."

Dain appeared at her elbow, ash dusted across his shoulders, his face the color of old tallow. Behind him his crew was remounting, moving fast and silent, professionals eager to be anywhere else.

"That was—" he began, and stopped, and started again. "Ten years you told me you couldn't do that anymore."

"I believed it."

"You *believed* it." He laughed, but there was no humor in it, only the thin edge men get when the world rearranges itself faster than they can catalogue it. "Rosalind. That wasn't a squall. I've seen citadel callers work caravans through the Dust Season. That was a war-storm. That was the kind of thing they tell stories about." He lowered his voice. "And it didn't cost you a drop. I watched your face. You enjoyed it."

The worst part was that he was right.

"I need you to listen to me," she said. "Whatever I was before the exile—whatever they took out of me or put into me—I'm starting to think I was never exiled for what they said I did. I think I was exiled so I'd never find out what I am."

"And what are you?"

She looked at her hands. Steady now, though they hadn't been a minute ago. Capable. Empty of answers.

"I don't know," she said. "But the storm knew me, Dain. It's been waiting for me. Things that wait that patiently aren't natural, and neither am I."

Above them, far to the south, the clouds were gathering again—thin, distant, organized in a way clouds shouldn't organize, ranked along the horizon like soldiers. Watching. The Hollow Court had seen what happened here. Whatever had sent the wraiths would send something else next, something patient and vast, and it would know now not to send anything she could burn.

Wren tugged at her sleeve and pointed south at those ranked clouds, then north, deeper into Thornfell, toward the high passes and the frozen shrine country beyond. Then the girl looked up at her with terrible, ancient certainty and spoke two words of dead Vessic, and this time Rosalind understood them completely, without trying, as though the understanding had been sitting in her all along waiting for lightning to shake it loose:

*"They're coming. Faster now."*

Rosalind pulled her hood up against the returning wind and gave the only order that mattered.

"Dain. New route. We go north tonight."
