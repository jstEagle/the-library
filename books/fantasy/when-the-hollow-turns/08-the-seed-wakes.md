## 8. The Seed Wakes

Warm.

The blanket is wool and it smells of smoke and of the woman — Rosalind, Ros-ah-lind, the name like a stone dropped down a well, three syllables falling and falling — and Wren curls into it because outside the window the snow is a white animal eating the world. The fire ticks. The cabin breathes. Somewhere below, floorboards speak in their old wooden language: *settle, settle, be still.*

She is almost asleep when the taste comes.

Iron. Not blood-in-the-mouth iron, not the copper penny taste of a bitten tongue, but older. Deeper. Iron that has been sitting in water for three hundred years, rusting quietly in the dark. It blooms on her tongue like a fruit she never ate.

*No,* she thinks, in Vessic, the word small inside her. *Not now. Not here.*

The seed does not listen. The seed has never listened.

It opens the way frost opens on glass — slowly at first, one bright finger of cold reaching across a pane, then all at once, everywhere, a whole winter arriving between two heartbeats.

* * *

Fire.

Not the good fire in the hearth with its patient orange breathing. This fire is green where it burns the temples and gold where it burns the books and it makes no heat at all, which is the worst thing, which her hands will not stop telling her: *there should be warmth, there is always warmth in fire, why is there no warmth—*

She is standing in a courtyard and she is also lying under wool and she is also very far away watching herself stand there, and all of these are true at once. That is how the seed works. It does not show her things. It puts her *inside* them.

The courtyard is white stone gone black with soot. Banners hang from the colonnade, heavy with rain that fell an hour or a century ago — banners she knows without knowing how she knows them, the nine-pointed star of the Order split by a sword, the crowned stag of the old kings, and beneath them both, sewn smaller, as if ashamed to be there, the silver leaf of Vessic.

Her people's leaf. Her people's —

A bell begins to ring.

She knows this bell. She has known it since before she knew her own name, which is a strange thing to know about knowing, but knowing has stopped making sense tonight. The bell is bronze as tall as three men and it hangs in the Tower of Witnessing and it only rings for one reason. It rang once before, the seed tells her, when the first sanctuary opened its doors. Tonight it rings again.

Tonight it rings for the last time.

*Run*, says some small surviving piece of Wren, the piece that is eight years old and likes honey and is afraid of deep water. *Run run run.* But her feet in the memory are bare on frozen stone and they do not run, because the woman whose eyes she is looking through — the priestess, Anthe, Anthe who smells of cedar oil and ink — is walking toward the bell tower, and Anthe's feet have decided something Wren's feet do not understand yet.

Soldiers in the courtyard. So many soldiers. Their armor is wrong — storm-grey, unmarked, no crest — and they move like men who have been told they are doing a kindness. That is what frightens her most, later, when later exists again. They move gently. One of them kneels to help a child up from the snow, and his voice, when he speaks, is soft:

"It's all right. We're here to save you."

He believes it. She can see that he believes it, the way you can see a candle burning behind a shuttered door.

* * *

The tower stairs spiral up and up and Anthe climbs them and Wren climbs them inside Anthe, wearing her body like a coat that doesn't fit, feeling the ache in her knees (old knees, kneeling-knees), the stitch in her side, the weight in her arms.

The weight. There is a weight in Anthe's arms wrapped in altar cloth, and it is warm, and it is breathing, and it is —

Wren surfaces out of the memory gasping, back in the cabin, back in the wool, and for one merciful moment there is only the fire and the dark and her own heart going like a bird against a window.

Then she looks across the room and sees the cradle of blankets by the hearth where she herself sleeps, and understands what was in the cloth, and screams.

No sound comes out. That is the cruelty of it. Inside the memory she screamed and screamed and the seed keeps her scream the way amber keeps an insect — perfect, preserved, silent. Anthe screamed too. Everyone in the tower screamed. And above them all the bell went on ringing, enormous and indifferent, filling the night with bronze, drowning every human voice in the city, so that the soldiers below would hear nothing but a bell and think: *a ceremony. A holy night. How fortunate we came on a holy night.*

* * *

There were four hundred of them in the tower.

Wren learns this the way she learns everything now — all at once, from the inside, knowledge arriving like water through a cracked hull. Four hundred souls in the Tower of Witnessing on the Night of the Turning Leaf. Priests and archivists and singers and children, the children hushed into the inner vaults, the elders standing at the doors with their palms open in the gesture of greeting, the gesture that means *we are unarmed and glad you came.*

The soldiers had come at moonrise. They had brought wagons. The wagons, the elders said, smiling, must be for the relics — how thoughtful, how careful, the Order sends wagons. Only when the doors were barred from the inside did anyone notice that the wagons carried pitch, and barrels, and chains for the gates of the sanctuary below, so that no one could leave and no one could enter and no one, anywhere in Vessic, would ever learn what happened until it had finished happening.

Anthe reaches the bell chamber. The ringer — old Sova, deaf as stone, which is why he alone did not flinch when the bell began — steps aside for her, and she sets down her burden among the bell ropes, and unwraps it.

A baby. Days old, maybe hours. Sleeping through the ringing, through everything, in the way of babies, in the way of miracles.

And now the seed shows Wren the rest of it, and Wren wishes — she will wish this forever, in every language she ever speaks — that it had let her look away.

* * *

They are singing.

Below, in the courtyards and the sanctuaries and the burning archive halls, the people of Vessic are singing. Because they do not know yet. Because the bell told them it was a holy night, and because the soldiers were gentle, and because three hundred years of peace had taught them the unthinkable thing about the unthinkable: that it takes time to arrive, that you will hear it coming, that someone will warn you.

The song rises up the tower stairwell and fills the bell chamber, and it is the Lament for the Founding, the oldest song, the one every Vessic child learns before learning to walk, and the words go through Wren like arrows made of light:

*We were the leaf upon the water.*
*We kept what others burned.*
*When all the kingdoms forgot their names,*
*we remembered ours.*

Anthe holds the baby and sings with them, her voice cracking on *remembered*, and around her the other priests gather, the ones who understood first, the ones who ran up the stairs while there was still time, and among them Wren recognizes faces she has seen her whole life carved into sanctuary walls — faces she thought were saints, and they are, oh they are, but they are also just people, terrified people holding each other in a bell tower while the world below turns to smoke.

"We cannot save them," says a priest whose name is Tallo, and his voice is flat with a grief so complete it has passed through grief and out the other side into arithmetic. "Four hundred in this tower. Eleven thousand in the city. Whatever we carry out of this night, we carry for all of them."

"Then we carry everything," Anthe says.

She looks down at the baby. The baby opens its eyes.

And the seed — the seed that Wren now understands has been waiting inside her since before she had language, since before she was anything but a warmth in a bundle of altar cloth — the seed opens its final door.

Because the baby is her.

* * *

She sees herself. She sees the small red face she has never been allowed to remember, and around that face the priests' hands rise, all of them together, and begin to work.

It does not hurt. That is the horror she will never be able to explain to anyone, not Rosalind, not Maren Kestrel if she ever meets her, not the kind smuggler man with the laugh like gravel: *it does not hurt*. Memory-work is supposed to hurt; the Order's own texts say so; tearing a soul's stitching leaves a wound. But the priests of Vessic were better than the Order at this, better than anyone, and they loved her — eleven thousand dead and dying below, and the ones left alive spent their last free hour making sure that this one small thing they did would be painless.

They take everything the city was. Every song. Every archive page. Every name of every person in the sanctuaries. The truth of what is happening in the streets right now, the wagons and the pitch and the gentle voices — they fold it all, the way Anthe folds linen, corner to corner, smaller and smaller, until it is a seed. A living memory-seed, dense as a collapsed star, carrying a nation folded up inside it.

And they plant it in the baby.

"Not a grave," Anthe whispers into the small ear, into the ear that is Wren's ear, that has always been Wren's ear. "A garden. You hear me, little leaf? This is not a grave. Nothing we give you is a grave. It is a garden, and it will sleep, and one day it will wake, and when it wakes, the world will finally have to look at what it did."

The bell stops ringing.

In the sudden silence — the first true silence of the whole memory — Wren hears what the bell was hiding. What it had been hiding all along.

Screaming, from below. So much screaming. And fire that has found its heat at last, and the crash of the sanctuary gates coming down, and the soldiers' gentle voices saying *it's all right, we're here to save you* to people who are past being saved, and over it all, thin and terrible, one voice singing the Lament for the Founding alone, off-key, defiant, a child's voice maybe, or an old woman's, singing *we remembered ours* while the smoke takes the words.

Anthe carries the baby to the tower window. Below, the courtyard is full of light that is not warm.

"Hold her," Anthe says, passing the bundle to Tallo, and her arms are empty and shaking. "Whatever happens — hold her."

Then she goes down the stairs toward the doors, toward the soldiers, toward the gentleness, with her palms open in the gesture of greeting, and the seed folds shut behind her like a hand closing over a flame, and Wren is falling —

* * *

Falling up.

Out. Back into wool and woodsmoke and the small ticking cabin, back into her own eight-year-old chest, which is heaving, which hurts, which has been screaming silently for — she doesn't know. Minutes. Hours. Snow-light moves on the ceiling. The fire has burned low. Someone has put a cup of water by her bed that she doesn't remember anyone bringing, and beside the cup, a sprig of dried mountain sage, because the woman noticed, days ago, that Wren rubs sage between her fingers when she is frightened, and remembered, and did this small wordless thing while Wren slept.

Wren lies still and lets the cabin hold her.

It is morning, or near it. Grey light. The storm has passed. And downstairs she can hear the woman moving — the clink of the kettle, the scrape of the chair, the particular rhythm of boots that belong to someone who checks the locks twice and then pretends she didn't. Rosalind. Ros-ah-lind. The name falls down the well of her and lands.

*She lost her memories too,* Wren thinks, and does not know where the thought comes from, except that she does know, she knows exactly where it comes from, it comes from the garden sleeping inside her, from the priests who stitched it there, from Anthe's last folded secret, which the bloom left resting on the surface of her mind like a coin on a pillow:

*One of us survived the tower. The one who carried the second seed. They took her memories and cast her out and wrote a lie over the wound — but the lie will not hold forever. Find her, little leaf. She is the key to the Gate.*

*Find her. She is already yours.*

Downstairs, the kettle begins to sing.

Wren gets up, wraps the wool blanket around herself like a cloak, and goes to find the woman with the missing memories — the woman who, though she cannot possibly know it yet, once held her at a window while the world burned, and swore, in a language three centuries dead, that no one would ever take her again.
