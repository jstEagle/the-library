## 9. An Old Debt in Ironhollow

Ironhollow smelled of coal smoke and wet stone, the way it had smelled ten years ago and would smell a hundred years from now. The town clung to the mountainside like a barnacle — terraced streets of slate-roofed houses stacked one above another, connected by stairs worn hollow in their centers by generations of boots. Above it all loomed the headframe of the Kestrel Deep mine, its great wheel turning slowly, endlessly, hauling ore and men out of the dark.

Rosalind kept her hood up and her hand on Wren's shoulder as they descended into the town. Dain walked ahead with two of his people, moving with the easy swagger of a man who belonged here, which he did; half of Ironhollow owed him money and the other half had lost money to him, and both halves respected him for it.

The blizzard that had nearly taken Wren two nights ago still haunted Rosalind's dreams. She could see the girl's face when Dain had carried her back inside — blue-lipped, snow-crusted, her small fists clenched so tight around a splinter of wood that they'd had to pry them open finger by finger. She'd been walking toward the north. Toward the Hollow Gate. Asleep, or something like asleep, her eyes open and fixed on nothing, her feet finding the road through drifts deep enough to bury a horse.

*She's being pulled,* Maren had once written, in a letter Rosalind had burned. *Seeds want soil.*

That letter was why they were here.

"You're sure about this woman?" Dain murmured, falling back beside her. His breath fogged in the cold. "Kestrel?"

"I'm sure she owes me."

"Everyone owes you something, Roz. That's not the same as them paying."

"She'll pay." Rosalind adjusted the strap of her pack. "Ten years ago I carried a crate of sealed archives out of the Stormspire for her. Never asked what was in it. Never told anyone she'd asked."

"And now you're collecting."

"I'm asking a favor of an old friend. Collecting is uglier."

Dain grunted. He hadn't argued when she'd told him where they were going, but she'd seen the calculation behind his eyes — the smuggler's arithmetic of risk. Ironhollow was a Company town, Company meant contracts, contracts meant clerks, clerks meant someone might be writing down names. A storm-caller in exile and a child who screamed in a dead language were exactly the kind of names worth writing down.

They crossed the market square, where miners' wives haggled over winter turnips under a sky the color of old pewter. Wren's grip tightened on Rosalind's hand. The girl had barely spoken since the Saddle Rocks — since the night the seed had woken in her and she'd screamed words that made the fire gutter and the dogs howl until dawn. But she walked close now, pressed against Rosalind's side like a burr, and when a cart rattled past too fast she flinched hard enough that Rosalind felt it in her own ribs.

"It's all right," Rosalind said, though she no longer believed anything was.

Wren looked up at her. In the grey light her eyes seemed almost silver, and there was something in them that wasn't a child's expression at all — something patient and old and terribly sad. Then it passed, and she was just a tired girl again, and Rosalind's chest ached.

Maren Kestrel lived at the top of the town, because disgraced archivists apparently climbed even when no one made them. Her house was the last before the spoil heaps began — narrow, tall, its windows crammed with books visible through frost-furred glass. A wind chime made of broken bell-bronze hung above the door, and it rang a low, wrong note as Rosalind knocked.

The door opened on a chain. One eye appeared in the gap, sharp and brown and ringed with wrinkles.

"We're closed," said a voice like gravel in a sack.

"Maren. It's Cyrus— " Rosalind caught herself. "It's Rosalind Sterling."

The eye didn't blink. The chain didn't move. Ten full seconds passed, long enough for the wind chime to ring twice more.

Then: "You look terrible," Maren said, and the chain rattled free.

* * *

The house smelled of dust, lamp oil, and something faintly botanical — dried herbs hung from every beam, bundled and labeled in a cramped, precise hand. Books covered every surface: stacked on chairs, wedged under table legs to steady them, piled in columns that reached the ceiling and leaned at angles that suggested either genius or catastrophe. A kettle already sat on the stove, which struck Rosalind as either remarkable hospitality or remarkable paranoia. With Maren, it was probably paranoia. She'd always known when visitors were coming.

Maren herself was smaller than Rosalind remembered — a stooped woman in a man's coat, iron-grey hair cropped short, her hands stained with ink to the second knuckle. She poured tea without asking preferences, set three cups down, and then stood looking at Wren for a long, silent moment.

"Well," Maren said quietly. "You actually found one."

"You know what she is."

"I know what she *carries*." Maren lowered herself into the room's only intact armchair. "Sit down, girl. Both of you. And you—" this to Wren, gentler, in accented but fluent Vessic, "*sitha vael*, little bird. No one here will hurt you."

Wren went very still. Then she crossed the room, took Maren's ink-stained hand in both of hers, and pressed her forehead to it — a gesture formal and old, the kind of thing Rosalind had seen only in illuminated manuscripts, courtiers greeting kings.

Maren's breath caught. When she looked up at Rosalind, her eyes were wet. "Three hundred years," she said. "Three hundred years since anyone's done that properly. Do you understand what's sitting in your kitchen drinking my tea?"

"I'm starting to."

"Starting to." Maren laughed, short and bitter. "I've spent forty years starting to, Sterling. Sit. Drink. Then tell me everything, and don't leave anything out because you think an old woman can't stomach it."

So Rosalind told her. The doorstep in the whiteout. The language. The lexicon. The sleep-speaking, the place-names. The vault beneath the ruined waystation, the records of the memory-seed ritual, her own name among the priests. Sereth and the wraiths at the Saddle Rocks. The storm she'd called, too easily, too eagerly. Wren's flight into the blizzard, walking north with her eyes open. Ilvane's flag of truce, and the soft, reasonable voice saying *we only want to protect her.*

Maren listened without interrupting, which Rosalind remembered was unlike her. Only twice did she move: once to refill the tea, and once, when Rosalind described the ritual records, to close her eyes and press two fingers against her lips, as if holding something in.

When it was done, the stove ticked. Outside, the headframe wheel turned with its slow, patient groan.

"The name in the ledger," Maren said at last. "Say it again. Exactly as written."

"*Sterling, R. Rite of Hollowing, assistant anchor.*"

"Assistant anchor." Maren nodded slowly. "Not principal. Not witness. Assistant anchor. That's specific, and it's damning, and it's also — if you'll forgive me — not what you think it is."

"Then tell me what it is, because I've spent two weeks wondering if I helped murder a nation's memory and then had my own mind scraped clean to hide it."

"You did help. Or a version of you did." Maren set down her cup. "But here's what forty years in forbidden archives taught me, and what the Order will never put in a ledger: the rite requires more than one anchor. It requires *three*. A principal, who shapes the seed. An assistant, who steadies it while it's planted. And a vessel, who carries it out of the world." She looked at Wren. "Your girl is the third. The vessel. Chosen before she was born, most likely — bloodlines matter in these things, and the Vessic priestly lines run thin but they don't run dry."

"And my role?"

"Steadying a memory-seed is like holding a live storm in cupped hands. It wants to bloom. It wants to *be remembered*. The assistant anchor's mind is the mold it's poured into — the shape that keeps it contained." Maren's gaze was steady and pitiless. "Which means, Rosalind, that whoever performed that rite took pieces of your mind to use as the container. Whatever you've lost these last ten years — whatever gaps you've blamed on exile, on grief, on drink if you're honest about some of those years — some of it isn't missing. It's *occupied*. There's a war's worst night living in the space where your memories used to be."

The room tilted. Rosalind heard herself say, flatly, "That's not possible."

"You called a storm after ten years and it came too easily. You read a dead language and half understood it. You found a strange child on your doorstep and felt *pulled*." Maren counted each item off on ink-stained fingers. "The seed knows you, Sterling. You're part of its architecture. Of course it came to your door. Of all the hearths in the Thornfells, it came to *yours*."

Wren, curled in the corner with a blanket Maren had fetched, watched them both. Rosalind met her eyes and had to look away.

"So the Order erased my memory of the rite."

"The Order erased your memory of *agreeing* to the rite. There's a difference, and it matters." Maren leaned forward. "Think. Why would they wipe the architect's mind and keep the architect alive? Exile, not execution. A lie instead of silence. Because a dead storm-caller becomes a martyr and a question. A disgraced one becomes a warning. They turned you into the story they tell recruits: *this is what betrayal earns*. And the beautiful part—" her voice curdled, "—is that they didn't even need to fabricate much. They just needed you to doubt yourself. Guilt does the rest. Guilt is the cheapest prison ever built."

Rosalind's hands were shaking. She wrapped them around the teacup and the warmth didn't help. Ten years. Ten years of flinching from her own reflection, of rehearsing a crime she couldn't remember committing, of building a life in the cracks of the world because she believed she deserved nothing better. And it had been *installed*. Like a flaw in cut stone, placed there by a chisel she'd trusted.

"Dain says trust no one," she managed.

"Dain is a smuggler. He's right about most things." Maren rose, joints cracking, and went to a bookshelf near the window — one shelf conspicuously less crowded than the others, its contents few and thin. She drew out a volume bound in cracked grey leather, unmarked, and laid it on the table between them.

"What is it?"

"My life's work. Forty years on the seed rites. Confiscated twice, burned once — I kept copies, obviously, I'm not an amateur." She opened it to a page dense with diagrams: concentric rings, a seed-shape at the center, annotations in four scripts. "Now. Your actual question. You want to know if the truth inside that child can be *read* — witnessed, recorded, proven — without being *unleashed*. Without turning loose three centuries of grief on the world and starting the war again with better weapons."

"Can it?"

"Yes." Maren said it simply, and it landed like a hammer. "The seed has two states, the same as any seed. Dormant and bloomed. Bloomed, it forces itself on every mind in reach — that's what happened at the Saddle Rocks, that's what sent your girl walking into a blizzard. But there's a third path. A *controlled* flowering. The rite can be reversed at the place where the seed was made — the Hollow Gate. Reversed slowly, witnessed properly, the memory transferred to a record rather than radiated into minds. Read without being unleashed. Exactly what you asked."

Rosalind let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for two weeks. "Then we go to the Gate. We do the rite. We bring proof out — testimony, documents, whatever form it takes — and we end this. The Court gets its acknowledgment, the Order gets exposed, Wren gets to be a child again."

Maren didn't smile. Maren had gone very still, the way people go still around wounds.

"Say the rest," Rosalind said.

"The reversal needs an anchor. Same as the planting did. Someone's mind holds the channel open while the memory passes through — three hundred years of it, every scream, every name, every detail, held steady and *witnessed* rather than absorbed." Maren's fingers traced the diagram's outermost ring. "The anchor doesn't just hold it. The anchor takes the strain of it. And the Gate sits in the heart of Court territory, surrounded by revenants who have waited three hundred years for exactly this, and the Order will send everything it has once it understands what you intend, and—"

"Maren."

"—and the last person who served as an assistant anchor, during the original rite, lost—" Maren stopped. Started again, quieter. "The records are incomplete about what happened to the assistants afterward. Incomplete in the way archives get incomplete when the truth is unbearable."

"How much strain?"

"Enough that it should be someone old. Someone whose memories have already done their work." Maren looked at her own ink-stained hands, then up, and her eyes were fierce and frightened and absolutely clear. "Someone like me. Which is why I'm telling you I'll do it. I've been dead in every way that matters for forty years, Sterling. Let my last act be useful."

"No." It came out faster than Rosalind intended. "No. I'm not trading one person for another. That's just the Order's arithmetic with better manners."

"Then you'll need to find a third option between my offer and catastrophe, and you'll need to find it soon." Maren closed the book and pushed it across the table. "Take this. Memorize what you can, burn what you can't. And Rosalind — one more thing, and then I'll let you sleep, because you look like a woman who hasn't slept since the Saddle Rocks."

"What?"

"The Court let you go at the Saddle Rocks. Think about that. Revenants don't show mercy; they show *patience*. If Sereth wanted the seed, he could have taken it — the Court has never once in three hundred years failed to take what it could reach." She tapped the diagram, the seed at its center. "They want you to come to the Gate. Willingly. Consent matters in these rites — it always has. A seed given freely blooms true. A seed taken by force blooms *warped*." Her voice dropped. "And a warped bloom, at the Hollow Gate, with three armies standing around it? That's not revelation. That's apocalypse wearing a historian's coat."

Outside, the light was failing. Somewhere below, the mine whistle blew the evening shift change, a long mournful note that rolled up through the terraced streets.

Rosalind gathered the book, woke Wren gently, and thanked Maren with words that felt too small. At the door, the old archivist caught her sleeve.

"For what it's worth," Maren said, "the pull you felt toward that child on your doorstep? Don't fight it. The seed chose its keeper's shape when it chose your mind. Whatever happens at the Gate — you're not the Order's tool anymore. You're the only person alive who can hold that door open gently." A dry, crooked smile. "Try to be gentler than they taught you to be."

They were halfway down the first flight of stairs when Rosalind saw them.

Four figures at the mouth of the alley below, cloaked in travel-grey, standing in the lamplight with the unhurried stillness of people who had been waiting a long time and expected to be recognized. Not soldiers. Not revenants. Their hoods bore no sigil, but Rosalind knew the cut of those cloaks, knew the way they stood in a semicircle with the center left deliberately open — an honor guard formation, a *reception*.

And stepping forward out of the center, lifting back her hood, silver-haired and serene, was High Cantor Ilvane.

"Rosalind," her former mentor called up the stairs, her voice warm as banked coals. "We should talk. About the debt you owe — and the one you're owed."

Beside her, Wren's small hand went rigid in Rosalind's grip, and from somewhere deep in the girl's chest came a sound Rosalind had hoped never to hear again: the beginning of a scream.
