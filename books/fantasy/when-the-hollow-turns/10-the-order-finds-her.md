## 10. The Order Finds Her

The truce flag was white silk, and Ilvane had chosen it herself from the reliquary stores at Stormrest — the same silk that had once wrapped the bones of Cantor Aveline, three centuries dead. It seemed fitting. Everything about this mission was a kind of burial.

She rode at the head of twelve storm-callers through the Ironhollow switchbacks, and she had ordered them to leave their lances sheathed and their weather-hands empty. Twelve was already too many. Twelve said *we could take you if we wished*, and the whole point of Ilvane's approach was that they did not wish to take anything. Not yet. Taking was crude. Taking left scars, and scars left questions, and questions were the one thing the Order of the Tempered Sky could not afford right now — not with a Vessic memory-seed ripening somewhere in these mountains like fruit gone soft in a cellar.

No. What was needed here was gentleness. What was needed was an old woman coming home to her student with open hands and a story shaped exactly like the truth.

*Home.* The word sat strangely in her as the column climbed past the last waystation, its roof charred black by Court fire two nights ago. Sereth's work, no doubt. The revenant's patience was legendary, but his tidiness — the way he burned each link behind him as he tracked the child south — spoke of something older than patience. Hunger, maybe. Or ritual. The Hollow Court had always confused the two.

Ilvane had not seen these mountains in ten years. She had promised herself she wouldn't. And yet here they were, unchanged in the ways that hurt most: the pines leaning east like penitents, the wind combing down from the Saddle Rocks with that low mineral moan, the light at this hour going thin and silver as old frost. Somewhere below them, in a mining town that smelled of iron and tallow, Rosalind Sterling was buying supplies for a road Ilvane knew by heart, because Ilvane had taught it to her, because Ilvane had taught her everything, because —

Because that was the cruelty of it, wasn't it. Ten years of exile and Ilvane still thought of the girl as *hers*.

"She'll see the riders before we're within a league," said Cantor Brellan, riding at her left hand. He was young for the rank, ambitious in the dull honest way that made him useful, and nervous now in a way he was trying to hide. "Should I send a herald ahead?"

"No." Ilvane kept her eyes on the road. "A herald is a wall. I want no walls between us when she sees my face."

"You think your face will move her?"

"I think," Ilvane said mildly, "that faces are the oldest magic we have, and I intend to use mine well."

Brellan subsided. Below them, Ironhollow's smoke rose straight up into the still air — cookfires, forge-smoke, nothing military. Rosalind was not hiding, then. Or she was hiding in plain sight, which was more dangerous, and more like her.

*You were never good at being small,* Ilvane thought, and felt something that was almost tenderness, and put it away where she kept all such things: deep, labeled, and rarely opened.

They reached the town at dusk.

* * *

Maren Kestrel's lodgings stood above the assayer's office, and it was there, not at any inn, that Ilvane found her — which told Ilvane more than an hour of questioning would have. Of course Rosalind had gone to Kestrel. The disgraced archivist was the only living soul outside the Order's sealed archives who had studied the seed rites. Rosalind might have lost ten years of memory, but she had never lost her instincts, and her instincts had always been a kind of genius.

Ilvane climbed the stairs alone, as agreed, the truce silk folded over her arm like a gift.

Rosalind opened the door before she knocked.

Ten years had carved her leaner. There was gray at her temples now, and new lines around her eyes, and she wore a smuggler's oilskin over a storm-caller's high collar — half of each world, belonging fully to neither. But her stance was unchanged: weight on the balls of her feet, hands loose, the posture of a woman who had learned that doorways were chokepoints and every visitor was a question mark with teeth.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The stairwell lamp guttered between them.

"High Cantor," Rosalind said at last. Her voice was flat as slate. "You've come far to freeze on a landing."

"I've come under truce." Ilvane lifted the silk so the lamplight caught it. "White thread, consecrated. You remember what breaking it costs."

"I remember lots of things I'd rather not." But Rosalind stepped back, and Ilvane took the invitation for what it was — a battle won without a single blow — and entered.

The room beyond was small and warm and painfully domestic. A kettle on the iron stove. Boots drying by the hearth, one pair enormous and sea-worn, one pair so small they looked like something grown rather than made. A child's cloak hung on a peg, patched at the elbows. On the table, a game of stones sat abandoned mid-play, black pieces clustered defensively around a single white one.

So. The smuggler captain was close, and the child closer, and they had been playing games together in the evenings like — Ilvane searched for the word and found it with a small cold shock — like a family.

"Tea?" Rosalind asked, in the tone of a woman offering a knife.

"Thank you."

They sat across the table from each other, the abandoned game between them, and Ilvane let the silence stretch until Rosalind's jaw tightened. Patience. She had outlasted archdukes with less.

"You look well," Ilvane said finally. "The mountains agree with you."

"The mountains don't know me."

"They know you better than you know yourself, perhaps. That's rather why I'm here."

Rosalind poured the tea without ceremony, and Ilvane noted — filed away — that her former student drank first from her own cup before pushing the second across. Still careful. Still convinced the world was full of poisoners. Ilvane wondered, not for the first time, whether the memory-work they'd done ten years ago had taken too much. Whether they had cut away some load-bearing part of the girl along with the rot.

It didn't matter. What was done was done, and regret was a luxury for people whose orders hadn't been signed in blood.

"I'll be brief," Ilvane said, "because you'll want me gone, and because brevity reads as honesty, and I need you to believe me tonight." She set the folded silk on the table between them. "There are forces moving in these mountains that make the Order look like a village choir. The Hollow Court has returned, Rosalind. Not remnants. Not cultists playing at revenants. The Court itself, awake again after three hundred years, and it wants something that is currently sleeping under this roof."

Something crossed Rosalind's face — quickly mastered, but not quickly enough.

"You know what I mean," Ilvane said softly.

"I know a child fell asleep at my hearth three weeks ago." Rosalind's voice stayed flat. "I know she speaks a dead language. I know someone burned the north passes looking for her. If the Hollow Court wants a frostbitten eight-year-old, then whatever she is, it isn't *nothing*, and I'd like you to tell me plainly what it is, since apparently everyone else in this war already knows but me."

"There it is." Ilvane allowed herself a small sigh. "That's the wound under all of it, isn't it? Not the exile. The *not knowing*. Ten years, and the worst part was never the cold or the smugglers' roads. It was waking each morning with a hole in you shaped like a betrayal you couldn't remember committing."

The teacup in Rosalind's hand did not tremble, but her knuckles went white around it, and Ilvane knew — with the certainty of a surgeon who has mapped every nerve — that she had struck true.

"You came here to dig at me," Rosalind said quietly.

"I came here to offer you a shovel." Ilvane leaned forward, and let her voice drop into the register she had used in lecture halls, the one that made first-year callers lean toward her like plants toward sun. "Child. Listen to me. The night you were cast out, you begged the Conclave to show you what you'd done. They refused. I refused. I have regretted that refusal for ten years, and I am here to correct it — as much as I'm permitted to, under vows I cannot break even now. So I will tell you what I can, and you will hate me for how little it is, and then you will do the arithmetic yourself, because you were always better at arithmetic than anyone gave you credit for."

"Say it."

"The seed ritual requires seven anchors. Seven minds, bound at a single hour, holding the memory together the way ribs hold a lung." Ilvane watched her own hands, folded neatly on the table. "Six of those priests are named in the archives. The seventh name is redacted. Sealed by the High Conclave itself, at a level even I cannot breach."

"And you think—"

"I think," Ilvane said, gently, gently, "that you broke into the vault at Karst Fell two weeks ago. That you read what was there. That you found the list of six names and one absence, and that you have been lying awake ever since wondering why your own memories of that year are a smooth blank wall with no doors in it." She lifted her eyes. "I think you already suspect, Rosalind. I think you suspected before you ever saw my face on that stair, and that's why you let me in. Because some part of you has spent ten years wanting someone to come and confirm the worst."

The stove ticked. Outside, far off, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a crowd deciding whether to applaud.

"If that's true," Rosalind said, and her voice was very controlled now, the control of a woman standing on ice and hearing it crack, "then the Order didn't just exile me. It *made* me. It wrote me into a ritual and then cut the pages out and punished me for the missing text."

"The Order protects the world from things it cannot explain," Ilvane said. "Including, sometimes, itself. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But hear the rest." She drew a breath, and made it audible, and let her shoulders stoop a fraction — an old woman's tiredness, genuine in every particular except its timing. "The child carries the founding memory of the Hollow War. Complete. Undamaged. Every face, every name, every act committed in that atrocity — preserved the way amber preserves the fly. If it opens in the wrong place, in the wrong hands, it will not merely tell a truth. It will *ignite*. Three centuries of grief given a body and a voice, and every descendant of every victim and every perpetrator standing in the blast radius. Do you understand what that means? It doesn't end wars, Rosalind. It restarts them with the original fury intact."

"And the Order's solution?"

"To contain it. As we contained it once." Ilvane met her eyes steadily. "And to protect the vessel. The girl is innocent in every way that matters. No one in the Conclave wishes her harm. Bring her to us — to Stormrest, to wards older than the Court, to people who have kept worse secrets safely for longer — and I swear to you on the Tempered Sky itself that she will be fed, schooled, sheltered, and loved. Loved, Rosalind. She is a child. Whatever is inside her, *she* is only what has happened to her. We know the difference. We have always known the difference."

"You knew the difference ten years ago too," Rosalind said. "When you cut pieces out of me and called it protection."

Ilvane let the blow land. Did not flinch, did not defend. Sat with it in the lamplight like a stone placed carefully on a scale.

"Yes," she said simply. "And I have carried that longer than you have carried the gap. Which is why I'm here, in the cold, at my age, instead of sending Brellan and his lances. Ask them to wait outside if you doubt me. Ask them anything. I came alone to this room, unarmed, under white silk, to say to your face what the Conclave would prefer I said through three layers of protocol." She paused, and then — the final card, played softly, almost as an aside — "And there is more I can give you, if you come. Not the sealed name. I cannot give you that; the seals are real, and older than my authority. But the *key* to it. Because a seal is only a lock, and locks answer to certain hands, and I believe — I have come to believe, watching what the seed does when it dreams near you — that the seventh anchor's memories were not destroyed that night. They were *stored*. In the only vault secure enough to hold them."

Rosalind went very still.

"In you," Ilvane said. "Your missing years aren't gone, child. They're archived. And the Order knows how to open the archive — safely, surgically, with you awake and consenting at every step. You could have your betrayal back. Your true history. The reason for all of it. Or—" and here she spread her hands, an old gesture from a thousand lectures, *the world is yours to choose* — "you can keep running south with a smuggler and a time bomb, waiting for the Hollow Court's revenants to catch up, gambling a child's life on mountain roads you no longer know as well as you think."

Silence. The long kind. Ilvane drank her tea and waited, because she had learned across ninety years that silence did more persuading than any speech, that people filled quiet with their own arguments and then believed those arguments were theirs.

At last Rosalind said: "If I bring her in. If I do this. I stay with her. Wherever your wards are, I'm inside them, and nobody touches her mind — nobody — until I've seen this archive-opening myself and judged it safe. And if I judge it unsafe, we walk out, both of us, and your silk stays unbroken."

"Agreed," Ilvane said, before the relief could reach her face. "All of it. You have my word."

"Your word." Rosalind almost smiled. It was not a warm expression. "Ten years ago I'd have taken that word to the bank of heaven. Now it weighs about as much as this cup." She turned the cup in her hands. "I need to speak with the others. Dain. Maren. The girl. It's not my choice alone anymore — I made it theirs when I took her in, and I won't unmake that because a High Cantor finally found her conscience."

"Of course." Ilvane rose, gathered the white silk, and paused at the door with her hand on the latch — the picture of restraint, of respect for boundaries, of a mentor yielding the last word to her student.

"One thing more," she said. "For your own safety, tonight. The Court's agent — Sereth — is closer than you think. He does not burn waystations randomly; he burns them in a line, and the line bends toward wherever the child sleeps. He has been patient for three hundred years, and he is patient now, but patience is not mercy. Whatever you decide — decide quickly."

She descended the stairs into the dark without looking back, and only at the bottom, in the shadow between lamps, did she allow herself to exhale.

*Well played,* she told herself, and found the praise tasted like ash, and decided that ash was simply what duty tasted like for women of her rank, and swallowed it.

Behind her, above the assayer's office, a lamp moved past the window. Rosalind, pacing. Gathering the others. Doing exactly what Ilvane had predicted, in the order Ilvane had predicted it.

By morning, they would be moving toward Stormrest. Toward the wards. Toward the rite that would open the archive of Rosalind Sterling's stolen years — and, in the same motion, lay the child and the seed bare on the Conclave's table, ready for the cutting that must come before the Court arrived.

Ilvane pulled her hood up against the mountain cold and walked to where Brellan and the others waited among the wagons.

"She'll come," she said. "Prepare the receiving chamber."

Brellan hesitated. "And if the seed resists containment? The archivist Kestrel claims there are rites that—"

"Maren Kestrel," Ilvane said pleasantly, "claims a great many things. See that she is not present at the working."

"But the truce—"

"The truce covers the child and the storm-caller," Ilvane said, climbing into the wagon and drawing the furs around her knees. "It says nothing whatsoever about archivists."

Above them, on the dark shoulder of Thornfell, the wind changed — swung suddenly out of the north, carrying with it a smell like snow and old smoke and something underneath both that Ilvane had hoped never to smell again in her lifetime.

The Court knew they were here.

The clock had started, and every party in the mountains was now racing the same dawn.
