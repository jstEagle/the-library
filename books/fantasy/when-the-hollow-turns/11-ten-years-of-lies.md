## 11. Ten Years of Lies

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Rosalind laid the two accounts side by side.

On her left, the vault records she'd copied in Ilvane's own cipher-hand — pages stolen from a place the Order pretended did not exist, ink browned with age and something older than age. On her right, the sheet of paper High Cantor Ilvane had pressed into her palm under a flag of truce, written in the same elegant script Rosalind had once copied a thousand times as an acolyte, learning to shape storms out of syllables.

She read them both again. Then again. The cabin was very quiet around her, except for Wren's slow breathing from the loft and Dain's boots creaking where he sat by the door, sharpening a knife he had no need to sharpen.

*Subject retains full somatic memory of the rite,* said the vault record. *Memory suppression holds. Subject believes herself guilty of the Vessic correspondence as instructed. Exile recommended — exile serves better than execution; a named traitor draws less scrutiny than a missing priest.*

*You were young,* Ilvane had written, in that same warm, regretful voice she'd used across the truce-fire. *You were chosen for the seed ritual because you were strong enough to hold it, and you were never told because knowing would have endangered everything. Your grief afterward was real. The betrayal you confessed — the letters, the sold sigils, the dead courier — I grieved it too, child. But you must understand: your punishment was mercy. Come home. Let us restore what was taken.*

Rosalind set the pages down. Her hands were steady. That frightened her more than shaking would have.

"Dain," she said.

He looked up. Ten days on the road from Ironhollow had carved new lines into his face, and the wound along his forearm — the one he swore was nothing — had gone the color of old rust. "Found something?"

"I want you to tell me what you see." She slid both papers across the table. "Don't read them. Just look."

Dain squinted. He was no scholar, but he'd spent thirty years reading contracts, forged manifests, customs seals, and the small printed lies of border towns. He turned each page toward the light, lips moving, and Rosalind watched his eyes do what smugglers' eyes do — skip past meaning and land on structure.

"Same hand," he said at last. "Both of them. Same ink, near enough. Same... I don't know the word. The way the letters lean." He looked up. "Your Cantor wrote both?"

"The vault record is eleven years old." Rosalind's voice came out flat, distant, like someone else speaking through her. "Ilvane wrote the other one nine days ago."

The knife stopped moving against the whetstone.

"So either she's been writing in the same hand for a decade without drifting," Rosalind continued, "which archivists can do, which is why they train them — or—" She stopped. The sentence wouldn't finish itself. It sat there on the table between them like something dead.

"Or the old record's not old," Dain said quietly.

"Or the old record isn't old." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Eleven years ago I stood in the Hall of Censure while three cantors read my crimes aloud. Letters to Vessic sympathizers. Sigils sold to agents of the old kings. A courier found dead with my mark burned into his chest. Eleven years, Dain. I built a life in the shape of that guilt. I learned these mountains because I thought I deserved to be nowhere."

Dain pushed back from the table and crossed to the window, checking the treeline out of habit — the snowfields silvered under a fat, indifferent moon, nothing moving. He'd been checking every hour since Ironhollow, since Maren's confession, since the word *anchor* had entered their lives like a splinter working toward the heart.

"Say it plain," he said. "What are you telling yourself?"

"That the crime was written before it was committed." Rosalind pressed her palms flat against the table, feeling the grain, feeling something solid, because nothing else was. "That Ilvane drafted my confession before I ever made it. That they didn't catch a traitor. They *made* one — they put the guilt in me like a stone in a fruit, and then they threw me out here, ten years' walk from anyone who might ask questions, because a woman who remembers committing treason asks no questions about memory-seeds."

The fire popped. Up in the loft, Wren murmured in her sleep — a soft run of Vessic syllables, rising and falling like water over stones — and both of them flinched, then made themselves relax.

"And the part that doesn't fit," Dain said slowly, "is that if they wanted you forgotten, why let you live? Why not—"

"Because a dead storm-caller gets investigated. A disgraced one gets pitied." Rosalind heard her own voice hardening, and underneath the hardness something vast and cold was rising, the way pressure rises ahead of weather. "Because I wasn't hidden away. I was *planted*. Think about it. Who lives above the smuggler roads, knows every pass, every cave, every route south, and has spent ten years being trusted by people who move contraband for a living?" She gestured at herself. "If the Order ever needed to move something no one could find — someone, some*thing* — through Thornfell, they'd already placed the perfect instrument. They just hadn't told her."

"You're saying they exiled you here to wait for the girl."

"No," Rosalind said. "I'm saying I don't know anymore. That's the point. That's what they took." She stood, abruptly, and the chair went over behind her, and she didn't pick it up. "I have spent ten years being certain of exactly one thing — that I betrayed my order and my own conscience, and that this wilderness was my penance. It was the floor I stood on. And it was *written*. Drafted. In her hand, in advance, like a play." Her breath came short. Outside, far up the valley, thunder muttered though the sky was clear, and she realized distantly that it was answering her. She pulled it back in, clenched it, held it. "They didn't take my memories, Dain. They took my *certainty*, and they left me the guilt to keep me company."

Dain crossed the room and righted the chair. Then, awkwardly — Dain Ferro was many things, but gentle the way gentled things are gentle, broken-then-mended — he put a hand on her shoulder.

"Rosa." Only he called her that; only he'd earned it, over ten winters of routes and debts and one night in a Kestrel tavern when they'd both drunk too much and traded wounds instead of coin. "Listen to me now, because I've buried friends who trusted the wrong face one time too many. Everything that's come at us wearing a kind expression has been lying. The Cantor with her truce flag. The revenant who let us walk out of the Saddle instead of taking us — that one still turns my stomach, because nothing hunts that long and gives up free. Even your archivist, bless her ancient bones, sat on the truth about that rite until it suited her to spill it." His grip tightened. "Trust no one. Not the Order, not the Court, not Maren Kestrel, and — don't look at me like that — not even me, past what you've seen with your own eyes. We get to that Gate, we do the rite, we save the girl, and after that everyone involved can go hang."

"Even me?" Rosalind managed.

"Especially you." A ghost of his old grin, there and gone. "You're the one they built all this around. If I were plotting against us, I'd start with you."

It was meant as a joke. It landed like a blade finding a gap in armor, and she saw him realize it a half-second too late.

But it was also true, and true things had a way of cutting regardless of intent. Because here was the thought she'd been circling all night like a moth around a lamp, the one she couldn't say aloud yet: *if my guilt was planted, what else was?* The vault record spoke of somatic memory, of suppression, of a subject who *believes herself guilty as instructed.* Believed. Past tense, eleven years past. What else had been folded into her in those lost weeks — the ones the records called *stabilization* and she had never been able to remember at all? Had they left instructions in her, sleeping, waiting for a trigger? Was there a word, a sign, a bell-tone that would reach down into whatever they'd built inside her and turn her hand against Wren?

She looked up at the loft, at the small shape curled under Dain's spare coat, and felt the full vertigo of it: she had taken this child in because something in her *pulled*, some inexplicable gravity she'd called conscience or fate. What if it was neither? What if the pull was engineering — a thread tied to her ankle eleven years ago, and someone, somewhere, holding the spool?

"Ilva— the Cantor said my lost memories held the key," she said. "I thought she was baiting me. Now I think she was telling the truth, and that's worse. Because if the key is in my head, then so is the lock. And locks are built by the people who want doors to open at the right time."

Downstairs logic, upstairs dread. She gathered the papers, struck a taper, and held Ilvane's warm, forgiving letter over the flame. It caught, curled, brightened. She almost enjoyed watching it go.

Then she held the vault record over the flame too — and stopped.

Her hand wouldn't move. Because that page, at least, was evidence. That page was the one honest thing in eleven years, even if its honesty was monstrous. She thrust it back into the oilskin packet with Maren's notes and the lexicon, and stood there trembling with the taper guttering, hating how much of her life now fit into a packet small enough to lose.

"Sleep," Dain said. "I'll take first watch. Tomorrow we push for the Hollow Gate road, and you'll want your head clear, planted or not."

"There is no tomorrow version of my head, Dain. There's just this one, with cracks in the floor."

"Cracked floors still hold weight." He nodded at the loft. "She's proof."

Rosalind climbed up and settled beside Wren, careful not to wake her. In sleep the girl looked younger than her years and older than nations, frost-pale lashes, chapped lips slightly parted. As Rosalind watched, Wren's fingers twitched, and she murmured again — and this time the dream-speech resolved into a single repeated phrase, soft and urgent, the way children repeat a name looking for someone.

Rosalind knew the word. She'd seen it in the sealed archives, carved on a war memorial older than any kingdom standing: *the Hollow Gate.*

"Yes," Rosalind whispered, in Vessic, badly pronounced, the way you'd answer a sleeper. "I know. We're going."

Wren stilled, as if she'd heard.

Rosalind lay back and stared at the rafters, and did not sleep. Rage and grief took turns with her, two horses on a wheel — rage at Ilvane, who had signed her ruin in advance and then offered her a mother's warmth across a truce-fire; grief for the woman she'd been at twenty-six, standing in the Hall of Censure, genuinely weeping over crimes she'd never committed, begging forgiveness from the people who'd authored her. Ten years. Ten years of penance performed for a lie, of solitude chosen as punishment, of refusing Dain's friendship for the first three winters because a traitor shouldn't be loved.

And beneath the rage, colder and quieter, the question that would not stop turning:

*If they could write guilt into me once — what are they writing into me now?*

Far off toward the north passes, thunder rolled again across a cloudless sky. This time Rosalind knew she wasn't calling it.

Something else was.

She sat up fast, heart slamming — and found Wren already awake beside her, eyes wide and black in the dark, staring at the eastern wall as though it had turned to glass. The girl's lips moved. What came out was not Vessic this time, and not common tongue either, but a low, ringing tone that Rosalind felt in her teeth, in the scar-tissue of her erased weeks, in whatever locked room the Order had built inside her.

The door of the cabin rattled in its frame. Dain surged up from his watch, knife out.

And Wren, without taking her eyes from the wall, began to cry — silent, terrible tears — and stood, and reached for her boots.
