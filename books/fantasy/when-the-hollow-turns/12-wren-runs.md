## 12. Wren Runs

The dream did not come as a dream this time. It came as a door.

Wren stood inside it — inside the night the priests had folded into her bones — and the door was burning. Doors should not burn from the inside out, but this one did, and through the splitting wood she could see snow that fell upward and a city with its bells still ringing though everyone who had rung them was already ash.

*Vessa,* said the thing inside her, in her own voice, which was not her voice at all. *Remember Vessa.*

She did not want to remember. Remembering was like swallowing a hot coal and being told to hold it in her mouth all night. But the seed did not ask. It bloomed whether she willed it or no, and tonight it bloomed wide open.

She was on the temple steps. She was small — smaller than she was now — and the woman holding her hand wore gray robes with silver thread at the hem, and the woman's name was *Saret*, and Wren knew that the way you know your own heartbeat, because these were not memories given to her but memories *become*, pressed into her blood by nine dying mouths chanting over a copper bowl.

The bells stopped ringing.

That was the worst part. Not the fire. Not the soldiers coming up through the smoke with white armbands and faces like closed doors. The silence after. The way a whole city's voice could simply — stop.

*Saret, Saret, they're coming—*

The memory tore sideways. Now she was under the mountain, in the dark, in the room of nine candles, and the oldest priest was saying words she understood without knowing how: *It cannot be destroyed. It can only be carried. Small things carry best what great things cannot hold.*

They were going to put the whole night inside her. All of it. The bells and the fire and the names of the dead, three hundred years of a people's worst hour, folded down small, down small, down into a child who could run where armies could not follow.

*I don't want it,* Wren tried to say, in the dream, in Vessic, in the tongue of the dead city. *I'm too small.*

*You are exactly the right size,* said the old priest, gently, terribly. *Smallness is the safest place in the world to hide a truth. No one looks inside children. They only look at them.*

And then — the bloom turned, the way a flower turns toward sun, except what it turned toward was Rosalind's cabin, and the mountain, and a direction Wren's feet knew better than her head. South-southwest. Down through the black pines. Toward a gate made of nothing at all.

The seed wanted to go home. And it was wearing her legs.

* * *

Dain Ferro woke to the sound of the wind changing, and lay very still in the waystation loft, counting the sounds that were wrong.

Wind wrong. That was the first. It had swung north in the last hour, and north winds in Thornfell in the dying of winter meant one thing: whiteout coming down off the high shoulders, fast and mean. The second wrong sound was simpler. The loft above him was empty.

He'd slept with his bedroll between Wren and the wall every night since Ironhollow, not because anyone asked him to but because some animal part of him needed to know exactly where she was breathing. He'd told himself it was practical. Children wandered. Mountains killed wanderers. That was arithmetic, not affection.

The loft was empty and the shutter latch was lifted and her boots — her stupid, thin, smuggler-surplus boots he'd meant to replace in Ironhollow and hadn't — were gone.

"Wren." He said it once, low, so as not to wake Rosalind and Maren below, and then he was up, and then he wasn't being quiet anymore. "Rosalind."

She was awake before his foot hit the third stair. Storm-callers, he remembered, slept like cats; ten years out of the citadels hadn't worn it off her. She stood in the doorway of the common room with her hair loose and her eyes already reading the lifted latch, the cold spilling down the stairs, everything.

"Which way?"

"South door. Rosalind—" His voice did something he didn't authorize. "There's a whiteout coming down the north shoulder. I felt it change."

Something crossed her face — fear, yes, but also that other thing, the thing he'd seen growing in her for weeks whenever she looked at the girl. Guilt, maybe. Or recognition. "Maren stays. You and I go. Lanterns, rope, and Dain—" She caught his arm at the bottom of the stairs. Her grip was stronger than it looked. "If we get separated, you do not chase her deeper than you can walk back. Promise me. I've watched good trackers die doing it."

"Promises later," Dain said, and pulled on his coat.

* * *

Outside, the world had been erased.

Wren ran anyway.

Snow came at her sideways, then from below, then in great slow wheeling sheets that blotted out the moon and then gave it back for half a breath, and in each half-breath she saw the pines — black, bowed, streaming — and fixed on them like signposts in a burning alphabet. South-southwest. Down. The seed pulled in her chest like a fishhook set behind her ribs, and every step toward the Hollow Gate loosened something in her that had been screaming since the dream, and every step away from the waystation made the screaming worse again, so she ran, because running was the only position between the two pains that hurt least.

*The Gate,* the seed sang. *The Gate, where you were made, where you must be unmade or opened, little vessel, little bell, come home, come home—*

"I'm not a bell," she gasped, in Vessic, to nobody, to the snow. "I'm Wren."

But even her name felt borrowed tonight. Wren was the word Rosalind had given her on the first morning, pointing at the bird carved on the mantel, and Wren had accepted it the way you accept a coat from a stranger — gratefully, without asking whose it used to be.

Her feet went out from under her on a buried root. She got up. She went down again on ice, and her palms burned, and when she pushed herself upright the wind hit her like a wall of hands and shoved her two full steps sideways, and somewhere in that shove the trail vanished.

Not covered. Vanished. There was no direction anymore. There was only white roaring in every direction, cold working through the thin boots into her toes like water finding cracks in a dam, and the seed still pulling, south-southwest, *this way*, but the seed had never walked on human legs. The seed did not know about snow.

She thought of Rosalind's face in the lantern light, the night she'd taught her the word for *fire*. She thought of Dain pretending not to watch her eat. She thought: *I should turn around.*

She thought: *I can't feel my hands.*

She sat down in the snow, just for a moment, just to rest, and the snow received her like an old friend with terrible intentions, and the last thing she saw before her eyes closed was a shape breaking through the white — big, wrong-angled, roaring something she couldn't hear over the wind —

— and then nothing, which was almost a mercy, because in the nothing there were no bells.

* * *

He found her because he stopped looking with his eyes.

Twelve years on the Thornfell roads taught a man that in a whiteout your eyes are liars and your feet are prophets. So Dain walked with the slope, let the mountain decide, kept the wind quartering across his left cheek the way it would if he drifted south of the waystation, and he talked. Out loud. The whole time. Nonsense, mostly — route talk, cargo talk, the names of every pass from here to the Salt Marches — because a man talking can't hear himself despair, and because of a superstition he'd never admitted to anyone: that lost things sometimes follow voices the way moths follow lamps.

"—and the switchback below Grey Crag is washed out again, always is, every spring, and I've told Rosalind a hundred times you can't trust a road the water owns—"

A darker shape against the white. Small. Half-buried already.

His heart did something complicated and painful, and he dropped to his knees and dug her out with both hands, and she was — gods, she was light. When had she become light? When he'd carried her out of the hollow tree in the first week she'd had weight, substance, a sullen living density. Now she weighed nothing, a bundle of sticks wrapped in a coat, and her lips were the color of slate, and there was frost in her lashes.

"Wren. Hey. Hey." He slapped her cheeks, gently at first, then less gently. Nothing. Her breathing was there but slow, horribly slow, the shallow tide of a body banking its fires. "No. No no no, you don't get to do this. I did not haul myself across half a mountain to lose you to weather. Weather's mine. Nobody dies of weather on my roads. That's the rule."

He got her up against his chest, inside his coat, her cold face in the warm hollow of his throat, and stood, and the wind took his balance and nearly took her out of his arms, and he turned his back to it and began to walk.

He did not know the way back. That was the honest arithmetic of it. In a whiteout, with no stars, with the trail gone, he had perhaps a coin's chance of walking blind back to the waystation and a certainty of freezing if he stood still deciding. So he did the only thing left: he kept the wind on his left cheek and he kept talking, and he prayed — him, Dain Ferro, who hadn't prayed since his mother died — to whatever smuggler's god handled lost children.

"Your lullaby," he said into the wind, teeth chattering. "The one you tried to teach me at the waystation. I butchered it. I know I butchered it. But I'm going to sing it anyway, and if you can hear anything under all that cold, you hear this and you get angry, because I'm singing it *wrong*, and you don't take kindly to that—"

And he sang. Badly. Three notes of a melody whose shape he'd caught wrong, nonsense syllables that weren't Vessic and weren't anything, in a voice like a cart on gravel, into the teeth of the storm.

Halfway through the second verse, the fingers against his throat curled — barely, barely — into his collar.

"There you are," Dain said, and his voice cracked, and he decided the wind had done it. "There you are. Stay angry. Anger's warm."

He walked. The storm walked with him. And then — he never could explain this afterward, and didn't try — the wind shifted, quartered around to his right cheek instead of his left, which meant he'd overshot south, which meant the waystation lay behind his left shoulder, close, close enough that the mountain was giving it back to him. He turned. Twenty steps. Fifty. A hundred, each one a negotiation with his knees.

Then a yellow smudge in the white. Then lantern-glow. Then Rosalind, materializing out of the roar with a rope around her waist and her hand raised, and her palm open toward the storm — and the storm, impossibly, leaning away from it, curling aside around the two of them like water around a stone.

"Storm-caller," Dain rasped, and it was half curse, half prayer.

"Not out here," she said tightly, and he saw what it cost her, the effort showing in the tendons of her neck. "Inside. Now. I'll hold the calm while you move."

* * *

By the time the fire had color in it again, Wren was coughing, and coughing had never sounded so much like music.

Maren worked over her with the brisk hands of someone who has done triage in worse places, forcing melted snow and honey between the girl's lips, muttering archivist's curses under her breath. Rosalind stood at the hearth with her back to the room, and Dain — soaked through, hands split and blue at the knuckles, shaking with the delayed terror he'd refused to feel on the mountain — sat on the floor beside Wren's cot and found that he could not make himself stand up.

"You followed the wind," Rosalind said finally, without turning. "Back. How."

"Lucky."

"Nothing about tonight was lucky. The wind parted for you. I called a calm corridor and I aimed it at *me*, Dain, not at the south slope. Something steered you home."

He looked down at Wren. Her eyes were open now, fever-bright, fixed on him with an expression he couldn't read — until she lifted one hand, weakly, and hummed three notes. His three notes. His mangled, wrong-shaped, cart-on-gravel version of her lullaby.

"That's not how it goes," she whispered, in Common, in her cracked, careful, newly-learned Common — her longest sentence yet, and she'd spent it correcting his singing.

"No," Dain said, and his eyes were stinging, and he blamed the smoke. "It isn't. Teach me again in the morning."

But Wren had gone rigid on the cot, her hand pressed flat against her own sternum, and her whisper, when it came, was not in Common and not entirely in Vessic either, but in the strange braided tongue she used only when the seed spoke through her:

"It wants the Gate. It's pulling harder now. And it says—" She swallowed, and her fevered eyes went from Rosalind to Dain to the storm-shuttered window, and Dain felt the cold come back into the room ahead of her words. "It says we're already late. Because we're not the only ones traveling tonight. The ones in gray robes have reached the Saddle Rocks. And the dead ones are closer than that."
