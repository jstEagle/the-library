## 13. The Court's Invitation

Sereth had not been alive when the bells of the Hollow Court last rang, but he carried their sound the way other men carried scars. It was a low, brazen note that lived beneath his thinking, and it told him, always, what the Court wanted. Tonight it wanted patience.

He crouched on the ridge above the Ironhollow road and watched the caravan make camp in the bowl of an old quarry, thirty bodies and nine wagons ringed by firelight. The smuggler captain moved among them like a man counting his own heartbeats — Sereth knew him now. Dain Ferro. A name from the ledgers of the border wars, a man who had paid the Court's tolls for twenty years without ever asking who collected them. He was good at routes. He was bad at odds. No route survived what came down the mountain tonight.

The snow fell fine and dry, sifting through the dead pines, and beneath it the land here did not feel dead to ordinary eyes. Ordinary eyes saw a quarry, a road, timber. Sereth saw the hollowed places underneath, the seams where the Court's old hunger had drained the world thin, and he walked those seams the way a fisherman reads water. The Court's territory began two miles north. But its attention was already here. It had been here for days, coiled around the child like a hand around a candle flame.

*The seed is awake,* the note beneath his thinking said. *The seed is dreaming.*

He could feel it even from the ridge: a faint wrongness in the air above the fires, as though the flames were burning slightly out of true. The girl sat between the archivist and the storm-caller, wrapped in a blanket too large for her, her small hands cupped around a tin mug she wasn't drinking from. Her lips were moving. Even at this distance, with ears no living throat could match, Sereth caught the shape of it. Vessic. The dead tongue, spoken softly into the steam, like a prayer, like a report.

She was telling them something. Or it was telling them something, using her mouth while she slept on her feet.

Sereth let out a breath he did not need, and watched it hang silver in the cold.

Below, the storm-caller stood up. Rosalind Sterling. He had read her file in the Court's archive of the unforgiven — a thin file, thinner than it should have been, and he had wondered about that even then. Ten years of exile, ten years of silence from a woman whose order's records claimed she had once called lightning across three valleys in a single night. Files that thin were files that had been edited. Edited files meant someone above him was managing the truth, and managed truth was the one thing the Hollow Court existed to unmake.

Tonight he would learn why they wanted the child delivered *willingly*. He still didn't understand it. The Court's oldest instruments — himself chief among them — had been made to take. That was the whole grammar of the Hollow: reach, grasp, drain. And yet the voice that spoke through the bells had been explicit, thrice repeated, in words so old they predated his making.

*It must choose us. It must be given. What is seized will curdle; what is offered will open.*

Sereth had served the Court for a hundred and forty years and had never once been asked to ask permission.

* * *

They came down at moonrise, when the fires burned lowest.

There were eleven of them, which was generous. Wraiths mostly — the hollow-eyed footmen of the Court's outer court, gray as ash and quiet as falling snow — and Sereth at their head wearing the body he wore when he wished to be spoken to rather than fled from: a tall man in a long dark coat, frost in his hair that never melted, a face pleasant enough to pass in a market if you didn't look at the eyes. He had chosen the form deliberately. This night required manners.

The sentry saw them at forty yards, which was further than any living man should have seen wraiths moving. To his credit, the boy didn't run. He banged the iron triangle hanging by the fire, and the quarry erupted — men rolling from bedrolls, knives out, Dain Ferro stepping in front of the wagons with a pistol already levelled.

"Stand," Sereth said, and his voice carried the way cold carries, into everything, under everything. "We are not here for blood. If we were, you would already be gone."

Dain Ferro looked at him for a long moment, and Sereth watched the smuggler do arithmetic — count the wraiths, count his people, weigh the pistol in his hand against the futility of it. Twenty years of paying tolls had taught him something about collectors. His arm lowered by degrees, though he kept the pistol up, angled away, a courtesy and a threat at once.

"What do you want?"

"The same thing you want," Sereth said. "To see the child safe." He stepped closer, into the firelight, and let them all see him plainly. Somewhere behind the archivist, the girl Wren had risen to her feet, and Sereth felt the seed notice him — a small cold bloom of attention turning his way like an eye opening. "May I approach? I would rather this be conversation than siege."

"You'd rather," Ferro said.

"I have been given instructions," Sereth said, "and I confess they are stranger than anything I have carried in a century of service. I am to deliver an invitation. Not a demand. An invitation."

Rosalind Sterling came forward then, past Ferro's outstretched arm, and stopped an arm's length away. Up close she was weathered and wary, gray threading her dark hair, and her eyes had the flat calm of someone who had decided exactly how much fear she could afford. She had called a storm at the Saddle Rocks — he had felt it from miles off, felt the sky answer her like an old hound answering a whistle it thought had been forgotten. The sky remembered her. That was worth noting.

"Say it, then," she said.

Sereth inclined his head. "The Hollow Gate stands six days north of here, at the root of the Vale of Bells. The Court invites the bearer of the memory-seed to come there willingly, under safe passage guaranteed by the Court itself — safe from our agents, safe from your Order, safe from every instrument either of us commands. Bring the seed to the Gate, and the Court will permit the child to walk away whole."

"And if we don't come?" Sterling said.

"Then the Court comes for it. Not tonight. Not next week. But before the seed blooms fully, and when we come, we will not be eleven." He paused. "And we will take the child as well, because a seed torn from its bearer kills the bearer, and the Court has learned — slowly, over centuries — to regret waste."

Behind Sterling, the archivist Maren Kestrel made a small sound, quickly swallowed. So. She understood the grammar of it. Good. One of them did.

"That's not an invitation," Ferro said flatly. "That's a knife with better manners."

"It is both," Sereth agreed, because lying to these people would serve nothing. "I will tell you what I can, since you will learn it anyway. The Court has wanted the truth of the Hollow War spoken aloud for three hundred years. Not avenged — *spoken*. There is a difference, and the difference is the reason for this theater of courtesy. The Court believes — my masters believe — that a truth taken by force becomes a lie. That a witness dragged to the gate testifies to nothing but her own terror. The seed must open freely or what it reveals will curdle into just another weapon, another grievance, another war. They would rather wait a generation than seize it and lose everything."

"And I'm supposed to believe the hungry dead have developed scruples," Sterling said.

"No," Sereth said. "You're supposed to believe me, and I am the only one of us telling you the truth tonight. Your Order sent a High Cantor under a flag of truce. Ask her what the truce was covering. We, at least, are honest about wanting what we want."

The wind moved through the quarry, lifting ash from the fires, and in that wind Sereth heard — faint, far, at the very floor of hearing — the bells again. The Court listening through him. He kept his face still.

Sterling studied him for a long moment. "Why does consent matter so much to a thing like you?"

And there it was — the question he had carried up the mountain unanswered, the question the bells had declined to explain. He considered lying, found no profit in it, and gave her the only honesty he had.

"I don't know," he said. "In a hundred and forty years of service, I have never been sent to ask before. Whatever waits inside the Gate wants to be *given* something, and my masters are afraid — genuinely afraid, in whatever way such beings are afraid — of what happens if it is taken instead. That should frighten you more than I do. It frightens me, and I am difficult to frighten."

The girl spoke then.

Her voice was small and clear and it went through the quarry like a bell of its own. She spoke in Vessic, of course, but Sereth heard the meaning the way the seed intended it to be heard, and he saw Sterling flinch as half of it landed in her too:

*"I know what's in the Gate. It dreams about me. It says it's sorry."*

Silence. The fires cracked. Ferro's pistol had drifted up again without anyone noticing, and now hung pointed at the ground between them, forgotten.

Sereth knelt — slowly, hands visible, an old courtesy from a court that had forgotten its own manners — so that his eyes were level with the child's. Up close, the seed's light was visible through her pupils if one knew how to look: two faint silver rings, like moons behind water.

"When you arrive," he said quietly, in careful, accented Vessic, "no one will touch you. No one will speak to you until you speak first. The Court swears this by the bells themselves, and the bells do not forgive perjury. Whatever is asked of you, child, will be asked, never demanded. Do you understand the difference?"

She looked at him for a long time with eyes far older than her years, and then nodded once.

He rose. "Six days," he said to Sterling. "The passes north of the Saddle will be open. Your Order's patrols will not be — that much protection we can give. After the sixth day, the invitation expires, and what follows will not be an invitation." He reached into his coat and produced a small object: a bell, no larger than a thumb, cast in dull gray metal that drank the firelight. He set it on a flat stone between them, well away from any hand that might snatch it. "Ring it if you are pursued. Ring it if you change your mind. It carries only one direction."

Ferro stared at the bell as though it might bite. "Why give us a leash to your own masters?"

"It isn't a leash," Sereth said. "It's a door. There's a difference, Captain. You of all people should appreciate doors." He turned, then paused, and looked back at Sterling one last time. "A word of advice, storm-caller, free of charge, because I suspect you will need it. The Cantor who came to you under truce — whatever she told you about your missing memories, verify it twice. The Order edits files. I have read yours. It is the thinnest file in the archive of the unforgiven, and I promise you, no one earns exile that cheaply."

Then he turned, and the wraiths folded around him like smoke closing over a lantern, and the eleven of them went up out of the quarry into the falling snow, and the seam-lines of the hollowed land received them, and were still.

* * *

From the ridge, an hour later, Sereth watched the fires burn on below and waited for the report to leave him.

It went the way reports always went: not in words but in pressure, a tightening behind his sternum, a sense of being listened to from somewhere vast and patient and far beneath the roots of the mountains. He closed his eyes and let the Court read the night through him — the quarry, the child, the seed's silver attention, the storm-caller's guarded face, the smuggler's arithmetic, the archivist's swallowed sound.

When it released him, the note beneath his thinking had changed key.

*She asked why consent matters.* A pause, like a held breath across centuries. *Because it mattered to Them. Because the Gate was built to open only from the inside.*

Sereth stood motionless in the snow. In a hundred and forty years, the Court had told him almost nothing. Now, unprompted, unpurchased, it had given him a piece of the architecture of the world.

*Who built it?* he asked, into the dark behind his ribs.

The silence that answered was worse than any refusal. It was the silence of grief.

Below, the fires were being banked for the night. He saw the storm-caller pick up the gray bell from the stone, turn it over twice, and close her fist around it — not hiding it, he noted, but holding it, the way one holds a key to a room one dreads entering. He saw the girl watching him from beside the wagon, small and sleepless, and he raised one hand — farewell, or apology, or warning; he honestly no longer knew which — and she raised hers in answer, a child's solemn mirror.

Then he turned north, toward the Vale of Bells, where something old and sorrowful waited behind a door built to open only from the inside, and began to walk.

Six days, he thought. Enough time for the Order to find them. Enough time for the Cantor's truce to show its teeth. Enough time for the storm-caller to decide whether the enemy offering safe passage was more honest than the family that had exiled her.

Enough time, perhaps, for the child to dream again — and Sereth had heard enough of what lived in those dreams to pity whatever woke her.

He walked, and the snow filled his tracks behind him, and far to the south a signal fire bloomed briefly on a peak he recognized: Order watchers, marking the caravan's position. So. The truce was already fraying.

Good, he thought, with something that in a living man might have been satisfaction. Let them see how many doors close tonight. Let them chase a choice they cannot bear to allow.

Let the girl choose.
