## 14. Maren's Confession

The lamplight in Maren Kestrel's cellar did something strange to her face. Rosalind had noticed it the night before, when they'd arrived half-dead through the postern door of the archivist's cluttered rooms beneath Ironhollow's old assaying house, and she was noticing it again now: at certain angles, the deep lines of the woman's face seemed not to deepen but to *smooth*, as if age were a garment she wore loosely and could, if she chose, set aside.

She had thought it was exhaustion. Her own, mostly. Three days from the Saddle Rocks with a child who screamed in a dead language in her sleep and a smuggler captain who kept checking the window slats like a man expecting the mountains themselves to come for him — Rosalind had little attention to spare for anyone else's face.

But this morning Wren was asleep upstairs under Dain's watch, fed on honey porridge and finally, blessedly quiet, and Rosalind sat across a table heaped with paper from a woman who would not stop looking at the girl's door.

"You knew," Rosalind said. "Before we came. You knew what she was carrying."

Maren turned her eyes from the stairs. They were gray eyes, unremarkable except for their stillness. "Sit down, Rosalind."

"I've been sitting down all morning. I want an answer."

"Then let me give you all of them." Maren drew a candle closer, and its flame bent, though there was no draft, bending toward her hand as if it recognized something. "You asked me yesterday how I came to study the seed ritual. I told you I found fragments in the archives at Vel Sarath before it burned. That was true, as far as it went. But it didn't go far enough."

"Go further, then."

Maren folded her hands on the table. Rosalind watched them — long fingers, ink-stained at the nails, a scar across the left knuckle shaped like a crescent moon — and felt the first cold touch of understanding move up her spine, because she had seen that scar before. In the vault beneath the Thornfell citadel, on a marble relief three centuries old: a line of priests with hands raised over a stone cradle, and one of them, the fourth from the altar, with a crescent-shaped flaw in the carved knuckle where a mason's chisel had slipped.

No. Not a slip. She had stood in that vault two weeks ago with her lantern shaking and read the names of nine priests, and she had thought her own mind was playing tricks when the fourth figure's hand seemed familiar. Stone did not keep scars. Living hands did.

"How old are you?" Rosalind asked quietly.

Maren smiled, and it was the saddest expression Rosalind had ever seen on a living face. "Older than the Hollow War. Older than Vessic's burning. I was born in a village whose name is now a boundary marker between two kingdoms that didn't exist when I was a girl in it."

The lamp guttered. Rosalind realized she had stopped breathing and made herself start again.

"That's not possible. The rites for extended life are—"

"—exhausting, dangerous, and forbidden, yes. And I never used them." Maren's voice stayed level, a lecturer's voice, and Rosalind understood dimly that the levelness was itself an effort of centuries' practice. "I simply failed to die when it was my turn. There are souls that the world doesn't fully take back, Rosalind. Rare ones. The Order has hunted them for ages out of doctrine, and the Court made them once, deliberately, out of hunger — your revenant friend Sereth is what happens when you try to manufacture what I am by accident. I have lived three hundred and eleven years. I helped build the thing sleeping inside that child."

* * *

The words hung in the cellar air among the hanging bundles of dried herbs, the shelves of ledgers, the smell of lamp oil and dust and something older underneath, a smell like a tomb that had been kept clean.

Rosalind found her voice somewhere below her ribs. "Say the rest."

Maren rose, crossed to a shelf, and pulled a ledger bound in gray leather gone soft as cloth. She set it on the table between them like a confession already written.

"The Hollow War did not begin the way any book says it began. Not the Order's books, not the kingdoms'. The atrocity at Vessic's Heart — the burning of the sanctuaries, the nine thousand souls — that happened. But it wasn't madness, and it wasn't the Vessic summoning what they couldn't hold, whatever the Cantors teach. It was policy. A joint act. The old kings wanted the Vessic memory-holds broken because a people who remember everything cannot be conquered quietly, and the Order wanted—"

Maren stopped. Her thumb moved along the ledger's spine.

"What did the Order want?" Rosalind asked.

"To be needed forever." The answer came flat and immediate, worn smooth by repetition. "A war gives an order purpose. Peace makes it furniture. The High Cantor of that era — Ilvane's predecessor in everything but name — agreed to help the kings commit the atrocity and shape the blame, so long as the Order emerged as the only authority on memory, on truth, on the past itself. Nine thousand dead so that a handful of men could remain arbiters of what happened."

Rosalind thought of Ilvane's face in the truce tent, the gentle tilt of her head, *you were always my best student.* Her stomach turned over slowly, like something shifting in deep water.

"And the seed?"

"Some of the Vessic priests saw it coming. They couldn't stop the burning — no one could — but they could make sure it could never be denied. They took the complete memory of that night, every detail, every scream, every order given and by whom, and they bound it into a seed. Living recollection. Undeniable, unerasable, self-sustaining. Evidence that walks and breathes and grows." Maren opened the ledger. The pages were covered in diagrams Rosalind half-recognized from the vault — the ritual circles, the anchoring lattice — but these were working papers, annotated in a cramped hand, corrected, cursed at, wept over, if ink could weep. "They needed someone to design the vessel that would keep the seed dormant until the world was ready to hear it. Someone skilled in binding, and someone who would live long enough to see it through. They came to me because I was both."

"You designed the hiding ritual."

"I designed all of it." Maren's voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture in old stone. "The dormancy lattice. The carrier-bonding. The way it passes from host to host when a host fails. Every child who has carried this seed for three hundred years carried it in a structure I built. Most of them lived quiet lives and died quiet deaths and passed it on unknowing. Some suffered. I have their names. I say them every year on the anniversary of the burning, all four hundred and eleven of them, and it takes most of a night."

Rosalind sat very still. The lamp flame had steadied again; it leaned toward Maren continuously now, like a plant toward sun.

"Why are you telling me this? You had ten years of me running contraband past your window to tell me. Why now?"

"Because until you walked into my cellar with that girl upstairs, the seed had never woken early. Never bloomed ahead of schedule. Something changed — the Court's resurrection, perhaps, or the Order's recent digging, resonance seeking resonance. Whatever the cause, the seed is opening in Wren faster than any host before her, and an open seed does two things, Rosalind. It shows the truth to whoever stands before it. And it burns out the vessel carrying it."

"Burns out how?"

Maren looked at her with an expression of terrible patience, the look of a woman who had rehearsed this moment for longer than Rosalind had been alive and had hoped never to perform it.

"A memory-seed is not a book, child. It isn't information. It's experience, held at full weight. Nine thousand deaths. The heat of the fire. The sound. The certainty of every dying soul that the world had abandoned them. When it blooms fully in a mind, that mind must either become vast enough to hold it—" she spread her hands, indicating three centuries of herself, "—or it must break. Wren is seven years old. She will not break cleanly. She will shatter, and the seed will scatter with her, and scattered, it can be claimed. By the Court. By the Order. By whichever hungry thing arrives first at the pieces."

* * *

Rosalind stood. She hadn't decided to stand; her body had simply refused to keep sitting under the weight of it. She walked to the far wall, where Maren had pinned maps of Thornfell, and stared at them without seeing them, and finally turned around.

"There's another way. There's always another way. You built the thing — you can unbuild it."

"Yes," Maren said. "It can be destroyed."

Relief surged up Rosalind's throat like a shout — and then she saw Maren's face, and the shout died somewhere behind her teeth.

"But," Rosalind said.

"The seed requires an anchor. Always has. The dormancy lattice I built holds it in stasis, but destruction isn't stasis — you cannot simply cut it. A seed of living memory must be *unmade*, and unmaking means taking its whole weight into a single mind at the moment of dissolution, so that the memory has somewhere coherent to end. An anchor must receive all of it. Every death. Every scream. All at once, willingly, held steady while it comes apart." Maren closed the ledger with a soft sound like a coffin lid. "No human mind can do that and survive. Mine might. I have considered it. I have had three hundred years to consider it."

"Then you'll do it," Rosalind said quickly. "That's the answer. You anchor it, you destroy it, Wren goes free—"

"It can't be me."

The words landed like a dropped stone.

"Why not?" Rosalind heard her own voice go thin and hard. "You just said—"

"Because the rite requires an anchor who is *given* the seed, not one who takes it. That was built into the design — my design — precisely to prevent what you're suggesting. The priests feared a thief seizing the seed and holding it hostage, so they bound it to consent. It must be offered freely by its current bearer, and accepted freely by its anchor, in the same act, at the same gate. Wren can offer. I can accept. But I cannot force the transfer, and I cannot accept secondhand through another ritual. The chain must be direct." Maren's gray eyes were dry, and somehow that was worse than tears. "And there is one more condition, and I need you to hear all of it before you speak."

Rosalind waited.

"The anchor must be someone the seed recognizes. Memory answers to kinship — that's why hosts pass it bloodline to bloodline, household to household, why it woke in a Vessic-speaking child. At the Hollow Gate, with the bloom at its height, the seed will choose whom it will trust enough to dissolve into. Three hundred years ago it would have chosen me; I helped make it, it knows my signature. But I have hidden from it since. I have masked myself, decade after decade, so the Court could never use me to find it. To it, I am a stranger wearing a familiar face. It may refuse me. If it refuses me mid-rite, everyone present dies and the seed scatters." She paused. "There is one person alive it already knows. One person whose signature is woven into its making, recorded in the ninth circle of the binding, named among the priests."

The cellar seemed to tilt. Rosalind gripped the edge of the table.

"No."

"You found your name in the vault," Maren said gently. "Among the nine. You told me yourself you remembered none of it. Rosalind — the seed knows you. Whatever was done to your memory, whoever wrote your exile into your own mind, the seed's lattice holds the record of your part in its creation. It will accept you."

"I was a child. I'm thirty-four years old. The war ended three centuries before I was born. My name can't be—"

"Your name is there. I read it when your letter reached me on the north road. I knew it before that, when Dain's runners first whispered about a storm-caller hermit and a mute girl speaking Vessic. I knew, and I let you come anyway, because I am a coward who has spent three centuries hoping someone else would carry this to the end." Maren's composure broke at last, all at once, like ice letting go of a roof — and beneath it Rosalind saw not a monster but something exhausted beyond the reach of ordinary grief. "I am sorry. I am sorry for every year of it. But you asked me for the cost, and here it is, named aloud as you deserve to hear it: the seed can be destroyed, and the destruction will kill whoever anchors it, and the only person it will reliably accept is you."

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. Above them, floorboards creaked — Dain pacing with Wren, or Wren pacing with Dain; lately it was hard to say which of them soothed the other. Somewhere out in Ironhollow, the mine bells rang the third hour, and the sound traveled down through rock and soil into the cellar like a heartbeat from the bones of the mountain.

"If I do nothing," Rosalind said at last, and her voice came out strangely calm, the calm of a woman reading her own death warrant for errors, "the seed blooms in Wren and kills her."

"Within weeks. Perhaps days, now. She screamed last night for an hour. You heard the words."

"I heard them." She had. *Ash on the water. The doors were barred from outside.* Phrases she had no right to understand, arriving whole in her mind.

"And if the Order takes her, they erase the seed and everyone who has heard of it — you, Dain, me, likely every soul in Ironhollow who saw you arrive. If the Court takes her, they claim the truth as a weapon and start the war again with three centuries of grievance poured into the opening blow. And if the seed scatters—" Maren shook her head. "Scattered memory doesn't die, Rosalind. It haunts. Whole regions of the Hollow lands are haunted by less."

"So the choices are: the child dies, I die, or the world burns." Rosalind laughed, short and ugly. "Ten years I've told myself I survived exile by never letting anyone put me between a hammer and an anvil. And here you are offering me both at once."

"I'm offering you a third thing too." Maren came around the table, and up close the strangeness of her face resolved at last — not age smoothing away, Rosalind saw now, but *time* pooled in her, standing still, a lake that had forgotten how to flow. "I'm offering you the truth entire. Before you decide anything, I will show you the ninth circle of the binding. Your name, your signature, whatever the Order planted in your memory and cut away. You deserve to know exactly what was taken from you before you choose whether to spend what remains."

"And if what I find tells me I really was one of them? That I'm not some innocent they framed, but a priestess of theirs grown new memories over an old crime?"

"Then you'll know," Maren said simply. "You've been walking ten years on a floor you suspected was rotten. I'm handing you the plank. What you build from it is yours."

From above came a small thump, then Dain's voice, low and rough, saying something Rosalind couldn't make out — and then Wren's voice answering, clear and high, in Vessic, and then, haltingly, in the common tongue: *"Bad dream. Fire. But also... home. The dream says home."*

Rosalind closed her eyes.

"Show me the ninth circle," she said. "Tonight. Before I lose my nerve, and before that child loses anything more to the thing inside her."

Maren bowed her head — the first true bow Rosalind had ever seen her give, deep and old-fashioned, a gesture from a century with no surviving portraits. Then she lifted the lamp and led the way toward the deepest shelf, where the shadows gathered thickest, and as Rosalind followed, she felt the mountain holding its breath around them, and beneath her feet, far below the mines and the older tunnels and the buried things even miners wouldn't name, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.
