## 15. Night at the Waystation

### DAIN

The waystation at Kell's Rest had not sheltered travelers in forty years, and it showed. The roof sagged like a tired back, the hearth was a black throat choked with old ash, and something — probably marten, possibly worse — had made a kingdom of the loft. But the walls stood against the wind, and after three days of the high passes, standing walls were luxury enough.

Dain Ferro set his lantern on the table and watched the light catch the four of them: Rosalind shaking snow from her braid like a wet dog, Maren Kestrel already cataloguing the room with those pale archivist's eyes, and Wren — small Wren, hollow-cheeked Wren — standing just inside the door as if she expected it to be taken away from her.

"Home," he said, and meant it as a joke.

It didn't land. It never landed with this crowd anymore. There had been a time when his caravans ran loud — singing teamsters, dice games by firelight, Rosalind herself laughing once or twice a season when she thought no one was watching. Now they moved like people carrying water across ice: careful, silent, waiting for it to crack.

He got to work anyway. Work was what he knew how to give.

The stove needed kindling, and there was kindling — the waystation's own broken shutters, split into splinters with his hatchet while Maren cleared the hearth of a decade of bird nests. He got a fire going that smoked for five ugly minutes before the flue remembered its business, and then it caught, and the heat came off it in waves, and Wren crossed the floor in three small steps and held her hands out to it like a woman greeting the sun.

Dain's chest did something complicated. He ignored it. He was good at ignoring it.

"Rabbit," he announced, hauling the carcass from his pack. "Shot this morning before the fog came down. I know none of you said grace in your lives, but I'm saying it now, so bow your heads."

Maren bowed hers with grave irony. Rosalind didn't, but she looked over, and there was something almost soft in her face — the ghost of the woman who'd guided his first run through Thornfell, twelve years back, who'd charged him double because he was green and then given half of it back because his grandmother's medicine had saved his brother.

Wren tilted her head at him, puzzled, and he realized she was waiting for translation. Rosalind murmured something low in Vessic, and the girl's solemn little face cracked into a smile, and she bowed her head so deeply her chin nearly hit her chest.

"That's not what I said," Dain protested.

"It's close enough," Rosalind said.

* * *

They ate. It shouldn't have mattered, sitting on broken crates around a dented stove eating rabbit and the last of Dain's hardtack softened in melted snow, but it mattered. He could see it mattering to all of them. Rosalind ate slowly, deliberately, the way soldiers eat before battle. Maren ate like a scholar reading a text — every bite considered, annotated. And Wren ate like a child who had known hunger recently enough to be grateful for each mouthful without being ruled by it anymore, which was its own quiet miracle.

Afterward, Dain dug to the bottom of his pack and came up with the treasure he'd been sitting on for two days: a clay jar of plum preserves, bought from a farmwife in the foothills for an outrageous price, wrapped in three shirts to keep it safe.

"Last one," he said. "Save it for the Gate?"

"No," Rosalind said.

"Yes," said Maren, at the same moment.

They looked at each other. Then Rosalind shrugged, and Maren opened the jar, and they passed it around with one spoon, and nobody spoke about tomorrow, and the plums were dark and sweet and tasted like a world where the Hollow Gate was just a name on a map.

Later, when the fire had settled and Rosalind took first watch by the shuttered window and Maren curled up near the stove with a book she pretended wasn't written in a dead language, Dain sat beside Wren on the bench, and she leaned against his arm without asking, the way she'd begun doing somewhere on the third day out, casual as breathing.

He let her. He'd have let her lean there until spring.

"You know I'm not the fatherly type," he told her quietly, in case any part of her believed otherwise. "Ask anyone. Ask my crew. Ferro's heart is a ledger, that's what they say. Everything entered, nothing given away."

Wren looked up at him. She reached out and pressed one finger flat against his chest, right over the complicated thing his chest kept doing, and held it there.

"Aye," he said gruffly. "That's the ledger. Don't go telling tales."

She smiled, closed her eyes, and stayed.

### WREN

Warmth first. Warmth was new, still, even now — warmth was a gift that arrived and might leave. Wren kept her side pressed to the big man's arm because his warmth was steady as a wall, and the fire painted orange ghosts on the ceiling, and for a little while the thing inside her was quiet.

Not gone. Never gone. It breathed in her like a second heartbeat, slow and vast, turning over in its sleep. Some nights it dreamed, and when it dreamed, she dreamed with it, and the dreams were not hers: banners burning in rain, a bell tolling with no tongue, a valley full of people kneeling and the sky above them the wrong color, a color that hurt to remember. She had learned to tell the difference between her own dreams and its. Hers had her mother's kitchen in them. Its had ash.

But tonight the fire was warm and the plum taste was still in her mouth, sweet and strange, and the seed-thing only turned and turned like a cat in sunlight.

The woman with white in her hair — Maren — sat by the stove with a book. Wren knew books were dangerous; the priests who had hidden her inside herself had feared them, or loved them, both. But this book the woman read aloud sometimes, under her breath, in Vessic, and hearing the language was like putting her hand in cold river water: shocking, clean, *hers*.

Wren slid off the bench. Maren glanced up, and Wren pointed at the page.

"This?" Maren said. "It's a hymnal. Songs for the turning of the year." Her voice in Vessic was accented, careful, an old scar of an accent, but real. "Would you like me to read you one?"

Wren shook her head. She touched her own throat. Then she pointed at the book again.

Maren understood. Her eyes went soft and sad in that way grown-ups' eyes went soft when they understood too much. "You want to sing it yourself."

Wren nodded.

She chose the song by feel, running her finger down the page until the shapes under her finger seemed to hum. The lullaby. The one the priests used to hum when they thought she was asleep, the one that lived deeper than the seed, in the place that was only hers. She climbed up onto the crate beside Maren's chair so everyone could see, because some things should be witnessed, and she folded her hands the way the old priest Ossa used to fold his, and she sang.

The words came out of her small and clear:

*Sleep, the mountain keeps its count.*
*Sleep, the river carries names.*
*What was taken will be spoken.*
*What was buried waits for rain.*

She didn't know all the verses — the priests had sung only fragments, guarding the whole — but the melody carried her, and where the words failed she hummed, and the humming filled the gaps like mortar fills stone. When she finished, the room was very quiet. The fire popped. Rosalind had turned from the window and was watching her with an expression Wren couldn't read, something between grief and recognition.

Then the big man — Dain — cleared his throat and said, in his terrible, butchered, wonderful accent: "*Sleeeep, the mountain keeps its count.*"

He had been listening. He had memorized one line. His vowels were crimes against the language and he knew it, and he sang it anyway, twice, until Wren laughed — actually laughed, the sound surprising her more than anyone — and corrected him, taking his hand and shaping the word against his palm like she was teaching a very large, very foolish child.

"*Kep,*" she said. "*Not keep.*"

"*Kep,*" Dain repeated, gravely. "The mountain keeps its count. Aye. That'll do."

They sang it through together, four times, and each time the seed inside her turned over gently and did not wake, and Wren thought: *this is what we were, before. People teaching each other songs by firelight. This is what it is for.*

She fell asleep mid-fifth verse, leaning against Maren's shoulder, and dreamed of nothing at all, which was the kindest dream there was.

* * *

### MAREN

Two hundred and eleven years, and Maren Kestrel still could not sleep well indoors. Habit outlived bodies. She lay on the pallet near the stove with her eyes closed and listened to the waystation breathe: Rosalind's measured pacing at the window, the settle and tick of cooling iron, the child's breath going long and even against her shoulder until Rosalind carried her, gently as lifting an ember, to the blankets by the fire.

When she was sure the others slept, Maren opened her eyes.

The hymnal lay where Wren had left it. Maren picked it up and held it a while without opening it, the way one holds an old letter from someone dead. She had helped bind books like this one. She had helped bind other things, less innocent. The ritual that hid the seed inside a living child — she had drafted its central verse, in a century when she still believed truth could be safely buried like a sword in a stone, drawn only by the worthy. She had been wrong about swords ever since. She intended to be right about this one, whatever it cost, and she had a fair idea now of what it would cost.

*Ros*, she thought. *Ros, who does not yet believe me about the anchoring. Who thinks I am being dramatic, or fatalistic, or testing her nerve.*

Maren was none of those things. Maren had done the arithmetic centuries ago and checked it twice.

She rose quietly, went to the window, and stood beside Rosalind in the dark. Outside, the storm had spent itself; the sky was scoured clean, brutally full of stars, and below the waystation the pass fell away in white terraces toward the black line of the Hollow lands.

"She has your stubbornness," Maren murmured. "And you've never met before this winter. Strange, how souls find their shapes."

"She has her own stubbornness," Rosalind said. "I'm just borrowing it till she grows into it." A pause. Snow-light moved across her face. "Maren. Tomorrow, when we cross into Court ground — the echoes. You said they walk the landscape. Things the Court keeps. Whose memories are they? Really?"

Maren considered lying, decided against it — she had done enough of that for ten lifetimes — and said, "Everyone the Court ever hollowed. Every soldier who walked in and forgot why. Every priest. Every king's man. They don't keep prisoners, Rosalind. They keep *witnesses*. Empty ones. The Court's great crime and its great grief are the same act: they cannot forget, so they make others carry the forgetting."

"And mine?" Rosalind's voice was level, carefully level, the voice of a woman holding a rope over a drop. "If my memories were taken — if Ilvane did what you think she did — will one of those echoes be wearing my face?"

"I don't know," Maren said honestly. "But if it is, remember what Sereth told us at the ford. The Court wants consent. They want the seed brought willingly. Whatever walks those hills wants to talk to you far more than it wants to hurt you."

"That's somehow worse," Rosalind said.

"Yes," Maren agreed. "It always is."

They stood together a while longer. Below them, far off at the edge of sight, a light appeared in the black country — small, gold, steady. Not a campfire. Campfires flickered. This burned like a lamp in a window, patient, placed there on purpose.

Maren felt Rosalind go very still.

"You see it," Maren said quietly.

"I see it."

"The Gate knows we're coming. It's been knowing since the ford." Maren turned from the window and looked back at the sleeping room — Dain sprawled and snoring, one arm hanging off the bench; Wren curled small as a comma; the hymnal closed in her hands. "So we rest. All of us. Because tomorrow we stop running toward it or away from it, and start walking in."

In the corner, without waking, Wren began to hum the lullaby in her sleep — *what was taken will be spoken* — and outside, far down in the Hollow lands, the little golden light began, slowly, to move toward them.
