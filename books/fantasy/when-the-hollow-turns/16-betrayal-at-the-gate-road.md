## 16. Betrayal at the Gate Road

They made the Gate Road by midmorning, and Rosalind knew before she saw them that something was wrong.

She had spent ten years reading mountains the way other people read faces, and the mountain was holding its breath. No birdsong from the scree slopes. No wind in the black pines that crowded the road's northern edge. Even Wren felt it — the girl had gone quiet an hour past, her small hand pressed flat against Rosalind's back where she rode behind her on the pack pony, as if she could hear through her palm what Rosalind heard through the soles of her boots.

*Storms gathering,* Rosalind thought. *But not mine.*

Maren Kestrel walked beside the pony with her hood down, gray hair loose in the cold. She had been silent since dawn, and when she finally spoke it was barely above a whisper.

"They're here already."

"Who?"

"Does it matter? Someone is." Maren's eyes, old beyond any accounting of years Rosalind had yet managed to wring out of her, flicked along the ridgeline. "I told you the Order would not let a truce stand. Truces are for people who intend to meet again."

Ahead of them, Dain Ferro reined up his mule and raised a fist — the old caravan signal, *hold and listen*. He sat motionless a long moment, a broad shape against the pale sky, his weathered face tilted toward the bend in the road where it narrowed between two shoulders of granite.

Then he laughed. Short, bitter, without humor.

"Well," he called back over his shoulder. "There's your flag of truce, Roz. Flying over forty crossbows."

Rosalind urged the pony forward and saw it: the road ahead blocked by a line of storm-callers in their slate-gray field cloaks, hoods back, faces bare in the manner of parley. Above them, on a pole planted in the frozen mud, hung a white banner worked with the silver spiral of the Citadel. And before the line, hands folded, expression gentle as a grandmother's, stood High Cantor Ilvane.

She looked exactly as she had ten years ago, standing in the Hall of Judgments while they read out Rosalind's crimes. Tall, silver-streaked, her voice like warm water poured over broken glass. Time had not touched her. The Order's arts were good for many things; apparently longevity was one of them.

"Rosalind," Ilvane said, and her voice carried easily across the still air without effort or amplification, the way only a master storm-caller's could. "Thank the winds. We feared you'd lost your way."

"You feared nothing," Rosalind said. She slid down from the pony and put her body between the line and Wren, who had gone rigid, her breath coming fast and shallow through her nose. "You don't fear. You plan. That was always the difference between us."

Ilvane sighed, a teacher's sigh, patient and disappointed all at once. "Ten years in the wilderness and you've learned only suspicion."

"I learned it from you."

Behind her, she heard Maren murmur, low and urgent: "Count the crossbows. Count the flanking positions. She hasn't posted sentries behind us because she doesn't think we'll run. She thinks we'll come to heel."

"She always did," Rosalind said.

Dain swung down from his mule and came to stand at her left shoulder, arms crossed, thumbs hooked in his belt — the posture of a man at ease, though Rosalind had seen him gut a man from that exact stance. His eyes never stopped moving. "High Cantor," he drawled. "Your truce banner's crooked. Want me to fix it?"

"Dain Ferro." Ilvane's gaze slid over him the way one looks at furniture. "Still selling other people's roads. I'd have thought the Order's pardon for your smuggling would have bought better manners."

"It bought nothing. I never asked for it." Dain smiled with all the warmth of a drawn blade. "But it's kind of you to confirm you've been watching my caravans for a decade. Very thorough. Very flattering."

Ilvane ignored him. Her attention settled on Rosalind again, and there it was — the softness, the coaxing note Rosalind had once mistaken for love. Perhaps it had been love, once, in whatever way a woman like Ilvane could afford to love anything: as an asset. As a promise of future use.

"Child," Ilvane said, pitching her voice past Rosalind entirely, toward the pony. Toward Wren. "Come away from that woman. You're among friends. We've come a long way to keep you safe."

Wren made a sound — not a word, not quite a scream, but something between. Her fingers dug into Rosalind's coat. The temperature around them dropped three degrees in a heartbeat, frost feathering out across the mud in a perfect ring, and Rosalind felt it: the seed stirring inside the child, responding to her terror the way a struck bell responds to a hand.

*Careful,* Rosalind thought wildly. *Careful, little one. Not here. Not yet.*

"She doesn't understand you," Rosalind said. "And even if she did, she wouldn't come. Look at her, Ilvane. Really look. You call yourself her protector and you can't see that she's terrified of exactly one thing in this world, and it's wearing your face right now."

Something moved behind Ilvane's composure — brief, quickly smoothed. Regret, maybe. Or irritation at a tool that had grown opinions.

"The seed is waking," Ilvane said quietly. "Maren has told you as much. Every day it blooms further, and every day it becomes more dangerous to the child carrying it — and more dangerous to everyone within a hundred leagues. The rite to unmake it requires facilities we alone possess. Sanctified ground. Anchoring lattices built before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath." She spread her hands. "We are not your enemy, Rosalind. We are the only ones equipped to save her."

"And afterward?" Rosalind's voice came out flat. "After the rite. After the seed is erased. What happens to what's inside it?"

The pause lasted half a heartbeat. To anyone else it would have been invisible. To Rosalind, who had spent a decade learning to survive by noticing what powerful people declined to say, it might as well have been shouted from the peaks.

"What's inside it is horror," Ilvane said. "Atrocity. Three centuries of grief compressed into a single living wound. It serves no one. It saves no one. It can only hurt."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only answer there is." Ilvane took a step forward, and the line of crossbows shifted with her, a ripple of gray. "Bring me the child. Bring Maren. Come with us willingly, and I swear by the First Storm that no harm will come to any of you. You'll be restored, Rosalind. Your exile reversed. Your name cleared. Your memories—" a soft, terrible tenderness, "—we may even be able to give some of them back."

And there it was. The hook beneath the bait, gleaming.

Rosalind felt Maren go very still beside her.

"You knew," Maren breathed. "You knew about her memories. About what was taken. You knew all along, and you let her rot in exile for ten years—"

"I did what the Order required." For just a moment the mask slipped, and underneath it Rosalind saw something colder than cruelty: arithmetic. A woman doing sums with human lives and finding the totals acceptable. "As I am doing now. The seed cannot be allowed to bloom. Whatever truth it holds, whatever ancient grievance, whatever *convenience* for Vessic sympathizers and Court apologists — the war is ended. The peace is fragile. Some stones are best left unturned, and this one best left *buried*."

"So it's true," Rosalind said. Her own voice sounded distant to her, as if someone else were speaking through her mouth. "It isn't protection. It never was. You're afraid of what's in the seed. You're afraid the world will find out what really happened at the Hollow Gate three hundred years ago."

Ilvane's expression did not change. But the air did.

Pressure rolled down the valley like a held exhalation — Rosalind felt it in her teeth, in the fillings of old scars, in the water freezing in the pony's water skin. The storm-callers along the line raised their hands in unison, and above the western ridge the clouds began to turn, slowly, deliberately, like a great wheel deciding its direction.

"Last chance, Rosalind," Ilvane said. "Step aside. This is bigger than your guilt. Bigger than your lost decade. Hand over the seed and walk away, and I will let every one of you live."

"*Every* one of you" — spoken as if Wren were a vessel and not a child. Spoken as if Dain and Maren were loose threads to be snipped or spared at the weaver's whim. Rosalind looked at the white banner, at the forty crossbows, at the woman who had taught her everything worth knowing and lied to her about everything that mattered.

"You know what I remember most about my trial?" Rosalind said. "Not the verdict. Not the reading of my crimes. I remember that you wouldn't look at me. Ten years I wondered why. Was it shame? Was it grief?" She shook her head slowly. "It was neither. You couldn't look at me because I was evidence. Because somewhere under my skull was proof of what you'd done, and every time you saw my face you had to look at what you were willing to spend."

"Take them," Ilvane said.

* * *

The world went white and loud.

Rosalind had perhaps two seconds — she counted them later, in the long dark hours after, and there were two — between Ilvane's order and the first bolt. Two seconds in which ten years of buried instinct came home like a falcon to the wrist.

She dropped, dragging Wren off the pony with her, and slammed both palms into the frozen road. *Come.* Not a request. Not a careful, rationed trickle of power such as she'd used at the Saddle Rocks, such as she'd hoarded and feared for a decade. She reached into the mountain's bones and *pulled*, and the sky answered the way it had answered at the Saddle Rocks — eagerly, hungrily, with the enthusiasm of a thing long starved.

Thunder cracked the valley open. Lightning fell in sheets along the road ahead, striking the granite shoulders on either side of the Order's line, shattering stone into shrapnel that shrieked overhead. The crossbow volley that should have killed them all went wide, bolts splintering against rock or veering wild in the sudden screaming wind.

"MOVE!" Dain roared, hauling Maren up by the arm. "Back! Back the way we came!"

"There's nowhere—" Maren began.

"There's ALWAYS somewhere!" Dain shoved her toward the rocks. "Thirty years running roads nobody's supposed to run, lady, there is ALWAYS somewhere!"

Rosalind stayed low, one arm locked around Wren, feeding the storm in pulses — strike, withdraw, strike — buying seconds the way a miser buys bread, coin by precious coin. She could feel the Order's callers pushing back, a dozen wills braided together, grinding against hers like millstones. They were stronger together than she was alone. She had perhaps a minute before they found the seam in her defense and split it.

A minute. Maybe less.

They ran — Dain in front with his axe out, absurd and magnificent, Maren clinging to his belt, Rosalind half-carrying Wren whose feet barely touched the ground, the pony bolting riderless into the trees. Behind them the line broke apart, storm-callers fanning out, and Rosalind heard Ilvane's voice cut through the gale, calm as ever, giving orders in clipped cadences: *flank left, take the archer's position, do not kill the vessel.*

*Do not kill the vessel.*

Wren wasn't even a person to them. Just a jar to be carried home intact.

Fury hit Rosalind like a second storm, rising up through her from somewhere older than her training, and for a moment the lightning came when she didn't call it — leaping from her fingertips in jagged white branches, splitting a boulder twenty yards away, close enough that the heat of it seared her cheek. Wren whimpered. Rosalind clamped down hard, jaw tight, and forced the power back into channels.

*Control. Control, or you're just another weapon in someone's hand.*

They gained the tree line. Black pines swallowed them, branches whipping, snow exploding off the boughs. The pursuit behind them was methodical, unhurried — they weren't chasing, Rosalind realized. They were herding. Pushing them uphill toward the high shoulder where the trees thinned and the crossbows would have clean lines.

"We need to cut east," Rosalind gasped. "Down into the ravine—"

"East is mined with wards," Maren snapped. "I can feel them from here. Old ones, Order-made. We go through them, we light up like festival lanterns."

"Then west—"

"West is the cliff."

Dain stopped so suddenly they nearly ran into him.

He was looking back down the slope. Rosalind followed his gaze and saw why: three storm-callers had gotten ahead of them somehow — no. Not ahead. *Below.* Rising through the trees with the unhurried grace of people walking on ground they owned, cutting off the descent. One of them raised a hand, and the wind died in the pines all at once, a silence so total Rosalind's ears rang.

Trapped. High ground behind, warded ground east, dead drop west, and patient gray death climbing from below.

"Well," Dain said.

He handed Maren his axe. She took it without understanding, then did.

"Dain—"

"Ravine floor," he said, talking fast, low, eyes on the three below. "There's a culvert where the old mine drainage crosses under the ward line. I used it in '09, moving Vessic relics for a collector who paid too well to ask questions. The wards span the slopes, not the rock under them." He was shrugging off his coat as he spoke, stuffing it into Maren's arms. "You go through the culvert. It comes out past the second ridge, near the shrine ruins. You remember the shrine ruins, Roz. You got me through them blind drunk and half-frozen and you swore you'd never tell anyone."

"I remember," Rosalind whispered. Her heart had begun to beat very slowly, very heavily, the way it does before a blow lands. "Dain, what are you—"

"Somebody's gotta convince those three gentlemen that we came down this way." He grinned at her, and it was his real grin, the crooked one, the one from a hundred campfires and a hundred narrow escapes. "Somebody loud. Somebody big. Somebody who owes you more debts than a man can count in one lifetime."

"No." The word came out of her like a bullet. "No. Absolutely not. We fight—"

"You can't fight twelve. You can't even fight six, not carrying the girl, not keeping that storm of yours leashed enough not to cook her." He crouched, briefly, to Wren's level. The child stared at him, wide-eyed, understanding nothing of his words and everything of his tone. "Hey, sprout. You take care of her. She forgets to eat."

Wren's lip trembled. Then she did something that stopped Rosalind's breath: she reached up and touched Dain's face with one small mittened hand, and spoke — one syllable, soft, in Vessic, a word Rosalind had read in Maren's forbidden lexicon and never expected to hear given freely.

*Ath-vela.* *Safe passage. Go well.*

Dain's grin wobbled at the edges. "Yeah," he said roughly. "Same to you, sprout."

He straightened. Squeezed Rosalind's shoulder once, hard, the way men do instead of saying things. Then he turned and crashed downslope through the pines, shouting at the top of considerable lungs — "*OVER HERE, YOU GRAY-BELLIED CROWS!* COME AND GET YOUR SMUGGLER!" — and the three below turned toward him like compass needles, and the wind rose, and Rosalind grabbed Maren and Wren and dragged them east toward the culvert because stopping was unthinkable and moving was betrayal and she could not do either so she did the one her body chose.

They were thirty yards into the rocks when the lightning came down.

Not hers. She felt the signature of it — Order-work, precise, disciplined, four callers acting in concert — and she felt it strike, and she heard Dain roar, defiant even now, and then she heard the roar stop.

Rosalind did not stop moving. Later she would not be able to explain how; her body simply refused, some animal engine deeper than grief driving her legs onward while her mind tore itself apart behind her. Maren sobbed as she ran. Wren pressed her face into Rosalind's back. The culvert mouth appeared — a black slot in the hillside, half-choked with ice — and Rosalind shoved them through it and crawled in after, into the dark, into the dripping cold, away from the light on the slope where a broad-shouldered man with a crooked grin lay smoking in the snow.

In the black of the culvert, knee-deep in freezing runoff, Rosalind finally let herself make a sound. It was not a scream. It was worse — a low, continuous noise, animal and stripped, the sound of a woman discovering that grief and fury are not two things but one thing wearing two faces, and that both of them were hers now, forever, fused like slag.

"He's alive." Maren's voice shook in the dark. Her hands gripped Rosalind's arms, hard. "Rosalind. Listen to me. He's alive."

"How could you possibly—"

"Because they didn't kill him." Maren's old eyes caught what little light there was. "If they wanted him dead, they'd have used the killing stroke — I felt it, the working was a paralysis charge, a capture pattern. They want him breathing. Hostages, leverage, testimony — I don't know which. But Ilvane does not waste assets, and a smuggler who knows these mountains is an asset." Her grip tightened. "Which means we have time. Do you understand me? We have time, if we move, if we reach the Gate, if we finish what we started before Ilvane finishes what *she* started."

Rosalind knelt in the icy water with a child trembling against her chest and a dead man's coat still clutched in her fist — when had she taken it from Maren? — and somewhere above them the storm she had summoned was dying over a battlefield where her oldest friend lay in Order chains.

*Dain.* The name was a wound that would not close. *Hold on, you stubborn ox. Hold on.*

Because there was only one direction left now. Forward. Down. Into the Hollow, where the Court waited with its invitation and its patience, where the Gate stood open like a throat, where the truth that had cost Dain everything waited to cost Rosalind the rest.

She pulled Wren closer. She stood.

"Move," she said, and her voice came out like iron dropped on stone. "Both of you. Move."
