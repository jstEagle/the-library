## 17. Into the Hollow

They buried what was left of the road two miles past the last waystone, and after that there was only the Hollow.

Rosalind carried Dain on her back.

It was not a dignified way for a smuggler captain to travel — slung across her shoulders like a sack of grain, his boots knocking against her hips with every step, his blood long since dried to a black crust on her cloak. She had tried to dig a grave three times in the first day. Each time, Maren had put a hand on her arm and said nothing, and each time Rosalind had stood over the half-turned earth with her knuckles white around the spade and understood why she couldn't do it. Not yet. Dain Ferro had spent thirty years hauling contraband through mountains that wanted him dead, and he had died on a gate road holding off soldiers so a mute girl could run. He deserved better than a hole dug in haste by a woman who hadn't finished needing him.

So she carried him south, into the Court's country, where the ground itself had forgotten how to be ground.

The border was not marked. It simply arrived. One moment they were walking through grey pine and frost-stiffened heather; the next, the pines were gone — not felled, not burned, *absent*, as if the land had been asked a question about trees and declined to answer. The soil beneath their boots was the color of old ash and gave slightly, like flesh that had been left out too long. There was no wind here. No birdsong, no insects, no sound at all except their breathing and the soft knock of Dain's boots.

Wren pressed close against Rosalind's side. The girl had not spoken since the gate road — she rarely spoke at all — but her hand found Rosalind's and gripped it with a strength that had nothing childlike in it, as though she were afraid the Hollow would take her too if she let go.

"It doesn't kill," Maren said quietly, walking behind them. Her voice sounded wrong out here, flattened, like speech heard through a wall. "That's what people misunderstand about the Hollow lands. The war didn't scorch them. It *emptied* them. Everything that made this place itself — its memory, its name, its green — was pulled out and used as fuel. What you're walking on is what's left when a country is spent like coin."

"And we're walking into it." Rosalind shifted Dain's weight higher on her back. He was lighter than he should have been. She refused to think about why. "Because the Gate is at the center."

"Because the truth is at the center," Maren corrected. "The Gate is just where it was buried."

They walked until the light failed — though the light here did strange things, pooling without source, dimming without dusk — and made camp in the lee of a ruined archway that might once have been a temple door. Maren built no fire. There was nothing to burn. They ate cold rations and shared water from a skin, and Wren fell asleep with her head in Rosalind's lap, her small fingers still curled in the fabric of Rosalind's sleeve.

In the dark, the echoes came.

Rosalind saw the first one near midnight, if the Hollow kept midnights: a figure standing at the edge of the ruined archway, motionless, faintly luminous, like a person remembered rather than seen. She knew the silhouette instantly. Broad shoulders. A smuggler's rolling gait even standing still. A laugh she could almost hear.

"Dain?" The word left her before she could stop it, cracked down the middle.

The echo turned its head. It had no face. Where Dain's features should have been there was smooth grey blankness, the texture of the ash-soil, but the posture was unmistakable — the cocked hip, the tilted head, the way he'd always listened to her like whatever she said next might be worth money.

"Rosalind." Maren's hand closed hard on her wrist. "Don't speak to them. Don't answer them."

"They're not real."

"No. But they're not nothing, either." Maren's ancient eyes caught the sourceless light. "The Court's land is hungry for memory. It takes what it can find. You are full of things it wants — including things that were taken *from* you. And I think..." She hesitated, and Rosalind watched the hesitation, and hated it. "I think the Hollow recognizes stolen goods. Your echoes may be louder than most."

The faceless Dain raised one grey hand. Beckoned. And then, with terrible gentleness, it turned and walked away into the dark, and every instinct in Rosalind's body screamed to follow it, because following Dain into the dark was the oldest habit she had.

She stayed seated. Wren slept on. Somewhere out in the emptiness, other figures moved — shapes at the limit of sight, patient as tide.

* * *

On the second day, the echoes stopped being strangers.

They came at noon, walking out of a featureless plain in single file, and Rosalind knew every one of them. Cantor Veyl, who had taught her to hear the storm before she learned to call it. Sister Anthe, who had bandaged her hands after her first botched lightning-work. Ilvane herself, younger than Rosalind had ever known her, robed in citadel blue, smiling the smile she'd worn in the years before everything went wrong. They walked in procession across the dead earth toward a destination only they could see, and none of them had faces, and all of them, when they passed within ten feet of Rosalind, paused — just briefly — as if in greeting.

"The Order sent people into the Hollow during the war," Maren said, watching the procession dwindle into the grey. "Storm-callers. Hundreds of them. The records say they never came back. The records also say a great many things that aren't true." She glanced sideways at Rosalind. "You're looking at them like you know them."

"I did know them." Rosalind's throat was tight. "Veyl trained me for six years. He—" She stopped. Tried again. "He taught me... he..."

Nothing came.

There should have been more. She could see his silhouette, his stride, the particular way he'd crack his knuckles before a working. But the substance of him — his voice, his lessons, the thing he'd said to her the last time they spoke — was gone, scooped out like the pines at the border, and where memory should have been there was a smooth grey shape exactly the size of a missing man.

"Rosalind." Maren had stopped walking. "How much of your time at the citadel can you actually remember?"

"Enough."

"How much?"

Rosalind opened her mouth to say *enough* again and found the word wouldn't come. She cast backward instead, the way she always did, down the familiar corridors of her own history — initiation, training, the great storm-halls with their singing spires, the night of her betrayal, the trial, the exile. The landmarks were all there. She'd navigated by them for ten years. But now, walking through a land that had been stripped of everything it remembered, she felt the absence in her own mind the way you feel a missing tooth: not as pain, but as a wrongness of contour, a place where the tongue keeps going to check.

"I remember the betrayal," she said slowly. "I remember the trial. I remember Ilvane's face when they read the charges." She frowned. "I don't remember what I supposedly *did*. Everyone says I know what I did. Everyone has always said that. 'You know what you did, Rosalind.' And I've spent ten years agreeing with them because—" Because what else does a traitor do? Because shame is easier than doubt? "—because I thought grief was why I couldn't look at it directly."

Maren said nothing. That silence was worse than any accusation.

Ahead of them, Wren had stopped in the ash and was staring at something only she could see. When Rosalind reached her, the girl was trembling, her breath coming fast and shallow, and her eyes — those too-old eyes — were fixed on the empty air at the height of a grown woman's shoulder.

"Wren. Look at me. There's nothing there."

The girl shook her head. Then she lifted her small hand, very carefully, and pointed — not at the air, but at Rosalind. At the space just beside Rosalind's head. At the exact spot where, if Rosalind had an echo walking with her, her echo would be.

Then Wren did something she almost never did. She spoke.

Not in common tongue. In Vessic, low and urgent, three syllables repeated twice, and though Rosalind had never studied the dead language, the seed inside the child seemed determined to lend her its meanings, and the words assembled themselves in her mind like frost forming on glass:

*She walks with you. She has always walked with you.*

* * *

The third day, the Hollow showed her the rest.

It began with weather, or the memory of weather. Clouds gathered overhead out of a sky that had held none, and rain began to fall — except the rain didn't wet anything. It fell in slow silver threads and vanished an inch above the ground, and where it vanished, images rose up out of the ash like breath on cold stone.

Rosalind stopped walking. Behind her, Maren made a low sound of warning. Ahead, Wren turned in a slow circle, arms out, face upturned, letting the dry rain pass through her, and her expression was one Rosalind had never seen on a child: recognition.

The images came faster. A citadel hall, rendered in ghost-light — the storm-hall, the singing spires, *her* hall. Figures in robes around a table of black stone. Her own hands, younger, unscarred, pressing something small and seed-shaped into a lattice of woven light while voices chanted in Vessic. Another figure beside her, hooded, guiding her hands — and when the hood slipped in the vision's flicker, the face beneath was Ilvane's, and Ilvane was smiling.

"No." Rosalind took a step back. The image followed her, hovering. "No, that's not — I never—"

"You did," Maren said softly. "Or part of you did. The part they cut out."

"I would remember—"

"That is precisely the point of cutting."

More images. The ritual ending. Hands wiping her memory clean with a working that felt, even secondhand, like drowning. Then the trial — but the trial was wrong, warped, the charges read aloud in voices that overlapped and contradicted, and threaded through every word was Ilvane's calm, kind cadence shaping the narrative the way a smith shapes iron. *She betrayed us. She knows what she did.* Planted. Hammered. Quenched. Ten years of exile built on a forgery, and the forgery had been signed with Rosalind's own stolen face.

Rosalind sank to her knees in the ash. Dain's body slid from her shoulders and she caught him, lowered him gently, cradled his head the way she'd once cradled Wren's on the first frozen night.

"I carried it," she whispered. "Ten years. I built a whole life in exile around a crime I never committed, because it was the only self anyone would give me." She laughed, and the laugh came out broken. "Dain told me. He told me to trust no one, and I trusted my own head instead, and my own head was the one thing in the world I should have known was compromised."

Wren crouched beside her. The girl looked at the fading images, then at Rosalind, and her small hand rose and pressed flat against Rosalind's chest, over the heart. Warmth spread from the touch — not magic, or not only magic. Something older. Something like witness.

And in the warmth, for one clear instant, Rosalind felt the edges of what had been taken. Not returned — she understood now that it couldn't be, not fully, not cleanly — but *mapped*. She felt the shape of her lost years the way a sailor feels a coastline in fog. And at the center of the map, wrapped in layer upon layer of careful erasure, was a single point of heat that beat in time with the seed sleeping inside the child.

Her memory and Wren's burden were the same object. They had been all along.

"Come on." She got up. Lifted Dain onto her back again, though her shoulders screamed and her knees shook. "We're close. Aren't we, Maren?"

Maren pointed. Through the thinning rain-ghosts, far ahead, the dead plain rose into hills that were not hills — vast, smooth, grey shapes like sleeping animals, arranged in a ring. And at the ring's center, barely visible, a vertical seam of darkness split the horizon from earth to sky: the Hollow Gate, older than the war, older perhaps than the kingdoms that had fought it. Waiting. It had always been waiting.

As they started forward, the echoes came out one final time. Not the faceless ones this time — these were new, dozens of them, hundreds, rising out of the ash on every side and falling into step around the four travelers, silent escort, and every one of them wore a robe of citadel blue.

Every one of them had Rosalind's face.

They walked with her toward the Gate, an army of the selves she might have been, and ahead of them all, at the threshold where the world's memory ended, the dark seam of the Gate began — very slowly, very patiently — to open.

Inside it, something that had been sleeping for three centuries turned over, and Wren began, softly, to sing.
