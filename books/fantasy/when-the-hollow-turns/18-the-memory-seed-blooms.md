## 18. The Memory-Seed Blooms

The Gate is not a door.

Wren has known doors all her short life — the crooked one on Rosalind's cabin that sticks in winter, the flaps of Dain's wagons, the iron-banded thing at the waystation that groans when the wind leans on it. Doors open onto places. The Hollow Gate opens onto *time*.

It stands alone in a valley of grey glass, two pillars and a broken arch, black stone drinking the last light. Nothing grows here. Even the wind goes quiet at the edge of the valley, as if it is afraid to be heard.

Maren stops first. Maren, who is older than she looks, older than anyone, whose hands shake now for the first time since Wren has known her. "This is where I leave my courage," Maren says softly, and Wren does not understand the words exactly — but she understands them anyway, the way she has understood everything lately, words arriving through her skin instead of her ears.

Rosalind's hand settles on Wren's shoulder. It is warm. Everything else is cold.

"Wren." Rosalind crouches so their eyes are level. Her face is hollowed out from the march through the dead lands; there are gaps behind her eyes now, Wren can see them, places where things used to be. She gave pieces of herself away on the road without saying so, and Wren grieves for her in a language neither of them can speak aloud yet. "You don't have to do this. We can still turn around."

But the seed inside Wren's chest is already turning.

It does not feel like a seed anymore. It feels like a second heart, one that beats out of rhythm with hers — faster, older, desperate. All through the mountains it has been waking in small ways: the taste of smoke in water, the sound of bells in empty rooms, the dream of hands pressed flat against a door that is getting hotter. Little splinters of a night three hundred years gone.

Now, standing before the Gate, the splinters begin to pull together.

"It wants this," Wren says. The Vessic comes out of her mouth like breath; she stopped being surprised that others half-understand her a long time ago. "It's been walking me here the whole time."

"I know," Rosalind says. Her thumb brushes Wren's cheek, wiping away ash or a tear — they look the same in the Hollow lands. "I know, little bird. So we walk you back. Every step after this one, you choose."

Wren chooses.

She walks between the pillars.

* * *

The world folds.

Not darkness — *before*. Before color, before the valley, before the grey glass. The seed splits her open like a hand splitting a loaf, and everything that was hidden inside her rises up, and Wren falls backward into a night she never lived.

She is standing in a city.

Vessa. She knows its name the way she knows her own — it is written into her bones now, into the marrow the priests filled when they hid their memory inside a dying child. The city glows. Lanterns strung between white towers, canals carrying lamplight like spilled honey, ten thousand windows warm against the dark. Music somewhere. Drums, low and slow, a festival rhythm. People laughing in the streets below her, wearing bright woven sashes, calling blessings on each other in the tongue that lives only in Wren's chest now.

It is the longest night of the year. The Night of Open Doors, when every house leaves its latch lifted so no traveler sleeps outside. Wren knows this because everyone in the city knows this. Nobody locks anything tonight. That is the whole point. That is the promise.

That is why it works.

She smells the smoke before she sees it — wrong smoke, sweet and oily, not woodsmoke. Then the sky over the eastern quarter blooms orange, and the drums falter, and someone screams a word Wren's body understands before her mind does: *fire-oil*. Set deliberately. Fed, not fought.

She is running. She does not remember becoming a runner; the memory carries her the way water carries a leaf. Through streets filling with panicked light, past doorways hanging open — open, all of them open, because it is the Night of Open Doors and the city kept its promise and the promise is what kills them — and men in grey-and-gold come through those open doors.

She knows the grey-and-gold. She has seen it stitched on robes in Rosalind's stories of the citadels, seen it in the vault records they stole, seen it on Ilvane's truce banner. Storm-callers. The Order. And behind them, in mail, torches in hand, the soldiers of the old kings — the alliance marching together, the way they will deny ever having marched together for three hundred years.

They are not looting. They are not conquering.

They are *erasing*.

Wren watches a storm-caller raise her hands at a tower full of families and call down lightning in perfect, disciplined strokes, the way you would fell timber, the way you would clear a field. Methodical. Sanctioned. A king's officer stands at her shoulder with a ledger, checking names off as the towers fall, and Wren understands, with a horror that has no bottom, that the ledger came first. This was planned before tonight. The festival was chosen *because* the doors would be open. Because the Vessic trusted the world enough to leave themselves unguarded once a year.

The memory-seed holds everything. It was made to hold everything. The dying priests poured the whole night into it — every name, every street, every face — so that someday, somewhere, someone would have to know.

So Wren knows.

She knows the woman who pulls three children into a canal and holds them under the bridge until the soldiers pass, then surfaces to find her own son gone from her grip. She knows the old archivist — young then, dark-haired, quick-eyed — who carries armfuls of books out of the burning library and buries them in a wine cellar, and whose face Wren has seen across a campfire a hundred times. *Maren.* Maren was there. Maren carried the books. Maren helped hide the seed centuries later because she could not save the city, and she has been walking toward this valley for three hundred years to set it down.

She knows the priests. Nine of them, in a cellar under the falling city, cutting their memories loose one by one — the ritual Rosalind found in the vault, the ritual with Rosalind's name already written in it, decades before Rosalind was born. They had no vessel left. Their bodies were failing. So they waited, and prayed, and three hundred years later other hands — hands that owed them, hands that were *paid*, hands whose payment was a lie planted in a promising young storm-caller's mind — carried the seed to a sanctuary and put it where no one would think to look.

Inside a child born mute. Inside a child no one would question. Inside Wren.

And she knows the lie. The worst part, the part the seed saved for last, the part that makes her scream in a language dead for three centuries: the Order and the kings burned Vessa together, and then they stood on the ashes and told the world the Vessic had done it. Told the world the Vessic had turned their gift — the memory-weaving, the seed-craft, the gentle art of carrying what must not be forgotten — into a weapon. Told the world that the Hollow War began because Vessa struck first.

The war did not begin that night. The war was *born* that night, dressed in a lie, and it has been marching ever since, and the Court rose from the ashes hungry for acknowledgment, and the Order built citadels on top of graves and called it peace.

*We did not start it,* the seed says, in nine voices, in a thousand voices, in Wren's voice. *We only remembered it. Remembering is not the crime. Forgetting was.*

* * *

She comes back to herself on her knees between the pillars, screaming.

The scream tears out of her in Vessic and keeps going, and she cannot stop it, because stopping it would mean the memory folding closed again, and it will not fold, it is *bloomed* now, wide open, a flower of fire and grief filling her chest to bursting. The valley hears her. The grey glass sings with it. Somewhere behind her Rosalind is holding her, arms locked around her, saying her name over and over, and Dain's voice too, rough and frightened, and Maren's — Maren is weeping openly, murmuring in Vessic, answering the seed's cry with words of witness: *I saw it. I saw it. You are believed.*

But the seed is not finished.

Bloomed, it needs an anchor — Maren told them this on the mountain road, in the low voice she used for terrible truths. A truth this size cannot live loose in the world. It must root in something. In someone. If it roots fully in Wren it will grow through her like frost through a windowpane, and the child will become nothing but a vessel, a haunted archive, a girl erased by a night three hundred years old.

Wren feels it happening. Feels the roots going down into her, wrapping her small self — her own memories, her own few years, her mother's face which she can suddenly *not see*, the seed's fire eating hers — and she claws at Rosalind's coat and tries to say it, tries to say *it's taking me, it's taking everything*—

And Rosalind, who has been losing pieces of herself the whole road here without complaint, looks down at her with eyes that are missing things and full anyway, and says:

"Then we give it somewhere else to live."

She turns to Maren. "Do it now. Anchor it to me."

"No," Dain says.

"Yes," Rosalind says, and begins pulling off her gloves.

The seed surges in Wren's chest, eager, and beyond the broken arch the valley's far rim flickers with lights that are not lanterns — grey-and-gold descending the southern slope, and pale shapes rising from the northern passes like fog given intent. The Order has found them. The Court has found them. Both armies converging on the Gate, on Wren, on the blooming truth that could end the war or drown the world —

— and Rosalind Sterling steps between Wren and both of them, and lifts her hands, and the sky above the Hollow Gate begins, at last, to answer.
