## 20. The Price of Remembering

The seed had bloomed, and the world was drowning in it.

Wren hung in the center of the Hollow Gate like a lantern with a sun inside her. Light poured from the girl's open mouth, her eyes, the seams of her small fists—not light exactly, but *memory*, three centuries of it made visible, and every second of it was killing her. The Gate's ancient archway drank some of it; most of it poured back into the child, wave after wave after wave, and each wave was a war crime given weight.

Wren was screaming in Vessic. She had been screaming for what felt like an hour. Her voice had gone past hoarse into something rawer, and still the memories came, because they had nowhere else to go. They had been folded small inside a six-year-old body for ten years—no, longer, Rosalind realized, the priests had hidden them in Wren before Wren was even born—and now the fold was undone and there was far too much of them for one small girl to hold.

"She's coming apart," Maren said. The old archivist knelt at the base of the arch, chalk lines already drawn on the black stone, her hands shaking so badly the chalk chattered. "The rite can take it off her—but something has to hold the memory once it leaves her. An anchor. A living mind large enough to carry what she can't."

"Then you anchor it," Rosalind said. "You designed this ritual. You've had four hundred years to prepare."

Maren looked up at her, and there was no evasion left in the old woman's face. Whatever masks Maren Kestrel had worn—a disgraced archivist, a frightened scholar, a friend—they were gone. What remained was very old and very tired and honest for the first time since Ironhollow.

"The anchor dies," Maren said. "Or worse than dies. The seed doesn't rest in a mind, Rosalind. It *eats* one. It takes everything the mind holds and replaces it with the truth it carries. Whoever anchors it becomes nothing but the atrocity, remembering itself forever." She swallowed. "I helped design it that way. So no one would ever want to be its keeper."

Beyond the arch, the storm Rosalind had called roared against the armies of two powers. Thunder rolled down the dead valleys in sheets. Somewhere out in that grey chaos, Ilvane's storm-callers and Sereth's wraiths pressed against a wall of wind and rain that Rosalind was holding up with nothing but her own fury—and fury, she knew, was a fuel that burned out.

She turned back to Wren.

The girl's eyes had rolled back white. The light under her skin was burning through her clothes in threads. And beneath the horror of it, Rosalind could see—the way you can see a face through frosted glass—the shape of the memories themselves. A valley full of people kneeling. Storm-callers in their high collars. Kings in gold. Fire called down not by Vessic hands but by the Order's own, and the blame written afterward in ink that had dried into history.

The truth. The whole, unbearable truth, the thing both the Court and the Order would burn mountains to own or erase.

And it was killing a child who had never asked to carry it.

"Maren," Rosalind said quietly. "How much time?"

"Minutes. Perhaps less."

"Then listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once." Rosalind crouched, took the old woman's chalk from her fingers, and finished the last line of the sigil herself—her storm-caller's hand steady as bedrock, drawing a script she hadn't been taught but somehow knew, the same way she'd half-understood Vessic the night a frostbitten girl knocked twice on her door. "You're going to run the rite. I'm going to be the anchor."

"You don't understand what—"

"I understand perfectly. You told me at Ironhollow. Destruction kills whoever anchors it. Anchoring eats whoever anchors it. I did the arithmetic on the mountain, Maren, while you slept." She met the old woman's eyes. "There is no version of this where I walk away whole. There is only a version where Wren does. That's the version we're doing."

Maren's face crumpled and reassembled itself, older and harder. "It will take your memories. All of them, if you let it. The false ones will burn first—they're shallow roots, lies always are—but the true ones..." Her voice caught. "The true ones it will take too, unless you give them *deliberately*. That's the only mercy in the design. A mind that surrenders willingly keeps its shape. Mostly."

"Mostly," Rosalind repeated.

"Mostly."

Rosalind looked at Wren again—at the small, blazing, breaking girl—and felt something in her chest go quiet and enormous, like the pause between lightning and thunder.

Ten years ago she had lost everything: her order, her name, her place in the towers above the clouds, exiled for a betrayal she could not remember committing. Ten years she had built a life on the wreckage—a cabin, a trade route, a reputation among smugglers, solitude worn smooth as river stone. She had thought the wreckage was her fault. She had thought herself guilty of something too terrible to recall.

She knew better now. The betrayal was planted. The exile was engineered. Her own mind was a document someone else had edited.

Well then. If her memory was already a lie, she would spend it buying something true.

"Do it," she said.

* * *

Maren began the rite with words in Vessic, and Rosalind understood every one of them, which should have frightened her more than it did.

*Take the burden from the small vessel. Let it pass to the willing vessel. What is false, burn. What is true, keep. What is given freely, honor.*

The Gate answered.

The archway, silent for three hundred years, hummed like a struck bell, and the light pouring out of Wren reversed itself—flowed backward, out of the girl, across the chalked lines, toward Rosalind.

It hit her like a wave hits a seawall.

The first thing to come was the valley. She saw it whole: Vessic Vale in autumn, orchards heavy with fruit, the sanctuaries with their bell-towers full of doves. She saw the kings' soldiers arrive under banners of truce. She saw the storm-callers of her own order descend from the citadels, invited as witnesses—or so the Vale believed. She saw the High Cantor of that age give the signal with a raised hand, and she saw the sky answer him, because the sky had always answered them, and fire fell on the kneeling people of Vessic until the bells melted in their towers and the doves fell out of the air like burnt petals.

Then the writing of the lie. The archives sealed. The survivors' names struck. The blame laid on the murdered, so thoroughly that within a generation even the murderers' grandchildren believed it.

And threaded through all of it, faint as watermark, she saw herself—younger, hooded, one of twelve priests around a newborn child, folding the stolen memory of that night into a seed and pressing it into a bloodline chosen to survive. She saw her own hand sign the record. She saw her own face agree.

*I was one of them,* she thought, and the knowledge arrived without shock, because she had already read her name in the vault at Thornfell. But seeing it—*feeling* it, standing inside the memory of her own complicity—was different from reading it. It was like swallowing a stone.

The seed wanted more. It reached deeper.

And it found the lies.

They burned first, just as Maren promised—and oh, they *burned*. The memory of the betrayal itself, the invented night of treachery the Order had grafted into her mind a decade ago: it went up like oiled paper. She felt it tear free of her, shrieking, and in tearing free it showed her its roots—Ilvane's hands on her temples in a tower room, Ilvane's voice saying *you did a terrible thing, Rosalind, you know you did*, over and over, patient as water wearing stone, until the lie grew its own false memories around itself like scar tissue. Every sleepless night of guilt. Every replayed conversation hunting for the moment she must have turned traitor. Ten years of shame, all of it manufactured, all of it kindling.

It burned. And beneath the ash of it, something in Rosalind laughed—a wild, cracked sound that the storm outside seemed to echo back with thunder.

"Let it burn," she heard herself say. "Take it all. Take the guilt. Take the exile. Take the tower room and the mentor's face and every hour I spent hating myself for a crime I never committed. *Burn.*"

But the seed kept reaching, because it didn't distinguish. It couldn't tell her lies from her truths. To it, a mind was a mind was a harvest.

So Rosalind did the only thing left. She chose.

She stood inside herself, in the white roaring space where her life was being unmade, and she began to give.

*Dain Ferro,* she gave first, deliberately, pressing the memory out like breath. Dain at twenty-two, cocky, half-drunk on cheap brandy, talking his way past her drawn blade on the Thornfell road because he'd heard there was an exiled storm-caller who guided caravans and he intended to hire her or die trying. Dain laughing at his own jokes. Dain teaching her dice games she always won anyway, because storms favor her kind and he pretended not to notice. Dain's gruff voice calling her *Sterling* like it was an insult and an endearment at once. Dain lying bleeding on the Gate Road with Ilvane's callers' lightning still smoking off his chest, and his last words—his last words, which she would not write here even in her own dying mind, because they were hers.

She gave him away, and it was like cutting out her own heart and handing it to the dark, and she did it anyway, because the alternative was letting the seed rip him out carelessly and leave nothing of him anywhere, ever again.

*Maren,* she gave next. The old woman's crooked smile over tea in Ironhollow. The confession in the lamplight: *I am older than I look, child. I helped build the cage.* The long walk together through the dead lands, arguing theology like schoolgirls.

*The mountain,* she gave. Her cabin with its iron stove and its view of the north passes. The smell of pine smoke. The particular silence of snowfall at midnight. The whiteout night a small fist knocked twice on her door.

And Wren. She saved Wren for last. She gave the girl slowly, tenderly, piece by piece: the frostbitten child wrapped in her one good blanket. The Vessic words Rosalind half-understood. Wren asleep by the fire, murmuring a dead language. Wren teaching Dain a lullaby at the waystation, his big smuggler's voice mangling the vowels while the girl giggled. Wren's hand in hers on the road to the Gate, small and trusting and warm.

*I give these freely,* she told the seed, in Vessic, in the old tongue she had never learned and had always known. *Honor what is freely given.*

The seed paused.

And then—it honored. Where it had torn, now it received. Where it had burned, now it carried. The memories did not vanish; they *changed vessels*. She felt them settle into the vast dark architecture of the bloomed truth, become part of the witness, part of the testimony. Dain's laugh became evidence that kindness existed after the atrocity. The lullaby became proof that the Vessic tongue still lived somewhere besides graves. Her ten years of exile became part of the record—not the lie of her guilt, but the truth of what the lie had cost, and what it had built: a woman stubborn enough, at the end, to walk into the fire on purpose.

The pain was beyond describing, so she stopped describing it and simply endured, the way a mountain endures weather.

Somewhere outside her own head, she dimly registered Maren chanting, Wren falling—caught, caught, small arms catching her before she struck the stone—and the storm overhead beginning to falter, because the storm was her, and she was emptying.

The last thing she held was the newest memory: Wren's face, minutes ago, going quiet as the burden lifted from her. Brown eyes opening. Looking straight at Rosalind with an expression no child should have to wear—grief and gratitude fused into something older than childhood.

Then Rosalind gave that away too, and let the dark come.

* * *

She woke to rain on her face.

Real rain, ordinary rain, the storm spent and the clouds shredding apart to show a bruised grey dawn. She was lying at the base of the Hollow Gate with Maren's coat draped over her and Maren's ancient hand gripping hers hard enough to hurt.

Above them, the archway stood dark for the first time in three centuries. Not dead—she could feel it humming faintly, like a bell after the ringing—but closed. The seed had passed fully into her. She could feel it now, vast and quiet, coiled where her past used to be: the whole terrible truth of the Hollow War, complete, witnessed, anchored. It no longer hungered. It only waited, patient as any archive, to be read.

"Wren?" Her voice came out ruined, a whisper of gravel.

"Asleep. Truly asleep, for the first time since I've known her." Maren's eyes were red-rimmed. "The bloom is broken, Rosalind. She'll carry echoes, scars—no child comes through that clean. But the seed is yours now. All of it."

"And me? What's left of me?"

Maren hesitated, and Rosalind saw the answer before it was spoken, saw it in the careful blankness of the old woman's face—the look people give a stranger they are deciding how much to explain.

"I remember the rite," Rosalind said slowly. Testing. "I remember giving... things. Names. Faces." She groped for them and found shapes without contents, doors without rooms behind them. "Dain. His name is—I know his name is Dain. I know I loved him. I don't—" Her throat closed. "I don't remember his face."

"No," Maren said gently. "You wouldn't. The freely given ones are the deepest cut, child. The seed took what you surrendered and made it part of the testimony. He's in there." She touched Rosalind's temple, feather-light. "He's in the truth now. Anyone who reads the Gate's record, ever, will know a smuggler named Dain Ferro died covering a child's escape, and that a storm-caller loved him enough to pay for the telling."

"That's not the same as remembering him."

"No," Maren agreed. "It isn't. I'm sorry. I am so sorry. It was your choice to make, and you made it, and I will spend whatever years I have left making sure it was not wasted."

Rosalind lay back against the cold stone and looked up at the thinning clouds, and took inventory of herself like a merchant counting stock after a raid.

She remembered her childhood—or pieces of it, islands in fog. The citadel towers. Learning to call her first thunderstorm, the terror and joy of it. She remembered the vault records, the planted betrayal, the truth of her exile—those had burned *with* the lie, strangely, and yet their conclusion remained, verified fact without the wound attached, like a case file read rather than a life lived. She remembered Wren's name. She remembered the road, in outline: Saddle Rocks, Ironhollow, the Gate Road. She remembered the storm she had called, though the calling itself felt like something she'd read about in someone else's journal.

Everything else was white. Ten years of white, with a few names floating in it.

She started to laugh, and it hurt, and she did it anyway.

"Sterling," she rasped. "Is that my name? It feels like my name."

"It's your name," Maren said. "One of the few you have left. Guard it."

Down the slope, where the storm-wall had collapsed and the armies had stood frozen through the final act of the rite, movement: the wraiths of the Hollow Court withdrawing in perfect silence, ranks dissolving into the morning mist like frost off glass. Sereth stood a moment longer at the rear of them, looking back at the Gate—at her—and then bowed, deeply, formally, the bow of a herald acknowledging a coronation, and was gone.

And beyond the wraiths, at the edge of sight, storm-caller white cloaks, and a tall figure in silver-grey who did not retreat. Ilvane stood alone in the ruin of her assault, staring at the dark archway, understanding—too late, far too late—that everything inside it was now beyond her reach, and that the woman she had unmade ten years ago had just unmade the Order's greatest secret in return.

Ilvane began to walk up the slope toward the Gate. Alone. Unarmed.

Toward her.

Rosalind pushed herself upright on shaking arms, the seed humming vast and patient behind her ribs, a whole buried nation's truth waiting in her hollowed-out heart—and watched her former mentor climb toward her through the rain, and found, in the empty rooms of herself, nothing left to fear.

"Maren," she said. "Help me stand. I believe I'm about to remember something honestly."
