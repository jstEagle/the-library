## 22. When the Hollow Turns

The sanctuary at Vessic's Rest had been a ruin when Rosalind was born, and it was not yet whole now, but the scaffolding of new cedar rose against the old white stone like ribs around a healing heart. She stood at the gate for a long time before she knocked, because the last time she had passed through this valley, the mountains had been screaming and her hands had been full of storm.

That was eight months ago. Some days it felt like eight years. Other days, like yesterday.

The gatekeeper was a young woman with a scar across her jaw and the patient eyes of someone who had learned to wait out worse things than weather.

"You're expected," she said, which surprised Rosalind until it didn't. Word traveled strangely here, carried on channels older than roads. "She talks about you."

"She can't—"

"She can." The gatekeeper smiled faintly. "You'll see."

* * *

Wren met her in the courtyard, where the old bell tower had been rebuilt just high enough to ring again. The girl was nine now — or they had decided she was nine, since no one knew for certain and Wren had chosen the number herself with the gravity of a queen selecting a crown. Her hair had grown back in from the frostbite, dark and thick, and she wore it braided in the Vessic style, coiled at the nape like a sleeping bird.

She did not run to Rosalind. She never ran; that was something the seed's blooming had taken from her, the heedless abandon of childhood sprinting. Instead she walked, quickly, deliberately, and stopped one pace away, and looked up with those gray eyes that had seen three centuries of grief before they'd ever seen a summer.

Rosalind crouched to her height. It was getting harder to do. Her left knee ached in cold weather now, an ache she could not remember earning, because the memory of whatever had injured it had been spent along with so much else.

"You've grown," Rosalind said.

*You have too,* Wren signed, then spoke aloud, carefully, each common-tongue word placed like a stone across a stream: "I am learning. Maren says my accent is terrible."

"It is," Rosalind said, and Wren laughed, and the sound went through Rosalind like warm water through frost-cracked ground.

They walked the courtyard together. Wren pointed out everything: the herb beds the children had planted, the bell rope woven from mountain hemp — *your mountains,* Wren signed, *I told them your mountains make the best rope* — the wall where survivors' names were being carved, one by one, by a stonemason who worked slowly enough that the list would take decades, which everyone agreed was right. Names should be earned into stone slowly. They held better that way.

Rosalind read the names as they passed. Some she recognized from the seed's blooming, the terrible night at the Gate when the truth had opened like a flower made of fire and she had understood, finally, completely, what had been done to the Vessic people three hundred years ago — not by the Vessic, but by the kings and the Order together, the atrocity blamed on its victims, the war built on a lie so large that entire nations had marched into it willingly.

Her name wasn't on the wall. But her name was elsewhere, in the records Maren had restored, among the priests listed as having carried the memory-seed out of the burning city. Priests plural. One of them had been her — or a version of her, ten years ago, before Ilvane had hollowed out her mind and planted the false betrayal and set her loose in the world like a letter no one would ever open.

*I remember some of it,* Wren signed as they walked. *From inside. Not all. Maren says I'm not supposed to try to hold all of it anymore.*

"Maren is right."

*But I remember you.* Wren stopped walking. She took Rosalind's hand, small fingers against scarred knuckles, and turned it palm-up, studying the calluses there. *In the seed, when it was too big for me, you put yourself between me and it. You were the bank of the river. I could break against you instead of breaking.*

"I don't remember doing it," Rosalind said quietly. That was the honest truth, and honesty was the only currency she dealt in now. "I know that I did. I chose to write it down afterward so I would know. But the remembering itself—" She shook her head. "It's gone. Spent."

Wren frowned, the fierce frown of a child confronting injustice. *That's not fair.*

"No."

*You lost more than anyone. You didn't even do anything wrong.*

"Neither did you." Rosalind squeezed her hand. "We're even, then."

* * *

Maren Kestrel found them at midday, in the scriptorium where Rosalind had claimed a desk by the eastern window. The archivist moved slowly these days, leaning on a cane of black ironwood, though whether from age or from the ritual she had helped design four centuries ago, she never said. Her eyes were still sharp enough to cut.

"You've been writing all morning," Maren said, nodding at the pages spread across the desk. "May I?"

Rosalind slid them over without ceremony. There was no privacy between them anymore; Maren had seen the inside of her sacrifice, had conducted the rite that let Rosalind pour her memories into the anchor while the truth bloomed safely in the space she emptied. Maren knew exactly what Rosalind no longer knew.

The pages were plain. That was deliberate. Rosalind wrote in a flat, unadorned hand, recording only what could be verified: dates cross-referenced, sources named, claims marked *confirmed*, *disputed*, or *unknown*. It was the least romantic document imaginable, and she loved it fiercely.

"The Order fractured further," Maren said, scanning. "Three citadels have formally renounced the exiles' verdicts. Ilvane is in custody at Silvercrown, awaiting judgment. Sereth testified."

"Sereth." Rosalind turned the name over. The revenant agent, the Court's patient hunter, who at the Gate had knelt before the bloomed truth and wept — if what he did could be called weeping, a sound like wind through a dead forest. "The Court let him speak for them?"

"The Court has no voice of its own. That was always the tragedy." Maren set down the pages. "They were silenced so thoroughly that even their ghosts forgot how to testify. He learned, in the end. From watching you."

Rosalind looked out the window. Below, in the courtyard, Wren was teaching a circle of younger children a game involving knotted cords and counting songs in Vessic, the dead language alive again in small mouths, growing new speakers the way grass grows through rubble.

"And the kingdoms?" she asked. "The war councils? All the people who were ready to march?"

"Reading." Maren smiled thinly. "The full record went out under seal to every throne, every order, every archive. No one can claim ignorance now. Whether they choose peace or war—" she spread her hands, "—they will at least choose it with open eyes. That's all truth ever guarantees. Not outcomes. Clarity."

"That's more than I hoped for."

"It's more than anyone hoped for. Three hundred years, Rosalind. Wars have ended over less than clarity, and started over less than its absence." Maren lowered herself into the chair across the desk, wincing, and fixed Rosalind with a look that had probably been unnerving students since before the Thornfell passes had names. "Now. The real question. Have you decided?"

Rosalind had known this conversation was coming. She picked up the top page of her manuscript and smoothed it flat, buying a moment.

"There's a gap," she said. "Right at the center. Ten years of exile, and before that, the ritual itself — I gave those up to anchor the seed. Everything else I've rebuilt from documents. Letters I sent Dain. Vault records. Testimony from the rite. But the middle of me is still missing, and every account of it comes from other people."

"Yes."

"Ilvanne's trial begins next month. They want me to testify about the ritual — the planting of the false memories, the original seed-carrying. And I can't. Not from memory. Only from paper." She looked up. "So I've been thinking about what Maren-the-younger wrote in her notes. The reconstruction rite. The one that reads an anchor's residue without unbinding it."

Maren's expression did not change, which meant it had changed enormously. "That rite is theoretical."

"It was theoretical. Then you performed a version of it at the Gate. I've read your notes."

"At the Gate, the alternative was watching a child die. Desperation is a fine teacher." The old woman leaned forward. "Listen to me, Rosalind Sterling, because I am very old and very tired and I will only say this once. What you are considering — reaching back into the anchor, pulling fragments of your own given memories out of the truth you saved — it may not work. If it works, it may hurt. If it works and doesn't hurt, it may bring back things you gave away for good reasons. You gave up the ritual-night itself. You gave up ten years of exile. Do you truly want those back? The loneliness? The cold cabins? The years of being a stranger to yourself?"

Rosalind thought about it honestly, which was the discipline she practiced daily now: honesty first, comfort second, always in that order.

"I don't want them back," she said. "I want to know them. Those years made me who I am standing here, and I built myself on them blind. I'd like to see the foundation, even if I leave the stones where they lie."

Maren studied her for a long moment. Then, slowly, she began to smile — the rare, crooked smile of a woman who had waited four centuries for people to start making choices this sane.

"I'll prepare the rite," she said. "Spring, when the ley-lines settle. Until then—" she tapped the manuscript, "—keep writing. Verified truth, plainly stated. It's the most radical thing anyone in this valley has done in three hundred years, and I include the storm you threw at two armies."

* * *

Dain Ferro's caravan came through in late autumn, and Rosalind walked down to meet it, because she always would now.

He looked older. Everyone did; the Gate had aged them all, some in years and some in other currencies. His limp had worsened, the wound Ilvane's blade had given him never quite healing clean, and his beard had gone gray at the chin in a way that made him look distinguished until he grinned and ruined it.

"Well," he said, swinging down from the lead wagon. "If it isn't the terror of two armies, standing in a doorway like a landlady."

"Pay your road-toll, captain."

"Toll?" He pressed a hand to his chest. "I brought supplies. Medicine. Three crates of southern wine that Maren specifically requested, which frightens me deeply. And—" his voice gentled, the way it did around exactly one subject, "—letters from the lowland sanctuaries. News."

They sat on the wagon bench while he unpacked, passing a flask between them, and he told her the news the way he told all news: sideways, buried in complaints about weather and tariffs and the quality of southern horses, so that you had to dig for it, and digging made it yours.

The Order's reformists had won control of two more citadels. The Hollow Court had sent envoys — actual envoys, living ones, revenants retired at last to whatever rest revenants could find — to negotiate the return of the stolen Vessic lands. Trade routes were reopening through the northern passes, unburned, unbarricaded. Children were being born in towns whose grandfathers had died in skirmishes neither side remembered starting, because no one had ever known why the war began.

"Now they know," Dain finished. He looked at her sidelong. "Because of you."

"Because of a lot of people."

"Because of you, and a mute child, and a smuggler who never learned when to quit, and a four-hundred-year-old woman with a cane." He drank. "Mostly you, though. Don't argue with a wounded man, it's cruel."

"How is the wound?"

"Boring. Heals slow, aches in rain, gives me an excuse to sit down at parties." He capped the flask. "And how are the gaps?"

Rosalind considered lying, decided against it — with him it had never once been worth it — and answered plainly. "Smaller than they were. Or maybe I've just gotten better at building around them. Some days I reach for something and it's not there, and I feel like a house with rooms I've never entered. But the house stands."

"And the writing helps?"

"The writing is the house."

He nodded slowly. Then, gruffly, looking out at the mountains rather than at her: "The girl asks after you every time we pass within fifty miles. Won't stop. Drives my crew mad. 'When is she coming, when is she coming.' You should visit more."

"I'm here now."

"You're here now," he agreed. "That's something." A pause, and then, quieter, the closest Dain Ferro ever came to sentiment: "Ten years I paid you to walk my wagons through the dark. Best money I ever spent. And the year you walked them for free — for a child, for the truth, for all of us—" he shook his head. "There aren't words. Good thing you're the writer."

* * *

Winter came early to the high valley, soft and dry, the kind of snow that fell straight down as if the sky were setting the world aside gently for sleep. Rosalind stayed through it. There was nowhere she needed to be, and for the first time in her life, that felt like freedom rather than exile.

She wrote. Every morning, in the scriptorium, by lamplight and then by the thin blue light off the snow: the verified record, page after page. *On this date, confirmed by three witnesses. On this date, per vault records 44 through 61. This claim rests on testimony alone and is marked accordingly.* She wrote the history of the war. She wrote the history of the seed. She wrote, in careful neutral language, the history of herself — what was documented, what was attested, what was unknown — and she resisted, every day, the urge to fill the unknowns with invention. Gaps were gaps. A house that admitted its empty rooms was stronger than one papered over with pretty lies.

Some evenings, Wren came to the scriptorium. The girl would sit across the desk with her own work — she was transcribing recovered Vessic texts now, her handwriting precise, her patience bottomless — and they would work in silence broken only by nib-scratch and the tick of the stove. Twice a night, usually, Wren would look up and sign a question, or say a new common word she'd mastered, testing it aloud like a coin bitten for gold.

Tonight, near the end of the evening's light, she set down her pen and studied Rosalind across the desk with an expression Rosalind had learned to recognize: the gathering of something important.

*Maren says you might try to read the anchor in spring,* Wren signed. *To see your missing pieces.*

"If it's safe. If Maren thinks—"

*Will you give them back?* The signs were quick, almost angry. *The pieces. If you see them, will you take them back into you?*

Rosalind set down her pen. "Why does it matter?"

Wren's hands hesitated — a rarity, in a child who signed with the fluency of a native speaker, which she was, in both tongues now. Finally:

*Because the Rosalind who stood between me and the seed was the one who had already given everything away.* Her gray eyes were steady, ancient, certain. *The gaps are where you kept your courage. I don't want you to fill them in and become someone else.*

The words landed in Rosalind like a stone dropped down a well — falling a long way, striking water somewhere deep.

"Wren," she said carefully, "the woman who stood between you and the seed made that choice *because* of who she was before. The exile years, the ritual, all of it. The gaps didn't create her courage. They're just where it cost her."

*Maybe.* Wren shrugged, unconvinced, nine years old and immovable. *But promise you'll think about it. Before spring.*

"I promise."

*Good.* And then, gathering her papers, heading for the door, the girl paused — and turned back, and said, in her careful, accented, hard-won common tongue, the sentence she had clearly been building toward all evening:

"Goodnight, Rosalind."

Not *goodnight* in general. Not a word learned from a lesson. Her name. Her own name, in Wren's mouth, spoken aloud, the first time the child had ever said it — Rosalind knew it instantly, the way you know a bell you've heard ring true, and she sat very still after the door closed, listening to the silence where the sound had been, feeling it settle into her like snowfall settling on a roof: weightless, cumulative, and strong enough to bear what came next.

She pulled a fresh sheet toward her. Dipped her pen. At the top of the page, beneath the date, she wrote the words that began every entry now, the creed of the house she was building room by room from truth:

*I do not remember this yet. But I am learning to, honestly. And that is enough to go on.*

Outside, the snow kept falling on the sanctuary, on the bell tower, on the graves and gardens and the long slow carving of names into stone, and in the east, past the mountains she had once fled through and now called home, the first pale hint of dawn touched the peaks — the hollow turning, as it always did, toward morning.
