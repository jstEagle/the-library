## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The trick was never the taking. Anyone with steady hands and a cold heart could take. The trick was the leaving—walking out of a room with something that had never been yours while the person you'd taken it from still smiled at you, still poured your drink, still thought the evening a pleasant one.

Kestrel Kavanagh had been doing it for eleven years, and he had never once been caught.

He sat now at the corner table of the Drowned Goose, where the lamplight pooled amber and the noise of the taproom wrapped him like a second coat. Across from him, Odile Ferrant—a cloth merchant with salt-and-pepper hair and hands that had counted coin for forty years—was telling him about her daughter's wedding for the third time.

"And the cake," she said, her eyes shining in a way that told Kestrel exactly which memory to want. "You should have seen the cake. Six tiers. Six! And Marisol laughing so hard when the wind took her veil that she cried."

"Tell me everything," Kestrel said. "I love weddings. Never get to see them myself."

It wasn't even a lie. He did love them—the way a starving man loves the smell of bread through someone else's window.

Odile leaned forward, delighted to be asked, and this was the part people never understood: they *wanted* to give it away. Not consciously, not in any way they'd admit by daylight. But there was a hunger in everyone to hand their brightest moments to a stranger who listened properly, because carrying joy alone was heavier than anyone liked to confess. Kestrel had learned that early. People didn't guard their happiness. They flaunted it, offered it, pressed it into his hands like a gift.

He let her talk until the third telling wore smooth as river stone, until every detail sat in his mind like furniture in his own house—the six tiers, the veil snatched by wind, the groom's father weeping into his sleeve, the fiddler who played until dawn. Then he reached across the table under the pretense of refilling her cup, and his fingertips brushed her wrist, feather-light, the touch of a man steadying a cup he'd nudged.

And he *pulled*.

It came away clean, the way a ripe plum comes off the branch. The wedding slid out of Odile Ferrant and into Kestrel, whole and gleaming, and what remained behind was a soft-edged absence she would fill by morning with something plausible—a small wedding, perhaps, or an ordinary one. She'd grieve a little without knowing why, feel that her life had a draft in it somewhere, and then the valley's great mercy would do its work.

Because here was the truth of the Hollow, the truth carved into every fencepost and funeral stone: *the valley forgets.* Memories left unattended faded fast here, like ink in rain. The mist that ringed the peaks saw to it. Whatever else the mist kept out—and it kept out magic, all magic, absolutely—it also kept the valley's own people from holding too tightly to anything. Griefs softened. Joys dimmed. The dead became photographs, then names, then weather.

Which meant no one missed what Kestrel took. Which meant he could eat.

"I must be more tired than I thought," Odile said, blinking, her cup halfway to her lips. "What were we—ah. Your bread, wasn't it? I promised you bread for the road." She frowned faintly, patting her pockets, then laughed. "Well, I'll owe you. Next market day."

"No need," Kestrel said smoothly. "The story was payment enough."

She smiled at him, warm and vague, and he felt the familiar thing twist in his chest—the one he refused to name, because naming it would mean admitting that the warmth she gave him cost her something, even if she'd never know what.

* * *

He walked home through streets the fog had claimed early, past shuttered windows and gutters running with meltwater, and the stolen wedding played behind his eyes the whole way. He couldn't help it. It was the best part of the work, the only part he allowed himself to enjoy: the replaying. The groom's nervous speech. Marisol's laugh, high and startled. The moment the wind lifted the veil and the whole crowd gasped as one breath.

In the Hollow, nobody got to keep moments like that. They happened and then they thinned, like frost on glass. Kestrel had decided long ago that if the valley insisted on forgetting, somebody ought to remember—even if the somebody was a thief, even if the keeping was done in a locked cellar by a man with no right to any of it.

His safehouse was a crooked cottage at the end of Tanner's Row, the kind of place no one looked at twice. Inside, past the false wall behind the wood stove, his hoard waited: hundreds of little clay vessels, each one stoppered with wax, each one humming faintly if you held it to your ear. A memory in a jar. His whole cellar full of other people's lives, sorted and shelved like a grocer's goods.

*Weddings, north shelf. Births beside them. Funerals in the back, where the dark ones belong.*

He set tonight's harvest in its place between a fisherman's first catch and a child's first snowfall, and stood a moment in the cold cellar air, listening to the shelves murmur.

Eleven years of this. Eleven years since he'd learned—no, since he'd been *taught*, though the memory of the teaching was itself a strange blurred thing he'd never sold and never examined—that his hands could do what they did. Eleven years of trading other people's brightness for bread, lamp oil, boot leather, silence.

It was a good living. It was a careful living. Nobody in the Hollow knew his name meant anything at all.

That was the design, and he had built it himself, stone by stone: be forgettable, be useful to no one's suspicion, take only what fades anyway. Control. He told himself the word often enough that it almost sounded true.

* * *

Maren received him at her shop off Coop Lane the next evening, as she always did, with the shutters half-drawn and the ledger already open. She was a narrow woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes that priced everything they landed on, including him.

"The usual?" she asked.

"The usual." He laid three vessels on her counter. The wedding. A cooper's memory of his late wife singing—he'd taken it kindly, he told himself, from a man who flinched whenever the song surfaced unbidden. And a farmer's recollection of a green summer before the blight, bright enough to make Maren's eyebrows rise.

Her fingers hovered over the farmer's jar longer than necessary. "This is good stock," she said. "Beyond the mist, they pay for this in silver. Real silver. People out there are starving for—" She stopped herself, the way she always did.

"For what?"

"For proof," Maren said. "That places like this exist. That forgetting can be survived." She uncorked the wedding memory just a breath, and the smell of cake and cut grass drifted into the room, absurd and heartbreaking, before she sealed it again. Her face did something complicated. "Bread, oil, and two bits of tin."

"Bread, oil, and three bits."

Maren smiled thinly. "Three. Because you're my best supplier and I'm feeling rich." She counted out the coins, then paused, and for a moment something old moved behind her eyes—something that looked almost like conscience, or the ghost of one. "Kestrel. Do you ever wonder what happens to them? The memories, after they cross the mist."

"They're memories. They don't happen. They just *are*."

"That's what worries me about you," she said quietly, and wrote the entry in her ledger with a flourish that seemed designed to end conversations.

He pocketed his coins and went out into the wet street, annoyed to find her question had followed him. He ate half the loaf walking home and told himself the tightness in his chest was just the bread going down dry.

* * *

The rules of the Hollow were simple, and he recited them sometimes to himself like a prayer, or a fence:

*Mist keeps magic out. Mist keeps memory loose. Nothing crosses the mist except what Maren smuggles, and nothing comes in at all.*

That last rule was the one he loved best. Nothing came in. No soldiers, no priests, no debt collectors, no one carrying questions. The valley had been closed for longer than anyone could accurately remember—which was, of course, the point. Generations ago, something had happened at the mist-line, something sealed and sworn, and whatever covenant had been struck back then still held, silent and absolute. Magic could not follow a man into the Hollow. Neither could the outer realms' wars, their wild sorceries, their hungry gods. The Hollow was the one place in the world where a man's soul was his own, unhaunted.

Kestrel had staked his whole existence on that fact.

So when he reached his door that night and found his lamp guttering though he'd trimmed the wick that morning, he told himself it was the damp. When the dog down the lane stared past him into the dark and would not bark, he told himself dogs were fools. And when the wind coming off the fells carried a sound he hadn't heard in eleven years—not a word, exactly, but the *shape* of one, spoken in a language that made his teeth ache and his mother's face flicker, unasked, through his mind—

When that came, he stopped in the middle of Tanner's Row, bread under his arm, oil can swinging from his fist, and stood very still in the fog.

Nothing came in.

The wind said the shape again, patient as a knock.

Kestrel stood in the empty street with his careful, controlled, well-ordered life held under one arm, and understood—with the flat certainty of a man watching the first crack spread through ice—that something had just come in.
