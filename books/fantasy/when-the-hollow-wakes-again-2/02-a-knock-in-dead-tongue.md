## 2. A Knock in Dead Tongue

The storm came down the valley an hour after dark, the way storms always did in the Hollow—shouldering through the western pass like something with a grudge, bending the pines flat, flinging rain against my shutters hard enough to rattle the iron latch. I counted the gusts out of habit. Seven between lightning and thunder. Close.

I was counting coin when the knock came.

Three raps. Small ones. The kind of knock that didn't expect to be answered.

Nobody knocked on my door. That was the point of living where I lived—a crooked house at the ragged end of Lowmere village, past the tannery stink, where the lane gave up pretending it went anywhere and became mud and bramble. People came to me only when they wanted something taken from them, and they came by daylight, hat in hand, ashamed. Nobody knocked at night. Nobody knocked in a storm.

I put the knife back in the drawer where I kept it—not for people, for the rare occasions when a traveler's mind fought harder than their body—and went to see who was foolish enough to be standing in weather that could drown a goat.

The girl couldn't have been more than eight or nine. Barefoot. Soaked to the color of wet slate. She stood on my step with her chin up and her fists at her sides, and she was not shivering, which was the first wrong thing. The second was that she looked at me the way nobody in the Hollow ever had: without wanting anything. Or rather, wanting something so enormous that everything else—warmth, shelter, sense—had been boiled away beneath it.

She spoke.

Not the common tongue. Not the trader's pidgin the caravans used, not the lowland drawl, nothing I could name. It was a language of open vowels and falling ends, words like water going over stones, and every syllable of it did something impossible—it *meant* something. Half-meant. Like hearing your own name called across a crowded market: you can't make out the sentence, but you know you've been summoned.

*Vess iru keth. Vess iru keth, na hallow.*

Hallow. My own word, buried inside hers.

"Come inside," I said, because whatever else was happening here, she was nine years old and barefoot in a flood, and I had not yet sunk so far as to leave a child on a doorstep. I stepped aside. She walked in past me without hesitating, the way someone walks into a place they already know, and stopped in the middle of my one room, dripping, turning slowly like a compass needle finding north.

Her eyes snagged on the floorboards near the hearth. On the loose plank there, under the rug. The one over my hoard.

I felt that look go through me like a fishhook.

"Sit," I said. "Don't touch anything."

She sat on the stool by the fire, hands folded, spine straight, patient as a churchgoer. I got a blanket off the chest and dropped it around her shoulders, mostly so I wouldn't have to keep looking at how blue her lips weren't. Because they weren't. Blue lips were what nine-year-olds got after an hour in cold rain, and this child had been out in it longer than an hour—I could tell by the state of her feet, cut and gray with chill—and she wasn't shivering and she wasn't pale and she was watching me with eyes the exact brown of river water over gravel.

"Where's your mother?" I asked.

She said a string of that language again, soft, and shook her head. Then, deliberately, she pointed at her own mouth and made the smallest gesture I'd ever seen anyone make: two fingers, parted slightly, closing. A door shutting. A voice shutting.

Mute. Or choosing to be.

"All right," I said. "You don't talk. Fine. You're talking anyway, just not with your mouth."

That earned me nothing. No fear, no confusion. Just that level, patient stare, as if we were both waiting for me to catch up.

I put water on to warm and dug out bread that wasn't too stale. While the kettle muttered, I studied her the way I study everyone, because studying is the trade I practice. Hands: no rings, no calluses beyond what any child gets. Clothes: wool, good weave, dyed a deep madder-red that hadn't faded—which meant either money or somewhere far away where dye was cheap. And around her neck, on a leather cord gone black with rain:

A pendant. Bronze. Shaped like a crescent moon, except it was broken—one horn snapped clean off, the fracture old, its edges worn smooth by years of handling. Someone had rubbed that bronze until it shone. Someone small, with a nervous thumb.

I knew crescents. Every soul in the Hollow knew crescents; they hung over doors against bad luck, they stamped the coins of the old kings nobody remembered, they got carved on boundary stones along the mist-line. But those were decorative. Full circles, closed shapes, luck locked up tight where it couldn't leak. This was different. This crescent opened eastward, toward where the moon rises instead of sets, and it had writing on it—tiny chased marks running along the inner curve, worn nearly flat.

And I knew the marks.

That's the part I still can't make peace with, even now. I looked at that broken little scrap of bronze and the marks on it arranged themselves in my head the way letters do, and behind the arrangement stood meaning, half-formed, like shapes seen through fogged glass. I understood maybe one word in five. Enough to know it was a warning. Enough to feel the shape of the rest pressing against the fog.

Because I'd heard this language before. Once. Years ago.

There's a memory I stole off a dying scholar on the high road, eleven years back—the kind of job I don't do anymore, taking from a man too weak to object. He'd spent his life chasing the First Covenant, the old binding, the reason the Hollow is the Hollow. Everyone in the valley knows the story the way children know nursery rhymes: long ago magic ran wild through these mountains, and when it turned cruel, our ancestors made a bargain and sealed it out, drew the mist around the valley like a lid on a pot. Inside the mist, no magic. Ever. The price was forgetting—the valley forgets, travelers forget, memories fray at the edges like cheap cloth. We live safe and we live shallow, and most people thank their ancestors for it and never wonder what the ancestors actually did.

The scholar had wondered. His head was full of it—dead languages, covenant-lore, fragments of oath-texts he'd spent forty years failing to translate. When I lifted his memories I took them all, because that's how it works, you take whole cloth, and then I sold most of them to Maren and kept a few scraps back, the way I always do, the way a magpie keeps the strangest shiny things. One scrap was him sitting in a tent at midnight reading aloud from a rubbing he'd taken off a stone, sounding out syllables in a tongue with no living speakers, laughing at himself for wasting his life on it.

*Vess iru keth,* the dead man had said in my stolen memory, to a dead language, alone in the dark.

The same words this child had said on my doorstep.

I set the bread down in front of her and stepped back and made myself breathe. Outside, the storm threw itself against the house again, and somewhere down the lane a shutter tore loose and banged away into the dark.

"Eat," I told her.

She ate. Small neat bites, hungry but controlled, the eating of a child who has learned food is not guaranteed. I watched her over the rim of my cup and felt the dread arrive properly now, settling into my bones like the damp.

Here's what I knew, laid out plain. Children don't walk out of the mist. Nothing walks out of the mist—the mist isn't a wall, it's a forgetting, things go in and come out the other side having lost the way, the why, the want. Travelers wander in confused and wander out poorer in years than they can account for. In thirty years of living here I had never once heard of anything arriving *through* it intact. And this girl had crossed the fells in a storm, barefoot, and stood at my door speaking a language that died before my grandmother's grandmother, holding a piece of the Covenant's own regalia, and looking at my floorboards like she knew exactly what slept under the rug.

She wasn't lost. Lost children cry. This one had come *for* something.

For me? The thought arrived uninvited and unwelcome. Nobody comes for Kestrel Kavanagh. I had spent twenty years making sure of it. I was the man people forgot to be angry at, forgot to suspect, forgot between one market day and the next. My entire survival rested on being forgettable, unremarkable, a smudge in the corner of the valley's eye. And now a mute child with covenant-bronze around her neck had walked through the one barrier that defines my world and knocked on my door in a dead tongue I could half-hear through a thief's memory, and the first thing her eyes had found was my hoard.

The kettle ticked as it cooled. The fire popped. She finished the bread and folded her hands again and watched me.

"What am I supposed to do with you?" I asked her.

She tilted her head. Then she reached up, slow, giving me time to stop her, and lifted the cord over her head. She held the broken crescent out on her open palm.

Every instinct I owned said don't touch it. Instincts are just memories wearing armor, though, and mine had never once protected me from anything that mattered. I picked the thing up.

It was lighter than it looked, warm—her warmth, nothing more, I told myself—and the moment my skin met the bronze, the scholar's stolen voice rose up loud in my skull, and this time the fog thinned further. Not words. Not sentences. But the *shape* of a sentence. Something formal. Something sworn. An instrument of promise, or of breaking.

And threaded through the shape, faint as a watermark, another memory surfaced—one I hadn't known I was carrying, because it wasn't the scholar's at all. It was mine. Mine and older than my trade: rain on stone, a woman's hand, a flash of bronze, a voice saying words in that same falling cadence. My mother's voice.

I put the crescent down on the table very carefully, the way you put down a snake.

My mother had been dead fifteen years, drowned in the tarn below the fells when I was fourteen, and in fifteen years I had sold and stolen and hoarded thousands of other people's yesterdays precisely so I would never have to sit alone with my own. I did not think about her. Thinking about her was a room in my head I kept locked, and I was good at locks.

The child watched me set it down and nodded once, satisfied, as if a message had been delivered and receipted. Then she pointed—at the crescent, at herself, at the floorboard over my hoard, and last at me. Four points. A sentence built from gestures.

*This. Me. What you're hiding. You.*

"No," I said.

She pointed again. Same order. Same patience. Rain hammered the roof and the fire spat and outside the storm walked its rounds, and this small soaked person sat in my kitchen pointing at the sum total of everything I'd spent my life protecting, and I realized with a lurch that I wasn't afraid of her.

I was afraid of what she knew.

"You can't stay here," I said, and it came out harsher than I meant. "Whatever you think I am, whoever sent you, you've got the wrong man. I take memories. That's all. I'm a pickpocket of the past. There's nothing in this house worth a covenant or a curse or whatever it is you're carrying." I heard my own voice climbing and made myself stop. "In the morning I'll take you to Maren's. She deals in strange goods. She'll know what to do with you."

At the name *Maren* something moved across the child's face—the first expression she'd shown besides that grave patience. It looked, absurdly, like recognition. Like I'd confirmed something she'd already suspected.

She reached out and touched the back of my hand. Just briefly. Her fingers were warm—impossibly, wrongly warm, given the night she'd had—and where they touched, I felt it again: that cadence, that falling speech, rising in me like water coming up through a floor. As if she weren't speaking to me at all but *tuning* me, the way you'd tune an instrument left too long in the cold.

Then she withdrew her hand, pulled the blanket tighter, and lay down on the rug beside the fire without asking permission, curling small, eyes closing, done with me for the night. Trusting. Completely, outrageously trusting, in the house of a stranger who robbed people for a living.

I banked the fire and sat at the table with the broken crescent in front of me and did not sleep.

Somewhere past midnight the storm broke apart and the clouds shredded, and through the window I saw the moon—waxing, three nights off full, hanging fat and white over the mist-line. Ordinary. Except that as I watched, a band of cloud slid across it and the light dimmed, and for the space of four heartbeats the moonlight through my window fell in a color I had no name for. Greenish. Old. The light of something reflected off bronze.

Then the cloud passed and the moon was just the moon, and I told myself I was tired, and I almost believed it.

On the rug, the girl slept with one hand curled around the empty cord at her throat.

In the morning, I told myself, I'd take her to Maren. Let the broker's sharp eyes and sharper ledger sort out what this was. Wash my hands of it. Go back to being a smudge.

But I caught myself, twice before dawn, looking at the floorboard over my hoard—and catching myself wondering, for the first time in eleven years, what else might be buried in the memories I'd never sold.

What else my mother's voice might be saying in there, if I dared to listen.
