## 3. What the Broker Buys

The token sat on my table all morning, and I did not touch it.

That was the discipline of the trade. You learn early that a thing you don't understand is a thing that understands *you*—it waits, patient as a spider, for your hand. I'd spent eleven years in the Hollow learning what to take and what to leave, and every rule I had said leave this.

But rules are for men with options.

I turned it over at last with the edge of a spoon, careful not to skin it with my fingers. Bronze, greened at the seams, shaped like a crescent moon caught mid-wane. It was broken clean through the middle, and the break was old—old enough that the metal had weathered on both sides of the wound, so whatever had snapped it hadn't been dropped or lost but deliberately sundered, and long ago. Along the inner curve ran a line of script in that dead language Wren spoke, the characters thin and deliberate, like footprints left by something walking very carefully.

The girl herself was asleep in the loft above, curled under my spare blanket with her wet boots drying by the stove. She'd slept like the drowned all night—no stirring, no dreams I could hear—and woken and eaten two bowls of barley porridge without a word, watching me with those grey eyes that made me feel like the one being studied. Then she'd gone back to sleep, because children do that, because they can afford to.

And I sat with a broken moon in my kitchen and thought about Maren.

Maren Solace kept her shop in the low town past the tannery row, where the smell of the hides covered whatever else needed covering. She was the only broker in the Hollow worth the name—the only one with lines running through the mist to buyers who paid in coin that wasn't local, coin minted for people who'd never seen this valley and never wanted to. Everyone knew what she was. Nobody said it out loud, because everyone in the Hollow had sold her something. A first kiss. A dead father's voice. The memory of a betrayal too heavy to carry. She bought grief by the ounce and sold it on to strangers beyond the mist who consumed other lives the way we consume bread, and she'd grown comfortable and plump and careful on the trade, and she had never once asked where I got what I brought her, which told me she suspected exactly where I got it.

She was also the most educated person I knew. If anyone could read crescents and dead tongues, it was Maren.

I wrapped the token in flannel, tucked it into my coat against my ribs, and stood at the bottom of the loft ladder for a long moment, listening to the small sound of the child's breathing.

Here's what nobody tells you about stealing memories: you get good at reading risk. Every mark has a texture—a merchant is different from a soldier is different from a widow—and you develop a sense, almost a taste, for danger. And standing there listening to a mute orphan breathe, I felt the taste of danger so strong it coated my tongue like copper. Not from her. From her *arrival*. Things don't walk barefoot through storms to the door of the Hollow's only thief unless something sent them, or something drove them, and either way the something would come looking.

Best to know what I was carrying before it came looking.

"Maren," I told the sleeping loft. "Just Maren. In and out."

The Hollow heard me and, as usual, said nothing.

* * *

The low town was waking when I came down through it: fishwives arguing over the morning catch, a smith hammering out his first heat, two boys chasing a dog that was clearly winning. Normal sounds. Valley sounds. I let them wash over me and tried to be a man doing an errand, a man with nothing stranger about him than a bad night's sleep.

It half worked until I passed the well on Tanner's Square and realized three separate conversations had paused when I walked by. Not stopped—paused, the way water pauses around a stone. Then resumed. I told myself I imagined it. I'm a thief; imagining things is part of the toolkit. But the back of my neck stayed cold the whole way to the tannery row, and when I glanced back from the corner of the lane, a woman I didn't know was still looking at me. Not hostile. Curious. Like I'd become interesting.

Interesting is death in the Hollow. Interesting is how they found the last man who dealt outside the rules, though what they actually found was his coat, and his boots inside it.

Maren's shop sat behind the tannery like a barnacle on a whale, squat and dark-windowed, its sign a painted eye with the pupil scraped out. I'd been coming here eleven years. I knew the bell over her door had its clapper muffled with wax so customers could pretend their shame was private. I pushed inside into the smell of lamp oil and dried lavender—she used the lavender to mask the smell of the memories themselves, which some buyers swear go sour if stored near grief—and waited while my eyes adjusted.

"Maren."

She emerged from the back room wiping her hands on an apron already ruined, a broad soft woman built like a flour sack, with small quick eyes set in a face that had learned forty years ago to give away nothing. Her eyes gave away everything, if you knew where to look, and I knew where to look. Right now they were doing the thing they always did when I arrived: cataloguing me, weighing me, deciding what mood I was selling in.

"Kestrel Kavanagh." My full name, always, delivered flat as a ledger line. "You're early. Early means desperate, and desperate means discount—for me." She smiled without warmth. "What have you got?"

"Question first. Answer second." I put the flannel bundle on her counter. "I need to know what this says."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction—an expression of professional offense. "I buy and sell memories, Kestrel. I'm not a scholar of antiquities."

"You read four languages and collect books you can't sell to anyone in this valley. Open it."

A pause. Then she shrugged, all affront abandoned, and unwrapped the flannel with fingers surprisingly delicate for their size.

I watched her face.

Here's the thing about Maren Solace: in eleven years I had watched her appraise memories of weddings and funerals, betrayals and births, a soldier's last stand and a bride's first night, and her face had never changed once. Never. It was the flattest face in the Hollow, professionally flattened, deliberately, the way a road is flattened so nothing trips on it.

It changed now.

Not much. That was the horror of it. Just a stillness that went a shade too deep, a blink held half a beat too long, and then—there, in the small quick eyes—something I'd never seen in them before.

Fear.

She looked up at me, and for one unguarded moment Maren Solace looked *afraid*, and then she looked down again fast, and by the time her gaze came back up the fear had been folded away and something else was sitting in its place, smooth and shining, and I recognized that something because I'd seen it a hundred times across this counter.

Greed.

"Where," she said quietly, "did you get this?"

"A question for a question. What does it say?"

"It's a covenant-token." She turned the broken crescent in the lamplight, and her thumb traced the inner curve with something close to reverence, which frightened me more than the fear had. "First Covenant. Pre-mist. There are maybe six of these in existence that I've ever heard referenced, and five are accounted for—in collections, beyond the mist, behind locks that cost kingdoms. This one—" she weighed it in her palm, "—this one should not exist. And it should certainly not be *broken*."

"Broken how? On purpose?"

"The break is ritual. Deliberate. Someone severed it to release—" She stopped herself. Closed her mouth. Opened it again. "Kestrel. Where did you get it?"

"A child carried it to my door last night. Through the storm. Barefoot."

Something flickered across her face—too fast to name. "A child."

"A girl. Maybe eight years old. Can't speak common. Talks in the old tongue."

The lamplight seemed to gutter. Or maybe that was just Maren going very still.

"Describe her," Maren said. Her voice had changed register entirely—all the shopkeeper's banter stripped out of it, leaving something raw and careful underneath, the voice of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like.

"Grey eyes. Dark hair, cropped short like someone cut it in a hurry. Scar on her left palm, old, deliberate—like the token. Cut, not earned." I hesitated, and then gave her the rest because lying to Maren about inventory was a habit I couldn't afford today. "And she talks in her sleep. Same language. Whole sentences. Like she's reciting something."

Maren set the token down on the counter between us with exaggerated care, the way you set down a live coal.

Then she straightened up, folded her hands over her ruined apron, and became someone else. I watched it happen—watched the fear get buried deeper, watched the greed come up to the surface and spread, warm and golden, across her whole bearing like butter over hot bread.

"Well," she said brightly. "Well, well, well. Kestrel Kavanagh, you've brought me the most interesting item of my career, and I am prepared to make you rich."

"How rich."

"Richer than you've ever been. Richer than you can spend in this valley, and I'll arrange the spending beyond it. A house past the mist. Land. A life with no more midnight work, no more cold rivers, no more looking over your shoulder." Her eyes were shining now, and I understood suddenly that she wasn't performing; the shine was real. "Name a figure. Any figure. Double it. I'll pay it."

"For a broken bit of bronze."

"For the bronze, certainly." She waved a hand, dismissive, generous. "But the bronze alone—no. No, Kestrel." She leaned across the counter, and lowered her voice, and her breath smelled of lavender and old tea. "For the bronze *and the child*."

The word landed in the room and sat there.

"I don't sell children."

"Everyone sells everything eventually; it's the only universal law I've found in forty years of trade." She said it gently, almost kindly, like a physician delivering a diagnosis she'd given a hundred times. "You don't have to bring her to me. Tell me where she is. Walk away. You'll never see anything, never be involved, never bear any weight of it. My buyers beyond the mist will handle everything. They'll treat her well—I'm assured of it. Assured." She smiled. "They're *very* interested in a child who speaks First Covenant. Very interested indeed. Do you understand what I'm telling you? They've been looking for one for longer than you've been alive."

"They."

"My employers." For the first time, Maren's composure cracked—just a hairline, just enough to show the strain underneath. "My *employers*, Kestrel. Who have never once cared what I bought, or from whom, or why, and who yesterday sent word asking after unusual arrivals in the valley. Asking specifically. Yesterday. Before your child knocked on your door. Think about what that means."

I thought about it. The thinking tasted like the copper again.

"No," I said.

"Kestrel—"

"No." I picked up the token and rewrapped it in the flannel, slowly, making her watch my hands do it. "I asked you a question. You answered it. We're square. I'll pay for the reading—one memory, standard rate, my choice of yours. Pick something pleasant off your shelf, I don't care what. And then I'm leaving."

"You're making a mistake."

"Probably."

"You're making a mistake that will find you." All the warmth had gone out of her now, and what remained was worse than anger—it was pity, real pity, the pity of a woman who had watched clever men die of their own decisions for forty years. "I like you, Kestrel. You've been good to me for eleven years. So listen to me once, freely given, no charge: you cannot hide a covenant-child in a valley the size of your fist. If my employers want her, they will have her, and the only variable is whether you're standing next to her when they arrive. Sell her to me. Be somewhere else. Live."

"And if I say no again?"

"Then heaven help you," Maren said simply, "because I won't be able to."

I put one memory on the counter—my choice, standard rate, I took a vintner's recollection of a perfect harvest morning thirty years gone, mostly to watch her log it, mostly to make the transaction ordinary, ordinary as armor—and I walked out of her shop with the crescent against my ribs and my heart going like a smith's hammer.

Behind me, I heard her move, quick and soft, toward the back room.

Toward the writing desk.

Toward the ink.

* * *

I took the long way home. Up through the sheep-paths, along the ridge where the mist hangs closest, checking my backtrail the old way—the way you check a backtrail when you've decided, somewhere below thought, that you might need one.

Nobody followed. That should have comforted me. It didn't, because I kept hearing Maren's voice saying *yesterday*, and I kept seeing her face change—that flat professional mask cracking open to show fear underneath, real fear, the kind that comes from knowing exactly how large the thing is that's hunting you.

My employers. Yesterday. Before Wren ever reached my door.

Which meant they weren't hunting something that had just arrived. They were hunting something they'd known was coming. Something expected. Something *promised*.

By evening I was back at the cottage, and the storm had cleared, and the light came slantwise gold through the mist-wall to the east the way it does after rain, and the valley lay quiet and ordinary and beautiful, and I hated it a little for that.

Wren was awake. She was sitting at my table—my table, my chair, my chipped bowl pushed aside to make room—and she had laid out the contents of my memory-hoard with both hands, handling each vial and locket and paper scroll with a delicacy that made my skin prickle, sorting them into rows. When she saw me she went still, caught, and I braced for guilt or defiance.

Instead she pointed at one row—the oldest pieces, the ones I'd taken years ago and never sold—and then pressed her scarred palm flat against her chest, and then pointed at the tokens, and said a stream of liquid syllables in that dead language, urgent, insistent, her grey eyes fixed on mine with a ferocity no eight-year-old should own.

I didn't understand the words.

But I understood the shape of them, somehow, the way you understand a tune from childhood you can't place—and the shape said: *one of these belongs to me. One of these is mine.*

And I stood in my own doorway, marked and hunted and afraid, holding a broken moon, and realized I was about to do something I'd never done in eleven years of careful, joyless competence.

I was going to open my hoard.
