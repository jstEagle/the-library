## 4. Thirteen Turns

The dream always began the same way: a door.

Not any door. The one she had knocked on three nights ago, in the storm, when the lightning had walked the ridge on legs of white fire and the rain had tried to wash her name out of her mouth. In the dream the door was taller than the sky and older than the moon, and it was closing, closing, closing, and behind it something vast was counting.

She could not see what counted. She could only hear it, and the hearing was worse than seeing would have been, because the counter's voice was made of every silence in the world stacked end to end like stones in a wall.

*Twelve,* said the voice behind the door, in the tongue that had no living speakers except her, and the moon above the dream-valley thinned by one sliver of its light.

Wren woke screaming.

She did not know she was screaming until hands found her shoulders — broad hands, scarred across the knuckles, smelling of lamp oil and cold iron — and a voice was saying, low and rough, *Wren. Wren. You're safe. You're in the house. Nothing is at the door.*

But there was something at the door. There had been something at the door for eleven nights now, and its name was *thirteen*, and it was coming down the count like water coming down a hill.

---

Kestrel had learned, in the years since he'd begun lifting memories from travelers, that a man who takes other people's pasts ends up carrying more than he can put down. Some of what he carried was useful — the smuggler's trick of reading a face, the shepherd's knack for weather, a soldier's way of standing so that trouble thought twice. And some of it was language. A scholar from the outer realms, six years back, had sold Kestrel a winter's worth of grammar and half a lexicon of the First Covenant's dead tongue before the man even knew his pockets had been picked. Kestrel had never used it. There was nobody left to speak it to.

Until the child came knocking.

Now he lay awake in the dark beside the pallet where she slept, listening to her breathe slow itself back into rhythm, and he understood her screaming the way you understand a song you've heard once, long ago, in another country: not word by word but shape by shape. The old tongue lived in her like water lives in a well, and some part of him — some part he had stolen without knowing what he stole — could draw from it.

*Moon-counting,* she had gasped, between sobs, the first night. He had thought at first she meant a nightmare about wolves. Now, on the eleventh night, lying in the dark, he finally heard it whole.

The moon was counting down. Thirteen turns, the dream said. Thirteen turns until the door finished closing — or until it broke open. She didn't know which. Neither, he suspected, did whatever stood behind it.

He got up, lit the lamp, and watched her sleep. Small face, storm-grey eyes shut tight, fists curled around the broken bronze crescent she refused to let go of even unconscious. The token had two prongs where there should have been a full circle of metal. Something had snapped it off, deliberately, long ago.

*Thirteen turns,* the child had whispered tonight, in her sleep-scream, and then — this was the new thing, the thing that turned his blood to well-water — she had said a second sentence, and he had understood it perfectly, and it had nothing to do with moons:

*It is counting in the valley too. It is counting everywhere.*

---

In the morning she wouldn't eat.

This was itself an alarm. Wren ate the way small animals eat, fast and constantly, as if famine were a personal enemy lying in wait. But today she sat at the table with bread untouched in front of her, turning the crescent over and over, and when Kestrel asked what was wrong she looked at him with those grey eyes and said a single word in the dead language, and he felt it land in his chest like a stone dropped down a well.

*Listening.*

"What's listening?" he asked, in common speech, and she shook her head — not because she didn't understand, but because there wasn't a common-tongue word for it. She pressed her hand flat against the table, against the floorboards, against the window glass. Listening. Everything. The wood. The stone. The air.

Then Kestrel opened the door, and the silence walked in and sat down at his table with them.

---

He had lived in the Hollow for most of his life, and he knew its sounds the way a miser knows his coins. The dawn chorus in the birches along the stream — thrush first, then wren, then the rude comedy of starlings. Dogs in the village, four of them within earshot, each with a signature bark he could price. The geese on the tarn, arguing all day like merchants haggling over nothing. Sheep complaining. Crows scheming. Even at night the valley breathed: owls, foxes, the small industrious teeth of mice in the walls.

On the twelfth morning after the child's knock, the valley held its breath.

No birdsong. None. He stood in his doorway and looked at trees full of birds — he could *see* them, little shapes on the branches, heads cocked — and not one of them made a sound. Down the slope, Tam Ostler's dog stood at the end of its chain facing the mist-line, ears up, utterly mute. When Kestrel walked past, the dog looked at him, and there was such intelligence in the look, such terrible attention, that he quickened his step.

At the stream he knelt and put his fingers in the water. It flowed. It simply didn't say anything while it flowed. Water made noise; water always made noise; this water moved over stone like a secret being kept.

"Can you hear it?" he asked Wren, and hated how carefully quiet his own voice had become, as if loudness were a debt someone had come to collect.

She nodded. Then she pointed upstream, downstream, at the birds, at the far grey smudge of the village, and last — her hand steady, her eyes not — at the mist.

The mist that ringed the valley. The wall that magic could not cross, the covenant made visible, the reason the Hollow was the Hollow. It hung on the ridgeline the way it always had, pale and patient. But this morning, for the first time in Kestrel's life, it was moving inward. Not much. An arm's width, maybe less, since yesterday.

Counting down.

---

Here is what the child knew, though she could not yet make Kestrel understand it whole, and here is what the valley knew, though it could only say it in silence:

Before the mist, before the forgetting, there had been an oath. Thirteen voices had sworn it at the valley's heart, in the drowned chapel, under a full moon — thirteen keepers binding magic out of the Hollow so that something inside it could sleep, or be imprisoned, or heal; the child knew the shape of the oath but not its purpose, the way you know the walls of your house without knowing why they were built where they were. Twelve of the thirteen voices were long dead. The oath had endured anyway, written into the mist itself, renewed each month by the moon's turning — thirteen turns of waxing and waning, and at the thirteenth turn, the turn that happened only once a year, the covenant drank deep and held.

But oaths are not stones. Oaths are debts, and debts need payers, and the last payer — the thirteenth keeper, the one whose blood was supposed to carry the renewal forward generation after generation — had broken her word.

The child did not know the breaker's name. She knew only that a woman had once stood at the mist-line with a bronze crescent in her hand and had sworn *not* to pass the oath to her heirs, and that the crescent had snapped in her fist like a promise snapping, and that the mist had begun, very slowly, very patiently, to forget how to hold.

And the child knew — because the key knows the lock it was cut for — that the counting had started. That the animals had gone silent because silence was the sound of a covenant running out of breath. That when the thirteenth turn completed, one of two things would happen: the door would seal forever and the valley would forget everything, names and roads and children's faces, a clean white page — or the door would break open, and whatever the thirteen had bound would wake with thirteen centuries of hunger in its throat.

She also knew — and this she told no one, because there was a word for it in the old tongue and the word was heavy as a millstone — that she had not come to the valley by accident. The key does not wander. The key is called.

---

By noon, Kestrel had done the thing he was best at, which was gather information, and the information had done the thing information always did in the Hollow, which was make everything worse.

He went down to the village. He bought bread he didn't need from the baker, and listened. Old Sefa couldn't remember the name of the lane she'd lived on for forty years. The ferryman at the ford had poled halfway across before realizing he'd forgotten which side he was going to. Children were asking their mothers why the cows wouldn't low, and the mothers had no answer, and the mothers' faces had the particular blankness Kestrel recognized instantly, professionally, because he saw it every time he lifted a memory: the smooth absence where something used to be.

The valley was forgetting itself. Not slowly, the way valleys usually forget — season by season, generation by generation. Quickly. Wholesale. As if someone had opened his hoard-chest and was pouring it out onto the ground.

His hoard. His chest of stolen winters, hidden beneath his floor, wrapped in oilcloth. He thought of it with a strange new shame. All those lives he'd taken and kept locked away, and outside his window a whole valley was being robbed by something far more patient than he'd ever been.

He passed Maren's shop and did not go in. He had marked himself the day he showed her the crescent; he could feel it, the way you feel weather changing behind your back. Whatever Maren sold to whoever she sold to beyond the mist, she had already sold the news of the child. He would bet his last lamp on it. Which meant others were coming — others who crossed the mist as if it weren't there.

He walked home fast, and the silent birds watched him go.

---

That evening Wren stood at the window with the crescent cupped in both hands, and she spoke — not to him, not to herself, but to the room, or perhaps to the valley through the room. Her voice was thin but certain, and the old tongue poured out of her like the stream used to pour over its stones, before the stones went quiet, and because of a scholar's stolen winter six years dead, Kestrel understood every word.

*"I dreamed the moon again,"* she said. *"It has twelve faces left. Last night it lost one while I watched, and where the light went, the mist followed."*

She turned the crescent over. Its broken edge caught the lamplight.

*"I know what I am. I am the hinge. I am the thirteenth voice that hasn't spoken yet. And I know what happens at the turn if no one speaks: the valley forgets its own name, and then the door is unmade, and then—"*

She stopped. Her jaw tightened. Some things, even in a language built for oath-making, are too large for a child's mouth.

Kestrel crouched to her height. "Then we speak," he said in common speech, knowing she'd catch the shape of it if not the words. "You and me. Before the turn."

She looked at him for a long moment. Storm-grey eyes, older than their years by several centuries — he was sure of it now, sure the way you're sure of a load-bearing wall. Then she reached out and took his hand, the scarred thief's hand, and pressed the broken crescent into his palm and closed his fingers over it, and said one more sentence in the dead tongue, softly, the way you tell someone a diagnosis:

*"You already carry part of the answer. You've carried it for years. You just don't remember stealing it."*

Kestrel stood very still. Beneath his feet, beneath the floorboards, beneath the oilcloth and the lock, his hoard waited — dozens of stolen memories, sorted and sealed, including one he had never been able to bring himself to sell, one he told himself he kept only because it might fetch a high price someday, one that smelled faintly, when he opened its wrapping in his mind, of mist and bronze and a woman's voice swearing an oath at the treeline.

He hadn't thought of that memory in years. He had made very sure not to.

Now the child watched him with her ancient eyes, and the silent valley pressed against the windows, and somewhere beyond the mist, things were moving toward them through the outer dark, and Kestrel Kavanagh understood that tonight he would have to open his own chest of thefts and go looking for a memory he had spent a lifetime pretending he didn't have —

— and that what he found there would break his heart open like the crescent, like the covenant, like the door.

* * *

That night, for the first time, Wren did not scream in her sleep.

She slept deeply, peacefully, one fist still curled even without the token, because Kestrel had it now, and in her dream the moon stood full and still in a windless sky, waiting, patient as a held breath.

Eleven faces left.

And beneath the floorboards of the house that forgot nothing, a chest waited for its thief, holding a woman's voice, a broken oath, and the beginning of everything.
