## 5. Memories I Should Not Own

The hoard lived beneath the floor.

Three boards up from the hearth, two from the window, marked by a scratch shaped like a fishhook that only I could read. I pried them loose the way I'd done a thousand times before—slowly, listening to the house settle around me, listening for anything that wasn't the house.

Wren was asleep in the loft. Or I hoped she was. She'd been asleep when the moon came up wrong last night, and she hadn't woken screaming, and somehow that frightened me more than the screaming had. Silence in a child who had arrived already knowing how to count down to something was not peace. It was patience.

I lowered the lamp into the cavity and let its light find what it always found: rows of glass vials stoppered with wax, each one holding a curl of silver-grey vapor that moved when you looked at it. Moved like it knew it was being watched. Memories don't behave like smoke. Smoke doesn't *want* anything. These did. Every vial in my collection held something somebody had begged me not to take, or something they'd tried to press into my hands too quickly, glad to be rid of it—and either way, they all shifted behind the glass like animals scenting the door of a cage left open.

Forty-one vials. Forty-one lives I had carried so other people didn't have to.

I told myself that, sometimes. That I was a kind of relief. A porter for grief. It sounded better at night than *thief*.

I wasn't here for the whole collection tonight. I was here for one thing, or one kind of thing: the old tongue. Wren's language. The words that had come out of her small mouth on the first night like water out of a cracked jug—constant, urgent, impossible to stop up—and that had landed in my ear with a horrible half-familiarity, the way a tune lands when someone else is humming the song your mother used to sing.

Half-familiar. That word again. Half of anything means the other half exists somewhere.

I had stolen a memory once—a peddler, eleven years ago, a woman with river mud on her boots—who had carried fragments of the dead tongue in her head like broken pottery. I'd lifted the fragments because they were exotic, because fragments sold well to collectors beyond the mist, because lifting them had been easy and easy was the only criterion I'd ever applied to my work. But I'd never sold them. I remembered that now with a strange chill: I had taken them, catalogued them, and then—why? I couldn't remember why. There was a gap where the reason should be, smooth-walled, like a hole where a tooth used to be.

That should have frightened me more than it did. A thief who can't remember his own thefts is a man standing in a room full of doors he's never seen.

I found the peddler's vial by feel, fourth row, third from the end, and uncorked it over my open palm.

Memory, when you take it rather than live it, comes in through the skin. Cold first. Then weight. Then the world.

---

*Rain on canvas. The smell of wet wool and cardamom. The peddler—younger than I'd known her, laughing at something a man said in a market I didn't recognize—and then the market gone, and she is walking alone at dusk along a road of white stone, and there are others on the road, dozens, and every one of them is speaking it.*

*Not speaking it the way Wren speaks it—not gasping, not drowning. Speaking it the way people speak their mother tongue: lazily, beautifully, arguing about prices and weather and whether the harvest would hold. The words pour past her and the memory lets me catch them, lets me hold them up, and each one has a shape I know and a sound I know and a meaning that arrives a half-second late, like thunder after lightning.*

*Sella. Oath. Not the little oaths—the swear-words, the promises over card games. The other kind. The kind with a weight to it, a downward pull, the kind you build foundations on.*

*Vaelin. Covenant. The same root. Sella-vaelin, oath-covenant, the phrase repeating down the white road like a refrain, and everyone saying it with the particular boredom of people for whom a great and terrible thing has become ordinary.*

*And then the memory shivers, because the peddler's attention snags on a notice nailed to a waystone, written in the old characters, and she reads it without effort:*

*THE THIRTEENTH TURN COMES TO ALL THINGS. THE COVENANT STANDS WHILE THE KEYS REMAIN.*

*And beneath it, newer ink, a different hand, hurried:*

*TWO KEYS BROKEN. SEEK THE THIRD.*

---

I came back to myself on the floor with my cheek against the boards and the empty vial rolling slowly in a circle beside my hand.

Two keys broken. Seek the third.

Wren slept above me with a broken bronze crescent under her pillow.

I lay there and did arithmetic I didn't want to do. The covenant sealed magic out of the Hollow—that was valley gospel, the one scripture we had, repeated until it meant nothing: *the mist keeps it out, the mist keeps us safe.* Everyone knew it had stood for generations. Nobody asked what it stood *on*. Covenants are not walls. Walls stand on stone. Covenants stand on promises, and promises stand on people willing to keep them, and keys—

Keys break.

I got up. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me, because shaking hands are a luxury for men with fewer problems. I lit the lamp properly, set it on the table, and looked at the hoard below the floor with new eyes.

Forty-one vials. And one question I had never thought to ask: *why do I have the ones I never sold?*

A thief keeps inventory. A thief knows his stock. I went down the rows, tilting each vial to the light, reading the labels in my own cramped hand—*wedding, merchant's daughter; funeral, ferryman; first snowfall, shepherd boy*—and found, near the back, tucked behind three vials of pure sensation I'd harvested from a dying man (sunlight, mostly, and the smell of cut grass), one flask of thicker glass with no label at all.

No label. In forty-one entries, mine was the only handwriting missing.

I knew before I touched it. Some part of me had always known it was there, the way you know about a wound you've decided not to look at. I picked it up and the glass was warm, warmer than the others, as if it had been waiting a long time and the waiting had heated it.

I almost put it back. I want that recorded somewhere, if anywhere records anything: I almost put it back. Because a man can live his whole life alongside one unopened door, and call the living a success.

Then, upstairs, faint through the floor, Wren made a sound in her sleep. Not a scream. A word. One soft syllable of the old tongue, and my ear caught it and understood it without my permission:

*Awaits.*

I pulled the stopper.

---

*Winter, but a gentle one. I am looking through eyes that are not mine and are mine, and the height of everything is wrong—I am seeing the mist-line from lower down, from a child's eye-level, no—from a young woman's.*

*My mother.*

*Younger than any version of her I ever knew. No grey in her braid yet, no stoop in her shoulders, and her face—gods, her face—is open in a way I never saw it open, unguarded, lit from within by the low green glow of the mist itself. She is standing twenty paces from the mist-line, close enough that the tendrils reach toward her like curious cats, and she is not afraid.*

*In her hands: a bronze crescent. Whole. Unbroken. Its surface alive with fine engraving—characters in the old tongue, chasing each other around the curve like fish in a circular pool.*

*There are others with her. I can see boots and hems at the edge of the memory, four figures, five, faces turned away or blurred the way witnesses blur—but one voice carries, an old man's voice, deep and tired, speaking the old tongue in cadences that are unmistakably liturgical, the cadences I heard in the peddler's memory, the cadences Wren uses in her sleep.*

*And my mother answers him. She speaks the old tongue fluently, easily, and though the memory gives me her words whole, it takes my heart a moment to translate what they mean, because I have spent thirty years not knowing this language and thirty years knowing it at the same time:*

*"I swear it on what I am and what I will lose. Let the key be hidden in the keeping. Let the blood carry it forward. When the thirteenth turn comes, let it wake where I cannot follow."*

*The old man says: "You understand what the keeping costs."*

*"Better than you know," my mother says, and there is such grief in her young face, such specific grief, that the memory itself seems to flinch around it—"and I will pay it gladly, and I will pay it twice, and I will never tell him. Swear me the other half: that he will not be made to remember until remembering is the only mercy left."*

*"That is not mine to swear," the old man says. "That belongs to the key herself."*

*Herself.*

*My mother lifts the crescent toward the mist. The mist leans in. For one heartbeat the boundary between the valley and everything else thins like ice going clear, and through it I glimpse—colors. Impossible colors. Magic, moving on the far side, vast and bright and hungry, pressing against the membrane of the world like a face against glass.*

*And then my mother speaks the final line of the oath, the binding line, the one that locks, and the memory begins to burn away from the edges the way memories do when someone has paid to have them sealed—to have them SEALED, gods, she'd paid a sealer, she'd paid good silver to lock her own confession inside her son's floor—*

*The last thing I see before it goes:*

*Her hand, pressing the bronze crescent against her belly. Against the place where I was.*

*"Grow strong," she whispers, in common, in plain valley common, the only words in the whole memory in our everyday tongue. "Grow strong and forgive me, because I am going to make you carry something heavy, and I am not going to be here to help."*

---

I opened my eyes on my knees. I didn't remember getting there.

The lamp had guttered low. The empty flask sat on the boards above the open hoard, and I stared at it the way you'd stare at a scorpion you'd kept in your house for years without knowing.

She knew. My mother had known—known about the covenant, known about the keys, known about the child who would someday knock on my door in a storm—and she had hidden the answer inside her own house, inside her own son's hoard, in the one place she knew no broker would ever buy and no warden would ever think to search. She had sworn me to ignorance before I could be sworn to anything. *Let it wake where I cannot follow.*

And I had spent thirty years in this room. Thirty years kneeling on these boards, counting my takings, pricing other people's weddings and funerals, and the answer to all of it had been sleeping six inches beneath my knees, wearing no label, patient as only my mother could be patient.

Betrayal is a strange word for what I felt, because betrayal requires trust broken, and I couldn't remember ever trusting her. She had been cold and competent and careful, and I had built myself in her image and called it character. But lying there on the floor with her young face still burning behind my eyes—her face, open, unguarded, brave in a way I had never once suspected her capable of being—I understood that the coldest thing she ever did was the warmest thing anyone has ever done for me, and she had done it in secret, and she had died with it, and she had left me to find it alone.

She had also lied to me my entire life. Both things were true. I held both of them, because holding unbearable things was the one skill I had ever mastered.

The crescent. She'd pressed it against her belly. Against me. Which meant—

Which meant the key didn't pass to Wren from nowhere. Keys don't appear; they're *kept*. Hidden in the keeping, she'd sworn. Carried forward in the blood.

I sat up very slowly.

Wren hadn't found me. Wren had come *to* me—barefoot through a storm, to this door, of all doors, in all the valley. Something had known the address.

And if my mother helped break the covenant—if *break* was even the right word, if there were things in that oath I still hadn't parsed, payments promised, prices named—then the token under that child's pillow was only half the mechanism, and I was beginning to suspect, with a coldness that started in my spine and spread outward, that the other half of the mechanism had been walking around inside my own skull for thirty years, unlabeled, waiting.

The lamp went out. Not guttered—went out, cleanly, all at once, though no draft moved.

Upstairs, in her sleep, Wren spoke again, and this time it was not one word. It was a sentence, unhurried, almost gentle, in the old tongue, and I understood every syllable of it:

*The keeper wakes. Bring him to the water. The moon will show him the rest.*

I knelt alone in the dark with forty empty vials and one full one, and outside, far off across the fells, the silence where the night-birds should have been pressed in against the walls of my house like deep water.

Thirteen turns. Two keys broken.

I was starting to understand why the third had been sent to find me.
