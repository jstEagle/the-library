## 6. The Warden at the Ford

The river at Hollow's Edge ran shallow and quick over grey stones, the kind of water that announced every crossing with a racket of splashing and cursing. Kestrel had watched a hundred travelers come through it—merchants swearing, pilgrims singing, smugglers wading hip-deep with their bundles held overhead like offerings. The ford was loud. That was why he liked it. Loud things could be heard coming, and in the Hollow, hearing something coming was half of survival.

So when he looked up from his snares that gray morning and saw a woman standing on the far bank with dry boots, his hands went still on the snare-wire before his mind caught up.

She had not crossed. She was simply *there*, as if she'd been standing on that stone for an hour, or a year, waiting for him to notice. The river slid around her ankles without touching them. No ripple. No sound. Water parting around her shins like it had been told to.

Kestrel had lived twenty-nine years in a valley where magic could not follow, where the mist above the ridgeline ate sorcery like salt eats ice, where the worst thing a man could fear was another man's hunger. He had built his whole careful life inside that promise.

The woman took one step forward and the river did not splash.

He stood up slowly, keeping his hands visible, the way you do with a dog you don't know. "Ford's to your left," he called across. "Water's only knee-deep if you use it."

"I know," she said. Her voice carried cleanly, without effort, the way sound carries in a chapel. "I'm not here for the water."

*No,* he thought. *You're not.*

She came toward him along the bank, and up close she resolved into something almost ordinary: mid-forties maybe, travel-stained wool coat, hair pulled back in a knot gone loose with weather. Her face was the kind you forget in a crowd—until you noticed the eyes, which were pale as frost on a window and did not move the way eyes were supposed to move. They tracked things too precisely. When she blinked, it seemed deliberate, scheduled.

"You're Kavanagh," she said.

"Depends who's asking."

"Ilse Vane." She didn't offer a hand. He got the sense she knew exactly what her hand would feel like. "Warden of the Outer Reaches, Third Vigil."

"We don't have those here."

"No." She glanced upward, past him, toward the mist that hung over the ridgelines like a held breath. "You have the opposite of those. That's rather the point."

A plover called somewhere upstream, thin and lonely. Otherwise the valley made no noise at all, and Kestrel realized the silence had weight now, had been building for days—not just the animals gone quiet since Wren's dreams started, but everything, as if the land itself were listening through its skin.

"What do you want, Warden Vane?"

"To talk. You're the only person in this valley who's touched the key recently. I checked." She said it mildly, the way you'd mention the weather. "The broker's ledger is very thorough. Maren sells more than memories, if you know how to ask."

His stomach dropped through the wet ground. *Maren.* Of course. He'd walked that token into her parlor and set it on her scale like a fool, and Maren weighed everything, and someone beyond the mist paid her to weigh certain things most carefully.

"The key," he repeated, keeping his voice flat. Stealing memories had taught him one thing above all others: faces lie better than words, but only if the voice stays boring. "Don't know what that means."

"Mm." She studied him with those scheduled blinks. "May we walk? I find conversations go easier when people aren't deciding whether to run."

"I'm not running."

"No," she agreed. "That's what interests me about you."

They walked upstream along the bank, her boots silent on the stones while his crunched. She moved wrong—he couldn't have said exactly how at first, and then he could. She didn't displace anything. Grass didn't bend under her. A spider's web strung between two alder branches passed her shoulder without breaking. The world treated her like a rumor of a woman rather than a fact.

"In the outer realms," she said, "magic isn't sealed away. It's a flood. It has been for thirteen years—ever since the covenant frayed. It comes down out of the high places and drowns the low ones. Villages where the rain falls upward. Children born knowing things they shouldn't. Whole provinces gone feral." She spoke without drama, which somehow made it worse. "My order holds what's left. We hold it badly. And eleven years ago, a woman crossed the mist into this valley carrying a piece of bronze, and my order lost the trail of the last living key to the covenant."

Kestrel kept walking. Kept his face bored. Inside, something cold opened its eyes.

"My mother died eleven years ago," he said.

"So I understand. Fever, the records say. Inconvenient timing, fevers." Ilse stopped at a flat boulder above a deep pool and turned to face him fully for the first time. "Here's what happens next, Kavanagh. My order believes the key is a child who entered this valley within the year. They believe she can be removed and the covenant remade from outside, by force, by rite, by whatever remains necessary. They are not gentle people, the ones who give the orders. I am—" she paused, and for a moment something almost human crossed the frost-colored eyes, "—I am the gentler option. I'm offering you passage out. Past the mist. Protection, papers, a life. In exchange for the girl."

"And if I don't know any girl?"

"Then you'll say so, and I'll believe you or I won't, and either way my colleagues will come, and they don't ask first." She let that sit in the cold air between them. "The valley won't protect her much longer. Can you feel it? The animals went quiet because they can hear the covenant failing. The moon is turning wrong. Whatever your mother helped break is breaking all the way through, and when it goes, this valley drowns too. Magic doesn't stay polite forever, Kavanagh. It's already leaking in." She nodded at his hands. "How else do you explain what you do?"

The words hit him like cold water down his collar.

Because he had always known, in the place where he kept the knowledge he never looked at, that taking a memory from a sleeping traveler wasn't a trick of knives or drugs or clever talk. He touched their brow, and the memory came loose, and he carried it away in a small glass vial that should not have been able to hold anything at all—and none of it should work, none of it had ever made sense, except that the valley forgot and so everyone assumed the thief must too.

Magic leaking in. Magic, pooling in the low places of a sealed valley like water finding cracks in a dam.

"I steal memories," he said slowly. "That's all. It's trade, not sorcery."

"It's both. Everything here is both, now. That's what dying seals look like." Ilse's voice softened by a degree. "Give me the child. I'll take her beyond the mist, my order will do what must be done, and you will never see any of it. You'll get a new life in the low country. Clean air. A trade. You strike me as a man who would like a trade."

"I have a trade."

"You have a compulsion with ledgers attached." She smiled faintly, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "I've read your file. There is no file, technically—the outer realms have no idea you exist—but I keep notes. Kestrel Kavanagh. Orphan of the mist-line. Takes from sleepers, sells to brokers, keeps nothing for himself except a hoard he never spends. Curious, that. A thief who steals and doesn't spend. What are you saving, Kavanagh?"

*Her voice. Her face. The oath I didn't know I was carrying.* He said nothing.

Ilse waited, patient as standing water. Then she reached into her coat and produced something small and set it on the boulder between them: a bronze crescent, unbroken, polished bright with handling. Its twin lay shattered in a drawer in his house, in a storm-child's fist, in his mother's buried memory.

"A casting of the seal-token," she said. "There are three in existence. One is broken. One is in my order's vault. This one is mine." She turned it once in the light, and for a heartbeat Kestrel saw etched around its curve a ring of characters in the old tongue—and understood them, the way you understand a word you've heard in a dream. *Hold fast. Hold closed. Hold until the thirteenth turn.*

Then the understanding slid away like water off feathers, and he was only a man looking at pretty metal again.

"Consider it," Ilse said. "I'll be in the village three days. I don't threaten men in their own valley—it's poor manners and worse tactics. But count what the flood takes from you, Kavanagh, against what surrender costs. You may find the arithmetic kinder than you think." She stepped back toward the ford. "You lied to me just now, incidentally."

He hadn't moved. "When?"

"When you asked what I wanted as though you didn't know there's a child asleep in your loft with a broken crescent in her coat pocket." She said it without heat, without triumph, like correcting a sum. "First lie of your life, I'd guess. Your file says you don't. Liars are hard to read; honest thieves are easy. You've been easy, Kavanagh. Until today." She paused at the water's edge. "It suits you. For what it's worth."

And she walked out onto the river.

Not across it—*onto* it, boots meeting the current and the current declining to notice, and she went downstream along the surface with her hands clasped behind her back like a woman strolling a garden path, until the bend of the river took her and the ordinary world rushed back in with a sound like breath released.

Kestrel stood alone on the bank for a long time. The plover called again. Somewhere behind him, far up the fells, the mist sat on the ridgeline, holding, holding, holding.

He thought about his mother at the mist-line with the crescent in her raised fist, her mouth shaping words in a language the valley was supposed to have killed. He thought about Wren's scream in the dark—*twelve, twelve, twelve*—and the animals gone silent, and Maren going pale and then greedy, and a warden who walked on water telling him the arithmetic of surrender.

Three days.

He walked home fast, not running, because running was what guilty men did and he had decided, somewhere between the boulder and the bend in the river, that he was done being easy to read.

* * *

Wren was awake when he came in, sitting cross-legged on the loft floor with the broken crescent cupped in both palms. Morning light came through the shutter-cracks in thin blades and lay across her knees. She looked up when he entered, and he saw she'd been crying, quietly, the practiced way children cry when they've learned nobody comes.

"She found you," Wren said.

Not a question. His chest tightened. "You know her?"

"She came to the village yesterday. I saw her from the well. The dogs wouldn't look at her." Wren turned the broken token in her fingers. "She smells like outside. Like the place where the magic drowned everything." She said it in common speech, halting, each word placed like a foot on uncertain ice—and then slipped into the old tongue, fluid and urgent, and the strange thing, the thing that raised the hair on his arms, was that he understood her. Every word. As if proximity to her were slowly picking the lock his mother had shut inside him.

*"The wardens are not all cruel,"* she said. *"But the ones who send them are. If she offers you something for me, it's because they can't take me themselves. Not yet. Not while the valley still remembers whose it is."*

"Whose it is?" Kestrel crouched down to her level. "Whose, Wren?"

She looked at him with those grave, unchildlike eyes, and lifted the broken crescent, and pressed its fractured edge briefly against his breastbone, right over the heart.

*"Yours,"* she said. *"By blood. By oath. By what she buried in you so the searching wouldn't find it. She hid the answer inside a thief, because no one robs a thief, and no one looks at what a man won't even look at himself."*

The room went very quiet. Outside, wind moved through the bare alders with a sound like slow counting.

Three days. Maybe less. A warden on the water, a broker's ledger with his name in it, and a child sitting in his loft holding the key to a door he'd spent his whole life pretending wasn't there.

Kestrel rose, went to the corner, and dragged back the floorboard over his hoard. Row after row of glass vials glinted up at him—weddings, funerals, first loves, last words, other people's lives in neat little cells. Eleven years of careful, joyless theft. Somewhere in those rows was a memory he'd never sold, never spent, never dared open twice: his mother, younger, swearing in a dead tongue at the edge of the world.

He'd been saving it without knowing what for.

Now he knew.

"We leave tonight," he said. "Before she counts her three days. Pack nothing that matters—we can replace what doesn't matter and carry what does." He met Wren's eyes. "Can you walk the fells?"

*"I was born walking the fells,"* she answered in the old tongue, and then, deliberately, in his language, word by word like a gift: *"I was born walking them to find you."*

He blew out the lamp, though morning was still bright, because some habits of hiding die slower than others—and because in the glass rows below his fingers, one vial caught the light and threw it back in a color he had never seen it throw before.

Faint. Flickering. Bronze.
