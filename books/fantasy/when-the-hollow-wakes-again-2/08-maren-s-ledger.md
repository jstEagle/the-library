## 8. Maren's Ledger

The ledger had three locks on it, and Maren had built all three herself.

Iron for thieves. Ashwood for the curious. And the third—small, brass, warm to the touch even in winter—for the version of herself she'd been twenty years ago, the one who still believed a person could keep honest books.

She opened it every night after the shop went dark, the way other women said their prayers. Not to read forward. The forward pages were just business: names, prices, weights of memory measured in hours and grief. She read backward. That was where she kept her conscience, filed chronologically, so she could watch it thin out year by year like hair going gray.

Tonight the candle guttered when she reached the old entries, and she let it. Darkness made the arithmetic kinder.

* * *

The letter had come at dusk, folded twice, sealed in grey wax with no sigil at all—which was its own kind of signature. Only one kind of sender bothered to be anonymous in the Hollow: the kind that didn't need to introduce itself because you'd already heard it in your dreams.

*The warden requires the residence of the thief Kavanagh. Compensation commensurate. Noncompliance noted.*

*Noted.* As if her whole life hadn't been an exercise in not being noticed. Maren had survived thirty years in a valley that forgot things by being useful and unremarkable, by buying what people couldn't bear to keep and selling it beyond the mist at a markup that would have embarrassed her if she'd ever let herself think about it. She knew every trick of staying small. She had a face people described as *fine, I suppose*, and a voice pitched to be forgotten before it finished a sentence.

But the letter knew her name. Not *the broker*. Not *Madam Quill*, the name she did business under. *Maren.* Which meant someone beyond the mist had been reading her ledger—or someone inside it had been copying it out.

She sat with the letter while the candle drowned in its own wax, and she thought about the word *commensurate*. They always used accounting language, these people from outside. As if everything could be balanced if you just kept tidy columns.

Her ledger didn't balance. It never had. She'd known that since the third entry.

* * *

The third entry was a memory of rain.

A farmer named Aldous Pell had come to her in the second spring of her trade, soaked through, hat in his hands, wanting rid of the day his wife died. He couldn't afford the standard rate, so Maren had taken the memory at half price and told herself she was doing him a kindness, which was the first lie she'd told as a broker and far from the last. Kindness. She'd sold that rainy-day memory within the month to a collector in the outer realms who liked weather with sorrow in it, and Aldous Pell had lived another eleven years without once knowing why he flinched when it rained.

She'd watched him flinch, sometimes, across the market square. He'd pull his collar up and look confused about it, like a man searching his pockets for something he couldn't name. And Maren would go back to weighing memories on her little brass scale, and the scale never tipped, and that was the whole obscenity of it: the trade looked fair. It *was* fair, entry by entry, coin for coin. What it wasn't was innocent, and no ledger line could carry that weight, so she'd learned to stop trying.

One memory at a time. That was how you sold a conscience. Nobody noticed the first ounce leaving. By the time you were light enough to float, you'd forgotten you'd ever had ballast.

Except she hadn't quite. That was the joke of it, the cruelty of it: a woman who dealt in forgetting, cursed with remembering. Every transaction filed behind her eyes in permanent ink. She could have sold her own memories, of course—she'd thought about it often, held the extractor over her own temple some nights, the cold brass kiss of it—but there was one rule older than any she'd written: *the broker does not drink her own stock.* Because then who would remember what anyone had paid? Who would keep the books?

So she remembered. All of it. Aldous Pell flinching at rain. The miller who'd sold the sound of his mother's voice and spent the rest of his life turning toward doors that never opened. The girl—sixteen, maybe seventeen—who'd traded away her first love for passage money and cried in Maren's back room, and Maren had handed her a handkerchief and charged her for it, two coppers, entered neatly in column four.

And one more. One she'd turned past tonight like she always did, slow, the way you turn past a grave you're not ready to visit but can't walk around either.

*Paid: Kavanagh, E. One (1) memory, sealed, contents undisclosed. Rate: waived.*

Waived. In thirty years of business, Maren had waived a fee exactly once.

* * *

It had been a bad winter, that one. Mist thick as wool for weeks, no travelers coming through, which meant no stock, which meant Maren had eaten through her reserves and started pricing her furniture. And into that famine had walked a woman Maren knew only as Kavanagh—young then, younger than Maren by a few years, with a boy of four or five asleep against her shoulder and frost in her dark hair and a bronze crescent on a cord around her neck.

Maren remembered the boy most clearly, though she'd tried for years not to. He'd slept through the whole negotiation, this small heavy warmth against his mother's shoulder, one hand curled in her collar, and when he stirred he'd looked at Maren with those gray eyes children have before the world teaches them to narrow them—and he'd smiled at her. At *her*. Like she was someone worth smiling at.

Kavanagh had wanted one thing only: to seal a memory. Not sell it. Seal it—pay Maren to take it and lock it away where no one, including herself, could ever reach it again.

*I can't do that,* Maren had said. *Everything I take goes out through the mist. That's the trade.*

*Not this one,* Kavanagh had said, and her voice had done something strange—not pleading, nothing so soft as pleading. It had the weight of a door closing. *If it leaves the valley, people die. I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to be paid well enough not to care.*

And here was the part Maren had spent twenty years refusing to examine: she hadn't cared. Not then. She'd taken the sealed vial—the only sealed vial in thirty years of trade, the only one she'd never weighed, never catalogued, never sold—and she'd put it in the deepest drawer of the ledger cabinet, and she'd told Kavanagh, *This stays until you come back for it,* and Kavanagh had said, in that closing-door voice:

*I won't be coming back. If anyone asks for it—a warden, a broker, anyone with grey wax or bronze in their hands—burn it. Don't open it. Burn it.*

Then she'd set the sleeping boy down on Maren's counter for one impossible moment, kissed his hair, and gone out into the mist, and Maren had stood there with a child's warmth fading off her countertop and a vial of somebody's amputated past in her hands, feeling—for the first and nearly last time in her career—like a woman holding something that mattered.

She'd waived the fee. Entered it in the book. Told herself it was charity.

It wasn't charity. She'd known that even then, in the place brokers keep their real accounts. It was fear. That woman had walked in carrying dread like a physical object, dense enough to bend the lamplight, and Maren had recognized it because she dealt in extracted emotion daily and knew the genuine article when she saw it. Kavanagh hadn't needed a broker. She'd needed a hiding place, and Maren's locked drawer had been the safest one in the valley.

And then Kavanagh had died—everyone said fever, and everyone said it too quickly, in the tone people use when they've agreed not to ask questions—and the boy had gone to whatever passed for kin in the low quarter, and Maren had kept the vial.

Kept it. For twenty years. Never opened it, because burning it felt like murder and opening it felt like worse. She'd moved it twice, during the flood scare and again when the tax men came, wrapped it in oilcloth, buried it under the false bottom of the ledger cabinet like a bone a dog can't bring itself to bury or chew.

Some nights she took it out and held it up to the candle. There was something moving in it—not liquid, nothing so simple. A slow silver turning, like weather seen from very high up. Whatever Kavanagh had cut out of herself to save the valley, it was alive in there, and patient, and waiting for a reader.

* * *

Now there was a letter on her desk with no signature, and a warden somewhere in her valley buying bread with coins that didn't match any mint Maren knew, and a mute child speaking dead words in a safehouse Maren could locate in under a minute because she'd sold Kestrel the house himself, years ago, back when he'd been a scrawny apprentice thief with his mother's gray eyes and none of her gravity.

That was the thing about the boy. She'd watched him grow up sideways, in fragments—buying lamp oil, selling a wedding, haggling over a grief like a fishwife—and every time he'd laid a memory on her scale she'd checked it against the mother without meaning to. Same economy of motion. Same habit of standing slightly apart from rooms. And once, only once, he'd brought in a memory that made Maren's hands shake on the brass weights: a kitchen, steam on glass, a woman humming in a language Maren had heard exactly one other time—from a dying stranger on the far side of the mist, who'd paid her a fortune for a single word of translation and wept when she gave it.

She hadn't bought that one from Kestrel. She'd sent him away with it, told him it was worth nothing, watched him frown because he could count value better than that—and then lain awake all night knowing she'd just lied to a child to protect a secret that wasn't hers anymore, if it had ever been hers.

Consciences are like that. You think they're gone, sold off ounce by ounce, and then one night there's a knock at the door of you, and it turns out something stayed behind after all, living in the walls.

* * *

The candle had burned to a thumb-width of tallow. Outside, the mist pressed against the shutters with the particular heaviness it had carried all week—heavier than weather had any right to be, heavier than the Hollow's mist had been in living memory, as if the valley were holding its breath.

Maren took out a fresh sheet of paper. Grey wax. No sigil. Her hand knew the form; she'd received enough of these letters to write one back in her sleep.

*The residence of the thief is the stone house above the tannery lane, third door, cellar access via the drainage culvert behind the cooper's yard. He keeps the child below ground. He will be home before moonset.*

Three sentences. Thirty years of careful smallness, undone in three sentences, and the terrible thing—the thing that made her press her knuckles to her mouth—was how easy it was. How clean the columns looked. *Compensation commensurate.* She could retire beyond the mist on what they'd pay. She could buy a little house in a green country where nothing was forgotten, and live out her years among people who didn't know her name, and never once have to weigh another human being's grief on a brass scale.

She dipped the pen again. Wrote the address. Looked at it.

Above the tannery lane. Third door. She'd chosen that house for him herself—south-facing, two exits, thick walls. She'd knocked ten percent off the rent because he was Kavanagh's boy and she'd never told him that, and now she was about to feed him to the outer realms with the same hand.

*Aldous Pell flinches at rain. The miller turns toward doors. The girl cries into a handkerchief she paid for.*

The ledger lay open beside the letter, and whether it was the candlelight or her own tired eyes, the old entries seemed to shift as she looked at them—not moving, exactly, but *listening*, the way a room listens when you say something true in it. Thirty years of transactions, and every one of them had seemed reasonable at the time. That was the devil's real trick, she understood suddenly, finally: not temptation. Bookkeeping. You never signed anything wicked. You just kept writing, one line at a time, until the sum of your life added up to something you'd have crossed the valley to refuse.

There was one entry left unwritten. One line she still controlled.

Maren set down the pen.

Then she picked it back up, and her heart hammered like a novice thief's, and she wrote—not on the letter, but on the margin of the ledger itself, in the space beside the waived fee from twenty years ago, the smallest entry in the book:

*Kavanagh, E. Paid in full. Outstanding balance: one sealed memory, one son. Interest accrued.*

She didn't know yet what she was going to do about the letter. Sell the address and damn them both, or burn it and spend her last quiet years looking over her shoulder. Both futures sat in her like stones.

But she blew out the candle, and in the dark she put her hand flat on the ledger cabinet—on the false bottom, on the vial sleeping beneath it, silver turning its slow weather—and she made herself one promise, the first honest line in thirty years of books:

Whatever happened next, the sealed memory would not leave this valley unread. Someone was going to open it. Soon.

And if the boy with the gray eyes came to her door asking questions—if he ever asked why his mother had visited a memory-broker the winter he was five—

She would tell him the truth, or she would die before she sold another line.

Outside, beneath the shuttered window, soft as a footfall on snow, someone began to knock.
