## 10. Salt and Ash

Wren dreams in the old tongue now, and when she wakes she cannot stop it spilling out.

They are three days into the high fells, above the tree line where the wind combs the grass flat and nothing grows taller than a knee. Her feet have stopped bleeding. Kestrel wrapped them in strips of his own shirt the first night, kneeling in the dark without a word, and since then they have walked, and walked, and walked.

She does not know this man. That is the truth she keeps folded small inside her, like the bronze crescent in her pocket. She knows *of* him. She knows his name was spoken to her in a language older than either of them, by a woman standing at the edge of a fire that was not a fire. She knows the shape of her task the way you know the shape of your own hands—without remembering learning it.

But she does not know him. And he does not yet know himself.

*Ash-mother, moon-daughter, keep the count.*

The words come up her throat like water finding a crack. She doesn't decide to speak them. This morning, when Kestrel handed her the last of the bread, she said *thank you* in the dead tongue of the First Covenant and watched his face change—watched something move behind his eyes like a shutter caught by wind.

"You said—" He stopped. Started again. "Say it again."

"*Thaen iri sulet,*" she said.

And he repeated it. Perfectly. The accent even better than hers, the vowels round and old, as if the language had been sleeping in his mouth for thirty years waiting for someone to knock.

He stared at his own hands then, as if they belonged to a stranger.

* * *

Now it is dusk, and they have made a rough camp in the lee of a stone that leans like a tired shepherd. Kestrel builds no fire. "Smoke shows," he says, which is the most he has said in an hour. He is a man made of closed doors. Wren counts them: the door of his mouth, the door of his eyes, the door he carries somewhere behind his ribs where she can hear, sometimes, a great many things rattling.

She settles beside him and speaks, because she is tired of silence, and because the tongue wants out of her the way a river wants the sea.

*"Ael suvven, dorath kellen."*

Kestrel goes still.

"In the beginning," he translates slowly, tasting each word, "the mist had a name, and the name was mercy." He frowns. "I don't know how I know that. I've never—I couldn't read a line of this two weeks ago."

"Your mother," Wren says.

It comes out harsher than she means. His head turns, sharp, a thief's reflex.

"What do you know about my mother?"

More than you, she thinks. Less than I need. She pulls the broken crescent from her pocket instead and holds it out on her palm, where the last light finds it. Bronze gone green at the break, but along its inner curve, letters. She has traced them a hundred times in the dark.

"Read it," she says.

He doesn't want to. She sees that—the same way she saw him want to refuse her bread, refuse her shelter, refuse her whole existence from the first storm-night at his door. Kestrel Kavanagh survives by refusing. But his lips move anyway, helplessly, soundlessly, and then aloud:

"*Iri sulet ven ash-kara. Thaen morath il ven.*" His voice drops lower, going strange. "By the moon's turning, the door is sealed. By the blood's remembering, the door is kept." He stops. Swallows. "There's more. It's like—it's not just words. It's *her* voice. I can hear her saying it." His hand closes over his mouth. "She's singing it. Why would she be singing it?"

Because oaths are songs, Wren thinks, before they are anything else. Because the First Covenant was never written down; it was carried, mother to child, in the only vault magic could not touch—a valley with no magic in it at all, and hearts to match.

But she says none of this. Instead she takes his wrist, gently, and puts his fingers on the crescent's letters, and watches the knowing move through him like a swallow of strong drink.

"She helped break it," Kestrel says flatly. "You told me. Whatever oath this is, she stood at the mist-line and broke it."

"No." Wren shakes her head. "*Ven.*" She searches for the common word. It comes slow; the two languages sit unevenly in her. "Not broke. *Opened.* There is a difference. A seal can open two ways."

"Meaning what?"

Meaning, she thinks, that the door swings both toward the dark and toward the light, and no one asked which way until it was too late. But her common speech fails her, so instead she sings.

It is only four notes, a fragment of the counting-song the moon taught her in her dreams—*one turn, two turn, the hollow holds, three turn, four turn, the hollow folds*—and the moment the sound leaves her, every bird on the fell that had been settling into dusk goes silent at once.

Then, far below them, down in the black spine of the valley, something answers. One low tone, like a bell struck under water.

Kestrel is on his feet before the echo dies. "What in the Hollow's name was that?"

"The valley," Wren says. "Listening."

* * *

They walk through the next day under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and the old tongue keeps rising in her like floodwater.

It frightens her a little, how much there is. For seven years she has been nearly mute—her own history a locked room even from herself—and now sentences crowd her teeth. She names things under her breath as they pass, because naming is the first duty of a key: *craeg*, stone; *sulet*, moon; *venn-ash*, the wind that carries memory. Kestrel walks half a step behind her, and she can feel him translating everything, helpless as a man reading over someone's shoulder.

By noon he has stopped pretending he isn't.

"The salt-word," he says suddenly. "Back at the ford—you said a word when you touched the water. *Hallen.* It means salt, but it also means—" he struggles— "grief? No. Grief that you *keep*. Grief you choose to carry instead of pouring out."

"Yes," Wren says, surprised. "Exactly that."

"And there's no word for it in common."

"There is no word for it in common," she agrees, "because people in the common lands pour their grief out. They give it away. To priests, to drink, to strangers." She glances back at him. "You have been carrying salt since I met you. Both hands full."

He says nothing to that. But at the next stream he crouches, cups water, and doesn't drink for a long moment—just watches his reflection, as if checking whether the man looking back has changed.

That night, sheltering beneath an overhang while sleet ticks against the stone, he asks the question she has been waiting for.

"How do I know this language, Wren? Truly. Not the stolen fragments—I'd know those, they have edges, seams. This is different. It's coming up from underneath." His jaw works. "Like something buried that's digging its own way out."

She considers lying, or at least softening it. Then she remembers: he is a man who deals in truths taken from others and never spends his own. Perhaps it is time someone gave him one, free and clear.

"An oath can be carried two ways," she says. "Spoken—or *hidden* in someone. Your mother did not only stand at the mist-line. She hid the covenant's heart where no warden, no broker, no power beyond the mist could ever reach for it." She touches his chest, lightly, over the sternum. "In a child who would grow up in a valley with no magic. Who would grow up to be a man whose whole talent was keeping things locked away."

"That's not—" He laughs, one sharp note with no humor in it. "I steal memories. That's all I am. A pickpocket of other people's lives because I didn't want one of my own."

"You are a vault," Wren says, "who learned to think he was a thief."

The sleet ticks. Somewhere down the dark slope, the drowned-bell tone sounds again, closer than before, or maybe only more patient. Kestrel sits with his arms around his knees, and she watches the idea work through him the way frost works through ground: slowly, splitting everything open.

"My mother," he says at last, very quietly, "put this in me. All of it. The language, the oath, whatever the crescent needs. And then she let me grow up thinking I was empty. Thinking I had nothing worth keeping, so I might as well take everyone else's." His voice frays on the last words. "Why would she do that? Let me hate myself for thirty years rather than just tell me?"

And Wren, who was sent to find him, who has known him for fourteen days and loved him for eleven of them in the fierce, uncomplicated way you love someone assigned to you by fate itself, moves closer and answers with the oldest word in the covenant tongue.

"*Suvven,*" she says. Which means: *because the thing hidden is the thing protected.* And means: *because love that announces itself can be robbed.* And means, simply, in the plain grammar of mothers everywhere: *because she was afraid.*

He looks at her then—really looks, the shutter finally off his eyes—and for a moment she thinks he will weep. He doesn't. He does something rarer. He reaches into his coat, past the lockpicks, past the little hoard of stolen moments he still carries loose in glass vials, and takes out nothing at all. Just opens his empty hand between them.

"I don't have anything to give you," he says. "I never have."

"You have everything," Wren says. "That's what frightens you."

* * *

Dawn comes gray and salt-smelling, though the sea is a week's walk away. They break camp in silence, and it is a different silence now—not two locked doors passing in a corridor, but something being built, plank by plank, in the space between them.

They are halfway up the shoulder of the last great fell before the valley's heart when Wren feels it: a change in the air's weight, like a held breath. She stops. Kestrel stops with her, hand already drifting to his knife.

Below them, spread across the moorland path they left yesterday, a figure moves. Small, unhurried, wrapped in a broker's gray traveling cloak. Even at this distance Wren recognizes the walk—someone used to weighing what people are worth.

"Maren," Kestrel breathes.

But Maren is not alone. Behind her, at the crest of the ridge, the morning light bends wrong—and Wren's skin goes cold, because she knows that bending. She has seen it in dreams. A warden walking folded through the light, Ilse Vane, closing like a hand.

Maren lifts her arm—not in threat, in *warning*—and shouts something up the slope that the wind shreds to pieces.

And Wren understands, in the old tongue that rises in her like a tide, exactly what the valley has been trying to tell them for three days. The bell-tone was never a summons for her alone.

*"Kestrel,"* she says, and the covenant word for urgency rings in it. *"The threshold is waking. We have until the moon turns. And she is not behind us anymore—"*

He turns.

Ilse Vane is standing ten paces away, on their side of the light, boots dry, expression almost gentle.

"I believe," the warden says, "we have unfinished business."
