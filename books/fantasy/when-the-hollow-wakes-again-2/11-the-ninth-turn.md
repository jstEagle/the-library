## 11. The Ninth Turn

The moon was wrong. I noticed it first the way you notice a stranger in your own kitchen—not by looking, but by the shape of the silence it left behind.

I'd kept track of the turns since Wren's first nightmare, scratching a crescent into the beam above my hearth each night she woke screaming. Thirteen scratches for thirteen turns; that had been the arithmetic of her dreams, and I'd trusted arithmetic the way other men trusted God. But on the ninth night, standing in the yard with my breath steaming, I counted what hung over the fells and understood that the sky had stopped keeping our accounts.

It should have been a half-moon. It looked like one, mostly—except the shadowed edge wasn't an edge at all. It was fraying. Light bled off the terminator like dye off a wet wound, thinning where it should have been clean, and when I narrowed my eyes against it, the whole disc seemed to shiver, as if seen through water. As if it were being *spent*.

"Wren," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I intended. "Come look at this."

She came barefoot across the cold ground, the way she always did, unbothered by frost or stone or anything the world could do to her. She stood beside me, small shoulder against my ribs, and looked up. Then she made a sound I hadn't heard from her before—a low, clicking syllable in the old tongue, and though I couldn't translate it, I knew its weight. It was the sound you make when you've been dreading something and it finally arrives.

"How many?" I asked her. Stupid question. She couldn't answer in words I owned.

But she held up her hands. Nine fingers. Then she folded them all down but three, and pressed those three fingers flat against her sternum, hard, like she was trying to hold something shut inside her chest.

Three more turns. And the moon above us was already dying wrong.

* * *

I went into the village two days later because we needed salt, and because a man can only live on dread for so long before he needs to see ordinary people doing ordinary things and tell himself the world still works.

The village did not work anymore.

I saw it at the baker's first. Old Tam Hessel, who has sold bread on that corner since before I could walk, stood behind his counter staring at a woman he'd known thirty years as if she'd asked him to pay in teeth. She wanted her usual loaf. He couldn't remember whether she took rye or white. That alone would have meant nothing—Tam forgot his own name some mornings—but it was the way he reached for it that stopped me: not groping, not confused. *Searching*. His hand drifted through the air in front of his chest like a man feeling along a wall in the dark for a door that used to be there.

"Rye," the woman said gently. "Always rye, Tam."

"Rye," he repeated, relieved. And then, under his breath, so quietly I almost missed it: "Was it? Was it always?"

On the green, children were playing a game none of them quite knew the rules to. I watched a boy of maybe seven stop mid-run, turn in a circle, and start crying—not tantrum-crying, but the lost, bewildered weeping of someone who has woken somewhere unfamiliar. His mother scooped him up and soothed him, and I thought: there, at least, that's fixed, mothers know their children.

Except that three stalls down, a woman was asking everyone who passed whether they'd seen a girl in a yellow coat. Describing her own daughter's face to strangers like a missing dog. *About this tall. Brown hair, I think. I'm sorry, I can't—I know her, I swear I know her, I just can't see her.*

The covenant wasn't failing at the edges anymore. It was failing everywhere at once, and the valley—the valley that forgot—was finally doing to its own what it had done to every traveler for two hundred years.

Magic couldn't cross the mist. But memory, apparently, could leave whenever it pleased.

* * *

I bought salt from a boy who tried to charge me twice because he'd forgotten he'd already weighed it out, and I carried it home fast, hating how normal the weight felt in my hand. Salt. Bread. Lamp oil. The liturgy of survival. I'd spent my whole life believing that if you kept your ledger balanced and your hoard hidden and your name out of anyone's mouth, nothing larger than you could touch you.

The moon had just told me otherwise.

Wren was waiting in the doorway when I came up the path, and she wasn't alone—or rather, she was surrounded by an absence so complete it might as well have been company. The birds were gone. Not flown; gone quiet. For two days I'd noticed the hedges falling silent and told myself it was weather. Now I stood in the yard and listened properly, and the silence had texture. It had layers. The rooks weren't calling from the big elm. The wrens weren't in the hedgerow—bitter irony, that. Even the insects had stopped their small eternal machinery, and the stream below the fell was running mute, as if the water itself had decided not to speak.

Every animal in the valley had gone silent, and they'd done it the way soldiers go quiet when an officer passes. Waiting. Listening for something the rest of us couldn't hear yet.

Wren tugged my sleeve. She pointed at the animals' absence, then at herself, then made the gesture I'd learned meant *dream*: fingertips closed, opening slowly, like a bud.

"You're dreaming it too," I said. "Whatever's counting the moon. They can hear it."

She nodded. Then, with the terrible patience she had, she took my hand and pressed it flat against her own chest, over her heart. Beneath my palm, her heartbeat came—and it was wrong, wrong the way the moon was wrong, skipping and stuttering, keeping time with something that wasn't blood.

Nine turns. Three fingers pressed against her sternum. Her heart counting down in a dead language, and everything alive in the valley holding its breath to listen.

I sat down right there in the dirt, salt bag and all, because my legs had made a decision without consulting me.

Here is what I understood, sitting in my own yard with a mute child's racing heart under my hand: I had built my life on a single belief—take only what won't be missed, sell only what can be replaced, keep yourself so small and careful that the great machinery of the world grinds past without noticing you. My mother had died believing something similar, or so I'd assumed, and I'd inherited her caution like her eyes, never questioning either.

But the covenant was breaking. The mist was thinning. The moon was spending itself like a merchant burning through stock before the tax man comes, and when it ran out, whatever had been held outside this valley for two centuries was going to come home—and it would not be gentle, because nothing that has been locked out that long comes back gentle. Villagers were already losing their children's faces. Roads would go next. Names. Then the valley itself would forget it was a valley, and then—

And then a girl of perhaps ten years had walked out of a storm speaking a language older than the mist, carrying a broken crescent, and knocked on *my* door. Out of every door in the Hollow. Mine.

That was no accident. That had never been an accident. And the fragments sleeping in my hoard—the oath my mother swore, the bronze token in her young hands, the words I'd carried for years without knowing they were kindling—all of it said the same thing: the great machinery of the world had not merely noticed me. It had been waiting for me.

I had spent my life stealing memories because I believed forgetting was safety. Now forgetting was flooding the valley like the mist itself had turned inward, and the only memories that mattered—the ones that could stop it—were locked in my own head, stolen from a woman I loved and lost and never once mourned properly.

"Wren." I crouched to her level. Her heartbeat hammered against my palm through her shirt. "Those fragments in my hoard. The ones in the old tongue. If I find them—if I learn what my mother swore—do you think it tells us how to fix this?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she did something new: she touched my temple, lightly, two fingers, and then drew her hand away and opened it, as if she'd taken something invisible from me. Held it up between us. *Gave it back.*

Give, not take. I didn't understand it yet. I noted it anyway, the way I note everything—hoarding even gestures now, even the shape of things I can't yet read.

We left at dusk. I took the hoard, every vial and wrapped bundle of it, sewn into a pack I carried against my spine, and I took the broken crescent, and I took Wren's hand, and we went up onto the fells where the road forgot itself and the mist glowed faintly along the western rim like a bruise showing through skin.

Behind us, the village lights came on one by one, and I found myself counting them, and finding fewer than there should have been, and understanding that soon the people in those houses would stand in their own kitchens unable to say why they lived there or who had lit the lamps before them.

Three turns left, and the moon was already bleeding out.

Halfway up the first ridge, Wren stopped. She gripped my hand hard enough to hurt, and pointed down the valley—not at the village, not at the mist, but at the dark heart of the Hollow itself, where the tarn lay and the drowned chapel slept beneath its black water.

Something out there was glowing. Faint, blue-white, patient, pulsing in rhythm exactly matching the stutter of her heart.

She looked up at me, and for the first time since the storm, she spoke—one word in the old tongue, clear as a bell across the freezing air.

I don't know what it means yet. But I will. Because tomorrow, we're going to the drowned chapel, and whatever my mother started there, I intend to finish it—before the thirteenth turn takes the last thing this valley has left to lose.
