## 12. Ilse's Bargain

The letter arrived at the ford, sealed in wax the color of a bruise, and Ilse Vane read it standing in the shallows with the water parting around her boots without ever touching them.

*The Ninth Turn has passed in the outer realms,* it said. *The tide of wild magic has reached the city walls. The Spire is drowning in its own light. You are instructed to secure the key by any means necessary and return with all haste.*

She folded the letter into quarters and burned it in her palm without flinching. The ash drifted down to the river and did not float away; it sank, obediently, as though even here the water knew what she was.

"By any means necessary," she said aloud, to no one. The words tasted like someone else's.

---

Ilse had crossed into the Hollow nine days ago, and she still had not gotten used to the silence inside her own chest.

In the outer realms, magic was a second heartbeat. You felt it always—a low thrum beneath the ribs, the pressure of an ocean you carried everywhere. Wards hummed against your skin. Spells sat on the tongue like coins. Even sleeping, you dreamed in light.

Here there was nothing. The valley drank her power at the mist-line and gave back only silence, and Ilse had spent nine days being, for the first time in thirty years, merely a woman. Her knees ached from walking. Her hands were cold. She had eaten bread bought from a suspicious baker who charged her double because she was a stranger, and she could not conjure so much as a spark to warm her tea.

Her order would have called it purification. The Votaries of the Sealed Gate believed that proximity to the old covenant sites stripped the soul to its foundations, and that this was holy work. Ilse had once believed it too, with the clean, uncomplicated faith of a girl of nineteen who had watched her village burn with a fire that would not die when the houses did.

She was forty-one now, and belief had grown calluses.

She climbed the ridge above the river as the sun went down and looked out over the Hollow: the crooked village huddled in its fold of hills, the woodsmoke rising straight in windless air, the mist wall glowing faintly at the valley's rim like a scar that had never learned to fade. Somewhere down there, a thief and a child were hiding from her.

Somewhere beyond the mist, her world was drowning.

---

You had to understand what the wild magic was doing to grasp why Ilse had walked nine days through a valley that hated her kind.

It had started small, the way floods do. A meadow outside Corvale that bloomed and bloomed and would not stop until the flowers stood taller than a man and strangled the cattle in their sleep. A well in Marrow's Reach that granted wishes—small ones, cruel ones, the way a monkey's paw is generous—and kept granting them long after anyone wished anything. A boy in the Spire itself, a student of the lesser arts, whose shadow peeled off the floor one night and walked out of his room and had not been seen since, though the boy screamed every time a candle guttered.

The covenant that had held the Hollow for three hundred years—the great sealing, the First Covenant, wrought in bronze and blood and oath—had been the anchor-stone of all ordered magic in the world. It did not merely keep magic out of the valley. It kept magic *in line* everywhere else, the way a keystone keeps an arch standing. And someone had broken it. Chipped it. Cracked the crescent seal that hung at the heart of the rite, and left the whole arch groaning.

Now the wild magic came through the cracks like water through a dam, and it did not ask permission, and it did not distinguish between the wicked and the innocent. It drowned a granary in living grain. It taught a flock of sheep to speak, and what they said made their shepherd take his own life. In the lowlands near the mist, entire villages had simply—stopped. Not died. Stopped. People wandering their own streets unable to remember which door was theirs, which name was theirs, while the magic poured through them looking for somewhere to settle, and finding nothing settled anywhere, moved on.

The Council called it the Rising Tide. The Votaries called it the Unbinding. The people called it the end of the world arriving slowly enough that everyone could watch.

And the scholars of the Spire, digging through three hundred years of sealed records by candlelight while the walls of their tower wept light, had found the answer.

Not an answer. A *person*. A child, born after the breaking, marked by the old tongue, carrying in her small body the last unbroken thread of the First Covenant's making. A living key. She could reseal the gate—or, some argued, remake the covenant entirely, forge something new that might hold without requiring what the old one had required. Either way, the key had to be brought to the Spire. Studied. Used. Whatever word you preferred that let you sleep.

There were seven keys once, the records said. Six had been found over the centuries and spent—one by one—in maintenance of the old seal. None of them had survived the spending. The seventh had vanished before the first turn of this crisis, spirited across the mist into the one place in the world where the Votaries' reach ended and their power failed.

Into the Hollow. Into the hands of whoever found her first.

Which was how Ilse Vane had come to be negotiating with a memory-broker in a village that smelled of tallow and wet wool, trying to buy a child.

---

Maren's shop was the only building in the Hollow with two locks on its door, and both of them were decorative. Ilse picked them in four seconds flat and let herself in at dusk, and the broker looked up from her ledger with the expression of a woman who had expected this visit for days.

"You're the warden," Maren said.

"I'm a traveler."

"You're a warden." The broker closed the ledger with deliberate care. "I've sold memories to wardens' couriers before. I know the posture. The stillness. You people stand like you're afraid of disturbing evidence." She poured two cups of something amber without asking. "Sit. Tell me what you're buying."

Ilse sat. The chair creaked under her like an accusation. "I want the location of a safehouse belonging to a man named Kestrel Kavanagh."

"And?"

"And the child he's harboring."

Maren turned her cup slowly on its saucer. Outside, the evening bell rang, thin and cracked. "The child," she repeated. "The mute one. The one who talks in a dead language." Her eyes came up, sharp as a fishhook. "Everyone wants that child lately. You're the third inquiry this month."

"The other two?"

"One was a merchant passing through. Lied badly about why he was asking." Maren smiled without warmth. "One was nobody. Or claimed to be." She leaned back. "What's it worth to you? And don't insult me with coin. Coin I can get. What do you have that I *want*, warden?"

Ilse considered her answer the way she had been trained to consider everything: completely, and without mercy toward herself included. Then she reached into the satchel at her hip and set a small glass vial on the table. Inside it, silver liquid swirled against the glass like smoke caught underwater.

"A memory," she said. "Extracted and preserved. From my own archive." She watched the broker's face change. "It's the day of the Spire's founding festival, three hundred years ago. Before the Covenant. The last recorded day the valley now called the Hollow was open to the world. There are perhaps five such memories in existence, and mine is the clearest."

Maren's hand hovered over the vial and did not touch it. That was discipline, Ilse noted. Greed with a bridle on it. Dangerous.

"That's worth more than this whole village," Maren said quietly.

"It's worth more than most cities." Ilse slid it an inch closer. "For the location. The child's nature I'll explain freely, because you should know what you're selling: she is the last living key to the First Covenant. The thing that broke three years ago broke *because of her birth*, or around it—I don't fully understand the mechanics, and neither, frankly, does the Spire. What I understand is this: while she lives unbound, the outer realms drown. Every week she stays hidden, more villages stop. More shadows walk off with their owners. My order believes that if we bring her through the mist and seat her at the gate, the covenant can be reforged, and the world gets to keep existing."

"And the man Kavanagh?" Maren asked. "He steals memories. Sells them to me. Half this valley's past has passed through my ledger via his fingers." Something flickered behind her eyes—old, tired. "He won't give her up easily. He's not the type."

"You know him."

"I've bought from him for eleven years." Maren looked at the vial again. "He's a thief. Cold. Careful. The best I've ever dealt with, and I've dealt with monsters." A pause. "But he took that child in during a storm, and thieves like him don't do that. So whatever you're planning, warden, plan for the fact that he's changed."

"People change when the world ends," Ilse said. "It's the only time they reliably do."

---

That night, camped alone on the ridge with no fire—for fire invited questions, and questions invited attention—Ilse took out her private papers and read them again, as she did every night, though she knew every word.

Her commission, signed in the Archvotary's hand: *Secure the key. Deliver her to the Spire alive and intact. The method is yours to determine.*

Her orders' addendum, unsigned, in a different hand: *If delivery proves impossible, ensure the key cannot fall into other hands.*

And beneath those, in her own writing, older, from her first year of service, when she still copied scripture into her reports because she thought devotion was the same as duty:

*Mercy is not softness. Mercy is doing the hard thing quickly so that suffering is short.*

She had built her whole life on that sentence. She had said it over burning villages where the fire-magic had to be cut off at its root, over children too far gone in wild-binding to be unwound, over her own sister, who had drunk from a wish-well in the early years of the Tide and had wished, in her madness, never to stop wishing—and Ilse had done the hard thing quickly, and suffered shortly, and told herself every time that this was love wearing armor.

So when she pictured the child—the small barefoot girl in the storm-lit doorway of a thief's house, whom Ilse had watched through a window for exactly ninety seconds before withdrawing—she did not picture cruelty. She pictured the Spire's white halls. Scholars who would be gentle, at first. Tests. Questions in a language the girl already spoke. And then the gate. The spending. The six keys before her, none of whom survived.

*Mercy,* Ilse wrote in her journal, by moonlight, because she dared not strike a match. *Is it mercy to save a world with a child, or to spare a child and lose a world? My order says the question answers itself. I have killed enough people to know that questions which answer themselves are usually the ones we've decided not to examine.*

She closed the journal. Below her, in the valley, the village slept. Somewhere in it, a thief lay awake beside a child who could end the drowning or become its final victim, and Ilse found—to her genuine surprise—that she hoped he was holding her, and telling her a story, and being kind.

Because kindness, in the weeks ahead, was going to be scarce.

---

Three days later, Maren sent the signal—a lamp in her window, lit and snuffed twice—and Ilse descended into the Hollow for the exchange.

The broker had the safehouse location written on a scrap of paper and pushed it across the counter without ceremony. But her hand stayed on it a moment longer than a sale required.

"There's an entry in my ledger," Maren said. "Eleven years old. A payment I made, not received. To a woman named Kavanagh. Same surname as your thief." She stared at the middle distance, at nothing. "I've been turning it over. Wondering if I want to remember why I paid her."

"Do you?" Ilse asked.

"No." Maren released the paper. "That's the trouble with my trade, warden. Everyone assumes the curse of it is remembering too much. It isn't. It's choosing, every single day, what not to." She straightened. "Take your paper. Take your child. Do whatever your order does. But hear one thing from a woman who has sold ten thousand memories and kept only the worst of hers: the thief loves that girl. However it happened, however fast—it's true. And men like him, loving something for the first time—" her mouth twisted, "—they don't bargain. They break things. Including themselves."

"I'll keep it in mind."

"Don't keep it in mind." Maren's voice dropped, and for one moment the broker's mask slipped entirely, and underneath was just a tired woman in a cold shop. "*Keep* it. It's the only thing I've given away free in twenty years, and it's the truest thing I own."

---

The safehouse was a tanner's cottage on the village's drowned edge, and Ilse reached it at dawn with two hired blades from the low country—men who owed the Votaries debts rather than money, which made them reliable in the way only shame can be.

She stood in the lane and looked at the little house. Smoke from its chimney. A child's shoes drying on the windowsill, too large for her, stuffed with straw to hold their shape. Through the window's gap: the girl at a table, drawing in the frost with one finger, and the thief watching her with an expression Ilse recognized from mirrors she'd stopped owning years ago.

The expression of a man guarding the last thing he had.

*Any means necessary.* The Archvotary's handwriting rose behind her eyes. And beneath it, her own younger self: *the hard thing, quickly.*

Ilse Vane drew a breath of valley air—clean, magicless, mortal air—and gave the signal.

But as the hired blades moved toward the door, and the first thread of smoke began to rise where smoke shouldn't, she found herself thinking not of the Spire, not of the drowning world, not of mercy in its armored coat.

She was thinking of the child's finger tracing shapes in the frost. Shapes Ilse half-knew. Old letters. Old tongue.

A word, maybe.

And she wondered—not for the first time, but for the first time with fear—if the valley itself had already begun answering.
