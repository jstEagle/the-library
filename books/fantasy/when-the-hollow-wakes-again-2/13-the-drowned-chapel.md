## 13. The Drowned Chapel

Wren did not sleep that night. She sat by the dead fire with her knees pulled to her chest, mouthing words in the old tongue like a woman counting coins she couldn't spend, and when the grey light came through the cave mouth she was already standing, already facing east, already certain.

"That way," she said. In common speech it was all she had. In the old tongue it would have been a paragraph.

"That way is the high fells," I said. "There's nothing up there but sheep bones and weather."

She looked at me the way she always did when I said something true and beside the point — a small, patient sorrow, as if I were a lock she'd already picked and was only waiting for me to notice the door swinging.

We went east.

I want to say I led us, because leading is what I do, because for eleven years my whole survival has been built on knowing every goat track and smuggler's cut in the Hollow better than the mist itself. But the truth is uglier and more useful: Wren walked, and I followed, and the fells opened before her like they remembered her. Twice we came to ravines with no crossing that I knew of, and twice she walked along the edge without hesitating until — there — a shelf of stone, hidden from above, wide enough for one frightened man and one unafraid girl.

The valley was wrong around us as we climbed. I kept expecting Ilse Vane behind us, or Maren's hired eyes, or the broker's reach, which is longer than people think. But the silence had swallowed everything else. Since the ninth turn, no birds. No wind in the grass, though the grass bent. The tarns lay flat as hammered tin, and our reflections walked beneath us upside down, and I could not shake the feeling that they were the ones doing the walking.

"Where are you taking us?" I asked, on the third morning.

Wren stopped. She put her hand flat against a boulder, and she said a word I knew — knew the way you know a face seen once across a market crowd — and the word meant *chapel*. Or maybe *drowning*. The old tongue does not separate a thing from what happened to it.

Then I knew too. Not remembered. Knew, the way the stones know rain.

"There's a tarn," I said slowly, "north of the Grey Fells. Shepherds won't drink from it. My mother—" The sentence died. It had been dying since the safehouse burned, since Ilse's torch took my hoard and my mother's oath-fragment with it. "There was something about that tarn."

Wren nodded once, gravely, and kept walking.

* * *

We smelled the water before we saw it. Cold and green and old, like the inside of a sealed room.

The tarn sat in a bowl of hills with no stream feeding it and none leaving it, which any child of the Hollow can tell you means trouble. Mist should have pooled there — mist loves standing water — but the mist kept its distance, ringing the far shore in a slow grey circle, never crossing some line only it could see. And rising out of the center of the tarn, no more than a long stone's throw from shore, was a broken bell tower.

Just the tower. The rest of the chapel was under the water, and had been long enough that the lake had made its peace with it. Weeds grew from the windows. The bell was gone; the yoke that held it hung rusted and empty, creaking in no wind at all.

"The Drowned Chapel," I said. Children's stories. You don't swim there. You don't fish there. You don't ask why, because in the Hollow asking why is how you end up knowing things that get you sold to brokers. "My mother came here."

Wren took off her boots.

"Wren." I caught her wrist. Her pulse under my fingers was slow and steady as deep water. "Whatever's down there, it isn't kind. Places like this don't keep their dead. They keep everything else."

She looked at my hand on her wrist, then at me, and said something in the old tongue — three soft syllables — and pressed her broken bronze crescent into my palm. Then she waded in.

I have watched people drown. It is not a thing the Hollow hides from you. So I will tell you plainly: watching her walk into that water, sinking to her waist, her shoulders, the dark closing over her hair — and watching her *keep going*, walking along the bottom as if the tarn were a road — was worse than any drowning I've witnessed. Because she wasn't struggling. She was expected.

I stood on the shore like a coward for the length of ten heartbeats. Then I put the crescent around my neck, cursed her, cursed my mother, cursed the covenant and everyone who ever swore one, and went in after her.

The cold hit like a hand. Below the surface the water cleared instead of darkening, which is not how water works, and I could see down and down: a roof first, slates sloughed off in patches; then windows glowing faintly green; then, through the great west door, which stood open as chapels should, a floor of pale stone with letters carved across every inch of it.

Letters I could almost read.

Wren stood on the threshold below, hair drifting like ink, looking up at me, and pointed deeper.

* * *

The crypt was under the altar, down a stair flooded to its last two steps. Air waited there — a pocket of it, old and close, trapped under the vaulted ceiling like a secret under a tongue. We broke the surface into darkness, and Wren struck flint from her pocket — the child carried flint, God help me, she'd been ready for this longer than I've been alive — and lit a stub of tallow candle, and light crawled across the walls.

Bones, first. I'll be honest about that. Rows of them, seated in niches, dressed in robes gone stiff as bark. Keepers, I thought. Whatever a keeper is.

And then the walls themselves, and I forgot the dead entirely.

Every stone was carved. Not decorated — *written*. The old tongue ran in columns and spirals and rivers across every surface, floor to vault, thousands upon thousands of characters, and where the carving was deepest the stone still shone faintly, moon-pale, as if the words had been poured in silver and the silver refused to die. The air in that pocket hummed against my teeth. This was not a record. This was a *machine*. Words laid in patterns the way a smith lays charcoal, built to hold heat, built to hold —

— built to hold magic out.

"It's here," I whispered, and my voice sounded obscene in that hush. "The covenant. It's not a treaty. It's not an oath sworn in a field. It's *this*. A working. The whole valley is the working." And the mist-line, the mist itself — I understood it suddenly, the way you understand a lock once you've seen its insides — the mist was not a wall. It was a wound. The place where the working met the world and ground against it, thirteen turns, thirteen teeth, a wheel turning and turning and holding the door shut.

And the wheel was failing.

Wren was already at the far wall, running her fingers over a panel of script denser than the rest, worn smooth in places by hands — recent hands, older hands, my mother's hands? — and she turned to me and pointed at the smooth patch, and then at me, and then pressed both her own hands flat against the stone.

*I can't read it,* her gesture said. *You can.*

"I can't read it either," I said.

But that was a lie, and the stones knew it, and so did I. Because the language had been waking in me for days now, ever since the tarn road, ever since her — every word she spoke settling into me like rain into cracked ground, filling channels someone had dug long before I was born. My mother hadn't just stolen my memories of her work. She'd buried the key to them in me, in the oldest vault there is: the one I didn't know I owned.

I am a thief. Let me tell you what that means when it matters. It means that when I touch a mind — skin to skin, usually the temple, sometimes the wrist — I can take. A memory, whole, lifts free like a lily pad off the water. I've done it hundreds of times. Faces, weddings, griefs, first snows. I take the sharp ones; they sell best. I leave the person standing there a little lighter and call it fair trade for bread.

I had never tried to steal from a stone.

But the candle was guttering, and the water was rising — slowly, so slowly, but rising, the tarn breathing us out a warning — and Wren was looking at me with those eyes that had crossed a mountain range to find me specifically, and pride is a luxury I gave up years ago.

I pressed my palm to the smooth patch of wall. And I took.

Stealing from a living mind feels like lifting fruit: warm, resistant, then suddenly free. Stealing from the chapel was nothing like that. It was like putting my hand into a river that had been waiting eleven years for exactly this hand. The memory didn't lift. It *poured*.

And I was standing in the crypt when it was dry, thirty years ago, and the walls were new-carved and blazing, and a woman stood at the altar with a bronze crescent raised in her fist.

My mother. Younger than I had ever seen her — younger than the burned fragment, younger than any picture in my hoard — her hair loose, her sleeves rolled, a cut on her palm still bleeding. With her stood twelve others, six and six, and among them a face I knew: Maren. Maren, forty pounds and thirty years ago lighter, her jaw set like a woman doing a thing she'd already decided to regret.

They were not sealing the covenant. They were *renewing* it — and failing. The words they chanted rose and thinned. The silver in the walls flickered. And my mother's voice rang out over theirs, furious and afraid:

*"It wants remembering! Not the words — the words are nothing — it wants a life given to it. Willingly. All of it. Everything a soul carries, poured into the stone, or the wheel turns empty!"*

An old man among the twelve spat: *"Then it stays broken. No one pays that price."*

*"Someone must," my mother said. "Or in thirteen turns of the bad moon, the mist comes down and everything the outer realms are drowning in comes flooding through."*

*"Then let it drown," said a young woman I didn't know, and turned away, and one by one they all turned away, my mother last, her hand pressed flat against the wall the way Wren had touched it, and I heard her whisper — not the ritual words, something small and private, something meant for no one:*

*"Forgive me. I bought us time instead. Let my children curse me for it. Let one of them finish it."*

The vision shattered.

I came back to myself on my knees in two inches of freezing water, the candle dead, Wren's arms around my shoulders, and the knowledge sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone.

The rite. I had the rite now, whole, every word, every turn of it — it unspooled behind my eyes in silver script, patient as architecture. Pour memory into the crescent. Pour *all* of it. A willing sacrifice, complete, of everything a soul carries — every theft, every hoard, every name, every face, every single moment of love and grief and hunger, given freely into the breaking wheel until the wheel had weight enough to hold.

Not death. Something the old tongue considered worse, and having met it once at a broker's scale, I had learned to agree: forgetting. Total, voluntary, chosen. To stand at the threshold and hand over your entire self to the stone.

"No," I said aloud, to the walls, to my mother, to the clever buried woman who had left this in me like a bomb in a loaf of bread. "No. You don't get to do this. You don't get to hide the answer in your own son and let him find it in a flooded hole in the ground—"

Wren's hand found mine in the dark. Small. Certain. Cold as the water and warmer than anything in that crypt.

She knew. Of course she knew — she'd dreamed thirteen turns her whole life; she'd crossed the mountains mute, carrying a broken token, looking for one specific thief. She hadn't come for the covenant. She'd come for the man willing to pay for it.

The water reached our waists. Above us, through the green-lit doorway, the surface of the tarn trembled, and far overhead the empty bell yoke creaked in no wind at all, counting.

I put my arm around her and kicked for the light.

There was one more thing the stones had shown me, one detail I hadn't told her, and I held it in my mouth like a coin I couldn't decide to spend: the rite required a full pouring, yes — but the visions of my mother's failed attempt had shown the mechanism, and the mechanism had a seam. One soul alone could not fill the wheel. That was why she'd failed. That was why the old man had refused and walked away, and why the words *no one pays that price* had echoed down thirty years into a flooded crypt and found me kneeling in the dark.

One thief alone cannot pour enough. It would take two kinds of loss.

I didn't yet know what that meant. But the tarn knew, and the walls knew, and somewhere out beyond the mist-ring, on a ridge I couldn't see, I felt — the way you feel a stare between your shoulder blades — that Ilse Vane knew too.

The water closed over our heads, and we rose through it toward a sky the color of pewter, where the moon hung thin and wrong in the afternoon, one turn from its twelfth.

Two days. Maybe three.

And I had a confession to make to a child, and no idea how a man tells a girl she came all this way to watch him forget her.
