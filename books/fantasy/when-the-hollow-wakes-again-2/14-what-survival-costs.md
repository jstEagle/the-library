## 14. What Survival Costs

The drowned chapel was behind us now, but its water was still in my ears.

I heard it in the silence between Wren's footsteps and mine as we climbed out of the tarn's basin and up into the birch woods on the far side—water that had spoken to me through stone, through the crypt where my mother had knelt a lifetime ago and sworn away everything she loved. The memory I'd pulled from the flooded masonry still sat in me like a swallowed coal. The full rite. Every word of it. And the price, which the stones remembered more honestly than any living soul would have dared.

A willing sacrifice of memory. All of it. Not a theft from yourself—a gift, poured out completely, until nothing remained that knew why it was pouring.

I had been turning that over for two days, walking with it, sleeping beside it, feeding Wren dried fish over a small fire while it sat in my chest like a stone I couldn't cough up. Two days of telling myself there would be another way. A loophole. A cheaper door.

There wasn't. The covenant didn't bargain. It only asked.

And tonight, sitting across the fire from a child who trusted me, I understood that I could not carry it alone one hour longer.

* * *

We'd made camp in a hollow ringed by birches gone gold at the edges, the first true touch of autumn, though the seasons had been lying all month—the moon waning wrong, thirteen turns crushed into weeks, the valley forgetting itself village by village. Wren sat cross-legged with her hands around a cup of pine-needle tea she wouldn't drink, because she never drank anything warm, and watched me with those gray eyes that were older than her face had any right to hold.

"Wren," I said. "I have to tell you something."

She tilted her head. Waiting. She was always waiting; patience was the first thing they taught you, wherever she'd come from, whatever order had shaped her into a key.

I looked at the fire instead of at her. That was cowardice, but I've never claimed otherwise.

"You know what I do," I said. "What I did. You've seen me take a memory. You held the merchant's hand that night outside Harrow Ford, when I lifted her grief for her dead son." I'd done it because she begged me to, but that didn't make it cleaner. Nothing I did was clean. "You know I sell what I take."

She nodded slowly.

"What you don't know is how long." I turned my hands over, palms up. Thief's hands. Calloused not from work but from other people's temples, other people's tears, the particular pressure it took to slide a life loose from its moorings. "Twenty years. Since I was barely older than you. My mother taught me the trick of it before she died—or left, or whatever she did—and after she was gone I discovered I was good at it. Better than good. People came to the Hollow to escape things. That's what the mist is for. It keeps magic out, and it keeps the rest of the world's cruelty mostly out too, and so the people who crawl under it are people carrying something they can't put down. A dead child. A betrayal. A face they can't stop seeing. And I would find them on the road, and I would be kind to them—" my voice caught on the word, actually caught, which surprised me— "and then I would take the thing they couldn't bear, and I would sell it to Maren, and Maren would smuggle it beyond the mist to buyers who wanted to feel someone else's worst day like a play performed just for them."

Wren hadn't moved. Her tea steamed untouched between her palms.

"There was a woman," I went on, because now that the dam had cracked I couldn't stop it, "a weaver from the lowlands, who came in with her sister's death lodged in her like an arrowhead. She wept on my shoulder in a wayside inn and called me the first kind person she'd met in a hundred miles. I took her sister's funeral from her. All of it. The singing, the burial, the last words. She walked out of the valley light as air and free as a bird and she never knew what it cost her, because that's the mercy of it—you don't miss what you don't remember. But somewhere beyond the mist some fat collector paid Maren four silver crowns to sit alone in a room and grieve a stranger's sister until he'd had his fill, and then he set it aside like a book he'd finished." I laughed, or something near it. "Four crowns. That's what a goodbye is worth."

"Kes," Wren said. Just that. His name for me, the only word of common speech she'd managed to shape, worn smooth from use.

"I'm not finished." I wasn't. God help me, I wasn't finished, and I think some part of me had been walking toward this confession since the night she knocked on my door, barefoot in the storm, and I'd felt something crack open in my chest that I'd bricked over years ago. "The worst ones weren't the griefs. Grief, at least, people want rid of. The worst were the good memories. The ones they clung to. A farmer who made me swear I'd only take the bad years, and I took his daughter's wedding too because the broker pays double for joy—it keeps better, travels better, rich buyers want to feel whole, not broken. He shook my hand afterward. Thanked me. Said he felt ten years lighter. He'd forgotten he'd ever been a father, and he thanked me."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us an owl should have been calling, and wasn't. The animals had been silent for weeks now, holding their breath along with everything else.

"And here's the thing I need you to understand, Wren, before you hear it some worse way." I finally made myself look at her. "The rite at the threshold. The one your dreams have been dragging us toward, turn by turn, the one the chapel showed me in the stones. It requires memory freely given. All of it. Not stolen from yourself—given. Poured into the crescent until the oath has material to bind with, until the covenant has something living to hold it shut. Everything I am. Everything I remember. My mother's face. The roads I've walked. Every theft, every silver crown, every—" I stopped. Started again. "Every moment of you. The storm. The knock. Teaching you to gut a fish on the riverbank while you laughed at me in your dead language. The night you fell asleep against my shoulder on the fells and I sat still until dawn so I wouldn't wake you. All of it goes. And what wakes on the far side of the thirteenth turn won't know your name. Won't know there was ever a name to know."

Her eyes had gone wide. Not frightened—I'd expected frightened. Something else. Something that looked almost like recognition, as if I'd confirmed a thing she'd already counted in her dreams.

"So when we get there," I said, quieter, "when the moon makes its twelfth turn and we stand at the threshold, you should know what you're walking me toward. And you should know that if you want to hate me for it—for the twenty years, for the weaver, for the farmer, for every hand I ever touched without asking—you're entitled to every hour of that hate. I'm not asking for forgiveness. I'm asking you to walk in clear-eyed. Because I've spent my whole life taking from people things they couldn't bear to lose, and the one time I give something back, it'll be everything, and no one will remember it was given. No one will remember me. That's the arithmetic of it. That's what survival costs, Wren. It costs the surviving."

The silence afterward was enormous. The birches stood around our little hollow like witnesses.

Wren set down her cup. She crawled across the space between us—not quickly, deliberately, the way she did everything—and took both my thief's hands in her small ones. Her fingers were cold, as always. Her grip was not.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"Not hating." She frowned, hunting for the words, her common speech coming apart at the seams the way it always did when she needed it to hold weight. "Not... entitle." She shook her head at herself, frustrated, and switched to the old tongue—three soft syllables, liquid and falling—and then pressed my hand flat against her own chest, over her heart, the gesture unmistakable even across the gulf of languages.

*This,* she meant. *In here.*

"You don't understand," I said. "What I am. What I've—"

"Yes." Firm. Then, struggling: "Understand. Yes. All." Her brow furrowed with effort, hauling words up from somewhere deep, one at a time, like a girl drawing water from a well too tall for her. "You take. Twenty year. Take, take, take." She said it without flinching, without softening it, and somehow that was worse and better than any comfort would have been. "Bad man. Maybe." A ghost of a shrug. "But."

She released one of my hands and touched the crescent token at her throat—the bronze half-moon, snapped clean in two, that she'd carried through the storm to my door. Then she pointed at me.

"Came for you."

My breath went out of me like I'd been struck. "Wren—"

"Not valley. Not threshold. Not—" she waved a hand at the dark trees, the silent sky, the whole failing world— "this." She tapped my chest, once, hard, right over the heart. "You. Storm night. Dream say: go under mist. Find thief." Her mouth twisted, trying for the next word, losing it, finding another. "Find... Kes. Only Kes. Mother-mother—" she stopped, and I saw her reach for a word that wasn't hers to have yet, and let it go, and choose a simpler one. "Dream say you have. Inside you. Piece. Oath-piece. From before."

"My mother's memory," I whispered. The one I'd found in my hoard and lost to Ilse's torch. The one whose echo still lived in me, because some fragments root deeper than fire reaches. "The oath. You dreamed your way to me because of what my mother carried."

"Dream say," Wren repeated, stubborn, as if the mechanics mattered less than the truth underneath them. "Say: thief have key-piece. Say: thief also..." She ground to a halt. Tried again. "Thief also *hurt*. Also empty. Also—" she pressed her fist to her sternum and made a hollow sound, low in her throat, the old tongue word for a thing I suddenly understood without translation, a word that meant *a room with the furniture taken out.*

"That's not a reason to forgive me."

"Not forgive." She scowled at the word, tasted it, rejected it. "Forgive is—" she fluttered her fingers vaguely, meaning transaction, meaning ledger, meaning the whole economy of debt and payment I'd lived inside for twenty years. Then she leaned forward and pressed her forehead against mine, the way she'd done exactly twice before, both times when the dreams had shaken her too badly to speak. Her breath was warm against my face. When she spoke again it was barely above the fire's whisper, and it was in the old tongue, and I understood every syllable of it now, fully, terribly, the way I understood rain and hunger and the weight of a sleeping child against my shoulder:

*I do not forgive you. Forgiveness is for strangers. I came across the mist for you. I will stand at the threshold for you. Whatever you pour out, I will catch what falls, and I will carry it back to you, and one day you will hear your own name and it will mean something again. That is not mercy. That is my oath, and I swore it before I ever saw your face.*

I didn't answer. There was nothing in me equal to it. I sat there with my forehead against a child's forehead, this mute girl sent by dreams and moons and whatever powers had shattered the old covenant, this living key whom brokers and wardens and dying worlds all wanted to own, and she had looked straight into the ugliest accounting of my life and answered it not with pardon but with *promise.*

Shame doesn't die from kindness. I know that now. It doesn't die from being forgiven, either—especially not from a forgiveness you haven't earned and can't repay. What shame does, when it meets something like that, is break open. Like a hoard burning. Like a dam cracking. Like a thief discovering, at the end of twenty years of taking, that the only thing left worth stealing is a future he's about to give away.

Wren pulled back and looked at me, and wiped her thumb across my cheekbone, and I realized my face was wet.

"One more thing," I managed. My voice was wrecked. "When we get to the threshold. If I hesitate—if some part of me tries to keep a piece, tries to cheat the rite the way I've cheated everything else—you stop me. Do you understand? Whatever it takes. Shout the old tongue. Break the crescent if you have to. Don't let me survive cheaply. I've done enough of that for one lifetime."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, grave as a judge.

And that was when the fire guttered—all at once, all three flames bending flat toward the north as though a door had opened somewhere in the world—and Wren's head snapped up, her eyes going wide and fixed on the ridge above our hollow, where the birches stood black against a sky that had begun, very softly, to glow.

Not dawn. Dawn was hours off.

Moonlight, wrong-colored, silver shot through with green, pooling on the ridge like spilled mercury—and standing in it, unmistakable even at distance, a tall figure in a warden's grey coat, watching us.

Ilse Vane had found us again. And this time she wasn't alone.
