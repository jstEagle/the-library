## 15. Maren's Choice

Maren had kept the ledger for thirty years, and in thirty years she had never once gone out to meet a customer.

That was the rule of her trade. You sat behind your counter with your lamps and your ledgers and your little brass scales, and the valley came to you. Grief came to you. Hunger came to you. People walked down off the fells with their memories wrapped in oilcloth like cuts of meat, and you weighed them, and you paid, and you did not ask where they'd be going next. Asking was how you started caring. Caring was how you stopped making money. And money—real coin, smuggled up from beyond the mist in the false bottoms of spice crates—was the only thing Maren had ever trusted to stay.

But here she was, three miles past the village boundary at an hour when even the crows had given up on the day, standing in the middle of a road that was more mud than road, holding a lantern she'd lit against no one's orders but her own.

"You're a fool," she said aloud, to hear it in her own voice instead of the wind's.

The wind agreed with her.

She'd made the decision sometime after midnight, though she could not have named the hour it happened, because decisions like this didn't happen—they accumulated. Like silt. Like debt. It had started with the ledger entry, that old one she'd pulled from the bottom of the strongbox two nights ago: *Kavanagh, E. — one payment received — goods unspecified.* Her own handwriting, younger then, the loops still confident. She remembered taking the delivery now, if she let herself remember it—a woman at the mist-line with frost in her hair and a bronze crescent in her fist, who had looked at Maren the way you look at a door you already know is locked—and Maren had weighed whatever the woman gave her without asking, because asking was not the trade.

And then Ilse Vane had come into the shop with her dry river-stone voice and her questions, and Maren—who had sold better people than herself down worse rivers than any she knew—had heard her own mouth say, *east of the mill, past the second stile, there's a house that doesn't appear on anyone's tax rolls,* and had understood, with a clarity that arrived about twenty years too late, exactly what she was.

Not a broker. A fence for other people's lives. A woman who sold the road to a man's door for the price of staying invisible one more winter.

So. Here she was. In the mud. With the lantern.

The fells rose black on either side of the road, and somewhere ahead of her, if Wren's certainty held true—and Maren had learned enough about the child in three visits to believe that the girl's certainty held truer than most men's maps—the thief and his stolen key were walking toward the drowned chapel, following a path only the dead tongue could read.

She found them just before dawn, at the bend where the road crossed the old drover's track. Kestrel saw her first—he always saw everything first, that was his whole nature, a man built like a lockpick, all edges and angles—and he put himself between her and the child so smoothly it was almost graceful, almost funny, if you had the kind of humor that grew in dark places. Which Maren did. But she wasn't laughing tonight.

"Maren." His voice was flat. "Whatever they're paying you, I don't have it."

"They're paying nothing." She set the lantern down on a stone, deliberately, where he could see her hands. "They're paying *me* nothing. That's rather the point."

Behind him, the girl watched her with those eyes that made Maren think of wells—deep water, cold, older than the water had any right to be. Wren said something in the old tongue, soft, and Kestrel's jaw tightened as though the words had landed somewhere physical.

"She says you smell of ink," he translated. "And lies."

"She's not wrong." Maren opened her coat slowly—showing him, the way you show a dog you mean no harm—and drew out the package she'd carried through the dark hours. Oilcloth. Tied with waxed string. Small as a loaf, heavy as sin. "I'm not here to sell you, Kestrel. The warden already knows where you are. Bought that off me two nights ago. East of the mill, past the second stile—you'll want to keep moving, and you'll want to move like the road is already burning behind you, because by midmorning it will be."

Kestrel didn't move. Didn't blink. "Why tell me?"

"Because I'm done being the reason bad things find good doors."

"Bad things found my door because you pointed them at it."

"Yes," Maren said. "That too."

* * *

He wouldn't take the package at first. Of course he wouldn't. She'd have thought less of him if he had.

They stood in the grey light while the mist off the fells came down the slope like something breathing, and Maren explained it in the flattest voice she could manage, because the flat voice was the only one she trusted herself with anymore. "You brought me a memory once. Years back. A traveler, passing through—one of the last who still used the old roads. He sold it cheap. Said he couldn't carry it another mile."

"I don't remember it."

"No," Maren said. "You wouldn't. That's rather the shape of the thing." She turned the package over in her hands. "It was a childhood memory. Being loved. Being loved *specifically*—a mother's kitchen, a hand in yours, someone singing badly and meaning every note. The traveler wept the whole time he described what he was selling, and then he stopped weeping the moment it left him, and that frightened me more than the weeping had. I bought it. I meant to sell it beyond the mist, same as everything else."

"But you didn't."

"But I couldn't." She heard her own voice crack on the word and hated it, hated the crack, hated the thirty years of careful ledgers that had led to a cracked voice in a muddy road. "Do you know what happens to memories nobody buys, Kestrel? They fade. Not all at once—they go thin, like cloth worn at the fold. Except this one didn't. This one sat in my strongbox through eleven winters and stayed *whole*, and every time I opened that box for coin or silk or some widow's wedding day, there it was, being loved, being loved, being loved, and I could not for the life of me figure out why it wouldn't rot like the rest."

Kestrel's voice came out low. "Because it wasn't for sale."

"Or because it was waiting." Maren shrugged, briskly, before anything warmer could get loose. "I'm not a sentimental woman. I've sold confessions, first kisses, dying words. I sold a man his own name once—he wanted to forget who'd borne it. But this—" she held the package out, arm straight, the way you hold out a sword hilt-first, "—this one I regretted stealing the moment the string was tied. And I have very few regrets, Kestrel. I rationed them carefully. So take it, and get off my road, and let me have done."

"Why give it to *me*?" And there it was under his voice, the real question, the one he'd been carrying since the house burned: *why give anything to a thief.*

"Because you regretted stealing it too." She watched him flinch. Yes. She'd known. She'd always known—he'd come back to her shop twice that year asking after the traveler, asking if any similar lots had come in, wearing his wanting like a man wears a coat two sizes too small. "You stole memories for a living, boy, but that one you stole like a starving man steals bread. And then you tried to buy it back, which is the closest thing to prayer your trade allows." She pushed the package into his chest, hard, so he had to catch it or let it fall. He caught it. Of course he caught it. "Consider it a refund."

Wren stepped forward then, past Kestrel's guarding arm, and looked up at Maren with the well-water eyes, and spoke—not the old tongue this time, but common speech, broken and rust-hinged, each word placed like a stone across a stream:

"You... come also? Chapel. Water place. She is there. Mother-oath."

Maren laughed, short and graceless. "No, little key. I'm done walking toward oaths. I've spent my life selling people the exits. Someone has to stand at the door and lie convincingly."

"You'll die there," Kestrel said quietly. "If the warden finds out you lied."

"The warden won't find out I lied." Maren picked up her lantern. "The warden will find exactly what I show her: an old broker on a muddy road at dawn, headed home, having told her buyers precisely nothing because she saw nothing worth reporting. And if she doesn't believe it—" the shrug again, the armor again, "—then I'll have finally paid for thirty years of ledger entries, and honestly, Kestrel, the interest alone should have ruined me long ago."

She turned to go. Made it four steps.

"Maren."

She stopped. Did not turn around. Turning around would have been fatal, and she knew it, the way you know not to look down.

"The memory," Kestrel said behind her. "When I open it. Will I—"

"Will you feel it like it was yours?" She faced the fells, the grey east, the road home she'd never really had. "No. You'll feel it like it was *true*. That's better. Everything that's ours can be taken from us. Truth just sits there, being true, whether we own it or not." She started walking again. "Now move. The road burns by midmorning."

* * *

She reached the village as the sun came up the color of old pewter, and Ilse Vane was waiting outside the shop, leaning against the doorframe with the patience of standing water.

"You went out," the warden said.

"I went out."

"Where?"

"Checking a snare line," Maren said, unlocking her door with hands that did not shake, because she had spent thirty years teaching them not to, and by the gods they would not shake now. "Poachers on the north track. There usually are, this season."

Ilse studied her with eyes like coins rubbed featureless. "My employers pay for accuracy, broker. Not for loyalty. Loyalty is a currency with no exchange rate out here."

"Lucky for both of us," Maren said, stepping inside, setting her lantern on the counter among the scales and the ledgers and the ghosts, "that I've never had any."

She sat. She opened yesterday's ledger to a fresh page. And when Ilse Vane asked her question—*which way did they walk*—Maren dipped her pen, and smiled the smile that had closed a thousand bargains, and began to lie.

West, she said. Toward the high pass. Slow-going, with a child. Three days, maybe four, if the weather held.

Outside, far to the east, a road was burning.

Maren wrote it all down in her neat, confident, younger self's handwriting: *direction of travel, west; condition of parties, fatigued; probability of interception, high.* Every word a coin. Every coin a debt.

For the first time in thirty years, Maren hoped someone would come collect.
