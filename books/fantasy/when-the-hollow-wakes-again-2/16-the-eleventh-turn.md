## 16. The Eleventh Turn

**Ilse**

She found them at the tarn by the smell of woodsmoke, thin and guilty against the wet stone of the fells.

Ilse Vane had crossed three realms and one dead god's riverbed to reach this valley, and in all that distance she had never once needed to hurry. Wardens did not hurry. Hurrying was a confession that the world could outrun you. So she walked, and she let the hours Maren Doss had sold her stretch into nothing, because a broker who sells one client to save her ledger always lies about how much time the sale buys.

The delay had bought Kestrel Kavanagh four hours. Ilse gave him two.

Now she crested the ridge above the tarn in the last grey light of an evening that had forgotten how to end—the moon hanging wrong overhead, bloated and low, its wane stuttering like a heart losing rhythm. Below her, tucked into the elbow of a granite shoulder, a fire no larger than a cupped hand. A man hunched over it. A small figure beside him, wrapped in his coat, asleep or pretending to be.

Eleven turns. The moon had burned through eleven of its thirteen since the child crossed the mist-line, and each turn came faster than the last, as if the sky itself were running out of patience. In the outer realms, the wild magic was drowning coastlines. Rivers ran uphill singing. In Vharen's order they called it *the loosening*, and they spoke of it in the flat voices of people describing weather that kills.

Ilse believed in mercy. She had to; it was the only thing that made the work bearable. And mercy, tonight, looked like a small mute girl carried gently out of a dying valley before the valley took everything down with it.

She started down the slope. The scree did not shift under her boots. It never did.

* * *

**Kestrel**

He smelled her before he saw her—cold iron and river water, the scent of someone who crossed water without disturbing it—and he was on his feet with Wren behind him before the thought finished forming.

"Ilse Vane," he said. "You walk quiet for a woman who wants to be heard."

"I want to be understood," she said. "There's a difference." She stopped at the edge of the firelight, hands loose at her sides, and he noted—professionally, helplessly—noted everything: the grey warden's cloak, the crescent-and-bar sigil at her throat, the way her eyes went first to Wren and stayed there with something that was not hunger. That was the worst part. It looked almost like grief.

"You burned my mother's memory," Kestrel said. "My house. Everything I had."

"Yes." No apology in it. "And you lied to me at the ford, which cost me a day, which cost your valley another day. We are even in damage, Kestrel Kavanagh, and uneven in everything else." She crouched slowly, deliberately, bringing her face level with his. "I'm not going to fight you. If I wanted the girl taken, she'd be taken. I want her to come willingly, because what comes after matters more than you can imagine. The outer realms are drowning. Wild magic has no shape left to pour into, so it pours into whatever's nearest—children, rivers, graveyards. My order can contain her. Study her. Find the lock she fits. Or we can all stand here and watch thirteen turns finish falling while you play father to a key."

"She's not a key."

"Everything is a key. You were. Your mother was. The difference is that some locks are kinder than others." Ilse rose. She held out one hand, palm up, empty—a gesture of such practiced openness that it made his skin crawl. "Wren. Can you hear me, little one? I know you can understand. The old tongue runs in you like blood. Come with me and live. Stay here and the valley will spend you like coin, same as it spent every Kavanagh before you."

Wren stepped out from behind him.

Kestrel reached for her shoulder and caught air.

"No—" he said, and then stopped, because Wren hadn't moved toward Ilse at all. She had moved *between* them, squarely, feet planted on the wet stone, a child six years old standing in the seam between a thief and a warden like something set down by a careful hand.

And she spoke.

Not the broken handfuls of common speech he'd coaxed from her over the long cold weeks. Not the fragments. The whole voice came out of her, low and ringing, syllables that Kestrel's bones knew before his mind did—his mother's cadence, the mist-line oath, the language he had stolen pieces of across half a lifetime without ever once hearing it spoken whole and alive.

The tarn answered.

It began as a shiver across the black water, then a rising—not waves but *attention*, the entire surface leaning toward the girl like grass toward sun. The mist came down off the fells in ropes and coils, sliding through the granite seams, pouring around their ankles, and Kestrel felt it the way you feel a great animal lean its weight against you: vast, curious, deciding. The fire guttered and stood up straight, flames elongating toward Wren like drawn blades offering hilts-first.

Ilse Vane went very still.

In thirty years of wardenship, Ilse had stood in the presence of archmages and dying gods, and she had never once seen raw covenant-magic answer a speaker like this—unbidden, unshaped, *listening*. Her doctrine said the old tongue was dead everywhere beyond the mist. Doctrine, it turned out, had been written by people who had never heard a child say it right.

"Wren," she said carefully, "step away from—"

The mist coiled tighter. Not threatening. *Attentive.* A listening animal the size of the valley, and both of them—warden and thief—were suddenly very small inside its earshot.

Wren kept speaking. Her voice cracked on the hard consonants; she was six, and the words were older than stone, and she pushed through anyway, tears cutting clean lines down her face, saying something over and over that Kestrel realized, with a lurch, he understood perfectly:

*Not hers. Not theirs. Mine to carry. Mine to give.*

The moon overhead lurched visibly, dragging itself toward its twelfth turn, and the tarn threw its light back up at the sky like a challenge.

Ilse lowered her hand. Slowly. The way you lower a weapon you've only just realized you were holding.

"Kavanagh," she said quietly, without taking her eyes off the child, off the mist, off the impossible living language filling the hollow dark. "How far is it to the threshold?"

"A day's walk. Less, if the valley carries us."

"The valley will carry us," Ilse said. "Look at it. It's already carrying her." She swallowed, and Kestrel watched thirty years of conviction bend without breaking. "I'm coming with you. Not to take her. To witness. My order will demand proof of what happens at the thirteenth turn, and if I return without it they'll come themselves, and they won't ask the valley's permission."

Above them the moon ground onward, hungry and slow. The mist drew back from their ankles, unhurried, satisfied—as if something enormous had marked three names on a list only it could read.

Wren took Kestrel's hand. Then, after a moment, Ilse's.

They walked toward the valley's heart under a sky running out of turns.
