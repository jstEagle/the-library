## 17. The Threshold

The valley's heart was not what I expected.

I had imagined—because I had imagined it my whole life without knowing why—a black stone, a wound in the earth, something that would hurt to look at. What I found instead was a meadow so ordinary it seemed like a joke. Tall grass gone silver under the moon. A ring of standing stones no taller than my shoulder, lichened and leaning, the kind farmers stack at their field's edge when they can't be bothered to haul them away. And in the center, set into the ground like a coin dropped by a careless god, a shallow basin of bronze gone green with age.

Wren knelt beside it before I could stop her. She pressed the broken crescent into the basin's groove, and the two halves fit together the way a scar fits the memory of a cut.

The mist answered.

It came down off the fells on all sides at once, slow as breath on glass, coiling between the standing stones but never crossing them. It did not feel like the mist I'd lived under all my life—the flat gray ceiling that kept magic out. This mist *listened*. It pooled around the meadow's rim and stood there, waiting, the way a congregation stands when the door opens.

"Kess." Wren tugged my sleeve. In her mouth, my name still sounded like the old tongue, two notes falling. "Moon is almost."

She was right. Above us, through a tear in the cloud, the moon hung fat and white—and it was moving. Not drifting. *Turning*, the way a wheel turns, its light sweeping across the valley floor in a slow arc that made the grass glow and fade, glow and fade. The twelfth turn. Maren had bought me one night with her lie, Ilse had bought me an hour with her absence from our trail, and now there was nothing left to buy.

The thirteenth turn would come whether I was ready or not.

"I'm ready," I said, and discovered I was lying, and discovered I didn't care anymore.

* * *

The rite was in the stones' memory, and the stones' memory was in me. I had taken it from the drowned chapel's crypt three nights ago—I hadn't stolen it exactly; the stones had *given* it, which frightened me more—but I remembered every word of it now, in Wren's language, a language I understood fully for the first time in my life and would understand, if the rite worked, for the last time.

The covenant was simple, the way all terrible bargains are simple. Magic is a hunger, and hunger cannot be killed, only fed or starved. The First Covenant starved it here—walled the valley in mist and emptied magic out of it, so that this one place in all the world belonged to ordinary people, to bread and weather and work. To keep the wall standing, someone had to feed the seal. Not blood. Not life.

Memory.

A willing sacrifice of everything held dear, poured into the crescent until the pourer had nothing left to lose—because the seal feeds on loss, on the ache where love used to be. My mother had known this. My mother had helped break the covenant instead, because she could not pay, and the breaking had scattered the oath into fragments, and the fragments had ended up in the hoard of a thief who spent thirty years taking other people's memories because he couldn't stand the weight of his own.

There was a symmetry to it that would have made me laugh, once, if laughing were something I still knew how to do.

"Say it," Wren said. She had learned enough common speech on the road to give orders. "All of it. Don't stop. If you stop—" She didn't finish. She pressed the crescent into my hands instead, both halves joined, warm as a living thing.

I knelt at the basin. The moonlight swept over me, bright, then dim, bright, then dim. Somewhere beyond the mist, on some ridge I couldn't see, Ilse Vane was coming. I had perhaps the length of one turn left.

I began to speak.

* * *

The old tongue came out of me like water from a cracked jar. I didn't choose the words; they chose themselves, rising from wherever my mother had buried them in me, and each word I spoke opened a door inside my chest.

The crescent grew warm. Then hot. Then it began to *drink*.

The first thing it took was small: the smell of lamp oil, the particular smell of it, the one I'd traded for a thousand times and never thought about. Just—gone. A hole in the world shaped like lamp oil.

Then the smell of rain on the high fells. Then the taste of Maren's bitter tea. Then the name of the road east of the village, the one that wasn't really a road, just a habit the ground had learned.

Small things. Trivial things. The crescent was being gentle with me, or cruel in a more sophisticated way—it was showing me the shape of what was coming. Every word of the rite took something, and the takings were getting bigger, and I kept speaking because stopping was not in me anymore. Stopping would mean Wren went to Ilse's order. Stopping would mean the valley drowned in wild magic, villagers forgetting their children's faces, the whole Hollow unraveling into the same chaos drowning the outer realms.

Stopping would mean my mother broke the covenant for nothing.

So I spoke, and the crescent drank, and I felt my life begin to come apart in my hands like wet paper.

* * *

Here is what they don't tell you about giving up your memories: you feel them leave.

Not fade. *Leave.* Each one goes through you like a hook drawn slowly out of flesh, and for one terrible instant you feel its whole shape—every edge, every detail, everything you never knew you were holding—before it's gone and the place where it was closes up smooth and empty.

The merchant's wedding. Gone. I felt it go—the daughter in blue, the drunken uncle, the cake collapsing—and I felt, absurdly, a flicker of pride in the clean theft, the professional lift, and then even the pride was taken, and then the theft itself, and then the merchant's face, and then the fact that there had ever been a merchant at all. A whole thread pulled out of the weave, and the weave closed behind it without a wrinkle.

My first theft. I was nine. An old man asleep by his fire, and I'd put my palm on his forehead because I'd seen a peddler do it, and what came out was his memory of his wife singing. I sold it for two coppers and a heel of bread, and I ate the bread in an alley, and I remember thinking—

Gone. Even that. Even the alley, the coppers, the thought itself. The crescent wanted everything. Every theft, every hoard, every transaction, every face I'd lifted a life out of. Thirty years of my profession dissolving one memory at a time, and underneath the grief of losing them there was a cleaner feeling, almost like relief, that I refused to examine because examining it would cost me the examination too.

The moon turned. Bright, dim. Bright, dim. My voice was going hoarse. The words kept coming.

And then the crescent reached my mother.

* * *

I want to say I fought it. I want to tell you that when I felt it take hold of the oldest thing in me—the woman at the mist-line with the bronze crescent in her raised hand, swearing in a language I'd half-understood, her face turned back toward whatever she was leaving—I dug in my heels and howled.

I didn't. That's the truth of it. By then I had spoken too many words to stop, and the rite does not negotiate, and besides, some part of me understood that this was the payment. This was always the payment. Everything else was coins; this was the purse itself.

Her face went first.

I had not looked at my mother's face in years—not truly, not since the night I found the oath-fragment in my own hoard and understood what she'd done. But I had it, all this time. The strong jaw. The scar over her left eyebrow she never explained. The way her eyes went distant when she was deciding something final, which was often, which was always.

I felt the rite take her jaw first, then the scar, then the eyes, and I heard myself make a sound that was not a word in any language, and Wren's hand closed over mine on the crescent's edge, and I kept speaking.

Because here is the last thing I understood, kneeling in that silver meadow while the moon wheeled overhead: my mother had stood somewhere like this once, or meant to, and could not do it. Could not let her own face be taken from herself. So she broke the world's oldest bargain instead, and ran, and hid the fragments in the only vault she trusted—the head of her son, who grew up to become a thief, because a boy raised by a woman who couldn't pay her debts learns early that everything has a price and nothing stays.

I was finishing her sentence. That was all this was. Thirty years late, in a dead tongue, in a meadow full of listening mist.

Her face finished leaving. Then her voice. Then the mist-line itself, the night, the oath, the reason. Then *mother*—the word itself, scooped out of me like a stone from fruit, leaving the flesh smooth around the absence.

I did not know who I was crying for. I was crying. That was all I had.

* * *

The rite sped up after that, or I stopped being able to track it, and I honestly couldn't tell you which.

The village went. The forge-smoke and the ford and the crooked bell tower. Maren's ledger—her actual face, her voice saying *you're the only honest thief I know*, her tea, her greed, her guilt, the look on her face when she handed me the traveler's memory of being loved as a child, the one gift anyone ever gave me. Gone, gone, gone. I felt each one go and I grieved each one and the grieving was taken too, and what was left underneath was very quiet and very clear, like the bottom of a drained pool.

The fells went. The storms. The winters. The taste of stolen bread in alleys.

Ilse went—the ford, her boots dry above the water, her terrible reasonable certainty, her offer, my first lie. I was sorry to lose the lie. It had been the beginning of something.

And then the crescent reached for Wren, and I finally, finally tried to stop.

My hands locked on the bronze. My voice broke mid-word. The meadow shuddered—the mist surged against the standing stones like a tide against a seawall, and the moon's turning stuttered, and Wren grabbed my wrists with both hands, small and ferocious, and shouted at me in the old tongue, and I understood every word:

*"Don't you dare. Don't you dare keep me. If you keep me it doesn't close. Kess. KESS."*

"I can't," I said. My voice came out ruined. "I can't lose you. You're the only—" The only what? I searched for the word and realized, with a horror worse than any the rite had shown me, that I was about to lose the word along with everything else. "You're the only thing I ever took that I should have been given."

She went still. Moonlight swept across her face, bright, dim, bright. Then she leaned forward and pressed her forehead against mine, the way she had that first night in the storm at my door, barefoot and unafraid, speaking a language I somehow half-knew.

"Then take it," she whispered in common speech, clumsy and perfect. "Take it given."

And she let go of my wrists.

And I opened my hands, and I spoke the next word, and the crescent took her.

The knock at my door in the storm. Her bare feet on my floorboards. The crescent token in her fist. Her screaming dreams of the counting moon. The silence of the animals. The fells, the running, the salt wind, her voice unlocking the buried tongue in me word by word. The chapel in the tarn. The confession. *I came to find you specifically.* The forgiveness I hadn't earned. The road. The mist answering her voice. Every single moment, drawn out of me one after another after another, each one a hook pulled slow, each one closing up smooth behind it—

and I kept speaking, because the words were the last thing left that weren't hers, and the words were hers too, in her language, in her mother's language, in the language of everyone who had ever paid this price at this stone,

and the basin filled with light,

and the moon reached the top of its turn,

and the mist stood all around the meadow like a held breath,

and through it, breaking branches, crashing down the slope, torchlight swinging wild through the silver grass—

someone was coming. Someone shouting my name in a voice I suddenly, terribly, could not recognize at all.

The thirteenth turn had begun.
