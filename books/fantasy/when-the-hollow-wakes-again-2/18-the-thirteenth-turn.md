## 18. The Thirteenth Turn

Wren had counted the moon's turns since she was small enough to count on her fingers, and never once had the counting felt like this—like standing inside a bell as it is struck.

The twelfth turn was ending. The moon hung above the valley's heart, swollen and wrong-colored, a coin of tarnished silver, and beneath it the threshold opened like a wound in the meadow: a ring of pale stones half-sunk in earth that glowed faintly, breathing light in time with her own pulse. The broken crescent lay at the center of the ring, and Kestrel knelt beside it with his hands cupped around something invisible, pouring himself into it.

She could see it happening. That was the terrible part. She could see his memories leaving him the way heat leaves a body in winter—not flying out, not vanishing, but thinning. His shoulders were already narrower than they had been an hour ago. Not his body. Something behind his body. The shape of him was being subtracted, memory by memory, and each subtraction made the crescent burn a little brighter and the mist at the valley's rim pull a little tighter, like a great animal drawing in its breath.

*The thirteenth turn comes,* said the voice in her blood, the old tongue that was not a language so much as a place she had always lived. *One turn remains. The key must be whole.*

Wren stood at the edge of the stone ring because Kestrel had told her to stay there, and she had promised, and promises were the only currency the old tongue respected. But watching him kneel there alone, growing smaller and smaller in the way that mattered most, she understood something the dreams had been trying to tell her for thirteen turns of a dying moon:

One thief's memories would not be enough.

* * *

She had known it since the drowned chapel, if she was honest. She had watched him lift the covenant rite out of the flooded stones—that impossible theft, memory from rock—and she had seen the rite whole for the first time, unbroken, and one line of it had gone through her like cold water:

*Two hands broke the oath. Two hands must mend it.*

His mother had stood at the mist-line with another. Wren had carried that fragment in her chest since before she could speak; it was the reason she had walked out of the outer realms, through country that drowned in wild magic, barefoot across three rivers, following a thread of certainty older than her name. The covenant had been sealed by two. It could only be reforged by two. And Kestrel Kavanagh, kneeling alone in the ring of stones, was trying to do it with one.

He knew. She had seen him know it, down in the crypt, and choose to try anyway. That was the kind of man he was—the kind who would amputate his entire past on the chance that half a rite might hold.

It would not hold. The crescent was already telling her so. Its glow flickered between fills, guttering like a lamp low on oil, and each time it dimmed, the meadow grass nearest the stones went gray and forgot how to be grass.

Wren took a step toward the ring.

And the air at the meadow's edge tore open.

* * *

Ilse Vane came across the night grass without bending a single blade.

Wren had seen her do it at the ford—water parting around her boots without a ripple—and she had thought then that it was power. Now, watching the warden walk toward the threshold under the dying moon, she understood it was something else. Ilse Vane did not disturb the world because the world had stopped noticing her. A woman who had given up everything a person can give, walking through a valley that no longer registered her weight.

Her sword was still at her hip. Her hands were empty.

Kestrel heard her too—or felt her, the way you feel a door open in a far room. He turned where he knelt, and even from the ring's edge Wren could see what the last hour had taken from him. He looked at Ilse Vane the way a man looks at a stranger. Not wary. Blank. Whatever memory held her face—her threat at the ford, her offer, his first lie—he had already poured into the bronze.

"Ilse Vane," he said, and the name came out flat, a word from a ledger he'd never read.

"You don't know me," the warden said.

"No."

"Good." Ilse stopped at the ring's edge, an arm's length from where Wren stood. Up close, under the tarnished moon, Wren saw what she had been too afraid to see at the ford: the warden was hollowed. Not empty the way Kestrel was becoming empty—drained by choice—but worn through, like cloth rubbed thin at every fold. "That will make this easier."

"If you've come to stop the rite," Kestrel said, rising, "you're late. And I'm past caring."

"I haven't come to stop it." Ilse looked past him at the crescent, at its guttering light, and Wren watched her face do something complicated and quick—recognition, arithmetic, grief. "I've come because it's failing. You can feel it. Half a seal for a two-handed oath." Her eyes moved to Wren. "You knew. The child knew. Everyone knew but the man doing the bleeding."

"The rite takes willing sacrifice," Kestrel said. "Memory, freely given. I'm giving all of it."

"Yes." Ilse stepped over the ring's boundary stone, and the mist beyond the valley's rim shuddered—a long rolling tremor, like the flank of a sleeping thing. "And it isn't enough. Because your memories are stolen goods, Kavanagh. Every one of them. Taken from travelers, hoarded, sold. The covenant doesn't want your loot." She crouched beside the crescent, close enough that its light climbed her face, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into something Wren recognized from her own dreams: the cadence of the old tongue, spoken in accented common. "*The oath remembers its makers. It asks of each hand what that hand truly owned.*"

"And what did I own?" Kestrel's laugh was short and cracked. "Bread. Lamp oil. A locked room full of other people's lives."

"Your losses," Ilse said. "What have you lost? What has it cost you—really cost—to be the man who takes and never keeps?"

Silence. The moon ticked closer to its final turn. Wren could feel it in her teeth now, the countdown, each second a tooth falling out of some enormous mouth.

Then Kestrel said, quietly: "Everything I ever stole, I stole because someone else was done with it. Grief they wanted gone. Love they couldn't carry. I told myself I was a scavenger. Cleaning up." His hands curled at his sides. "But the truth is I never lost anything, because I never let myself have anything to lose. No friends. No family. No—" He stopped. Looked at Wren. Looked away. "No one."

"There it is," Ilse said softly. "There's the shape of it. You are about to lose her, and every moment of her, and you chose it anyway. That loss is real. That loss is yours." She straightened. "But it is one loss. One hand. The covenant broke because two people betrayed it together, and it will not mend on a single grief, however large. It needs a second kind."

"What kind?" Wren asked. Her voice came out in the old tongue and she let it.

Ilse Vane looked down at her for a long moment. And Wren saw the warden decide something—saw it happen, the way you watch a lock turn.

"The kind I brought," Ilse said.

* * *

She unbuckled her sword and laid it on the grass outside the ring. Then she pulled off her left glove, finger by finger, and Wren saw that the skin beneath was marked all over with fine silver scars, hundreds of them, like the tracks of snails written in moonlight.

"Do you know what a warden is, child?" Ilse said. "Not a soldier. Not a hunter, whatever Kavanagh thinks. A warden is a vessel. My order found me when I was younger than you, and they filled me—with oaths, with law, with the memory of every covenant ever sworn in the outer realms. That is how we hold the wild magic back from the cities. We remember the borders so the borders exist." She flexed the scarred hand. "Every year, a little more of me goes into the work. Every year, less of Ilse Vane remains. I crossed into your valley carrying the memory of the First Covenant itself—the original oath, word for word, the one your valley's founders broke. It is the last thing my order trusts me to hold."

Kestrel had gone very still. "You're offering—"

"My order sent me to take the child and remake the covenant their way, in their halls, on their terms. Sealed tight forever. Magic locked out of every realm, the Hollow included, until the end of the world." Ilse's mouth twisted. "I watched the mist move tonight. I watched your valley bleed forgetting into itself—names, roads, faces. And I read the rite in the boy's face when he began, and I understood that my order's way and this rite are not the same covenant at all. Theirs is a wall. This—" she gestured at the breathing stones, the tender gray grass, the girl standing witness— "this is a door. Held shut gently, by people who remember why."

"The outer realms are drowning," Kestrel said. "You told me. Wild magic flooding everything."

"They are. And a wall would stop it. A wall would also bury this valley alive, and every valley like it, and call it peace." Ilse knelt at the crescent's far side, opposite Kestrel, so that the broken token sat between them like a bridge with a gap in the middle. "I am going to disobey my order. Do you understand what that costs a warden? Everything I am is built on obedience. If I pour the First Covenant into that token—if I give up the last true thing my makers trusted me to hold—I will be cast out of the work. Unmade as a warden. And what's left of me afterward—" she smiled, and it was the first time Wren had seen her smile, and it was terrible and human— "what's left will be a woman named Ilse Vane, with a handful of years and a great many regrets, in a valley she helped hunt through. That is my loss. Freely given. Willingly paid."

The moon shuddered at the edge of its thirteenth turn.

"*Two hands,*" Wren said, in the old tongue, and both of them turned to look at her. "*Two hands must mend it.*"

"She's right," Kestrel said roughly. "Vane—why? Why throw away everything for a valley that isn't yours?"

Ilse looked at him for a long time.

"Because three days ago I offered to buy a child from you," she said, "and you lied to my face to protect her. You—a thief, a coward by your own account, a man who survives by never being noticed—you made yourself visible for her sake. I have spent forty years being nothing but useful, and I have never once done anything that wasn't ordered." She set her scarred hand against the crescent's cold curve. "I would like to know what it feels like. Before there's no me left to feel it."

The moon turned.

* * *

Wren felt it happen from outside herself, the way you feel weather.

Both hands touched the bronze. Kestrel's from one side, pouring out the last of himself—she watched his mother's face go, watched his childhood go, watched the storm and the door and the knock go, watched the moment he first heard her speak the dead tongue drain out of his eyes even as he looked at her. Ilse's from the other side, and her giving was different—slower, deeper, a well emptying rather than a purse spilling. Silver light ran out of the warden's scarred hand in threads, and each thread was a law, an oath, a border remembered, and the crescent drank them all and blazed.

The gap in the token closed.

Wren heard it—a sound like a bell the size of the sky, struck once, softly. The mist at the valley's rim stopped pulling inward and sighed outward instead, unraveling into ordinary fog, into morning. The gray grass greened from the stones outward in a slow ripple, remembering itself. And through the meadow, gentle as breath, magic woke—Wren felt it the way she had felt it everywhere else in the world, everywhere but here: a hum in the ground, a warmth in the air, the old tongue suddenly not dead at all but merely sleeping, turning over, opening one eye.

The covenant held. Two kinds of loss, braided into one seal.

Kestrel swayed on his knees. He looked at the crescent, then at his own empty hands, then at Wren, and there was nothing in his face—no recognition, no fear, only a vast and gentle blankness, a page washed clean.

"I know you," he said slowly. "Don't I?"

And Ilse Vane folded forward onto the grass like a coat slipping off a hook, breathing, alive, and utterly, peacefully empty—and the moon finished its thirteenth turn, and the valley, waking, began to remember everything except the two people who had saved it—

—and Wren knelt in the new grass between them, the only soul in the Hollow who still knew their names, and understood that the rest of the story was hers to tell.
