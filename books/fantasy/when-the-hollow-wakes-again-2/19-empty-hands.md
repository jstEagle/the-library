## 19. Empty Hands

Grass first.

That is what there is. Grass under his hands, bent and sweet-smelling, wet at the tips where dawn has not yet burned the dew away. He knows the word *dawn*. He knows the word *grass*. He knows his eyes open and light comes in, gold and slanted, and somewhere a lark is doing something reckless with its throat.

He does not know his name.

He lies still and takes inventory, because it seems like the thing to do—the way you'd count coins before spending them. He knows what bread smells like. He knows that rivers run downhill. He knows that when a door knocks at night, you answer carefully or not at all—though he cannot say where he learned that, only that the knowing sits in him like a stone worn smooth by water that has long since gone.

His name.

He reaches for it the way you reach for a rung in the dark and finds air. There should be something there. A shape. A weight. Instead there is a clean white absence, like snow on a field, like a page before ink.

*Empty,* he thinks, and the word does not frighten him as much as it should. That frightens him a little more.

He sits up. The meadow runs to the tree line on three sides; on the fourth it climbs toward a ridge where the morning mist is lifting off the slopes in slow ropes. The mist moves strangely—not drifting so much as *breathing*, drawing in and out along the valley's rim like the flank of some enormous sleeping animal. And threaded through everything, under everything, is a hum. Not sound exactly. More like the feeling of standing near a struck bell after the ringing has passed below hearing. It comes up through the grass, through the soles of him, through the bones of his hands pressed flat against the earth.

The grass hums back.

He watches, without surprise he can name, as the blades nearest his left hand lean toward him. Not wind. They simply turn, gently, the way faces turn toward a voice.

"Well," he says aloud. His voice is rough with disuse or with something else. "Hello."

The grass leans closer. The hum deepens, warm as banked coals. Somewhere above, the lark falters mid-note, as if listening too.

Magic. He knows that word too. He knows it the way a man who has never seen fire knows the word for burning—which is to say he knows it, and knows also, with a certainty that lives deeper than memory, that it was never here before. That this valley was a place where magic could not follow. That the mist at the rim was not weather but a wall. That something sealed it out.

That something has changed.

He looks down at his own hands. They are a working man's hands, nicked and calloused, nails cut short, a pale scar across the right thumb like a healed-over question. He turns them over. They are empty. He has the sudden, unaccountable conviction that they were never meant to be anything else—and just as suddenly, grief, sharp and total, for something he cannot find.

It passes through him like a cold current and is gone, leaving the meadow golden again and the hum steady beneath it.

He gets up. His legs work. His boots are scuffed but whole. In his pocket he finds nothing but a single seed, dark and crescent-shaped, which he does not remember putting there and cannot bring himself to drop.

* * *

He walks.

Not toward anything—he has no toward—but away from nothing, which amounts to the same thing, and the valley receives him the way a cupped hand receives rain. The path finds itself under his feet. When he comes to a stream he steps across stones that seem to have been arranged overnight for his convenience, and the water, instead of rushing past, slows where he walks, gathering itself into small bright lenses that hold his reflection a moment longer than water should.

He stops once and crouches and looks at the man in the stream. Thirty-some years old, maybe. Dark hair going early grey at the temples, though he couldn't say how he knows that's early. A face built for being overlooked: nothing cruel in it, nothing remarkable, the kind of face that makes people relax their voices around you. He touches his cheek and the reflection touches its cheek and neither of them knows the other's name either.

"Who were you?" he asks the water.

The water doesn't answer. But downstream, a fish rises, takes an insect, and rings the surface like a small struck bell, and the hum in the ground swells faintly, almost kindly, almost amused—as if the valley heard the question and found it charming, or sad, or both.

He walks on. Near midmorning he crests a low rise and sees the village below: slate roofs, chimney smoke standing straight in the still air, a bell tower with its bell hanging silent. People move in the lanes. Small figures. Ordinary. His chest tightens with something that wants very badly to be recognition and isn't.

He knows villages. He knows what markets smell like—onions, tallow, wet wool. He knows that you keep your hood up in crowds and your eyes down and your hands quick. He knows these things without knowing why, the way a riverbed knows the shape of water that hasn't flowed in years.

There is a hole where his life should be, and around the hole, the edges of all the things that used to fill it, sharp as broken crockery.

He goes down anyway.

* * *

The village is called Ferrowmere. He learns this from a woman selling apples off a cart, who tells him cheerfully enough until she looks closely at his face and her smile falters, the way a candle gutters in a draft.

"Do I know you?" she asks.

"I don't think anyone does," he says, and means it more literally than she can guess.

She gives him an apple anyway. "For the road," she says, though he's clearly not going anywhere, and then she calls to her boy—six, seven, gap-toothed—to mind the cart, and hurries him off down the lane, glancing back twice.

He eats the apple sitting on the churchyard wall. It is the best apple he can remember eating, which is a low bar and also somehow a high one, since he can't remember eating any apple at all. The sweetness is enormous. The sweetness is nearly unbearable. He wonders if that is what it is like for everyone, every day, or if things taste louder when there is nothing behind them. No other apples. No orchard of remembered apples. Just this one, ringing like a bell.

An old man comes out of the churchyard gate, pauses, squints at him.

"You'll be one of the ones that came back," the old man says. Not a question.

"Back from where?"

The old man considers this. He has a face like a dried riverbed and eyes that are still full of water. "That's the trouble, isn't it," he says. "Nobody remembers where. My wife forgot my name for most of a season. Came back to her last week, all at once, over breakfast. Said it like she'd been holding it in her mouth the whole time." He taps his temple. "Things come back here now. Didn't used to."

"Didn't used to?"

"Mm." The old man rocks on his heels, looking out at the hills, at the breathing mist. "Valley's changed. Everyone feels it. Mist opened up in the spring—just rolled back, quiet as you like, and the first travelers in forty years came walking down the high road, and half of us had forgotten there ever was a high road. And now—" he wiggles his fingers vaguely— "*this.* Things growing that shouldn't. Well. Shouldn't have. Lights in the tarn at night. Old words coming back to folk who never learned them." He shrugs. "World waking up, I suppose. Or us."

"And the ones who don't remember?" Kestrel asks. His own voice sounds strange saying it. "The ones who came back with nothing?"

The old man looks at him for a long moment, and there is something in his face—not pity exactly, but its cousin, the kind that keeps its distance out of respect.

"There's a girl," he says. "Down at the Hollow House. Been asking after folk who woke empty. Says she knows some of them." He hesitates. "Says she knew you, I'd wager, from how she asked."

The hum under the earth seems to lean in.

"What's her name?"

"Wren," the old man says, and the word goes through him like a fishhook finding flesh it was tied to all along.

* * *

Wren.

He says it once, silently, testing the weight of it, and it fits his mouth the way a key fits a lock it was cut for. Wren. A small brown bird. A girl's name. His heart is doing something unreasonable about it, hammering at a door, and he stands in the churchyard lane with apple juice sticky on his fingers and understands that this stranger's name is the closest thing he owns to a memory.

Because it isn't a memory. That's what unsettles him. When he reaches for his mother—nothing, not even a hole, just smooth blankness, like a wall painted over so many times the original plaster is gone. When he reaches for his childhood—a blank. His trade—blank, whatever it was, though his hands know locks and ledgers and the weight of other people's secrets the way a knife knows its sheath. All of it gone, and worse than gone: *unmournable*, because grief needs a face and his griefs have none.

But Wren is not blank. Wren is a presence. Wren is a shape of absence, the way a footprint is the shape of a foot. He does not remember her and yet he knows, with a certainty that hums in the same register as the grass, that she was the center of something. That everything else in him used to arrange itself around her the way iron filings arrange around a lodestone, and now the lodestone is lifted away and the filings lie scattered and meaningless, and still they remember, in their tiny metal hearts, the direction they used to point.

A word on the tip of his tongue. That's what she is. He can feel the whole sentence straining behind her name, and the sentence is his life.

He sets off for the Hollow House before he decides to. His feet apparently have opinions his head can't share.

* * *

The Hollow House sits at the end of a lane where the cobbles give way to packed earth, an old stone place with ivy eating one gable and a garden gone gloriously wild—flowers blooming out of season, in colors he is fairly sure flowers don't come in, humming faintly when the breeze combs through them. Magic in the garden. Magic in the walls, a little, seeping gentle as damp.

The door is blue. He knows—does not remember, *knows*—that the door used to be grey, that someone painted it recently, that the paint is still tacky at the edges if you press it. He presses it. Tacky.

His hand is shaking.

Inside, a voice is speaking. Low, halting, patient—the cadence of someone telling a story to someone who needs it told slowly. He stands in the doorway like a fool with dew still on his boots and listens.

"—and the moon counted down," the voice says, "thirteen times, and on the thirteenth turn two people gave up everything they had, and the covenant took the gift and held it, and the valley woke up. That part's true. I saw it."

Another voice, younger, skeptical: "Saw it how? You'd have been—"

"I was there." A pause. "Mostly. Some of it I've told so many times it's worn smooth, like a stair. But the shape of it's true. And the people who were emptied—they're not broken. Their stories aren't gone. They're just... kept somewhere. And kept things can be given back. That's the whole point of keeping."

He knocks. Of course he knocks. Some deep-buried etiquette surfaces in him—*you knock carefully at night, or not at all*—and then corrects itself, because it is day, and this door is meant to be knocked on. Painted blue on purpose. Waiting.

The talking stops. Footsteps. The door opens.

She is smaller than the hook in his chest said she would be, and younger—twelve, maybe thirteen—all elbows and solemn dark eyes and hair that has clearly lost several arguments with the wind. She looks up at him and her breath catches and her hand goes to something at her throat, a cord, and her eyes go bright and furious and glad all at once, all in the space of a heartbeat, and he watches her decide—visibly decide—not to throw herself at him, which is the strangest act of restraint he has ever witnessed, because every part of her is leaning toward him like the grass did.

"You came back," she says. Her voice cracks in the middle of it.

"I—" His voice fails. He tries again. "I don't know you."

"I know." She says it steadily, the way you'd say *the sun rose*, a fact already survived. "I know you don't. That's all right. That was always part of it."

"Part of what?"

She studies his face, cataloguing, comparing it against some version she carries. Whatever she finds seems to satisfy her and break her heart in equal measure.

"You're the thief," she says softly. "You gave everything away. Everything you stole, everything you kept, everything you loved. You poured it all into the crescent so the valley could live." Her hand closes around the thing at her throat, and for a second, in the morning light, he glimpses bronze—a broken crescent, mended with a seam of new metal, shining like it remembers being whole. "And it worked. You saved everyone. And nobody knows. Nobody *can* know. Except me."

"Why except you?"

Something moves behind her eyes—grief, huge and disciplined, held the way you hold a door shut against a storm.

"Because I paid my own price," she says, "to be the one who remembers. And because—" her voice wavers, steadies, "—because you taught me that kept things can be given back. So I'm going to give yours back. Piece by piece. However long it takes. Starting—" she takes a breath, squares her small shoulders like someone taking up a tool she intends to use for the rest of her life, "—starting with a storm, and a night, and a knock at a grey door."

The hum in the earth rises, gentle, expectant, like a held breath.

He does not know this child. He does not know this house, this village, this valley, this nameless man he apparently was. His hands hang at his sides, empty as the day he learned to use them.

But when she steps back to let him in, he crosses the threshold as if he had crossed it a thousand times before—and perhaps, somewhere in whatever keeps what was given, he has.

"Tell me," he says, and sits down in the lamplight, an empty man learning to be filled. "Start at the beginning."

"It starts," Wren says, closing the blue door behind them both, "with rain."
