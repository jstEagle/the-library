## 1. Charms for the Fearful

The trouble with selling magic you didn't believe in, Oscar Delacroix had learned, was that customers could always tell. Not the magic part—they never questioned that. It was the belief they sniffed at, the way dogs sniffed at a letter that had passed through the wrong hands.

So she believed in her luck-knots. Fiercely, theatrically, with the whole of her heart arranged on her sleeve like a market-stall banner.

"Three loops for fortune," she told the cooper's wife, holding up a knot of braided river-grass. "Four if your husband's been drinking. The fourth loop unties itself when he lies."

The woman laughed and paid for two.

That was the trick of it, the small honest dishonesty of her trade: the knots did nothing. The weather-whispers—little paper spirals you breathed on and hung from the eaves—did nothing. They were theater, comfort, cheap as a prayer and twice as pretty. And because they were nothing, nobody ever looked too closely at the woman who sold them. A charm-seller was a fixture, like a lamplighter or a rat-catcher. Harmless. Invisible, if she did it right.

Oscar had been doing it right for eleven years.

She'd been doing it in Vellamark for eight months, which was seven months longer than her usual stay, and that worried her more than she let herself think about. But the market here was good—generous, distracted, full of people who wanted very much to believe that luck could be bought for a copper—and the rent on the crooked little room above the chandler's was cheap because the roof leaked. Oscar liked the leak. It meant nobody else wanted the room.

It also meant pigeons, and where there were pigeons there were crows, and that was the one part of her life she couldn't call harmless or invisible no matter how carefully she folded it away.

* * *

Every morning before dawn, before the shuttered stalls and the fish-carts and the first smoke curling from the bakery, Oscar climbed through her window onto the chandler's roof with a paper twist of scraps in her pocket. Bacon rinds, gristle, the heels of loaves she'd traded for but never eaten—she didn't eat bread anymore, not since a magpie in Corleigh had explained what bakeries did to their day-old stock, and she'd spent three years pretending she hadn't heard.

She whistled. Low, two notes down, one note up—a sound that was almost a word if you had the ears for it, which nobody in Vellamark did, thank every patient saint.

They came out of the grey sky like torn pieces of it. Six crows this season, though only four were regulars: Bramble, who had a limp and an opinion about everything; the twins, who she suspected were actually one crow with a gift for being in two places; old Sorrel, missing an eye and rich in dignity; and the young one she'd never named, because naming things made them yours, and you could not afford to have things be yours when you might need to leave them.

"Good morning, liars," she said, and scattered the scraps.

To anyone watching—and she checked; she always checked—the woman from the chandler's roof was feeding birds, the way lonely widows did. Eccentric. Pityable. Safe.

But the crows talked back, and she talked to them, and the conversation went both ways down a road that had no business existing in a city where magic had been outlawed since before anyone's grandmother was born.

*The tall one with the red sash,* Bramble rasped, hopping close, head cocked. *He kicks dogs.*

"I know."

*He kicked the dog by the fountain. I took his hat.*

"Bramble."

*I dropped it in the tanner's pond.* A pause, feathers ruffling in something like satisfaction. *He cried. Men with red sashes cry very loudly.*

"That's exactly the kind of thing we don't do anymore," Oscar said, meaning we do constantly and I am a terrible influence and I love you. "Small town. Small mistakes. We keep our heads down until spring, and then I go somewhere the winters are softer, and that's that."

Bramble looked at her with one round black eye, and Oscar felt the eye's opinion settle over her like soot. Crows held grudges, everyone knew that. What fewer people knew was that they held promises too, and hers—to come back, to keep coming back—was going sour between them like milk left out.

"I know," she said quietly. "I know."

Sorrel sidled up and pressed his good eye toward her, which among his people was either an apology or an accusation, and she chose to take it as neither, just company, which was the rarest currency she knew.

* * *

Midmorning she worked her stall in Tallow Row, between a pickle-wife whose cucumbers could strip varnish and a man who sold knives sharp enough to make grown butchers weep. Maren found her there just past noon, as she always did, materializing out of the crowd in layers of shawls like a hedge that had learned to walk.

Maren Osgood was seventy if she was a day, kept goats, delivered babies, and knew more about the wild edges of things than any ten scholars of the Sundering. She was also, in the careful grammar of people like them, *safe*. You could count safe people on one hand and still pick things up.

"You're still here," Maren said, by way of greeting.

"I'm still here."

"Eight months."

"Nine next week." Oscar handed her a weather-whisper without being asked. "For the goat shed. Your leaks are worse than mine."

"My leaks are character." Maren turned the paper spiral in her brown fingers, then fixed Oscar with a look that had probably cured fevers all on its own. "There's talk at the assizes. New man coming from the capital. An Inquisitor."

Oscar kept her hands moving, sorting knots. "Inquisitors are for heretics and coin-clippers."

"This one's for the gifted. Census rolls, they're saying. Names written down." Maren said it lightly, the way you'd mention rain coming, but her eyes didn't match her voice. "Registry of afflicted persons. And after names, girl, come doors knocked on in the night."

"They've done sweeps before. Remember the Purge under old King Aldric? Nothing came of it."

"Aldric died before it started, and you weren't born." Maren leaned across the stall, lowering her voice into the space between pickle smells. "You've got a gift they can't tax and can't see, and you sit in the biggest city in the north talking to *birds* on rooftops—"

"Not talking. Communicating nonverbally. There's a difference. Legally."

"—and you think because you've been clever for eleven years, you'll be clever forever." Maren straightened. "Clever runs out. I've buried cleverer women than you."

Oscar smiled the smile she used on customers who haggled. "If I run every time a rumor blows through, I'll spend my whole life running, and then what was the point of being careful?"

"The point of being careful," Maren said, taking her weather-whisper and her leave, "is having somewhere left to run *from*. Think on it, sparrow."

Sparrow. She'd called Oscar that for nine months, some joke about small brown birds surviving hawks, and Oscar had laughed the first six times.

* * *

The sparrow itself happened on Thursday.

Market days blurred together, but Thursdays were quiet, and quiet was when trouble kept its appointments. Oscar was packing up early, knots coiled, awning half-down, when she heard it: a small wet sound of struggling near the gutters, and there, in the churned mud beside the pickle-cart, a fledgling sparrow drowning in the runoff channel. Wing bent wrong. Eyes already glazing, that particular terrible shine of a thing deciding to stop.

And here is what must be understood about Oscar Delacroix's gift, the true one, the one beneath the knots: it wasn't spells. There were no words, no gestures, no glow. It was simply that animals listened to her, and she to them, and between a listening heart and a listening body there is very little distance. Pain could be argued with. Fear could be gentled. A body on the edge could sometimes, if it wanted to live, be talked back up onto the ledge.

She had done it a thousand times in cellars and ditches and dark, alone.

She knelt in the mud in full daylight on Tallow Row, cupped the sparrow in both hands, and *spoke*—not aloud, nothing anyone could hear, but with everything else she had. *Easy. Easy, small one. The wing is only bent, not broken. Bend with me now. Yes. Again. Again—*

The sparrow shuddered. Its eyes came back from wherever dying things go, focused, and it sat in her palms trembling and alive, and its heart hammered against her thumbs like a fist knocking on a door saying *thank you, thank you, thank you*—

"Girl."

Oscar's stomach dropped through the cobbles, through the clay, through the bones of Vellamark itself.

An old woman stood over her. Not Maren. Someone she didn't know, wrapped in a coat too heavy for the season, with a face like a dried apple and eyes that were not confused, not curious, not any of the safe expressions. They were the eyes of someone recognizing a thing she had thought extinct.

"You healed it," the old woman said. Not a question.

"It fell in the gutter. I just—" Oscar's mouth, well-practiced, began assembling the lie: startled it back to life, warm hands, birds are hardy creatures, my grandmother swore by—

The old woman reached into her coat.

Everything in Oscar screamed—run, burn the stall, nine months gone to ash in nine seconds—but the old woman only drew out a crust of bread, broke it, and set it gently on the cobblestones by the fledgling, which hopped to it and began, absurdly, miraculously, to eat.

"Forty years ago there was a woman in Harrowgate who could do what you just did," the old woman said softly. "My mother. They came for her in autumn." She looked up, and her apple-cheeks might once have been capable of smiling; they managed something now, thin and terrible and kind. "You kneel too long in one place, girl, someone builds a church on you."

Then she shuffled off into the crowd, leaving Oscar in the mud with a living bird, a dead lie, and the sudden vertiginous knowledge that invisibility, once cracked, does not crack neatly.

It cracks the way ice does—all at once, with you standing on it.

* * *

That night Oscar lay in her crooked room above the chandler's shop, listening to the roof leak, doing arithmetic. Nine months of goodwill against one pair of knowing eyes. Rumors traveled at the speed of gossip; Inquisitors traveled slower but arrived anyway, like winter.

Through the window she heard them settle around her: six soft landings on the shingles, six sets of claws, and then Bramble's voice, muffled and familiar:

*You smell of fear. Why do you smell of fear?*

"Just an old friend of the trade," Oscar said to the ceiling. "Go to sleep."

*We don't sleep while you smell of fear,* said Sorrel, from nearer the window, and the young one made a low sound of agreement, and Oscar pressed her palm flat against the cold glass as if she could hold them there through it, all of it, the whole fragile life she'd built out of knots and silence.

Two streets away, in the direction of the assizes, a herald was nailing a fresh notice to the courthouse door, and though Oscar could not have known it, the notice bore a seal she'd never seen before, black iron on white vellum, and beneath the seal, words that would reach Tallow Row by morning:

*By order of the crown, a census shall be taken of all persons afflicted with unlawful gifts.*

The crows stayed on the roof all night. None of them slept.
