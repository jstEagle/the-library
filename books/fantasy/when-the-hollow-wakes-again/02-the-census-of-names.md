## 2. The Census of Names

The proclamation went up on the market square's oldest post on a Tuesday, which Oscar always thought was the crown's way of insulting people twice—once with the content and once with the timing. Nobody read proclamations on Tuesdays. Tuesdays were for haggling over turnips and pretending you hadn't noticed the fishmonger's prices had crept up again.

She was selling luck-knots when the crier climbed onto his box, unrolled his parchment with a flourish meant for coronations, and began to read.

"By decree of His Majesty Aldric the Third, let it be known that a great census shall be taken of all persons afflicted—"

The word *afflicted* landed in the square like a dropped plate. Conversations didn't stop so much as flinch.

"—afflicted with the taint of wild-speech and other unlawful gifts. All such persons must present themselves at the Hall of Records within forty days to be entered upon the Registry of Afflicted Persons, there to be assessed, pardoned where warranted, and bound to lawful employment under crown supervision."

Oscar kept her hands moving on the knot she was tying. Over-under, pull, tuck. Her fingers knew the pattern the way her tongue once had known the speech of sparrows, and both knew better than to draw attention.

"Assessed," said the woman next to her stall, a broad-shouldered dyer named Petra who smelled permanently of madder root. "That's a kind word for it."

"Pardoned *where warranted*," said the baker's son from across the way. "Warranted by whom?"

The crier pressed on, voice bright as brass. "Any person harboring or concealing an afflicted individual shall be subject to fine, forfeiture, and—" here he paused, consulting the parchment with the air of a man hoping he'd misread it, "—denouncement being both permitted and encouraged."

*Encouraged.* Oscar tied off the knot. Somewhere behind her ribs, something cold settled in and made itself comfortable, like a cat claiming a chair.

Petra leaned over and bought three luck-knots she did not need. "You want to be careful," she murmured, not looking at Oscar. "People are already talking. Old Hobb was seen letting a fox lick salt off his hand last month, and now everyone remembers it."

"Everyone remembered it before," Oscar said. "They just hadn't thought of it as evidence yet."

"Aye." Petra tucked the knots away. "That's the trouble, isn't it. The crown's not giving people anything new to fear. It's giving them a new name for what they were already afraid of."

The crier finished, rolled up his parchment, and stepped down from his box into a silence that followed him like a debt. By evening, someone had nailed a smaller paper beneath the proclamation: *I saw what you did, Mistress Ellery. The cat knows your name.* No one claimed it. No one took it down either.

* * *

Inquisitor Serane Halveth arrived on the ninth day, and Vellamark learned what cold efficiency looked like when it wore good boots.

Oscar watched her from the roof of the tannery, where she'd gone with a pocketful of scraps to feed the crows—a habit too ingrained to break and too dangerous to continue, which was, she reflected, the story of most habits worth having. Below, Halveth walked the length of the registry queue outside the Hall of Records with a ledger under one arm and an expression that suggested the ledger had personally disappointed her.

There were eleven people in the queue. Eleven, out of a city of thirty thousand, and every one of them looked like they wished the ground would open and offer a more discreet alternative. A boy of maybe fifteen, hands shaking. A laundress with her eyes fixed firmly on her shoes. Old Hobb, gods help him, standing straight as a fence post because pride was the only thing he had left to hold.

Halveth stopped in front of each one in turn. She did not raise her voice—Oscar could see that much even from the roof. She asked questions, wrote answers, moved on. The whole thing had the brisk, bloodless rhythm of a woman counting spoons.

"She'll not fill that book from a queue," said a voice behind Oscar, and she nearly lost her footing on the shingles.

Maren stood on the roof access ladder, half in and half out of the hatch, holding two cups of something steaming and smelling of juniper and bad decisions.

"You're going to get us both killed climbing around on roofs," Oscar said.

"I'm going to get us both killed doing nothing, which is less exercise." Maren handed her a cup and lowered herself onto the warm tiles with a groan that suggested her knees held opinions about the whole enterprise. She was seventy if she was a day, built like a barrel of nails, and had been practicing hedge-craft openly enough to be careless and carefully enough to survive, which Oscar considered the finest balancing act in Vellamark. "Look at her. That's not a hunt, girl. Hunts have passion in them. That's an *inventory*."

Below, Halveth had reached old Hobb. She spoke to him for longer than the others. Then she wrote in her ledger, and Hobb's shoulders came down like a puppet with a cut string—not broken, just... filed. Assessed. Bound to lawful employment. He shuffled off toward whatever labor the crown had decided suited a man who'd once been kind to a fox.

"That's the part that turns my stomach," Oscar said quietly. "It's not the fear. Fear I understand. It's that she makes it *bureaucratic*. Like they're not people, just entries."

"Mm. And how does a book get full, do you think? Eleven volunteers?" Maren sipped her drink. "A book like that fills itself, given time. People love to write in other people's names. It's the only authorship most folk ever get."

Oscar looked down at the ledger, small and dark in Halveth's hands, and felt the cold thing behind her ribs shift its weight.

* * *

It took six days to prove Maren right.

The first denunciation came from the cooper's wife, who reported that the miller's daughter had "been seen conversing with geese in an unnatural manner." The geese, Oscar happened to know, had simply been rude, but no one consulted them. The miller's daughter went into the queue.

Then the tanner reported the lamplighter. Then two brothers denounced each other simultaneously, each apparently hoping to preempt the other, and were entered together in an entry that Oscar heard about secondhand and treasured anyway: Halveth had reportedly looked at them both for a long moment and written only, *See: spite.*

But it was the small kindnesses that broke the city's heart fastest, because small kindnesses were everywhere once you started looking for them. The widow who left bread crusts for pigeons. The carter who never whipped his mule and was heard, once, humming to it. The child who'd buried a dead robin under a flat stone with a flower on top, and whose mother wept at the gate of the Hall of Records while officials explained that the family would be "monitored."

"They're not hunting sorcerers," Maren said one night, in her cluttered kitchen that smelled of drying herbs and candle smoke. She was grinding something that smelled worse than it sounded, which was saying something. "Sorcerers are rare. Kindness is common. You can fill any ledger you like if you criminalize kindness, and best of all, people do the work for you. Every neighbor becomes a little Inquisitor with a memory and a grudge."

"So I run," Oscar said. She turned her cup in her hands. "Is that what you're building up to? You've been circling it for days like a dog around a bone."

Maren set down the pestle. "I'm an old woman, Oscar Delacroix. I've buried a husband, two children, and every friend I started out with, and I will tell you plainly, because plainness is the one luxury of age: run."

"I can't just—"

"You can. You've done it before. You've got your trinkets, your charms, your clever little harmless face. There's nothing keeping you."

"There's everything keeping me." Oscar heard her own voice come out sharper than intended. She softened it. "My stall. My room. My *life*, Maren. I've built something here. Three years in one place—I haven't managed that since I was twelve. If I run every time the wind shifts, then what was any of it for? And besides—" she gestured vaguely at the window, beyond which the city lay under its usual haze of chimney smoke and river fog, "—I've done nothing. I sell knots. I whisper at weather the way anyone might talk to a cloud. The registry is for the *afflicted*. I'm not afflicted. I'm careful."

Maren looked at her for a long moment, and there was something in the look that Oscar would remember later, in the dark, in the running, with a terrible clarity: pity without surprise. The look of a woman watching someone step confidently onto ice and admiring their confidence.

"Careful," Maren repeated. "Child, I once knew a man who was careful. Kept his gift folded up so small you couldn't find it with a lantern. Lived forty years in Corren's Ford without a whisper. And then one day a magistrate's horse threw a shoe in the rain, and the man knelt down and told the farrier what was wrong with the hoof—and the farrier told his brother, and his brother told the tax collector, and inside a month the man was on a cart. Forty years, undone by a lame horse on a wet Tuesday." She resumed grinding, harder than the herbs deserved. "Being careful doesn't save you. It only decides *when*."

"And running saves me?"

"Running keeps you alive long enough to find out."

Oscar set down her cup. "Forty days," she said. "The registry closes in thirty-one. After that, the book closes with it, and the Inquisitor moves on to some other city with her ledger and her boots. Storms pass, Maren. I've outlasted four magistrates and a plague scare. I can outlast one woman with a book."

"One woman with a book," Maren said, "who has already filled nine pages."

"How do you—"

"Because I'm old, and old women hear things, and one of the things I hear is that Serane Halveth pays for information. Good coin, too. Not threats—coin. Do you know what that means?"

Oscar did, and hated knowing. "That she doesn't need to threaten anyone. They'll line up to sell each other."

"She's not filling that ledger with the guilty," Maren said. "She's filling it with the *named*. And in a city this size, given enough coin and enough time, everyone gets named eventually. Even careful girls who sell luck-knots and feed crows on rooftops."

The fire popped. Outside, somewhere in the middle distance, a dog barked twice and stopped, and Oscar found herself listening to it the way she listened to everything now—with one ear cocked toward meanings she wasn't supposed to hear. *Stranger,* the bark said. *Stranger, stranger, mine.* Even the dogs were nervous.

"I'll think about it," she said finally, which was what people said when they had decided not to decide, and Maren nodded, which was what wise people did when they recognized the phrase for what it was.

* * *

On the seventeenth day, the crows stopped coming.

Oscar noticed it the way you notice a clock stopping—not at once, but as an absence that accumulates. She sat on the tannery roof with her pocketful of scraps, and the roofline stayed empty. The next day, same. On the third day she found a single feather caught in the gutter, black shot through with oil-green, and something in her chest went tight and cold.

Crows did not abandon a feeding ground. Crows abandoned a *trap*.

She went to Maren's that night, walking fast through streets that seemed to watch her, past doors that used to nod open and now stayed shut, past the wall where someone had chalked a fresh list of names under the words *THEY LIVE AMONG US*—and stopped dead in the doorway of the hedge-witch's kitchen, because Maren was packing.

Not tidying. Packing. Bundles of dried herbs wrapped in oilcloth, candles bundled by the dozen, a leather bag already bulging, and on the table, laid out with terrible care, a map.

"What's this?" Oscar asked, though she knew.

"This," Maren said, "is me leaving tonight, and this—" she tapped the map, "—is the only reason I'm telling you where. The hollow country, north past the border stones. Old roads nobody keeps, old places nobody wants. The crown's maps end at the treeline because the surveyors refused to go further, and I don't blame them." She pushed it across the table. "Take it. Take the east road out, walk all night, and don't stop until the trees close over your head."

"Maren—"

"Don't *Maren* me." The old woman's voice cracked like a whip, and then, just as fast, gentled. "Girl. Listen to me. Today a fishwife sold the Inquisitor the name of the woman who sells luck-knots near the fountain stall. Described her to the last freckle. Said she talks to birds." Maren's eyes were bright and fierce and frightened. "They'll come for you tomorrow, maybe the day after. There won't be a queue for you. There'll be iron charms and a closed door and questions you can't answer safely. So you can stand there believing rules will protect you, believing storms pass, believing in your careful little life—or you can take this map and *go*."

Oscar looked at the map. She looked at her friend. And the cold thing behind her ribs—the one that had settled in on proclamation day—finally spoke in her own voice:

*You should have left on the first Tuesday.*

"The crows," she said hoarsely. "That's why the crows stopped coming. Someone's already caught them, hasn't she. Someone's already got them in iron."

Maren didn't answer. She didn't need to. Outside, far off toward the Hall of Records, a bell rang once—the census bell, marking another name entered—and Oscar stood in the warm cluttered kitchen of the only home she'd dared make in ten years, holding a map of the wild places, and understood at last that the storm was not passing over her.

It had been circling. And it had found her name.
