## 3. What the Crows Remember

The trouble with teaching crows your name is that crows never forget anything.

Oscar had known this for years, the way you know a stove is hot — abstractly, academically, without ever expecting to be burned by it. She'd fed the rooftop flock of Vellamark's Tanner's Row for six winters now: crusts in autumn, suet through the freeze, and in spring the soft-boiled eggs that made old Crookbeak do his tumbling flight, the one that looked less like flying and more like a feathered man falling downstairs with tremendous confidence.

She'd been careful. She'd never spoken to them where anyone could see, and she'd never used her true voice — the deep-down one, the one that wasn't words at all but *meaning*, poured straight from her chest into theirs. She'd only ever used the flat human tongue, the way a baker talks to his ovens.

But six winters is six winters. And a crow who has been loved for six winters knows things about the person feeding it that the person does not know about herself. They knew her step on the stair. They knew the particular shape of her silence when the census criers came through. And they knew her name — not "Ossy, the charm-seller," but the name she kept folded away like a letter too dangerous to open.

*Delacroix,* they said among themselves, in their rusted-hinge voices. *Delacroix feeds us. Delacroix listens.*

They said it because it was true. They said it because crows keep accounts the way merchants keep ledgers, every kindness entered and carried forward. And they said it on a gray Tuesday morning, from the back of an Inquisitor's wagon, in front of forty witnesses, because iron charms were clamped around their legs and something cold was poured down their throats, and no one — not crow, not woman, not king — can refuse a thing that has been poured into them.

Oscar heard it from three streets away.

*DELACROIX. TANNER'S ROW. DELACROIX FEEDS US. DELACROIX LISTENS.*

Her hands went still over a luck-knot she'd been tying — the cheap kind, just wool and a whispered word that was more suggestion than magic, harmless as a lullaby. The knot slipped loose. She stood in her stall amid her weather-whispers and her fortune bones and her little brass charms that did nothing at all except make people feel accompanied, and she listened to her name being screamed across the market square by six birds she had raised from eggs.

The crowd reaction was worse than the screaming. She could hear it — the particular hush, the wet sound of a city drawing breath before it starts pointing fingers.

"Delacroix," someone repeated, tasting it. "There's a Delacroix on Tanner's Row."

"Charm-seller," said another voice, uncertain. "Sells knots. Sells luck."

"Luck," said a third voice, and now it had teeth in it. "Where does a charm-seller get *luck*?"

Oscar began packing her stall. Not fast — fast was a confession — but with the unhurried efficiency of a woman closing early because her feet hurt. Weather-whispers into the basket, wrapped in oilcloth so they wouldn't whisper to each other and give themselves away. Fortune bones into the false bottom. Luck-knots left out deliberately, scattered, devalued — let them find the wool and the nothing inside them. Let them think all of it was wool and nothing.

Her heart was doing something complicated behind her ribs, and she made herself ignore it, because grief is a luxury you schedule for later, and later was looking increasingly theoretical.

* * *

Maren found her before she'd finished.

The hedge-witch came through the market like weather — everyone noticed her and nobody looked directly at her — and planted herself at the stall with the expression of a woman who had rehearsed a speech and intended, this once, to deliver it.

"You're leaving," Maren said.

"I'm closing early."

"Oscar." Maren's voice dropped to the register she saved for sick animals and stubborn ones, which in Oscar's experience overlapped considerably. "They put iron on the birds. Iron on *birds*. Do you understand what that means? It means Halveth knows how the gift works. It means she knows the beasts will answer truth when asked in their own tongue, and she's found a way to ask. Birds today. Dogs tomorrow. Then rats in the walls, Oscar, rats in the walls telling her everything, and there is nowhere in this city a rat doesn't go."

"I know what it means."

"Then why are you tying a knot?"

Oscar looked down. Her hands, traitorous and calm, were indeed tying a knot — muscle memory filling the silence the way water fills a footprint. She unpicked it slowly.

"Maren. If I run, I'm guilty. That's how it works. You know that better than I do. Half the people on the registry ran first and were named after." She kept her voice level, reasonable, the voice of a sensible woman. "If I stay quiet and sell my knots and mind my own affairs—"

"—the storm will pass over you," Maren finished. "Yes. I've watched people believe that my whole life. It's a very comfortable belief. It fits nicely in the mouth right up until the moment it doesn't." She gripped the edge of the stall, and Oscar saw, with a small cold shock, that the hedge-witch's hands were shaking. Maren, who had survived three registries and two purges by being exactly as forgettable as furniture. Maren, afraid.

"They'll come tonight or tomorrow," Maren said. "Not today. Today she lets the name travel. Serane Halveth is not a hammer, girl, she's a *ledger*. She writes everything down first and burns afterward, and she likes to give people one night to imagine the burning. Sleep on that if you can. But be gone by dawn, or be ash by noon." She pressed something into Oscar's hand — a twist of paper, warm from her pocket. "And take this, since you won't listen, which I've known for six years and have made my peace with."

Oscar opened the paper. Inside was a map — hand-drawn, ink faded, showing the country south and east of Vellamark in loving, unflattering detail. The hollows. The wild country where the land grew strange. Rivers with no names, marked instead with small warnings in Maren's crabbed hand: *will not hold a boat that lies.* A forest labeled simply *DO NOT WHISTLE.*

"Maren, what's out here?"

"Everything the crown forgot to claim," Maren said. "Which is most of everything. Go east until the trees stop being polite." She turned to go, then paused, her back to Oscar. "I'm sorry about the birds."

Oscar's throat closed. She managed, barely, "They didn't—"

"No," Maren said quietly. "They didn't. Nothing with iron in its blood betrays anyone. Remember that, whatever else you remember." And she walked off through the market, unremarkable as furniture, while Oscar stood holding a map of everywhere she'd sworn she would never go.

* * *

She waited for dark. Of course she waited for dark; running in daylight was how you got a mob, and Oscar had spent her whole life avoiding mobs the way other people avoided debt.

So she sat in her room above the stall with a single candle, and she listened to the city talk about her.

It traveled the way fire travels through dry grass — street by street, gaining speed, shedding accuracy. By suppertime she was a beast-speaker who could call wolves into the market square. By full dark she could raise the dead, which was flattering and also completely outside her skill set, and she found herself almost laughing at it, alone, in the dark, with her life packed into two baskets and a satchel.

Almost laughing. Because underneath the absurdity was the other thing. The real thing.

Crookbeak's voice, cracked and screaming. *Delacroix. Delacroix feeds us.*

Six winters of crusts and suet and soft-boiled eggs, and the last thing she'd given those birds was a reason for men in gray coats to put iron on their legs. She thought of the charms — cold iron, cruel work, made by someone whose trade it was to think of new ways to make living things obey. She thought of Crookbeak's tumbling flight. She thought of the youngest of the flock, Speck, who liked to steal buttons and had once spent an entire afternoon trying to teach Oscar that shiny things were superior to food, an argument he lost but never conceded.

They hadn't betrayed her. That was the part that sat in her chest like swallowed glass. There had been no choice in it at all — no moment where some small feathered conscience weighed loyalty against fear and chose wrong. They had been emptied out and filled with orders, the same as a puppet, the same as a bell rung by someone else's hand. And they would scream her name until their throats gave out, and somewhere under the compulsion, in whatever place a crow keeps its true self, they would know they were doing it.

That was the cruelty of it. Not the screaming. The knowing.

Oscar blew out the candle and sat in the dark and allowed herself, briefly, the luxury she'd been postponing. Grief came in and sat with her like an old friend who doesn't need conversation. She grieved the stall, which had been hers for four years and had a floorboard that squeaked in E-flat. She grieved the city, which she had loved in the way you love a house with bad foundations — constantly, anxiously, listening for the crack. She grieved the version of herself who had genuinely believed, right up until this morning, that care and caution would be enough.

Then she stopped, because dawn was coming, and Maren had been specific about dawn.

* * *

Tanner's Row at three in the morning smelled of tallow and river mud, and it was empty in the way streets are empty when everyone is pretending to sleep with their shutters slightly open. Oscar moved through it with her hood up and her baskets balanced, and she told herself she was not going to look at the rooftops.

She looked anyway. Habit. Love. Whatever you call the thing that makes you check a window for a face that isn't going to be there.

The rooftops were bare. Of course they were. Gray coats had taken the whole flock in wagons, iron-clanked and screaming, and there was nothing on the eaves of Vellamark now but frost.

Except.

At the corner of the chandlery, half-hidden in shadow, sat a single crow.

Speck.

He didn't move when she stopped. He didn't speak. His head tipped — that quick, mechanical, sideways attention crows pay to the world — and she saw, even in the dark, even from ten feet, the glint of wire around one leg. Not iron. Just wire, twisted crudely, the sort of restraint a soldier uses when he's run out of proper charms and can't be bothered. It had held him badly. He had chewed free of the rest.

He couldn't say her name. Whether the compulsion still lived in his blood or whether he simply understood, in the wordless way of his kind, what saying it had cost — he said nothing. He hopped once along the ledge. Twice. Toward her.

And Oscar Delacroix, standing in a frozen street with her whole life in two baskets, felt the grief in her chest turn over and become something with edges. Something you could carry. Something you could use.

Resolve, she supposed, was what people called it. It felt less noble than that. It felt like anger with a destination.

"I know," she whispered to him, in the flat human tongue, safe and meaningless. "I know what they made you say. It doesn't count. None of it counts." She dug in her satchel and set the last of her suet on the chandlery step — foolish, wasteful, the act of a woman with no sense of priorities — and Speck dropped down to it without hurry, because hunger is honest and grief is not.

She left him eating and walked east, out of Tanner's Row, out of the wards, past the last lamp where the city's politeness ended. In her pocket, Maren's map crinkled against her hip. *Go east until the trees stop being polite.*

Behind her, faint across the frozen roofs, she heard the first cock crow of morning — and beneath it, thin and far away, the sound of boots. Many boots. Moving toward a stall on Tanner's Row where a scatter of worthless luck-knots waited in the dark, and a floorboard squeaked in E-flat, and nobody home.

Oscar pulled her hood lower, turned her back on the only city she had ever loved, and walked into the hollow country — not knowing that three days ahead, in a snare-line in the border woods, a disgraced lawkeeper was about to catch something far more dangerous than a thief.
