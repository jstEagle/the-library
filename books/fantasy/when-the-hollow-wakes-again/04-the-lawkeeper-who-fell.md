## 4. The Lawkeeper Who Fell

Dain Corvel had been a lawkeeper for eleven years, and he had learned in that time that most trouble announced itself. Trouble knocked. Trouble shouted. Trouble came up the road wearing a uniform and a bad temper.

The trouble in his snares said nothing at all.

He heard it first as a wrongness — the hares should have been thumping by now, or the fox should have made off with something worth taking — and then he heard the soft, deliberate sounds of a person being very quiet, which is a sound experienced ears can pick out of any forest. He set down the split kindling he'd gathered and moved through the birches without hurry. No hurry. Hurry was for lawkeepers, and he wasn't one anymore. He was just a man with a cabin, three snares, and a beard he kept meaning to deal with.

The stranger crouched at his second snare line, and she was not what he expected. He'd expected a poacher: broad, sly, apologetic in the way of men who do this every season. Instead there was a young woman, thin as a winter branch, with ink stains on her fingers and a market-seller's shawl wrapped over what looked like a runaway's clothes. She was easing a hare out of the snare with a gentleness that annoyed him more than theft would have. Thieves were honest. Kindness to stolen goods was something else entirely.

"Morning," he said.

She went still the way small animals go still — all at once, completely, as if she had never existed before this moment and did not intend to exist after it. Then, very slowly, she turned her head and looked at him with the flat, calculating eyes of someone pricing her odds.

"Morning," she said back, holding the hare against her chest like a shield.

"That's my snare."

"I can see that."

"And that's my hare."

"It's *its own* hare," she said. "I'm just borrowing it briefly."

Dain scratched his jaw. Eleven years of settling disputes at the Vellamark gates, and here was the first criminal in his career to argue taxonomy. "You're stealing from me."

"I'm redistributing," she said. "Poorly. You clearly need it less than I do — you have a whole cabin."

"You're living in my woods."

"Your woods?" Something flickered behind the flat eyes then, quick and hot. "Whose name is on them? Whose mark? The trees didn't sign anything."

"No," Dain agreed, "they didn't." And because eleven years of lawkeeping dies hard, he heard himself say, in his old gate-sergeant voice, "Name?"

She weighed it. He watched her weigh it — watched her decide whether to lie, and then decide, for reasons he couldn't guess, not to bother.

"Oscar Delacroix."

The name landed on him like a stone dropped down a well. He'd read it twice in the last month: once on a broadsheet nailed to the border marker, once in Halveth's census pages that some fool courier had left unattended at the waystation. *Delacroix, Oscar. Beast-speaker, suspected. Reward.*

She saw him recognize it. He watched that happen too — watched her weight shift onto her back foot, watched her glance at the treeline, the hare still tucked against her chest like a child. She was going to run. She was fast; she'd probably make the birches.

"Put it back in the snare," Dain said.

Her chin came up. "No."

"Not for me." He nodded at the animal. "It's a doe hare. Look at her belly. She's got kits in a form somewhere waiting on her, and if you take her they'll starve inside two days. There's a buck trail forty paces east, by the split stone. Take him instead. He's fat and rude and nobody's mother."

Oscar Delacroix stared at him. The hare stared at him too, which Dain tried not to think about.

"You're letting me go," she said. Not asking. Checking the arithmetic.

"I'm feeding your kits," he said. "You're the one going free. Come on, I'll show you the trail. It's easier than arguing with you, and you look like you argue professionally."

* * *

She didn't run. That was the strange part. She followed him twenty paces to the buck trail, took the second hare when he reset the snare and caught it an hour later — she waited, arms crossed, watching him work with the critical eye of a woman who had opinions about knots — and then sat on his woodpile eating roasted hare while he pretended he hadn't just committed treason over breakfast.

"You knew what I was," she said, around the food. "From the name."

"Broadsheets reach even the border woods. Your face is on one. Bad likeness. They gave you a villain's chin."

"They gave everyone a villain's chin. Halveth's artist hates people." She turned the spit. "Why aren't you reporting me? There's a bounty. Enough to restock that cabin ten times."

Dain fed a stick into the fire and considered how much truth to spend. He found, to his surprise, that the answer was most of it. Maybe exile thinned the blood-brain barrier that sensible men keep between themselves and strangers.

"Had a sister," he said. "Wren. She could mend things. Not heal — mend. Broken cups, broken fences, broken bones, all the same to her. She'd hold a thing and it would remember being whole." He watched the fire. "When the registry started, a neighbor reported her. A man whose roof she'd fixed for free, mind. I was told to bring her in for assessment. Assessment means the iron charms, and the iron charms mean—" He shrugged, as if the word weren't sitting in his chest where it always sat. "I said no. Loudly. In front of the garrison."

"So they stripped your rank."

"They stripped everything but the beard." He tugged at it ruefully. "I keep thinking about growing it out properly. Make myself look like a hermit on purpose instead of by accident."

"What happened to Wren?"

"She ran south ahead of me. Letters come sometimes, unsigned, from towns I've never heard of." He glanced up. "That's the whole story. That's why you're not in irons. You want the rest of the hare?"

They sat in the kind of silence that follows honesty — uncomfortable, then not. Oscar finished eating, wiped her hands on her shawl, and looked around the clearing with an appraiser's eye.

"You're alone out here," she said. "Don't you get lonely?"

"Constantly. It's why I talk to hares."

"Do they answer?"

And there it was — the question under the question, offered lightly, her eyes doing none of the lightness. Dain held her gaze and decided she'd earned the truth back.

"No," he said. "But I once knew a man who talked to horses, and he swore they answered. Said horses had terrible opinions about fences." He paused. "I believed him. For whatever that's worth, Delacroix. I believe such things are possible."

Something in her shoulders came down half an inch — a knot loosening that must have been tied years ago. She didn't thank him. She looked at the fire instead, and after a while she said, "The crows used to tell me things. Gossip, mostly. Who'd died, who'd cheated at cards, where the good bread was." Her voice went quiet. "I miss it more than I thought I would. Like losing hearing in one ear. You don't notice how much of the world came through until it stops."

Dain nodded, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't be either pity or a promise he couldn't keep, and she seemed to understand, because she didn't ask for either.

* * *

They found the child three days later, and neither of them ever quite agreed afterward on who found whom.

Dain had gone to check the far snare line and taken Oscar along, partly for company and partly because she'd started reorganizing his snares without permission, claiming his placement was "an insult to rabbits." They were arguing about anchor stakes — she was right, which irritated him — when the forest went quiet.

Not still. Quiet. The difference mattered. Stillness was birds holding their breath. This was the birds leaving, in order, oldest trees first, like guests departing a house where something has begun to smell wrong.

Then, from the hollow beneath the lightning-split oak at the creek bend, came singing.

A child's voice, thin and cracked, singing words in no language Dain knew — except that some part below his knowing did know them, the way you know a lullaby from before memory. The melody bent strangely, doubled itself, as though two voices shared one throat and only one of them was alive.

Dain put his hand on his knife out of pure habit and hated himself for it. Oscar put her hand on his wrist and moved it away, and stepped forward alone.

"Hello," she called softly. "We're not soldiers. We're nobody. I'm a thief and he's unemployed."

The singing stopped.

"We heard you," Oscar said to the dark under the roots. "Whoever's singing. We heard."

Silence. Long enough that Dain started forward — and then a child crawled out of the hollow like something being born backward: a girl of perhaps eight, mud to the eyebrows, hair full of twigs, eyes enormous and gray as creek water. She stopped six feet away and studied them both with a seriousness no eight-year-old should carry.

"You heard," she said. Her voice was rough from disuse. "Nobody hears. Grown-ups hear *me* and they don't like what comes with it."

"What comes with it?" Dain asked gently.

"The dead talk through things." She said it flatly, the way children state facts adults flinch from. "Through roots. Through stones. Through the split places, mostly — where the world's cracked, they lean close and speak. Grandda said I was cursed. Then the men with iron came and Grandda told them where I slept, so." She shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "Now I live in the split oak. It's warmer than it looks. The dead keep saying so."

Oscar crouched to the child's level. "What's your name?"

"Tam." A pause. "The dead call me Tamtoo. They think it's funny. Dead people are worse than grandfathers for jokes."

Despite everything — despite the burned stall and the broadsheet and the sister-shaped grief standing next to him — Dain felt his mouth twitch. "Tamtoo," he repeated solemnly. "An honor."

Tam looked from him to Oscar and back, and Dain watched the calculation happen behind those gray eyes — the same arithmetic he'd seen Oscar do at the snare, the same weighing of odds and exits. But Tam was eight, and eight is faster than grown folk. The child had already done sums they were still working on.

"You're hiding," Tam said to Oscar. "The dead mentioned you. A crow-shape, they said, walking wrong through the world since its song got cut. They said to tell you—" the girl frowned, listening to something Dain could not begin to hear, head cocked toward the split oak— "*the hollow remembers.*"

The wind moved through the birches then, all at once, although there had been no wind all morning.

Oscar had gone very pale. "Remembers what?"

Tam shook her head. "They don't finish sentences. Dead people are also rude." The child looked past them both then, down the creek, toward the deep country where the maps went blank and the trees grew old and crooked and close, and said, in the tone of someone announcing supper:

"They also say something's coming. Big. Old. It's been following the crow-shape since the burning." Gray eyes lifted to Oscar's. "I think it wants to meet you."

Behind them, far off toward the border road, a hawk screamed — or something shaped like a hawk's scream, but wrong, doubled, as if two voices shared one throat.

Oscar and Dain looked at each other, and in that look Dain felt the strange kinship click shut around the three of them like a snare closing — gentle, and inescapable, and home.

"Well," he said. "I'll pack the good blanket."
