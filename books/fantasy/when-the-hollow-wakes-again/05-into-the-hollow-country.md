## 5. Into the Hollow Country

The map Maren had given Oscar was drawn on sheepskin, and it lied.

Not maliciously—Oscar was fairly sure of that. It lied the way old things lie, out of habit and a certain contempt for the reader. The road marked in careful brown ink as running "two days to the Hollow Ford" had, by the third morning, stopped resembling a road at all. What remained of it was a suggestion, a rumor of cart-wheels pressed into moss, and then not even that. The trees closed over the place where the road should have been like water closing over a dropped coin.

"We're lost," Tam announced, with the cheerful certainty of a child delivering excellent news.

"We are *reorienting*," Oscar said.

Dain Corvel, walking behind them with his hood up and his expression set to its usual setting of long-suffering granite, said nothing. He'd been saying nothing for two days in a way that managed to be louder than most men's shouting.

"The roots say we're going the right way," Tam added, patting an oak's trunk as she passed it, the way another child might pat a dog. "The roots don't know what the right way *is*, but they say we're going it."

"That's very comforting," Oscar said.

"They also say you're loud."

Oscar decided not to dignify that.

Three days out from Vellamark, and the world had already begun to change. She noticed it first in small ways: no birdsong past the second ridge, though she saw birds—small brown flittings in the underbrush, watching them pass without ever singing. Then the light itself. In Vellamark, even on grey days, light fell. Here it seemed to arrive by invitation, pooling in certain places and refusing others, so that they walked through alternating rooms of gold and green shadow.

And the quiet. Not silence—the forest was full of sound, wind and water and the creak of living wood—but none of it was aimed at them. In the city, everything spoke *at* you: vendors, bells, neighbors, the endless civic grumble of ten thousand people pretending not to notice each other. Out here, the noise went on around them the way weather does around a stone.

Oscar had spent her whole life being spoken to by animals. Crows with opinions about her hair. Sparrows demanding tribute. A horse once who had held a grudge against her for eleven years over an apple. She had never stood inside a place that declined to address her at all.

It felt less like emptiness than like being watched by something too large to bother opening one eye.

"Stop," Dain said suddenly.

Oscar froze. Tam, three steps ahead, kept walking, then stopped when Dain caught her shoulder.

Ahead of them, the path—they were calling it a path now, generously—forked around a fallen beech tree, and across the trunk, laid in a neat line, were seven dead mice.

"Fox cache," Dain said quietly. His hand had gone to the short sword he carried, the lawkeeper's blade he'd refused to surrender with his badge. "No. Foxes don't—" He frowned. "They don't arrange them."

He was right. They weren't strewn; they were *presented*. Laid tail to nose in a row, neat as candles on an altar.

"It's a gift," Tam said.

"It's a threat," Dain said.

"It can be both." Tam knelt and looked at the little bodies with a solemnity that sat strangely on her thin face. She was maybe nine years old, all elbows and tangled dark hair, and she had the habit of speaking to invisible company that made strangers cross the street. "The dead ones are saying sorry for something. I think somebody else did this, and the dead are just... passing it along."

Oscar crouched beside her. "Sorry for what?"

Tam tilted her head, listening to whatever she listened to—the way Oscar listened to crows, except downward instead of up, into root and stone and bone instead of feather and wing. Her brow creased.

"For the winter," she said finally. "The dead are always cold here. Even in summer." She looked up at Oscar with eyes far older than nine had any business owning. "They want to know if we brought spring."

Oscar opened her mouth to make some gentle nonsense-noise, the kind adults make when children say unsettling things, and found she couldn't. Because the air around the mice did smell cold. Cold in a way that had no season, a cellar-cold, an under-the-world cold.

"We didn't bring spring," she said honestly. "We brought ourselves."

Tam considered this. "That might be enough. The roots aren't sure yet."

* * *

They walked until the light began to fail, and made camp in the hollow beneath a yew tree so old its trunk had folded into architecture—a green room with a doorway of drooping branches. Dain built a fire the way he did everything: efficiently, silently, with the economy of a man who had been stripped down to essentials and found he liked the fit.

Oscar rationed out the last of the bread and hard cheese. Tam ate hers in tiny bites, making it last, an old habit neither of the adults commented on.

"You knew him," Tam said suddenly, to Dain. "Before."

Dain looked up from sharpening his knife. "Knew who?"

"The last speaker. The one who died." Tam hugged her knees. "The stones have been talking about him since yesterday. They keep saying his name wrong. *Aldous. Alder. Alms-giver.* They can't remember it right because nobody says it anymore." She frowned at the fire. "You went to his funeral. You stood in the back and you didn't cry because lawkeepers aren't supposed to cry, but your boots were muddy and you walked home through the river rather than take off the mud."

Dain's knife had stopped moving.

"I heard the stones," Tam said, apologetic. "I don't mean to. It's like eavesdropping through a wall."

"How do you—" Dain began, and stopped, and started again. "His name was Aldric Vane. Speaker-Adjunct to the Crown. Nobody came to that funeral. There wasn't one. He died alone in a charity ward with no family and no title left, and the registry burned his name off the rolls the same week." His voice was flat, but Oscar heard the thing underneath it, the same wound she'd glimpsed when he'd told her why he'd fallen. "How could stones remember a funeral that never happened?"

"Stones remember what people almost did," Tam said simply. "You almost cried. That counts."

Oscar looked away, into the fire, to give him privacy. She thought of Aldric Vane, whose existence she hadn't known until three days ago, and whose death had apparently unmade a treaty older than the city walls. One man, dying unmourned in a charity ward. And the whole wild world leaning toward Vellamark like a listener cupping a hand to a door.

"The covenant," she said carefully. "Tam says the forest remembers it. Sealed in blood and song, she says. Between human kings and—" She still stumbled on the word. "—the feral courts. Three years ago the last speaker died, and the human side of the pact went empty. Is that true? Do you know anything?"

Dain turned the knife over in his hands. "I know that three years ago, the winters started coming early. I know my sister used to leave milk on the doorstep for the hedge-things, and after that year they stopped taking it. I know the fish left the Vell. All the fish, upstream, gone in a single autumn." He shrugged, a gesture that tried to be careless and failed. "Lawkeepers get reports. Most of them get filed under *superstition* and forgotten. But there was a pattern. Everything wild moved away from us at once, like a house clearing out before the bailiffs come."

"And nobody asked why."

"Somebody probably asked. Asking isn't the same as hearing." He glanced at Tam. "Turns out we'd gotten rid of everybody who could hear."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, the yew's branches shifted in a wind Oscar couldn't feel at ground level.

"Aldric Vane," Oscar murmured. "Speaker-Adjunct. So the crown *knew*. Kept the office, kept the man, let him die in a ward like a stray cat, and never once thought to wonder what he was *for*."

"People are very good at forgetting what things are for," Dain said. "Look what happened to me."

That startled a laugh out of Oscar—short, surprised, real. "To be fair, you're much more useful as a fugitive."

"Flattery. From a woman who steals from snares."

"Borrowed. I borrowed from your snares. And I left a luck-knot."

"You left a *knot*."

"It's worth money in Vellamark!"

"In Vellamark," Dain agreed gravely, "people pay good coin to be lied to. Out here, I suspect the local clientele has different standards."

Tam giggled, and the sound was so rare and so bright that both adults pretended not to notice how much it mattered.

* * *

Oscar took the middle watch, as agreed. Dain slept sitting up, back against the yew, sword across his knees even in sleep—she suspected he slept that way the way other men breathed. Tam slept curled against the old man's coat they'd draped over her, breathing slow and even, and even asleep her lips moved faintly, answering voices Oscar couldn't hear.

The night was enormous.

Oscar sat at the edge of the firelight and did the thing she had done every night since fleeing the city: reached out, gently, with the gift, listening for anything that might speak to her. A sentry-bird. A fox's passing commentary. Even a beetle's grumble would have been welcome.

Nothing.

It wasn't silence exactly. It was more like standing outside a great hall where a conversation was happening—one voice, vast and low and layered, so deep she couldn't pick out words, only the shape of speech. Something was talking. It simply wasn't talking to *her*, or perhaps hadn't yet deigned to notice her, the way a queen doesn't notice an ant crossing the palace floor.

Then she noticed the quiet.

Not the forest's quiet. Their quiet. The insects that had droned at the edges of the fire had stopped. The wind had stopped. Even the yew had stopped shifting overhead, its branches frozen mid-sway like a painting of a tree rather than a tree.

Something was coming.

She woke Dain with a hand on his shoulder and he was instantly, completely awake, which she was learning was simply what he was. "Company?" he whispered.

"I don't know. Nothing's spoken. Everything's *stopped*."

He rose without a sound and put himself between the darkness and Tam, and Oscar hated how natural it looked on him, hated that a man built for protecting people had been given so few to protect.

The dark between the trees thickened. Gathered. Became a shape.

It was vast. That was the first and last thing her mind managed clearly—that whatever stood at the edge of the firelight occupied the space of a barn, of a chapel, of a hill that had decided to go for a walk—and yet it made no sound, broke no branch, pressed no footprint into the loam. She caught antlers first, or what she took for antlers, a crown of pale branching wood that scraped the stars, and then a body like a shadow given muscle and patience. Its eyes—if those were eyes—were the color of deep water under ice.

It did not attack. It did not retreat.

It *looked* at them. At Dain's raised blade. At sleeping Tam. Last—at Oscar. And when its gaze settled on her, the pressure of it was physical, like a hand resting flat on her sternum, and in the marrow-deep hall of that attention she finally heard a single word, not spoken so much as *remembered aloud*, the way stones remember funerals:

*Speaker.*

Then it stepped backward into the dark, and the dark accepted it, and the insects resumed their drone all at once as if a held breath had been released.

"Oscar." Dain's voice, tight. "What in the Sundered hells was that."

She realized her legs were shaking. She realized she was smiling.

"That," she said, "was someone who knows my name."

Behind them, unnoticed by either adult, Tam had opened her eyes in the dark, and was looking at the empty space where the vastness had stood, and whispering, delighted and terrified in equal measure:

"He says you're late."
