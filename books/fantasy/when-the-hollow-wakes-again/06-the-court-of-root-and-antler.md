## 6. The Court of Root and Antler

The roots took them in their sleep.

Oscar woke to the smell of turned earth and the pressure of something vast and patient holding her ankles, her wrists, her ribs — not cruelly, but with the unmistakable firmness of a parent collecting a child from a market stall they had no business being in. The fire Tam had insisted on building was a cold black smudge. Dain was already awake beside her, knife out, sawing at a root thick as his forearm, and accomplishing precisely nothing.

"Save your edge," Oscar said. "It's not angry. It's *careful*."

"That's worse," said Dain.

"It's better. Angry things make mistakes."

Tam sat up in the middle of it all, hair full of leaf mold, and said, with the flat certainty of a child reporting the weather: "They've been watching us since the river. They think we're slow."

"We *are* slow," Oscar admitted. "We slept on their floor and didn't knock."

The ground opened like a well-bred door.

* * *

They went down into the hollow country the way a stone goes down a well — smoothly, without any say in the matter. Roots braided overhead into arches hung with pale lichen that glowed faintly, the way old cheese glows, if old cheese were trying to be dignified about it. The passage smelled of rain on stone and something sweeter underneath: sap, blood, honey left too long in the comb.

Tam walked between them, unafraid, head tilted as though listening to a conversation just below the range of hearing. "They're arguing about us," she reported. "The foxes want to eat Dain. The owls say he's mostly gristle."

"Thank you, owls," said Dain.

"The badgers don't care what you are. The badgers want to know if you can cook."

"I can cook," said Dain, and something in his shoulders loosened half an inch, which for Dain was practically a jig.

Oscar kept her eyes forward and her hands open at her sides, palms out, the way she'd approach an injured animal. Every instinct she had — every charm-warmed, carefully hidden instinct — told her this was the moment she'd been walking toward since she'd healed a sparrow in a market square and let one old woman see her do it. She had spent her whole life making herself small. There was nowhere smaller left to go. So she made herself *true* instead, which felt terrifyingly large.

The tunnel opened.

* * *

She had imagined courts from storybooks: thrones of gold, courtiers in silk, heralds with trumpets. What she stood in instead was a cathedral built by patience — a hollowed hill where living oaks grew inward to form pillars, their branches woven so tightly overhead that no sky showed, only a ceiling of leaves going gold at the edges. Autumn had come early here, or had never left. Frost silvered the moss in patterns too deliberate to be weather.

And filling the hill, silent as held breath, were the beasts.

Foxes in ranks along the walls, winter-thick coats gleaming. Owls on every branch, eyes like dropped coins. Wolves at the front, sitting in perfect rows like a congregation that had heard this sermon before and did not expect to enjoy it. Stags, hares, ravens — and crows, dozens of them, and Oscar's heart clenched painfully at the sight, because she knew crow posture now, knew the particular slump of birds who had done a thing they could not unsay.

At the center, where an altar would stand, stood the Stag Sovereign.

He was larger than any stag had a right to be, tall as two men, his antlers grown through and around a crown of iron — actual crown iron, rusted, broken, the circlet of some dead human king dragged up from wherever it had been buried and worn deliberately, insultingly, like a trophy taken off a corpse. His hide was gray at the muzzle. His breath steamed. When he looked at Oscar, she felt the look land on her skin like snow.

"*Speaker,*" he said. His voice was the sound of a tree falling in a country with no one left to hear it.

"I'm Oscar Delacroix," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Of Vellamark. Formerly."

"Formerly." The word rolled around the hill, and the wolves' ears all moved at once. "You speak our tongue. You fed the winged ones on your roof for nine years. You healed a sparrow before forty witnesses because you could not help yourself." He stepped down from the altar, each hoof-fall a small earthquake. "And when the census came, when the names burned, when the last of your kind were dragged into squares and made examples of — where were your words then?"

"Hidden," Oscar said. "Same as my gift."

"Yes." Something moved behind his eyes that was not quite contempt and not quite grief. "Hidden. Buried. Denied. Do you know what your city smells like to us, little speaker? It smells of *closed doors*. Of mouths shut on kindness until the kindness rots. We sent you the covenant three hundred years ago in good faith — blood and song, sworn at a stone beneath your first cathedral — and your kings sealed it with a promise: there will always be a voice on your side of the wall. A speaker, living, who hears us and answers."

"And then?" Tam asked, because children ask.

"And then," the Sovereign said, "your crown outlawed the listening. Outlawed the answering. Burned the speakers one by one and called it cleansing, and the last of them died three winters past in a cell so deep no bird could find her, and no one on your side of the wall has answered us since." He lowered his great head, antlers nearly brushing the frost-silvered moss. "A pact is a bridge, speaker. Your side collapsed. And the weight of everything the bridge carried has been falling ever since. You feel it as winter. We feel it as hunger. The long cold is not our wrath. It is simply what happens when a promise dies and nothing is built to hold what it held."

Oscar thought of Maren's warning. *Run.* She thought of the ledger, the burning names, Halveth's neat handwriting. She thought of the crows screaming her street under iron charms, and how she had run not from them but from the men who'd bent them.

"You blame us," she said slowly. "All of us. For what the crown did."

"I blame the silence," said the Sovereign. "The crown merely enforced it. Your neighbors practiced it. Your markets sold charms against us — luck-knots, weather-whispers, trinkets to keep the wild *out*. I am told you sold them yourself."

The hill went very quiet. Somewhere above, an owl shifted its weight.

"I did," Oscar said. Her face was hot. "Because staying invisible was the only way to stay alive, and staying alive meant someday being able to—" She stopped. She had never finished that sentence before, even to herself. "To matter. To do something. Someday."

"Someday is the anthem of the buried," said the Sovereign, and there was, horribly, a ripple of agreement through the foxes. "Three hundred years of somedays. And now the cold comes for both realms, and you arrive at last with your someday in your mouth, asking—"

Horns. Not beast-horns — brass, ugly, human horns, sounding from the tunnel mouth they'd come through.

* * *

The court did not panic. That was the frightening thing. Three hundred years of dignity arranged itself around the intrusion like water around a dropped stone, and the wolves rose as one, and the Sovereign turned his crowned head toward the dark of the tunnel with an expression Oscar could only call *weariness*.

"They followed me," she breathed. "Halveth's hunters. My trail—"

"Your trail, and ours," said the Sovereign. "Iron charms drag a line through the world that things like us can see from a hilltop away. You brought your crown's dogs to the last quiet place left." He looked back at her, and now the grief in him was naked. "*This* is what your kind does, speaker. Even running from the hunt, you carry it with you."

Torches bloomed in the tunnel. Voices — boots — the jingle of chain. Dain moved to stand in front of Tam without seeming to decide to, an old lawkeeper reflex dying hard. Oscar's mind raced through arithmetic she hated: the tunnel was the only way out, the court would fight or scatter, either way the hollows would be breached for the first time in living memory—

"No," she said aloud, surprising herself. "Nobody fights. Nobody scatters." She stepped forward, out beyond the wolves, into the open ground between the court and the tunnel, palms out, heart hammering. "Inquisitor Serane Halveth!" she called down the dark. "I know you can hear me. I know what you are!"

Silence. Then, from the torchlight, a voice like a ledger closing: "Come out, Delacroix. Bring the child. The lawkeeper is irrelevant."

"She's right," Tam whispered. "About what she is. The dead in the stones are *yelling* about it. She's got a whole storm shut up inside her and it's screaming to get out."

Oscar's breath caught. She looked at the Stag Sovereign, at the assembled wild, at the frost creeping across a court that should have been green. Then back at the tunnel, where torchlight wavered, and a woman stood in it holding iron charms and a ledger and three years of self-loathing dressed up as duty.

"You want to know why the pact broke?" Oscar called, loud enough for both courts — the furred and the armored. "It didn't break because someone betrayed it. It broke because everyone stopped listening — the crown, the neighbors, *me*, selling charms to keep the wild out because listening was dangerous. And you—" her voice cracked, and she let it, "—you're still not listening. Not to them. Not to yourself. You're hunting the same wound you carry."

For a long moment, the only sound was torch-crackle and the Sovereign's slow breath.

Then Halveth stepped fully into the light, and raised her hand, and every iron charm on her belt began to sing.
