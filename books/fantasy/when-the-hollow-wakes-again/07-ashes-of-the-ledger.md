## 7. Ashes of the Ledger

Dain Corvel had held worse positions than a narrow pass in the Hollow Country, though he was hard-pressed at the moment to remember when. There had been the siege at Marrow Ford, where he'd spent three days hip-deep in freezing water holding a bridge with six men and a bad attitude. There had been the riot outside the granary, the winter he'd still been someone whose word carried weight.

That had been two years ago. Before the ledger. Before his sister's name went into it and his refusal to write it there went into another one.

Now he stood alone in a gap between two leaning stones that the locals called the Shepherd's Teeth, with a quarterstaff, a hunting knife, and the certain knowledge that somewhere behind him, Oscar Delacroix was bleeding and arguing about it.

"You don't have to do this," she'd said, pressing her palm against the gash along her ribs, which was doing approximately nothing.

"I know," Dain said. "That's rather the point."

Tam had looked up at him with those unsettling gray eyes — the eyes of a child who heard dead people muttering through tree roots — and said, "The stones say you'll live." A pause. "Mostly. The stones argue about the details."

"That," Dain had told her gravely, "is the most comforting thing anyone's said to me all year."

So now here he was, doing arithmetic. Not the kind they taught lawkeepers-in-training — distances, numbers, angles of bowshot — but the other kind. The kind nobody taught because nobody wanted to learn it. One man against however many soldiers Halveth had brought through the pass. Two lives ahead of him worth more than his own. The cold coming down off the high hollows like a debt collector, and somewhere beyond it, a covenant-stone that would seal itself under the first snow for another hundred years if Oscar didn't reach it first.

He rolled his shoulders and settled the staff across his body and waited.

They came an hour past dawn. He heard them before he saw them — boots on frost-stiffened bracken, the particular clink of crown-issue mail that no amount of marching ever taught a soldier to quiet. Eight men. Dain counted twice, hoping the second count would be wrong out of sheer politeness. It wasn't.

The lead soldier stopped when he saw him, a broad man with a sergeant's knots on his shoulder and the weary expression of someone who'd rather be anywhere else.

"Corvel." The sergeant said the name like he'd read it off a list, and maybe he had. "Dain Corvel. Struck from the rolls. You're wanted."

"Everyone's wanted these days," Dain said. "It's the fashion."

"Step aside. We've orders for the woman and the child."

"The woman's not here. The child's not here. It's just me and my staff, and I'm afraid we're both very attached to this pass."

The sergeant sighed, and Dain felt a flicker of kinship for him — a man doing a job he didn't love for wages he probably hadn't seen. That kinship lasted exactly as long as it took the sergeant to gesture, and the eight became a formation, and the formation came forward.

Fighting eight men is nothing like the songs say. In the songs it's graceful. In truth it is mostly noise and breath and the sudden stupid geometry of bodies, and you win by making yourself more trouble than you're worth. Dain had always been very good at being trouble.

He broke the first man's wrist with the staff and put the second into the rocks face-first. He caught a spear-thrust in the wood and turned it, hooked a knee, dropped a third. For perhaps thirty seconds he thought — wildly, gloriously — that the arithmetic might come out different this time.

Then something struck the back of his head, and the pass, the stones, the whole gray morning tilted sideways like a spilled cup.

* * *

He woke to hands on his arms and the taste of blood and pine. His staff was gone. His knife was gone. His dignity, on inspection, appeared to be intact, which surprised him.

"He's awake," someone said.

"Then get him up. The Inquisitor wants him breathing."

Dain blinked until the world resolved. Soldiers. Six of them standing, plus two sitting on rocks cradling their wounds and looking deeply resentful of him personally. And at the edge of the group, wrapped in a gray traveling cloak with her hands folded neatly at her waist, stood Inquisitor Serane Halveth.

She was smaller than her reputation. Her ledger was open in her hands — the actual ledger, the one with names burning off its pages like paper moths — and she regarded Dain over the top of it with the mild patience of a clerk waiting for a signature.

"Sergeant Orlan reports you fought well," she said. "Eight to one. He says you were laughing."

"I might have been. Hard to recall. People say strange things about me."

"They do. They say you refused a direct registry order to name your sister. They say you let her run. They say you chose exile over duty." She turned a page. The ink on it writhed. "I've read your file, Corvel. It interests me. Most men break one way or the other — toward the law or away from it. You broke *sideways*. You kept your oath and lost everything keeping it. Do you understand how rare that is?"

"I understand you talk a great deal for someone who could just hang me."

"Oh, hanging's off the table." She closed the ledger with a soft, final sound. "You're bait, Corvel. The woman will come for you — she's already wounded, and wounded people make sentimental choices. When she comes, I'll be there. That's all you are to me now. A hook." Something crossed her face then, quick and involuntary, gone almost before it arrived — something that wasn't cruelty at all but looked terribly like exhaustion. "I'm sorry it's you. Of everyone in this wretched country, I'm sorry it's you."

"That," Dain said slowly, "is the strangest thing anyone's said to me all year."

* * *

They marched him westward through the afternoon, bound at the wrists, toward the old watch-fort at Greyhollow. Dain counted everything, because counting was what he had left: soldiers, paces, streambeds, the angle of the sun behind the peaks. If Oscar came — and she would come, that was the terrible part, he knew her already, three days of knowing her and he knew she would come — then she deserved every number he could give her.

Except.

Except that if she came, she wouldn't reach the stone. The snow was coming; Tam had said the trees were whispering about it, hunched and urgent. A century of sealed silence, and the wild's wrath falling on Vellamark like a hammer, and all of it because one stubborn beast-speaker chose a battered ex-lawkeeper over a treaty.

"Don't," he muttered to himself, and the soldier behind him prodded him with a spear-butt. "Sorry. Talking to myself. Occupational hazard."

But the arithmetic wouldn't stop. That was the thing about the terrible kind: it never showed its work, it only delivered the sum. His life weighed against a covenant. His freedom weighed against a city. And underneath it all, the question he'd been carrying since the day he tore his sister's name out of the registry with his own hands: what was justice, exactly, when the law itself was the wound?

His sister would have told him to stop being dramatic and eat something. He missed her fiercely.

* * *

Oscar reached the ridge above Greyhollow at dusk, and Dain knew it before he saw her — felt it, some animal certainty prickling along his neck, the way you feel weather change. He looked up at the dark line of pines and found her silhouette against the last light, one hand pressed to her side, swaying slightly.

And beside her, small and solemn, Tam. Who should have been leagues away by now. Whose gray eyes were fixed not on Dain but on the fort itself, lips moving, listening to whatever the stones beneath it were saying.

Later — much later, in the wreckage of everything that followed — Oscar would tell him what passed between them on that ridge. How she'd laid out the choice with brutal honesty, the way she did everything: the stone lay east, the snow lay everywhere, and Dain lay in chains between them, and there wasn't time for both. How Tam had been silent a long moment, head tilted, listening to the dead in the roots.

How Tam had finally said: *"The stones say the stone has waited a hundred years. It can wait one more night. But the living man can't wait at all."*

How Oscar had laughed — a cracked, wet, furious laugh — and said, "You're sure?"

And how Tam had answered, with the terrible clarity children carry like lanterns: "The dead are sure. They say you're going to need him. They say the Inquisitor isn't what you think she is." A pause, a frown. "They also say something about ashes. They keep saying it. *Ashes of the ledger.* Nobody will explain what it means."

On the ridge, in the failing light, Oscar Delacroix looked down at the fort where Dain Corvel sat in chains, and made her choice, and began to climb down.

And in the fort below, Dain — who couldn't know any of this, who only felt the night press close and the snow begin to fall in fat, deliberate flakes, the first of the season — watched the Inquisitor Halveth stand alone in the yard, looking up at the sky with an expression he recognized too well, because he'd worn it himself the day he lost everything.

The expression of a person about to do something unforgivable, and knowing it, and doing it anyway.

The snow touched her shoulders and did not melt.

Behind her, unwatched, the ledger in her saddlebag stirred on its own, and every name on every page began quietly, softly, to burn.
