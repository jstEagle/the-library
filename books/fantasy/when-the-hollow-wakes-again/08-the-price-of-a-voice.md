## 8. The Price of a Voice

The river that ran through the hollow country did not so much flow as mutter. Oscar had been following it for two days now, Tam's hand in hers, and in all that time it had never once said anything useful.

"It's complaining again," Tam reported, tilting her head the way she did when she listened to things no one else could hear. "It says the hags took its best bend and never gave it back."

"Did it say where the hags keep their bend?"

"No. It just complains." Tam considered. "Rivers complain a lot. You'd think with all that water they'd be less bitter."

Oscar almost smiled. Almost. Her side still ached where a hunter's bolt had grazed her three days past, and every step away from Dain Corvel — left behind at the pass, taken alive because Halveth wanted bait, because Halveth was clever that way — felt like walking on a bone that hadn't set right yet.

Ahead, the river widened into a pool the color of old pewter, and three shapes rose out of it like questions nobody wanted to ask.

River-hags were supposed to be beautiful, if you believed the market stories: drowned maidens with pearl eyes. These were not that. They were long and grey-green and folded up like unmade beds, and the eldest of them fixed Oscar with eyes like river stones worn smooth by centuries of being unimpressed.

"A beast-speaker," the eldest said. Her voice was wet gravel poured through honey. "Come down from the hills with a dead man's debt in her pocket and a child who stinks of graves. What do you want, little crow-feeder?"

"You know what I want," Oscar said. "You've been listening to the water since I crossed the ford. Rivers carry everything. Including gossip."

The middle hag laughed, a sound like a bucket dropping down a well. "Oh, I like this one. She's rude."

"I'm desperate. It looks similar from the outside."

The eldest hag drifted closer. Up close she smelled of silt and iron and something older underneath, something like the inside of stones. "You want into Vellamark's black cells. And you want to know where the covenant-stone sleeps. Two secrets, one price." Her smile was an accident of anatomy rather than an expression. "We know both. The question is what you'll trade."

"I have six silver bits and a lucky knot."

"We don't take coin. Coin is for people who have nothing better." The hag's stone-eyes glittered. "We take secrets. Real ones. The kind that cost."

Oscar had known this was coming. She'd rehearsed it on the road, lying awake while Tam slept against her shoulder, turning over lesser secrets like coins of decreasing value — the way she healed the sparrow, the names of her crow-friends, Maren's back-room still. Small change. She'd known the hags would wave it off, and they did, with the weary contempt of creditors shown counterfeit bills.

Then she said the thing she had sworn she would never say aloud, to anyone, ever.

"The way into Vellamark's prison is the culvert under the tannery row, where the wall sinks into the marsh. The grate's iron but the mortar is salt-sand; any hedge-witch worth her kettle can unmake it. There's a gap wide enough for a thin man and a guilty conscience." Her voice stayed steady. That surprised her. "And the covenant-stone isn't lost. It never was. It's buried beneath the old cathedral — the crown built their cathedral on top of it deliberately, after the Sundering. The foundation-stone of their god-house is our treaty-stone, and they've been singing hymns over its face for three hundred years without knowing — or worse, knowing exactly."

Silence, except for the river muttering somewhere behind them. Even Tam had gone still.

The youngest hag made a sound that might have been hunger or might have been reverence. "The crown prays on top of the pact," she breathed. "Every dawn. Every dawn for three hundred years."

"They didn't know," Oscar said. "Most of them didn't. But some of them did, and those ones counted on everyone else forgetting how to listen." She swallowed. "That's my secret. The whole shape of it. Now give me yours, hag-for-hag, as agreed. How do I get into the cells, and how do I get Dain Corvel out alive?"

The eldest hag studied her for a long moment, and something in her ancient face shifted — not softening, exactly, but settling, like sediment finding its level.

"You already know the way in. You traded true." She raised one long hand before Oscar could speak. "Here is the rest, freely given, because you gave more than we asked. The Inquisitor Serane Halveth keeps her prisoners in the deep cells herself, apart from the common jail. She interrogates alone. She trusts no one near them." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "And there is a reason for that, little crow-feeder. A reason you should carry with you when you go."

"What reason?"

"She is like you."

The words dropped into Oscar's chest like a stone into a well, falling and falling without reaching bottom.

"Suppressed," the hag went on. "Bound. Choked down with iron charms of her own devising — she invented half of them, did you know? Every charm that hunts the gifted, she built from the inside. She knows exactly how the gift feels when it's caged, because hers has been caged since she was nine years old, when her mother held her hands over a brazier and swore before a priest that whatever lived in the girl was burned out of her." The hag's voice went almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "It wasn't burned out. It never is. It just goes quiet, and hates, and waits."

Tam tugged Oscar's sleeve. "She hears animals too," the child whispered. "I heard the dead say it once, in Vellamark, before. The Inquisitor used to talk to rats when she was little. They liked her."

Oscar stood very still at the edge of the pewter pool, and the terrible arithmetic of the last week rearranged itself into a new sum. Halveth burning names off the census rolls. Halveth hunting the gifted with a precision no outsider could match. Not zealotry. Recognition. The hunt was a mirror, and every name struck from the ledger was another reflection she couldn't bear to see.

"Go," the eldest hag said, sinking back into the water. "Rescue your lawkeeper. And when you stand in front of her — and you will, the water insists on it — remember that you bought this secret with the last one you had. Spend it well."

* * *

Vellamark at night was a city holding its breath. Oscar came up through the tannery culvert smelling of everything she'd hoped never to smell of again, with Tam waiting outside the walls in Maren's cellar — the hedge-witch had opened her door without a single question, which was its own kind of courage — and the salt-mortar crumbling behind her like it had only been waiting for permission.

The deep cells were colder than the river. Stone corridors lit by lamps turned low, and at the end of them, a cell door standing open, which was either carelessness or a trap, and Serane Halveth was not a careless woman.

Dain sat inside on the cot, bruised about the face but whole, and his eyes went wide when he saw her. "Oscar. You mad, glorious fool—"

"Quiet." She stepped into the doorway and there, seated across from him at a small table as though this were an ordinary interview between a lawkeeper and his Inquisitor, sat Halveth.

No guards. No soldiers. Just the two of them, and now Oscar.

Halveth looked tired. That was the first shock. The second was that she didn't reach for a weapon, or call out, or do anything at all except look at Oscar with an expression Oscar recognized from her own mornings — the flat, guarded exhaustion of someone who has spent a lifetime making sure nothing shows.

"You came through the tannery culvert," Halveth said. "Salt mortar. Clever. My people sealed that years ago." A pause. "Or thought they did. Hedge-craft is stubborn stuff."

"I came for him."

"I know. I let you come." Halveth folded her hands on the table. "I wanted to see what you'd risk. It tells me what you are."

"And what am I?"

"Someone who comes back for people." Something flickered across the Inquisitor's face, quickly buried — grief, or its fossil record. "I know what that costs. I've read enough confessions to recognize the ones who mean it."

Oscar didn't move toward Dain. Some instinct told her that moving now would end everything. "You were going to burn him as bait. Draw me out."

"Yes."

"Why does the great hunter of the gifted need bait? You find us faster than anyone. Your charms are better than ours. You knew about my crows before my crows did." She took one slow step forward. "Because you're not hunting from the outside, are you, Inquisitor. You're hunting from the inside."

The lamp flame bent, though there was no draft.

For a moment — one long, suspended moment — Oscar saw it happen: the mask slipping, the ledger-face cracking, and underneath it a nine-year-old girl with her hands held over a brazier while her mother lied to a priest. Halveth's jaw tightened. Her eyes went wet and furious at the same time, which is the worst combination eyes can manage.

"Get out," Halveth said quietly.

"Heard it from the river-hags. Heard it from the dead, through Tam." Oscar kept her voice low and even, the voice she used for frightened animals. "You talk to beasts. Or you did. Before they burned it out of you. Except it doesn't burn, does it? I'd know. I've spent my whole life hiding the same thing behind cheap charms and careful silence. We built different cages out of the same fear, you and I."

"I am nothing like you."

"You're exactly like me. That's why you hate me." Another step. Close enough now to see the fine iron chain at Halveth's throat, hidden beneath her collar — a suppression charm, self-made, worn smooth from years of touching. "You think I came here to fight you. I came here to offer you something no one has ever offered you."

"Which is?"

"A choice." Oscar held out her empty hands. "The covenant is broken, and the wild is coming down on this city like winter itself, and no amount of burning will stop it — you must feel it, even choked down, even bound. The land is waking. When it does, there will be a new pact, and there will be a reckoning for everyone who hunted the gifted. You can be on the wrong side of that reckoning with your ledger and your chains. Or—" she let the word sit, "—you can help me open the stone beneath your own cathedral, and stand on the right side of the only law that's ever actually mattered. The one between people and the world."

Halveth stared at her. Behind her, Dain had gone very still, watching the two of them like a man watching a fire decide which house to take.

"You're asking me to betray everything I've built," Halveth said.

"I'm asking you to stop betraying what you are."

The silence stretched until the lamp guttered. Then Halveth reached up — slowly, as if the motion hurt — and drew the iron chain from around her neck, and laid it on the table between them like a confession.

"The stone's warding is keyed to my office," she said. "My seals will open it. But if anyone learns I helped you, the Crown burns us all, gifted and traitor alike, and they'll do it with my own methods, because I wrote those methods down and taught them to forty men." Her voice cracked, just once, on the last words. "Forty men who trusted me."

"Then we move tonight," Oscar said, "before morning finds out."

Dain rose stiffly from the cot, rubbing his wrists, and managed a crooked smile at the Inquisitor. "For what it's worth," he said, "arresting me was the most honest thing the Crown's done in years."

"Shut up, Corvel," said Halveth — but she picked up the lamp, and led them toward the stairs, and above them, faint through the cathedral's bones, something vast and patient began, very softly, to wake.
