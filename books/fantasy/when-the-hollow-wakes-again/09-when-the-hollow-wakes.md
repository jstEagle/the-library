## 9. When the Hollow Wakes

The old cathedral of Vellamark had burned two hundred years ago, and no one had ever rebuilt it. Its bones still stood — ribs of blackened stone open to the sky, a nave gone to grass and birch saplings pushing up through the cracked flagstones like children through a fence. The city had grown around it and then, embarrassed by it, grown away from it again, until only the desperate and the dreaming came here.

Oscar came because Tam said the dead under the floor had been arguing about it for three days.

"They say the stone is *under*," the girl reported, kneeling with both palms flat against a mossy slab, her eyes rolled back to white. "Under-under. Under the altar where the altar isn't anymore. They're very smug about it. Dead people are smug, did you know that? It's because they already know how everything ends."

"Cheerful," said Dain Corvel, hauling aside a fallen beam with his one good arm. The other was still bound from Halveth's prison; the river-hags' toll had bought his freedom but not his healing, and he moved like a man carrying a sack of wet sand on one shoulder. "Anything else from the departed? Lottery numbers? Weather?"

"They say it's going to snow," Tam said, and opened her eyes, and all three of them looked up through the broken roof at the sky.

It was the color of pewter. The first flakes of the year were already sifting down over the hollow country beyond the walls, and everyone — human, beast, and whatever the feral host counted as — knew what that meant. When the snow sealed the covenant-stone, it would seal it for another hundred years. Another century of winter creeping out of the wild places. Another century of Halveth's ledger, and burnings, and names scraped off census rolls like frost off glass.

Oscar knelt where the altar had been and put her hands against the stone.

She felt it before she heard it: a hum, deep as a heartbeat buried in a hillside. And beneath the hum, faint as a voice carried across water, the old words waiting. She'd been hearing them since the Court of Root and Antler, since the Stag Sovereign had pressed his antlered brow to hers and spoken the truth she'd been fleeing ever since: *the pact requires a living voice on each side. Ours has never been vacant. Yours has.*

"You don't have to do it this way," Dain said quietly. He'd crouched beside her, careful of his arm. "There's always another way. There usually is. I spent eleven years as a lawkeeper learning that the hard way."

"And how many times was the other way just the coward's way with better manners?" Oscar managed a smile. It didn't hold. "Dain. A living voice must be *given*. Not lent, not borrowed, not whispered into the stone and taken back after. Given. All of it." She touched her own throat. "You know what I am. You've known since you caught me robbing your snares and let me go anyway."

"A beast-speaker," he said. "The last one."

"The last one. And if I pour my gift into this stone — every word of crow and fox and river-things, every scrap of the only magic I have ever actually owned — it doesn't come back. I won't hear them anymore. Ever. I'll be as deaf to the wild as any other soul in Vellamark." Her voice cracked on the last words, and she hated it, and let it crack anyway. "And I keep thinking — that's such a small price. That's *nothing*, compared to a hundred years of winter. But it's mine, and it's everything I am, and I'm allowed to be afraid of losing it."

Tam sat down between them, uninvited, the way children do. "The dead want to know something," she said. "They want to know if you'll still talk to *them* after."

Oscar blinked. "I— yes? Of course?"

"Then they say do it." Tam shrugged. "They say you're being dramatic. Their words. Well, one of theirs. He was a bishop. He's very rude for a bishop."

Despite everything, Oscar laughed — a wet, ragged sound that turned halfway into something else. She pulled Tam against her side and pressed her lips to the top of the child's head, breathing in woodsmoke and river-water and the particular smell of a person who had walked too far and slept too little and kept going anyway.

"All right," she said. "All right. Help me move the flagstone."

* * *

The stone beneath took an hour to uncover, and it was nothing like she expected. She'd imagined something grand — a monolith carved with kings and stags, oaths in dead languages. Instead it was a flat river-boulder, ordinary as any in any streambed, worn smooth by centuries of hands touching it. Only when she brushed the moss away did she see the carvings: not words, but pawprints. Bird tracks. The print of a child's bare foot, right beside the print of a grown man's boot, side by side, walking the same direction.

"It was never a treaty between rulers," Oscar breathed. "Look. It's just... people. And animals. Agreeing to walk together."

"That's why they're angry," Dain said softly. "Not because we broke a law. Because we forgot we'd ever made one."

The snow was thickening now, catching in Tam's hair, dusting the boulder white. And Oscar felt them coming before she saw or heard anything — a pressure in the air, in her gift, like the moment before a flock takes wing all at once.

"They're here," she said. "Both of them."

They came from opposite ends of the ruined cathedral, which felt exactly right, exactly like the whole sad shape of the last hundred years.

From the west, through the broken doors: the feral host. Not an army so much as a weather front made of fur and feather and antler — wolves moving with the unhurried certainty of things that have never once been prey, great elks with frost on their backs, crows in numbers that turned the sky above the roofless nave into a boiling black cloth. At their head walked the Stag Sovereign, taller than any natural stag, his crown of bone hung with lichen and small blue flowers that bloomed out of season simply because he passed near them.

From the east, over the crumbled wall: Inquisitor Serane Halveth and thirty soldiers in grey-and-gold, crossbows leveled, breath steaming. Halveth herself walked in front of her men with her iron charms swinging at her belt, and her face was the face of a woman arriving late to a funeral she had spent years pretending wasn't happening.

"Hold!" Halveth called, and her voice rang strangely in the hollow nave. "Delacroix! Step away from the stone. Whatever bargain you think you're striking with beasts, the crown will not honor it. The crown honors *nothing* the wild offers. That is rather the point of the crown."

"You're not wrong," Oscar called back, rising to her feet, placing herself between the stone and the crossbows. "That's exactly the problem."

The Stag Sovereign stopped ten paces away. Up close, his eyes were the brown of turned earth, patient and terribly old. *"Little speaker,"* he said, and his voice filled her skull like roots filling soil. *"The snow is falling. Speak now, or sleep for a century."*

"I know the words," Oscar said. "Maren taught me the frame of them, before—" She stopped. Maren, who had told her to run. Who had probably already been taken. *Later,* she told herself. *Grieve later.* "But the words need a voice given freely. Fully. Not lent. And I need them all to hear me say what it costs, so no one can call it trickery later. Not your courts. Not her crown." She turned to face Halveth across fifty feet of trampled grass. "Do you understand what I'm about to do?"

"I understand you're about to hand our enemies a weapon," Halveth said coldly.

"I'm about to make sure there are no enemies left *to* hand anything to." Oscar's heart was hammering. "Inquisitor. Serane. I know what you are. The hags told me. They taste gifts like wine, and they said yours was the strongest they'd ever tasted, bottled up so tight behind those iron charms that even you can barely feel it anymore." A ripple went through the soldiers. Someone's crossbow wavered. "You hunt us because you hate what you are. I could fight you. I could die fighting you, and Tam could grow up in another century of winters and ledgers and burnings. Or — I could offer you the choice nobody offered either of us."

Halveth's jaw worked. For one naked moment, the Inquisitor of Vellamark looked like a girl holding her breath underwater, and had been for twenty years.

Then she raised one hand, and her soldiers lowered their crossbows — not all the way, but enough.

"Speak your words, Delacroix," Halveth said hoarsely. "I'll decide after."

*"Decide fast,"* rumbled the Stag Sovereign, and the snow fell harder, and Oscar knelt at the stone.

She put both hands flat on the river-boulder. It was warm. Of course it was warm; it had been waiting two hundred years for this, patient as only stones are patient.

She thought of the sparrow she'd healed in the market, its heartbeat stuttering back to life under her palms. She thought of the crows on the rooftop, screaming her name under iron compulsion — *Delacroix, Candle Lane, the stall with the green awning* — and forgave them, again, properly, out loud, because some things needed saying in front of witnesses. She thought of Tam's cold fingers in hers. Of Dain's snare-line, and his sister's name, and every kindness done in secret by frightened people who thought kindness itself was contraband.

Then she began to speak.

The old words rose in her like water finding its level — not a language she'd learned but one she'd always half-known, the tongue underneath all tongues, the one that said *root to rain and hoof to grass and hand to wing, we walk together, we wake together, none of us alone.* And as she spoke, she gave. She felt her gift leave her the way heat leaves a body into cold water — not torn, not stolen, but *poured*, willingly, every syllable of crow-speech and fox-cunning and the deep grammar of growing things flowing down through her palms into the stone.

It hurt. She would tell Tam, later, that it hadn't, because some mercies are lies worth telling, but it hurt like setting down a limb that had been part of her since birth. The world went quiet in layers — birdsong first, then the wind-voices, then the vast slow murmur of trees, then, last and hardest, the near-at-hand chatter of sparrows in the birch saplings, going silent one by one like lamps being shuttered.

By the time she spoke the final word, she was crying without knowing it, and the stone was glowing.

Not gold, not fire — it glowed the green of new leaves, the green of the first spring anything had ever seen, and the light went *down*, into the earth, and then everywhere. Oscar felt the land wake the way you feel a sleeping house wake when the first person rises: a shift, a stir, a thousand small attentions turning on at once. Every rooted thing in Vellamark shivered. The birch saplings in the nave straightened. Grass grew visibly through the snow, green spears shouldering up white. Out beyond the walls, she somehow knew — though she couldn't hear it, couldn't hear *anything* anymore — that every creature in the hollow country had lifted its head at once, and understood, and become witness.

The covenant was no longer a contract between a king and a court. It was written into everything alive. No crown could revoke it, because no crown could reach into the roots of every tree in the world and tear the words out.

The Stag Sovereign bowed his great head to her — lower, she thought, than a sovereign bows to anyone. Then the host turned and flowed away westward like a tide going out, and the snow kept falling, gentle now, ordinary, just snow.

Which left Halveth.

The Inquisitor stood alone in the falling white, her soldiers hanging back, staring at Oscar with an expression caught between fury and drowning. Slowly — watched by thirty crossbows, by one child, by one lame lawkeeper, by every waking thing in the world — her hand went to the iron charms at her belt.

Her fingers closed around them.

And somewhere in the quiet of Oscar's newly silenced mind, she realized she couldn't hear the answer coming — couldn't hear what Halveth was about to say, or what the land might be whispering to her — and for the first time in her life, Oscar Delacroix would have to find out the way everyone else did:

by waiting.
