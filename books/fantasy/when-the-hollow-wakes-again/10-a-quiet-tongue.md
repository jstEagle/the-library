## 10. A Quiet Tongue

Winter broke early that year, and it broke the way an egg breaks—not all at once, but with a hairline crack of warm wind down from the north, and then a slow giving-way, and then green things pushing up through the ash-streets of Vellamark before anyone had agreed to let them.

Oscar watched it happen from the window of what had been Maren's hedge-shop, which was now, by a series of events no one had quite authorized, a school.

The sign had gone up three days ago. Dain had painted it himself, on a board salvaged from the old census office, which everyone agreed was a fitting use for it. It read: **THE DELACROIX SCHOOL OF LISTENING**. Beneath it, in smaller letters that Dain insisted were necessary, someone—almost certainly Tam—had added: *and also arithmetic.*

"You can't run a school on listening alone," Tam had argued, hands planted on hips in a way that made them look alarmingly like Halveth reviewing a ledger. "People will ask what the children are learning, and you'll say 'the voices of all living things,' and they'll say 'yes but can they read.'"

"They're not wrong," Dain had said, and Oscar, who could no more argue with either of them than she could argue with weather, had surrendered.

That had been the strange thing about losing her gift, she was discovering. In the old days she'd have known what every sparrow on the street thought of her sign, what the horses passing her door made of the smell of chalk, what the rats in the wall considered the general state of affairs. Now there was a silence around her, vast and clean as new snow. At first it had felt like deafness. Lately it had begun to feel more like quiet—the kind of quiet a room has after a storm has passed through it.

The silence inside the school, by contrast, was nonexistent. Eleven children sat on benches salvaged from three different chapels, and not one of them was capable of sitting still. A boy named Piet kept a beetle in his pocket that he swore told him where lost coins were; a girl called Essa had been found at age four sleeping in a barrow-mound and hadn't stopped dreaming loudly since; and little Wren—no relation to anything, she insisted, though Oscar had her doubts—could make houseplants lean toward her like sunflowers, which was useless and lovely and exactly the sort of thing the old census would have burned her for.

Under the new covenant, it was worth a scholarship and a hot lunch.

"Teacher Oscar," said Piet, hand raised, beetle in the other fist. "The beetle wants to know if it's lunchtime."

"The beetle asks that every hour."

"He's very committed to the question."

"It is not lunchtime. Copy your letters."

Oscar walked between the benches while they scratched at their slates, and she caught herself doing the thing she still did, out of habit—listening below the level of hearing, reaching for the hum of small minds, finding nothing, and then, oddly, finding something anyway. Because here was the truth nobody had warned her about: children who talked to animals glanced up when a moth passed the window. Children who heard the dead went still when the floorboards settled. She couldn't hear their gifts anymore, but she could read them the way she'd once read weather—a tilt of the head, a held breath, a whole conversation happening just past the edge of the world.

She had lost the beasts' language. She had never lost fluency in this one.

* * *

Tam came to the licensing on a bright cold morning in early spring, wearing formal clothes that fit badly and a expression that fit worse—terrified, mostly, under a thin varnish of dignity.

The ceremony was held in the cathedral square, in the shadow of the ruins that no one had rebuilt, because rebuilding them felt rude. The covenant-stone stood where it had always stood, beneath the broken arches, except that now it was visible, and warm to the touch, and faintly green, as if spring had struck a private deal with it months ahead of schedule. Moss grew on it in the shape, some claimed, of antlers. Others claimed it looked like nothing at all and that people should stop romanticizing moss.

Dignitaries had come. Half the feral court had come too, which made the dignitaries nervous, since half the feral court meant a stag the size of a haywagon standing at the edge of the square being patiently rained on by blossoms from a birch tree that had leaned down to attend.

The Stag Sovereign did not speak. He didn't need to. His presence said everything the covenant needed saying: *we came, we are watching, we remember.*

Master of ceremonies fell to the new Lord Keeper of Records—which was to say, Inquisitor Serane Halveth, pardoned, re-titled, and visibly uncomfortable in robes of soft grey instead of iron-buckled black.

She read from the registry. That was the point of it, now. What had been a ledger of the hunted had been re-bound, page by careful page, into a record of apology—every name Halveth had entered during the hunt copied out in full, followed by the words *wrongly taken*, followed by whatever amends had been made or promised. She had done most of the copying herself. She refused help with it. Oscar understood why. Some penances you have to carry with your own arms.

"Tam of the Hollow Country," Halveth read, and her voice did not waver, though her eyes flicked once to the stone and away, quick as a guilty thing. "First licensed speaker under the renewed covenant. Sworn to hear truly, to speak gently, and to serve as bridge between the courts of human and wild."

Tam stepped forward. Oscar's chest did something complicated.

The child put one small hand on the stone—it hummed, softly, the way a cat hums—and repeated the oath in a clear voice. Then, because Tam was Tam, they added an amendment.

"I also swear to keep doing arithmetic. Somebody has to."

A laugh went up from the crowd, and the birch tree dropped blossoms directly onto Halveth's head, which everyone pretended not to see, and which Oscar chose to interpret as a blessing.

Afterward, Halveth found Oscar at the edge of the square. They stood together in awkward silence for a moment, two women who had once been each other's ruin and were now, improbably, each other's witnesses.

"I never apologized properly," Halveth said at last.

"You copied eleven hundred names by hand."

"That was justice. Apology is different."

"Is it?"

"Yes." Halveth looked at the stone, at the blossoms, at the stag already fading back into the treeline like smoke deciding to be elsewhere. "I hated what I was, so I hunted it in everyone else. You offered me a door instead of a fight, and I've spent a year trying to deserve that." Her jaw tightened. "I'm still trying. I expect I'll be trying a long time."

"Good," said Oscar. "The trying is the apology. The rest is just words."

Halveth almost smiled. It was a strange sight, like frost deciding to become dew. "The crow's been on your roof again," she said, and walked away before Oscar could answer.

* * *

It was true. The crow had been on her roof—or her windowsill, or her chimney, or the school's sign, where it perched some mornings looking down at the children filing in with the proprietary air of a landlord collecting rent in an unusual currency.

Oscar fed it anyway. Of course she fed it anyway. Some habits are older than grief.

She knew this crow. She was almost sure of it—the notch in its left wing, the insolent tilt of its head, the way it took bread from her hand without ever quite touching her fingers, maintaining the professional distance of an old business partner. It had been among those the iron charms had compelled, years ago, screaming her name and street into Serane Halveth's ledger. She had forgiven them within the hour. The crows had never forgiven themselves, which was a very crow-like problem, and she suspected this one had appointed itself her sentinel as a form of ongoing restitution.

The trouble was, she couldn't understand a word it said.

*Caw*, it would say. *Caw-caw.* And then a longer sound, low and layered, that her ears registered as noise but her chest registered as meaning—like hearing music in a language she'd forgotten, and understanding the sadness anyway.

Today it landed on the windowsill of the little room above the school where she lived, and it did not ask for bread. It stood looking at her with one bright eye, and then it made the long sound, and then it ducked its head and made a shorter one, softer, and waited.

And Oscar Delacroix—who had given her voice to a stone, who had poured her gift into the earth like water into cracked ground, who had been silent to the wild for a year and would be silent for all the rest of her years—felt the meaning arrive anyway. Not through her ears. Not through the old channels, which were closed and would stay closed. Through somewhere lower and older than either. Through the place in her that had always listened, before it had ever spoken.

*You are remembered,* the crow said, in the only language left to them both.

And then, softer: *Spring came early. We counted. It counts as your doing.*

Oscar laughed, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and reached for the bread.

"I know," she told the crow, in plain human speech, inadequate and sufficient. "I know exactly what you mean."

The crow took the bread, and stayed a while on the sill, and neither of them needed to say anything else. Outside, the sounds of the school drifted up—children laughing, a slate clattering, Tam's voice patiently explaining long division to Piet and, presumably, to the beetle. The city smelled of rain and woodsmoke and green things. Somewhere beyond the walls, the hollow country lay open and awake, every rooted thing a witness now, every creature party to the pact, the land itself keeping watch so that no crown, no ledger, no fear would ever again claim it belonged to anyone alone.

Oscar sat down at her desk, opened the school register to a fresh page, and wrote the day's date at the top.

Then, after a moment's thought, she added a second line, the only motto the school had ever officially needed:

*Listen first. Everything else follows.*

Below the window, the first student of the morning arrived, knocked, and called up through the glass, "Teacher Oscar? There's a fox in the yard and it says it has an appointment."

Oscar smiled, and capped her ink, and went down to hear what it had come to say.
