## 1. The Weight of Unpaid Ink

The trouble with cataloguing miracles is that miracles don't keep regular hours, don't file proper manifests, and above all — this is the part the Guild never quite grasps, despite two centuries of institutional wisdom and a truly obscene collection of ledgers — miracles do not appreciate being *inventoried*.

Take the thunder.

It sat on Shelf Fourteen, Row C, in a bottle the size of a fist, and it had been sulking there for eleven years. Bottled thunder, acquired from a storm-witch in the Greymarch who'd traded it for three hens and an apology. The manifest described it as *Atmospheric Residue, Convulsive, one (1) unit, provenance disputed*. Provenance disputed because the storm-witch had stolen it from someone else, who had stolen it from a church, which had — if you believed the marginalia in the acquisition ledger, written by some long-dead appraiser with a taste for gossip — shaken it loose from the sky during a wedding nobody wanted.

Every quarter I was required to certify that the thunder remained contained. Every quarter I climbed the ladder, unstoppered my inkwell, dipped my pen, and wrote *Item verified intact* beneath the last ninety-odd entries of *Item verified intact*, while the thunder pressed against the glass like a cat against a window, watching me write.

"Nobody's going anywhere," I told it. It did not answer. Thunder rarely does, once it's been bottled. That's rather the point of bottling it.

My name is Ulyana Coldharbour, and I am — was, is, the grammar of debt resists tense — an indentured scribe of the Ledger Guild of Vaskarand, Third Rank, Cataloguing Division, at your service and considerably at the Guild's. My mother sold me into indenture at nine years old for the price of a winter's coal and a doctor who could actually read. This is not a sob story; it is an accounting entry. Entry four thousand two hundred and eleven, in fact: *Coldharbour, U., minor, transferred to Guild custody. Assets received: coal (1 cord), medical services (1). Outstanding balance: incalculable.*

I have spent nineteen years trying to calculate it anyway. It's what I'm good at. Numbers behave. Numbers stay where you put them, which is more than can be said for most things in this building.

* * *

The Hall of Acquisitions occupied the guildhouse's second floor, all oak shelves and iron ladders and windows set high enough that the light came down like an afterthought. Vaskarand fog pressed against the glass, the particular grey-brown fog that the city brewed from river smoke and chimney soot and something older underneath, though nobody liked to say what the something older might be. The fog got into everything. It got into the books. Some of the oldest volumes in our care had gone soft at the edges, as though they were slowly deciding to become weather themselves.

I worked the morning shift alone, which suited me. Cataloguing is a conversation you have with objects, and objects are better conversationalists than most people — they hold still, and they don't ask about your balance.

"Good morning," I said to the saint's shadow.

It did not answer either, but then, shadows are shy. It lay coiled in its reliquary box like a length of black ribbon, and even at this hour, even in this light, it was darker than anything around it. A saint's second shadow — the one a holy man sheds when he becomes too bright for ordinary darkness to cling to. The Guild had bought it off a monastery in the Salt Reaches for a sum I preferred not to think about, mostly because the interest compounded weekly.

*Item 8841: Shadow, secondary, ecclesiastical. Note: subject wept when parted from it. Note: shadow appears to weep also.*

I wrote *Item verified intact* and added, because it was Tuesday and I was feeling generous, *no change in disposition.*

Below the saint's shadow, a jar of last winter's sunset. Above it, a key that unlocked nothing but opened every door that had ever been locked against its owner — we kept it wrapped in three layers of velvet because it made the junior clerks cry. Farther down, a mirror that showed rooms instead of faces; a coin from a country that no longer existed, though the ledger entry only said *provenance obscure*; a birdcage containing, according to six separate appraisals, silence, and according to my own seventh appraisal, patience.

You learn things, working the shelves. You learn that impossible things are mostly lonely things. You learn that the Guild pays appraisers by the item and scribes by the line, and that the difference between those two rates is where my entire life has gone to die. And you learn — this took me longest — that the ledgers remember everything except what matters. We have records of every miracle that ever crossed these doors, cross-referenced and annotated and bound in calfskin, and not one page anywhere tells you what any of it *felt like*.

That used to strike me as a flaw in the system.

Lately it strikes me as the system working exactly as intended. But I'm getting ahead of myself, and ahead of myself is somewhere I can't afford to be. Interest accrues on anticipation too. Everything accrues here. It's the house style.

* * *

At noon I went down to the counting room to settle my quarterly reckoning with Mistress Ferrow, who managed the debt rolls the way other women manage gardens: with patience, pruning shears, and a certain grim satisfaction in what grew.

She had my ledger open before I reached her desk. Of course she did. Mistress Ferrow had my ledger open before I woke up, I sometimes suspected. Possibly before I was born.

"Coldharbour." She didn't look up. Her pen moved with the small, dry sounds of arithmetic. "Do you know what a *week* is?"

"It's seven days, mistress."

"It's seven days in which interest compounds," she said, "and you have submitted your hours late twice this quarter. Twice." She turned the ledger toward me. There it was, my life in columns: wages credited, rations debited, lodging debited, candle allowance debited — yes, they charged me for the candles I catalogued by — tuition for the paleography course I'd taken at twenty because better hand meant better pay, which it did, which is why the tuition had seemed wise, which is how the Guild gets you, everything wise costs and everything costly compounds — until the final figure sat at the bottom of the page like a stone at the bottom of a well.

I looked at the number. The number looked back. We had this arrangement, the number and I. Nineteen years, and in nineteen years the figure had never once gone down. It had plateaued twice, during good seasons, the way a fever plateaus.

"I'll take extra inventory shifts," I said.

"You'll take whatever shifts are assigned to you." She blotted the page. "Though as it happens, there is a night audit coming. Full physical inventory, Sub-Cellar through Attic. Extra credit against principal for volunteers." Now she looked up, and her eyes had the flat shine of a woman about to offer something that benefited her far more than me. "Volunteers work nights. Alone."

Alone meant quiet. Quiet meant I could think, and thinking was the one luxury the Guild hadn't figured out how to debit.

"When does it start?"

"Tomorrow night." She closed my ledger with a small decisive thump, the sound of my next three months being decided without my input, which is the traditional method. "Sign the roster on your way out. And Coldharbour—" I paused at the door. She was already writing again, already gardening. "Your handwriting is the only clean thing about your account. Try to keep it that way. Clean hands, clear pages. In this house, that's worth more than honesty."

I signed the roster. My name looked strange under the roster's rules — the letters cramped to fit the column, the flourish amputated. Even my signature was in debt.

* * *

The rest of the day went the way days go here: slowly, and slightly downhill. I recatalogued a drawer of letters addressed to people who had died before sending them, which the Guild classifies under *Correspondence, Undeliverable, Potentially Haunted*. I certified a jar of a drowned man's last breath — *Item verified intact; contents agitated; recommend no singing nearby*. I ate my supper ration standing at the shelf ladder, because sitting costs time and time costs money and money, in my case, was theoretical.

It was near the ninth bell when I came to the old acquisition ledgers — the deep back-catalogue, the ones kept in the cold cabinet because the vellum goes brittle otherwise. Quarterly certification requires spot-checks of the historical entries, matching old descriptions against current items, and so I pulled Volume Nine, dated a hundred and forty years back, and began the dull sacred work of comparison.

That was when I found the entry.

*Item 2210: Name, fragmentary, one syllable, recovered from ruins. Provenance: [illegible]. Disposition: transferred to Master Ledger. Appraiser's note: do not speak aloud. Do not transcribe in full. Value: incalculable.*

Nothing remarkable about it, except everything. Except that the hand was mine.

Not similar. Mine. The particular forward lean of my capitals. The way my *h*s loop shut like little padlocks. The tiny hook I put on terminal *r*s because a copyist named Dossel mocked my roundhand when I was fourteen and I overcorrected out of spite and it stuck. Nobody else writes an *r* like that. I've checked; checking is my whole profession.

And the date beside it was yesterday.

I stood in the cold cabinet's lamplight with a hundred-and-forty-year-old book open on my knees, reading my own handwriting in an entry dated the day before, describing an item I had never seen, catalogued in a volume I had never touched.

There are moments when the mind does a curious thing: it reaches for the mundane explanation first, the way a drowning man reaches for driftwood that cannot hold him. Ink fades differently in old books; perhaps the archivist who wrote it simply wrote the way I write, the way strangers sometimes share a face. Perhaps a colleague had borrowed my pen. Perhaps — and here the driftwood dissolved — perhaps I had written it and forgotten, because I write thousands of entries and memory is a ledger too and everyone's ledger has gaps.

Except I would remember. Writing is the one thing I do completely. When I make a letter, I am entirely inside it, the way other men are entirely inside prayer or drink. I would know my own strokes the way I know my own teeth.

I copied the entry out — my hand shaking now, the padlock loops rattling open — and carried the transcription to the Master Ledger room to check the disposition claim. If the fragment had been transferred, there would be a receiving mark.

There was not. The Master Ledger's index ran to Item 2210 and stopped, the way an index runs when the thing it indexes was never there. Or was never allowed to be there. Or —

Or had been taken *out*.

I stood a long while in that tall silent room, among shelves of ledgers that held, as far as anyone knew, everything the Guild had ever recorded, and I felt the specific vertigo of a man who checks his accounts nightly and has just found a figure he cannot source. Small thing. One entry. One syllable, even. Recovered from ruins, the note said, and warned itself not to be spoken.

Value: incalculable.

In my experience, when the Guild calls something's value incalculable, it means one of two things. Either they haven't priced it yet.

Or pricing it would cost them everything, and they know it.

I put Volume Nine back in the cold cabinet. I locked it. I checked the lock twice, which is unlike me, and then a third time, which is unlike me more.

Outside the high windows the fog had come down close against the glass, closer than fog usually bothers to press, and somewhere below the floor — below the floors, below the cellars, down where the guildhouse keeps its roots — I heard, or almost heard, or invented entirely, a sound like a very large thing turning over in a sleep that was never very deep.

Tomorrow night the full inventory began. Sub-Cellar through Attic, they'd said. Alone, they'd said.

Nobody had mentioned what was under the Sub-Cellar. Nobody ever does. But ledgers, I reminded myself, walking back up through the darkening stacks, ledgers record everything.

Everything except what matters.

And one entry too many.

* * *

That night I did not sleep so much as lie in my cot rehearsing explanations. A coincidence of hands. An archivist's jest across the centuries. A copying error propagated backward through time, which is nonsense, but comfortable nonsense, and I have learned to budget for comfort the way I budget for candles.

Near morning I dreamed — or half-dreamed, in that grey country between bells — of a door. Not a guild door. A door standing free in snow, in a courtyard, with a bell tower behind it, and a language coming from somewhere beyond it, low and sung, a language I did not know and understood perfectly, and the words it sang were:

*Come home. Come home. The count isn't finished. Nothing is finished that hasn't been counted.*

I woke with ink on my fingers.

Fresh ink. Black, wet, my pen still uncapped on the sill where I had no memory of setting it down, and on the margin of my personal daybook — the private one, the one with my debts and my mother's face and nothing else — in my own hand, dated tomorrow:

*Begin at the beginning. Count everything. He is almost here.*
