## 2. A Boy at the Loading Door

The trouble with night inventory is that everything in the Ledger Guild of Vaskarand is worse in the dark.

By daylight, the acquisition hall has the dignity of a church: vaulted ceiling, brass lamps, the long oak counters where we catalogue the impossible with the same weary competence a fishmonger brings to herring. By lamplight at three in the morning, it becomes something else entirely. The bottled thunder on Shelf Fourteen hums against its cork like a wasp thinking angry thoughts. The saint's second shadow — acquired last spring from a widow in Corvess who swore her husband had no use for it — lies pooled in its glass case like spilled tar, and I would swear on my mother's grave, which is to say on my mother's debt, which is to say on nothing, that it shifts position between counts.

"Item one hundred eleven," I read aloud, because saying things out loud keeps them ordinary. "Bottled thunder, provenance disputed, appraisal pending two hundred and six years."

The thunder hummed. It always hums. Somewhere in this building there is presumably an entry for the day it stops humming, and I do not want to be the scribe holding the pen when that happens.

I made a mark in the margin. My hand ached. It always aches. Eleven years of copying other people's acquisitions into ledgers will do that — you develop a callus shaped like your whole misfortune, a small hard ridge on the middle finger of your right hand that I have privately named Havel, after the guildmaster, because it seems only fair that he should be present for every hour of my labor in some form.

My debt, as of this inventory: four thousand, two hundred and ninety-one marks, nine coppers. As of yesterday it had been four thousand, two hundred and eighty-eight marks, four coppers. The guild charges interest in a currency of its own invention — not just coin but *time*, not just time but *attention*. Every hour I spend cataloguing is billed to me at a rate calculated by men whose entire profession is making arithmetic feel like weather. You don't notice the rain starting. You just look up one day and you're drowning.

I had been drowning for eleven years, since the week after my mother died and I discovered that grief makes terrible collateral and excellent leverage. The guild had lent me the funeral. The guild had lent me the winter after. The guild had lent me, in a moment of weakness I still flinch remembering, a new coat, which they had repossessed the following spring when I missed a payment, so that for one humiliating week I did inventory in a shirt with holes in it while Senior Archivist Maren looked through me as though I were an empty shelf.

The coat was returned to me eventually. That's the thing about the Ledger Guild. They never take anything outright. They take it and then sell it back to you, slowly, at interest, until you can no longer remember whether anything you own was ever truly yours — including, I sometimes suspect, the contents of your own head.

But that's morbid, and it's three in the morning, and morbid plus tired is how mistakes happen. So I counted the bottled thunder again, and I counted the second shadow again (it had moved; I chose not to enter that), and I worked my way down the aisles toward the loading door, where the night porter's post sat empty, because the night porter, Toma, had called in sick with what he described as "a moral objection to Tuesdays," and no one had replaced him because replacing him would cost money, and the guild's relationship with money is the relationship of a dragon to gold: acquisitive, jealous, and entirely without use.

That's why I heard the knock myself.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a fist makes when the fist belongs to someone who isn't sure knocking is allowed.

I stood very still among the shelves, the way you do, and told myself it was the wind. The loading door of the Ledger Guild faces the Sump Canal, and the canal wind gets up strange ideas at night, pressing its mouth against every crack in the building and whispering. Except wind doesn't knock in threes. Wind doesn't wait, and then knock again.

I took down the ledger lamp and went to see what wanted borrowing.

* * *

He was maybe seven years old, though it's hard to judge age in a child who looks like he's been excavated rather than dressed. Mud had gotten everywhere mud could reach and several places philosophy says it shouldn't — behind his ears, under his eyelids, in the whorls of his fingerprints, so that when he raised his hand to knock again his knuckles left brown crescents on the iron door. His clothes had once been fine. That was the first wrong thing I noticed, standing there with my lamp: the coat was good wool gone gray with wear, cut in a style I didn't recognize, with buttons of some pale material I wanted to say was bone and refused to say was bone. Fine clothes, ruined thoroughly, worn by a boy who had walked a long way through somewhere wet.

The canal gate was locked. I checked it later, twice, and it was locked both times. The bolts were drawn, the chain was fastened, and the water beyond was black and flat as poured ink. There are exactly two ways into that loading yard: over the wall, which stands twelve feet and is topped with the kind of ironwork that makes burglars consider other careers, or through the door, which opens inward, from inside, and had been shut since Toma left.

So: a boy standing in a locked yard, in front of a bolted door, at an hour when even the thunder sleeps badly.

"You're late for inventory," I said, because apparently my survival instinct had clocked out early along with Toma.

He looked up at me. He had the particular stillness children get when they've been frightened for so long that fear has stopped being an event and become a climate. And then he said something.

It wasn't Vaskarandi. It wasn't Low Corvessian or trade-tongue or any of the harbor pidgins I'd picked up doing customs entries for the guild's less reputable shipments. It was a language with too many soft sounds in it, vowels that opened like doors, and it went through the bars of the grate and into the yard and out of his mouth in a shape that was unmistakably a sentence, unmistakably addressed to me, and utterly without meaning.

Except.

Here is the part I have not told anyone, and am telling you now only because you are a book and books keep their mouths shut. When he spoke, something behind my sternum turned over. Not recognition exactly. Recognition is meeting a face you know. This was more like — you know how you can walk into a room you've never entered and know where the window is? Some animal certainty older than memory? It was that. His voice hit a note and some instrument in me that I did not know I carried answered it, one low hum, and then went quiet as if embarrassed.

"I don't understand you," I said, which was true and insufficient.

He tried again. Different words, same language. And this time — I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only thing the guild ever taught me that turned out to be worth owning — this time I almost understood. Not the words. The *shape* of them. There was a rising lilt at the end that meant a question, I'd have sworn it on anything, and the question had the cadence of *are you going to help me* or possibly *is it safe here*, and neither of those is translatable from a language that has supposedly never existed.

"Where are your parents?" I asked, stupidly, in Vaskarandi, as if repetition across languages were a crowbar.

He answered in his impossible tongue, and whatever he said, he said it looking past me, into the hall behind my shoulder, at all those shelves of bottled impossibilities, and his face did something complicated — hope and horror fighting for the same ground — before settling into the flat patience of a child who has learned adults mostly mean well and rarely manage it.

I should have fetched the porter. There was no porter. I should have fetched Maren. Maren slept in the archivists' quarters on the third floor and had made it abundantly clear over eleven years that she considered my existence a clerical error. I should have closed the grate and gone back to counting thunder and let whoever's problem this was remain whoever's problem this was.

Instead I unbolted the door.

Understand, I want this on the record, in whatever ledger keeps track of my stupidity: I knew better. The first rule of the Ledger Guild, printed inside every apprentice's primer in letters you could read from across a street, is *Nothing Enters Unappraised.* No object, no document, no creature. Everything that crosses the threshold gets weighed, valued, entered, and owned. I had watched them do it to a crate of singing moths. I had watched them do it, God help me, to a man — a debtor like me, who'd tried to pay off his account with a family heirloom that turned out to be a sleeping angel, and the guild had taken the angel and repossessed the man right along with it, since he was, technically, in proximity.

And I opened the door and let a child walk into that.

In my defense — and I have been assembling defenses for years, it's practically a hobby — I did not think of it as letting him in. I thought of it as keeping the cold out. There is a difference, I would learn, and the difference would be measured in blood and ink, but at three in the morning with a mud-caked boy shivering on the wrong side of an iron door, the difference felt like mercy.

He stepped past me into the acquisition hall and stopped dead, and I watched him take in the shelves — the thunder, the shadow, the reliquary drawers, the jars of weather — and I watched him begin, quietly and completely, to cry. Not sobbing. Just crying, the way stone weeps groundwater, steadily, without expression, tears cutting clean tracks down through the mud.

"It's all right," I said. "You're all right." Both statements false. Neither statement mine to make. But I crouched down to his height anyway, and set the lamp where he could see my face, and held out my hand palm-up the way you do with strange dogs and stranger children, and after a moment he put his muddy fingers in mine.

His hand was freezing. His pulse was fast and thin. And underneath the mud, on the inside of his wrist, there was a mark — a small scar, deliberate, surgical, in the shape of a circle with a line struck through it, like a crossed-out sun.

I knew that shape.

I want to be careful here, because knowing is a strong word and I am, professionally speaking, a weak man. Let me say instead: the shape was familiar in the way the boy's voice was familiar. It lived in the same cellar of me, next to that answering hum. A crossed-out sun. I had never seen it before in my life, and some part of me that predates my life recognized it the way a bell recognizes its own clapper.

"Ulyana."

Maren's voice, from the top of the stairs. She stood in her nightdress with a candle, sixty-three years old and carved out of the same material as the guild's counters, looking down at us with an expression I had never seen on her before and would not be able to name for a long time. It took me a while to identify it, and when I finally did, it frightened me more than the boy in the locked yard had.

It was grief. Fresh grief, arriving ahead of its reason.

"Go back to bed, Maren," I said. "It's just a lost child."

"There is no such thing," Maren said, "in this building." And she came down the stairs anyway, slowly, holding the rail, watching the boy the way you watch a tide come in.

* * *

Guildmaster Havel arrived within the hour, which should have told me something right there. Havel did not get out of bed for fires. Havel had famously slept through the collapse of the west archive in the flood year, and been annoyed about the noise. But a mud-caked child at the loading door brought him down in forty minutes, fully dressed, beard combed, eyes bright with an appetite I mistook at first for concern.

He circled the boy twice, hands clasped behind his back, while the boy sat wrapped in a counter blanket at the appraiser's table, clutching a cup of broth he wouldn't drink, and Maren stood by the wall with her arms folded, and I stood by the shelves pretending to be furniture.

"No identification," Havel murmured, half to himself. "No entry in the census rolls — I've sent for confirmation. Found in a locked yard. Speaking—" He stopped. Started again. "Say something else, boy."

The boy looked at me first. That's what I remember most clearly afterward, when I replayed it, which I did many times: he looked at *me*, not Havel, as if I were the one giving orders, as if I were the one whose answer mattered. Then he spoke, that door-voweled language, soft and unhurried, maybe six words, and the hum in my chest stirred again like something turning over in sleep.

Havel went very still.

Then he smiled, and I had worked for the man eleven years and had never seen that smile, and I want to go on record again, book, because you're the only colleague I'll have left by the end: that smile was the single worst thing I saw in all the years that followed, including the things that came later, including the things beneath the city. Because Havel's ordinary expressions were greed and irritation, and both of those are honest. This was delight, and delight from a man like Havel meant that something had arrived which was worth more than everything else in the building combined.

"Do you know what he's saying?" I asked.

"No," said Havel, pleasantly, lying. "But I know what he's *worth*." He turned to me, brisk now, the transactional warmth of a man closing a deal. "Coldharbour. Fetch the transfer ledger. We'll need a provisional entry."

"Entry for what?"

"For him." Havel gestured at the child the way he gestured at crates. "An unclaimed asset of unknown origin, found on guild property, containing—" and here he paused, and I watched him choose his word with the care of a jeweler setting a stone, "—content of considerable interest. He goes in the vault."

"The *vault*?"

"For appraisal." Havel was already moving toward the stairs, already elsewhere in his mind, already spending money the boy hadn't earned yet. "Standard procedure, Coldharbour. Secure storage pending valuation. You know the drill."

"He's a child. Not an *acquisition*."

"He's an undocumented minor of unknown linguistic origin discovered inside a locked facility at three in the morning." Havel stopped at the foot of the stairs and looked back, and for one moment the pleasantness dropped away and I saw the actual machinery underneath, the gears that turned behind the guild's face, patient and enormous and cold. "Which makes him either a miracle or a liability, Coldharbour, and in this building those are the same category. Vault. Now."

The boy didn't struggle. That was the part that broke something in me, quietly, irreversibly. He understood — you could see him understanding — that the tall man's decision was already made, that the words flying around above his head were about him and none of them were *to* him, and he had been through this before, wherever he'd come from, however he'd gotten here. He slid off the chair, took my hand without being asked, and walked with me toward the vault stairs like a small gentleman attending his own funeral.

Halfway there he tugged my sleeve. I crouched. He pointed at my chest, then at his own, then pressed his muddy palm flat over his heart, and said something in the dead language — three words, gentle, deliberate — and waited, watching my face with those excavated eyes.

And the hum in my chest rose all the way up into my throat, and for one vertiginous second I nearly *answered*. Not in Vaskarandi. In whatever was buried in me, under the debts, under the eleven years, under the name — some syllable-shaped thing stirring, reaching, almost forming —

"What did you say?" I whispered.

He shook his head. Not *I won't tell you*. More like *you wouldn't hear it yet.*

They put him in Vault Nine, the deep cell with the iron door and no lamp bracket, because the vault cells were built for objects and objects don't need light. I argued. I used up a full year's worth of goodwill arguing, and got the blanket kept, and a stool, and a promise that the boy would be fed, and nothing else. Havel posted a guard on the door — a double guard, which for a seven-year-old was its own kind of confession — and went upstairs to send messages I would later wish to God I had intercepted.

At dawn I signed the provisional entry myself, because I was junior and it was my shift and that is how the guild works: the smallest hands hold the pen when the ink might stain.

*Asset 4,417. Unknown male child, approx. seven years. Recovered loading yard, condition poor. Language: unclassified. Contents: pending appraisal.*

Four thousand, four hundred and seventeen. I noticed the number, of course. I notice numbers the way other people notice faces. Asset 4,417 — and my debt was four thousand, two hundred and ninety-one. Close enough that the coincidence itched. Close enough that I wrote the boy's entry directly beneath the running total of my own ruin, in my own handwriting, and felt — I couldn't have explained it then — like the two lines were leaning toward each other in the dark, like figures in a sum that hadn't finished adding itself yet.

Before I capped the ink, I went down one last time to Vault Nine. Through the grate, by lamplight, the boy lay curled on the stool with the blanket around his shoulders, awake, watching the door. Waiting for me, I realized. Or waiting for whoever came. Hard to tell the difference, in a child that calm.

"Sleep," I said, uselessly, kindly, in the wrong language.

He closed his eyes. And just before sleep took him, he murmured something — one word, this time, soft as snowfall — and though I did not know the word, did not know the tongue, did not know the country it belonged to or the century it had died in, I swear to you that some cellar-deep part of me heard it perfectly, and translated, and the translation was:

*Home.*
