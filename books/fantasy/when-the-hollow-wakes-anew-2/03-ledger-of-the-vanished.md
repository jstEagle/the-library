## 3. Ledger of the Vanished

The boy had been in the vault for six days when I finally worked up the courage to admit I couldn't stop thinking about him.

I want to be clear about what a professional embarrassment that was. The Ledger Guild of Vaskarand does not encourage thinking. Thinking is what apprentices do before they learn better; thinking is how clerks end up questioning entries instead of copying them; and questioning entries, as old Corvus used to say while rapping my knuckles with a ruler, is how clerks end up *unemployed*. The guild's whole architecture — physical, financial, spiritual — was built on the principle that a ledger records and a scribe does not. We are pens. Pens do not wonder.

And yet there I was, third night running, sitting in my garret above the tannery with a candle burning down to its collarbones, trying to reconstruct from memory the sounds the boy had made at the loading door.

*Vess-ka-torren. Ilum vesk. Ilum vesk, ama.*

Six days, and I could still hear it. That's not normal. Scribes have good memories for text and terrible ones for sound; we live in ink, not air. But those syllables had lodged somewhere behind my sternum like a fishhook, and every attempt to pull them free only set them deeper.

So I did the thing a responsible debt-bound employee absolutely should not do.

I went to the archive.

* * *

A word about the archive, because people who haven't been inside tend to imagine something grand — vaulted ceilings, shafts of dust-gilded light, that sort of nonsense. In truth it was three subterranean floors of cramped shelving beneath the counting hall, lit by lamps that smoked more than they burned, maintained by an archivist named Maren who had worked there so long she'd begun to smell faintly of vellum. Maren was seventy if she was a day, with hands like dried riverbeds and eyes that catalogued you the moment you entered: subject, condition, estimated value.

"Ulyana Coldharbour," she said, before I'd said anything at all. "Debt class three. You're not authorized for the deep stacks."

"I'm not asking for the deep stacks."

"You're standing like someone asking for the deep stacks."

I looked down at my own posture, betrayed by my own spine. "There's a language," I said. "Spoken by — an acquisition. Recent. It's not in any of the standard grammars. I checked Vaskari, Old Vaskari, coastal trade pidgin, the mountain tongues, and the liturgical forms."

Something happened to Maren's face then. I want to describe it accurately because it matters: nothing happened to her face. She went still the way water goes still, which is to say completely, and in that complete stillness there was a kind of motion, the way a pond goes motionless precisely because something moved beneath it.

"Describe the acquisition," she said.

"A child. Male, perhaps eight years. Arrived at the loading door during night inventory. The guildmaster has ordered him held for appraisal."

"For appraisal," Maren repeated. Her voice did something complicated around the word. "And the guildmaster heard him speak this language himself?"

"He gave the order personally."

"Then it's true," she said, mostly to her shelves. "After all these years." Then, briskly, as though she hadn't: "Third floor. Case forty-one through sixty. Anything older than the founding of the current guild charter. Don't touch case fifty-eight without gloves, and don't read case sixty-three at all."

"What's in case sixty-three?"

"That's the point of not reading it," said Maren, and turned back to her ledgers, and our interview was over.

* * *

From *Guild Acquisition Records, Vol. IX* (undated; binding predates the current charter):

> *Item 114. One (1) grammar, hand-copied, 340 folios, titled in a script resembling no living alphabet. Vendor claims descent from "the northern kingdom." Vendor could not name the kingdom when asked. Vendor became distressed when asked. Vendor wept for eleven minutes without being able to say why. Item purchased for 4 crowns and one forgotten afternoon.*

> *Item 115. One (1) song, transcribed phonetically by the acquiring clerk. Clerk notes: "The melody is simple, almost childlike. I cannot stop humming it. I have never heard it before. I am certain I have never heard it before."*

> *Item 116. Three (3) maps of the northern coast. All three show identical coastline. All three omit the bay. The cartographer has drawn the bay anyway — a blank space where the water should be, ringed with the notation "here was" in his own hand, though he swears he wrote no such thing.*

Here was.

I sat in the lamplight with item after item stacked in front of me, and I felt the first cold finger of vertigo slide down the back of my neck, because I was reading about a hole. Not a gap in the record — the guild's records were full of gaps, gaps were inventory like anything else — but a hole in the world itself. A place where a kingdom had been, and where now there was only a coastline with a blank space in it and a cartographer crying over a notation he didn't remember making.

Every acquisition followed the same pattern. A vendor arrives with something — a coin, a christening gown, a jar of honey, a child's shoe — claiming it comes from somewhere north, or sometimes northeast, or sometimes just *away*. Asked to name the place, they can't. Asked to point to it on a map, their finger stalls. They know the object. They know it came from *there*. There is simply no *there* left in them to point from.

One entry, dated a hundred and ninety years back, recorded a man who arrived carrying a bell rope. Just the rope, frayed at one end, smelling of smoke. He stood in the receiving hall and rang the rope as though ringing a bell that wasn't there, and made a sound with his mouth to match it, and forty clerks wrote down in their private journals that night that they had dreamed of snow. The entry ends: *Item purchased. Vendor retained on staff. Vendor's name unrecorded. Vendor no longer remembers having a name.*

*Vendor no longer remembers having a name.* Written down, filed, indexed, and never once questioned in a hundred and ninety years, because the guild does not question entries. The guild records. The guild does not ask why a man would forget his own name, or why nobody found that alarming, or why the alarm itself seemed to have been erased along with everything else.

I rubbed my eyes until I saw stars that weren't there. Somewhere above me, muffled through three floors of stone, the city of Vaskarand went about its evening: carts, bells, the fishwives shouting, the ordinary gorgeous noise of ten thousand people who all knew their own names.

*Vess-ka-torren,* I thought. *Ilum vesk.*

And underneath my ribs, in a voice that wasn't mine and somehow was, something answered.

* * *

Case forty-one held the grammars. Most were copies of copies, the script degrading generation by generation like a face reflected down a corridor of mirrors. But the oldest one — undated, unsigned, its leather cover gone soft as skin — opened onto a page headed with a word I could suddenly, impossibly, read.

Not because I knew the language. Because the word was a name, and names are hooks, and this one caught on something deep in me that I had no memory of putting there.

**Ostmere.**

I said it out loud, quietly, in the dark of the stacks, and the lamp flame leaned toward me. I'm going to note that plainly: the flame *leaned*, the way a plant leans toward light, and I told myself afterward that it was a draft, because the alternative was to leave the archive immediately and I wasn't done yet.

Ostmere. The grammar called it a kingdom. It gave declensions — Ostmere, of Ostmere, to Ostmere, O Ostmere, a vocative form, which means somewhere in that dead country people had stood on hillsides and called the name of their home aloud, the way you'd call to a beloved across a field. The grammar gave proverbs. *"Ilum vesk, ama torren"* — the phrase the boy had cried at the loading door, and here it was, two hundred years old, meaning *the snow keeps faith, little one*. A lullaby fragment. A mother's words.

The boy had come to our door speaking his mother's words into a country that had forgotten his mother's country existed.

I kept reading. The grammar's later pages grew strange. Marginalia in different hands, decades apart, all circling the same unease:

*"Copied from the original at [word obscured]. The original was destroyed? No — I wrote 'destroyed,' but that is not right. Unwritten. The original was unwritten. I do not know what I mean by this."*

*"Asked the guildmaster why we keep books of a country no map acknowledges. He said: because a debt must be documented even when the debtor is forgotten. Especially then. I did not sleep well."*

*"My predecessor wrote in this margin that the kingdom fell. I have crossed out 'fell.' I do not know what to write instead. There is no verb for what happened. There is no verb, and I think the absence of the verb is the whole of the matter."*

I put the book down. My hands weren't steady. Outside, muffled and distant, the evening bells of Vaskarand rang the hour, and I counted them out of habit — six, seven, eight — and realized I was counting because some part of me wanted to confirm that time still worked normally, that bells still meant hours, that the world was still stitched together in the ordinary way.

Then I went looking for maps.

* * *

The guild kept a full survey of the northern coast, commissioned eighty years ago, beautiful work, every inlet named. I laid it flat under the lamp and ran my finger up the coastline, past the salt marshes, past the grey fens, to where the grammar said a kingdom with a harbor and a bell tower and snow that kept faith had stood.

Blank space. Not empty sea, not uncharted territory with the honest hatching cartographers use — blank. A rectangle of untouched paper, as if the surveyor had reached that stretch of coast and simply stopped being able to see. And in the corner of the blankness, in faded iron-gall ink, a notation in the surveyor's hand:

*Here was.*

Two words. Present tense hiding inside past tense. Here, *was*. The paper remembered even though the man hadn't.

I fetched the other surveys. Forty years apart. Sixty. A hundred and twenty. Every one, the same blank, the same ghost-note in different handwriting, as though each surveyor had inherited the compulsion without inheriting the knowledge — *here was, here was, here was* — a message passed hand to hand down two centuries by men who didn't know what they were saying.

And then, on the oldest chart, one more line, smaller, squeezed into the margin as if written quickly and in fear:

*It was struck. Not destroyed — struck. Ask the ledgers. Do not ask anyone alive. No one alive knows, and asking teaches them to forget.*

I sat back on the stool. The lamp smoked. Somewhere in the dark between the shelves, the archive breathed its dry paper breath, and I understood — in the way you understand things at midnight, understanding that dissolves by morning but leaves sediment — that I was not researching an acquisition.

I was reading the autopsy of a world. And whoever had killed it had been thorough enough to murder the verb *to fall* along with everything else.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that my candle needed trimming twice. Long enough that when footsteps came down the archive stair, I flinched like a thief, which was accurate, since that's what I was.

Maren appeared at the end of the aisle with a lamp of her own, and she didn't look surprised to find me surrounded by open cases and stolen knowledge. She looked tired. She looked tired the way the sea looks heavy — a settled, permanent weight.

"Case sixty-three," she said.

"I didn't—"

"No. You didn't. You're not ready for sixty-three." She came closer, set her lamp beside mine, and looked down at the spread of charts, the blank space, the marginal scrawl. For a long moment she said nothing at all.

"Maren," I said. "What was Ostmere?"

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, and her expression was the strangest I've ever seen on a human face: grief with nowhere to attach itself, mourning with no corpse, tears for a country she could not have told me one fact about.

"I don't know," she said. "That's the horror of it, Ulyana. I've worked in this archive for fifty years. I've read every word you've read tonight and a thousand worse. I know its grammar, its proverbs, the shape of its harbor. I know that its kings were crowned with river-pearls and that its children sang a song about snow keeping faith." Her voice cracked, very slightly, like ice under a boot. "Ask me where it was and I can point. Ask me what it was and I can recite. Ask me *that it was*—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, hard, as if holding a door shut against something. "It's like a tooth pulled long ago. The tongue keeps going back to the gap."

"The boy," I said. "The acquisition. He speaks it. Fluently. Natively."

"Yes."

"He's what — eight years old? Nothing natively speaks a language two hundred years dead unless—"

"Unless the country isn't finished dying," Maren said. "Or unless something of it survived. Or unless it was never properly killed at all, only buried, only *filed*." She gathered the charts together with those riverbed hands, brisk again, closing the subject like a ledger. "You should go home, Ulyana. Sleep. Come back tomorrow and forget all this, the way you're supposed to."

"And if I can't?"

She paused at the end of the aisle, small and stooped in the lamplight, and for a moment she looked less like an archivist than like a woman standing at the edge of a well she'd spent her whole life avoiding.

"Then come find me," she said, "before you go to the guildmaster. Promise me that much. Whatever you conclude, whatever you decide to do — talk to me first." A beat. "There are things in this building older than the ledgers, and one of them is the reason the ledgers exist. If you go digging, you'll find it. And child, it has been *hungry* for a very long time."

She climbed the stairs and was gone, leaving me alone with the lamps and the blank space on the map and the word sitting on my tongue like a pearl with a hook in it.

*Ostmere,* I thought.

And far below the archive — far below the counting hall, below the vaults where a mud-caked child slept under appraisal-lock, below the foundations and the clay and the drowned dark — something vast turned over in its sleep, and every lamp in the archive guttered once, in unison, like a held breath.

On the table in front of me, untouched by any hand I saw, the oldest chart slid half an inch toward the edge of the desk. Toward the door that led down.

*Here was,* said the margin.

*Here is,* said something else, in a language with no alphabet, from a depth with no bottom.
