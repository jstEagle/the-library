## 4. What the Vault Keeps

They put me in a room with no windows and call it a vault, which is a funny word for it, because where I come from — where I *came* from, which is a sentence I have to keep relearning — a vault was where they kept the crown jewels, and the crown jewels were just three bent spoons and a stone that hummed when you lied to it.

I am the last thing in the vault now. I suppose I hum when you lie to me too. Everyone lies to me. They lie with their faces, mostly, the way people do: *you're safe now, little one, drink your soup.* Nobody says what happened. Nobody says her name. So I keep it myself, the way you'd keep a coal in your coat pocket through a long winter, cupping your hand around it so it doesn't go out and doesn't burn through.

Her name is — 

No. That's not how this works. Her name comes later, or it comes never, or it comes in the middle like a bell at noon, and when it does you don't get to choose whether to hear it.

Here is what I have instead of sleep. Here are the things the dark gives back to me, one at a time, like a miser counting coins he intends to spend on something terrible.

* * *

Snow.

Not the gray city snow here, which falls already tired, already dirty from its fall through all that smoke. Ostmere snow came down white and *serious*, like it had been sent on an errand. It fell in the high passes first and then walked down into the valley over three days, and my mother said you could watch it coming if you climbed the bell tower, a white tide moving through the pines, patient as anything.

I climbed that tower more times than there were reasons to climb it. There was a gap in the railing on the north side where a stonemason named Pell — I remember his hands, enormous, cracked like dry riverbeds — had dropped his chisel and leaned too far after it, and they'd never fixed the rail because Pell said the gap kept children honest. It did not keep me honest. I hung my boots over the edge of the world and watched the snow-tide come and felt like the only person awake in a sleeping kingdom, and I thought: *this is mine, all of this is mine* — not in the way the tutors meant, the heir-and-inheritance way, but the way you own a thing by loving it without permission.

The bells up there had names. The big one was Grandmother, because she tolled slow and deep and complained about everything. When snow was coming, the ringer — old Vess, who smelled of tallow and peppermint — would climb up and wake Grandmother once, a single note, and every house in the valley would hear it and know: bank your fires, bring in the goats, the sky is sending its letter.

One note. That's all it took to tell a whole country to take care of itself.

I would give — well. I would give quite a lot for one more note.

* * *

My mother sang in the kitchen with her sleeves pushed up.

This is the memory that hurts least and most, which I didn't know was possible until I had it. It hurts least because nothing bad happens in it. It hurts most because nothing bad happens in it, and then it ends.

She made bread on the days she was sad. She never said she was sad; she just made bread, four loaves instead of two, kneading harder than dough requires, flour to her elbows like she'd been arm-deep in a cloud. And she sang. The song had no title that anyone told me, or maybe the title was just her voice doing a thing at the top of the phrase, a small crack upward, like a door opening onto somewhere warmer.

The words went something like:

*Sleep, small kingdom, sleep,*
*the mountain holds your hand.*
*Whatever wakes beneath the stone,*
*let it not understand.*

I used to think that last line was about me — that she was singing about how I never understood anything, why we couldn't have sugar on ordinary days, why Father's face went shut whenever the ambassadors from the low countries visited and spoke too quietly near the walls. Children think everything is about them. It's the one luxury childhood affords, and I spent mine entirely.

It wasn't about me. It was about the thing under us. She always knew there was a thing under us. Maybe all the mothers did. Maybe that's what mothers are for, in part: knowing about the thing under everything and singing anyway, louder, so the children hear the song instead of the silence underneath it.

I can still sing it. I checked, in the vault, in the dark, whispering it to the iron door. The words came out whole. Whatever else the dark took, it left me the song, and I have decided to be furious about the arrangement rather than grateful, because gratitude implies the taking was fair.

* * *

Now the other kind of memory. The kind I'd trade the song away to lose, if trading were possible, which it isn't, which is the whole problem with losing things.

It began as a sound like someone swallowing.

That's genuinely how it started. Not thunder, not an earthquake, none of the respectable disasters. A swallow — vast, wet, unhurried — somewhere below the cellar of the world. And then the lamps went wrong. Not out; *wrong*. The flame stayed lit but the light got thin, like broth stretched with water, and shadows stopped agreeing with their owners. My shadow lay pointing east. Everything's shadow pointed east, toward the palace hill, even the shadows of things west of the palace hill, and I remember thinking, with the calm idiocy of an eight-year-old: *that's funny.*

Then people began forgetting mid-scream.

I want you to understand what that means, because I've had two hundred years — no. No, that's wrong, I haven't, time in here is a wet page, the numbers slide off it. I've had some amount of nights, and I have turned it over every single one, and I still can't make it make sense, so I'll just say it plainly:

The screaming started, and then it stopped *in the middle*, not because the fear stopped, but because the reason for it was eaten while the mouth was still open. I watched our neighbor, the cooper, Herr Brandt, run into the square with his face full of terror, and halfway across the cobbles his terror simply — closed. Like a book being shut by a hand you couldn't see. He stood there blinking. He looked around at the burning sky and the running crowds and the east-pointing shadows, and his face said: *how curious, I seem to have come outside, I wonder why.*

And then he forgot that too, and stood very still, and the darkness came up through the cobbles like water through cloth and drank him standing, politely, without spilling.

Everyone forgot mid-scream. That's the shape of it. The dark didn't kill us first. It ate the *knowing* first, so that we died unalarmed, so that the city's last sound wasn't a scream but a sort of puzzled quiet, thousands of people going *oh?* at the same moment, and then nothing, and then me.

Why me? I asked the dark that, in whatever language you ask questions when your whole country is going down your neighbors' throats. Why leave the smallest dish?

I think — and this is only a thought, held loosely, the way you hold something that bites — I think it wanted a taster. I think it left one mouthful alive the way you leave a crumb on the plate, so the meal remembers itself. Or maybe it simply hadn't finished, and I was the last sip, and something interrupted it. Something with ledgers and ink and a guildmaster with cold hands, something that found me mud-caked at a loading door and said *appraisal* like I was a bolt of cloth.

Here's the worst detail. It's a small one. Small ones are always the worst.

When Mother grabbed me, we ran toward the bell tower. Don't ask me why; you run to the tallest thing when the ground betrays you, it's instinct older than sense. And as we ran, the tower rang. One note. Grandmother, waking on her own, no ringer, no rope pulled, one enormous grieving note rolling down the valley telling every house: *take care, the sky is sending its letter.*

Mother heard it and — I saw it happen — she almost smiled. In the middle of the end of everything, one note of home and her face started making the smile before the rest of her could stop it. Then the dark took the smile. I watched it go. It went out of her face the way heat goes out of a stone at dusk, slowly, evenly, everywhere at once, and her hand in mine didn't loosen, but it stopped gripping, and her eyes slid off me like I was weather.

She forgot me mid-run. The dark ate her knowing, and I was part of her knowing.

I screamed her name into her ear and she looked directly at me with the politest blankness in the world and said, in our language, the beautiful dead language, the language that lives now only in me and in whatever books the guild keeps behind its locked doors:

*I'm sorry, little one, have we met?*

And then the light went thin again, and the cobbles opened, and I learned what it looks like when a country is swallowed: it looks like everyone quietly deciding, all together, to look elsewhere.

* * *

In the vault, the dark is different. I want to be clear about that, because I know how this sounds — a boy afraid of the dark, in a vault, ha ha. But the vault-dark is empty. Honest emptiness, like a swept floor. The other dark, the Ostmere dark, was *full*. Full of swallowed streets and swallowed bells and a swallowed woman with flour on her elbows, all of it still in there, still warm, being digested at leisure for two hundred years.

Sometimes I put my ear against the vault floor and listen, because the floors here are thick and old and sit on top of other, older floors, and I am fairly sure — fairly, the way you're fairly sure of a toothache — that the same dark runs underneath this city too. That it followed the recipe. Found a rich country, lent itself patiently, and waited under the cellars for the meal to ripen.

And sometimes — this is the part I haven't told the tall sad scribe who brings the soup and watches me like I'm a fire he's been ordered not to put out — sometimes the dark listens back.

Last night, or what I'm calling night, since the vault has no windows and I've decided night happens when the soup arrives twice, I whispered the bread-song against the floor. All of it. Even the last line, the true line, the one about letting it not understand.

And the floor answered.

Not in words. Floors are bad at words. It answered the way Grandmother answered — one enormous slow note, felt rather than heard, coming up through the stone and through my knees and through my teeth, a note so deep it was less a sound than a decision. And threaded inside the note, faint as a hair in milk, was a voice. Not my mother's. Older. Hungrier. Patient the way debt is patient.

It said — or it meant, meaning travels better than saying through two hundred feet of stone —

*You kept the words. Good. Bring them closer.*

I sat back against the wall and held my own knees like Mother taught me, when storms shook the tower, *hold what's yours, little kingdom*, and I understood something with terrible clarity:

It isn't finished eating.

I'm not the leftover. I'm the dessert course, kept fresh all this time. And the guild — the ink-people, the ledger-people, the ones who lock boys in vaults *for appraisal* — they feed it. I don't know how I know. I know the way you know a song: completely, without ever having decided to learn it. They carry memories down to it in books, spoonful by spoonful, and it lets them, and it sleeps a little, and they call it accounting.

Tomorrow the tall scribe comes back. Tomorrow they'll start asking me questions and writing down my answers, and every answer will be a street, a bell, a loaf of bread, a woman singing — every answer will be Ostmere, spooned out of me onto a page and carried down to the dark like tribute.

I should refuse. I know I should refuse. But here is the arithmetic of a boy alone in a vault: my memories are the only country I have left, and they are being eaten either way, slowly by the dark or quickly by the ink, and if I must be devoured I would rather be *read* first. Would rather someone, even a stranger with ink-stained fingers, hear my mother's song and write it down and know, briefly, that we existed. That there was snow, and it fell seriously, and there were bells with names.

Let him transcribe me. Let him carry me down in pages.

But I will hide one word inside the song, one syllable tucked where only someone reading with love would find it — and when the dark leans in to take its dessert, it will swallow the one thing it has been starving for these two hundred years without knowing it:

Its own name.

The soup comes again. Night number whatever. Footsteps in the corridor, tired ones, kind ones, getting closer.

Good, I think, pressing my palm flat to the cold floor. Come and write me down, scribe. Bring your ink.

Bring your ears.
