## 5. Terms of Indenture

The summons came at the worst possible hour, which is to say any hour at all. In the Ledger Guild of Vaskarand, a summons is never a good sign — it is merely a question of how bad. A summons delivered by a junior clerk means paperwork. A summons delivered by a senior auditor means your accounts have developed an opinion about you. And a summons delivered by Guildmaster Havel himself, in person, standing at the foot of your desk while you pretend very hard to be absorbed in cataloguing a jar of bottled thunder that has begun, worryingly, to condense —

Well. That one means you're already dead; you just haven't been entered into the ledgers yet.

"Ulyana Coldharbour," Havel said, though we both knew my name perfectly well. He was a tall man built like a piece of municipal architecture — all vertical lines and load-bearing silences — and he had the particular gift of making a room feel audited simply by entering it.

"Guildmaster." I set down my pen with the exaggerated care of a man who does not want his hands to tremble visibly. "If this is about the thunder, I've flagged it twice. It's leaking. Whatever it's leaking."

"It isn't about the thunder." He glanced at the jar anyway, the way one glances at a dog that has bitten someone before. "Walk with me."

Nobody walks with Havel. Havel walks *at* you, and you are merely terrain. I followed him out of the acquisitions hall, past shelves of impossible things — the saint's second shadow coiled in its cedar box like a sleeping cat, a taxidermied grief I'd stopped asking questions about months ago — and toward the administrative stair that climbed into the guild's upper floors, where the air smelled less of ink and more of decisions.

Here is what you must understand about my position: I owed the guild four hundred and eleven marks, fourteen coppers, and — as of the last audit — some quantity that the clerks had taken to writing in a smaller and smaller hand, as though the numbers might be embarrassed of themselves. My father had signed me over at age nine when his own debts came due. Eleven years of indenture later, the interest had grown faster than my wage could chase it, the way weeds grow faster than the boy sent to pull them. Every year on the anniversary of my signing, the guild recalculated, and every year the number came back larger, and every year I did the arithmetic myself in the margins of my personal ledger just to confirm that yes, mathematics was still against me personally, specifically, by name.

So when a guildmaster summons a debt-bound scribe to the upper floors, the scribe does not think, *I wonder what this is about.* The scribe thinks, *This is where they tell me the price of my spine.*

* * *

His office occupied the tower's top floor, and its single window looked down over Vaskarand's rooftops to the harbor beyond. I noticed, with the idle attention of a man looking for anywhere else to look, that from this height the city's streets made a pattern. Not the usual pattern — not the grid the surveyors' maps showed. Something older underneath, concentric, like ripples around a stone dropped long ago. The palace district sat at the center of it. Odd. The maps showed no such arrangement. But then, maps are written by people who want cities to be legible, and cities are under no obligation to cooperate.

"You found the entry," Havel said.

Not a greeting. No pleasantries. Just that, laid on the desk between us like a knife.

"I don't know what you mean." Which was true, and also a lie, and also the exact kind of sentence that sounds brave in your head and cowardly out loud.

"The audit flag." He opened a ledger on his desk — my ledger, or rather the guild's copy of my ledger, which is a distinction with teeth in it. "Page two hundred eleven. An acquisition logged in your hand, dated three nights ago, for an item you cannot describe because you were asleep in the dormitory when it was written." He turned the book toward me. There it was: my handwriting, my ink-slant, even my habitual little flourish on the numeral — a flaw I'd been flogged for at nine years old and had never managed to kill. The entry read:

*Item 8,441: One (1) voice, recovered. Provenance unknown. Condition: hungry.*

"I didn't write that," I said.

"No," Havel agreed. "You didn't. Which interests me considerably more than if you had."

I looked at the entry until the letters stopped behaving like letters. Three nights ago. The night of the storm. The night before — no. The same night. The night the boy came to the loading door, mud to his knees, speaking a language that made the vault lamps gutter. The night I'd stood there with my lantern and felt something in my chest turn over like a key in a lock I hadn't known my ribs contained.

Havel watched me arrive at the connection. He had the patience of a man who owned the clock.

"The child," I said.

"The asset," Havel corrected, gently, the way you'd correct a child misnaming an animal. "He has been appraised. The appraisal is ongoing. That is why you're here."

"Appraised for what?"

"For value." He folded his hands. Outside, gulls argued over the harbor; inside, the silence had weight enough to be taxed. "Ulyana. How much do you owe this guild?"

"Four hundred and eleven marks—"

"Four hundred and eleven marks, fourteen coppers, and accruing. Yes. Do you know what your debt purchases annually? Lodging in a room shared with five others. Meals graded by your output. Ink allowances. The privilege of continuing." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. Cruelty implies the cruelty is about *you*. This was merely weather. "You will work it off in approximately thirty-one years, assuming no further recalculations. You've seen the recalculations."

I had. I carried them under my sternum like swallowed stones.

"So here is an alternative," Havel said. "A single commission. Completed, it extinguishes the whole of your debt. Four hundred and eleven marks, forgiven. Fourteen coppers, forgiven. Eleven years of indenture, dissolved retroactively — your record amended to show a free man's history. You would walk out of this building owing nothing to anyone."

The words went through me like liquor through a starving man. I want to tell you I was suspicious. I want to tell you some part of me stood apart, cold-eyed, counting exits. And perhaps a sliver of me did — but I'll be honest, since honesty is apparently the theme of my recent humiliations: mostly what I felt was the vertigo of a number going to zero. Eleven years of arithmetic reversing itself. Thirty-one years handed back to me like a coat returned from a thief who'd died.

"What's the commission?" I asked, and hated how fast I asked it.

"The transcription of the asset's memories into the master ledger."

I blinked. "Memories."

"The boy remembers things," Havel said. "Things that predate him. Things that predate everyone. The guild requires those memories recorded — completely, accurately, verbatim. You have the steadiest hand among the junior scribes, you have experience cataloguing items that resist description, and—" here he paused, and something moved behind his eyes like a fish under ice, "—you appear to have an affinity. The loading door. Your reaction to him. Don't trouble yourself denying it; Denner reported it, and Denner reports everything, including things that didn't happen, so believe me when I say I checked."

Affinity. Is that what it was? I thought of the moment the boy had looked up at me from the mud, said a word in that buried language — *sennu*, low and round, a word I had never learned and somehow knew meant *cold, but the kind you survive* — and how the knowing had arrived not like learning but like remembering. Like a door in me opening onto a room already furnished.

"And the risk to me?" I asked. "There's always a risk column."

"There is a contract," Havel said, "which will make everything explicit."

He drew it from the drawer. It was longer than contracts usually are — a proper scroll, sealed at intervals down its length, the guild's wax stamp repeated like a pulse. He unrolled it across the desk and turned it toward me, and I did what any sensible debtor does when offered absolution in writing.

I read the first page. The first page was fine. The first page named me, named the debt, named the sum to be forgiven, named the term of service: *until transcription complete.* The second page began with standard clauses — confidentiality, custody of materials, conduct — and I was reading along at speed, skimming the way one skims, because hope is the great accelerator of reading comprehension, and somewhere around clause nineteen my eyes caught the phrase *the transcriber consents to such deductions from his person as the work may require* and my mind supplied the gloss *ink, paper, lamp-oil, normal expenses* and moved on, because I wanted it to move on, because the third page had a line about retroactive manumission and I wanted to reach it again and touch it again, the way you touch a coin to confirm it's real.

That's the thing they never warn you about complicity. It doesn't arrive wearing a mask. It arrives wearing your own optimism. It hands you the pen while you're busy being grateful.

"Sign at each seal," Havel said, offering the pen.

And here is the moment I could have stopped. I want that on the record, whoever ends up reading this — and someone always ends up reading these things, that's the entire business model of ledgers. There was a moment, pen in hand, first seal under my fingers, when Maren passed the open office door. Senior Archivist Maren, gray as paper ash, moving with her usual gliding economy, and her eyes crossed mine through the doorway for exactly one heartbeat, and she shook her head. Not dramatically. Barely a tremor. The way you shake your head at someone about to step off a dock because they haven't noticed the water is frozen.

Then she was gone, and Havel said, "Whenever you're ready," and I thought: she's old, she's strange, she once spent six hours alphabetizing a drawer of unlabeled keys, and I am four hundred and eleven marks tired.

I signed. All nine seals. My name got progressively more hurried down the scroll, so that the man who finished the contract barely resembled the man who started it, which felt prophetic in a way I chose not to examine.

"Wonderful," said Havel, and for the first time he smiled, and I discovered that Guildmaster Havel smiling was considerably worse than Guildmaster Havel not.

* * *

They took me down to see him that same evening. Not the vault — a side chamber off the archive stacks, furnished with a cot, a chair, a table, two lamps, and an absence of windows that I only noticed after I'd been inside for a minute and my body started quietly complaining about the missing sky. The boy sat on the cot with his knees drawn up, and when I came in he watched me with that flat, ancient patience children sometimes have, as though he'd been waiting for me specifically and had already accounted for my lateness.

"Hello," I said.

He said nothing. His mud had been washed off — someone had bathed him, clothed him in guild-gray — but there was still river-silt in the creases of his knuckles, and I found that detail unbearable for reasons I declined to investigate.

"I'm Ulyana," I said. "I'm going to... sit here. With you. We're going to talk, and I'm going to write some of it down."

At the word *write* something flickered across his face — not fear exactly. Recognition, maybe. Or pity. It's a terrible thing, realizing a nine-year-old is pitying you.

He held out his hand. On his palm, drawn in whatever grime survived the bath, was a spiral. Concentric rings around a dark center. I stared at it, and the room tilted slightly, because I had seen that shape an hour ago from Havel's window, traced in the streets below, and I had seen it last week in the grain of the master ledger's oldest pages, and I had seen it — I was nearly sure — in dreams I didn't remember having.

"Do you know what this is?" I asked.

He closed his fist. Then he pointed at me, and said the word again — *sennu* — and this time it landed differently. Not *cold, but the kind you survive.* Something adjacent. Something like: *you, specifically, are cold, but the kind you survive.*

"Right," I said, to nobody, to the lamps, to the sealed contract burning a hole in my breast pocket like a coal. "Let's begin."

I opened the master ledger to a fresh page. I dipped my pen. The boy took a breath, and I swear the lamps leaned toward him like listeners.

What happened next — what the ink did, what it did *to* me — that belongs to the next hour, and the next chapter of my ruin. But I will say this much now, because I earned the right to say it honestly at least once: as my pen touched the page, I thought of Maren's shake of the head, and I understood, finally and far too late, that she hadn't been warning me away from the water.

She'd been telling me I was already in it.

The boy began to speak. And the page began, softly, gladly, to drink.
