## 6. First Transcription

The contract was four pages long and I read perhaps half of one. In my defense, the half I read contained the phrase *all outstanding balances shall be considered satisfied upon completion of services rendered*, and a starving man does not ask the baker about the provenance of his wheat.

The other half — Maren would later tell me, in her way of telling things, which is to say sideways and too late — concerned ownership. Of what, she did not say. She only looked at my right hand, then at me, then back at my right hand, as though the two were not currently attached.

But that morning I signed with the good pen, the guildmaster's own, whose nib was silver and whose inkwell was blacker than any ink has a right to be, and I told myself: eleven sessions, maybe twelve, and Ulyana Coldharbour walks out of the Ledger Guild of Vaskarand owing nothing but his breath.

I have since had occasion to reflect that a man who owes nothing is a man who owns nothing, and there are worse arrangements than debt. Debt at least means someone expects you to exist tomorrow.

* * *

They brought the boy down to Transcription Room Nine at the second bell after midnight, which should have been my first warning. Nothing honest happens in Vaskarand between the second and fourth bells. That is when the pawnshops count their teeth and the constables practice looking elsewhere.

The room itself was older than the rest of the guild. The other transcription rooms — I'd seen them on errands — were cramped clerical cells, all pigeonholes and paste. Room Nine was vaulted, stone gone soft at the edges like candle wax, and the ceiling rose up into a dark the lamps declined to enter. There was a table. Two chairs. A ledger so large it lay open on its own stand like a lectern Bible, its pages the grey-white of old ice.

And there was Maren, already seated in the corner, hands folded, watching.

"I'm told you're to observe," I said. "Seniority being what it is."

"I'm told nothing," said Maren. "I come because the chairs here are comfortable." Then, as I sat: "Boy. What's your name?"

He'd been given a guild designation — Acquisition 4-1-9, Unaccompanied, Verbal — which even by guild standards was a masterpiece of the grotesque. He looked at Maren now, this small mud-caked creature in a borrowed coat three sizes too large, sleeves rolled and rolled again, and he answered in Vaskarandi for the first time since they'd fished him out of the loading yard.

"Yevva," he said. Then, correcting himself with the gravity of a child who has thought hard about names: "That was my nurse's word for me. It means *small hunger*. My mother's name for me is—" and he said something else, something that moved through the room like weather, and both lamps guttered though there was no draft, and Maren's folded hands went white.

"Write that one down exactly," she told me, very quietly, "and I will personally throw you down the stairs."

"Noted," I said.

The guildmaster's instructions had been simple. Sit the boy comfortably. Open the master ledger to the marked page. Ask him to remember. Write what he says. *Do not*, Havel had emphasized, with the particular care of a man describing a tripwire, *add anything of your own.*

"What if he says nothing?" I'd asked.

"He will say everything," Havel said. "That is rather the difficulty."

* * *

So we began.

I asked Yevva to tell me about snow, because it seemed a gentle place to start, and because I am a coward who begins all difficult conversations with the weather.

"In Ostmere," he said, in that language first — and I heard it properly now, under lamplight, without the panic of the loading door and the guildmaster shouting — and oh, it was not a dead language at all. Dead languages are husks; this one still had marrow in it. It sounded the way rivers sound under ice: going somewhere, whatever the surface believes. Then he translated himself into our tongue, word by careful word, a boy building a bridge across two hundred years with his bare hands:

"In Ostmere the snow came down the way flour comes through a sieve. My mother said the city swept its own rooftops. She said the snow remembered every roof it had ever landed on, and came back to them."

I wrote it. And here is where I must be precise, because precision is the last thing I have left.

I wrote it, and then the ink kept going.

My hand was on the pen but the pen was no longer entirely mine. The nib moved of its own accord, quick and sure, laying down lines in a script I did not know — tall and narrow, letters like standing stones — and the words arriving under it were more than Yevva had spoken, were the *rest* of what he meant: the color of that snow, blue-grey as dove feathers; the sound of it underfoot, which was not a crunch but a chime, because in Ostmere they salted the streets with crushed glass from the bell-foundry and the whole city walked on music; his sister — he hadn't mentioned a sister — his sister Lark catching flakes on her tongue and declaring each one's flavor, *this one is Thursday, this one is church, this one is you, Yevva, taste it, this one tastes like you.*

"That isn't what I said," Yevva whispered, watching the page.

"No," I agreed. My voice sounded like it was coming through a wall. "It's what you didn't say."

The ink finished its line, lifted, waited. Like a dog that has fetched something and wants you to approve.

I want to describe what happened next accurately, because I have thought about it many times since, insofar as I can think about anything since. When I looked at those moving words, I did not merely read them. I *remembered* them. I remembered the taste of a snowflake shaped like my own self. I remembered a sister named Lark, freckled, gap-toothed, bossy in the way of older sisters who love you and resent the loving. I remembered a street called the Silverwalk and the chime of glass underfoot and a bell tower with a green roof, and none of it was mine, and all of it was in my head with the same weight, the same texture, the same warm horrible familiarity as my mother's kitchen, my father's cough, my own debts.

Someone had poured a stranger's life into the jug of mine and I could not tell where one liquid ended and the other began.

"Maren," I said.

"I see it," she said. "Keep writing."

"Is this—"

"*Keep writing,* Ulyana."

There was something in her voice I would learn, over the following weeks, to hear properly: a wire pulled taut. So I kept writing.

* * *

For an hour, the boy remembered, and the ledger drank.

It did not drink the way I expected — I had imagined some vulgar gulping, pages tearing, ink vanishing off the vellum. No. It was polite. It was *gracious*. The words settled into the page and became part of its weave, and the ledger grew no heavier, took up no more room on its stand, and gave every appearance of having always contained these stories and merely been reminded of them.

And the boy grew lighter.

That is the only word. As he spoke, the exhaustion of a child who had walked out of nowhere into a hostile city fell away from him by degrees. His eyes cleared. His spine straightened inside the enormous coat. By the third quarter-hour he was almost laughing, remembering the time Lark convinced him the river froze flat on the bottom before it froze on top, and made him go check, and he fell through, and their mother —

Their mother.

He stopped. The lamp nearest him flared, steadied.

"She was singing," he said. "At the market. She sang while she chose the fish, always, everyone hated it, everyone loved it." His face did something terrible and young. "She was singing when it came. Everyone forgot her mid-scream. Do you understand? Not just — people didn't run, they didn't even look, they *forgot she was screaming*. The whole city forgot at once, like a page turned. And then the page was blank. And then there wasn't a book."

The ink on my page had gone very still, the way an animal goes still.

"Tell me about *it*," I said, because I am, as established, a coward, and cowards walk toward the thing they fear the fastest, so long as someone else is holding the lantern.

And the boy looked at me — looked *through* me, pupils wide as wells — and said:

"It doesn't have a face. It has a *place*."

* * *

Here is what I saw. I say *saw* knowing the word is inadequate, the way *wet* is inadequate for drowning.

I saw downward.

Not with my eyes. My eyes were open on a lamp-lit room, on a boy and a ledger and an old woman with white knuckles. But behind them, beneath them, something in me tilted like a ship rolling in a swell, and I looked straight down through the floor of the world.

Beneath Vaskarand — beneath the Guildhall, beneath Cellar Row and the Sighing Stairs and the whole fat, smug weight of the city — there was a hollow. Of course there was. Every city sits atop its own absence; ours simply had the courtesy of scale.

It was vast. That word too is inadequate. *Vast* suggests edges you might someday reach. This had no edges I found; it had *inclinations*, curves of darkness suggesting that somewhere, eventually, there might be a far side, the way a dream suggests morning. It was not empty. Emptiness is a quality of containers, and this contained everything it had ever eaten, held it all the way a stomach holds a meal or a miser holds coins: completely, and with attention.

And it was patient. That is what I cannot make anyone feel who was not there. Monsters in the penny dreadfuls rage and gnash. This did not rage. It had eaten a country and then waited two hundred years between courses, and it regarded the small bright thread of a child's memory unspooling down toward it from Room Nine with what I can only call *appreciation*. A diner recognizing the next course by its smell.

It knew I was looking. I understood this the way you understand a hand on the back of your neck.

And it was not angry, or startled, or even especially interested in me. It adjusted. The way the sea adjusts around a dropped coin. Something in the dark shifted by a fraction, and the fraction was continental, and a sensation moved up through the guild's foundations, through the table, through the chair, into my bones — a hum below hearing, a bell rung underwater, a vibration of such low patience that my teeth ached and my heart fell briefly out of rhythm, and in that moment I felt, with perfect clarity, its single thought, which was not a thought in words but was as clear as one:

*Soon.*

Then the vision — the tilt, the fall, the gaze downward — snapped shut like a purse, and I was in a chair in Room Nine with ink on my fingers and blood in my mouth where I'd bitten my tongue.

* * *

The boy was asleep. He had simply stopped mid-sentence — "*...and then the ferryman said*" — and slid down in the big chair like a coat slipping off a hook, breathing slow and deep, and the borrowed coat rose and fell, rose and fell, and he looked, for the first time since the loading door, like a child instead of a problem.

The ledger lay closed. I did not close it. I want that on the record, whatever records remain: I did not close it. It closed itself, softly, with the sound of a mouth being wiped.

Maren crossed the room and put her hand flat on the cover, as if taking its pulse.

"You saw it," she said. Not a question.

"There's something under the city."

"There's something under everything," said Maren. "Mostly it stays put." She looked at the sleeping boy with an expression I had never seen on her face, which I would later learn was the expression she wore whenever she almost remembered something. "How much of his did you keep?"

"I don't—"

"The snow," she said. "The sister. Tell me you can't still taste the snow."

I opened my mouth to lie, and found, disturbingly, that I could not remember how lying worked — for one vertiginous moment the skill itself had been misplaced, filed under someone else's name — and then it came back, and I lied, badly.

Maren nodded, unsurprised, unsurprising.

"Session two is tomorrow night," she said. "Eat a large meal beforehand. And Ulyana—" She paused at the door, small and bent and silhouetted against the corridor lamps. "When you got lost down there, under the city, under the dark. For a moment. Did you feel it notice?"

"Yes."

"And did it feel," she said carefully, "to you, like noticing? Or like *reading*?"

I did not answer, because I had already answered, hours ago, in silver-nibbed signature on a four-page contract I had not finished reading.

The lamps guttered again — no draft, no wind, the guild's foundations settling, I told myself, merely settling — and far below us, deeper than any cellar ever dug, patient as arithmetic, the Hollow turned the page.
