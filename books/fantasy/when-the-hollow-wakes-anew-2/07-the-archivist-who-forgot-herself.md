## 7. The Archivist Who Forgot Herself

There is a shelf in the deep stacks where the ledgers go to be forgotten properly. Not archived — archived implies someone might come looking. Forgotten. The guild calls it the Quiet Range, and it runs beneath the east wing like a spine of grey spines, forty thousand volumes of other people's lives, none of them mine.

I know every one of them by heart.

That should tell you something about me. It tells you almost nothing about me, which is rather the point of my profession.

* * *

My name is Maren Voss-Sandh. I am senior archivist of the Ledger Guild of Vaskarand, thirty-one years in service, keeper of the Quiet Range, and I can recite from memory the acquisition ledger of the year 1189, including the entry for a jar of preserved applause — three seconds' worth, harvested at an execution, faintly warm to the touch even now.

What I cannot tell you is my daughter's face.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last dignity left to a woman like me. I have not forgotten that I had a daughter. That memory was taken cleanly, surgically, the way the guild takes everything — with a receipt. I remember her existence the way you remember a room you've only been described: there was a girl, she lived in my house for some years, I loved her, the word "loved" still fits the shape of her the way a coat fits a hook long after the shoulders have gone. I remember that her name began with a sound like water over stones. I do not remember the sound itself.

I remember every line of every ledger I ever transcribed.

You will understand, then, why I spend my days down here among the forgotten books, running my fingers along their spines like a woman reading braille. These are not my memories. But they are memories, and they are *held*, and holding anything at all is a comfort when your own hands keep coming up empty.

The boy arrived nine days ago. I felt him before I saw him — felt him the way you feel weather through a wall. Something old moving under the floorboards of the world.

* * *

Let me explain how transcription works. Not how the guild says it works; how it actually works.

The official doctrine, as taught to junior clerks, is that the master ledger *records*. A subject speaks, the transcriptionist writes, ink remembers what flesh forgets. Simple. Clean. Commerce.

Here is what I know after eleven years at the desk: the ink does not wait to be written. On my fourth session with my first subject — a dying ferryman who remembered a river that no longer exists, though I did not then understand *why* it no longer exists, or what ate it — the words began arriving on the page before I wrote them. I would sit with my pen raised and watch letters bloom out of the blank vellum like frost forming on glass, and my task was merely to keep pace, to press my nib into grooves already opening, as if the page were soft earth and something were walking across it.

And here is what I know after eleven years at the desk and one daughter fewer than I started with: nothing blooms without cost. The ferryman's river came out of the ferryman. And the ink — the ink takes its fee from the nearer hand.

From the writer.

* * *

I went up to the transcription room on the evening of the ninth day. I told myself it was to inspect the new binding stock. I am very good at telling myself things; it is one of the few skills they cannot eat.

Ulyana Coldharbour was at the desk. I have watched that young man for years — watched him tot up his debts in the margins of work ledgers with little annotations like *bread (owe Tulla)* and *mother's medicine (owe self)*. He has the particular posture of the permanently indebted: shoulders drawn in as if apologizing to his own skeleton. But he also has steady hands, and steadier eyes, and when the boy spoke in that language — that impossible language, that language two hundred years dead, that language I have read in the oldest ledgers until I could taste it, cold and green and full of snow — Ulyana's hands did not shake.

That is rarer than you'd think.

The boy sat opposite him with a cup of barley tea going cold between them, and he was speaking, low and halting, and the ink on the master ledger was moving. Ulyana hadn't noticed yet. New transcriptionists never notice until it's too late to matter; the mind protects itself with disbelief right up until disbelief becomes unaffordable.

I stood in the doorway with my armful of imaginary binding stock and listened.

The boy was describing a market day. Fish stalls, awnings the color of eggplant, a woman who sold honey by letting customers dip their own fingers. Ordinary things. Sacred things. And as he spoke, the ledger filled with those things in a hand that was almost Ulyana's but not quite — older, more certain, the hand of whoever held the pen before either of them existed — and I watched Ulyana's face while he read what appeared under his own nib.

Wonder. That's what I saw first. The wonder of a man watching the impossible behave impeccably, like a well-trained animal.

Then the wonder curdled, just slightly, around the edges. Because he was feeling it too — I could see it land on him, wave after wave, the fish-stall smell, the honey-woman's laugh — and those were not his memories. They could not be his memories. And yet they arrived in him with the horrible intimacy of things remembered, wearing the little badges of true memory: sensory weight, emotional residue, the sense of *having been there*.

He looked up. Saw me. Did not startle, which I appreciated.

"Archivist," he said. "Is there — the ink is doing something."

"The ink does many things," I said. "Most of them are not in the orientation materials."

* * *

I sent the boy away with the tea and half a wheel of the good cheese from my own stores, because grief makes children hungry and hunger makes grief louder, and because I am sentimental in precisely one direction and it points at small frightened persons. Then I sat down across from Ulyana and did the thing I had come to do, which was lie to him badly and hope he'd catch me at it.

"Your technique is acceptable," I said. "Keep the sessions short. Forty minutes, no more. The subject fatigues."

"He doesn't seem tired afterward. If anything he seems—"

"Lighter. Yes." I smoothed the ledger's corner flat with two fingers, the way you'd smooth a sheet over a body. "Memories have mass, Coldharbour. When a thing loses mass, we say it is lighter. We do not generally ask where the mass went."

He frowned. He is clever; he is simply clever in the direction of arithmetic rather than dread, which is a common affliction in this building and nearly always fatal.

"You're saying the transcription drains him."

"I'm saying," I said, "that the guild's ledgers hold four hundred thousand entries of things that do not exist anymore. Bottled thunder. Saints' shadows. Rivers. Kingdoms. You have catalogued acquisitions yourself. Tell me — when the guild acquires the last living memory of a thing, where does the original go?"

"That's not— the ledgers don't take anything. They record."

"Mm." I turned the ledger toward him and opened it to a page near the middle, one of the ones that fills itself. "Read me the marginalia."

He read. His voice slowed. "*Entry 44,117. Subject recalls the bell tower of [word rendered in Ostmerean script]* — and then, underneath, smaller, in a different hand, in Ostmerean, a single word repeated twice, and I confess I do not know—" He stopped. Looked at me. "You read this."

"Every line I ever transcribed," I said, "is in my head. Perfectly. Permanently. Ask me any entry from any year of my service and I will recite it to you, punctuation included." I let him look at me for a moment — let him see the thing behind my face, the vast tidy library with one wing burned off. "Ask me my daughter's favorite color."

He didn't ask. He was kind enough not to ask, and perceptive enough to understand that not-asking was the whole examination.

"What happened to her?" he said quietly.

"She grew up. She is, presumably, grown now. Somewhere. With a face." I closed the ledger. "I wouldn't recognize her. I paid for the privilege of remembering the ledgers, Coldharbour. Or rather — I transcribed, and transcribed, and transcribed, and the fee collected itself, quietly, in the currency I least guarded. The ledger doesn't record memory. Recording implies the original remains somewhere. It eats. What goes onto the page comes *out* of the speaker, and what holds the pen pays handling charges, and the guild banks both."

"And you kept transcribing anyway."

"Eleven years," I said. "Yes."

"Why?"

Because the work was beautiful. Because the river came back when I wrote it, all cold and silver, and for forty minutes at a time I owned a river. Because each session I thought: one more, and then I'll stop, and stop being the sort of person who says one more. Because the guild held my contract the way it holds everyone's contract, and contracts in this building are not documents, they are weather.

But that was too many true answers, so I gave him the one that was safest and most damning:

"Because it seemed worth it," I said. "At the time. Everything always seems worth it at the time. That is the entire business model."

* * *

I will tell you what I did not tell him, since I am telling you.

I did not tell him about the Quiet Range. About the nights I've spent down here among the forgotten ledgers, reading the oldest acquisitions, the ones from two centuries back, the ones written in a hand that shakes. I have read the account of a kingdom ending mid-sentence. I have read inventory lists of a country — streets, bells, names, the specific blue of a specific door — itemized with the loving thoroughness of a tax assessor, because something needed to *eat* it and eating goes better with a catalogue.

I did not tell him that the final entry in the oldest ledger is not a description at all, but an address. That whatever sits beneath this city keeps its meal organized.

And I did not tell him about my daughter's name, which I have reconstructed letter by letter from the margins of my own old working notes — little doodles I made during long sessions, unconscious, a mother's hand writing itself while the rest of her was busy elsewhere. Four letters begin like water over stones. I have narrowed it to three possibilities. I recite them sometimes, at night, like a prayer with a coin flip at the end.

This is what the ledger leaves you: not nothing. Nothing would be mercy. It leaves you the *shape*, and lets you spend the rest of your life pressing your palm against it.

* * *

Before I left him, I did give Ulyana one true thing. I stood in the doorway of the transcription room with the lamplight behind me, and I said:

"A piece of advice, free of charge, which in this building makes it priceless. Read your contract. All of it. Especially the parts in small script, especially the clauses with words like *residual* and *accrued* and *in perpetuity*. You signed something once without finishing it — everyone here did, it's practically the entrance exam — and I promise you that the missing pages are the ones with your name on them."

He went still in that way of his, the way of a man doing sums he doesn't want the total of.

"Archivist," he said. "Why are you telling me this?"

Because you write like someone who checks his work. Because the boy trusts you and trust is a currency the Hollow accepts at par. Because someone told me once, in this same doorway, in these same words, and I smiled the way you're smiling now — politely, dismissively, a person declining a gift they'll spend their whole life trying to buy back.

"Because I am the archivist," I said, "and archiving is just memory with the sentiment removed. Good night, Coldharbour. Forty minutes. No more."

* * *

That night I went down to the Quiet Range and pulled the oldest ledger again — the shaking-hand one, the one I am not permitted to read and read anyway — and I sat with it under my lamp like a woman visiting a grave.

Near the end, in the margin beside the inventory of a drowned bell tower, someone — the shaking hand, the last transcriptionist of Ostmere, whoever she was — had written a note in the old tongue. I have translated it perhaps ten thousand times. It says:

*It is not a record. It is a mouth. We built it a mouth and taught it our names.*

Underneath, added later, in different ink, smaller, almost ashamed:

*I fed it my daughter's birthday so it would sleep another winter. Forgive me. Forgive me. Forgive*

The sentence stops there. Two hundred years, and the sentence still stops there.

I sat in the dark of the Quiet Range with forty thousand forgotten lives against my back, and I did what I do every night, which is check the margins of my memory for what's missing today. The library is stable. The wing that burned is still burned. My daughter is still a shape, not a face.

But tonight — tonight there was something else. A new absence. Small, recent, fresh-edged, like a tooth lost overnight.

I ran through the inventory of myself the way I run through everything: methodically, alphabetically, by shelf.

I no longer remember the ferryman's river.

Eleven years it has lived in me, silver and cold. Gone. And in its place, a fact, clean as a ledger line: *there was a river; I transcribed it.* The memory of the memory. The receipt without the goods.

The ink is not finished collecting from me.

And if it is coming back for its earlier payments — if the accounts are being called in order, oldest debts first — then it is working forward through my life, entry by entry, and I have done the arithmetic, because arithmetic is what I have instead of a daughter's face.

At the current rate, I have perhaps a season left of knowing her name began with water.

And Ulyana Coldharbour, who signed without reading, who is sitting upstairs tonight feeling a dead kingdom arrive in his chest wave after beautiful wave —

Ulyana has so much more to lose than I do.

I put the old ledger back in its place, and I climbed the stairs toward the transcription room, rehearsing how to make a clever young man afraid of a book.
