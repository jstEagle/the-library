## 8. Debts Written in Bone

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in the Ledger Guild's contract vaults, and I have come to believe it is not the absence of sound but the presence of attention. The ledgers down there are listening. They have been listening for two hundred years, and they are very good at it.

I told myself I was only going down to check a citation.

That's what I told Maren, too, when she caught me at the stair with a lamp and my heart behaving like something small and cornered. She looked at the lamp. She looked at me. She said nothing, which from Maren is a paragraph, and stepped aside.

* * *

Here is what had kept me awake since the boy arrived: the entry in my own handwriting that I did not write. I had spent three nights telling myself it was fatigue. Guild scribes develop hands like everyone develops habits — mine has written *Ulyana Coldharbour* so many times, on so many indenture renewals and interest confirmations and apology letters regarding late payments, that it could plausibly produce my signature without consulting me. Hands learn. That is practically the guild's founding principle.

But hands don't learn phrasing. And the entry — *Coldharbour, U., one (1) life, assessed at birth, held in trust* — had a phrasing I knew. It was the phrasing of the old ledgers, the ones from before the guild stopped letting anyone read them. The ones about Ostmere.

So: the contract vaults. Sub-level four. Where the guild keeps everything it has ever promised anyone, filed under their names like specimens under pins.

The clerk of record for sub-level four is a man named Ostrevve, who may be the most appropriately named person in Vaskarand and who has not blinked in front of witnesses in eleven years. He asked for my requisition slip. I gave him the one I'd forged, because I have learned forgery the way other children learn prayers — out of necessity, nightly, under supervision.

"Coldharbour," he said, reading it. "Family records?"

"An audit discrepancy."

"Ah." He said it the way priests say *amen*. "Discrepancies resolve themselves, usually. If you leave them alone."

"I'm paid to not leave them alone."

"You're not paid at all," he observed, with no malice whatsoever, because it was simply true. "You're indebted. There's a difference. Paid things belong to themselves."

He retrieved the file anyway. I have decided to believe this was mercy, though with Ostrevve it may equally have been curiosity, or the long slow boredom of a man who files promises for a living and wants, just once, to watch one break.

* * *

My family's file was thinner than I expected. That should have warned me. Thin documents in the guild mean either that nothing happened or that everything happened so early there wasn't yet paper to catch it.

The first page was my parents' marriage bond — standard, dull, both parties already carrying debt, which is how marriages get approved quickly here. The second page was dated nine months later.

It was titled **Instrument of Future Consideration**, which is guild language for *we will lend you money now against something that does not exist yet*, and the something that did not exist yet was me.

I read it standing up, because I suspected — correctly — that I would want to sit down eventually and preferred to choose my own moment.

The terms were simple, in the way that knives are simple. My mother and father received a discharge of their combined debts — forty-one marks, eleven shillings, an amount I have carried in my head since childhood the way other people carry songs. In exchange they conveyed to the Ledger Guild "all mnemonic rights, present and prospective, appertaining to the issue of this union, in perpetuity."

Mnemonic rights. Memory-rights. My memories.

Not my labor. Not my future earnings, which was common enough — half of Vaskarand is born already sold. My *memories*. Every recollection I would ever form, held in trust by the guild from the hour of my birth, assessable, collectible, drawable against.

There was a clause about the accrual rate. Memories, apparently, appreciate. A childhood is worth more than its weight in coin; a first love compounds; grief pays dividends. The guild had valued my mother's face, my father's voice, every friend I ever made, every night I wept over a ledger line, at rates laid out in an annex I could hear, faintly, breathing in the dark of the file like something asleep.

At the bottom, two signatures. My father's, careful and cramped. My mother's, which trailed off at the end the way her handwriting always did, as if she were already leaving the room.

And beneath them, countersigned on their behalf by the guild's registrar — a name I recognized, because he is still guildmaster now, two hundred years of office folded into one unaging man — Havel. And beside Havel's signature, the seal.

I had seen that seal before. Everyone in the guild has seen it; it stamps every contract, every debt notice, every foreclosure. A circle with a spiral inside, drawn inward.

I had also seen it three nights ago, pressed into the margin of a ledger entry describing something that fed on Ostmere until there was no Ostmere left to feed on.

* * *

I want to be precise here, because rage makes imprecise writers and I refuse to give it the satisfaction.

I had always understood my debt as a moral condition. That is the genius of the guild, and I say *genius* with hatred, which is a form of respect. Debt in Vaskarand is framed as failure — you borrowed, therefore you erred, therefore your repayment is penance. We are taught to feel our ledgers the way sinners feel their sins. I have lain awake more nights than I can count doing arithmetic on my own worth, arriving each time at a figure slightly larger than the night before, and concluding, as one does, that the fault was arithmetic's — no. Mine.

But my debt predates me. There was no error. There was no borrowing. There was a man and a woman drowning in forty-one marks and eleven shillings, and a guild that arrived with a bucket made of their own unborn child, and offered them a rope.

They took it. I do not blame them. I have tried to blame them, lying awake, because blame is easier than grief, but they were drowning and I was not yet anything at all — not a name, not a face, not a memory worth collecting. Just prospective value. Just collateral with a due date.

The guild had lent me to myself. That was the shape of it, once I sat down on the cold floor of sub-level four and let the document finish its work. Every memory I own, I own on credit. My mother singing off-key in the kitchen: drawn against, interest accruing. The first time I saw snow on the harbor: drawn against. The boy looking up at me through the loading-door mud with recognition in his face — that instant, that absurd instant of *knowing* him — already entered somewhere in a master ledger, already appreciating in value, already, possibly, promised onward.

Promised to whom?

You already know to whom. I knew too, standing there. Because the seal was the same. The circle and the inward spiral. The guild's mark and the mark in the oldest books beside descriptions of the Hollow — the thing beneath the city that ate a kingdom and forgot to stop being hungry.

They are the same system. That is the sentence I keep turning over like a coin I suspect is counterfeit. The Hollow devours memory; the guild lends it back at interest. What is a debt, after all, but a memory of what you owe? What is the guild's whole apparatus — the notices, the audits, the shame — but a machine for making people carry their pasts in a form someone else can seize?

Ostmere was not eaten and then exploited. Ostmere was eaten *by the same mouth*. The lending is the feeding. Every indentured child in Vaskarand, every widow foreclosed on, every scribe calculating his worth at three in the morning — we are not the guild's customers. We are its crop. And the Hollow below us is the granary.

* * *

I stayed down there a long time. Sub-level four does not encourage lingering — the lamps dim in sympathy with your hope, I swear it — but no one came for me, and I began to understand why. The ledgers were content. I was exactly where the system wanted me: holding the evidence of my own sale, unable to imagine any action it hadn't priced in.

Then I heard footsteps, and Maren sat down beside me on the floor without a word, which is how she does most things.

She didn't ask what I'd found. She read the instrument over my shoulder, slowly, her lips moving slightly at the legal Latin of it. When she reached the clause on accrual rates her lips stopped moving entirely.

"Mine is on level six," she said at last. "Different form. Same seal."

"Maren—"

"I transcribed for eleven years." Her voice was flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on a frozen lake they've seen others fall through. "Do you know what I was paid in, Ulyana? Forgiveness. Line by line. Each session, a little of my debt erased. Do you know where the debt went?"

I did not answer. Down in that cold, listening dark, I found I could not.

"It didn't go anywhere," she said. "Debt doesn't vanish. It *moves*. It moved into the boy's language, into the ink that writes itself, into whatever opens its mouth under this city when the ledgers fill." She closed the file gently, the way you close a coffin. "I had a daughter. I'm told. There's an entry in my hand about her birthday that I read every year like a stranger's diary. The guild holds the memory of her face as an asset, Ulyana. Assessed. Appreciating. I checked the valuation once." Her jaw tightened. "It's gone up."

We sat in silence, the two of us, amid two hundred years of other people's futures filed alphabetically.

"They'll collect him," I said finally. The boy. The last heir of a dead country, locked in a vault upstairs 'for appraisal.' "When the transcription's done. They'll collect all of him."

"Yes."

"And no one signed his papers either. He's not even collateral, he's — salvage. Flotsam from a swallowed kingdom, washed up at our loading door."

Maren stood, brushed off her skirts, and became once more the senior archivist, spine straight, face composed, the woman who remembers every ledger line and almost nothing else. But at the stair she paused.

"There's a word for what the guild did to you," she said. "For what it does to everyone. It's in the old ledgers, in the boy's language. I can't pronounce it anymore — I may never have been able to. But I remember what it means, because some meanings are too stubborn to be collected."

"What does it mean?"

"'To sell someone their own reflection and then charge them for the mirror.'" She almost smiled. "Two hundred years ago, a country refused the terms. You might look up what happened next. It's in the files."

She went up. I stayed.

Because she was wrong about one thing, bless her careful, hollowed-out heart. She said no one had signed the boy's papers. But I had. Three sessions of transcription, my hand moving with ink that wrote itself, my debt shrinking line by lovely line while something beneath the floor grew fatter on a child's stolen snow.

I had signed them in my own handwriting.

Whether or not I remembered doing it.

* * *

Upstairs, the guild slept, if guilds sleep, and I walked its corridors with a lamp and a decision being born somewhere under my ribs, in the place where a person keeps the things that haven't been assessed yet.

The master ledger stood open in the transcription room where I'd left it. The boy's memories marched across its pages in my hand — the bell tower, the snow, a mother's song in a language two centuries dead. Beautiful entries. Valuable entries. Each one appreciating.

I found a blank leaf at the back, where no auditor looks, and I did something I had never done in nine years of indenture.

I wrote a debt notice.

*From: U. Coldharbour, debtor.* *To: the Ledger Guild of Vaskarand, creditor.* *Regarding: one (1) life, sold before issuance, held in trust.* *Notice is hereby given that the collateral intends to contest the terms.*

Below it, where the guild's seal should go, I drew instead the only symbol I had from the boy's world: the little coiled knot he scratches in the dust when he thinks no one is watching, the one Maren says means *homecoming*.

Then I blew out the lamp and went to find the vault where they keep the heir of Ostmere, because I had finally done the only arithmetic that mattered, and it came out simple:

Whatever the Hollow is, whatever the guild is, they have been feeding on the wrong child's memories for two hundred years —

and tonight, for the first time, something in this building intended to feed back.
