## 9. The Bell That Rings Underwater

I have decided that remembering is a country, and I am its worst cartographer.

The scribe with the tired hands — Ulyana, though he does not know yet that I know his name before he tells me, and when he does tell me I will have to pretend to be surprised, which will be my first lie in this new language — says memory works like a ledger. You write the day down. The ink dries. The entry is finished.

Mine do not dry. Mine run backward off the page and pool on the floor, and lately they have begun to arrive *early*.

Here is what happened this morning, which has not happened yet:

A door opens. It is not a door in a wall; it is a door in the idea of a wall, and behind it there is snow falling upward, and someone's hand is holding mine, and the hand belongs to Ulyana, and his fingers are ink-stained to the second knuckle, which is how I will always know them, even after — 

No. Even after what? I don't have an "after" for that sentence. The memory stops at the threshold like a horse refusing a bridge. There is a word I almost remember, a long word made of many smaller words stitched together, and saying it would be like saying *home*, except louder, except with the whole sky listening.

Then the memory ends, and I am back in the vault, sitting on a cot that smells of cedar and old arithmetic, waiting for breakfast and for whatever the dark under the floor wants from me today.

I have named the dark. The scribes call it the Hollow, in the way you might call a storm *weather*, politely, as if naming it made it smaller. But I was there when it ate my city. I felt it think. And I think it knows I've started dreaming forward, because last night the cot vibrated like a struck drumhead, very softly, the way a dog trembles in its sleep.

It's hungry. It's been hungry for two hundred years. And now it can smell tomorrow on me.

* * *

The morning inventory came to me early, as all things come to me early now.

Master Quillon unlocked the vault at the seventh bell — I knew he would, because I had already watched him do it twice, once from inside this moment and once from somewhere slightly ahead of myself, watching over his shoulder like a boy peeking through a keyhole into his own life. He carried bread and a cup of milk and the expression of a man who had rehearsed being kind and wasn't sure the rehearsal had taken.

"You'll want to eat," he said. "Appraisal continues today."

"I know," I said. "You'll ask me about the river first. Then the harvest festival. Then my mother."

Quillon went still the way furniture goes still. "Who told you—"

"Nobody. It already happened. I just got here late." I took the bread because my stomach insisted, even while the rest of me stood slightly outside my body taking notes. "That's the trouble with me, Master Quillon. I'm always late to my own past. It arrives before I do."

He wrote something on a slate. I read it upside down, which was easy, because I had also already watched him write it: *Precognitive recall? Report to Havel.* Underneath, in smaller letters, he had added: *The Hollow stirred at dawn. Foundations hummed. Third time this week.*

Third time. I could have told him it would be four by evening. I did tell him, actually, later, when it was four by evening, and he looked at me the way people look at weather reports that turn out to be true, which is to say gratefully and with a low continuous dread.

Here is the thing nobody in this guild understands, or perhaps they understand it perfectly and simply prefer not to say it out loud where the walls can hear: everything I remember, something else remembers too. My memories are not private property. They are a table set for two. When I dream of snow, the dark beneath the floor tastes snow. When I sing my mother's song under my breath — I only do it when no one is listening, which means I did it eleven times yesterday, because no one is ever listening, they are only *recording* — the dark hears it too, and turns it over, and wants more.

And lately, when I remember things that haven't happened, the dark gets them fresh. Unspoiled. A meal that hasn't even been eaten yet.

I am not a boy to these people. I understand that now. I am a window left open in winter, and something outside is smelling the kitchen.

* * *

The appraisal room smelled of lamp oil and fear that had been aged properly, like cheese. Ulyana sat across from me with the master ledger open, and I watched him try not to look at me the way you try not to look at a hole in the floor.

"Tell me about the bell tower," he said. His pen hovered. "Start anywhere."

"Anywhere," I repeated. "All right. Here's anywhere: In three days you will drop your pen during transcription and it will roll under the table, and when you reach for it you'll find a coin from Ostmere, a silver stag with antlers like branches, and you won't know how it got there, and neither will I, and we will both decide not to mention it."

Ulyana's pen did not move. "That hasn't happened."

"No," I agreed. "Give it three days."

He set the pen down carefully, as if it might go off. "What else do you see?"

"That's just it. It isn't *seeing*. Seeing is light doing things. This is—" I searched for the word and found it arriving early, which was fitting. "This is *remembering* things that haven't gotten around to happening. Like the past sent invitations and I opened them by mistake." I picked at the blanket's hem. "Do you want to know what happens to you?"

"Does something happen to me?"

"Everything happens to you. That's the problem. But specifically—" and here I hesitated, because some futures feel private even when they belong to other people, "—you open a door. Not one of these doors. A real one. The real one. And your hand shakes, and you do it anyway, and behind the door is my whole country, put back together like a dropped plate, every piece in the right place, and none of the pieces are people who are alive. They're just... remembered so hard they hold their shape." I looked up at him. "You cry. I don't mean that as a criticism. It seemed like good crying."

Ulyana sat very still. Somewhere above us, faintly, a chandelier swayed — I heard the crystals tick against each other, tick-tick-tick, though there was no draft and no footsteps and no bell ringing anywhere in Vaskarand at that hour.

Neither of us said anything about it. We were both becoming experts in the art of not mentioning things.

"The guildmaster will want to know about this," he said finally.

"The guildmaster already knows. He knew before I did. He's known since the day I arrived, possibly longer, possibly since before I was born, which sounds impossible until you meet him." I hugged my knees. "Ulyana. Can I say something strange?"

"I suspect you're contractually obligated to."

"When I remember forward, the floor gets warmer. Just a little. Like something underneath rolling over in its sleep and pressing closer to the fire." I pressed my palm flat against the stone. "It's closer now."

He put his own hand on the floor beside mine. I don't think he meant to. I think his body knew things his mind hadn't approved. And I saw — remembering-forward is generous like that, it lets you borrow — I saw the exact moment, weeks from now, when he would press that same palm against a different floor, far below this one, in a palace that drowned without drowning, and the stone would be warm as skin.

I didn't tell him that part. Some memories you keep in your mouth until they're ripe.

* * *

That night the bell rang underwater.

I use the phrase literally, because I don't know another way to say it. There was no sound. Sound requires air, and the bell I heard — felt — *remembered* — was buried beneath the city, beneath the guild's foundations, beneath the drowned palace where my ancestors had once bargained away the deed to their own souls, though I didn't know that yet; I would learn it standing in the dark heart of everything, and I am telling you this out of order because that is simply how I live now, chronologically, like everyone else, plus sideways.

The bell rang, and nothing rang.

But every chandelier in the Guildhouse swayed. Every glass in the pantry chimed against its neighbor. The ink in forty wells shivered into little concentric rings, like rain falling upward into the bottles. And in the vault, I lay awake and listened to a silence so loud it had texture, and I understood — with the terrible clarity of a child who has already lost everything and therefore has nothing left to protect — what was happening.

It was waking up. Not all at once. The Hollow was waking the way ice melts: invisibly, continuously, and then suddenly all at once, and by then the river has already moved.

And it was waking *early* because of me. Because every future I remembered, it tasted. Because my presence here — the last living heir of Ostmere, a walking reliquary of a dead country — was to the Hollow what the smell of bread is to the starving. I hadn't meant to wake it. But hunger doesn't require permission, and appetite doesn't check the calendar.

I sat up in the dark and did the bravest thing available to me, which was nothing dramatic at all. I whispered my mother's song. All of it. Every verse, including the one about the river keeping our names safe, which I now suspected was less lullaby than legal document.

If it was going to feed on me, let it choke a little on the truth.

* * *

In the morning — which arrived on schedule, unlike most of my thoughts — the guildmasters held an emergency convocation. I know this because I dreamed it the night before, in detail, down to the coffee ring Havel left on the agenda, and also because Maren told me afterward, in her flat archivist's voice that hides tenderness the way a ledger hides debts: in plain sight, in small numbers, adding up.

"They're frightened of you," she said. She brought me tea she pretended was standard issue, though standard issue did not include honey. "Not of what you are. Of what you *know before it happens*. A guild can price the past, boy. We have rates. But the future—" her jaw tightened, "—the future is uncollateralized. It terrifies men whose entire religion is arithmetic."

"What will they do?"

She looked at me for a long moment, and I remembered-forward, just slightly, just enough to see her choosing what to lose, later, and I had to grip the cot to stay in the present tense.

"They'll feed it faster," she said. "They'll convince themselves it's mercy. Men always do the arithmetic before they do the ethics; it's easier that way." She set down the cup. Her hand was steady, which cost her something; I could see the cost. "When the time comes, boy — and it's coming, I've read enough margins to smell it — remember that doors only open from the side where someone is willing to knock."

"What's on the other side of the door?"

Maren smiled, and it was the saddest, most precise thing I had ever seen. "A name," she said. "Whole. For the first time in two hundred years."

* * *

That afternoon, the chandeliers began to sway in earnest.

Not one. Not gently. All of them, throughout the Guildhouse, swinging in slow unison like a congregation turning to look at something behind them. Scribes stopped mid-sentence. Ink froze mid-word. Master Quillon dropped a tray of appraisals and did not stoop for them. And through it all ran that soundless ringing, that bell beneath the waterline of the world, tolling a hour that no clock in Vaskarand admitted to keeping.

I stood at the barred window of the vault and watched dust fall from the ceiling in fine grey threads, and I remembered forward — hard, deliberately, shoving through the fog of tomorrows the way you shove through a crowd — looking for the door. *My* door. *Our* door.

I found it. It was far. It was paid for. Oh, it was terribly, unforgivably paid for, and the currency was a man's whole self, spent syllable by syllable by a scribe who would end with no name and consider it a fair trade, and I tried to hate that future and found I could only grieve it, the way you grieve a gift too large to carry.

But I found something else too, farther along the shelf of not-yet, and this I pulled down and examined with shaking hands:

The Hollow, rising. Not to devour.

To be *witnessed*.

Two hundred years it had eaten a country, and no one had ever once turned around and said: yes. Yes, you did this. Yes, we erased them. Yes, it was a wound and we called it progress. The monster and the kingdom were the same bargain, the same broken promise, the same debt compounded past all redemption — and what it wanted, what it had *always* wanted beneath the hunger, was so simple and so awful that I sat down on the cold floor and laughed until the laugh turned into something else entirely.

To be remembered. Even as a wound. *Especially* as a wound.

The chandeliers stilled. The bell beneath the water fell silent, mid-toll, like a breath held.

And far above me, in a high office full of ledgers and lamplight, Guildmaster Havel set down his pen, looked at the trembling ink in his well, and spoke aloud to the empty room, in a language two hundred years dead, one syllable of a name he had spent his whole long life feeding away.

I remembered him doing it.

Which means, I suppose, that somewhere ahead of me, I am already forgiven for what I'm about to cause.

The floor grew warm beneath my feet, patient as a held note.

Three days, I thought. Three days until the pen rolls under the table and the silver stag rolls out.

Time to start counting down instead of counting up.
