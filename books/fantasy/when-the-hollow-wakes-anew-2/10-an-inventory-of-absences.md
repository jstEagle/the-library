## 10. An Inventory of Absences

There is a particular kind of insomnia reserved for people who work with paper. It is not the insomnia of guilt or grief — those at least have the decency to be dramatic. It is the insomnia of the half-remembered footnote, the citation you can almost see, the entry that sat on the third shelf of your mind and has since been moved by an unlicensed hand. You lie awake not because something is wrong but because something is *misfiled*, and a misfiled thing, in my experience, is always more dangerous than a lost one.

Three nights after Maren told me the ledger doesn't record memory but eats it — three nights after she said it in the tone one uses to remark that it looks like rain — I lay in my cot above the counting room doing arithmetic I did not want to do.

If the ledger eats memory, whose memory had produced the entry in my own handwriting that I never wrote?

And a second question, quieter and worse: if the ledger ate something out of me, how would I know? Absence does not ache. That's the whole trick of it. A missing tooth aches; a missing memory doesn't, because there is nothing left to do the missing. You cannot miss what has been invoiced.

I got up. I dressed. I took the stair down past the counting room, past the appraisal hall where the bottled thunder still ticked faintly in its jar like a wasp in amber, and I let myself into the Deep Stacks with Maren's spare key, which she had pressed into my hand that same evening without a word, the way you'd pass salt.

She was already there, of course. Senior archivists are like cats: they arrive first and pretend they were always going to.

"You look terrible," she said, by way of good morning.

"I've been lying awake wondering if I used to be someone more interesting."

"Mm. Everyone in this building wonders that eventually." She lit the second lamp. "Sit down, Coldharbour. We have perhaps four hours before the night clerks finish their supper, and we're going to need all of them."

"For what?"

"For the most tedious project ever undertaken by two people who should know better." She set a ledger on the table between us — not the master ledger, thank every merciful power, but one of the old concordances, its spine cracked like river ice. "We are going to count holes."

* * *

I should explain what I mean by erased pages, because the guild does not erase things crudely. We are not vandals. Vandalism is for people with convictions.

When the Ledger Guild removes an entry, it does not tear the page out. Tearing leaves a scar you can count — you can hold a book to the light and see where the binding remembers. No. The guild *palimpsests*. The page is scraped, washed with gall and lye, dried under weights, and returned to circulation carrying something new. To any auditor, the ledger remains whole. But vellum has a longer memory than ink. Scrape a page and hold it over a flame at the correct angle — not too close, never too close — and the pressure of the old letters rises up in the grease of the skin like bruises coming out on flesh. The words aren't gone. They're only shy.

Maren called this "an inventory of absences." She said it the way she said everything, which is to say as though she were reading from a shipping manifest, but her hands shook slightly as she laid out the tools: the tallow lamp, the flat iron, the soft brushes, the trays of wheat-starch paste for when we inevitably damaged something.

"Where do we start?" I asked.

"Where all frauds start," she said. "The founding."

The guild's official founding record occupies exactly one page in the oldest public ledger and says almost nothing: a date, eleven signatures, a charter clause about "the faithful custody of difficult acquisitions." No founding patron. No founding debt. For an institution that exists to record what everyone else owes, we had been remarkably incurious about what we ourselves had paid.

"Eleven signatures," Maren said. "I have counted them since I was an apprentice. Eleven names, none of which appear anywhere else in the historical record. Not in a baptism roll, not in a deed, not in a gravestone. Eleven people who existed for exactly one document and then never again."

"That's not suspicious at all."

"It's the most suspicious thing I've ever seen, and I once catalogued a saint's second shadow." She pulled the founding ledger toward her. "Now. Hold the page flat. Don't breathe on it. And whatever happens, don't think about anything you'd hate to lose."

"Why would you say that?"

"Because it helps," she said, which was not an answer, and turned the page toward the flame.

* * *

The heat rose off the vellum in slow ghosts.

First came the scrape-marks themselves, silver-grey, like frost climbing a window. Then, as Maren tilted the leaf through the lamplight, the frost resolved into strokes, strokes into letters, letters into lines — the old script, the pre-Guild hand they teach you to recognize and never to read, and beneath the official charter, in a different ink that had soaked deeper than any lye could reach, a second text.

I read it aloud before I understood it, because reading aloud is how scribes steady themselves, and I needed steadying.

"*Received in settlement of the Ostmere obligation: one kingdom, whole, together with its roads, its bells, its names, and its dead.*"

I stopped. Maren didn't tell me to go on. She was already pulling the next ledger from the shelf, the one from forty years later, and laying it open beside the first.

"*Interest accrued on the Ostmere obligation:*" read the second, "*further remembrance payable quarterly.*"

"Remembrance," I said. "As a line item."

"What did you expect?" Maren's voice was very level, which is how I had learned to recognize her fury; it went flat and precise, like paper before a cut. "We're a lending house, Coldharbour. Everything is a line item. Kingdoms. Mothers. You."

I didn't ask what she meant by *you*. I wasn't ready, and she saw that I wasn't ready, and for once she spared me.

We worked. The hours went by in the rhythm of pages: lift, tilt, read, transcribe, paste where we'd cracked the skin. And the fragments assembled themselves across the table like the skeleton of some enormous animal being articulated bone by bone.

The guild had not found the Hollow. The guild had been *founded* to manage it — or rather, to manage the debt the Hollow represented. Two centuries ago, something had eaten a country, and eleven people had signed a document acknowledging the transaction, and then those eleven people had been struck from every record except their own, because the first act of any lender is to obscure the original crime. The guild did not inherit the Hollow's table scraps. The guild was built on them, of them, out of them. Every coin in our vaults had passed through the shape of that absence. Every impossible acquisition — the bottled thunder, the saint's second shadow, the mirror that showed yesterday — every one was, in the oldest ledgers' phrasing, "*residue of the Ostmere obligation*." Leavings. Crumbs scraped from the table of a meal.

"We're a junk shop," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "Two hundred years of solemn custody, and we're a junk shop selling the crumbs of a swallowed country."

"No," Maren said quietly. "Worse. Look at the fourth fragment. The one from the year of the Great Audit."

I looked. Held it to the flame. Read it, though my eyes wanted very much to be anywhere else.

"*The city of Vaskarand is granted charter upon the foundations of the drowned palace, its streets laid upon the sunken avenues, its guildhall raised above the throne court, its deepest vault adjoining the feast-hall of the Hollow, that the obligation may be serviced conveniently at source.*"

I set the page down very carefully, because if I had set it down carelessly I think I would have thrown it.

Vaskarand was not built near the ruin. Vaskarand was built *as* the ruin — a lid on a pot, a tablecloth draped over a corpse. The streets I walked every morning followed the drowned avenues. The counting room floor sat over the throne court. The loading door where a mud-caked boy had appeared speaking a language no living tongue should know — I thought of him standing there, small and patient, and something in my chest turned over hard. He hadn't wandered to the guild by chance. He had come home by the only door left, and we had locked him in a vault directly above the thing that ate his country.

"The guildhall," I said. "Havel's office. That would be—"

"Above the throne court. Yes." Maren folded her hands. "He sits at the exact center of it. He must know. He has always known."

"And the boy—"

"—is sleeping thirty feet above the thing that ate his family," Maren finished, "with a transcription contract binding his memories into the ledger, and the ledger feeding them downward. Quarterly remembrance payable at source." She closed the old ledger with a sound like a door shutting. "Coldharbour, do you understand what we are? All these years I thought we were a parasite on the city. We're not. We're the *digestive tract*. The city eats its own forgetting and calls it commerce, and we are the organ that processes it, and everything we have ever appraised or catalogued or lent against was once somebody's *home*."

The lamp guttered. Neither of us moved to fix it.

I thought about my debt. I have thought about my debt every waking hour since I was nine years old, when the Guild first stamped the figure into the front of my mind alongside my name, so that I have never once in my life been able to wonder who I am without also wondering what I owe. I had always assumed the debt was misfortune. Bad luck, bad parents, bad interest rates — the ordinary weather of being poor. But sitting in that lamplight with the ghost-text rising off the pages, I understood for the first time that my debt was not weather. It was architecture. The guild had not stumbled into the Hollow's service. The guild had drawn up the papers, countersigned the hunger, and built its counting room on top of the wound so that no one would ever have to look at it while it bled.

And I was a pen in that counting room. A well-behaved, debt-bound, self-scraping palimpsest of a man, transcribing a child's memories into the mouth of the thing below, and calling it employment.

"I want to burn it down," I said, and was surprised to find I meant it — me, Ulyana Coldharbour, who returns borrowed pencils, who apologizes to furniture.

"Burning it down feeds it," Maren said. "Fire is just another kind of forgetting. No." She began stacking the ledgers, restoring each to its false place on the shelf, erasing even our trespass. "You don't burn a ledger, Coldharbour. You *audit* it. Line by line. Until the numbers stop agreeing with each other and the whole beautiful lie collapses under the weight of its own arithmetic."

"Against Havel?"

"Especially against Havel." She paused, hands still on the last ledger. "There's something else. Something I found three nights ago and haven't told you, because I kept hoping I'd dreamed it."

"Tell me anyway."

She opened the last ledger again, to a marker near the end, and tilted the page to the flame one final time. The ghost-text came up slower here, fainter, as if even the vellum were reluctant.

It was a list. A short one. Under the heading "*Transcriptionists, prior*" were four names, each struck through. The fifth name, not yet struck through, was mine.

The sixth name had been written and then scraped away so thoroughly that no fire could raise it. But the scraping tool had slipped, once, at the end of the stroke, and left a single surviving letter pressed deep into the skin:

*M.*

"There have only ever been five of us," Maren said. "Four transcriptionists before you. I was the fourth." Her face in the lamplight was perfectly composed and perfectly white. "But the ledger lists six. Someone was a transcriptionist before the guild was founded. Someone wrote the first entries. Someone whose name even the scrapers weren't allowed to take completely."

"Who signs like that?"

"Nobody alive," Maren said. "Which means, Coldharbour, that either our founding records are lies from top to bottom—" she closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin, "—or the person who taught the Hollow to eat is still on the payroll."

Above us, faint through six floors of stone and drowned palace, a chandelier swayed on its chain. There was no wind in the Deep Stacks. There never is.

And somewhere overhead — or perhaps underneath; by then I honestly couldn't say — something rang once, softly, like a bell heard from underwater.

Maren blew out the lamps.

"Go to bed," she said. "Tomorrow, ask the guildmaster about the founding. Watch his hands when you do."

"Why his hands?"

"Because honest men talk with their faces," she said, "and liars talk with their hands. And Havel, Coldharbour, has the most eloquent hands I have ever seen."
