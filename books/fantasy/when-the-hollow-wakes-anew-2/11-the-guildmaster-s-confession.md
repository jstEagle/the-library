## 11. The Guildmaster's Confession

There is a particular pleasure in being the oldest thing in a room.

I do not mean that vainly. I mean it the way a stone might mean it, or an anchor, or the foundation stone of a building that has outlived its architect. When you are two hundred and eleven years old and everyone around you is temporary, you develop a certain tenderness for permanence. For ledgers. For things written down in ink that does not fade.

Ulyana Coldharbour came to my office at the fourth bell of the morning, as I had instructed Maren to arrange, and he looked — as he always looks — like a man doing arithmetic behind his eyes. That is what I have always liked about him. The boy is a romantic; Maren is a ruin; but Ulyana counts. A counting man can be reasoned with. A counting man understands that everything has a price, and that someone, somewhere, must pay it.

"Guildmaster," he said. "You wanted to see me."

"Sit, Coldharbour. Would you take tea?"

"I'd take answers."

I smiled at that. "Then we shall have to negotiate. Tea first. Answers after. You'll find that's how most of the world works, though I grant you the guild has refined the process into something of an art."

* * *

Let me tell you how old I am, because Ulyana did not know, and because the knowing changes everything.

I was born in Ostmere, in the last hour of its existence, which makes me — depending on your accounting — either the luckiest man alive or the unluckiest. My mother was a laundress in the palace city of Vellharrow. She had crossed the border three days earlier with nothing but me inside her and a bundle of linens she intended to sell. She was in the birthing house when it happened.

You will want to know what *it* is. Everyone does. But there is no word for it in any living language, because every living language conspired to forget it. The closest I can come is this: imagine hunger given patience. Imagine appetite that does not chase but simply waits, and while it waits, it *unmakes*. Not violently. Violence leaves evidence. The Hollow — that is the guild's name for it, coined by my predecessor, who had a flair for the dramatic — the Hollow simply subtracts. A city stood there. Then it didn't. And more terrible than that: no one noticed. Two hundred thousand lives, an entire kingdom with its own kings and songs and grievances against its neighbors, and the world closed over the absence like water over a dropped stone.

My mother screamed her way through labor while her homeland ceased to be remembered by everyone she had ever known. She herself only survived the forgetting because she was outside the borders when they went down — and because I was arriving, and arrival, I have found, is very difficult to interrupt.

The guild found us. Of course it did. The guild has always been exactly where the Hollow feeds, like a gull following the plough.

"You're telling me," Ulyana said, setting down his teacup with the careful precision of a man who suspects his hands might betray him, "that you were born in a country that doesn't exist."

"A country that never existed," I corrected gently. "That is not the same thing. Ostmere exists now only as a wound. It was erased so thoroughly that history itself bent around the gap. There are treaties in this building that mention clauses about Ostmere without mentioning Ostmere. There are border maps with rivers that flow to nowhere, and the cartographers who drew them slept soundly. Erasure, Coldharbour, is the most complete art ever devised. Its masterpieces leave no trace of the artist."

"Except you."

"Except me," I agreed. "And except the boy."

And there it was. He flinched. Good. I needed him flinching — not from cruelty, but because a flinching man listens, and what I had to say required listening more than anything I have said in fifty years of guildmastership.

* * *

Here is what the guild does, stripped of its ledgers and its marble halls and its self-importance: we keep the monster asleep.

The Hollow sleeps when it is fed. Not flesh — it has no interest in flesh, which is why it is worse than any beast of that kind. It feeds on memory. Specific memory, in fact: memories of Ostmere. Every fragment of that vanished kingdom that still survives anywhere — in a song misremembered, a surname whose origin nobody questions, a child's nonsense rhyme, a pattern woven into a carpet — is a crumb on the world's table, and the Hollow smells every one of them. Left unfed, it stirs. Stirring, it takes. And it does not take selectively.

For two hundred years the guild has managed this diet. We collect impossible things, Coldharbour — bottled thunder, saints' shadows, all the curiosities that make our acquisitions ledger famous among the few who know of us — because impossible things accrete impossible memories around themselves. We harvest those. We distill them. We feed the Hollow through the roots of this building, which go down considerably farther than any of my archivists suspect, and it sleeps, and the world goes on remembering everything else, which is the entire point.

"You've been feeding it the boy's memories," Ulyana said quietly. "In the transcriptions."

"Yes."

"The sessions I've been doing. The debt-forgiveness contract—"

"Was entirely genuine. Your debts are forgiven as specified. I am a scrupulous creditor, Coldharbour; it is one of perhaps three virtues I possess. Everything else in the arrangement was also genuine. The boy's memories are the richest feeding we have ever had. Firsthand material. Living bloodline. Do you know how thin our supply had grown? In my grandfather-master's day we could scrape together a feast from a single recovered artifact. Now I send teams across three continents chasing fragments — a coin here, a half-legible ballad there. And then this child walks up to our loading door speaking the language itself, carrying whole decades of it in his skull." I spread my hands. "You must understand what that is like for a man in my position. It is as if a starving garrison woke to find a granary at the gates."

"And when the granary is empty?"

"Ah," I said. "There is the question, isn't it? There is always the question."

* * *

I want to be fair to Ulyana, because fairness matters to me more than he yet understands. He sat in that chair and hated me — I could feel it coming off him like heat off a stove — and his hatred was honest, and honest hatred is worth more than most love. But he did not yet understand the shape of the choice, because he believed there was a version of events where no one paid.

There is never a version where no one pays. I have had two centuries to look for it. I have read every ledger, including the ones written in languages older than the Hollow, and I can report with professional confidence: the universe balances its books. Someone always pays. The only question within your power to answer is *who*, and *how much*, and *whether they knew*.

"If we don't feed it," Ulyana said, "what happens?"

"Vaskarand happens," I said. "First. The Hollow nests beneath this city because this city was built — deliberately, by my predecessors — atop the drowned palace of Vellharrow. Proximity is control, in matters of appetite. If it wakes hungry, it will begin here, and it will begin with forgetting. Small things first. Names. Faces. The way home from market. Then larger things. Then the city itself, subtracted, and the world closing over it, and no one — Coldharbour, *no one* — will grieve Vaskarand, because grief requires memory and there will be none. A hundred thousand people, unmade twice over: once in fact, once in remembrance. And then it moves on. And the next city. And the next. Until the world is a smooth blank page with nothing written on it at all."

He was silent for a long moment. Outside my window, the morning bells rang, and I confess I listened to them with particular attention. Bells are a luxury. Appetites do not respect them.

"So the boy," he said finally. "You're going to feed the boy to it."

"I am going to give the boy's memories to it, yes. Gradually. Over years, if we can manage it, which is far kinder than the alternative. And when the feeding is complete — when the last heir of Ostmere has surrendered the last memory of Ostmere — the kingdom will truly end. Cleanly. Painlessly, as such things go. And the Hollow will sleep for another century, and children will grow up in Vaskarand who never learn how narrow their escape was." I leaned forward. "Do you know what I call that, Coldharbour?"

"I'm sure you'll tell me."

"Mercy." And I meant it. God help me, I meant it with my whole ancient heart. "One child's past, weighed against every future that will ever happen. One kingdom already dead, spent to save all the living ones. Show me the arithmetic where that is not mercy, and I will retire tomorrow."

"That's not—" He stopped. Recalculated. I watched him do it; it is my favorite spectacle. "That's not arithmetic. He's a *person*."

"He is," I agreed, "a person. So were the hundred thousand of Vellharrow. So was my mother, who died when I was nine, whispering the lullabies of a country no one else had ever heard of, in a language that by then existed only in her mouth and mine. I remember them, Coldharbour. I am the only creature on this earth who remembers them, and let me tell you what that memory is worth: it is worth nothing. It buys nothing. It saves no one. Memory hoarded is memory wasted. Memory *spent* — memory converted, at great cost, into continued existence for everyone else — that is the only charity the universe honors." I poured myself more tea. My hands do not shake. They haven't shaken in a century, and I sometimes regret it. "I have fed that thing my mother's voice, note by note, over forty years. I kept the last verse until I was ninety, and then I gave it too, because sentiment is a debt like any other, and I pay my debts. Tell me again that I don't understand what I'm asking of the boy."

For the first time since he'd entered, he had nothing to count.

* * *

He rallied, of course. I would have been disappointed otherwise.

"The transcription sessions," he said. "When I transcribe his memories — where do they go? What happens to the ledgers?"

"Routine archival storage," I said, and my voice was smooth as a settled account.

He looked at me. I looked at my tea. There was a silence with a great deal of interest accruing in it.

"You're lying," he said. "Or rather — you're telling me something true-shaped. Where do they *go*, Guildmaster?"

And here I made an error, and I record it here because even I am owed an honest ledger. I told him the truth. Not all of it — not the part that would have broken him, not yet — but enough.

"They go down," I said. "Everything goes down eventually. The ledgers are not records, Coldharbour. They are rations. We write the memories down because writing preserves them long enough to be... withdrawn. Consumed. The guild did not invent this system. The guild *is* this system. We were founded — by men who understood far better than I the nature of the bargain — as a kitchen for that thing beneath us. Our oldest charter is a menu."

I expected outrage. Instead he asked the question I should have feared more:

"The entry in my handwriting. In my own hand, in the audit ledger. The one I never wrote. Was that a ration too?"

I set down my cup. Somewhere below us — three floors, a sub-basement, and a hundred fathoms of drowned palace below us — something turned over in its sleep, and the chandelier above my desk gave the faintest shiver, though there was no draft.

"What did it say?" I asked him.

"It said: *the scribe remembers his mother's face, and the smell of her hands, and the name she called him.*" His voice did not break, which took, I suspect, considerable effort. "It was dated yesterday. It was appraised. It was valued. It was filed under pending withdrawal."

I am an old man. Old men are permitted certain silences. I gave myself one.

"Coldharbour," I said at last, gently, and I promise you the gentleness was real, "when you signed the indenture, did you read clause nineteen?"

"There is no clause nineteen. The page ends at eighteen."

"The page ends at eighteen," I agreed, "because clause nineteen is written on the next page, and the binding is designed to discourage curiosity. Clause nineteen states that the guild may assess collateral value against any asset named in the scribe's own hand, whether or not the scribe recalls writing it." I met his eyes. "Your parents sold your memory-rights at your birth, Ulyana. You have been guild property since before you could walk. Everything you have ever loved is already in inventory. It always has been. I did not put it there. I have merely, for fifty years, tried to spend the collection as slowly and as painlessly as possible, so that the rest of the world could go on not knowing it exists."

He stood. He did not shout. I respected him for it, and feared him for it, in roughly equal measure — shouting men have spent their anger, but quiet men are still doing sums.

"I'm going to get him out," Ulyana said.

"No," I said, with all the kindness at my disposal, which is more than he thinks. "You are going to go back to your desk, and you are going to continue the transcriptions, because the alternative is that I assign them to Maren, who will do it competently and be hollowed out entirely by spring, and because the Hollow is waking early, Coldharbour — you felt the bell just now, everyone in the building felt it — and the boy's presence accelerates it, and every hour we debate mercy is an hour closer to the morning when I have no choices left at all. I have carried this alone for fifty years. I would genuinely prefer not to carry it badly. Help me carry it well."

He walked to the door. His hand was on the handle when I spoke once more, because fairness demanded it, and because some part of me — the part born in a birthing house in Vellharrow while the world forgot my mother's country — wanted, desperately, to be stopped.

"The door you're looking for," I said. "Ostmere's true name. The original way in and out of a country that no longer exists. Yes, it's real. Yes, it's in the ledgers, shattered across ten thousand entries. And yes, Ulyana — the Hollow ate the final syllable two hundred years ago, along with the founding memory that holds it, and the only place that syllable still exists is in the belly of the thing you're trying to rob."

He went pale. He went very still. And then he did the thing I will think about for whatever remains of my impossibly long life.

He smiled.

"Thank you, Guildmaster," he said. "Now I know it's real."

The door closed behind him. Below my feet, faint as a heartbeat underwater, something vast began to wake — and for the first time in fifty years, Guildmaster Havel reached for the ledger of his own memories, and began to calculate how much of himself he had left to give.
