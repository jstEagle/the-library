## 12. A Door Out of Nowhere

There is a particular species of insomnia that belongs exclusively to clerks. It is not the romantic kind — not the tossing and turning of lovers or the haunted pacing of generals. No. It is the insomnia of a man who has spent nine hours adding columns of numbers and has just realized, somewhere around hour seven, that the columns do not balance, and that they will never balance, and that this fact will follow him into his dreams like a debt collector with excellent stamina.

I had that insomnia now, but for once it was not my own arithmetic keeping me awake.

It was Ostmere's.

Three nights since Maren had pressed her hand flat against my ledger — the private one I kept sewn into my coat lining, because even in a guild built on recording everything, a man must be allowed a few unrecorded thoughts — and said, in that voice like dust settling: *The ledger doesn't record memory, Ulyana. It eats it.* Three nights since I had learned that my entire life was collateral, sold before my first breath by parents I could no longer ask about without feeling something in my chest grind like bad gears. Three nights since I had started doing what desperate men in Vaskarand have always done when the legitimate doors close.

I started reading the old books. The *really* old ones.

* * *

The Ledger Guild's deep archive is not open to junior scribes. Or senior scribes. Or, officially, to anyone at all, since the fire of 1411 that officially destroyed the Pre-Consolidation Collection. Officially, the Pre-Consolidation Collection is ash. Unofficially, ash does not have a smell of vellum and old salt, and official ash does not live behind a door on Sublevel Four that requires three keys, a spoken phrase, and the willingness to lie to a night-warden whose salary I had been quietly supplementing from my expense account for two years.

The supplement had been an act of petty corruption. It turned out to be the best investment of my life, which tells you everything about the moral character of this story.

The archive beneath Sublevel Four was cold in the way that places are cold when they have been forgotten by the heating budget and by God in roughly the same decade. Shelves ran back into darkness farther than lamplight cared to follow. And the books — the books were wrong. Not damaged. Wrong. Ledgers from before the Consolidation, before the Guild standardized its ink and its cruelty, and every single one of them had gaps. Pages razored out. Lines struck through with a violence you don't usually see in bookkeeping. Whole signatures excised so cleanly that the binding still creaked with the ghost of their weight.

I had been through forty-one volumes in three nights, cataloguing the absences, and I want to tell you about the moment the pattern arrived, because it did not arrive the way discoveries do in novels. In novels, the scholar gasps and knocks over an inkwell. What actually happened was that I was cross-referencing shipping manifests from the year 203 — dull work, the kind of work I could do with half my brain while the other half worried about a boy locked in a vault who was losing words the way other children lose teeth — and I noticed that the missing entries were never random.

They were always exactly where a word should be.

Not sentences. Not paragraphs. *Words.* Single entries cut from the middle of otherwise intact inventories. A manifest would read: *Received, six barrels of pitch, four coils of rope, [ABSENT], one crate of dried cod.* And the absent entry, when I checked the guild's master index — the great alphabetical catalogue that listed every acquisition ever recorded — the index would give me a page number, and the page would be gone, but the index entry itself survived, and the index entry always began the same way:

**OST—**

Ostmere. The index entries were fragments of the name. Every erased word, indexed under its first letters, and the first letters were always *ost*, or *mer*, or syllables that fit between them like bones of the same hand.

I sat down on the cold floor of a forbidden archive and laughed until it turned into something else, because I understood. Two hundred years ago, when they unmade Ostmere — when they ate it out of maps and treaties and mothers' mouths — they didn't destroy the name. They couldn't. Names like that don't die; they're load-bearing. So instead they had done the only thing a guild knows how to do.

They broke it up. They broke the country's true name into thousands of pieces, entered each piece as a line item in an inventory — *received, one syllable, condition: priceless, storage: perpetual* — and then they fed them, piece by piece over two centuries, to the thing sleeping beneath the city. They paid the Hollow in the only currency it wanted. They paid it its victim's name, in installments.

And a name, spoken whole —

A name like that, restored complete, doesn't just identify a thing. It *summons* it. It opens the original door, the first bargain's threshold, the way in which is also the way out.

If you could reassemble it.

* * *

Here is the mathematical problem, stated plainly, because I am a clerk before I am anything else and plain statement is all I have left of my dignity.

The Consolidation-era indexes list approximately eleven thousand four hundred acquisitions flagged with the seal of the First Feeding. Of those, perhaps three thousand pages survive in the deep archive, each containing one fragment. The remaining eight thousand-plus fragments are not in the archive at all. They are in the Hollow's stomach. Fed to it, one ledger-line at a time, across ten generations of transcriptionists who sat in rooms very much like mine and wrote down the memories of people very much like the boy, and felt very much the way I feel, and told themselves very much the stories I tell myself at three in the morning.

So: to speak the name whole, I needed eleven thousand four hundred syllable-shards. I possessed three thousand. The Hollow possessed the rest.

You cannot retrieve something from the stomach of a god by asking politely. You cannot subpoena it. There is no appeals process. Believe me, if there were a form for it, the Guild would have invented the form, and the form would cost extra.

But.

But — and I clung to this *but* the way drowning men cling to driftwood that is demonstrably also a shark — the boy remembered things. That was the whole horror of the transcription sessions, wasn't it? He remembered Ostmere, and when he spoke, the ink moved, and the memories poured out of him into the ledger. He had lived there. He had heard the name spoken. His mother had sung it, maybe, in lullabies, the way mothers hide important things inside songs because songs are the only containers adults never think to search.

The boy might hold fragments the ledgers never captured. Fragments that were never entered, never indexed, never fed to the dark. The living remainder.

Which meant that to finish the name, I would need everything he had left.

And everything he had left was himself.

I did the sums again, hoping the numbers would be embarrassed and rearrange themselves. They did not. Three thousand shards in the archive. Eight thousand eaten. One child, small enough to fit inside a vault cot, holding whatever scraps of home remained in a head that grew lighter every time I sat down across from him with a pen.

To open the door, I would have to empty the last person who remembered the country I was opening the door into.

Unless.

* * *

"Unless," I said aloud, to the archive, "the eaten pieces can be remembered back."

The archive declined to answer, being an archive, but I had noticed something in Volume 209 of the First Feeding records — a marginal note in a hand I now recognized as Maren's predecessor's, a transcriptionist named Odile whose fate was recorded, in typical guild fashion, as *contract fulfilled*. Odile had written, beside an entry for a shard labeled **—MERE, third inflection**: *It gives change.*

I had assumed it was a complaint about currency. Clerks complain about currency the way rivers complain about being wet. But sitting in the dark with my lamp guttering, I read it differently. *It gives change.* The Hollow pays for what it takes? The Hollow returns something?

Or — worse, better, stranger — the memories it eats don't vanish. Nothing in this city vanishes. Everything is *held*. The Hollow doesn't digest; it hoards. It is the largest vault in Vaskarand, and every syllable of Ostmere's name sits in it like coin in a miser's cellar, intact, waiting, *countable*.

Countable. Auditable. And if something can be counted, it can be balanced. If it can be balanced, it can be *reconciled*. I am aware that proposing to perform accounting fraud against an eldritch entity is not the behavior of a well man. I propose it anyway. It is the only skill set I possess, and I refuse to believe the universe gave me a talent for double-entry bookkeeping purely as a joke.

The plan assembled itself in my head with the terrible clarity of a completed column:

One. Extract the surviving three thousand shards from the deep archive without Havel noticing. Difficult. The guildmaster counts everything, and lately I had caught him watching me with the mild patient expression of a man watching bread rise.

Two. Get the boy to supply the fragments that exist nowhere else — in his memory, in his mother's songs, in whatever a royal heir carries in his blood. Each word costing him more of himself. Each session another withdrawal from an account that was already overdrawn.

Three. Find a way to make the Hollow disgorge the eight thousand. This step was, admittedly, underdeveloped. Steps one and two had consumed all my optimism and there hadn't been enough left over for step three, which currently consisted of the phrase *figure something out*, written in my mental margin in the same handwriting as the note I never wrote, which I had stopped finding mysterious and started finding ominous.

Four. Assemble the name. Speak it whole. Open the door.

Five. Walk through it.

Step five was the one I permitted myself to imagine while walking home through streets that smelled of canal-water and frying onions and the particular municipal despair of Vaskarand at dawn. I imagined the boy in a country that knew his name. I imagined snow that was actual snow and not a memory leaking sideways through a transcription session. I did not imagine myself in the picture. I have learned, in this guild, that imagining yourself into futures is how they take root in the ledgers, and everything that takes root in the ledgers eventually gets collected.

* * *

The vault was quiet when I came down before morning bell, carrying stolen archive pages sewn into the lining of a second coat, because I have become the kind of person who smuggles a country's dismembered name in his outerwear and no longer finds this remarkable.

The boy was awake. He was always awake lately. He sat cross-legged on the cot with the guild's inventory tag still tied to his wrist — LOT 7, PROVENANCE UNKNOWN, AWAITING APPRAISAL — and he was drawing in the frost on the floor with one finger. Letters. His letters. The ones no living tongue should know, drawn from memory in condensation that evaporated almost as fast as he could write, so that he was perpetually writing a word that was already disappearing behind him.

He looked up when the lamp light reached him.

"You found some," he said. Not a question. Lately he could feel the shards on me the way you can feel rain coming in a knee that was broken once.

"Three thousand," I said. "Give or take the ones I dropped running from the night-warden."

"And the rest?"

"The rest," I said, sitting down across from him, arranging my lamp, uncapping my pen with hands that wanted to shake and were not permitted to, "we are going to get back."

He studied me for a long moment. He had gotten good at studying me. Children who are appraised like merchandise learn to appraise back.

"It will cost," he said.

"Yes."

"Me."

"Some," I said, which was a lie, and we both knew it was a lie, and he nodded anyway, because he was eight years old and already fluent in the arithmetic of sacrifice, which is the single most obscene sentence I have ever had cause to write.

I opened the first stolen page. The fragment at its heart glowed faintly, the way things glow when they have been cut out of something larger and miss it. **OST**, said the shard, *first inflection, dawn-conjugation, meaning: to begin by breaking ground.*

"To start," I translated, my voice rough. "The name starts with *to start*. Someone in your country had a sense of humor."

The boy almost smiled. Then he closed his eyes and began, softly, in the dead language, to sing — and the ink on my page stirred like something waking, and above us, far above, through eleven floors of stone and ledgers and lies, I felt the city shift its weight, and somewhere beneath us, vast and patient and full of swallowed names, something turned over in its sleep.

And in the margin of the stolen page, in handwriting that was mine and was not mine, a new line appeared, small and neat and addressed directly to me:

*Eight thousand pieces, clerk. Count carefully. One of them is counting you.*
