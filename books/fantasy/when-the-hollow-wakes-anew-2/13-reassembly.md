## 13. Reassembly

*Ulyana*

The ledgers lie to you, if you let them. That was the first thing the Guild ever taught me, back when I still believed the Guild taught things out of generosity. *A ledger is a machine for making one version of events true,* Master Corvel had said, tapping his pointer against a column of figures. *Your job is to make sure it's the right version.* And for eleven years I had made sure, faithfully, that the right version was whichever version kept my debt from being called due.

But lying in the dark of my cell with a stolen fragment of a dead kingdom folded into my sleeve, I discovered there is an older rule than any Corvel ever taught: *a ledger lies only as well as its ink.* And Ostmere's ink — the old iron-gall script in the vault's deepest shelves, the entries no auditor had touched in two centuries — did not lie. It simply stopped. Mid-word. Mid-sentence. Mid-kingdom.

Which meant that if I wanted to find the missing pieces of the name, I didn't need to read what the Guild had written.

I needed to find where it had failed to write.

* * *

We worked at night, because nights were the only hours that belonged to us. The boy — Tam, though that wasn't really his name, that was just the shape we'd given the sound of him — slept through the days in the vault like something being appraised, which technically he was. The Guildmaster's arithmetic was simple and I had learned to hate it fluently: keep the asset alive, keep the asset calm, feed the asset on schedule so the transcription quota could be met. Every morning Maren brought him bread and water and a face that pretended not to remember how to be kind, and every evening I came down with lamp and ledger and pen, and we committed our slow treason.

"Tonight," I told him on the fourth night, "we're going to try something different."

He looked up from the floor where he sat cross-legged, small hands around the tin cup he always held even when empty, as if the cup were proof he existed somewhere outside of ledgers. He'd lost weight since they brought him in. Or maybe he'd always been this thin and I was only now learning to see it.

"You remember the bell tower," I said. "The one in your city. You told me about it in session six."

"The bells rang wrong," he whispered. His voice had that hollow quality it got whenever he spoke of home, like wind through an empty chimney. "After. They rang wrong because there was nothing under them anymore."

"Right. Now." I spread the ledger open across both our knees. "Here's an entry dated two hundred years ago. A customs record. Look here—" I turned the page toward him. "*Item: three hundredweight of bronze, declared for casting, origin: Vellmark foundry, consignee: the Bellwrights' Company of*—"

And there the sentence stopped. Not faded. Stopped. The ink just gave up, the way a man gives up mid-stride, and after the last legible word the parchment went blank with a blankness that felt less like absence than like swallowing.

"That's your bell tower," I said. "Or its bones. The entry ends where the city ended."

Tam touched the page with one finger, very lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "It hurts," he said. "Looking at it."

"I know. But here's the thing." I pulled out my own pen, the good one, the one I'd bought with my first wages and resented ever since. "The Guild fed pieces of Ostmere to the Hollow for two hundred years. Every entry they transcribed into the master ledger, they cut a piece off first and fed it down. But they weren't careful. They were clerks, not butchers. Some pieces fell through the cracks. Some words survived in the margins. And some—" I tapped the broken customs record— "some are only half-eaten. Damaged, not destroyed."

"So?"

"So the Hollow ate the *middle*. The beginnings and endings are still here, scattered across four thousand pages. If we can gather enough of them—if we can rebuild the true name of Ostmere from its own wreckage—we can speak it whole. And a whole name opens doors."

He frowned at me, and in the lamplight his face went suddenly, terribly adult, the way children's faces do when they understand something they should be spared. "Doors to where? There's nowhere. It's all eaten."

"Not if the name comes back. A country isn't buildings, Tam. A country is a *name that things agree to answer to.* Speak it right, and everything that was ever Ostmere remembers what it was."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then: "And you know all the pieces already?"

I laughed, or made the sound that passes for laughing among debt-bound men. "No. I have perhaps forty fragments. Out of what I estimate to be several thousand. Hence the crime spree."

"What's a crime spree?"

"It's when you do crimes quickly, before anyone catches you. Come on. We start tonight, and we work until dawn, and tomorrow night we do it again, and we don't stop until either the name is whole or we are."

* * *

*Tam*

Ulyana talked too much when he was frightened. I learned this the way you learn weather — not by studying it, but by living under it long enough that you feel the change coming in your bones. When he explained things, when he filled the silence between us with plans and numbers and the names of dead scribes, that was when his fear was loudest. When he went quiet and just *worked*, pen scratching, brow furrowed, muttering fragments of Old Ostish under his breath in an accent that would have made my grandmother laugh herself sick — that was when he was calmest. Or closest to it.

I started giving him words the way you feed a stray animal. Carefully. A little at a time. Watching to see if it hurt him.

It didn't hurt him. That was the strange part. It hurt *me*.

Every word I remembered aloud took something with it on the way out. I don't mean it tired me, though it did that too. I mean the memory itself grew thinner, like cloth worn by washing. The first time I said my mother's song into Ulyana's ledger — the lullaby she sang while the snow came down past the window of our house on Candlemaker Street — I could hear every note, feel her hand heavy and warm on my head. By the fifth time, when Ulyana needed the third verse again to cross-check a fragment in a customs manifest, I could remember that she sang, and that it mattered, but the melody had gone somewhere I couldn't follow.

By the tenth time, I knew only that there had been a song. That there had been a mother. That I had loved her.

That was all. That was what remained. *There was. There was. There was.*

But here is what I decided, alone in the vault at night while Ulyana slept at his desk with his cheek on the ledger like a boy himself: better that the song live in ink than die in me. Better that someone — anyone — carry it forward, even if that someone was a book. Even if that someone was a monster underneath the world. A thing sung once is a thing that existed. A thing written twice is a thing that argues with oblivion.

So I fed the pages. And Ulyana fed the pages. And between us we built a country back up out of its own crumbs.

* * *

*Ulyana*

Here is what nobody tells you about resurrection: it is bookkeeping. Glorious, desperate, blasphemous bookkeeping, conducted by lamplight in a basement that smelled of dust and cold iron, but bookkeeping nonetheless.

The method, such as it was: I would find a damaged entry — a truncated trade record, a half-effaced legal decree, a marriage register whose final line dissolved into blankness — and I would extract whatever syllables survived. Then Tam would take those syllables and tell me what they'd been attached to. *"Vellmark foundry"* — yes, that was real, the foundry stood on the Tanners' Canal, you could smell it from the bridge. *"Bellwrights' Company"* — yes, but the proper form was *Bellwrights' Guild of the Ninth Bell*, and the ninth bell was named *Sorrow-Wakes-Morning* and it cracked the winter Tam was born, and the recasting of Sorrow-Wakes-Morning had been funded by a tax on candle-wax, and the tax had been levied by the Office of the Under-Chancellor of Weights—

And each correction he gave me, I wrote into my stolen notebook, and each correction cost him. I watched it happen. I watched the color of things drain out of his telling. First the details went — smells, textures, the specific gray of a particular winter sky. Then the people thinned into names, then the names into shapes, then the shapes into a warm blur that might have been anything. And through it all the boy kept talking, kept feeding, kept giving, with the terrible patience of someone who has already decided the price is worth paying and refuses to be talked out of it.

"You could stop," I said to him once, near midnight of the eleventh night. "You know that. Nobody's making you."

He looked up at me with eyes that seemed, lately, to have more ledger in them than child. "You could stop too," he said. "Your debts. You could just stop paying them and run away."

"That's different."

"Why?"

Because my debts were only money, and money is a fiction we all agreed to believe in, whereas his memories were the only country he had left. Because I had spent eleven years learning that some prices are worth paying and some are only worth *arguing about*, and I had never once in my life paid a price willingly, and watching him do it nightly was teaching me something I didn't want to learn. Because—

"Because," I said finally, "if I run, I'm still me when I stop running. If you keep going, you won't be you anymore."

He considered this with the gravity of a chancellor weighing taxes. "If I stop," he said, "then there's no Ostmere anywhere at all. Just a hole where a country used to be, and a boy who doesn't know why he's sad." He turned back to the ledger. "Ask me about the candles again. The tax on candles. I think I almost have the Chancellor's name."

* * *

Something else happened over those nights, something I did not plan and did not notice arriving until it had fully moved in.

Somewhere around the fifteenth night, I realized I had stopped thinking of him as *the asset*. Somewhere around the twentieth, I realized I had stopped thinking of him as *the boy*. He had become simply Tam, the way my own name had become simply mine — a fact of the world, unremarkable, load-bearing.

He began waiting for me. Not the anxious waiting of a captive counting hours, but the specific, insulted waiting of a younger sibling whose elder is late. When I arrived at the vault he would already have the lamp trimmed and the ledgers sorted into the order he knew I preferred — he had memorized my preferences without asking, the way children colonize your habits — and if I was late by even a quarter hour he would greet me with the flat stare of the profoundly wronged.

"You're late."

"There was an audit."

"There's always an audit. Sit down. We're doing the harbor district tonight, and I found something in yesterday's pages you missed." And he would tap the ledger with one finger, imperious as an emperor, and I would sit, and we would work.

Once — I will set this down honestly, because if I'm writing myself a new account then let it be accurate — once I caught him copying my handwriting. He had taken my spare pen and was practicing loops on scrap parchment, tongue between his teeth, trying to reproduce the way I crossed my *t*s with a single impatient stroke.

"What are you doing?"

"Learning to be a scribe." He said it defensively, chin up, daring me to mock. "In case you get eaten too. Someone has to finish the name."

Something happened in my chest at that, something structural, like a joist settling under new weight. "First," I said, aiming for dryness and missing by a wide margin, "no one is getting eaten. Second, you cross your *t*s too carefully. Like this." I guided his hand through the stroke, quick and careless, and he watched the motion with the fierce concentration of a scholar receiving a secret doctrine.

"Like that?"

"Better. Still too reverent. Ink isn't sacred, Tam. It's just memory with a job."

He practiced the stroke seven more times. On the eighth, he got it right, and looked up at me with such naked triumph that I had to turn away and pretend great interest in the lamp's wick.

* * *

*Tam*

I taught him a song. I didn't mean to. It came out sideways, the way true things do.

We were working on the harbor records, and he needed the word for the mooring-posts — the black posts where the fishing boats tied up, the ones the gulls fought over — and the Old Ostish word rose in me easily, *hvalsker*, and I said it, and the saying of it took the smell of fish-tar and salt with it, and I felt that loss go down into me like a swallowed stone. And to fill the space where the smell had been, without deciding to, I hummed.

It was a working song. Harbor-song. The women sang it when they mended nets, four notes over and over, rising at the end like a question. My grandmother's voice lived inside it, and my aunts', and the whole brown-smelling room of net-menders and gossip and lamplight, and I put all of it into the humming because I couldn't put it anywhere else.

Ulyana went completely still.

Then, quietly, badly, he hummed it back. Four notes, rising at the end. Wrong key. Wrong rhythm. He held the last note too long, like he was afraid to let it go.

"Again," he said.

I hummed it again. He followed. Third time, he almost had it. Fourth time, our voices fit together the way voices do when neither is trying to lead, and the vault filled up with a dead city's net-mending song, four notes rising like a question asked two hundred years too late and answered anyway.

When we finished, he cleared his throat and said, "Well. That's one entry the Hollow will never get," in a voice that was absolutely not steady, and I said, "You hold the last note too long," and he said, "I hold it exactly as long as it needs holding," and we went back to the ledgers, both of us blinking more than the work strictly required.

That was the night I understood what we were building. Not a name, exactly. Not only a name. We were building the thing the name was *for*. A country is people agreeing to remember together. We were two people. It would have to be enough.

*Ulyana*

On the twenty-third night, the arithmetic turned against us.

I had charted our progress on the inside of my cell door, in pencil, in columns only I could read: fragments recovered, fragments projected, rate of recovery, rate of loss. And the columns had begun to diverge in a way I did not like. We were finding pieces faster than we had been — Tam's memory, tapped nightly, was yielding deeper strata now, older words, founding-era speech that the ledgers had never recorded at all. But each piece cost more. The early recoveries had nicked him. These drew blood.

That night he gave me the word for *snow-that-falls-on-a-city-that-is-listening* — eleven syllables, a word with a whole cosmology folded into it, the kind of word you only need when your entire culture agrees that cities have ears — and afterward he sat very still for a long moment, and when he spoke, his voice came from somewhere far away.

"Ulyana. What color are my eyes?"

The question went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor. I looked at him. I looked properly, for the first time in weeks, instead of looking at the ledger-shaped space where he sat.

They were brown. Dark brown, nearly black in the lamplight, with a ring of amber near the pupil that I had noticed the first night at the loading door and had apparently never noticed since, because I had been looking at ledgers.

"Brown," I said. "With amber near the middle. Like resin. Like—"

"Honey in tea," he said. "Yes. Good." He exhaled. "I couldn't remember. I checked this morning, in the water basin, and I couldn't remember, and I thought — if I can't remember my own eyes, soon I won't remember why the name matters. I'll just say the words because someone asks, like Maren does with the ledger lines. Empty and perfect."

I reached across and closed the ledger, which I had never once done mid-session. "Then we rest."

"We don't have time to rest."

"We have less time if you dissolve. Listen to me." I took his shoulders — they were so small under my hands, when had he become so small — "The name needs a speaker at the end of it. Not an echo. If you spend yourself to nothing, we assemble a perfect name and no one home to say it. So we rest. That's not weakness. That's *strategy*, and I am the strategist here, and you are the — the asset I have inexplicably grown fond of."

He almost smiled. "You're bad at strategy. You've owed the Guild eleven years."

"And yet I remain undefeated, because I have never once paid early." I lit the second lamp, and pushed the tin cup across the table toward him, and pretended my hands were steady. "Drink. Sleep. Tomorrow we do the founding era, and it's going to be the worst part, and I need you *there* for it. All of you. Even the parts that look at me like I owe you money."

"You do owe me money," he said, settling against the wall, pulling his knees up. "You borrowed my grandmother's harbor song and never gave it back."

"I'll repay it with interest," I said. "When we're somewhere with interest rates we can trust."

His breathing slowed. Before sleep took him he murmured, mostly to himself, "Four notes. Rising at the end. In case I lose it — it's four notes, rising at the end, and you have it too now, so it can't all be gone."

"No," I agreed quietly, to a boy who was already asleep. "It can't all be gone."

I sat a while longer with the lamp, doing sums I didn't write down. Twenty-three nights. Perhaps sixty fragments recovered, of a name I estimated at — conservatively — nine hundred syllables. At our current rate, spending him at our current rate, we had perhaps five weeks of boy left before the ledgers had eaten him down to a warm blur, and perhaps a tenth of the name assembled.

The mathematics were obscene. The mathematics said no.

But the mathematics assumed we were working alone, with honest tools, against an enemy who played fair. And I had learned, over twenty-three nights of treason, that none of those assumptions held. The ledgers had gaps the Guild didn't know about. Maren's warnings had shapes in them. And the Hollow itself — patient, vast, buried — had been eating for two centuries without once checking whether anything was being rebuilt behind its back.

I banked the lamp. In the dark, I heard the boy breathe, and beneath the boy, far beneath, through stone and clay and the drowned bones of a palace, I heard — or imagined I heard, which amounts to the same thing at three in the morning — something turn over in its sleep.

And above us, faint through fathoms of guild, the chandeliers swayed to a soundless bell.
