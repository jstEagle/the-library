## 15. The Hollow Wakes

*From the ledger of Ulyana Coldharbour, transcriber third-class — though I no longer trust the word "transcriber," which sounds so tidy, like a man who moves things from one page to another without losing anything in between.*

The forgetting began, as far as I can reconstruct it, with a baker.

I know this because Maren told me, and Maren knows everything that happens in Vaskarand below the level of guildmasters and above the level of gutter-children, which is to say she knows the city's actual business. The baker was named Oskar Pell. He had baked bread on Candlewright Lane for thirty-one years, and at four minutes past six on the morning of the Hollow's waking he stopped mid-knead, looked down at his flour-white hands as though they belonged to a stranger who had been rude to him, and asked his apprentice — politely, Maren stressed, *politely* — who he was.

Not "who am I." That would have been tragic but comprehensible. He asked, "*Who is this?*" about himself. The way you'd ask about an uninvited guest.

His apprentice, being fourteen and pragmatic, said, "That's Oskar Pell, master, and if you don't finish the kneading we'll both be out on the street."

And Oskar Pell looked at his own name the way a man looks at a coin from a country that no longer exists, turned it over twice, and went back to work. By noon he could bake perfectly and remember nothing else. His wife came by at midday and he served her a loaf with a smile and charged her double, because she was, to him, simply a customer, and customers pay what the sign says. The sign said one thing only: BREAD. He'd forgotten how to read anything else.

Maren told me this over the vault door, her voice pitched low, while above us the guild shuddered like a dog shaking off rain.

"It starts small," she said. "It always started small before. A word here. A face there. Then it gets greedy."

"Before?" I said. "Before when?"

She gave me the look she gives junior archivists who ask questions they'll regret knowing the answers to. Which is most looks, actually, but this one had a new weight to it.

"You know when," she said. "Two hundred years ago. When a whole country forgot itself mid-scream, and something underneath us swallowed the sound."

The boy was asleep in the vault, or pretending to be. He'd been sleeping more lately, and each sleep cost him. I'd begun keeping a private tally — not in ink, never in ink, ink belongs to them now — just marks scratched into my boot heel with a pin. Seven sleeps since our night-work began on the name. Each morning he woke with less. Not less strength; less *self*. Tuesday he couldn't recall the color of his mother's hair until I described it to him, and then he cried, because describing it back to him was like handing a starving man a picture of bread.

But the name was nearly whole. Eleven syllables recovered. One missing. And beneath our feet, patient for two centuries, the thing that ate a kingdom had decided it was done waiting.

The first tremor hit at half past nine. Not an earthquake — earthquakes move stone, and stone doesn't frighten anyone in Vaskarand, where the buildings have survived worse than geology. No. This tremor moved *words*. I felt it pass through me like cold water through cloth: for three full seconds I could not remember what a ledger was called. I stood in the middle of the Scriptorium surrounded by forty thousand of them and the concept simply wasn't there. Book-shaped objects. Rectangles full of meaning. What were they *for*?

Then it returned, and I understood, with the particular horror of a man who has just watched his own reflection blink out of sync, exactly what was happening in the streets above.

I grabbed the nearest junior scribe — a nervous boy called Tam with ink permanently on his chin — and shook him.

"What's your mother's name?"

He blinked. "Ulyana, sir, why—"

"Her name. Her *name*, Tam."

"Marigold Coldharbour, sir." A pause. The color left his face in stages, like a sunset running backwards. "Sir. Why did I say *Coldharbour*? Why did I say *your* surname?"

Because the Hollow doesn't take names cleanly. It blurs them. It reaches into the city's memory and smudges, and the smudge fills in with whatever's nearest. Tam's mother was suddenly Marigold Coldharbour in his mind, and somewhere across the city, some other young man was suddenly certain his mother's name was Marigold Tamsin-of-Candlewright, and neither of them knew anything was wrong, and that — that was the truly terrible part. Forgetting hurts. Being *rewritten* feels like nothing at all.

Above us, faintly, through five floors of stone and shelving, I heard the bells of the Assumption Tower ring — and ring wrong. Ring underwater, muffled and dragging, though the tower stands on the highest hill in the city and hasn't been near water in living memory. Living memory. Ha. There's a phrase that's going to need revising.

I went down to the vault. The boy was awake, sitting cross-legged in the dark, listening.

"It's loud," he said. "Down here. It's been getting louder all week, but now it's—" He pressed his palms against his ears uselessly, because the sound wasn't coming through his ears. "It's saying my name wrong. It keeps trying my name and getting it wrong, and every time it fails it gets angrier."

"Can it reach you?"

"It's reaching everyone." He looked up at me with those too-old eyes, eyes that had watched a city drink itself. "Ulyana. It isn't waking up angry. It's waking up *hungry early*. Because of me. I'm the last course, and I've been sitting in its dining room for weeks, and it can smell me."

"We're close," I said. "Eleven syllables. We need the twelfth and we walk out through a door two hundred years shut."

"And the city?"

I didn't answer fast enough. Children notice that. They always notice.

"The city will remember itself when Ostmere does," I said, which was true, and also possibly a lie, and I genuinely could not tell you which, and neither, I think, could the truth.

* * *

*Maren's account, dictated later to no one, written down anyway, because writing things down is the only prayer I know.*

I lost my daughter again today. Not the way I lost her before — the guild took her face from me once already, fair payment for lines I should never have memorized, and I made my bargain with open eyes. This was different. This was the *Hollow* reaching up through the foundations and pawing through what remained, like a beggar going through a coat I'd already emptied.

For one horrible hour I remembered her completely — her face, her laugh, the way she said "mama" with the second syllable chewed — and then it drained away again, and I understood what it was doing. It wasn't stealing. It was *tasting*. Checking the vintage. Two hundred years ago it drank a country in one long swallow and slept, and now, stirring, it runs its tongue over the city above and finds the cellar well-stocked.

Eight million memories in Vaskarand. Every love affair, every grudge, every grandmother's recipe, every child's fear of the dark under the stairs. And it wants the boy most of all, the last drop of the oldest wine, and it cannot wait properly, and waiting improperly makes monsters clumsy.

Clumsy is a weapon. I wrote that on a scrap and slid it under Ulyana's door. Let him make what he can of it.

In the streets, the forgetting moved like weather. I walked from the guild to the chandlery on Ropewalk — I still permit myself walks, small rebellions — and I saw:

A woman standing in the middle of Cordwainer Square shouting a name at the sky, any name, trying name after name like keys in a lock, because she knew someone was missing and could not say who. She tried forty names in the ten minutes I watched. None fit. Whatever hole the Hollow had opened in her, it had taken the shape of the person along with the person.

A man arguing furiously with his own house, which had overnight rearranged itself — the front door now opening onto the neighbor's kitchen, the stairs descending into a pantry that had never existed. He kept saying, "This isn't where the stairs go," and the house, being built on guild-foundations, on hollow ground, disagreed.

Children were mostly fine. Remember that. Children under seven barely noticed. Their memories are shallow-rooted; the Hollow's fingers passed over them like wind over grass. It was the old ones, the deep ones, decades of accumulated self, that it gripped and shook. Old Vaskarand is being skimmed like cream off milk, and what remains underneath is thin and sweet and does not know what it used to be.

At the chandlery, the shopkeeper sold me candles and then, as I turned to leave, said, "Did we used to sell something else? I feel like we used to sell something else."

"You sell candles," I said gently.

"No. Before that. There's a shelf behind the counter and it's the right height for..." He stared at the shelf. "For something people needed every week. Something everyone bought."

Bread, I thought. It was a bakery. It was a bakery until eleven years ago and the man has forgotten his own trade twice over, once by choice and once by hunger. But I said, "Candles," and paid, and walked home past a church whose congregation stood outside it in silence, because they had arrived to pray and discovered none of them could recall a single prayer, and praying to a god whose name you've lost feels presumptuous somehow.

By evening the guildmasters had sealed the doors. All of them. Iron shutters over the windows, chains on the loading dock, and Guildmaster Havel standing in the great hall with his hands folded, watching the chandeliers sway to the soundless bell, wearing the expression of a farmer counting clouds before a harvest.

I have served this guild fifty years. I have seen Havel frightened exactly twice. Tonight was the second time.

He is not afraid the Hollow will destroy us. Write that down, future reader, whoever you are, whoever you manage to remain. He is afraid it will wake *before the table is set*. There is a difference, and the difference is measured in children.

* * *

*Guildmaster Havel, in the privacy of his office, speaking aloud to an empty room, because the empty room is the only confessor left who won't forget the confession by morning.*

It is ahead of schedule. My whole life has been arithmetic — I was born in the last hour of a dying country, I have spent ninety-one years doing sums to keep the sum total from arriving at zero — and tonight the arithmetic has developed opinions of its own.

Fourteen days early. Perhaps fewer. I can feel it through the floor when I stand very still, a great slow turning-over, like something enormous rolling in its sleep and finding the bed too small. Every feeding for two hundred years has followed the same recipe: a memory-bearer, delivered whole, poured down the roots, and the Hollow sleeps another century with a kingdom's worth of dreams in its belly. Simple. Reliable. The single most reliable institution in Vaskarand, and I include the mint.

But the boy leaks. That is what I failed to calculate. A dead language walking around loose, a royal line's worth of memory breathing our air, dripping Ostmere into our soil day after day — it's like leaving a spoonful of honey on the windowsill of an anthill. The Hollow has been tasting him in its sleep for weeks, and now it wants the meal, and wanting has woken it ahead of the appointed hour, and a waking Hollow does not sip. It drinks the city around the boy like the foam off an ale.

Vaskarand can survive losing its edges. Streets rearrange; people re-shelve their griefs; the city has always been a palimpsest. What it cannot survive is the Hollow rising unfed and drinking *deep* — the whole aquifer of memory, eight million lives down to the silt. I have seen what a half-finished meal looks like. I was born looking at it.

So: accelerate. Final transcription within the week. The Coldharbour scribe has been useful — more useful than the last one, who ended as a footnote — and if he has grown attached to the asset, well. Attachment is just debt of a softer denomination, and I am, whatever else, a lender. Everything attaches. Everything can be called in.

Below me the bell rings underwater. Above me the city forgets its own address. And I sit between them with a ledger open to a blank page, doing sums, trusting the sums, because the alternative to trusting the sums is admitting that for ninety-one years I have fed a god to save a city that doesn't know it's being saved, and that the saving has a price I chose for everyone, and that mercy with a body count is still —

No. The sums hold. The sums hold.

Tomorrow I lock down the vaults. Tomorrow the final feeding begins. Tomorrow, if the scribe asks questions, I will finally answer one honestly: *yes, Ulyana, it eats the memories, and yes, it has been eating yours since the day you signed, and yes, your mother's face is already in escrow.* Honesty costs nothing when the listener can't afford to act on it.

The chandeliers sway. Somewhere below, something turns over and says a boy's name wrong, and hates itself for it, and gets hungrier.

Fourteen days. I have bought this city ninety-one years with other people's memories. I can buy it one more century with one more child, and then I will retire the debt personally, with interest, out of my own long account.

That is not villainy. That is bookkeeping.

God forgive me. God — whichever one I've forgotten — forgive me.

* * *

*The boy's account, spoken in the dark of the vault to the man who writes things down, in a language only two living mouths share, which means soon only one, which means eventually none.*

I heard the city forget. Did you know you can hear it? It sounds like snowfall, but backwards — like all the snow deciding at once to leave the ground.

I heard a woman lose her sister. I heard a street forget which way it ran. And underneath all of it, the great voice, the hungry voice, trying my name over and over — *Osten, Ostro, Os-mere-no-that's-the-country-not-me* — getting it wrong, getting it wrong, and each failure shaking the world like a dog shaking a rat.

Ulyana says we're close. Eleven syllables. He holds the pages like they're warm eggs. Last night he gave me a piece of the name — the fifth syllable, the one that means *shelter* — and I felt it settle into my chest like a swallowed coal, and I thought: this is what I'm for. Not the throne. Not the blood. I am the last native speaker of a country, and my mouth is the keyhole, and keys exist to be turned.

But I heard something else tonight, in the ringing. Underneath the hunger there was — I don't have the word in your language. In mine we'd say *homesick*. The Hollow sounded homesick. Like it was trying my name because it too had a name once, before, and it's been trying to remember it for two hundred years and getting it wrong, and getting it wrong, and getting it wrong.

Ulyana says the twelfth syllable is inside it. Inside the thing that ate everything. He says we may have to go down and ask it nicely.

The bell rang again just now. Underwater. Under *everything*. And the whole city paused — I felt it pause, millions of minds holding their breath at once — and in that pause, clear as a crack in ice, I heard the missing syllable.

One sound. Half a heartbeat long.

It sounded like my mother's voice.

Then the guild doors above us boomed shut, iron on iron, one by one, like a giant counting, and Ulyana looked up from the ledgers with chalk dust on his fingers and fear on his face, and said the words I'd been waiting for and dreading:

"They've locked us in. It's time. We're going down."
