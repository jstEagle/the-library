## 16. Havel's Arithmetic

The guild locked its doors at dawn, which was the first wrong thing.

Vaskarand's Ledger Guild did not lock its doors. Locking doors implied that something outside might want in, and the guild's entire theology rested on the conviction that everything worth having was already inside — every debt, every title, every scrap of memory worth collateralizing. The great bronze doors on Coinwright Square hadn't closed since the Soot Tax Riots of my grandfather's era, and even then they'd only been shut long enough to let the militia through.

Now they were barred, chained, and — I noticed as I pressed my palm to the cold metal — *singing*. A faint hum ran through the bronze like a struck tuning fork that refused to die. Wardwork. Old wardwork, older than the guild, older maybe than the city, the kind of working you didn't commission so much as inherit.

"Admiring the architecture, Coldharbour?"

Havel stood at the head of the grand stair with his hands folded behind his back, and for the first time since I'd known him he looked his age. Guildmasters are supposed to look ageless the way ledgers are supposed to balance; it's a professional courtesy. But the last two weeks had drawn lines down his face like a clerk ruling columns, and his eyes had the particular shine of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"The doors are closed," I said. "That's new."

"The doors are *necessary*." He came down the stairs slowly, one hand trailing the banister, and stopped an arm's length away — close enough that I could smell the ink and camphor that clung to every senior member of the guild like a second skin. "We feed the Hollow tonight."

"Not tomorrow? Not after a decent interval of pretending we're considering alternatives?"

"Coldharbour." He said my name the way he said all our names: as an account number wearing a coat. "Do you know what's happening above us right now?"

I did, actually. Everyone in Vaskarand did, though nobody was saying it aloud, because saying things aloud has always been dangerous in this city and is now dangerous in an entirely novel way. Three days ago, half the fishwives at Brackish Market had paused mid-haggle and stared into the middle distance for eleven seconds, then resumed arguing about eels as if nothing happened. Yesterday the street called Tanner's Row rearranged itself overnight into something the maps insisted had always been there, and the tanners woke up in each other's houses and wept without knowing why. This morning my landlady had asked me who I was, politely, twice, before her face remembered me.

"The forgetting is spreading," I said. "Faster."

"It is waking early. The boy accelerates it. Every day he spends above ground, breathing our air and remembering his dead country, the Hollow stirs a little more." Havel turned and gestured toward the vault stairs, and I followed because following was easier than thinking, which I suspect is the founding principle of every institution ever built. "Two hundred years, Coldharbour. Two hundred years of careful feeding — measured portions, regulated intake, memories doled out like medicine. And now the medicine stops working unless we increase the dose. You understand what that means."

"That the patient isn't sleeping anymore."

"That the patient is *dreaming*, and we need it to keep dreaming until—" He stopped himself. I watched him stop himself. It was like watching a man close a ledger so quickly you knew exactly which column he was hiding.

"Until what, Guildmaster?"

"Until we've secured another century," he said smoothly. "Tonight's feeding will be generous. The boy's full stock of memory, transcribed and delivered whole. That should settle it for a hundred years. Your debt will be forgiven, of course. All of it. You'll walk out of here a free man."

"And the boy walks out of here a blank page."

"He walks out of here *at peace*," Havel said, with such gentle conviction that I almost believed him, and hating myself for almost believing him is a feeling I've grown quite familiar with these past weeks. "You've read the transcripts. You know what those memories hold. Snow, and bells, and a mother singing while the world drinks itself. Would you wish that weight on anyone? We are not butchers, Coldharbour. We are hospice workers. There is a difference."

"There's a difference," I agreed. "It's mostly a matter of paperwork."

Something flickered across his face — annoyance, or possibly respect; in Havel the two were often indistinguishable — and he ascended back toward his office, pausing on the fourth step to deliver his benediction over his shoulder: "Be in the transcription chamber by moonrise. Bring your pen. Wear something you don't mind ruining. It's going to be a long night."

* * *

It took me three hours to find Maren's marginalia, and I want it noted for posterity that I found it at all, given the circumstances.

Maren Voss had been taken from us two nights ago. Caught smuggling a page of erased entries out of the deep archive — my page, the one with the second syllable of Ostmere's true name hidden in a shipping manifest from 1741 — she'd been brought before the tribunal of senior archivists and made to pay her toll on the spot. I wasn't allowed to watch. Nobody was allowed to watch. But afterward I'd found her in the scriptorium corridor, walking with the careful steps of someone carrying something fragile, and when I said her name she'd looked at me with perfect courtesy and no recognition whatsoever, and asked if I needed help locating a volume.

She still knew every ledger line in the building. She just no longer knew why any of them mattered, or whose face had mattered most. The tribunal had offered her a choice — the knowledge or the daughter — and she'd chosen the knowledge, because she understood, better than any of them, that the knowledge was the only weapon left in the building.

And she'd hidden a message where only a scribe would think to look: in the marginalia of the master index, disguised as a proofreader's correction. *Entry 88,412. Check your arithmetic.* Eight little words in her cramped hand, and it had taken me three hours to find them because grief makes a poor search engine.

Entry 88,412 was one of ours. One of the fragments Ulyana-and-the-boy had assembled over six stolen nights, whispered in the dark of the transcription chamber after Havel's clerks went home. Six syllables of Ostmere's true name, recovered from scattered entries across two centuries of the guild's oldest books: a syllable from a grain tax record, one from a marriage contract, one buried in the colophon of a hymnal, one scrawled in a margin by some forgotten archivist who'd written *this word tastes like snow* and been dismissed as mad. Six syllables, cross-checked against the boy's own fading memory, each one verified twice.

The name of a country, spoken whole, opens the original door — the first door, the one from before the bargain, before the erasure, before everything. We had six of what the old grammars suggested were seven parts. We'd assumed the seventh was lost, scattered too finely, digested beyond recovery.

*Check your arithmetic.*

So I checked. I spread the fragments across my desk in the small hours of the afternoon, with the guild sealed around me like a fist, and I did the thing scribes do best, which is count.

Six syllables. Seven positions in the name. And here is what I found, staring at the fragments until my eyes watered: they weren't seven equal parts. Six of them were complete units — self-contained sounds, whole and stable. But the sixth fragment, the one from the hymnal colophon, the one that tasted like snow — it was truncated. Cut off mid-sound. A syllable missing its own final note, the way a bell missing its clapper can be rung forever without ringing.

We didn't have six and a lost seventh. We had six sevenths of a seventh, and the remainder —

I sat back. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me, because shaking hands are useless for arithmetic.

The remainder wasn't scattered. Scattered pieces get scattered *somewhere*. This piece had been removed entirely, cleanly, deliberately — and there was only one entity in the world with both the means and the appetite.

The Hollow hadn't eaten the founding syllable. The Hollow had *started* with it.

* * *

"You look terrible," the boy said, when I let myself into the vault.

His name was Ivell, though only I used it now; the guild's records listed him as Asset O-1, Appraisal Pending, which is the kind of bureaucratic poetry that makes you want to burn a building down. He was sitting on his cot with his knees drawn up, and in the lamplight I could see how much thinner he'd gotten — how much of him had already been spent feeding me syllables night after night, each word costing him color, warmth, certainty. His hair had gone from black to a gray-brown I didn't like at all.

"I've been doing sums," I said.

"At this hour? You really are a scribe."

"Maren sent me a message. Before—" I didn't finish. He nodded anyway. He'd liked Maren. She used to sneak him honeyed bread when the tribunal wasn't looking, back when sneaking was something she could still remember how to do.

"Tell me the sums."

So I told him. The six fragments laid out on my desk. The truncated sixth. The clean removal. The mathematics of hunger — because once I'd seen it, I couldn't unsee it, and laying it out for Ivell was the only way to make it hold still. "Ostmere's name has seven parts. We have six, and one of them is broken. The missing piece isn't lost in some archive, because I've searched the archives until my fingers bled. It was never archived. It was the *first* thing taken. The Hollow ate the founding moment before it ate anything else — the memory of the kingdom being born. Which means—"

"Which means the last syllable is inside it." He said it quietly, without drama, the way children say the worst things, because they haven't yet learned to dress them up. "Inside the monster."

"Inside the wound," I corrected, because Chapter Eighteen of this particular nightmare was already drafting itself in my mind and I didn't love what it was implying. "There may be a difference. There's usually a difference. It's mostly a matter of paperwork."

He smiled at that, briefly, and the smile cost him something, and I hated the arithmetic of that too.

"So we go down," he said. "Into the drowned palace."

"We go down," I confirmed. "Tonight. During the feeding. While every clerk in this building is busy pouring you into the master ledger, you and I are going to be somewhere else entirely."

"They'll notice."

"They'll notice eventually. But Havel wants this feeding to be ceremonial. Moonrise, incense, the full tribunal in their gray robes. Ceremony is slow. Slow is a door, and we're going to walk through it." I crouched so we were eye to eye, and I made myself say the next part plainly, because he'd earned plainness and I owed him more than I could ever pay down. "Ivell. What's down there might not let us take the syllable back. The Hollow kept it first for a reason. And if we fail, there's no second attempt, no appeal, no tribunal of our own. Do you understand?"

He considered this with the grave attention he gave everything, and then he said: "My mother used to sing a song about a door. Before the dark came. She said every door is just a wall that decided to be brave." He looked up at me. "I'm tired of walls."

Somewhere far beneath us — beneath the guild, beneath the street, beneath the drowned palace where petrified citizens stood frozen mid-flight with their hands raised toward a sky two centuries gone — something shifted. Not an earthquake. An *adjustment*. The lamps guttered in unison, and for one clear moment I heard the underwater bell that lived under the world, tolling once, patiently, like a creditor marking an account.

Moonrise was four hours away.

I went to sharpen my pen.

* * *

They gathered in the transcription chamber at dusk: Havel and the twelve senior archivists in their gray robes, the great master ledger open on its lectern like a mouth, the incense smoking in braziers carved with symbols nobody living could read. They'd set a chair for Ivell in the center of the room, a comfortable chair, upholstered in wine-red velvet, because hospice workers care about comfort.

I stood at my station with my pen and my inkwell and my heart going like a debtor's knock, and I watched them arrange themselves, and I thought: *you have fed this thing for two hundred years and called it mercy. Tonight I'm going to show you what mercy actually costs.*

Havel caught my eye across the chamber. He smiled — kindly, terribly, the smile of a man who believes his arithmetic is sound.

He had no idea I'd redone the sum.

"One syllable short," I murmured to the inkwell, to myself, to the ghost of Maren Voss and the memory of her daughter's name, wherever those had gone. "But I know where the last piece lives. And tonight, monster, I'm coming to collect."

The great bronze doors hummed their sealed song. Above, the last light died. Below, the Hollow turned toward us in its sleep like a man hearing his name called from very far away.

Moonrise.
