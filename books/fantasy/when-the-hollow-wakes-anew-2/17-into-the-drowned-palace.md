## 17. Into the Drowned Palace

### Ulyana

There is a saying in the Ledger Guild: *every debt has a floor.* It means that no matter how deep a man sinks, there is a bottom to him, and at the bottom he can stand. I had believed this for thirty years the way you believe in the weather — without examination, because it had never once done anything to earn your trust.

The floor of the Guild of Vaskarand was forty-one levels below the counting hall.

I know because we counted them. The boy counted aloud in his own language, which I could now mostly follow, and I counted silently in mine, and where our numbers met we were both right and both wrong, which seemed an honest description of everything else we'd done together.

His name — his true name, the one I will not write here even now, even with most of myself already spent — meant something like *snow-that-arrives-before-the-bell*. In Ostmere, I have since learned, children were not named for what they were but for what they would interrupt. A bell, a silence, a war. He had been named for the moment just before something ended.

He interrupted nothing. The Hollow came anyway.

"Ulyana." His voice echoed strangely down the stairwell, as if the stone repeated it in two languages at once. "The walls are listening."

"I know."

"No. Not like the guild listens." He pressed his small hand flat against the descending wall. "Like a throat listens. Before swallowing."

I did not tell him he was being morbid. Morbid, in my experience, is a word comfortable people use to describe accurate people.

We carried between us: one lantern of bottled thunder (acquisition 4471, appraised value ruinous, current purpose desperate), a satchel of ledger pages bearing eleven syllables of Ostmere's shattered name, a pen, and half a loaf of black bread that Maren had left on my desk the night before she forgot her daughter's face. I still don't know if the bread was kindness or an accounting error. With Maren it was always both.

Havel believed we were locked in the transcription vault. Havel believed many things. This is what happens when a man lives two hundred years: he stops checking his assumptions, because checking takes time and time is the only currency he cannot print.

Behind us, far above, the city was forgetting itself street by street. Ahead of us, the stairs went down into the dark like a sentence that refused to find its verb.

At level thirty, the architecture changed. That's the only way I can put it. The guild above was built in Vaskarand's style — greedy verticals, everything reaching upward like a hand asking for more. But somewhere around the thirtieth landing, the proportions shifted under our feet. The steps widened. The ceilings rose. The stone stopped looking quarried and started looking *grown*.

"The palace," the boy said, and his voice went very quiet, the way voices go in sickrooms and cathedrals. "We're inside it already. We've been inside it for hours."

I looked back up the stairwell. It climbed away into darkness, but I could feel it now — the truth of it, sitting in my knees like weather. Every level of the guild had been built on a level of something older. The Ledger Guild of Vaskarand was not built *near* the drowned palace.

It was built *into* it. Floor by floor, ledger over memory, debt laid over debt until the original structure couldn't be seen from the surface — only descended into.

"Your guild," the boy said, with the terrible precision of eight-year-olds everywhere, "is a parasite with paperwork."

I had spent thirty years calling it an institution. I found I could not argue.

### The Boy

The snow remembers me before I remember it.

That is the first thing I understand when the stairs end and the corridor begins: this place is made of remembering. Not memories like the ones the guild took from me — those came out like teeth, leaving holes that whistled. These are different. These are memories that stayed. Memories that became the walls themselves.

Mama told me once, when I was very small and the world was still whole: *the palace is built on the first winter. Every stone holds a cold it refuses to give back.* I thought she was telling me a story about stones. She was telling me a story about people. Everything in Ostmere is a story about people pretending to be something else.

The corridor glows faintly, the way snow glows under moonlight even when there is no moon. Ulyana raises his lantern, but the light doesn't help; the glow comes from inside the walls, from shapes moving slowly beneath the surface like fish under ice.

Faces. There are faces in the walls.

I stop walking. Ulyana walks three more steps before he notices, because adults are built that way — they keep going toward the thing they fear until the fear physically picks them up and turns them around.

"Don't," he says.

"They're not dead," I say. "Look properly."

And he does look properly, because that is the thing about Ulyana: he is a coward in every direction except the one that matters. He lifts the lantern and he looks, and I watch his face do the arithmetic of horror — the slow adding-up of mouths open mid-word, hands raised mid-reach, a woman with her arm extended toward a door she never reached.

They are running. All of them, frozen in the walls, they are all running in the same direction, away from the heart of the palace, toward exits that closed two hundred years ago.

But their eyes —

Their eyes are not afraid. That is what makes me want to sit down in the glowing corridor and never get up. Their eyes are *waiting*. As if the running was only the outside part, and underneath, some patient piece of them knew they would be kept. Known. Held in the wall like flowers pressed in a book.

"Are they suffering?" Ulyana asks. His voice cracks in the middle, and he pretends it didn't, and I pretend I didn't hear, because that is also a thing we do for each other.

I touch the wall. It is warm.

Warm, like a sleeping animal. Warm, like bread. Warm, like the side of Mama's neck when I fell asleep against her and she sang anyway, quieter, so as not to wake me.

*I am here,* the wall says, without words, in the language under all languages. *I remember being me.*

"No," I tell him. "They're not suffering. They're waiting to be remembered back."

Ulyana writes something in his little notebook. He does this when he cannot bear a feeling — he converts it into ink. I think it is a way of carrying things without letting them sit in the chest where they get heavy. I have started doing it too. My notebook is full of drawings of his face making expressions he does not know he makes.

We walk on, through the running people who are not running anymore.

### Ulyana

Here is what I know about crystallized memory, which I learned the hard way, which is the only way I learn anything:

If you walk through a corridor of it long enough, it starts walking through you.

By the twentieth corridor — I had stopped numbering them accurately, because the numbers had begun to feel like an accusation — I was seeing things I had no right to see. Not visions. *Echoes.* A market square full of umbrellas the color of plums. A bridge that played music when the wind crossed it, low notes, like a cello being polite. A king whose face I could not hold onto, laughing at something a gardener said.

Ostmere. Leaking into me through my boot-soles.

"You're limping," the boy observed.

"I'm not limping. I'm walking with gravitas."

"You're limping *with gravitas*."

The child had developed wit. I blame myself entirely. There is no education so corrupting as a scribe's.

But my knee — my honest, unpoetic left knee, which had complained about stairs since I was nineteen — was indeed aching, and not from the descent. From the *pressure*. The deeper we went, the heavier the air became, and the heaviness was not air at all. It was attention. Something below us had noticed the weight of our footsteps and was listening the way a debtor listens for the knock of the collector.

The boy felt it too. He had gone quiet in the way he went quiet before his worst rememberings, holding his own wrist behind his back — a habit I recognized, because I bit my thumbnail to the quick during audits, and everyone in the world has one gesture they use to hold themselves together.

"There," he said suddenly, and pointed.

The corridor opened ahead of us into a gallery — a long hall with tall windows along one side, except the windows showed no sky. They showed *water*. Green-black water, pressing against glass older than any glass in Vaskarand, and in the water, pale shapes drifting past like the ghosts of curtains. We were beneath the reservoir. Beneath the river, maybe. Beneath everything Vaskarand thought it stood on.

The gallery floor was a mosaic. I knelt despite my knee, despite my dignity, and wiped two centuries of silt from it with my sleeve.

A map. Of course it was a map. I am a scribe; the universe knows my weaknesses.

Ostmere spread across the tiles in lapis and gold — every city, every road, every river named in a script that predated anything in the guild's oldest folio. And in the center, where a capital should be, the mosaic artists had placed a single tile of clear glass, and beneath the glass, deep down in whatever darkness the floor had been built over, something moved.

Slowly. Patiently. The way a debt moves through a life.

"That's us," the boy said. "That's where we're standing. On top of the glass."

I looked up at the ceiling of the gallery, then down at the tile, doing the geometry, and felt the floor of my understanding give way — the real floor this time, the one the saying promised me, the one I could stand on. There is no floor. There is only the next layer down, and the next, and somewhere at the bottom of the stack, a mouth.

"It ate the capital first," I said. "Then it ate the country's name. Then it ate the country out of the world. And then—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and high, the laugh of a man standing on glass above a mouth. "And then it slept for two hundred years under the one institution greedy enough to build on top of it. Because we smelled like it. Debt. Hunger. Taking things and calling it keeping them."

The boy put his hand on my shoulder. He had to reach up to do it. Someday soon he wouldn't, and I tried not to think about how few somedays either of us had left.

"We're not smelling like it anymore," he said. "Come on. The bell tower is close."

"How do you know?"

"Because I can hear it." He smiled at me, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen on a living face. "It rings underwater, Ulyana. Didn't I tell you? Everything here rings underwater. You just have to be deep enough to hear it."

### The Boy

The last door is made of mother-of-pearl, and it is warm, and I am afraid of it, and I am not going to say I'm afraid of it, because Ulyana is afraid too and someone in this pair has to pretend otherwise and I lost the coin toss years ago.

Before the door, the corridor narrows, and the walls stop being walls.

They become people again — not the runners from before, but standing figures, whole, clear as glass, arranged along the final passage like candles at a vigil. A guard with his spear grounded and his helmet off, held under his arm like a sleeping cat. A baker with flour still white on her forearms. A girl my age holding a wooden bird on a string.

They are petrified mid-flight like all the others. But these ones nearest the door — these ones turned around. At the last moment, at the very edge of the eating, they turned and faced the Hollow instead of running from it.

Facing it so it would have to see them.

I understand that now. I understood it the moment I saw the girl with the bird. She isn't frozen in terror. She is frozen in *defiance*, chin up, eyes open, making herself witnessed. Because that is the one thing the Hollow cannot digest. It can eat names, maps, treaties, mothers, bells, entire countries — but it cannot eat being seen. Everything it swallowed is still inside it, whole, waiting. Two hundred years of Ostmere, undigested, because nobody ever stopped to look at what they'd lost.

Nobody remembered it back.

Until a boy walked out of the erasure, speaking the dead language, and a cowardly scribe decided — badly, reluctantly, with much complaint and limping — to write it all down.

"Ulyana," I say.

"Hm." He is reading the mother-of-pearl door, running his fingers over the grain, looking for hinges or handles. There are none. Doors out of a country that no longer exists do not open from the outside.

"When we go through — if we go through — will you forget my face?"

His fingers stop moving.

"Yes," he says. Because he lies to me about everything except the things that count.

"Okay," I say. "Then look now. Properly. So at least one of us keeps it."

He turns. The lantern light makes shadows under his eyes, and his hair is gray at the temples though he's not old, and he has ink on his cheek from biting his pen, and he looks at me the way the girl with the bird looked at the dark — chin up, eyes open, refusing to be digested quietly.

"I'm looking," he says.

Somewhere below us, so deep it is less a sound than a shift in the blood, something vast turns over in its sleep. The mother-of-pearl door grows warmer under my palm.

And from beneath the water, beneath the floor, beneath two hundred years of deliberate forgetting, I hear it clearly at last:

The bell. Ringing once.

Calling the rest of my name home.
