## 18. The First Memory

I have died before. I want to say that plainly, at the start, so you understand what kind of boy is writing this — or rather, what kind of boy is being written, because I no longer trust the difference.

The first time I died I was four years old and the snow was falling on Vellharrow and my mother was singing a song about a bell that rings underwater, and then the darkness came up through the floorboards like water coming up through a hull, and everyone in the market square forgot their own names mid-scream, and I did not forget mine, which was the cruelest thing the Hollow ever did to me. It let me keep it. A name with nothing left to hold onto is just a stone in your pocket. You carry it until you sink.

The second time I died was in the vault of the Ledger Guild, when Ulyana Coldharbour began to write me down. Every word he took went somewhere. I used to think it went into the ink. Then I thought it went into him. Now I know it went down, down past the foundations, past the drowned palace, into the thing that ate my country — and every word we fed it made it stir a little more, like a sleeper turning toward a smell from the kitchen.

And now here is the third time, which may be the last, and this one I am choosing, which makes it either not a death at all or the only honest one I'll get.

* * *

Ulyana walks ahead of me down the corridor of crystallized memory, and his lantern throws our shadows long against the walls, where the people of Ostmere stand frozen in the amber of their own last thoughts. A baker with his hands still in the dough. A soldier turned around mid-march, looking back toward a sound none of us will ever hear again. A woman holding a child who is also a woman holding a child who is also — the memory repeats itself, folded like paper, because that is what terror does when you freeze it: it loops.

"Do not touch them," Ulyana says, for the fourth time. He has a way of saying things four times when he is frightened, as if repetition were a rope and we are both hanging off the same cliff by it.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking about it."

"I'm always thinking about it," I admit. "They're my people. Two hundred years I've been the only one of us left, and now there's a whole street of us, and you're telling me not to say hello."

He stops walking. In the lantern light his face does the thing it does when he is doing arithmetic he doesn't like — the small frown, the jaw set sideways, like a man biting a coin to see if it's real. "If you touch one," he says slowly, "the memory might recognize you. And memories that recognize their owners wake up. And waking memories—" He gestures vaguely upward, toward the ceiling, toward the city above the city, toward everything.

"Feed it. Yes. I know. Everything feeds it. Bread feeds it. Names feed it. Grief feeds it. At some point I'd like to meet something that *eats*, instead of everything just being food in different outfits."

That gets half a laugh out of him, the tired kind, the kind that's mostly air escaping a man under pressure. We have been laughing like that for weeks now, in the stolen hours between transcriptions, building something out of ourselves the way two people build a fire out of wet wood — badly, stubbornly, mostly out of spite. He is not my brother. My brother would have been older, probably, and worse at sums. But Ulyana is the closest thing the world has left to offer me, and the world has been offering very little for a very long time.

The corridor slopes downward. The amber thickens. And beneath our feet — I feel it before I hear it, the way you feel thunder in your teeth before your ears get the news — something turns over in its sleep.

"It knows we're here," I say.

"Of course it knows." Ulyana's voice goes flat and practical, which is how he sounds when hope has run out and all that's left is procedure. "We've been feeding it for weeks. We're not guests. We're the delivery."

* * *

The palace heart is a room, though calling it a room is like calling the sea a puddle. It is vast and round and its walls are not stone but memory compressed until it shines, and in the center of it stands the Hollow.

I had imagined teeth. Everyone imagines teeth. Teeth are what fear wears when it wants to be understood. But the Hollow has no teeth, no eyes, no mouth, no shape you could draw for a child and say *beware*. It is an absence wearing the costume of a presence. It is the space in a sentence where a word was removed. It is the hole in every map of the world, two hundred years wide and two hundred years deep, and standing in front of it I feel less like a boy facing a monster than like a drop of water facing the drain.

Ulyana sets the lantern down. His hands are shaking. So are mine. We shake together for a moment, which is its own kind of company.

"The name," he says. "Tell me what you have."

I recite it. Our true name, Ostmere's name, the one that was shattered across ten thousand ledger entries and fed piecemeal into this thing's bottomless gullet so that no one could ever say it whole again. I have carried my fragments since the vault. *Vess. Anu. Kareth.* Three syllables out of however many there were. Each one cost me something — the smell of my mother's bread, the color of the door of our house, the name of my dog, whose face I can no longer picture except as a shape of absence shaped exactly like a dog. I am a book that has been selling itself page by page to buy its own ending.

"One syllable short," Ulyana says. He says it without drama, which is somehow worse than screaming. "The founding memory. The first thing it took. It's in there. Inside it. The first syllable of the first day."

"So we go in and take it back."

"We go in and ask," he corrects. "There's a difference. There has to be a difference. If there isn't a difference, then we're just thieves with better manners than the guild, and I refuse — I *refuse* — to have come all this way to be a burglar of grief."

I look at him for a long moment. He is twenty-some years older than me and half my height of courage, or maybe it's the other way around, I've never been good at measuring other people's courage; I only know mine feels like a coin I keep flipping, and lately it keeps landing on the same side.

"I'll touch it," I say.

"No—"

"You said it yourself. Memories that recognize their owners wake up. I'm the owner. Of all of it. Every street, every bell, every frozen person in that corridor — they're mine, Ulyana. Not the guild's. Not the Hollow's. Mine. If anyone's going to knock on that door, it should be family."

He opens his mouth to argue, and I watch him do the arithmetic again — the coin-biting arithmetic, the ledger-keeper's reflex — and I watch the sum come out the same way it always comes out, and I watch him hate it. That's the thing about Ulyana Coldharbour that nobody who lent him money ever understood: he always pays. Even when paying ruins him. *Especially* when paying ruins him. It's the most expensive virtue in the world and he carries it like a debt.

"If it takes you," he says quietly, "I'm coming in after you."

"Don't be stupid."

"It's my best quality."

* * *

So I touch it.

I reach out my hand — the hand that held my mother's, the hand that held the pen while Ulyana wrote me down, the hand that is smaller than the task in front of it, as hands always are — and I press my palm flat against the Hollow, against the absence, against the drain.

And the world pours backward into me.

Not like remembering. Remembering is slow, it's warm, it's a room you walk back into. This is a floodgate breaking. This is two hundred years arriving all at once, uninvited, unsorted, demanding to be felt in a single instant, and I stand in the middle of it the way you'd stand in a river of fire and snow at the same time, and I do not let go, because letting go would mean all of this happened for nothing.

Here is what I receive. Here is the founding memory of Ostmere. Here is the thing the guild built its ledgers on top of and called it civilization:

There was no country yet. There was a valley, cold and green, and a people with no name worth keeping, and a hunger in the ground beneath them — old, patient, enormous, the same hunger, *the same hunger* — and the people were starving, because winters are honest creditors and the valley had borrowed against spring too many times.

And someone — a woman, her name comes to me whole, her name is *Vessa Anukareth*, and I nearly fall over when I realize what I am hearing, because that is the name, that is the name we have been reassembling, syllable by bleeding syllable — that woman walked down into the dark to bargain with the hunger.

She offered it a trade. Not food. Something better than food. She offered it *witness*. She said: you are lonely down there in the dark, and loneliness is the deepest hunger there is, deeper than stomachs, deeper than winter. Give us your strength to build, and we will give you our lives to remember. Every harvest, every birth, every song, every grief — ours to live, yours to keep. A country as a memory-palace, and you the keeper of it.

And the hunger — the Hollow, the monster, the thing under everything — said yes.

Ostmere was born from that bargain. Do you understand? Do you see it? The kingdom and the monster are the same wound. We did not build a country on top of a beast. We built a country *out of* a beast, the way you build a house out of timber, the way you build a life out of loss. Every bell tower rang because the Hollow held the note. Every harvest came up gold because the Hollow turned the soil of itself. And in return we remembered *for* it, we were its eyes and its ledger, and for two hundred years — no, longer, longer than the erasure, longer than the guild, back to the very first winter — the arrangement held.

Until the guild.

Until men with scales and contracts found the oldest ledger in the world — the bargain itself, written in the bedrock — and read it, and understood it, and did what lenders do. They refinanced. They took the payments and redirected them. They cut the country off from its own keeper and began feeding the Hollow secondhand scraps, bottled thunder, saints' shadows, the dregs of acquisitions, and the Hollow starved on a diet of strangers' memories, and a starving keeper cannot hold a country together, and so the country came apart — not destroyed, *repossessed*. Erased from the maps the way a name is struck from a ledger when the debt is called due.

The Hollow didn't eat Ostmere.

The Hollow *is* Ostmere. What's left of it. The bones of it. The keeper, abandoned, mad with hunger, gnawing on whatever fell through the floorboards of the world above.

I am crying. I notice it distantly, the way you notice rain on a window in someone else's house. The tears run down my face and I do not wipe them because both my arms are busy — one pressed against the absence, one holding myself upright — and besides, these are not only my tears. They are Vessa's. They are the baker's. They are the soldier's, turned around mid-march. Two hundred years of a country's grief, finally arriving somewhere it can be felt, and the somewhere is me, because I am the heir, and inheritance means receiving what nobody wanted to carry.

"Ulyana," I say, and my voice comes out strange, layered, like a choir of one. "It wasn't a monster. It was a promise. We broke it. *They* broke it. The guild took our payments and kept the interest and called the whole thing mercy."

Behind me, Ulyana makes a sound I have never heard him make — not a word, not a gasp, something between a prayer and a man dropping a heavy load he's carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

And the Hollow — the Hollow *shifts*. Not the hungry shifting of a beast smelling meat. Something else. Something like a sleeper hearing, after two hundred years, a voice that knows its name.

Because I do know its name now. That's the terrible gift inside the terrible truth. The founding memory isn't just history. It's the missing syllable. *Vessa Anukareth* — the founder's name is the country's name is the Hollow's name, all one word, all one wound, and I am standing here holding the final piece in my mouth like a coal.

One syllable. That's all that's left. One syllable, and the door opens, and the country walks out of its own grave.

But the syllable is not free. Nothing in this world is free; I learned that in a vault full of contracts before I was tall enough to reach the shelf they sat on. The founding memory comes with its price attached, the way everything in Ostmere did, the way everything everywhere does. And as the knowledge settles into me, I feel the price itemize itself, patient as a guild clerk, and I turn to look at Ulyana — at the scribe who sold himself page by page to walk me here, who has already paid his mother's face and his debts and is down to his name and his kindness and not much else —

— and above us, far above, through two hundred feet of stone and memory, I hear the vault doors of the Ledger Guild slam open, and boots begin to descend, many boots, in step, and Guildmaster Havel's voice rolls down the stairwell like a bill come due:

"*Step away from it, boy. The feeding begins now.*"

The Hollow stirs beneath my palm, waiting to hear what I'll say.

So do I.
