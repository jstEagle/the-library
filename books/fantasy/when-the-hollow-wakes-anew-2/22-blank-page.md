## 22. Blank Page

I woke to the sound of my own breathing and no idea whose it was.

That sounds like the opening of a bad joke, and for a moment I lay there in the dust expecting the punchline. The ceiling above me was cracked plaster and exposed beam, hung with cobwebs that swayed though there was no wind. Light came through a gap in the wall the shape of a door that had been torn rather than opened — which is an important distinction, I would later learn, though at the time I knew nothing, including this.

I sat up. My body ached in the specific way of someone who has been carrying something very heavy for a very long time and has only just set it down. My hands were ink-stained to the second knuckle. That much I could read about myself: *this person works with ink.* Beyond that, nothing. No name arrived when I reached for one. No mother's face. No ledger of debts with my figure at the bottom, growing like a mold.

I checked, actually. I reached inward the way you reach for a coin you're sure is in your pocket, and found only smooth emptiness, the interior equivalent of a swept floor. It should have terrified me. Perhaps some part of me understood that terror requires a self to be terrified *for*, and mine was currently out for delivery.

What I had instead was stranger than fear. I had habits.

I discovered this by standing up and immediately, without deciding to, brushing the dust off in three downward strokes, left hip first. I discovered it again when I caught myself stepping over a fallen rafter rather than on it — careful, economical, the step of someone used to navigating cluttered spaces without looking up from a page. Whoever I had been, he had walked through the world half-absent and precise, and his feet remembered even if nothing else did.

The room resolved slowly. Shelves, mostly collapsed. A counter. And scattered across everything — paper. Thousands upon thousands of loose leaves, ledger pages torn free, drifting like the world's most bureaucratic snowfall. Some were blank. Some were dense with columns of figures. A few near my feet were scorched at the edges.

I picked one up because my hands wanted something to do, and I read:

*Acquisition 4,112: bottled thunder (small), provenance uncertain. Assessed value: 3 marks, 4 pennies. Condition: rattling.*

And underneath, in a cramped, slanted hand that I recognized before I understood why — it was my own handwriting, though I had no memory of writing anything in my life — a small annotation: *do not shelve near glass.*

I laughed. It came out rusty, like a hinge in a house nobody lived in anymore, which was accurate.

So. This place was a guild of some kind. I worked here, or had. I catalogued impossible things and annotated them with dry little warnings, and apparently I had opinions about shelving. It wasn't much of a biography, but it was more than most people wake up with, and I have since learned to count my blessings carefully, having once paid an enormous price for them.

* * *

I explored. There wasn't much else to do, and my feet kept making decisions anyway, carrying me down corridors with the confidence of someone who had walked them ten thousand times.

The guild — I found the word carved over an archway, *THE LEDGER GUILD OF VASKARAND*, though half the letters had been blasted out, so that it read more honestly as *TH LEDG R GU LD OF V S K R ND*, which struck me as an improvement — the guild was ruined. Not burned exactly, not collapsed exactly. Emptied. As if something enormous had passed through and taken all the weight out of things. Walls stood. Doors hung. But the rooms felt hollowed, the way a bell feels after it cracks.

In what must have been the main hall, chandeliers lay in pieces on the floor, crystal scattered like ice after a thaw. I stepped around them carefully. For no reason I could name, my throat tightened when I looked at the largest one, the great iron wheel of it lying face-down among its own shattered light. Something about it made me want to say *steady, steady* to a room full of panicking people, and I didn't know why, and I stood there a while letting whatever it was move through me and out.

Grief, I think. Grief with no address. It has to go somewhere, and bodies are where it goes.

There were no other survivors. I want to report that plainly, because I looked, thoroughly, calling out as I went — and here's another thing my voice knew how to do: it called out in a tone meant not to frighten anyone, gentle, pitched for children and small animals. *Hello? Anyone?* The halls gave back only my own echo, and even that sounded borrowed.

But I found traces. In the vault level, deep down where the air went cold and mineral, I found the door. Or rather, I found the space where a door had been — a doorway-shaped absence in the stone, edges smooth as river glass, and through it a view that did not match the rest of the ruin. Through it I saw, briefly, green hills under a low gold sky, and a bell tower, and snow coming down slow and unhurried, and a country breathing.

Then it closed. Not slammed — closed, politely, the way a host shuts a door behind a departing guest so the cold doesn't follow them in. And I was left looking at blank stone, and I found that I was smiling, and crying, and had absolutely no idea why, and both felt correct.

Whoever I had been, he had done something there. Given something. I pressed my ink-stained hand flat against the stone and said, aloud, to whoever might still hear me from the far side of it: "Good."

That was all I had. Just *good.* No name to sign beneath it. But I meant it with my whole swept-clean self, and I have decided to trust that this counted.

* * *

By evening light — real evening light, ordinary, falling through the broken roof in long amber bars — I made camp in the scriptorium, because my feet took me there and refused every alternative. Long tables. Rows of desks worn smooth by centuries of elbows. And on one desk, the one nearest the window, arranged neatly as if set out for me: a ledger. Blank. Fresh-bound, black boards, unmarked spine.

Beside it, a pen. Good nib, freshly cut. And a stoppered pot of ink, full.

Now, I am not a fool, or at least I contain the muscle-memory of not being one, and I know that objects arranged neatly in abandoned ruins are either gifts or traps, and often both. But I also noticed something else: the dust. The whole guild wore dust like a second roof, thick everywhere, undisturbed. Except on that desk. Around the ledger and pen, a clear circle, wiped clean by someone's sleeve within the last day.

Someone had been here. Someone had prepared this. Someone who knew I would wake with nothing and start reaching for the nearest way to begin again.

I thought hard about who that might be. I turned over the faces my empty mind offered — a girl's face, laughing; an old woman's face, stern and kind at once; a boy's face, solemn, snow in his hair — but they were shapes without names, paintings with the labels peeled off. They rose and hurt and sank again, and I let them, because I suspected that holding on too tightly to what remained of them would cost me something I couldn't afford twice.

Instead I said thank you. Out loud, to the empty scriptorium, in the same gentle voice I'd used calling for survivors. If they heard, good. If they didn't, the words still needed saying, and I seemed to be the only one left to say them.

Then I sat down, uncapped the ink, and opened the ledger to its first page.

* * *

Here is what I know about beginnings, and I seem to know it in my bones even though I don't know where I learned it: they are not the absence of what came before. They are what you make of the absence. A blank page isn't empty. It's patient.

My hand hovered over the paper. The trouble — and I confess I sat with this trouble for the better part of an hour, while the light went from amber to violet — is that a book needs a name, and so does its author, and I had neither.

I tried inventing one. I tried the names that surfaced unbidden: *Ulyana,* said the shape of a girl's laugh. *Ulyana Coldharbour,* said a contract-shaped hole inside me, though the words themselves were gone and only their contour remained, like a fossil in soft stone. Was it mine? Had it ever been, or had it belonged to the debts? I genuinely could not tell. And there was something in me — call it pride, call it caution, call it the residue of a man who once read a contract to its end and regretted what he found — that flinched from claiming a name I couldn't verify.

Besides. Whoever I had been, he had spent himself entirely so that someone else could keep theirs. That was written into the architecture of my emptiness; I could feel its purpose the way you feel the purpose of a bridge by walking across it. To take up his name now, half-remembered, secondhand — it felt like wearing a dead man's coat. Warm, maybe, but not mine.

Better, I thought, to write myself fresh. From the first line. Which raised the question of what the first line should be.

Every instinct I owned — and I appeared to own several, all of them professional — insisted that openings matter. That a first line is a door, and doors, in my line of work, had apparently always been serious business. So I drafted. In pencil first, then ink. I filled three pages of false starts:

*I was born in debt, which is the Vaskarandi way.*

Too bitter. Also, possibly, plagiarized from my own past life, and I'd promised myself I wouldn't live there.

*The city of Vaskarand stands on top of a secret.*

True, I was almost certain, but wrong in spirit. Secrets are for keeping. I had a feeling — a strong one, arriving from nowhere like weather — that the thing this city stood on deserved better than keeping.

*Let me tell you about the year the ledgers burned.*

Closer. But it started in the middle, and middlings were what I was trying to escape.

Outside, the light failed properly. Somewhere in the ruined city a dog barked, and then another answered, and then a third, a ragged conversation passing street to street, and I listened to it the way you listen to music in a language you don't speak: completely, understanding nothing, moved anyway. People were out there. A whole city of them, most of whom had lost hours or days or small pieces of themselves to whatever had happened here — I saw the evidence of it everywhere, doors left open, meals abandoned mid-bite, a child's shoe on a windowsill with no child anywhere near it. They were reassembling tonight, I imagined. Asking each other *what happened, where were you, who am I.* Some of them would find gaps. Some of those gaps would never close.

I knew something about that. I was, arguably, the leading expert in the field.

And it occurred to me — sitting there in the dark with my three pages of failed first lines — that maybe the book shouldn't be about the ending at all. Maybe it shouldn't be about the Hollow, or the door, or the boy whose face I could almost see and whose name sat just beyond recall like a word on the tip of the tongue. Those chapters existed already. They were written in a ledger somewhere, or in the stone of a sealed doorway, or in the memory of a kingdom that remembers itself now, green hills and gold sky and snow falling on a bell tower, unhurried, home.

Maybe the first line just needed to be true, and small, and mine.

I dipped the pen.

* * *

Here is the line, exactly as I wrote it, because I have decided it is the truest thing I own:

*I woke with nothing, and for the first time in my life, nothing owed anyone.*

It isn't elegant. A critic would call it flat. But my hand shook when I finished it, and I watched the ink dry, and I felt the sentence settle into me like a key into a lock I hadn't known I was carrying.

No debts. That was the miracle hiding inside the catastrophe, the joke the universe had saved for last: the man who spent his whole existence calculating what he owed had finally paid it all, and what remained was *free*. Free of the guild. Free of the arithmetic. Free even of the self who had done the spending, who had given away his mother's face and his childhood kitchen and his own name so that a boy could walk out of a wound in the world into a country made whole. That man was a hero, probably, or close enough that the distinction no longer mattered, and he was gone, and I was what the giving had left behind.

A blank page. With a pen. And, I was discovering, a surprising amount of kindness installed at the factory level — kindness that survived the wipe, kindness that seemed to be load-bearing.

I wrote on. Not fast — carefully, the way you build anything meant to stand. I wrote about waking in the dust. About the bell tower through the closing door, and the smile-crying that followed me like a loyal dog. About the chandelier and the grief with no address. About the circle wiped clean in the dust, and the thank-you said to nobody, and the three failed first lines, included here in full because a book that hides its drafts is lying to you.

And then, near midnight, with the ink pot nearly drained and my hand cramping in a way that felt wonderfully, ordinarily human, I wrote the part I had been circling all along. The part about the faces. The girl laughing. The old woman, stern and tender. The boy with snow in his hair, solemn as a cathedral.

I wrote: *I don't remember them. I remember that they mattered. I have decided that this counts as remembering, and no power in heaven or below the ground gets a vote.*

When I finished, I sat back and read the whole first chapter through, start to finish, and for the first time since waking — since ever, as far as I knew — I felt something settle in me that I can only describe as *grounded*. Not happiness. Heavier than that. The feeling of a story with its feet under it.

Then I turned the page, ready to begin chapter two, and stopped.

Because there, faintly, on the fresh paper — so faint I thought at first it was shadow, then water damage, then wishful thinking — there was writing. Not mine. Small, precise, tucked into the corner of the page where marginalia lives. And though I had never seen this hand before in my life, my body reacted to it the way you react to a familiar voice in a dark room: every part of me turning toward it at once.

I bent close. I read it. And I laughed out loud, alone in the ruined scriptorium at midnight, because it was a warning — of course it was a warning, whoever she had been, she died with her warnings in her mouth — and it read:

*Check the ledgers that survived the fire. The ones that burned were copies. Ask yourself who needed you to think the originals were lost. — M.*

I looked up at the dark. Somewhere out there was a fire I didn't remember, originals I didn't know existed, and an old woman's ghost leaving me homework.

Well. I had a pen, a full night ahead of me, and nothing whatsoever to lose.

I dipped the nib, and began.
