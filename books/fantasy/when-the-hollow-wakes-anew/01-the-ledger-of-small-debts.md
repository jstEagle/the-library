## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

There were three hundred and eleven ways to make a name disappear from a ledger, and Cyrus Amberdale knew every one of them.

She had learned the first at nineteen: the simple strike-through, done in the same brown-black ink as everything else, so that even under lamplight the correction looked like nothing more than a scribe's tidy hand. She'd learned the last at twenty-six, in the year Overseer Vess caught her humming while she worked and decided to read her pages over her shoulder for a full month — the method of the transposed line, where a debt was not erased but quietly moved to a column no one audited because the column itself had been written out of the index.

Nine years in the temple scriptorium, and Cyrus Amberdale did not exist on paper.

It was harder than it sounded. Most people imagined erasure as a single dramatic act — a page burned, a register struck with a knife. The truth was that staying invisible required maintenance, the way a roof required slates. Every quarter, the temple received copies of the Guild of Impossible Things' trade ledgers, because the temple handled the guild's tithing and the guild handled everything else that could be bought, sold, pawned, or foreclosed upon in the Nine Valleys. And every quarter, somewhere in those columns, Cyrus's name appeared — as witness, as counter-signatory, as the small neat *C.A.* that certified a transaction had been properly recorded.

Every quarter, she removed herself again.

"You're smirking," said Pellum from the next desk.

"I'm balancing."

"Balancing and smirking. Dangerous combination." Pellum did not look up from his own work. He was sixty, half-blind in his right eye, and had been copying psalms onto vellum for the temple since before Cyrus was born. He was also, though he didn't know it, the only person alive who remembered her mother's face — because he'd once sold the memory of it to the guild, and memories sold to the guild were copied into the master vaults before they were ever transferred. Cyrus had found the record four years ago. She had never told him what he still carried. Some things you kept the way you kept a coal banked in ash: carefully, and without letting anyone see the light.

"It's the shadow," she said. "The one that got sold twice."

Pellum grunted. "Which entry?"

"Third shelf down, the winter book. A cooper from Harrowgate sold his shadow in the famine year — good price, too, forty marks, because shadows from people who've truly gone hungry are thin and valuable. Then two years later his widow sells what she claims is the same shadow." Cyrus turned the page with two fingers, the way you'd turn the page of something sleeping. "Except the guild bought it twice and only paid once, because the second sale is recorded as a *recovery*, not a purchase. There's a whole column of them. Recovered goods. Except nobody recovered anything. The man just died."

"And you're going to fix it."

"I'm going to balance it." She dipped her pen. "Fixing would mean telling someone. Balancing means the columns agree by morning and nobody has to feel anything about it."

"That," said Pellum, "is the most honest thing anyone's said in this room in twenty years, which is why I'm pretending I didn't hear it."

Overseer Vess's footsteps came down the central aisle at that moment, unhurried, the particular tread of a man who wanted to be heard approaching. Cyrus let her shoulders curve inward, let her pen slow, became exactly what the room expected: a slight, unremarkable woman of thirty with ink-stained fingers and a posture like an apology. It was a skill, being forgettable. She practiced it the way other people practiced instruments. She wore grey. She sat in the third desk from the window, always the third desk, because eyes slid off repetition. When spoken to directly she gave answers two words shorter than the question seemed to want, and somehow this made people stop asking.

"Camberly." Vess stopped at her desk. He always used the wrong name. It wasn't malice; it was worse — it was accuracy. Camberly had held this desk before her, a girl who'd left after two years to marry a chandler, and in Vess's internal ledger Cyrus had simply been filed in the space she'd left vacant. Filing error. She had never corrected it. Correcting it would have meant existing.

"Overseer."

"The guild's courier comes at dusk. The autumn books go out tonight, sealed and countersigned. I want your certification on the impossible-trades folios before then." His gaze drifted over her work without landing anywhere. "You're slow lately."

"I'm thorough."

"Thoroughness is a virtue in moderation." He moved on. Cyrus watched him go and felt the familiar small cold satisfaction of having passed, again, through the net of another person's attention without leaving a hole shaped like herself.

Then she went back to the shadow of the dead cooper, and the morning resumed its shape.

* * *

The scriptorium occupied the temple's east wing, forty desks under high windows, and it smelled of size and lamp-oil and the faint mineral tang of gall-ink. Work began at first bell and ended at dusk bell, and between those bells the scribes lived inside a weather system of paper: ledgers coming up from the archive on wheeled carts, ledgers going back down, the constant rustle like wind through a field that had never grown anything.

By midday Cyrus had balanced the winter book and moved to the autumn folios — the ones Vess wanted sealed. This was delicate work. The guild's impossible trades came through in batches, each entry countersigned by a guild factor and then certified by a temple scribe, which made the temple, technically, complicit in everything the guild had ever done. Nobody at the temple thought about this. That was the arrangement. The temple provided absolution-shaped paperwork and the guild provided the temple's entire operating budget, and both parties agreed, wordlessly and forever, not to look too closely at the other's hands.

Cyrus looked closely. That was her secret vice, the one she indulged the way Pellum smoked his pipe in the privy: she read the entries.

A memory pawned and unredeemed — a woman had put up her wedding day against a winter's coal, and the redemption window had closed in spring, and now somewhere in the guild vaults a woman's happiest afternoon sat in a glass jar while she went on living beside a husband whose face she loved without knowing why.

Six inches of a river, sold. A lullaby, sold twice — no, wait, that one had been corrected already, someone upstream had caught the double-sale and struck it. A man's talent for whistling. A village's luck, leased annually, the rent paid in firstborn lambs that were never actually collected but had to be recorded anyway, because the guild recorded everything.

And beneath all of it, threaded through every folio like a dark thread through pale cloth, the fire-tithe. Dragon-fire, the ledgers called it, though everyone knew the dragons were gone — had been gone for a century, since the Hollow War, since the king of Vaelmoor had done whatever it was he'd done that the histories refused to say plainly. Dragon-fire was what heated the cities now, what kept the great hearth-temples burning through winter. The guild collected it. The guild distributed it. The guild charged for it, quarterly, and the amounts had been rising for nine years, which was why the outer villages had started going cold.

Cyrus entered the figures, checked them twice, and certified the folio with a small neat *C.A.* that she then, per her private ritual, altered by a single stroke — turning the A's crossbar so that it read, to any future forensic eye, more like *H*. Not her initial. Not anyone's initial. A ghost in the machine of the alphabet, a signature that pointed nowhere.

She was halfway through the second folio when she saw the book.

It hadn't been there at first bell. She was certain of it. The archive cart had come up at dawn with its usual load — twelve folios, two registers, a box of misfiled correspondence — and she had counted them, because counting was the first prayer of her profession. But now, wedged at the bottom of the return-shelf behind a stack of tithe receipts, there was a book she had never catalogued: tall, narrow, bound in leather gone black with age, its spine unlettered, its edges sealed shut with wax the color of dried blood.

Temple seal-wax was white. Guild seal-wax was green. This was neither.

She should have sent it down to the archive. That was procedure. Unregistered volumes went to the Undercroft, where the archivists opened them with the proper rites and filed their contents accordingly. Cyrus had followed procedure for nine years with the devotion of a woman hiding inside a rulebook.

Instead she picked it up.

It was lighter than it looked. The wax seal had cracked along one edge long ago — not broken, just fissured, the way old things fissured if you breathed on them wrong — and when she tilted the book toward the window, the crack widened by the width of a hair, and she could see paper inside. Dense paper. Hand-ruled lines. And ink.

Not black. Prophecy-ink, the old texts called it, though nobody at the temple had seen prophecy-ink in living memory: a red so deep it was nearly purple, the color the old orders had supposedly ground from dragon-scale and oath-blood. The histories were vague on whether that was literal. Looking at it now, catching the light, Cyrus thought it might be.

She glanced down the aisle. Pellum was dozing over his psalter, pen still in hand, a drop of ink drying on the vellum below it. Vess was at the far end, berating a junior scribe about margins. The rest of the room bent over its work like a field of grey stalks.

Cyrus carried the book to her desk, set it flat, and worked one fingernail along the fissure until a page corner lifted free of the wax.

She expected scripture. She expected the kind of antique devotional verse that the Undercroft filed by the yard. What she found instead was a list — or something shaped like a list, though the entries weren't trades or debts or names of the dead. They were dates. Dozens of dates across dozens of pages, each followed by a line of text in the old liturgical shorthand, the compressed notation scribes had used a century ago when vellum was dearer than food.

She could read shorthand. Of course she could. It was the first thing they taught you here, before they taught you to hide in it.

Most of the entries meant nothing to her — *the well at Corren dries, the ninth child speaks true, the road south forgets its way* — fragments, predictions, some apparently fulfilled, some not. She turned pages faster. The dates marched forward through the century, growing sparser, until the final quarter of the book, where the entries thinned to one every few years, and then—

Tonight's date.

Inked in that deep impossible red, in a hand that had been dust a hundred years: the day, the month, the year. Tonight.

Her breath went strange. She made herself keep reading, made herself parse the shorthand beneath it, and the shorthand was short. Fourteen characters. She read them twice, three times, translating mechanically because translation was safer than comprehension:

*The hour turns on the Amberdale hand.*

The scriptorium noise receded as if a door had swung shut somewhere in her skull. She heard her own pulse. She heard Pellum's pen scratching in the distance, absurdly loud.

Amberdale. Her family name, worn smooth by nine years of careful removal — scrubbed from parish rolls, from guild registries, from the census copies that crossed her desk each spring. She had erased it everywhere. Everywhere except the one place she could not reach, which was a hundred years in the past, where some dead prophet had already written it down in ink that did not fade, in a book that had waited in the dark until precisely today to surface on an archive cart she had personally counted.

*Camberly.* Vess's voice, right beside her.

Cyrus's hand moved on instinct — the oldest instinct she owned — and snapped the book shut, sliding it beneath her folio stack in the same motion, her body curving into its forgettable shape, her face arranging itself into mild blank attention.

Vess stood over her desk. He had never stood over her desk. In nine years he had addressed her from the aisle, from the doorway, from a comfortable distance, the way one addressed furniture.

"What are you working on?"

"The autumn folios, Overseer. As instructed."

He didn't move. His eyes — small, wet, precise — traveled over her desk, her hands, the edge of black leather just visible beneath the folio. Something shifted in his face. Not suspicion. Recognition. Like a man finding, in a room he'd cleaned a thousand times, a door he'd somehow never noticed.

"Courier at dusk," he said softly. "Don't be late with the sealing."

He walked away. Cyrus sat perfectly still for the length of ten heartbeats, then twenty, then drew the book back out from under the folios with fingers that had gone cold.

Through the cracked wax, the red ink glowed faintly, patiently, like a coal that had been waiting a century for air.

Tonight. *The Amberdale hand.*

For nine years she had made herself into nothing, and the world had obligingly agreed. And now a book older than the temple had reached out of the dark and called her by name.

Cyrus Amberdale looked at the sealed prophecy, and at the window where the light was already beginning its long slide toward dusk, and understood, with the clarity of a woman reading her own death warrant in excellent shorthand, that invisibility had just failed her —

— and that somewhere below, in the Undercroft archives, someone was almost certainly already counting the carts.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing the most dangerous thing she had ever done in the temple, which was nothing at all out of the ordinary.

She balanced folios. She corrected a double-entry in the fire-tithe. She ate her bread at the third desk and spoke to no one and was spoken to by no one, and all the while the black book lay beneath her blotter like a second heartbeat, and she planned.

Dusk bell brought the guild courier — a lean man in travel-grey who signed the transfer sheet without looking at it, the way couriers did, the way everyone did, trusting the machinery of records to hold the world together. Vess sealed the folios with green wax. Cyrus certified them with her ghost-stroke *H*. The routine performed itself around her, ancient and seamless, and she moved through it thinking only: *after the last bell, after the lamps are trimmed, the Undercroft stairs are unguarded for one hour.*

One hour to return the book before it was missed. One hour to decide what a hundred years of red ink wanted from her.

But when the last bell rang and the scribes shuffled out into the cold evening, and Cyrus slipped alone down the spiral stair into the archive dark with the book hidden in her coat — when she reached the bottom step and lifted the lamp —

Sef was waiting for her.

The disgraced archivist sat on a crate among the shelved centuries, a candle stub burning beside him, watching her with the expression of a man who has been expecting someone for a very long time.

"You counted the carts this morning," Sef said. "Twelve folios. Two registers." He nodded at her coat, at the shape of the book beneath it. "That makes thirteen."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft. Down in the dark, among a thousand years of paper, Cyrus stood very still and understood that the hour named in the prophecy had already begun.

*And it had a witness.*
