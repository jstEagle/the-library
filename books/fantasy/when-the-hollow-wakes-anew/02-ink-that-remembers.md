## 2. Ink That Remembers

The prophecy named me at the hour of Vespers, and I had scraped it away by Matins.

That was the order of things. That was how the world worked: ink went down, and a scribe with a good knife and better patience could take it back up again. Nine years in the temple scriptorium had taught me every way a word could be unmade. I could lift iron-gall from vellum until the page blushed white. I could sand a ledger line so fine the parchment forgot what had been written on it. I had erased myself from tax rolls, tithe books, the guild's census of licensed trades, the temple's register of foundlings—four hundred and eleven documents that once held the name Cyrus Amberdale, and now held nothing but the faint ghost of my knife.

I believed, in the way you believe in sunrise, that anything written could be unwritten.

I want to be clear about what happened next, because later there were people who said I hesitated, that some part of me wanted to read those words and let them stand. They were wrong. What I felt when I saw *the Amberdale hand* glowing up out of a book sealed for a century was not longing. It was the specific, animal cold of a rabbit that has just understood the shape of the hawk's shadow.

* * *

It began the evening before, which is to say it began like any other evening, which is how all the worst things begin.

The scriptorium after dark was my favorite place in the world, and I don't mean that warmly. I mean it the way a thief means it. The day-scribes had gone; Overseer Calloway had gone, trailing his smell of tallow and disapproval; and the great room belonged to the lamps and to me. Forty desks. A thousand ledgers. And me moving among them like a rumor, balancing accounts no one else would touch.

Because here is the thing about the Guild of Impossible Things that nobody outside its walls understands: it does not trade in wonders. Wonders are the advertising. What the guild actually trades in is *everything else*—the residue, the runoff, the small impossible things too minor to matter to anyone but the person who has them. There is a ledger—I balanced it that night—recording the sale of a man's shadow, twice. The first buyer returned it within a month, complaining it didn't fit anymore. The second buyer kept it. There is an entry, three lines long, for the pawning of a memory: a woman's memory of her mother's voice, held against a debt of nine crowns, interest accruing. There is no record of redemption. Somewhere in this city a woman walks around with a silence where her mother used to be, and the guild holds the sound in a vault, collecting on it quarterly.

My job was to make these entries *balance*. Every impossible thing sold must be accounted against something gained. A shadow sold twice: one entry of loss, one of gain, and somewhere in the margins a note explaining why the second sale wasn't theft. (It was theft. Most of the guild's business was theft wearing arithmetic.) I wrote the reconciliations in my own hand, neat and small, and then I wrote myself out of the record of having written them. The guild's masters believed their night-ledgers were maintained by a scribe they called only "the quiet hand." They had never seen my face. As far as their records showed, no Cyrus Amberdale had ever existed.

Nine years. Four hundred and eleven erasures. You get good at being nobody. You get so good at it that you start to mistake it for safety.

The prophecy book came up from the deep stacks on a routine transfer order—a batch of pre-Temple acquisitions being re-catalogued after the flood damage in the west cellar. It arrived in my tray between a merchant's prayer roll and a child's arithmetic slate, wrapped in oilcloth, and it looked like nothing. Water-stained boards. A spine cracked like old knuckles. No title.

But the clasps were wrong. Temple books are clasped with lead seals; guild books with wax. This book wore neither. Its clasps were iron, and they were not locked so much as *closed*—the way you close a door on a room you don't go into anymore.

I should have sent it back. I have thought about that often since—the version of my life where I wrap the oilcloth, mark the tray *return unread*, and go on being nobody until somebody finally notices the nobody is gone. But the clasps gave when I touched them, quietly, like they'd been waiting, and the first page was a colophon in a hand I recognized.

I recognized it because it was almost mine.

Not literally. The Amberdales died out—or were erased out—generations before I was born; I'd found the name only once before, scratched into the flyleaf of a hymnal, and I'd taken it for my own because everyone needs a last name and orphans are allowed to steal. But the letterforms on that colophon were the letterforms I'd been taught, the ones Calloway called "old-fashioned" and made me modernize: the tall ascenders, the crossbar on the T that hooked left instead of right. Someone with my training—my family's training—had made this book.

Someone with my hands.

I turned pages. Almanac material, mostly. Weather auguries, harvest predictions, the usual profitable nonsense the guild licensed out to farmers and ship-owners. Then, near the middle, a gathering sewn in backwards, as if the binder had wanted it buried, and there—

No date. That's the thing people never believe when I tell it. Prophecies in stories always arrive with trumpets. This one was a work document. A column of dates running down the left margin like a schedule of deliveries, and beside each date, in that familiar hooked-T hand, a short entry in the guild's own trade shorthand. Most entries were mundane. *Grain up. Fire steady. No action required.* A century of nights, checked off.

And then tonight's date.

I knew it without counting. You know your own name in a crowd. Tonight's date, written a hundred years ago by a hand shaped like mine, with an entry beside it that took me three passes to decode because I did not want to understand it:

*Vespers. The Amberdale hand comes to the book. Ledger opens.*

Below that, in different ink—fresher, though still decades old—a single line added by another writer:

*Verify before spending.*

Spending. Not saving. Not warning. *Spending.* The guild's verb for assets it intended to use up.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the temple bell rang Vespers, one hundred years late and exactly on time, and I sat very still in the great empty scriptorium while my whole architecture of invisibility settled over me like snow—and failed, failed, failed, because you cannot hide inside a book that has already read you.

* * *

I did what I do. It was nearly midnight. I sharpened my knife on the leather strop I keep oiled with goose fat, and I laid the prophecy open under the best lamp, and I began to scrape.

I started with the date. Dates are safe; dates can be copyist error. I held the blade near-flat, the way you must, letting the edge kiss the surface rather than cut it, lifting the ink in pale curls that fell like ash onto my desk. The words *Vespers. The Amberdale hand comes to the book* went next, each stroke careful, my breath held, sweat despite the cold. Then *Ledger opens.* Then *Verify before spending,* though that ink fought me—it was newer, harder cured—and I had to go back twice, and by the end the vellum was thin as a moth's wing and shining where the light came through it.

When it was done, the page was blank.

I sat back. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me; shaking hands are a scribe's disgrace. I blew out the lamp, closed the iron clasps, wrapped the book in its oilcloth, and put it at the bottom of the return tray where the morning cart would carry it back down to the deep stacks and the dark.

Then I walked home through the sleeping temple quarter, and I told myself a story. Copyist's fancy. Flood-damaged ink, reactivated. A coincidence of names—Amberdale might have been common once; names die and get stolen all the time; I had stolen mine, hadn't I? Tomorrow the book would go downstairs and I would balance the memory-pawn ledger and I would be nobody again, safely, forever.

You can hold a story like that in front of yourself for about six hours. That's roughly how long the dark lasts, this time of year.

* * *

I was back at my desk before dawn—not my desk; a desk, any desk, the anonymity of furniture—re-sharpening my knife against a task list I had invented purely to have something ordinary in my hands. And at first light, when the lamplighters finished and the grey came through the high windows, I let myself walk past the return tray. Just past. Casually.

The oilcloth had come loose overnight. Books do that; cloth relaxes; it means nothing.

The clasps stood open.

The page was not blank.

The date was there. *Vespers* was there. *The Amberdale hand comes to the book* was there, every hooked ascender standing crisp and black as the hour I'd flayed them off, and beneath the restored lines, in ink so fresh it shone wet in the grey light, a new entry had appeared—one that had not existed in any version of the page I had seen:

*First erasure attempted. Noted.*

I did not scream. I am proud of that. I stood in the waking scriptorium while day-scribes drifted in around me, nodding to me the way you nod to furniture, and I looked down at a book that had *answered* me, and I did not scream, and I did not run, because running is a thing recorded people do, and some stubborn iron core of me understood even then what the book was really saying.

It was saying: I see you. Which meant, for the first time in nine years, someone could.

I closed the clasps with both hands, gently, the way you close a mouth.

* * *

Sef found me at noon, or I found him—it was always ambiguous with Sef, who had a talent for being exactly where he was least permitted, in the gaps of the building's attention. He'd been the chief archivist once, twenty years ago, before whatever he did or refused to do got him stripped of rank and reassigned to the flooded cellars to catalog things the guild preferred forgotten. He was sixty-ish and built like a rolled carpet, with ink permanently staining his fingers to the first knuckle, as if he'd been caught in rain made of writing. He was the only person in the temple who spoke to me as if I existed, and he was careful, always, never to say my name aloud.

"You look," he said, falling into step beside me in the relic corridor, "like a woman who has read something."

"I look like a woman carrying boxes."

"You look like a woman carrying boxes *who has read something*. There's a difference. The shoulders." He nodded at mine. "Reading settles in the shoulders."

We reached the copying alcove, empty at midday. I set down my boxes. Then I told him. All of it—the colophon, the column of dates, tonight's entry, the scraping, the regrown ink, the new line. *First erasure attempted. Noted.* I told him in a flat inventory voice, because if I let the other voice out, the one underneath, I would have had to admit I was afraid, and I had spent nine years practicing not admitting things.

Sef listened the way he catalogued: completely, without interruption. When I finished he was quiet for a long moment, looking at the wall, at nothing.

"Show me the book," he said.

"It goes back down with the afternoon cart."

"Cyrus." He never used the name. Hearing it stopped my breath. "Show me the book."

* * *

He read the page standing, holding it at arm's length like something venomous, and his face did something I had never seen it do. Sef's face was professionally featureless—he'd survived two decades of disgrace by expressing nothing—but watching him read, I saw the featurelessness crack along old fault lines, and what came through was grief. Old, banked, patient grief, the kind that has given up expecting company.

"When did you scrape it?" he asked.

"Near midnight."

"And it regrew by dawn." He set the book down with exaggerated care. "Ordinary ink doesn't do that. Blessed ink doesn't do that. Cursed ink doesn't even do that—curses sit still and rot. Ink that regrows is *contractual* ink. It's not writing. It's an agreement, and agreements renew themselves as long as the terms hold." He tapped the margin, below *First erasure attempted.* "And someone is reading the correspondence."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning this isn't a prophecy sitting in a book. It's a prophecy *reporting*. Everything that happens on this page gets copied forward—to wherever the original contract lives. You scraped this page, and somewhere, whoever countersigned this agreement received notice of the attempt." He looked at me, and his eyes were very tired and very kind. "You didn't erase anything, girl. You filed a report."

The cold went through me then, properly, all the way down, the rabbit-cold, the hawk-shadow. "Who countersigned?"

"I don't know. But I know what the guild does with assets that turn up in ledgers they didn't open themselves." He said it plainly, the way you state weather. "There was a scribe before me. Aldous Renner. Clever man, better than clever—found discrepancies in the founding accounts, forty years back. Started asking questions. Within a season his name was gone from every roll in this temple. Not struck—*gone*, as if it had never been entered. His quarters reassigned. His wages reissued to a scribe nobody remembered hiring. His mother—" Sef's jaw tightened. "His mother lived three streets away. She told neighbors she'd never had children. Whatever they paid her, or did to her, she believed it by the end."

"What happened to him?"

"No record exists." He spread his hands, ink-stained, empty. "That's the point. That's the entire point. The guild doesn't kill people who become inconvenient, Cyrus. Killing leaves a body, and bodies leave questions. The guild does what it does best. It *balances the account*. An asset that turns against the ledger gets written off—and a write-off, done thoroughly, means the world itself loses track of you. People look at you and their eyes slide. Your debts vanish and so does your credit. Your friends remember a face they can't place. You become a rounding error." He paused. "Or worse. Sometimes write-offs get *spent*. Used up on whatever the discrepancy requires, and then erased after, so even the using-up never happened."

"*Verify before spending*," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "That's in the margin. In newer ink."

"Yes. I noticed." Sef rubbed his stained fingers together, thinking. "Which tells us two things. One: someone has known for years that an Amberdale would come to this book, and they've been waiting to confirm you're real before committing resources. Two: they expected resistance. Why add a verification clause unless you anticipate the asset will try to deny what it is?" He almost smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of respect. "You already tried to erase yourself from the prophecy. The one thing it says you'll do. You're following the script, girl, and proving the prophecy accurate with every move."

"So I should stop."

"You should stop doing what's *written*. Which means finding out everything that isn't." He closed the iron clasps and rested his palm flat on the cover, and for a moment he looked less like a disgraced archivist and more like a man laying his hand on a grave. "The deep stacks. The pre-flood collections. If there's a countersignature to find, it's down where they sent me to be forgotten—which means, conveniently, down where they forgot to watch me. Give me until tomorrow's Vespers."

"Vespers," I repeated. The hour of the entry. The hour the book had scheduled me.

"A fitting deadline." He tucked the book under his arm, and then he did something stranger still: he looked back at me from the alcove doorway, and dropped his voice though we were alone. "One thing more. Whatever they offer you—forgiveness, wages, a clean ledger, a name in good standing—they will offer it the moment they've verified you. That's how it works. First they confirm you exist. Then they make existing worth something. And the moment you accept payment, you're theirs, because acceptance is a signature, and they collect on signatures the way the sea collects on shores." His eyes held mine. "Whatever they offer, the price will be the only thing you actually value. Count on it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Refusal is also noted." He lifted the book slightly. "This page keeps accounts, Cyrus. It has kept yours for a hundred years. The question was never whether the ledger opens tonight." He turned into the shadow of the stairwell, and his voice came back to me dry as the dust he lived in.

"The question is who's holding the pen when it does."

I stood alone in the alcove with my empty boxes and my hammering heart, and I realized my fear had changed shape somewhere in the last hour without my permission. It had cooled. Hardened. Set like ink.

For nine years I had erased myself to stay safe, and the book had known my name all along, had *waited* for me, had priced me like cargo. Well. Let them verify. Let them spend. I had a knife, a strop, goose-fat oil, and four hundred and eleven successful erasures behind me—and now, at last, a target worth the blade.

Tomorrow's Vespers, Sef would bring me what the deep stacks held.

Tonight, I had a prophecy to answer—and this time, I intended to write first.
