## 3. What the Guild Buys

The summons arrived the way frost arrives—silently, everywhere at once, and with the certainty that something green was about to die.

Cyrus knew it before she saw it. She felt it in the way the scriptorium's rhythm shifted around her, the way Novice Pell stopped mid-sentence when she passed, the way the ink in her well seemed to sit a fraction deeper, as if bracing. Nine years in the temple taught you to read a room the way you read a ledger: not for what was written, but for what had been quietly crossed out.

The summons itself was absurdly small. A slip of grey paper, no larger than her palm, tucked beneath her inkstone while she was fetching sand. No seal. No signature. Just four words in a hand she didn't recognize:

*Upper Hall. Ninth bell.*

She read it twice, then folded it into eighths and put it in her mouth.

This was an old trick of hers, one of the first she'd invented after arriving at the temple with nothing but a name she wanted gone. Paper swallowed and softened by spit could later be pulped and pressed into blank stock; whatever was written on it dissolved into possibility. It wasn't perfect erasure. Nothing was. But it was fast, and right now speed mattered more than elegance.

*Ninth bell.* That was the detail that frightened her. The Upper Hall received petitioners at first bell, merchants at third, clergy at fifth. By ninth, the day's business was done and the hall belonged to the guildmasters alone. Nobody was summoned at ninth bell unless the guildmasters wanted the meeting to have happened without witnesses.

She spent the remaining hours doing exactly what she always did, because the alternative was letting them see her hurry. She balanced the ledger of small debts. She entered a shadow—sold twice, poor thing, no wonder it kept fraying at the edges—and noted the arbitration fee. She recorded the memory of a summer afternoon, pawned by a woman in Harrowgate who had needed coin more than she needed joy. Cyrus wrote the entries in her clean, deliberate hand, and she did not think about the sealed book in the restricted vault, the one older than the temple itself, with tonight's date inked on its page like a hook waiting in dark water.

She did not think about it, which is how she knew it was the only thing there was to think about.

At the eighth bell, Sef appeared at her elbow with an armful of misfiled folios he did not actually need to shelve anywhere near her desk.

"You've been summoned," he said. Not a question. The disgraced archivist had a nose for paper the way hounds had a nose for blood.

"Ninth bell."

Sef set the folios down, one by one, taking far too long about it. He was a thin man made thinner by twenty years of temple air, with ink stains up both forearms like old bruises and the particular stoop of someone who had learned early to make himself smaller than his opinions. "I archived for the Upper Hall, once," he said quietly. "Before my fall. Do you know what gets decided at ninth bell?"

"Tell me."

"Nothing good is ever decided at ninth bell." He straightened a folio that was already straight. "But here is what I can give you, scribe to scribe. They will be kind. That's the part everyone forgets to prepare for. The guildmasters are kind the way moneylenders are kind—precisely, deliberately, and with interest accruing."

"I don't owe them anything."

"Nobody owes them anything," Sef said, "until they decide otherwise. Go. Listen. Sign nothing. And Cyrus—" he never used her name; names were currency in this place and he hoarded his like a miser—"whatever they offer you, ask yourself why they need *you*. Guilds do not summon tools they could replace."

Then he gathered his folios and shuffled away, and Cyrus sat with her clean hand and her clean ledger and understood that her nine years of careful invisibility were over. You cannot unsee a date written a century before your birth. Apparently, neither could they.

* * *

The Upper Hall smelled of candlewax and cold stone, and it was built to make you feel exactly one thing: small. Vaulted ceiling lost in shadow. Twelve pillars, each carved with the guild's founding motto—*ALL THINGS MAY BE ACCOUNTED*—in letters worn soft by centuries of deference. At the far end, behind a table of black oak older than the temple, sat three guildmasters in the grey robes of their office.

Cyrus knew two of them by reputation. Master Corvane, who administered debts and had personally ruined four merchant houses she could name. Master Ilsette, who administered memories, and whose gentle voice was famous in the temple for the cruelty of its gentleness. The third was ancient, hooded, and unnamed, and Cyrus had been a scribe long enough to know that the unnamed ones were where the actual power lived.

"Scribe Amberdale," Corvane said, and her name in his mouth sounded wrong—not because he pronounced it poorly, but because he pronounced it *completely*. Every syllable accounted for. "Thank you for coming."

As if she'd been given a choice worth mentioning.

"You're wondering why you're here," Ilsette said, and smiled the smile of a woman about to explain arithmetic to a child. "Please. Sit. There's no need to stand on ceremony at this hour."

There was one chair. It had been placed in the center of the floor like an exhibit, facing them, its back to the empty hall. Cyrus walked to it and sat, and the distance she crossed felt longer than nine steps. It felt like nine years, played backward.

"We'll come directly to it," Corvane said. "You found the Vellum Codex."

Not a question either. These people traded in questions disguised as statements; she would remember that.

"I catalogued its location," Cyrus said. "Cataloguing isn't finding."

"No?" Ilsette tilted her head. "And the page you scraped? Was that cataloguing as well?"

The candles guttered, all of them, in perfect unison, though there was no draft. Cyrus kept her face still. Somewhere beneath her ribs, her heart was doing rapid, unlicensed accounting.

"The Codex predates this temple by sixty years," the hooded figure said. Its voice was dry as flaking gold leaf. "It is a prophecy ledger—the only one of its kind we have ever recovered. Prophecy, scribe, is simply a debt owed by the future to the present. And like all debts, it accrues. Like all debts—" a pause, deliberate, "—it comes due."

"You've known about it," Cyrus said. Her voice came out flat. "For years."

"One hundred and four years," said the hooded figure. "We have known since before your grandmother drew breath. We have known the date. We have known the hour." A gloved hand emerged from the robe and laid something on the oak table: a single sheet, covered in the same regrowing ink she had scraped from the Codex page two nights ago, the letters standing up off the paper like scar tissue. "What we did not know, until very recently, was the name."

"And now you do," Cyrus said.

"Now we do," Ilsette agreed warmly. "The Amberdale hand."

The words hung in the vaulted dark. Cyrus discovered that she had prepared, all these years, for exposure—for the moment someone looked at her and finally saw—but she had never prepared for being *read*. Being looked at was bearable. Being parsed was not. They weren't seeing her; they were auditing her.

"May I tell you what we admire most about you, Scribe Amberdale?" Corvane leaned forward. "It isn't your scholarship, though your scholarship is fine. It's your discretion. Nine years in our employ and not one entry altered, not one secret sold, not one whisper out of place. You erase yourself from every record you touch—we know; we let you; it was cheaper than paying for your silence—and still you balance every book perfectly. Do you understand what that makes you?"

"Invisible," Cyrus said.

"*Reliable*," Corvane said, and the correction landed like a stamp on wax. "The rarest quality in the world. Anyone can be brave or clever or loyal for a season. But reliable, year upon year, ledger upon ledger—that is worth more than all of it. That is what the guild buys, when it buys anything at all."

*When it buys anything at all.* Cyrus turned the phrase over and found the trap already inside it, waiting, patient. She thought of the memory-pawned woman in Harrowgate. She thought of the shadow sold twice. Everything the guild bought became an entry, and every entry became theirs.

"What task," she said.

Ilsette's smile deepened, pleased, and Cyrus hated how much she noticed herself noticing that pleasure, how the old hunger to be seen—to be *approved*—still lived in her somewhere despite everything. That was the seduction, she realized. Not the offer. The attention. Nine years starving in plain sight, and now three masters of the impossible were leaning toward her in candlelight, drinking in her every word, and some traitor part of her wanted to purr.

"Vaelmoor," Corvane said. "A city in the far north, past the ash country. Perhaps you know the stories."

"Hollow King. Dead king who doesn't fall down. Crown that won't come off." She'd copied tax records from Vaelmoor's factors; even their trade seals showed a crowned silhouette with no face.

"Stories," Ilsette agreed, "which are, like prophecies, merely debts told badly. The crown of Vaelmoor is real. What animates the king who wears it is real. And once each generation, the guild sends an assessor north to verify that the crown remains... stable. This generation, the task falls to you."

"Why me? You have assessors. You have archivists. You have—" she gestured at the hooded figure, "—whatever that is."

"Because the Codex names you," the hooded figure said simply. "The prophecy does not say *the Amberdale hand shall verify the crown*, scribe. It says the Amberdale hand shall be present when the binding is tested. We do not send anyone near an active prophecy except the person the prophecy has already claimed. It is a matter of..." the glove flexed, "...containment."

*Containment.* The word sat down inside her chest and got comfortable.

"And if I refuse?"

The three masters exchanged glances—or rather, the two named ones exchanged glances, and the hooded one remained perfectly still in the way that made the other glances look like fidgeting.

"Refusal is always permitted," Ilsette said gently. "The guild does not compel. We merely keep accounts. And your account, Scribe Amberdale—" she drew a slim ledger from her sleeve and opened it with one finger, and Cyrus caught a glimpse of her own name, her own handwriting, entries she had made privately in her own cell, transcribed in someone else's neat hand, "—is extensive. Unlicensed access to the restricted vault. Removal of a sealed document. Attempted destruction of guild property, in that the page you scraped was, I'm afraid, guild property. Nine years of small irregularities, all of them forgiven, none of them forgotten. Forgiveness is also a service we provide. For a fee."

The hall was very quiet. The candles had stopped guttering and now burned perfectly straight, as if even fire had chosen a side.

"So here are our terms," Corvane said. "Travel to Vaelmoor as the guild's assessor. Verify the crown. Return with your findings. In exchange: full forgiveness of your account. Every irregularity struck. Every debt dissolved. You will walk out of this hall cleaner than you have ever been—no record, no claim, no leverage held over you by anyone." He paused, and added, with what was almost tenderness, "You will finally be free, Scribe Amberdale. Truly invisible. Isn't that what you've wanted?"

And there it was. Laid out on black oak like a jewel: everything she had built her whole life around, offered back to her by the people who had been watching her build it. Erasure as reward. Invisibility as wages.

She should have felt terror. She mostly did. But underneath the terror, shamefully, persistently, was the other thing—the little warm animal part of her that heard *you are the one we need* and leaned toward the fire. They had found the exact shape of her want and priced it precisely. That was the insult. Not the threat. The precision.

"When do I leave?" she said.

"At dawn," said Corvane. "A escort will take you to the river road. Your credentials are being drawn up tonight." He rose, and the other two rose with him, and for a moment the twelve pillars and the vaulted dark and the three grey figures all seemed arranged around her chair like the parts of a scale, weighing her against something she couldn't see.

"One more thing," the hooded figure said, as Cyrus stood. "The Codex. You touched it. Scraped it. Do not do so again. Ink that remembers does not forgive, scribe, and unlike us—" the hood tilted, and beneath it, for half a second, she glimpsed something that was not a face so much as an absence wearing one, "—it does not negotiate."

* * *

She walked back through the sleeping temple with the summons-dissolved-to-nothing sitting sour on her tongue and Sef's advice ringing in her ears: *ask yourself why they need you.*

She had her answer now, and it was worse than any she'd imagined. They didn't need her skill or her discretion or her famous reliability. They needed her name. She was not a person being sent on a mission; she was a component being inserted into a mechanism that had been running for a hundred years, and the guildmasters had waited, patient as interest, for the component to grow up and walk into their hall on her own feet.

In the scriptorium, one candle still burned at Sef's desk among the misfiled folios. He looked up as she came in, took one look at her face, and began clearing space on his worktable without a word.

"They're sending me to Vaelmoor," she said.

Sef's hands went still. Something crossed his expression—fear, certainly, but underneath it, briefly, something that looked almost like recognition, like a man hearing his own name called in a foreign country.

"When do you leave?"

"Dawn."

"Then I'll help you pack," Sef said, and there was a strange finality in it, and stranger still, when she glanced back a moment later, he was pulling a blank folio and his sharpest pen toward him, as if dawn were a deadline *he* had to meet.

Outside, the last bell of night rang across the temple grounds, and in the restricted vault below, she knew, the ink was already growing back—patient, obedient, coming due.
