## 5. The Road Through Ashfall

The guild's travel writ was a single page, and even that felt like too much paper spent on me.

*Bearer to proceed to Vaelmoor by the eastern road. Purpose: verification of asset. Expenses authorized against future service.*

Future service. As if I had a surplus of futures lying around.

Sef read it over my shoulder in the courtyard before dawn, his breath fogging in the cold. "They didn't even give you a horse."

"They gave me a mule."

"They gave you *half* a mule. Look at it. That's a mule that's been written off already." He slung his pack over one shoulder—the pack he wasn't supposed to have, since officially Sef was not traveling with me at all. Officially, the disgraced archivist had requested leave to visit an ailing cousin in the hill country. The guild had stamped the request without reading it, because the guild did not read things that belonged to people it had already erased.

"Your cousin," I said.

"Dying slowly. Very far east. Practically Vaelmoor."

I should have told him to stay. I told myself I would tell him to stay. Instead I checked the mule's girth strap and said nothing, and we passed through the temple gate as the bell counted five, and the city of my nine invisible years let us go without noticing either of us had left.

That was the last easy morning for a long time.

* * *

The eastern road ran through country I had only known as entries in ledgers. Grain tithes from Harrowmere. Hearth-tithe from the villages past it—coal and oil and, every seventh year, a measure of something the ledgers called simply *fire*, entered without unit or source, as though fire were a thing that came in bushels.

I had copied those entries for years. I had never once asked where the fire grew.

By the third day I understood why no one asked.

Harrowmere looked fine from the ridge—roofs intact, fields stubbled and neat—but when we came down into it, the village was quiet in the wrong way. Not empty. Quiet like a held breath. Doors stood open because there was nothing inside worth shutting out the cold from. A woman was sweeping a step that needed no sweeping, just to keep her hands moving.

"When did your hearth go out?" Sef asked her, because I couldn't make my voice work.

"Went out?" She looked at him with mild, tired eyes. "It didn't go out. It's going. Slow. Like a lamp running low on oil, except there's no oil to buy. The fire-wardens came through in spring, took the year's measure, said the veins were thin this season." She shrugged. "Everyone's veins are thin this season. Everyone's been thin for six years now."

"What do you burn?" I asked.

She showed us. In the hearth of the common house, where the whole village used to gather, there was a small bed of coals giving off less heat than a cup of tea. People sat around it anyway, packed close, because it was still warmer than their own homes. An old man held his hands out to it the way you'd hold them to a dying bird.

"We ration," the woman said. "We sleep in shifts, huddled. We're not the worst off. Corben's Ford went dark entirely last winter. Eleven dead, they say, though the guild's ledger says four, so who knows." She said it without bitterness, which was somehow worse. Bitterness requires believing someone could have done otherwise.

I stood in that cold common house and thought about the ledger entry I'd balanced a hundred times. *Fire, received: 40 measures.* Forty measures of warmth, out of a village that slept in shifts around a coal-bed, and I had carried the number to the next column without once wondering what forty measures meant in children's chilblains.

"You all right?" Sef murmured as we walked out.

"No," I said.

"Good," he said quietly. "Means you're paying attention."

* * *

The villages got worse the further east we went, which I hadn't thought possible, because I hadn't understood the direction of the arithmetic. I had assumed the tithe flowed toward the capital and got spread thin across its comforts. It didn't. The tithe flowed *east*. Toward Vaelmoor. Every measure of fire these dying villages surrendered traveled the same road we did, and none of it stopped along the way.

At a crossroads shrine, someone had scratched new graffiti over the old gods' names: *THE FIRE GOES TO THE KING AND THE KING GIVES NOTHING BACK.* Someone else had tried to scrub it off. They'd gotten as far as *THE FIRE GOES* before giving up.

In Duncarrow, the miller sold us bread at triple price and apologized for it, showing us his hands, blue at the nails. "Cold makes everything dear," he said. "Flour won't rise proper in a cold house. You get used to eating bricks." He laughed. His wife didn't.

In Duncarrow's churchyard there were fresh graves with wooden markers, and I made the mistake of counting them, and then made the worse mistake of estimating what the count would be by spring, and then I went and sat behind the church until Sef found me.

"You knew," I said. "Didn't you? Before we left. You knew the provinces were going cold."

"I knew the numbers," Sef said. He sat down beside me against the cold stone wall. "Numbers aren't the same as knowing. I've spent twenty years in archives watching people confuse the two. Including me." He pulled his coat tighter. "The guild calls it attrition. Natural decline of the fire-veins. It's in every report. Natural. Like weather."

"And it isn't."

"It isn't anything natural. Natural things fluctuate. This is a curve, smooth as a drawn line, falling exactly fast enough to never quite cause a riot." He looked at me. "You've read more guild contracts than anyone alive, Cyrus. What kind of clause produces a curve like that?"

I didn't answer, because I was already answering it in my head, in ledger-hand, in the neat columns where I had spent nine years making impossible trades look balanced. A draw that never varies. A harvest that never fails. Something being taken at a fixed rate from a source that cannot refuse.

Something bound.

* * *

We met the hearth-keeper on the sixth day, in a village so small it didn't appear in the guild's ledgers at all, which meant that as far as the guild was concerned, whatever happened to it happened to no one.

Her name was Maren. She was the last keeper of the last true hearth for thirty miles—a woman of perhaps seventy who had tended the village flame since she was twelve, back when the flame was worth tending. Now she lay in the hearth-house itself, wrapped in every blanket the village owned, because the sacred fire she'd kept alive for fifty-eight years had shrunk to a fist-size glow that gave off light and almost no heat, and she had chosen to spend her final days as close to it as she could get.

The villagers had carried her in and then, mostly, gone home to their own cold rooms. There wasn't fuel for vigils anymore. Vigils were a luxury of warm places.

Sef waited outside. I don't know if he understood that she wanted to talk to me specifically—I didn't understand it myself yet—or if he just knew when a room needed one person fewer.

Maren looked at me for a long time before she spoke. Her eyes had the particular clarity of the nearly finished, who have run out of everything except attention.

"Scribe," she said. My guild badge was hidden under my coat, but she'd seen it anyway, or seen something. "You've come from the temple."

"I'm traveling east. To Vaelmoor."

"Vaelmoor." She said the name the way you'd say the name of a rich relative who never writes. "Warm city, Vaelmoor. Always warm. Even in the hard winters, even when we were scraping snow into buckets to melt for water, Vaelmoor's fountains never froze. Did you know that?"

"I'd heard."

"Heard." She smiled with cracked lips. "Yes. Everyone's heard. Nobody asks." She shifted under the blankets, wincing. "Come closer, girl. I haven't the wind for shouting and I'm done with whispering to fires."

I knelt beside her pallet. Up close she smelled of ash and old wool and, faintly, underneath it, of something hot and mineral, like a forge banked for the night.

"Fifty-eight years I kept the flame," she said. "My mother kept it before me, and hers before her, back to the founding. Do you know what the flame actually is, scribe? Not the symbol. The actual fire in the actual hearth."

"Dragon-fire," I said. "Old lore. It's supposed to be extinct."

"Extinct." She tasted the word. "That's what the wardens say when they come for the tithe. *The dragons are extinct, grandmother, this is just old fire, legacy fire, burning down fair and slow.* Legacy fire." Her laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing took a long time to stop. When it did, her hand shot out and gripped my wrist with shocking strength. "Child, I have fed that fire since I was a girl. I know its moods like a husband's. And a fire that is merely burning down does not *grieve*."

The word landed in the cold room like a dropped glass.

"Grieve," I repeated.

"Dragons grieve. Fires that are merely fire don't. Ours has been grieving for sixty years—burning lower every year, yes, but also sadder, if you know how to listen. Dimming the way a person dims when everyone they love is gone." Her grip tightened. "And every year the wardens take their measure, and every year the measure gets bigger while the flame gets smaller, and I have lain here doing the sums that you, scribe, with all your letters, have apparently never done."

I didn't pretend otherwise. "What sums?"

"If the fire is dying naturally, why does the taking accelerate? If the veins are thin, why do the wagons roll east heavier every autumn?" She released my wrist, exhausted by the effort. "Someone is drinking deeper from a well they've told everyone is drying up. And they've hired clever people with neat handwriting to keep the books saying otherwise."

The fire in the great hearth flickered. I swear—it flickered, at that moment, like something listening.

"I have something for you," Maren said. "Wasn't sure I would. Prayed I'd live long enough to give it away, because keeping it means keeping it safe, and safe has gotten very expensive." She fumbled beneath her blankets and brought out a small clay jar, sealed with wax, wrapped in cloth gone soft with age. "A coal. From the heart of the flame, struck the night I was named keeper. My mother said: *this goes to whoever asks the right question.* Fifty-eight years, nobody asked the right question. Wardens ask how much. Villagers ask how long. You—" she pushed the jar into my hands— "you asked *why*."

The jar was warm. Not clay-warm, not body-warm. Warm like a living thing, with a pulse to it, faint and steady, that I felt through my gloves and up my arm and into a place under my ribs I hadn't known was listening.

"What question will it answer?" I whispered, feeling absurd and certain at once.

"None," Maren said. "It's not a book, child. It's a compass. It doesn't point north." Her eyes drifted toward the guttering hearth, and something moved in her face—not fear, not sorrow. Recognition. "It points *home*. To the fire everything else came from. To the last of the ones who grieve." She closed her eyes. "Take it east. Take it to Vaelmoor. And when you find what's at the end of the pointing—"

She never finished. Her breathing changed, slowed, and settled into the rhythm of someone conserving the last of everything. I sat with her until Sef touched my shoulder, and by the time we stepped out into the grey afternoon, the hearth-house behind us was quieter than any building I had ever left.

The villagers buried her the next morning. There wasn't wood enough for a pyre, so we burned the blankets she'd been wrapped in, and the smoke rose straight up in the windless cold, and I stood there holding a jar with a heartbeat in it and understood that I was carrying something the guild would burn cities to reclaim.

* * *

That night, camped in a ruined waystation with the undying coal between us for warmth—we didn't need a bigger fire; the little jar threw heat like a hearth—Sef finally asked the question I'd been dreading.

"How much fire does the guild collect in a year?"

I knew without checking. Nine years of balancing those columns leaves marks. "Last full accounting: three hundred and forty measures. Declining provinces, rising quota. The numbers shouldn't coexist, but they do, because the quotas are set by contract, not by yield."

"And Vaelmoor consumes?"

"A fraction. The city's hearths, the palace, the public baths, the everwarm fountain. Call it eighty measures, generously."

"So two hundred and sixty measures a year vanish." Sef stared into the coal's steady glow. "Into what?"

"That's the wrong question," I heard myself say. "Ledgers don't record where things go. They record where things are *owed*." I turned the jar in my hands, watching the coal pulse. "There's an entry I've seen a hundred times and always treated as boilerplate. Every fire-contract, every tithe agreement, all of them carry the same rider. *All measures rendered unto the Crown's keeping, per the Old Accord, until the debt is discharged.*"

"The Crown's keeping," Sef repeated slowly. "Not the guild's vaults."

"The Crown's keeping." I looked east, where the road ran down out of the hills toward a city that had never once frozen. "Sef, what exactly is the hollow king *keeping*?"

The coal pulsed once, twice, and then—there was no other word for it—it *leaned*. Faintly, unmistakably, the warmth in the jar shifted eastward, straining toward Vaelmoor like a dog at the end of a rope.

Behind us, the west went on growing colder. Ahead of us, something at the end of that pull was waiting to be found—and I was beginning to suspect it had been waiting, and grieving, and *counting*, far longer than any of us had been alive.
