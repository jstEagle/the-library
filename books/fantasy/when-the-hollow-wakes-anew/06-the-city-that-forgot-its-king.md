## 6. The City That Forgot Its King

Vaelmoor announced itself three days before we reached it, the way a fever announces itself before the shaking starts.

First came the roads. In Ashfall and the villages behind us, the highways had been ruts stitched together by mud and stubbornness, but here they were cut stone, swept clean, each mile marked with a carved pillar bearing the crown's sigil. Then came the traffic—carts heavy with grain and wool, merchants with fat ledgers, all of it flowing toward the city with the smooth, uncomplaining motion of water finding its level.

Then came the light.

I had spent nine years indoors, but I knew what lamplight was supposed to look like: yellow, wavering, honest about its own effort. The glow that rose over Vaelmoor's walls at dusk was none of those things. It was white. It did not flicker. It sat over the city like a held breath, steady as moonlight in a season when there should have been no moon at all.

"Dragon-fire," Sef said quietly beside me on the cart bench. He had said little since Ashfall, since the hearth-keeper Marta pressed the coal into my palm with her blackened fingers and made me promise to find out where it came from. He'd been quieter still since I caught him copying pages from the prophecy book by candlelight, though he didn't know I'd seen. "Or something wearing its clothes."

"You've been here before?"

"Once. As a boy. My master sent me to copy tax rolls." He paused. "I remember the ledgers. I don't remember anything else."

"That's not strange?"

He looked at me then, and his face in the white glow was older than his years. "Cyrus. Everything about this city is going to be strange. The question is whether it's strange *because* of something, or strange because someone wants it to be."

The gates took our toll—a copper apiece—and asked no names. I found that almost funny. Nine years I had worked to erase myself from every ledger in the temple's reach, and here was a city that couldn't be bothered to write anyone down.

*Everyone is accounted for already,* said the small cold voice in the back of my head, the one that balanced impossible trades. *Why would you record what you already own?*

* * *

Vaelmoor was beautiful the way a corpse can sometimes be beautiful—everything arranged just so, nothing moving except when moved.

The streets were wide and clean. The buildings were tall, their facades freshly painted in colors that seemed chosen by committee: muted blues, muted greens, nothing that shouted. Shop windows displayed goods behind glass so clear it looked like the goods were floating. And everywhere, on every corner, on every third lamp post, stood the braziers.

They were iron, waist-high, unadorned, and each one burned with a fist-sized flame that gave off no smoke, cast no shadows worth mentioning, and never—not once, in all the hours we walked the streets—wavered. People gathered at them to warm their hands the way you'd drink from a public fountain: automatically, without pleasure, without looking up.

"It's warm," I said, standing at one brazier longer than I meant to. "It's the middle of winter and it's warm everywhere."

"Yes," Sef said.

"The villages are freezing. Marta's whole town is down to one hearth. They're burning furniture. And this city has enough fire to heat the cobblestones."

"I noticed that too." He rubbed his hands over the flame without seeming to feel it. "I also noticed nobody here seems glad about it."

I had noticed. That was the thing crawling up the back of my neck like a cold finger. Warmth without gratitude. Plenty without appetite. I watched people buy bread from a baker who handed it over with the expression of a man counting someone else's money. I watched children play a game that involved no laughter—I checked twice; there wasn't any. They ran and shouted and tagged each other with the mechanical diligence of apprentices doing penance.

"Ask someone," Sef murmured.

So I did. A woman at a fruit stall, arranging apples into pyramids so precise they looked load-bearing.

"Lovely city," I said. "Is it always this bright?"

She looked up. Her eyes were brown and ordinary and for one moment something moved behind them—something tired, maybe, or frightened—before it settled again like sediment in water.

"The fire doesn't go out," she said. "It hasn't gone out in a hundred years."

"That must be a comfort."

"It's what we pay for." She said it flatly, the way you'd recite an address. "Quarter-day tithes. Hearth-levy. The king's assessment." She resumed stacking apples. "You'll want the palace district if you're here for court."

"We're scribes," Sef said smoothly, producing the guild credentials I still hadn't gotten used to carrying. "Records work. We'd like to see the archive."

"Archive's under the palace. Ask at the third gate." She placed the final apple. "Mind you don't touch the ledgers without gloves. The ink stains."

We walked on. At the end of the street I glanced back, and the woman was staring after us with that same submerged look, her hands stopped mid-motion, as if she'd forgotten what apples were for. Then a bell rang somewhere—the quarter-hour, though it sounded wrong, too deep, more felt than heard—and she blinked and went back to her pyramids.

"What was that bell?" I asked.

Sef had gone pale. "There was no bell," he said. "Listen."

I listened. The city hummed—braziers, cart wheels, ten thousand quiet lives—but underneath it, so faint I might have invented it, was a sound like a page turning. Very slowly. Somewhere below us.

* * *

We found lodging at an inn called the Crowned Hearth, which told you everything about Vaelmoor's relationship with its monarchy. The common room was full, the food was good, the beds were clean, and the conversation was nonexistent.

Not subdued. Nonexistent.

In Ashfall, inn talk was currency—grievances, jokes, gossip traded across tables like contraband. Here, people ate, paid, and left, or ate and stared into the middle distance with the patience of livestock. I tried twice to draw out the innkeeper. Both times he answered correctly and completely and gave away absolutely nothing, like a man reading answers off the inside of his own eyelids.

"Your king," I said on the second try. "What's he like?"

"He provides," the innkeeper said.

"Provides what?"

"Everything we pay for."

"But what's he *like*? Tall? Old? Kind?"

The innkeeper's brow furrowed—an actual furrow, an actual effort, like a man trying to recall a dream from childhood. "He presides," he said at last, with the satisfaction of a student giving the only answer the lesson ever covered. "Every quarter-day. Everyone sees him."

"And his face? Describe his face."

Silence stretched long enough to curdle. Around us, three separate conversations—insofar as they could be called conversations—had also gone silent. Not listening. Just... stopped, the way clocks stop.

"I—" The innkeeper's mouth worked. Sweat appeared at his hairline despite the warmth. "The face is the king's face," he said finally, with desperate, shining certainty. "It has always been the king's face."

"Yes," said everyone at the nearest table, softly, in unison.

I thanked him and got out of there before my skin finished crawling.

Upstairs, in our narrow room, I lit a candle—my candle, temple-wax, guttering and human—and sat on the bed while Sef paced.

"They can't picture him," I said. "An entire city, and not one of them can describe their own king."

"People forget faces."

"Not like this. You forget a face, you remember a beard, a scar, a voice. They don't even have a *voice*. They have 'he presides.' They have tax assessments memorized to the copper—did you notice that? The fruit-seller quoted the levy schedule without thinking. Ask them what their mother looks like, I'd wager half of them hesitate. Ask them the hearth-levy and it comes out like a heartbeat."

Sef stopped pacing. "That's not forgetting," he said slowly. "That's displacement. Something filled the space where the memory should be."

"Something," I agreed. "And it's shaped exactly like an invoice."

We sat with that. Outside the window, the white light lay over the rooftops, shadowless, patient.

"There's another thing," Sef said. "The bells. Or whatever that sound was. Did you feel it pull?"

I had. Twice now—once in the market, once as we climbed the stairs—something had tugged at the edge of my awareness, gentle as a hook through cloth. And both times, the coal in my pocket had answered.

I took it out now. Marta's coal sat in my palm, small and black and utterly unremarkable, except that in a city flooded with dragon-fire it burned brighter than it had in Ashfall, and as I watched, a hairline crack of orange opened along its surface and closed again, rhythmic as breathing.

Rhythmic, I realized, matching the slow turn of that enormous invisible page beneath the city.

"It knows where it came from," I whispered.

"Cyrus." Sef crouched in front of me, voice low. "Tomorrow we do the job. We verify the crown, we file the report, we get our debts forgiven, and we leave. Whatever this place is, we don't have to solve it."

"Marta's village freezes every winter a little worse. This city burns enough fire to thaw it ten times over. Somebody chose that arithmetic, Sef. Somebody wrote it down." I closed my hand around the coal, and its warmth bit pleasantly into my palm. "I've spent nine years writing down other people's impossible trades. Shadows sold twice. Memories pawned. I thought I understood what the guild does." I shook my head. "I didn't understand anything. This is the biggest ledger I've ever stood inside, and we're standing on the debt column."

* * *

Court convened at dawn, and everyone in Vaelmoor attended.

That was the phrase the herald used—*attended*—as though it were weather rather than obligation. The great plaza before the palace filled with thousands of citizens who stood in neat rows in the white light, faces tilted toward the balcony, hands folded, patient as furniture. Sef and I stood among our credentials like two moles at a garden party, unnoticed precisely because we fit the pattern: present, silent, accounted for.

"He presides every quarter-day," I murmured. "This isn't even a special occasion."

"No," Sef said. "Which means whatever happens next happens all the time."

The doors at the top of the palace steps opened.

I want to describe the king honestly, and I find I can't—not because my memory failed, but because it worked too well and kept contradicting itself. He wore a crown of dark metal, old-fashioned, its points uneven as broken teeth. His robes were rich and correct. His posture was perfect. And his face—

His face was the problem. Every time my eyes traveled up from the collar, they arrived somewhere different: a jaw, then no jaw; grey eyes, then eyes the color of wet paper; a man of sixty, a man of thirty, a man whose age was simply a number someone else had written down. It wasn't glamour. I'd seen glamours in the guild's trade records, and glamours *push*—they insist, they flatter, they make your eyes lie to you gladly. This was the opposite. This was absence wearing the shape of a man, and my mind scrambling to fill the vacancy like a scribe filling a blank line, inventing entry after entry and striking each one immediately.

Beside me, Sef made a small wounded sound.

The crowd did none of that. The crowd looked up at the hollow shape of their king with expressions of mild, contented attention, the way you'd watch a fountain. And when he raised one hand—slowly, too slowly, the joints moving like a puppet discovering its strings—they bowed, all of them, in a single wave that rolled from the palace steps back past us and beyond, silent, synchronized, obedient as wheat.

The crowned figure spoke. I heard the words clearly and I will set them down exactly, because they were the most frightening part:

*"The accounts are kept. The fire is provided. Be content."*

Nine words. No inflection. A voice like dry paper being folded. And the crowd sighed—one vast, satisfied exhale, ten thousand people contented on command—and straightened, and turned, and dispersed to their shops and stalls and homes, their quarter-hour of governance complete.

But I stayed. I planted my feet and made myself look, truly look, while the figure stood alone on the balcony above the emptying plaza.

And I saw it blink.

Or rather, I saw it *not* blink, through the whole length of my watching—four minutes by my count, four minutes of dust-free, moistureless, utterly unwavering stare directed at nothing, at everything, at the middle distance where the future is supposed to be. Courtiers flanked it, human courtiers, who shuffled and coughed and glanced around like men standing next to a well they'd been told not to look down. But the king did not shift his weight. His chest did not rise.

The accounts are kept. The fire is provided.

A hundred years, the fruit-seller said. A hundred years of never going out.

I thought of Marta's coal breathing orange in my pocket. I thought of the sound beneath the city, the slow turning of some vast page. I thought of the prophecy book with tonight's date inked in a dead century's hand, and of the guildmasters' smooth assurance that verification was a simple errand, a formality, a task beneath mention.

Nobody sends scribes to *verify* something everyone can see.

You send scribes to audit.

As the balcony doors closed behind the crowned figure, the coal against my ribs flared hot enough to sting through my coat, and far below the plaza, beneath the flagstones, beneath the palace foundations, beneath everything, I felt—or heard, or remembered—that page stop turning.

And then, very distinctly, something down there in the dark began to read me back.

---

They say the royal archive opens to credentialed scribes at midmorning. I intend to be first in line. Because whatever the guild sent me to Vaelmoor to check, I am suddenly, urgently certain it wasn't the crown.

It was what the crown is *eating*—and how much of it is left.
