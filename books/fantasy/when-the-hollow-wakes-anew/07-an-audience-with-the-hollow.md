## 7. An Audience with the Hollow

The royal archive of Vaelmoor did not want visitors.

Cyrus understood this the way she understood most things—through paperwork. Three days in the city had taught her that everything here was smooth on the surface and locked beneath it. The streets were clean. The bakeries smelled of honey bread. The guards at the palace gates smiled with the easy warmth of men who had never once been afraid, and that was the most frightening thing about them.

Every request she filed was answered within hours, and every answer was no.

*Access to the royal archive is restricted to crown appointees.*

*Petition for scholarly entry: denied.*

*Petition for scholarly entry (revised): denied.*

*Petition for scholarly entry (revised again, with groveling): under review.*

She sat in the reading room of a public records house—a smaller, duller cousin of the archive—and watched the clerk across from her stamp DENIED onto her fourth petition with the contentment of a man buttering bread.

"You could try honesty," he said, without looking up.

"I'm a scribe," Cyrus said. "Honesty is above my pay grade."

"Then try flattery."

"I'm a scribe," she said again. "Flattery is below it."

That earned her a flicker of a smile, which she noted and filed away. People in Vaelmoor smiled like they were remembering how. It never quite reached anything.

She had spent three days walking the city, ledger in hand, doing what she actually did best: reading what a place concealed by reading what it recorded. Vaelmoor's tax rolls were immaculate. Its birth and death registers were flawless. Its grain stores were audited to the half-measure. And nowhere—in ten thousand pages of civic memory—was there a single record of the king's coronation.

Not the date. Not the officiant. Not the oath.

There were records of kings before him, four reigns' worth, each ending neatly. Then a gap of one year where the archive simply said *transition*, in the same hand, the same ink, as though a single clerk had written history's most important sentence and then gone home to supper.

And every record after that—the taxes, the harvests, the laws—was signed with the same seal. The crowned circle. Pressed so many times the wax had worn grooves into the stamp itself.

A kingdom that remembered its money but not its monarch.

Cyrus closed the tax roll and felt Sef's warning settle over her like a wet coat. *They burn assets when ledgers turn against them.* She was in a city whose entire history had been edited by someone very good at editing, and she had come here carrying a prophecy that named her hand.

* * *

The break came, as breaks do, through a flaw in the system rather than a door in the wall.

On the fourth morning, a runner delivered her fifth denial, but this one was different. The refusal clause cited an old statute—*Archive Access, Crown Charter 12, Subsection 9*—and Cyrus, being who she was, went and found the charter.

It took her half a day. It was worth it.

Subsection 9 granted archive access to *any accredited scribe bearing letters of certification from a recognized guild or temple order, for the purpose of debt audit, provided such audit be conducted in the presence of an appointed witness and concluded within a single day.*

The statute was two hundred years old. It predated the current restrictions. It had simply been forgotten—which, in a city built on forgetting, meant it still existed, pristine and patient, waiting for someone to remember it.

Cyrus wrote out her claim in formal charter-law hand, cited the statute verbatim, attached her temple credentials—nine years of erasing herself, and finally her name was useful—and submitted it before noon.

By evening she stood before the archive doors with an appointed witness, a dry woman named Mistress Corvel who held the keys and her own counsel in equal measure.

"One day," Corvel said. "Sunrise to sunset bell. You touch nothing older than the Charter. You copy nothing you cannot justify to me. And you are never alone in the deep shelves." She unlocked the first of six locks. "The king's private collection is not included."

"Naturally," said Cyrus, who had already noticed that the statute made no such exclusion, and that Corvel had just confirmed which shelf mattered most.

* * *

The archive swallowed light.

Lamps hung at long intervals down aisles of shelving that rose past the reach of any ladder Cyrus could see, and between the lamps the dark was complete, respectful, like the dark inside a held breath. Their footsteps came back to them wrong—too soft, too fast—as though the room ate echoes along with dust.

Corvel led her through the civic sections first, hovering, correcting, watching. Cyrus performed diligence beautifully. She pulled charters, requested inventories, filled three pages with legitimate notes. She let Corvel see her working, let the witness grow bored, let boredom do what boredom always does.

By the time the midday bell rang, Corvel had retreated to the entrance table with her tea, and Cyrus was standing in front of a shelf labeled only:

**REIGN — CURRENT**

There was no king's name on it. Just the word, and beneath it, in smaller type: *by appointment of the crown.*

Every other reign had a name. *REIGN OF ALDERIC III.* *REIGN OF THE QUEEN MARGARET.* This one had a job title.

Cyrus glanced back. Corvel was reading something, tea cooling, attention elsewhere.

The statute said *in the presence of an appointed witness.* It did not say the witness had to watch.

Cyrus pulled the first ledger from the shelf.

It opened to a page of ordinary accounting—grain tithes, garrison wages—and then, running down the outer margin of every page, in ink that looked fresher than the text beside it, a second column of figures she didn't recognize. Not currency. Not measures. The numbers were written in an old notation she'd seen only once before, in the sealed prophecy book in the temple scriptorium: the founders' cipher, the counting-hand of the binding arts.

She copied three lines into her notebook, heart tapping against her ribs. Whatever the crown was, someone was keeping books on it. Someone was *measuring* it.

The next volume down was thicker, older, its spine cracked like river clay. There was no label. When she opened it, the first page read:

**ACCOUNT OF THE OATH. FORGIVE US. WE DID NOT KNOW WHAT WE WERE WRITING.**

Below that, the page had been scraped clean. Every line of it. Scraped with the desperate, gouging strokes of someone trying to erase something too large for the page.

Cyrus knew those strokes intimately. She had made them herself, nine years' worth.

But she also knew what lived underneath scraping. Ink remembers. She pressed her thumb flat against the blank space, leaned close, and tilted the page toward the lamp until the light raked across it at a slant.

Grooves. Words pressed deep enough that even a scraped page couldn't forget them entirely. Fragmentary, ghosted, but legible in pieces:

*...the crown shall drink...*

*...what is given freely cannot be...*

*...and the king shall remain, that the fire may...*

The last visible fragment made her cold all the way through:

*...he asked us to stop. we could not. the contract was...*

Someone had begged. In this book. In this room. And someone else had kept the account anyway, because a contract is a contract, and the guild has always believed that.

"Clerk Amberdale."

Corvel's voice, directly behind her. Cyrus hadn't heard her cross the room. Of course she hadn't. Nothing in this city made a sound it didn't want to make.

"You're at the restricted shelf," Corvel said.

"The statute permits—"

"The statute permits audit of *crown accounts.*" Corvel's face gave away nothing, which in Vaelmoor was itself a kind of eloquence. "You have found them. Congratulations. You will note their condition and move along."

Cyrus looked at the old woman for a long moment. At the eyes that had watched this shelf for years, maybe decades, and had never once opened the book with the apology on its first page. Or perhaps had opened it once, long ago, and chosen the tea and the boredom ever since.

"What is his name?" Cyrus asked quietly. "The king. His actual name."

Corvel's teacup, halfway to her lips, stopped.

"That information is not in the audit scope," she said.

"That wasn't my question."

For one heartbeat—one—something moved behind Corvel's face. Something old and tired and buried under two hundred years of immaculate bookkeeping. Then it was gone, smoothed over like a scraped page.

"Your day concludes at sunset bell, Clerk Amberdale. I would spend less of it asking questions whose answers have weight." She turned. "This way. There is a final section you are required to initial before departure."

* * *

The final section was not a section. It was a corridor, narrow and descending, lined with ledgers so old their leather had gone the color of ash. Corvel carried the lamp. Neither of them spoke. At the bottom stood a door of black wood bound in iron, and on the door, a plate:

**BY APPOINTMENT OF THE CROWN ONLY.**

Corvel produced a letter—sealed, official, bearing the crowned circle—and handed it to Cyrus with the expression of a woman handing someone a live coal.

"His Majesty requires an audit of his personal accounts," she said. "He requires it annually. No scribe has ever passed the review." Her voice dropped, just slightly, just enough. "He will ask you questions. Answer them precisely. Do not offer comfort. He does not know what to do with it."

She knocked once, and the door opened inward on darkness, and Cyrus understood that she had not gained access today through cleverness or statute.

She had been *invited*. By the thing at the top of the city. The thing the prophecy called hollow.

Every instinct said run. Nine years of training said: an asset walks forward when summoned; that is what assets are for.

Cyrus walked in.

* * *

The room beyond was small and round and utterly bare except for a desk, a chair, a single candle, and a wall of ledgers. No throne. No banners. No portraits of ancestors glaring down. A counting room. The king of Vaelmoor worked in a counting room.

He stood with his back to her, hands folded behind him, and the first thing Cyrus noticed was the stillness. People shift. Weight moves from foot to foot; fingers twitch; breath happens. This man did none of it. He stood the way furniture stands.

"You are the scribe from the temple," he said.

His voice was the worst part. It was not cold or cruel. It sounded like dry paper being unfolded—creak and whisper and the faint rasp of something that had been folded in the dark for a very long time.

"I am," Cyrus said. "Cyrus Amberdale."

"Amberdale." He said the name strangely. Carefully. Like a man testing ice. "You will audit my accounts."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Turn around," he said, "so that I may look at you."

She turned to face him, and he turned to face her, and Cyrus's fear—which had been a low hum for days—rose sharply into her throat. Because his face was wrong in a way that took a moment to parse. It was a handsome face, or had been. But it did not *move* right. The expressions arrived a half-second late, like words translated twice. And his eyes—

His eyes never blinked. She watched him for a full minute, waiting, and they never blinked. They shone faintly in the candlelight with a luster that no living eye has, the luster of glazed ceramic, and behind that luster there was nothing looking out.

Except that wasn't true either. Because when he spoke again, something did look out. Something far back, deep down, like a face pressed against the far side of frosted glass.

"The accounts are on the desk," he said. "You will find them in order. They are always in order. That is—" a pause, a paper-creak, "—that is all I am permitted to be."

Cyrus looked at the ledgers. Perfect columns. Perfect sums. The same crowned-circle seal on every page.

"Your Majesty," she said carefully, "I'm obliged to tell you that your personal accounts show no irregularities."

"I know," he said. "I write them myself. I write them perfectly, because if I write them imperfectly, they will send another auditor, and then another, forever, and each one takes a little more of..." The sentence wandered off and did not return. He tried again. "Each visit costs me something. I don't recall what. I only know that I have less afterward."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved.

"Why did you summon me?" Cyrus asked. "No scribe passes the review. Corvel told me. So why am I here?"

For a long moment the king was silent, and in that silence Cyrus heard it—the faintest sound, rhythmic and weary, coming from somewhere inside him. Like wind moving through a dry reed bed. She realized, with horror that arrived slowly and then all at once, that it was his breathing. Or whatever the crown allowed to stand in for breathing.

"Because you are an Amberdale," he said.

The name dropped into the room and rang there.

"My grandmother's grandmother wrote in that cipher," he continued, in his voice like unfolding paper. "The one in the margins of my accounts. I have read those margins for—I don't know how long. Time doesn't pool here; it drains. The margins are the only things in this room that change. And lately—" the creak again, worse, "—lately the margins have been saying your name."

Cyrus's mouth went dry. "What do they say?"

"They say the account comes due." He stepped closer, and up close she could see that the glaze over his eyes was thinnest at the center, thin as frost on a window, and behind it something moved—not hunger, nothing so simple. *Longing.* The specific, unbearable longing of someone watching a world they can no longer touch. "They say an Amberdale hand is coming to read the true ledger. And I thought—" the voice frayed, and for one moment the paper-rasp broke and something almost human came through, raw and cracked and desperate, "—I thought, *please.* Please let it be someone who can read. Please let it be someone who can ask the question."

"Which question?" Cyrus whispered.

The king raised his hands—slowly, with the terrible patience of a puppet—and touched his own temples, where the crown sat fused to flesh and bone, metal grown into skin like roots into soil.

"What was I," he said, "before this?"

The question hung in the candlelight. Not *who am I.* Not *free me,* not *avenge me.* Just: *what was I before?* Asked the way a man asks about a home he can barely remember. And Cyrus felt her fear do something she did not expect—it bent. Bent into pity, sharp and painful, pity piercing fear like a nail through wax.

Because she knew this question. She had spent nine years asking it of herself in reverse—erasing her name from every ledger, scraping her own page, choosing invisibility because visibility had teeth. She had made herself hollow on purpose, a little at a time, and she had always believed she could stop whenever she wanted.

This man had not been able to stop.

"I don't know yet," Cyrus said honestly. "But I know where the answer is written."

"Then read it." He stepped back, and the stillness closed over him again, furniture-smooth, but his eyes stayed fixed on her with that pressure from behind the glass. "Read everything. The margins. The scraped pages. The gaps in the city's memory. Read what they took and what they keep taking." The paper-voice dropped lower. "And when you learn what I was—come back and tell me. Even if it's nothing. Even if I was no one. Tell me anyway. A prisoner should be allowed to know the size of his cell."

Behind her, the door opened. Sunset bell. Corvel's silhouette, patient as arithmetic.

Cyrus gathered her notes with hands that were not quite steady, and at the doorway she paused.

"Your Majesty. The margins say the account comes due. Do you know what that means?"

The king looked at her, and for the first time the glaze seemed to thin, and what looked out was so tired, so old, so achingly human, that she had to grip the doorframe.

"It means," he said, in a whisper like the last page of a burned book, "that soon either I will be finished—or the finishing will be. And I do not know which one I am praying for."

The door closed between them.

In the corridor, climbing back toward the lamplit shelves and the waiting world, Cyrus opened her notebook to the copied cipher lines and found, beneath her own transcription, in ink she had not written, in a hand she almost recognized from a prophecy book nine hundred miles away:

**THE AMBERDALE HAND HAS BEGUN TO READ.**

And below that, freshly drying, three words that turned her blood to well water:

**TELL NO ONE. TELL SEF.**
