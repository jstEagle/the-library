## 8. Sef's Inheritance

Sef had been carrying the pages for eleven days.

They rode against his ribs now, wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, and every mile from the temple they grew heavier—not in weight, which stayed the same, but in the way a debt grows heavier the longer you pretend it isn't yours. He had copied them by candlelight in the scriptorium's dead hour, when even the overseers slept, and he had told himself it was scholarship. Preservation. The instinct of any archivist who has watched one too many books go into the furnace.

That was a lie. He'd known it was a lie while his pen was still wet.

Cyrus slept across the inn room's narrow bed, boots on, the way she always did—ready to leave at the first wrong sound. The undying coal sat in its iron lantern on the table between them, throwing light the color of old honey. Outside, Vaelmoor murmured its prosperous, joyless murmur. Somewhere up the hill, behind walls Sef could feel through the floorboards like a headache, the thing wearing a crown sat on its throne and did not blink.

He should have told her on the road. He'd rehearsed it a hundred times against the rhythm of his own footsteps. *Cyrus, my family wrote half of that spell. Cyrus, my grandfather paid the guild every quarter for forty years so they would never look at our name. Cyrus, I knew what you were before you did.*

Instead he had talked about weather. About the price of oats. About whether the ashfall villages burned their dead or buried them, as if the answer mattered more than the truth sitting folded against his heart.

Tonight the lie had gotten expensive. At supper she'd asked him, casually, terribly casually, why he flinched whenever she said the word *binding*. And he had watched her file the question away with that scribe's precision of hers—the same precision the guild wanted to spend like coin—and he understood that he had perhaps two days before she found out without him. And if she found out without him, there would be no coming back from it. Not for them. Not for whatever this was that he refused to name because naming things was how the guild got its hooks in them.

So he sat down across from her sleeping shape, and he took out the pages, and he waited for her to wake, because Cyrus Amberdale woke at the sound of paper the way other people woke at the sound of their own name.

She surfaced in under a minute. Of course she did.

"What is that," she said. Not a question. An accusation with a delay on it.

"My family," Sef said. "Or part of it. The part we paid to forget."

* * *

He started where all honest accounts start: with the oldest entry.

"The year the last dragon was chained," he said, "there were three hands on the binding spell. You know two of them already—you've read the founders' cipher in your own way, even if you don't know you have. The Amberdale hand drew the circle. The Vaelmoor king gave his oath to be bound. And the third hand—" he turned the first page toward her, and the honeyed light caught the ink, ink that had regrown itself twice since he'd copied it, ink that remembered better than people did— "the third hand was a Quillon."

Her face did something complicated. He watched her do the arithmetic. Quillon. His name, before the guild struck it and gave him a number instead. *Sef-7*, they called him in the lower stacks, as though archivists were shelved like books.

"Your line helped write it." Her voice came out flat, which was worse than angry. Flat was how she sounded when she was keeping herself level over a long drop.

"Helped write it, and then was paid to maintain it." He laid the second page down. "Every generation, one Quillon swears the maintenance oath. We renew the anchor-phrases. We check the crown hasn't loosened. And in exchange, the guild leaves our name out of every ledger it keeps—which sounds like protection until you understand what it actually is. It means we can't be traced. It means if anything goes wrong down there, there's no record that anyone named Quillon ever existed to blame."

"That's not protection," Cyrus said. "That's pre-erasure."

"Yes." He'd known she'd see it instantly. That was the whole problem with her; she saw the shape of a trap faster than anyone he'd ever met, including the people who built traps for a living. "My grandfather used to say we weren't the guild's servants. We were its contingency plan. If the binding ever failed, the failure would land on a name that didn't exist."

"And your oath," she said slowly. "The maintenance oath. Is it still live?"

Sef looked at the coal instead of her. "I swore it at nineteen. Before I was disgraced. Before I understood what I was swearing to maintain." He turned his left hand over. On the palm, faint as an old scar, sat a sigil he had spent years pretending was a birthmark—a closed circle with a vertical stroke through it, the mark of the third hand. "It doesn't wash off. It doesn't fade. When the crown is near, it warms." He held his palm toward the lantern, toward the window, toward the palace on the hill. "It's warm tonight. Warmer than it's been in nine years."

Cyrus stood. She crossed to the window in four steps and looked up at the citadel, and Sef watched her shoulders change—the set of someone deciding something.

"You've known since the scriptorium," she said. "Since the prophecy named me. You knew my blood was in that spell and yours was too, and you let me walk into the guildmasters' hall alone."

"I followed you," he said. "Across half a country. With the only copy of the prophecy the guild doesn't own."

"After. After you let me walk in." She turned around. In the coal-light her eyes had gone the flat grey of ledger paper. "Why, Sef? Why follow me at all? If your family's whole strategy for a century has been silence and payment and staying off the page—why break it for me?"

And there it was. The real question, the one under all the others, and he had run out of road to avoid it.

"Because my grandmother broke it first," he said. "Sit down. Please. This takes the whole night, and I'd rather tell it standing than lying, but you should sit either way."

* * *

His grandmother's name had been Maren Quillon, and she was the first in six generations to refuse the payment.

"She read the founders' marginalia," Sef said. "The same cipher you're going to find—I'm certain of it now, after what you described in the archive. She worked the royal correspondence shelf in the old days, back when the guild still pretended the vault under the temple was just storage. And she found the invoice." He tapped the third page. "Because that's what the prophecy is, Cyrus. You said it yourself, or you will say it soon enough. It's not a warning. It's a bill. A century ago the founders wrote down exactly what the crown costs, exactly when the payment comes due, and exactly whose hand settles it—and then they sealed it and called it prophecy so no one would think to audit it."

"Maren tried to audit it," Cyrus guessed.

"Maren tried to stop paying." He laughed, and it came out wrong, dry and small. "She withheld the renewal. One quarter. She thought if the guild missed a payment cycle they'd come to the table and negotiate. Instead they came to the house." He made himself keep going, because stopping was how this kind of story died. "They didn't kill her. Killing leaves records. They did what they did to you—what they'll do again if we fail. They struck her. From the ledgers, from the parish rolls, from the memory of everyone who'd ever spoken to her. My grandfather was nine years old. He watched his mother become a stranger in her own kitchen. By the time the guild's men left, he couldn't remember loving her. He could only remember that the chair she sat in was warm."

The coal ticked softly in its lantern. Down in the street, a cart passed, and neither of them moved until the sound faded.

"So my grandfather paid," Sef said. "For forty years, on time, every quarter, he paid. And he taught my father to pay, and my father taught me, and the lesson underneath the lesson—the one nobody said out loud but everybody learned—was *love is a liability. Anything you're attached to, the guild can take, and the only defense is to owe them nothing and be nothing.*" He spread his hands. "You wondered why I lived alone in the archive stacks. Why I never joined anything, signed anything, belonged to anything. Now you know. I wasn't hiding from the guild. I was hiding from having something they could use."

"And then a scribe started erasing herself from every ledger she touched," Cyrus said quietly, "and you couldn't look away."

"You were doing the only thing my family ever dreamed of," he said. "You were making yourself unownable. Badly, dangerously, with terrible technique—but deliberately. Everyone else in that building was trying to get *into* the ledgers. A place in the record, a wage, a pension, a name. And there was this woman in the corner of the scriptorium spending nine years perfecting the art of absence, and I thought—" His voice caught. He let it. "I thought, *she knows.* She knows what the ledgers are for. She knows what gets done with names. And I needed to know how she'd figured it out, because my family had spent a century learning the same lesson the hard way and we'd learned it as fear, and she seemed to have learned it as—"

"As spite," Cyrus said.

A beat. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled, and the flat grey went out of her eyes.

"All right," she said. "Spite. But here's what I don't understand yet, Sef, and I need you to be exact about it, because exactness is the only currency I trust." She sat down across from him, elbows on the table, close enough that the coal-light touched both their faces. "If your line maintains the binding—if the Quillon oath is what keeps the anchor-phrases tight—then why is it failing? Why is the crown eating the king faster than the maintenance can slow it?"

"Because the maintenance was never meant to hold forever," Sef said. "It was meant to hold until the invoice came due. Read the fourth page."

She read it. He watched her lips move slightly over the old formula, translating the founder's shorthand the way only a second-generation eraser could—reading not what the words said but what they were designed to conceal. He saw the moment she hit it. Her breath stopped and restarted.

"'The binding renews by blood,'" she read aloud, very quietly. "'The hand that drew the circle draws it again—or the debt passes to the crown's choosing.'" She looked up. "It's not failing, Sef. It's *expiring*. It was written to expire. Tonight. Because the founders built a renewal clause into it, and the renewal requires the Amberdale hand, and the guild has spent a hundred years making sure that when the date arrived, there would be an Amberdale available and ignorant."

"They didn't make sure," Sef said. "They *waited*. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole crime. They didn't hunt your family. They didn't need to. They just kept the prophecy sealed and let time do the work, and when a girl showed up in their scriptorium who couldn't stop unmaking herself from the record—a girl with exactly the right name—they recognized the invoice walking through the door." He pushed the pages toward her. "You weren't summoned to Vaelmoor to verify the crown, Cyrus. You were delivered. Like a payment."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Then Cyrus did the thing he had learned, over eleven days and five hundred miles, to recognize as the most dangerous thing about her. She didn't rage. She reached for a pen.

There was none on the table, so she used her finger, tracing in the spilled coal-light on the wood: the circle, the vertical stroke. "Third hand," she said. "Show me the maintenance phrases. All of them. Every phrase your family has renewed for a hundred years."

"Why?"

"Because if your blood maintained the binding and mine created it," she said, and her voice had gone steady in the particular way it went steady right before she did something irreversible, "then the spell has two locks, not one. And the guildmasters only know about one of them. Which means somewhere between your oath and my name, there's a seam. And seams are where everything I've ever escaped from has come apart."

Sef looked at her—at this woman he had followed across a country on a lie of omission, who had just taken the worst thing he'd ever confessed and turned it into a mechanism within thirty seconds—and felt the strange lightness that comes when a weight finally changes hands.

"There's one more thing," he said. "Before we start. You deserve it straight."

"Go."

"The night the guild struck my grandmother, my grandfather kept one thing of hers. A letter. It's been in my family since, passed down with the oath, and it says—" He recited it from memory, because he had memorized it at nineteen and never once stopped hearing it. "*Tell whoever holds the oath after me: the third hand was never meant to serve the crown. We were meant to be its undoing. We were the failsafe. When the Amberdale hand comes, give her everything.*"

Cyrus stared at him.

"Give me everything," she repeated. "What does that mean? What does your grandmother mean by *everything*?"

"I don't know," Sef said honestly. "But I know where the answer is. Because there's a final page to the founders' cipher—one page the guild never sealed, because they never realized it existed. My grandmother found it. It's not in the temple, and it's not in the royal archive." He stood, and pulled his coat on, and nodded toward the window, toward the dark bulk of the citadel above the joyless city. "It's in the vault. Under the throne. Where the crown keeps everything it's eaten."

Cyrus was already reaching for her boots.
