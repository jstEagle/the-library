## 10. The Last Dragon

The coal woke before I did.

I had kept it in a clay pot wrapped in wool since the hearth-keeper pressed it into my hands three days ago, and every morning it did the same small impossible thing: it brightened at dawn, as if it knew dawn was coming, as if it were counting. Sef said coals didn't count. Sef said a lot of things about what dead fire couldn't do, and he was wrong about all of them so far.

We had come down off the ashfall road into the hills south of Vaelmoor, following a warmth that behaved like a compass needle. The coal leaned in its pot—leaned, physically tilted toward one direction and held there, straining like a dog on a leash—and wherever we walked, it corrected us back. North by northeast. Toward the guild's original vault, according to the fragmentary maps Sef had copied from the royal archive: a structure older than the temple, older than the guild's own founding charter, built into the roots of a hill the locals called the Sleeping Mother because no one remembered why.

No one remembered much of anything here. That was the first thing I noticed about the villages around the hill. Not poverty—the houses were sound, the fields tended—but absence. No songs. No festivals with any history attached to them. When I asked an old woman mending nets outside Ferrow's Bend why her village had no midsummer fire, she looked at me the way you look at someone who has asked a question in a language you've forgotten.

"We used to," she said. "My grandmother said so."

"What happened to it?"

She thought for a long time. "It went to the city," she said finally, and there was something in her voice—not bitterness, not grief, just the flat tone of someone reciting a fact they'd been given rather than learned—that made me understand I was walking through the ledger itself. These villages were entries. Their warmth was written down somewhere, owned by someone, drawn out of them line by line until only the shape of a life remained.

The coal strained harder as we climbed.

* * *

The vault's entrance was not hidden. That was the thing that unsettled me most. It stood open at the hill's base behind a screen of birches—a door of black iron gone soft with rust, hanging on one hinge, and beyond it stairs descending into the dark in long shallow flights, each step worn in the center by centuries of feet. Worn steps mean traffic. Traffic means this place was never secret; it was simply never *asked about*. The guild's oldest trick, I was beginning to understand, wasn't concealment. It was boredom. Nobody questions the room everyone walks past.

Sef wanted to go down slowly, marking our path with chalk, the way archivists do. I let him mark the first two flights before I told him the truth I'd been carrying since Ferrow's Bend: I didn't think we'd get lost. The coal wouldn't allow it. And whatever waited below had been waiting a very long time, and I was tired of arriving politely.

So we went down fast, chalk be damned, the coal held out ahead of us in its pot like a lantern with opinions.

The stair turned four times. On the third turn the air changed—it warmed, but not evenly, not like sunlight or a hearth. It came in pulses. A slow rhythm, maybe one breath every ten seconds, warm then cool then warm again, rising up the stairwell like something exhaling far below. Sef noticed it before I said anything. "That's not geothermal," he whispered. "Geothermal doesn't breathe."

On the fourth turn, the walls changed too. The rough-cut stone gave way to fitted blocks, and on those blocks, carved deep and repeated every few paces, ran a line of script I recognized from the founders' marginalia—the old binding-hand, the one my bloodline had helped write. I had spent nine years copying that script without knowing what it said. Here it covered the walls floor to ceiling, thousands of repetitions, and between the repetitions, smaller marks: tallies. Counting marks, scratched in later, in different hands, some of them centuries apart. Someone had been down here counting for a very long time.

The stair ended at a landing, and the landing ended at a door that was not a door. It was a slab of iron the height of a cathedral gate, and it bore no lock, no handle, no hinges. It bore a contract.

I saw it before I understood it. Set into the metal, inlaid in silver, was the full text of a covenant—the same covenant structure the guild used now, the same clauses, the same elegant cruelty of terms arranged to look mutual. But this one was older, and stranger, and one clause near the bottom stopped my breath:

*...in consideration of fire freely given, the undersigned grants warmth in perpetuity to the city of Vaelmoor and its dependents, such grant to continue until the giver's name is spoken aloud by one who owes it nothing.*

Below the clause, where a signature would go, the silver had been gouged away. Deliberately. By many hands, over many years, anyone who might have spoken the name scraped out of the metal again and again, so thoroughly that even the shape of the letters was lost.

"They erased the name," Sef breathed. He had his notebook out, copying everything, because that was how Sef prayed. "They put the name in the contract and then they made sure no one could ever say it. That's—" His voice caught. "That's what they did to me last week. To my records. They do it to *everything*."

The coal in my pot flared so hard the wool smoked.

And beneath us, through the stone, something exhaled.

* * *

There was a way around the door—I found it by following the warmth, which seeped through a fissure in the wall behind a fall of rubble, a crack barely wide enough for me, certainly too narrow for Sef. We argued about it for exactly as long as it took him to lose. He would wait. He would keep copying the covenant. If I screamed, he should run for help that wouldn't come, and then come find me anyway, because that was who Sef was.

The fissure opened onto a gallery—a maintenance passage, cut narrow, running along the inside of the vault like the space between an eyelid and an eye. I crawled. The pulses of warm air grew stronger, and now I could hear under them a second sound, fainter, rhythmic in a different way: a chain. Somewhere below, something vast shifted its weight, and chain links dragged over stone with a sound like a slow bell.

Then the gallery opened, and I looked down into the heart of the hill, and I forgot how to breathe.

The chamber was a hollow sphere, bigger than the temple nave, and it was full of light—or what I had spent my whole life calling light. A glow filled the space the way water fills a bowl, gold-red, moving, alive, and at the center of it lay the source: a dragon.

I need to be precise, because everything I thought I knew about dragons came from temple bestiaries, and the bestiaries were wrong, or lying, or both. This creature was not vast in the way of monuments. It was vast in the way of weather. Its body curled around the chamber's center twice, and even guttered—even starved, even dimmed to embers—it filled the space the way a river fills a valley. Its scales, where the light still touched them, were the color of cooling bronze. Where the light had left them, they were gray. Ash-gray. Half of it was already ash-colored.

Chains ran from its limbs to anchors set in the chamber walls, and the chains were not iron. I got close enough to the gallery rail to see. They were silver, worked in the same script as the covenant above, and they moved—no, they *flowed*, almost imperceptibly, a slow current running along each link from the dragon toward the walls. The chains weren't holding it down. They were drinking.

From the walls, veins of copper rose upward and outward, out of the chamber, out of the hill, and I understood with a lurch that they ran toward Vaelmoor. Every lamp in that joyless city. Every comfortable hearth. Every festival they could no longer remember how to hold. It came from here. It came from *this*.

The dragon's chest lifted once, slowly, and fell. One breath every ten seconds. I had been listening to its breathing for an hour without knowing it.

I stood at the rail and did the cowardly human thing: I tried to be quiet. I failed, because my hands were shaking and the clay pot clicked against the rail, and the sound was nothing, less than nothing, a pebble in a canyon.

The dragon opened one eye.

It was not a reptile's eye. Bestiaries again. It was an eye like a well seen from the top, deep and dark and patient, and when it fixed on me I felt the specific vertigo of being *read*—of standing in front of something that took in my whole self at a glance the way I took in a ledger line. It looked at me for a long moment. Then it spoke.

Not in words at first. In heat. The glow around it pulsed in patterns, and the patterns resolved against my skin like pressure, like a hand writing on my back, and Sef would later tell me that what I did next was impossible, but I'm getting ahead of myself. What happened was this: I answered. Without deciding to, without knowing the language, I answered, because the script on the walls and the script in the founders' marginalia and the script my bloodline had helped write turned out to live somewhere in me deeper than learning. My family hadn't just written the binding. They had written it *in themselves*. It was in my blood the way a song is in a whistle-bone, and the dragon's speech found it and rang it.

"*Amberdale,*" it said, in a voice like dry paper catching flame, like a hearth speaking after everyone has left the room. "*Little scribe. Little heir.*"

I gripped the rail. The world tilted and steadied.

"You know my name."

"I have known your name longer than your temple has existed." The eye did not blink. Dragons, I noted, were also wrong about blinking, or perhaps they simply saw no reason to perform surprise. "Your ancestor Veyra Amberdale stood where you stand. She wrote half of these chains. She wept while she wrote them. I remember her tears hitting the stone. They are still here, if you know how to look. Salt keeps."

"Why?" My voice came out small. "Why would she—we—do this?"

"Because she was promised it was mercy." The dragon's breath stirred the whole chamber, a slow tide of warm air that smelled of hot metal and, underneath, faintly, of libraries. "The city was freezing. The old fires were dying. Your founders came to me and asked, and I said yes—do you understand? I *said yes*. Fire freely given. That was the covenant. I gave my fire to keep ten thousand people alive through a killing winter, and the covenant said the giving would end when my strength returned, or when my name was spoken by one who owed me nothing." A pause, one full breath. "My strength never returned. Because they built the chains to make sure of it. And the name—they put it in silver, and then they taught a city to forget it, and then they hired your family's hands to keep the forgetting written down."

The chamber seemed to tilt. Nine years of ledgers rearranged themselves behind my eyes. Every entry I'd ever copied, every impossible trade, every shadow sold twice and memory pawned—all of it, all of it, was downstream of this room. The guild didn't trade in impossibilities. It traded in one impossibility, the founding one, the theft dressed as gift: a dragon's promise, converted into perpetual income. Everything else was bookkeeping.

"The prophecy," I said. "The one with tonight's date. With my hand in it."

"I dictated it." The dragon's tone did not change, and that was somehow the worst part—the flatness of it, the patience of a creature that had been waiting a century and could wait another. "I dictated it to a scribe I trusted, and the guild sealed it and shelved it and called it a warning, so that when the date came, they could spend whoever it named. Prophecies make excellent invoices. Everyone reads them as fate. Almost no one reads them as *terms*."

"Invoice," I repeated. Chapter nine of my life, being written in real time. "It's a bill."

"It is a bill," the dragon agreed, "and you, little scribe, are the payment they intend to render. Your hand renews the binding. Your blood refreshes the chains. Another century of warmth for Vaelmoor, another century of me going gray." The eye turned fully on me, and in its depth I saw something that undid me more than anger would have: not hatred. Recognition. "Veyra wept too. And then she signed anyway. That is the Amberdale inheritance, child. Not the script. The signing."

I wanted to argue. I reached for every argument I had—that I had spent nine years erasing myself precisely so I could never be used, that I was nobody, that nobody owed anybody anything. But the coal in my pot flared, and the dragon's gaze dropped to it, and softened, if a thing that vast can be said to soften.

"You carry Ember-of-the-Long-Wait," it said. "Old Marta's coal. She was my keeper's daughter, once. Her line kept a piece of me alive in a clay pot for six generations, feeding it nothing, asking nothing, on the chance that someday someone would follow it home." A breath. "You owe me nothing. Do you see? You owe me *nothing*. You are the first soul in a hundred years to walk into this room owing me nothing."

The covenant clause. *Spoken aloud by one who owes it nothing.* My mouth went dry. The name had been gouged out of the silver—but the dragon was looking at me, and waiting, and I understood suddenly that the name was not missing. It had never been missing. It was just unspoken, and unspoken things were my entire profession. I had spent nine years making names vanish from paper. I knew better than anyone alive that a name erased from a record is not a name destroyed. It is a name *waiting*.

"Tell me," I whispered. "Tell me your name."

"No." The word came gentle as banked coals. "If I tell you, they will call it coercion, and coerced speech breaks covenants—they wrote that loophole themselves, in the third century, to protect themselves from their own contracts. You must find it the way scribes find everything." The great head lowered until the well-deep eye was level with mine, close enough that I felt its warmth on my face like a hand. "In the records. In the margins. In the place they were too arrogant to check, because they believed no one reads what they burn."

Above me, faint through the stone, I heard Sef's chalk stop scratching.

And then I heard something else. Boots. Many boots, on the worn stairs, descending in formation, with the particular unhurried cadence of people who own the building and know the appointment is kept. The guild had tracked the coal after all—or had known where it would lead from the moment Marta pressed it into my hands, because Marta's grandmother had been on their rolls too, because *everyone* was on their rolls, everyone had always been on their rolls.

The dragon's eye held mine, calm amid the approaching thunder of boots.

"They are coming to collect their invoice, little scribe," it said. "Decide quickly what kind of Amberdale you are."
