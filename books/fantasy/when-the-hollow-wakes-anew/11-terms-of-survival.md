## 11. Terms of Survival

The coal did not lead us out. It led us down.

I had assumed, when it first pulsed warm against my palm in the hearth-keeper's cottage, that it was a keepsake—a last ember pressed into a stranger's hands the way you'd press a coin into a dead man's. But it had opinions. It tugged, faintly, like a fish on a line, and every time I tried to walk away from the guild's original vault it grew heavier, until my arm ached with the weight of a lump no bigger than a plum.

"It wants to go home," Sef said, three days into the tunnels beneath Vaelmoor, his voice gone hoarse from disuse and the fine ash that coated everything down here like gray snow.

"Then it has a cruel sense of direction," I said. "Because home is apparently straight through a wall that shouldn't exist."

The wall shouldn't exist. That was the only honest way to describe it. We had followed the vault's oldest corridor past shelves of contracts older than the temple, past the founders' cipher still inked on my forearm in charcoal where I'd copied it that morning, and the corridor had simply ended in stone. Except the stone was warm. Except the stone breathed, if you laid your palm flat and waited. Except the coal, when I raised it, threw light *through* the wall, and the wall was not a wall at all but a door disguised so thoroughly from the inside that even the guild's own ledgers had filed it under *structural*.

The founders had been accountants before they were anything else. Of course they'd hidden a dragon behind a line item.

Sef put his shoulder to it. I put mine beside his. And the door, which had waited a century for exactly this kind of insolence, opened with a sound like a book falling open.

* * *

Heat hit us first—not the wet heat of a forge but something older and drier, the heat of a summer that ended before the temple was built. Then the light. Then the size of the thing, which my eyes refused for a long moment, the way they refuse the ocean.

The last dragon lay coiled in a chamber carved from the mountain's living rock, and she was dying, and she was still the largest living thing I had ever seen. Her scales had gone the color of banked coals, dull orange fading to gray at her edges, and where her fire should have shown through the gaps between them there was only a faint, guttering glow, like a lamp running out of oil. Chains crossed her wings, her throat, the long architecture of her spine—chains of black iron worked with script I recognized, because I had spent nine years writing its cousin in the temple ledgers. Binding-script. Guild-script. The founders' own hand.

She was chained to the vault above her the way a cellar is chained to a house. Everything warm in Vaelmoor was warm because of her.

She was siphoned. That was the word the cipher had used and the word was exactly right. Thin channels ran from the chains up into the rock, and along those channels moved a light I knew intimately: dragon-fire, thinned and filtered and fed upward, piped into the hearths of a city that believed its warmth came from the guild's generosity. The overflow. The crown ate the king, and the dragon's fire was stretched to hide the taste.

The coal in my palm flared. I opened my fingers and it drifted, weightless now, home at last, and settled into a crack between two of her scales like a spark returning to its fire.

Her eye opened.

I want to be honest, because I am a ledger-keeper and honesty is the only discipline I have left: every story I'd ever heard about dragons ended with teeth. I had my hand on Sef's sleeve and my weight on my back foot and every instinct I owned screaming at me to run. And then she looked at me—not at Sef, not at the door, at *me*—and her eye was old and tired and full of a grief so large it made my own look like a rounding error, and I stopped wanting to run. I stopped wanting anything except to know what she would say.

She said my name.

"Cyrus Amberdale." Her voice was the sound a fire makes when it finds a draft—low, rushing, everywhere at once. "Nine years of erasing yourself, little scribe, and the mountain still knows you. Your grandmother's grandmother stood where you stand. She wrote the first line of the first chain." The great eye closed, opened. "I have been reading her handwriting on my bones for a hundred years."

* * *

We sat with her. There was nothing else to do. Sef found a ledge of warm stone and lowered himself onto it like a man kneeling at a deathbed, which was, I suppose, exactly what he was doing. I stood closer, close enough to feel her breath move the ash in the air, and I asked the questions in the order accountants ask them: how much, how long, who profits.

The answers were worse than I'd feared, and I had spent the road to Vaelmoor training my fear to expect the worst.

She had been young when they took her—the last of her kind, drawn down from the ashfall passes by a fire she thought was one of her own. The founders had built a lure. A false call, pitched in the old tongue, and she had followed it into the vault like a moth, and they had closed the mountain on her, and the binding-script had gone on like a garment made for her by people who had studied her family for generations. The Amberdale line. The Sefarim line. Two bloodlines, sworn to the work, one to write and one to maintain.

"The guild does not collect fire," she said, and there was no heat in it at all, which somehow made it worse. "The guild collects *me*. Every hearth in Vaelmoor is a tap. Every winter festival is a withdrawal. They have been spending my life for a century and calling it public service, and the crown—" a shudder moved through her, chains singing faintly, "—the crown is their best customer. It devours the king, and my fire papers over the hunger so no one looks too closely. A dead man on a throne, kept warm. A dying dragon under a city, kept spending. And between us, the guild skims."

"How long," Sef said quietly. "How long can you last."

"Ask your ledgers." The eye rolled toward him, and I saw him flinch at being known. "You are the maintenance line, archivist. Your family has balanced my decline for six generations. You know the figures better than I do."

"I never—" He stopped. Started again, and I will give him this: he didn't look away from her. "I never knew what the figures were. My father gave me a column of numbers every year and told me it was temple tithing. I copied it. I copied it for twenty years."

"Yes," the dragon said, almost gently. "That is how the guild works. No one carries the whole debt. Everyone carries a piece, and no one can see the shape of the whole. It is very good accounting." A plume of breath, gray and thin. "It is also why I am dying in the dark, and no one in a hundred years has come to ask me my name."

"What *is* your name?" I asked.

The fire in her guttered, flared. For a moment the whole chamber lit orange and the chains blazed with reflected light and I understood, briefly and completely, what Vaelmoor's founders had stolen: not fuel. *This.* "Vessarax," she said. "But the script does not use it. The script calls me *asset*. Names are the first thing binding takes."

I thought of nine years of scraped pages. Of my own name struck from every ledger I touched, of walking through the temple like a draft through a room. I thought: they did to her what I did to myself, and they did it on purpose, and they called it stewardship.

"Then let's talk terms," I said, because I am my grandmother's granddaughter after all, and when the grief gets too large I reach for the ledger. "You said the founders' cipher was an invoice. Fine. I'm here to settle accounts. What do you want?"

* * *

What she wanted was simple. What it cost was not.

"Break the chains," she said. "Truly break them—not loosen, not renew, *break*—and I will burn the crown out of the world. I owe the kings of Vaelmoor that much; their first king broke an oath to my kind and the crown is what his brokenness became. I can unmake it. It is the one work left in me that is mine."

"And the city?" Sef asked. His voice had gone very careful. I recognized the care. It was the care of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

"The city," Vessarax said, "has been warm on my blood for a hundred years. When the chains break, the taps close. The fire I have left is mine, and I will need all of it—every spark—to burn out the crown and to climb out of this mountain and die somewhere under open sky, as my kind is meant to die." The eye fixed on me. "Vaelmoor will go cold, little scribe. Not slowly. In a night. The hearths, the forges, the greenhouses that feed forty thousand people through the winter. All of it. You have read the ledgers. You know what a cold Vaelmoor costs by spring."

I did know. I had copied the contingency figures myself in my third year at the scriptorium, filed under *northern assets, weather risk*. Forty thousand. I had written the number without once wondering what it counted.

"There is another way," she said, before the silence could become a verdict. "There is always another way. That is what binding is: a way to make one thing pay for another."

"Tell me," I said.

"The chains are not really iron." Her voice dropped, and the fire in it dropped with it, until she sounded almost like the hearth-keeper, almost like anyone's grandmother explaining an old, bad bargain. "Iron is only the shape. The chain is an agreement. Written in Amberdale blood, maintained by Sefarim hands, renewed every generation so the debt never comes due all at once. The founders were clever. They made the binding *renewable*—but they made it transferable too, because clever people always leave themselves an exit. The binding does not have to live in me. It can live in a name. In a hand. In a person who takes it willingly, into their own blood, and holds the crown's hunger the way I have held it. The fire stays in the city. The chains break. And someone—" the pause was very long, and very deliberate, "—becomes the new chain."

The chamber was quiet except for the thin sound of the taps, the fire of her draining away into a city that would never know.

"Someone," I said. "Meaning me."

"Meaning the hand the prophecy names." She did not soften it. I was grateful; I have never trusted people who soften prices. "The prophecy is not a warning, Cyrus Amberdale. It is a contract, and a contract requires a signature. Your blood wrote the first line. Your name is the only ink the binding will accept freely. That is why the guild has been waiting for you. That is why they sent you here. They have known for a century that the asset was failing, and they have known that the renewal requires you, and they intended to let you do it believing it was mercy—believing you were saving the king." A slow blink, the lid of the eye like a door closing on a lit room. "You would have. You would have saved the king and renewed the chains and gone home rich, and Vaelmoor would have gone on warm for another hundred years on a stranger's blood, and no one would ever have been told whose."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the mountain.

"They wanted me to be the new dragon," I said.

"They wanted you to be the new *anything*. Dragon, chain, crown-bearer. The guild does not care what you become. The guild cares that the accounts stay open." Her fire guttered low, and in the dimness she seemed suddenly smaller, suddenly only what she was: an old creature at the end of a long theft. "So. Here are the terms, scribe, and I will not dress them. Break the chains and take the binding into your blood: Vaelmoor lives, the crown dies, the king is freed, and you spend what remains of your life as the vessel of an agreement you did not write, holding a hunger that is not yours, in a body that will never again be only your own. Or break the chains and refuse the binding: you are free, I am free, the crown burns, and forty thousand people learn in a single winter what their warmth was made of. Or walk away. Renew the chains as the guild intends. Save everyone, save yourself, save nothing."

"And you?" Sef asked. "What happens to you, if the binding transfers?"

The eye closed.

"I die free," Vessarax said. "It is more than the guild intended to give me. It is enough."

* * *

Sef talked first. Of course he did. He had been doing arithmetic since we entered the chamber, and I could see the total in his face, and I knew—I knew before he opened his mouth—what number he had arrived at.

"Your line wrote the binding," he said, standing, ash falling from his coat. "Mine maintained it. Six generations of Sefarims kept those chains oiled, Cyrus. If anyone owes the debt—"

"Sit down, Sef."

"—then the debt is mine to—"

"*Sit down.*" My voice came out with an edge I didn't recognize, and he sat. I stood there in the firelight with my hands shaking, and I made myself say the true thing, the whole true thing, because I had spent nine years learning what half-truths cost. "The binding won't take you. You heard her. It needs the hand the prophecy names. It needs *Amberdale* blood freely given. Your sacrifice isn't on the menu, Sef. It was never on the menu. So don't you dare stand there and make yourself the hero of a bargain you can't sign."

He stared at me. And then, because he is Sef, and because he is the only person in two kingdoms who has never once looked through me, he said: "Then I'll find a way to share it. Or shorten it. Or break it after you take it. I'm an archivist, Cyrus. I find the loopholes. Give me the binding's text and I will find the flaw in it if I have to read until I die."

"There is no flaw. The founders were—"

"Clever. Yes. I've heard." He was on his feet again, and his voice cracked in the middle of the word, and I realized he wasn't arguing about the binding at all. "You erased yourself for nine years because you thought that was safety. Now you're about to sign yourself into a chain because you've decided it's justice. It's the same move, Cyrus. Hiding and sacrificing are the same move. It's deciding your life is a line item."

The words went in like a hook.

I turned to the dragon, because I could not keep looking at him. "How long," I said, "if I take it. How long does a bearer last."

"No bearer has ever been made willingly," Vessarax said. "The founders' bearers were taken. They lasted between nine years and forty. It depends on the hunger, and the hunger depends on the crown, and the crown—" a slow exhale, gray fire, "—the crown depends on what it is given. A name freely given feeds it differently than a name stolen. I do not know what that means for you. No one knows. You would be the first."

The first. The last. There was always one more ledger with my name in it, and it was always a ledger I hadn't read yet.

Above us, faint through a hundred feet of rock, I could hear Vaelmoor: the bells, the carts, the forty thousand small sounds of a city keeping warm on stolen fire. Somewhere up there a hollow king sat on a throne not blinking, begging a scribe in a voice like dry paper to tell him what he used to be. Somewhere up there the guild was counting the days until I did exactly what I was bred to do.

"Give me the terms in writing," I said at last, because it was the only prayer I knew. "All of them. Every clause. If I'm going to sign my blood into a chain, I want to read the whole contract first—and I want Sef reading over my shoulder, because I don't trust a bargain that only one person has ever examined."

Vessarax's eye opened, and for the first time since we entered the chamber, something moved through the old fire that might have been—almost—amusement.

"Then you had better read quickly," she said. "Because I just heard boots in the corridor above, and little scribe—the guild has found its coal."

And far up the tunnel, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the vault door opening.
