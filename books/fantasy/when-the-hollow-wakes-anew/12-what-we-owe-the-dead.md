## 12. What We Owe the Dead

Sef had rehearsed the speech for three days. He'd written it out twice, in the careful archivist's hand that had once earned him a place in the temple's inner stacks, and then he'd burned both drafts in the hearth of the waystation outside Vaelmoor, because a thing spoken from paper could be argued with, and he needed Cyrus to understand there was nothing to argue.

He was going to take the binding. That was the whole of it. His line had written half the spell; his line had kept the silence; his line had fed the guild its quiet payments for six generations while a king rotted alive inside his own crown. If a debt existed anywhere in this story, it sat on his family's ledger, not hers.

He just hadn't figured out how to say it to a woman who could erase herself from any record she touched, and who might, if cornered, erase him too — not out of malice, but out of habit. Out of love, even. He'd watched her do it to her own name often enough to know it came as naturally to her as breathing.

The vault beneath the old guildhouse smelled of iron and cold ash. The dragon's chain ran up through the ceiling into darkness, each link thick as Sef's arm, and beside it the undying coal sat in its clay dish, glowing with a patience that made him uneasy. Above them, somewhere in the palace, the hollow king waited for an answer they didn't have yet.

Cyrus stood at the chain's base with her hand flat against the metal, reading it the way she read everything — slowly, completely, letting the words surface on their own terms. She'd been down here since before dawn. Sef had counted the hours by the coal's light, which never changed, which gave no hours at all.

"Cy," he said. "We need to talk about the rite."

"I know what you're going to say."

"You can't."

She turned. In the coal's glow her face was all planes and shadow, and for a moment he saw the scribe again, the one he'd met nine years ago in the scriptorium, hunched over ledgers, making herself smaller than the furniture. Then he saw what nine years and one prophecy had built over that: someone who stood like a woman who had stopped apologizing for taking up space.

"Let me guess," she said. "You've decided it should be you."

"The binding requires a willing heir," Sef said. "Someone who takes the crown's hunger into their own blood and holds it there. My ancestors wrote that clause. My blood is already in the spell — it's been paying into it for generations. It's *owed* from my line, Cyrus. Not yours."

"Sit down, Sef."

"I'd rather stand."

"Then stand there and listen." She pulled the founders' cipher from her satchel — the pages they'd spent two nights decoding, marginalia layered over marginalia, three centuries of guild bookkeeping disguised as devotion. "I've read the rite eleven times. I've read every annotation. And every single time, the same phrase appears: *the hand named in the prophecy.*"

"That's a formality—"

"It's a mechanism." Her voice didn't rise. It never rose; that was the terrifying thing about arguing with Cyrus Amberdale. She simply got more precise. "The spell doesn't want willingness. Willingness is necessary but not sufficient. It wants a specific signature. The founders bound the magic to a name the way you bind a contract to a seal. Any other name offered to it won't anchor the crown — it'll just open a door. You'd be volunteering to become a vessel with no bottom."

"And you know this how?"

"Because I tried it."

The words landed in the cold air between them and stayed there. Sef felt his stomach drop through the floor of the vault, through the earth, into whatever dark held the dragon's dreams.

"When?" he managed.

"Last night, while you slept. A test. A single word of the counter-spell, spoken with your name substituted for mine." She held up her right hand. In the coal-light he could see the skin reddened across the palm, blistered along the lines where a scribe's calluses lived. "The spell rejected it. Violently. The ink in the cipher rearranged itself to spell my name before the backlash even finished. The magic isn't asking who volunteers. It's checking the guest list."

Sef sat down after all. There was a broken crate near the wall, and his legs simply decided for him.

"So we find another way," he said. "We rewrite the clause. You're a scribe — you told me yourself that prophecy is just text, and text can be amended. Amend it. Put my name in."

"With what authority?"

"With the authority of the person holding the pen!"

"The last people who amended that prophecy were the founders," Cyrus said quietly, "and look what they did with it. They turned a warning into an invoice. They turned a king into a battery. Every edit made to that document has been a theft dressed up as scholarship. You want me to add one more?"

"Yes," Sef said. "Yes, exactly that. Because the alternative is you."

* * *

Here is what Sef knew about sacrifice, learned the way everyone learns it — not in theory but in the specific, unglamorous currency of a single life:

He knew that at thirty-one he had no family left who spoke to him. The Vessic line — his line, the co-authors' line — had disowned him at twenty-four when he'd reported the archive fraud that got the previous Master of Records removed. They hadn't sent a letter. They'd simply struck him from the family's private rolls, which for a family obsessed with records was the only curse that mattered.

He knew that he'd spent seven years in the temple's lowest stacks, cataloguing receipts nobody wanted read, because the guild punished honesty with obscurity and he'd chosen obscurity over complicity and had told himself, every morning for seven years, that this was enough. That clean hands in a dirty room still counted as clean.

He knew that when he'd found the second name hidden beneath the first in the prophecy — *Vessic*, inked under *Amberdale*, buried in the parchment like a body under floorboards — he had copied the pages in secret and followed Cyrus onto the road without telling her why, and he had told himself that was loyalty, and some mornings, in the grey hour before dawn, he admitted it was also cowardice. He'd wanted to be present for the reckoning without being named in it.

And he knew — this was the newest knowledge, only days old — that he loved her. Not the dramatic kind. The kind that looked like memorizing how she took her tea, and standing between her and doors, and lying awake doing arithmetic on her behalf: *if the rite costs a life, and the life is hers, then everything I have ever sacrificed adds up to nothing, because I did all of it in a world that still had her in it.*

That was the arithmetic he brought to the vault. He laid it out now, all of it, in the flat voice he used for inventory.

"Seven years in the basement," he said. "A family that pretends I died. A career I torched with my own report. I have spent my whole adult life paying down debts that weren't even mine, Cy, because that's what my name means. Vessic. Do you know what it translates to, in the old tongue? *Keeper of accounts.* My ancestors named themselves for this exact job and then committed the greatest accounting fraud in history. Let me finish it. Let me be the last entry in their ledger and the first honest one."

"No."

"You haven't even—"

"No," she said again, and now her voice did change, not louder but lower, the way water gets heavier before it freezes. "Because you're doing the thing the guild does, Sef. You're converting a person into a payment. You've decided the story ends with a transaction and you're shopping for the currency, and you've picked yourself because you think you're worth less than me."

"That's not—"

"Isn't it? Count it honestly. You think your seven bad years outweigh my nine erased ones. You think being disowned costs more than what I did." She stepped closer, and the coal-light caught the scars on her palm, old ones, older than last night's burn. "Do you know why I started erasing myself? Not the guild training. Before that. I was nineteen, and my mother was dying, and the temple offered a trade: her debt forgiven in exchange for a daughter entered into service. I signed. And then I discovered what signing meant — that my name went into ledgers owned by men who thought a signed name was a spent coin. So I learned to take it back. Page by page. Ledger by ledger. Nine years, Sef. I have unmade myself ten thousand times, and every time it worked, and every time it worked I became slightly less sure that there was anyone underneath the erasures. That's my accounting. That's what I bring to the scale. Don't tell me yours weighs more."

The vault was silent except for the faint, vast breathing of the dragon above them — a sound less like lungs than like a building settling.

"You want to know the worst part?" Cyrus said. "Your plan would work on me. If the spell accepted any willing hand, I'd let you do it. I'd hate myself forever, but I'd let you, because watching someone else pay is easier than paying, and I am so tired, Sef. I have been tired since I was nineteen. But the spell doesn't accept you. The spell checked its books and found my name, and I have spent nine years learning that the books always win."

"Then we change the books."

"We *are* the books." She laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. "Don't you see? That's the whole horror of it. The founders wrote themselves into the magic and the magic wrote itself into us. Your name, my name, the king's name, all of it just entries in a document that keeps extending itself. The prophecy isn't predicting tonight. It's *drafting* tonight. And the only power we have — the only one — is what goes in the next line."

"Which is why it should be my name in the next line."

"Which is why," she said, "it will be mine, whether either of us likes it or not."

* * *

They fought for another hour. Sef would remember it afterward not as a fight but as an audit — two accountants going through a shared life line by line, disputing entries, refusing to round anything down.

He accused her of stealing his agency. She pointed out he'd stolen his own first, following her in secret, deciding his guilt privately instead of sharing it. He said that wasn't the same. She said everything was the same; that was the entire lesson of the ledgers; concealment was just theft with better manners.

He told her about the night on the road through Ashfall when she'd slept sitting up against a fence post, coal cupped in her hands like something rescued from a fire, and how he'd sat awake watching her and understood that he would follow her into any ending. She told him that was exactly why he couldn't have this one — because love that follows isn't the same as love that decides, and this decision belonged to the name in the book.

He said, finally, the true thing, the thing under all the arguments: "I can't watch you die. I did my grieving already. Seven years in the dark taught me exactly how much of me survives a loss, and there isn't enough left to survive you."

And she said, equally finally, the true thing back: "And I can't let you die for a crime you didn't commit, in a ritual that would kill you and leave the crown loose anyway. Your sacrifice wouldn't save anyone. It would just be one more entry the guild gets to harvest. You'd be giving them exactly what they've always taken — a Vessic, spent quietly, off the books."

The coal pulsed. Once. Like a heartbeat that had been waiting for them to finish.

They stood in the silence after, two people who had run out of arguments and found each other standing on the other side of them, closer than the fighting had suggested.

"I hate this," Sef said.

"I know."

"There has to be another clause. Another annotation. Some founder's footnote we haven't decoded."

"Probably," Cyrus said. "And we have maybe a day to find it, because the enforcers are coming. The coal leaves a trail, Sef. Warmth that never dims is a beacon, and the guild has been following beacons for three hundred years." She was already repacking the cipher, her hands steady, her voice gone practical, which he knew was how she carried fear. "So tonight we read everything. Every page. And tomorrow—"

Above them, muffled by stone and earth, a bell began to ring in the city. Not the palace bell. Not a temple bell. The gate bell — the one Vaelmoor rang for arrivals it hadn't invited.

Cyrus stopped packing.

"That's early," Sef said. "You said a day."

"I said *maybe* a day." She looked up at the ceiling, at the chain, at the sound tolling down through the stone like water finding cracks. "They didn't wait for the coal. They didn't need to. They always knew where the prophecy would end, Sef. It was their invoice. They've come to collect."

The bell rang a third time, and beneath the vault, in the deep dark, the dragon opened one eye the color of banked fire — and Sef, keeper of accounts, understood with perfect clarity that the final audit had arrived ahead of schedule.
