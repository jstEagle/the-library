## 13. The Guild Arrives

They came through the Ashgate at dusk, and Vaelmoor did not notice.

That was the first terrible thing Cyrus understood about them: the city had been built not to see them. Six riders in gray traveling cloaks, no sigils, no banners, no horses shod any louder than the traffic required. They moved down the Processional like water finding the low places, and shopkeepers stepped aside without knowing why, and a beggar held out his bowl to one of them and then stared at his own hand as if it had betrayed him.

Cyrus watched from the archive window, the undying coal cupped in her palm inside its iron lantern, and felt its warmth pulse once—like a heartbeat answering another.

"They know," she said.

Sef stood beside her, arms crossed against the cold that had crept into even this room. "The coal?"

"The coal. The prophecy. All of it. They've known every step we'd take since before either of us was born." She set the lantern on the table between them and watched the light hold steady, refusing to gutter even when the draft from the window should have killed it. "I used to think my talent for invisibility was mine. A gift I stole, one ledger line at a time. But Sef—the guild *let* me be invisible. For nine years. Why would an organization that tracks everything lose track of a scribe who erases herself?"

"Because you were useful," Sef said slowly. "Unnoticed hands do unnoticed work."

"Because I was *stored*." She heard the truth of it leave her mouth and hated how clean it sounded. "Like the coal. Like the fire under the vault. You keep assets in the dark until the invoice comes due."

The bell over the palace rang again—the fourth time since dawn—and below in the courtyard, servants were lighting lamps against nothing. It wasn't yet full dark. But the light in Vaelmoor had been thinning for weeks, hearths burning lower, bread rising less, wine going flat in sealed casks. The stolen warmth was running out, and everyone could feel it the way animals feel weather, and no one could name it because no one remembered what they'd lost.

"Master Corvane is among them," Sef said. He said it quietly, the way you name a disease. "I saw him at the Ashgate inn. He didn't see me." A pause. "Or he saw me and decided I no longer existed. Both are possible."

"Corvane." Cyrus turned the name over. Her overseer of six years, the man whose attention she had spent nearly a decade dodging, whose mild eyes had followed her through the scriptorium like a hook following a fish. She had assumed he never noticed her. Now she understood: he had noticed her perfectly, continuously, the way a lender notices collateral. "He signed my debt-forgiveness himself. Did you know that? Ink still wet when he handed me the commission. I thought it was carelessness."

"It was a receipt," Sef said. "He was marking you paid-for."

* * *

They came for her at the ninth bell, and they did not knock.

The archive door simply opened, the way doors open in dreams, and Master Corvane walked through it with two enforcers behind him and a third filling the doorway like a shadow that had learned posture. Up close, the guildmasters' gray cloaks were wrong in a way Cyrus couldn't immediately name, until she could: they cast no dust in the lamplight. Nothing clung to them. Moths didn't land on wool like that. The world itself declined to touch them.

"Cyrus Amberdale," Corvane said, and her whole body flinched, because in nine years almost no one had said her whole name aloud. Names were dangerous things in the temple. Names were handles. "You've been busy."

"I've been working," she said. "As commissioned."

"Yes. Verify the crown. A simple task." Corvane's voice was exactly as she remembered it: dry, patient, the voice of a man reading someone else's accounts aloud. He crossed the archive room unhurried, trailing a finger along the shelf-ends, and stopped before the prophecy book where it lay open on her worktable—to the page with her name. To the page with tonight's date. "And yet here you sit, surrounded by documents you were never authorized to read, in the company of a man the guild struck from record four years ago." He glanced back at Sef without turning his head. "Hello, Sefren. You're looking well. That must be strange for you."

Sef's jaw tightened but he said nothing, and Cyrus saw why: saying anything meant admitting he could be spoken to, and being spoken to by the guild was the first step of being owned by it again.

"You tracked us," she said.

"We don't track, child. We *invoice*." Corvane drew out a chair and sat, folding his hands, and the gesture was so familiar from a thousand scriptorium afternoons that her stomach turned. This was the man who had taught her the guild's double-entry shorthand. This was the man who had praised her small, neat hand. "Let us dispense with theater. You have read the founders' cipher. You have seen the dragon. You know what the crown does and what we harvest and why Vaelmoor has been warm for a hundred years while the rest of the road goes cold. None of that is a revelation to us. It is, in fact, our business plan."

"Your business plan," Cyrus repeated. "You're feeding a city off a dying king."

"We're feeding a city, period." For the first time, something moved behind Corvane's mild eyes—not anger, but the flicker of a man who has explained arithmetic to the slow a thousand times and grown tired of their gasping. "Do you know what happens if the binding fails and the crown burns out unchecked? I'll tell you, because you clearly haven't done the projections. The overflow stops. Vaelmoor cools in a season. Forty thousand people discover what Ashfall villages already know about winters. Riots. Famine. A century of careful stewardship, undone by one scribe with a conscience." He leaned forward. "We are not villains, Miss Amberdale. We are accountants. Villains want the world to burn; we merely decline to let it freeze."

"And the king?" Cyrus kept her voice level with effort. "The man in the crown who begged me—in a voice like dry paper—to find out what he was before? Is he part of your business plan?"

"He is capital," Corvane said, without hesitation, without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Appreciating, historically. Depreciating, currently. Which brings us to terms."

* * *

He laid them out the way you lay out contract clauses, because that's what they were.

"Option one." Corvane produced a document from his cloak and placed it on the table between the lantern and the prophecy book—a single page, densely written, with a blank space at the bottom where a name should go. "Renew the binding as written. Your blood, your hand, your name in the old formula, exactly as your ancestors composed it. The crown continues. The harvest continues. Vaelmoor stays warm. And in exchange—" he tapped the page "—the guild forgives every ledger entry that ever bore your hand. Nine years of erased debts, restored and cancelled. A stipend for life. A residence in the temple proper, not the scriptorium cells. And the thing I suspect you actually want, though you'd die before admitting it: your name entered in the guild rolls. Permanent record. Honored contributor. Never invisible again—by our choice rather than your curse."

Cyrus looked at the blank space. It seemed to breathe.

"You're offering me everything I spent nine years running from," she said.

"I'm offering you everything you spent nine years pretending you didn't want," Corvane corrected gently. "Invisibility was never your desire, child. It was your wound. We're proposing to heal it."

*And that was the trap of it*, she thought, and hated him for the precision. Because he was right. Some traitor corner of her heart had gone quiet and warm at the words *permanent record*. Nine years of scraping herself out of ledgers, and here was a man offering to write her in with golden ink.

"Option two," Corvane continued, before she could drown in it.

"There's always an option two."

"There is always a worse option two. That's how options work." He folded his hands again. "Refuse, and we execute erasure. Full erasure. Not the partial measure we applied to Sefren—that was, frankly, a warning shot, and I apologize for the distress it caused him. No. Complete erasure. Every ledger, every registry, every parish roll, every memory. Your mother's few remaining recollections of you—gone. The archivist who shares your cause will look at you and see a stranger. The dragon beneath this city will speak your family name to empty air. You will not be killed, Miss Amberdale. Killing leaves a body, and bodies leave grief, and grief leaves records. You will simply never have been. Even you will not remember you. There will be no terror in it, because there will be no *you* in it to be terrified."

The lamp flame bent. Or perhaps the room did.

Cyrus became aware, distantly, that her hands were shaking, and pressed them flat against the table to stop them, and found the wood cold as river ice. The undying coal pulsed in its lantern, faster now, like a heart hearing its own name threatened.

"That's not justice," she managed. "That's not even punishment. That's—"

"Bookkeeping," Corvane said. "The gentlest thing we do. Punishment implies the person continues to exist to suffer. Erasure is mercy, technically. Ask anyone we've erased." His mouth twitched, the ghost of a joke only accountants would find funny. "Ah. You can't."

Sef made a sound then, low in his throat, and took one step forward, and the enforcer by the door shifted weight with a whisper of cloth that should not have made sound at all. Corvane raised one finger without looking, and the enforcer stilled.

"Sefren," Corvane said. "Your line maintained the binding for six generations out of duty. Then your grandfather got clever, and your father got silent, and you got *sentimental*. I understand the lineage of failure intimately—it's in our files. But consider: help us bring her in peacefully, and we restore your name. Fully. Your family's service acknowledged at last. Your mother would know you again."

Cyrus watched her friend stand very still, and knew exactly what that offer cost him to refuse, because she had seen his face when he told her about the erasure—about walking through the temple where everyone he loved looked through him like glass. She watched him weigh it, honestly, painfully, the way he weighed everything.

"No," Sef said finally. "But thank you for doing the math where I could watch."

"Sentimental to the last entry," Corvane sighed. "Very well."

* * *

They gave her until midnight.

"One night," Corvane said, gathering his terrible page from the table. "Not mercy—procedure. The rite requires darkness, and the vault requires preparation, and frankly, child, I want you to *sit with it*. Most assets choose correctly by morning. The ones who don't—" he paused at the door, and for one moment the accountant's mask slipped and Cyrus saw something underneath that might have been genuine regret, or might have been the memory of someone else who'd refused, long ago, whose name no one remembered. "The ones who don't make our work simpler. One signature or none. Good evening, Miss Amberdale."

The door closed. The enforcers' presence lingered like smoke.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The coal burned on, steady, indifferent to the human arithmetic around it. Down beneath the city, faintly, Cyrus thought she felt the dragon breathing—slow, banked, waiting—and the thought arrived unbidden: *it knows my family name. If they erase me, it speaks my name to no one, forever.*

"Sef," she said at last. "When they erased you. Partially. What did it feel like? From inside?"

He was quiet a long moment. "Like being a rumor about yourself," he said. "Like walking around with proof of your own existence in your pocket and no one willing to read it." He looked up. "Why?"

"Because I need to know what I'm choosing between." She pulled the founders' cipher across the table, and beneath it, the pages of marginalia, and the sketch of the rite she'd drafted in the hours before the guild came. Her hands had stopped shaking. That frightened her more than the shaking had. "Corvane offered me a permanent record, and some part of me wanted it, and I need to understand why before midnight. Because if I renew their binding, I get written into their books forever—and if I refuse, I get written out of everyone's memory. Either way, I stop belonging to myself."

"There's a third option," Sef said quietly.

"Corvane doesn't believe in third options. Third options don't balance."

"Third options don't balance," Sef agreed. "That's what makes them worth trying." He laid his hand flat on the founders' cipher, over the clause they'd found three nights ago, the one written small and desperate in the margin of the original spell: *the crown must be bound to a name freely given.* "It says *freely given*, Cyrus. Not freely given to the guild. Not given under invoice. Given away." He met her eyes. "Midnight isn't their deadline. It's ours."

Outside the archive window, the false dark of Vaelmoor settled over a city that had forgotten how to be warm on its own. Somewhere below, chains shifted against ancient scale. And somewhere above, in the gray space between the bells, the guild waited for an answer it had already priced.

Cyrus picked up her pen.

She did not yet write her name. But she dipped the nib in prophecy-ink—the ink that remembers, the ink that regrows, the ink that had named her a hundred years before her birth—and began, carefully, in her small neat hand, to draft a new clause.

Corvane had given her until midnight.

She intended to use every minute of it writing terms of her own.
