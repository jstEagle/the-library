## 15. Reading the Crown

The deepest shelf of the royal archive did not appear on any inventory.

Cyrus knew this because she had spent three nights making an inventory. It was the kind of work that soothed her—cataloguing, cross-referencing, the quiet arithmetic of a collection—and it was also the perfect cover for what she was actually doing, which was hunting a book that Vaelmoor's own archivists had either never known about or had been paid very well to forget.

Sef stood watch at the archive door, pretending to be the disgraced scholar he technically still was, arguing amiably with a night clerk about cataloguing conventions. His voice carried just far enough to tell her the clerk was engaged and not suspicious. Cyrus pulled the iron ring at the back of the third shelf from the end—the one shaped like a closed eye—and the shelf swung inward on a hinge that hadn't been oiled in a century but moved anyway, smooth as guilt.

Cold air breathed out of the dark. Not the cold of stone. The cold of *withheld* warmth, like standing near a hearth that had been deliberately banked.

She went down seven steps into a room no bigger than her cell at the temple, lit by a single lamp whose oil smelled faintly of smoke and something older underneath. One desk. One chair. And on a lectern built for exactly one book, under glass gone cloudy with age:

*The Oath and Its Consequences.*

No author. No date. The guild's seal stamped on the cover—but not the guild's current seal. This was the founding mark, the one Sef had shown her scratched into the margin of the prophecy: a crown above an open hand. *What is given freely cannot be taken.*

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Nine years of erasing herself had taught her one useful skill: how to be calm in rooms where the truth was dangerous. She lifted the glass. She opened the book.

And the ink recognized her.

It happened the way it had happened with the prophecy—a shiver through the page, letters leaning toward her fingers like plants toward light. The Amberdale blood remembered this book. Or this book remembered the Amberdale blood. By now Cyrus could no longer pretend there was much difference.

She read.

* * *

The first pages were written in a hand she almost recognized. Formal, careful, the letters of someone trained in a scriptorium—which meant they were written by one of her ancestors, or by someone taught by them.

*We are asked to record what was done to King Aldric the First,* the opening line ran, *and we will do it honestly, though honesty has already been declared a treason.*

Cyrus sat down. Her legs decided the matter without consulting her.

Aldric the First. Every child in Vaelmoor learned his name the way children everywhere learn foundation myths: the wise king who made the Pact of Flame, who bargained with the last dragon to warm the city through the Long Winter, who promised—

She stopped. She went back two lines. She read the promise again.

*He swore before witnesses: the fire would be shared, not owned. The dragon's warmth for the city's loyalty, freely given and freely renewed each year at the Rite of Open Hands. He swore it on his name, which in those days was understood to be a thing of substance. A king's name could anchor a covenant. A king's name could also break one.*

The next page was in different ink. Darker. Hurried.

*He broke it.*

Not dramatically. That was the detail that made Cyrus's stomach turn—not treachery in the storybook sense, no dagger in the dark, but something smaller and more familiar. Aldric had discovered, three winters into the Pact, that the fire could be rationed. That scarcity was a tool. That a city warmed only where the crown permitted warmth would obey the crown in all things. He began selling warmth back to his own people. District by district. Hearth by hearth. The first ledger of impossible trades, Cyrus thought. Before there was a guild, there was a king who treated a covenant as an inventory.

*When confronted by the dragon's emissary,* the account continued, *the king refused to renew the oath. He said—and here the emissary's memory serves us verbatim—"A name is a coin. I have simply learned its exchange rate."*

The dragon had come itself, then. The book described it without awe, which somehow made it worse: *It filled the throne hall from floor to vaulting. It did not burn anything. It looked at him for the length of nine bells, and then it spoke the broken oath aloud, and the sound of a king's own promise spoken back to him by a creature he had wronged broke something in the air between them.*

The oath, denied renewal, had not simply expired. An oath sworn on a name was a living contract, and contracts do not die; they curdle. The broken promise condensed. It took weight, and shape, and hunger. The founders' account used the word *crown* for the first time on the next page, and Cyrus understood with a lurch that the word had not originally meant a circlet of metal at all.

*A crown is what remains when a king keeps everything and gives nothing. It is the residue of hoarded power. It needed a form because hunger always takes a form eventually, and the form it took was gold, because gold is what greed looks like when it cools.*

They forged it—her ancestors forged it, the Amberdales and their co-conspirators, Sef's line among them—as a containment. A vessel to hold the oath-residue so it could not spread into the city and take root in other ambitions. They bound it to Aldric himself, the only mind it matched, the man whose broken word it was. And they wrote the binding spell that had held for four hundred years.

*But understand what we did, and do not forgive us for it:* the founder wrote, and Cyrus heard the words as if spoken across a table. *We did not destroy it. We could not. You cannot destroy a broken oath; you can only transfer it. So we gave it a host, and we told ourselves the host was guilty and therefore deserving, and we let the crown begin its slow meal.*

* * *

Cyrus turned pages until her fingers ached. The mechanism revealed itself the way rot reveals itself under plaster—one wall at a time, each more load-bearing than the last.

The crown ate its host slowly. Not flesh—memory first, then will, then selfhood, drawn out over decades like sap from a tapped tree. What it drained did not vanish. It *overflowed*. Warmth, vitality, the raw life of a human soul poured continuously into a vessel that could not contain it—and the founders, practical people, horrified and clever and already compromised, had built taps.

Dragon-fire, Cyrus thought. There was no dragon-fire. There had barely been a dragon for centuries. What the guild harvested and sold as flame was rendered humanity. The king's rendered humanity. Every hearth in Vaelmoor burned on the slow combustion of a prisoner who could not die and could not remember why he wanted to.

And every few generations, the host thinned past usefulness. The overflow dwindled. The city's warmth guttered. And the binding spell required—

She found the page. She had known she would find it, and knowing did not soften the blow.

*Renewal requires a willing heir. The crown hungers for consent; it was born from its absence, and it can only feed where it is invited. Each renewal has therefore demanded a soul that opens itself freely—an heir who says yes. The guild has arranged such yeses four times in four hundred years. In every case, the willing heir was told they were performing a sacred duty. In no case were they told they would not survive the first winter of the binding.*

Four names followed. Four entries in a ledger of sacrifices dressed up as honors, each with a date, each with a small annotation in the guild's tidy administrative hand. *Compensation disbursed to family.* *Memory of service suppressed per standard agreement.* *No further correspondence.*

Cyrus read the fourth name twice.

Amberdale.

Her great-aunt. The woman her mother never spoke of, whose portrait had been removed from the family hall before Cyrus was born, whose absence had been explained with the vague phrase *she went into service.* Service. Four hundred years of institutional euphemism, and it had reached into her own house and hollowed out a woman and called it service.

She did not cry. She noted, distantly, that she should probably cry, and that some part of her had been spent too thoroughly for tears, and that this too was perhaps a thing the guild had done—to her, to everyone, teaching whole generations to metabolize grief into paperwork.

Then she kept reading, because hope, when it finally arrives, looks less like comfort and more like a diagram.

* * *

The final section was newer. The ink was fresher, the handwriting changed—several hands, in fact, across maybe eighty years, archivists adding observations the way doctors add notes to a chart. One entry stopped her cold.

*The host weakens faster than projected. Current king shows signs of terminal thinning. Overflow insufficient to warm even the palace quarter by next winter. Renewal overdue by eleven years. The guild delays, reasons unclear. Recommendation: identify candidate lineage. Note: the original binding-line (Amberdale) produces candidates of unusual resonance. The prophecy confirms. Recommend acquisition of subject when surfaced.*

Acquisition. Subject. She was a requisition. Somewhere in the guild's ledgers, filed between a shadow sold twice and a memory pawned, there was an entry describing *her*, with a column for cost and a column for expected yield, and somewhere a guildmaster had signed off on it with the same unhurried pen that signed off on coal shipments.

But beneath that entry, in a different hand entirely—shakier, hurried, pressed hard enough to tear the paper—someone had written a correction:

*The crown does not need a victim. Read the founders again. ALL FOUR TIMES it fed, it fed because the heir believed the lie. Consent extracted by deception is still consent in the eyes of the binding. But the founders' text is clear: freely given means FREELY GIVEN. Informed, chosen, named aloud by the giver's own hand. No heir has ever been told the truth. Which means the binding has never once been renewed as designed. Which means—*

The sentence stopped there. Below it, in the same desperate hand: *If anyone reads this, the way out is real. The crown can be unbound without a new sacrifice. It must be offered a willing heir AND REFUSED BY THAT HEIR, publicly, on the record, in the presence of the old host. Refusal breaks the hunger. I am certain. I am going to try. If I do not write again—*

No one had written again.

Cyrus sat in the cold little room with the lamp burning its strange smoky oil and let the shape of it settle over her. Hope acquiring a shape, Sef had said once—or no, she had said it herself, or thought it; the distinction hardly mattered anymore. Here was the shape. Not a clean escape, nothing so kind. A refusal performed correctly, witnessed, recorded—by a scribe, naturally, by *her*—could starve the crown's claim. The king could die free. The city could—

The city could go cold. Unless the dragon-fire returned. Unless the last dragon, chained beneath the guild's original vault, was freed to resume the pact Aldric had broken—shared this time, or not at all. Everything connected. The refusal, the dragon, the rite, the truth told publicly enough that no lie could grow back over it. It was a plan with a dozen moving parts and a hundred ways to die, and it was, for the first time since the temple scriptorium, an actual plan rather than a countdown.

She copied everything. Of course she copied everything—it was reflex, it was sacrament, it was the one prayer she had ever believed in. Page after page, her scribe's hand steady while the rest of her shook. She wrote the founder's warning. She wrote the dead archivist's correction. She wrote the four names of the sacrificed heirs, and she wrote them large, because names erased were how all of this had worked, and names written were the only counter-weapon she had ever trusted.

On the last leaf, tucked against the back board where only a thorough reader would find it, she found one more thing.

A list. Recent ink. Five names, four struck through with a single clean line each—the neat bureaucratic stroke of the guild's erasure protocol. People who had read this book and tried to act. Archivists, probably. Scholars. Someone's daughter, someone's friend. The fifth name was half-formed, the pen having lifted mid-letter.

The sixth line was blank.

Waiting.

Cyrus looked at that blank line for a long time. Then she dipped her pen, and beneath the five struck-through names she did not sign hers. Instead she wrote, in her smallest, clearest hand, a single sentence:

*The next reader will not be erased. I am coming back with witnesses.*

Above her, faintly, through the ceiling, she heard the night clerk raise his voice—not alarmed, not yet, but sharpened by something. Footsteps crossed the archive floor. More than one set. Moving with the particular unhurried confidence of people who have never once needed to hurry.

Sef's answering voice came a moment later, pitched easy, buying seconds the way he bought everything now: generously, and against his own interest.

Cyrus closed the book, swung the false shelf shut, and slid the founder's most dangerous page—the archivist's correction, the refusal-clause, the way out—inside the lining of her coat, against her heart, where prophecy-ink and Amberdale blood could keep it safe.

The door handle turned.
