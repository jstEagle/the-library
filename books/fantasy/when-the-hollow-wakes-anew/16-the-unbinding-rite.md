## 16. The Unbinding Rite

The vault beneath the guild's original holdings smelled of cold iron and old smoke—the smell of a fire that had been asked to burn for someone else's comfort for a hundred years.

Cyrus knelt at the center of the circle she'd chalked into the stone floor, and the chalk wouldn't stay where she put it. It kept drifting, faintly, toward the dragon chained against the far wall, as if even her materials wanted to be closer to the last living warmth in Vaelmoor.

"Stop fidgeting," Sef said from the northern point of the circle. He held the founders' cipher open on his knees, weighted at the corners with four coins he'd pried from the guild's own offering box. "If you smudge the third line we start over, and I don't think any of us has another night in us."

"I'm not fidgeting." She was absolutely fidgeting. Her hands hadn't been still since sundown. "I'm checking my work."

"You've checked your work eleven times."

"The twelfth time is when it counts." She looked down at the page before her—not paper but vellum, peeled that afternoon from the back of the prophecy book itself, because the counter-spell had to be written on the same skin that carried the original binding. The Amberdale hand. The guild had spent nine years paying her to erase herself, and now every stroke she made was the most visible thing in the city.

The king stood at the southern point, and it was strange, Cyrus thought, how much stranger he was upright and unarmored than enthroned and crowned. Without the crown his head was just a head—thin hair, a scar over one ear, the ordinary geography of a man who had once been young. His eyes were the problem. They moved like eyes that were learning again, darting to the dragon, to Sef, to her, cataloguing everything with the hungry attention of someone who had spent a century watching the world through a keyhole.

"What was my name?" he asked. He'd asked it four times since they'd come down. Each time it sounded less like a question and more like a prayer being tested for structural integrity.

"We don't know yet," Cyrus said, for the fourth time. "The rite will give it back. That's what unbinding means. Everything the crown took goes home."

"And if there's nothing left to go home to?"

Sef looked up sharply. Cyrus made herself hold the king's gaze. "Then you'll be the first person in history to know exactly what you're owed," she said. "And I'll write it down. All of it. In a ledger no one can erase."

Something moved behind his eyes—not hope exactly, but the space where hope could stand if it wanted to.

* * *

They began at moonrise, because the cipher said the binding had been laid at moonrise and what was unwound must follow the same thread backward.

Cyrus read first. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, and the words were not in any language she knew, and yet her mouth shaped them without hesitation, the way a hand remembers a door it opened in childhood. *The founders' marginalia* had called this the Reversal of Account: not a destruction but a refund. Every year of stolen fire returned. Every name swallowed given back whole.

The dragon stirred against its chains.

It was smaller than she'd first believed—or rather, it had been made smaller, compressed by a century of siphoning until it was barely larger than an ox, its scales gone the grey of banked ash. But when the first line of the rite left Cyrus's mouth, color moved through those scales like dawn moving through a valley. Deep red. Old gold. The grey retreated.

"*Amberdale,*" the dragon said, and its voice was a hearth, was a harvest fire, was every warm room she'd ever been afraid to enter. "*You read like your grandmother.*"

"I never met my grandmother."

"No," the dragon said, and there was something in it that was almost amusement, almost grief. "*She read like you.*"

Sef took up the second voice, the answering part, the part his family had written and then sworn generations of silence to maintain. His voice cracked on the third phrase and he started over without apology, and Cyrus understood suddenly that he wasn't reading words—he was returning them, handing back an inheritance he'd spent his whole life believing was a debt.

The king spoke last. He had only one line, repeated at each turning of the rite, and it was not in the cipher. It was the sentence he'd begged her with in the archive, the sentence that had started all of this: *Tell me what I was before the crown.*

"That's not in the text," Sef had protested, hours ago.

"It doesn't have to be," Cyrus had said. "The crown ate a man who was asking that question when it found him. We feed it back the same meal. Symmetry is the whole grammar of binding-spells—you taught me that."

"I taught you that?"

"You will," she'd said. "In about forty years, probably."

Now the three voices braided together in the vault, and the air began to do something air should not do. It thickened. It remembered. Cyrus felt the past pressing against the present like water against a dam—not visions, exactly, but weights. A wedding feast. A coronation where the crowd's cheering had a wrong note in it. A council chamber where men in guild robes smiled too smoothly and used the word *stewardship*. A king alone at a window saying *tell me what I was* to a room with no one in it, and a crown on a cushion across the room, listening.

"Oh," Cyrus said, mid-line, because understanding arrived like a blade between ribs. "Oh, it didn't take him. He *asked*."

"Keep reading," Sef said grimly.

"He was lonely. He was doubting himself, and the crown was forged from a broken oath, and it *hungered for a willing heir*, and he—" Her voice caught. "He thought it would make him certain. He thought it would make him good at being king."

"Cyrus." Sef's voice had gone very quiet. "Keep. Reading."

She kept reading.

* * *

The crown came off at the ninth turning.

There was no drama to it. That was the horror of it, afterward—the way the worst things arrive looking like paperwork. The king simply reached up, both hands, the way a tired woman lifts a heavy jar from a high shelf, and lifted it free, and set it on the stone floor between them, and the rite's final line closed around the moment like a lock clicking shut.

For three heartbeats, nothing happened.

Then the king sat down. Not collapsed—sat, deliberately, like a man setting down a load he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy. He put his palms flat on the floor. He breathed. And Cyrus watched a century leave him—not dying, not yet, but *draining*, the grey going out of his skin, the tremor going out of his hands, his face rearranging itself into the face of a man rather than the face of an office.

"Aldric," he whispered. "My name is Aldric. Aldric Vael." He laughed—a small, cracked, astonished sound. "I hated hawking. I lied about it for years. I told everyone I loved it. Why did I—why does that matter? Why do I remember *that*?"

"Because it's yours," Cyrus said, and her own voice broke on it. "All of it's yours. Even the stupid parts. Especially the stupid parts."

The dragon's chains fell away.

Not broke—fell, each link opening like a hand releasing, and the last dragon of Vaelmoor rose on legs that shook, and shook, and then held, and the light coming off it was not fire anymore but something older and gentler, the light of a thing remembering its own size.

"*Warmth,*" the dragon said, and turned toward the ceiling, toward the city above. "*I can give it back. I can—*" And then it stopped, and its great head swung around, and Cyrus saw its pupils contract to slits.

"What?" Sef said. "What's wrong?"

The crown moved.

It did not roll. It did not slide. It *moved*, the way a caterpillar moves, the way a slow tide moves—lifting one edge of itself, questing, setting down, lifting again—and it turned, with terrible deliberation, away from the dragon, away from the king, away from the circle that had contained it, and began to crawl toward the vault stairs.

"No," Cyrus said. "No, the rite finished, the binding is—"

"The binding needed a name," Sef said, and his face had gone the color of the ash-scales the dragon had shed. "The original binding. It anchored the crown to *him*. We cut the anchor. We didn't replace it. The rite didn't destroy the hunger, Cyrus—it just made the crown *unemployed*."

Above them, muffled by thirty feet of stone, the bells of Vaelmoor began to ring. Not the hour. Not an alarm. The *celebration* peal—the one the city rang at festivals, at coronations, at the lighting of the winter fires. Someone up there had felt the warmth return through their hearths and drawn the only conclusion joy knew how to draw.

The whole city was awake. The whole city was happy. The whole city was full of open doors and open hearts and minds unguarded for the first time in living memory.

The crown reached the bottom of the stairs and began, patiently, to climb.

"It can't wear all of them," Cyrus said, standing, snatching up the vellum, the cipher, the coins. "It's one crown. It can only—"

"It doesn't need to wear them," the dragon said. Its voice had dropped to the register of a warning given long ago and ignored. "It needs to *touch* them. One mind at a time. An ambitious man. A grieving widow. A child who wants to matter. It will go door to door, Cyrus Amberdale, and it will find the hungriest mind on every street, and it will whisper, and the whispering will be enough. You've seen what a century of one crown did to one king. Now imagine it distributed."

Aldric Vael got to his feet. He swayed. He was seventy years old in a body that had aged a day at a time for a hundred years, and he planted himself between the crown and the stairs anyway, arms out, ridiculous and magnificent.

"I'll hold it here," he said. "I know its weight. I can—"

"You'll die," Sef said flatly.

"I've been dead for a century, archivist. This would just be paperwork."

But the crown didn't test him. It flowed around his feet like water around a stone—patient, unhurried, *knowing*—and kept climbing, and Cyrus understood with a clarity that felt like falling that it had already chosen its first host. Somewhere above them, in a city ringing its bells, someone was standing at a window feeling the warmth come back and thinking: *this happened because of me. This is mine. I deserve this.*

The crown always found the willing heir. That was the mechanism. That had always been the mechanism.

"Sef." Cyrus was already running for the side passage, the one the coal had shown her, the one that came up inside the palace. "Get the dragon to the hearth-lines—it can flood the city with clean fire, drown the crown's whispering, buy us time. Aldric—stay with Sef, keep the vault sealed behind you."

"And you?" Sef shouted after her.

She took the stairs three at a time, vellum crushed against her chest, the ink on her fingers still wet, still *hers*.

"I'm going to finish the job," she called back. "The rite needs a name freely given. And I know exactly whose name fits."

Behind her, below her, the last dragon roared—a sound like a library burning, like a debt being called—and the crown went up into the singing dark of Vaelmoor, hunting.
