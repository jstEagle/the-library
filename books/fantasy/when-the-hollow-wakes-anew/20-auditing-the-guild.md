## 20. Auditing the Guild

I have spent my whole life among ledgers, so I should not have been surprised that justice, when it finally came, looked like an audit.

But I was surprised. I had imagined fire, or trials, or the guildmasters dragged through Vaelmoor's streets in chains while the crowd threw the ashes of everything they'd stolen. What I got instead was a long table in the restored council hall, three guildmasters seated on one side of it with their hands folded like men at their own funerals, and Cyrus on the other side with a stack of books so tall it needed its own chair.

The dragon was there too. That was the part no one had planned for.

She had insisted on attending—not in the vault, not chained, but curled in the great hearth of the council hall, which had been cold for a century and now roared like the sun's own kitchen. Her head rested on her forearms, one eye open, amber and patient. The magistrates had argued about whether a dragon could legally witness proceedings. The dragon had settled the argument by pointing out, in a voice like a landslide learning manners, that she was the oldest living creditor in the room.

Nobody argued after that.

* * *

The morning began with oaths, because Vaelmoor loved its oaths the way other cities loved bread. Each magistrate swore on the restored charter. Each guildmaster swore—this was the delicious part—on the Guild of Impossible Things' own founding contract, the one they had never let anyone else read, the one whose marginalia Cyrus had spent three nights deciphering in the royal archive.

They didn't know what they were swearing on. That was the trap, and Cyrus had built it out of nothing but patience and a scribe's eye for fine print.

"You understand," Magistrate Corren said, adjusting her spectacles, "that this proceeding is bound by contract-law as much as testimony law. Anything entered into the record becomes binding."

"We understand," said Master Halveth, the senior guildmaster, his voice smooth as cooled wax. "We have honored our obligations for two hundred years."

Cyrus opened the first ledger.

I want to describe what happened next properly, because I watched it from the gallery and I will be telling this story for the rest of my life. She read aloud. Not accusations—numbers. Dates. Entries. A shadow sold twice to two different buyers, both of whom still believed they owned it. A memory pawned by a grieving mother and never returned, though the redemption price had been paid in full eleven years ago. Four hundred hearth-coals drawn from the dragon's fire and sold to villages that were told they came from "the temple reserve." And beneath each entry, in the founders' cipher, the true accounting: what the guild had actually received, what it had actually paid, and the difference—the difference that had built their tower, funded their enforcers, bought their silence.

Every number she read became real the moment it touched the record. That was the contract-magic working. The guildmasters had sworn to honor every debt entered into the council's book, and now the debts were entering, one line at a time, spoken in a voice that did not shake even once.

Halveth's composure lasted until the third ledger. Then he tried the only move left to him: he claimed forgery.

"These books are copies," he said. "Anyone can write numbers in a book."

"True," Cyrus said. She turned the page toward him. "So let's check them against your originals."

And here is where I must confess my small part, my one contribution to the morning beyond standing beside her: I had them. Three days earlier, while Cyrus performed the unbinding rite and the crown sought its heir through the streets of a tearing city, I had walked into the guild's Vaelmoor chapter house wearing a scribe's robes and carrying a writ signed by a king who was, at that moment, dying peacefully in his chamber—and therefore unable to contradict anything written under his seal. The clerks handed me the vault keys because the paperwork was flawless. I am a disgraced archivist. Flawless paperwork is the one thing I have always been able to produce.

The originals matched. Line for line. Cipher for cipher.

Halveth sat back down.

* * *

It took six hours. I want to be honest about that, because the songs will make it seem swift and terrible, and it was neither. It was slow. It was arithmetic. A woman named Odellis came forward to claim the memory of her son's face, pawned when grief made her desperate, and the contract-magic required the guild to return it—which meant Master Veyn, who held it in a locket of black glass, had to unlock the thing in open court and pour eleven lost years back into a weeping stranger's mind while everyone watched.

Odellis remembered her son's face and laughed, right there, mid-sob, because he'd had a gap between his teeth and she'd forgotten she'd ever found it charming. The hall went quiet in a way I cannot describe. Not solemn. Full. Like a room inhaling.

Then the next entry. And the next.

The shadow sold twice was harder. Both owners presented claims; the contract could not give one shadow to two people. So the guild was ordered to refund both purchases at compound interest across forty years—a sum that would have bankrupted a lesser institution—and the shadow itself was released to walk free, whatever that means for a shadow. I choose to believe it means something good.

By late afternoon the pile of settled debts stood taller than the pile of unsettled ones, and the guildmasters had stopped objecting, because every objection they raised created a new entry, and every new entry bound them further. They had built a machine that turned words into obligations, and Cyrus had simply learned to drive it.

"The dragon-fire," Magistrate Corren said at last. "Four centuries of it. How do you calculate restitution for four hundred years of stolen warmth?"

"You don't," said the dragon, from the hearth. "You calculate the interest."

Everyone turned. She lifted her head, and her voice filled the hall the way her fire had once filled the vault.

"They took my flame and called it theirs. They sold my warmth and kept the coin. I do not want coin. I want the ledger closed and the debt converted: let the guild's wealth warm the villages they froze. Let the tower come down and the stones go to rebuild the hearths of Ashfall road. Let every clerk who profited spend ten years keeping those hearths lit with their own hands." She paused, one amber eye sweeping the guildmasters like a slow sunrise. "Or, if they prefer, I can calculate the interest myself. I remember every coal. I have been counting."

Master Halveth, to his credit—or perhaps simply to his survival—did not hesitate long.

"The guild consents," he said.

* * *

There was one more item. Cyrus saved it for last, and I knew she would, because she had shown it to me the night before and asked me, quietly, whether it was too much.

It was a single page. The original prophecy. The one that had started everything—*the Amberdale hand, the hour of the hollow waking*—with her name now written into its final line in her own ink, glowing faintly whenever the light caught it wrong.

She laid it on the table between the guild and the council.

"This document has been in your custody for a century," she said. "Your founders read it. Your masters read it. You knew what was coming. You knew the crown was eating a man alive, and you knew the fire feeding your coffers was the overflow, and you did nothing, because the prophecy was good for business." She turned the page to face them. "The prophecy wasn't a warning. It was an invoice. And you've been paying it with someone else's life for a hundred years."

Halveth looked at the page for a long time. I watched his face do something complicated—watch the understanding arrive that the woman across the table had rewritten the document his entire institution was founded on, and that the rewrite was legal, binding, and final.

"What do you want for it?" he asked.

"Nothing," Cyrus said. "That's the point. It's already paid."

She withdrew the page, rolled it, and did not sell it, donate it, or trade it. She gave it to the city archive, into the public record, where anyone could read it and no one could own it. The first impossible thing the guild had ever encountered that it couldn't put a price on.

I thought Halveth might break there. He didn't. He simply signed the final settlement, and his hand was steady, and somehow that was worse than if it hadn't been. Men like him survive anything, including justice. Especially justice done politely, in daylight, with witnesses.

The magistrates ordered the guild dissolved as a trading body, its charter voided, its remaining assets transferred to a restoration fund administered by the council and audited annually by—here Cyrus allowed herself one smile—"an independent archivist."

They meant me. I felt my ears go hot. Nine years ago they erased me from the rolls; now they were writing me back in at the top of a new one. I said yes before anyone could ask twice.

* * *

Afterward, the hall emptied slowly. The dragon banked her fire down to embers and went back to sleep beneath the temple, where the apprentices had already begun leaving offerings of roasted goat and, inexplicably, poetry. The magistrates departed in twos and threes, arguing happily about jurisdiction. Odellis stayed a long time, just sitting, remembering her son.

Cyrus and I walked out together into the evening, down the steps of the council hall into a street that smelled, for the first time in living memory, of woodsmoke and baking bread instead of cold stone. Every hearth in Vaelmoor was lit. Children were running through the lamplight, and the lamps themselves burned warmer than they had any right to, fed by fire that remembered where it came from.

Neither of us said much. There's a particular silence that follows a day when every number finally comes due, and we walked inside it like people walking inside a bell after it stops ringing.

At the corner by the fountain, Cyrus stopped.

"You know what bothers me," she said. "Not one of them apologized. Not once, all day."

"No," I said. "They won't. Apology isn't in the contract-magic. Only payment."

She considered that. "Is that enough?"

I thought about Odellis laughing through her tears about the gap-toothed boy. I thought about the shadow walking free. I thought about a king buried yesterday under a plain stone with his real name on it, human at last.

"It's a start," I said. "Justice is bookkeeping, Cy. Nobody throws festivals for balanced columns. But every warm house tonight is a line item, and every line item is a debt somebody can never pretend didn't exist. That's not nothing. That's the only thing that lasts."

She leaned against the fountain's edge, tired all the way through, and for a moment she was just a woman who had spent nine years trying to disappear and had failed so completely that a city now knew her name.

"There's one entry left," she said quietly. "In the prophecy. The final line. Have you read what comes after my name?"

I had. I'd copied that page twice, once for the guild and once for the archive, and both times my pen had hesitated over the same few words that followed hers—words in the founders' cipher that had only become legible after the rite, as if the ink itself had been waiting for the crown to go quiet before it finished its sentence.

"I've read it," I said.

"And?"

I looked up the street, at the lamplight, at the children, at the smoke rising straight and warm from a thousand chimneys. Then I told her what the last line said, and her face changed, and somewhere far below us, beneath the temple floor, something ancient shifted in its sleep and began, very softly, to dream aloud.
