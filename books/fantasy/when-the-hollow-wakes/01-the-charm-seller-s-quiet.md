## 1. The Charm Seller's Quiet

The morning Vaelmoor woke to find its gods still silent—like every other morning for thirty years—Gideon Winterbourne was already at her stall, arguing with a cat.

"Not that fish," she murmured under her breath, lips barely moving, the way she'd learned to speak without speaking. "That one's for the Widow Marsh. You get the tail end when she's done."

The cat—a gray tom with a torn ear and the moral convictions of a tax collector—stared at her with flat green eyes and did not blink.

"I'm serious."

He blinked. Slowly. The feline equivalent of *we'll see about that.*

Gideon sighed and turned back to her stall: three planks laid across two barrels in the shadow of the Tanners' Bridge, where the smell covered a multitude of sins and where, more importantly, nobody important ever looked. Luck-knots hung from the awning in rows of red cord, each one tied nine times, each one utterly meaningless, each one selling for a copper because people needed to believe *something* was watching over them, even if it was only a woman with cold fingers and a talent for knots.

Beside them sat her mended-bone charms: chicken bones snapped and rejoined with pine resin, painted white, sold as tokens of things made whole again. Broken bones knitting. Broken hearts mending. Broken luck reversing. Nobody in Vaelmoor could afford a priest anymore—not that there were priests—and so they bought chicken bones from a tired woman under a bridge and went away feeling, briefly, less alone.

It was, Gideon thought as she arranged her wares, a dishonest living. She just tried not to think too hard about which part of it was the lie.

* * *

Vaelmoor had been a different city once. Her grandmother used to say so, back when saying such things was merely foolish instead of fatal. There had been bells in the high towers that rang for no human occasion—rang for births, for storms coming, for the dead finding their way. There had been gods in the shrines, small local ones mostly, the kind who asked for bread and milk and gave back safe roads and easy calvings. There had been speakers like her grandmother, people who talked to beasts and were thanked for it, consulted, respected.

Then came the Godfall, though nobody agreed on what had actually fallen or why. The old king's line ended in fire. The new king's line began with an Edict written in ash and enforced with rope: no magic, no worship of the old names, no speech with beasts, no charms, no blessings, no prayers answered by anything that wasn't the Crown—which was to say, nothing at all. The gods hadn't died, exactly. They'd just gone quiet, the way a house goes quiet after everyone in it has been taken away.

Thirty years of quiet. Long enough that most people had stopped flinching at empty shrines. Long enough that children born after the Edict thought luck-knots were folk craft and nothing more, which suited Gideon fine.

What didn't suit her fine was the informants. Every city had them, but Vaelmoor had perfected the breed. The witch-finders in their ash-gray coats were bad enough—all lantern-jawed certainty and ledgers—but the real danger wore aprons and smiled at you over fences. A neighbor who remembered your grandmother talking to geese. An apprentice who noticed your knots were always tied in nines. A beggar who'd sell your name for a hot meal and wouldn't even feel bad about it afterward, because hunger made honest men into informants and informants into survivors.

So Gideon Winterbourne had spent three years becoming invisible. Not magically—invisible the slow way. She kept her stall small. She kept her prices fair but never generous. She laughed at the right jokes, complained about the rain, and spoke to animals only in whispers, only in crowds, only when the wind was loud enough to eat the sound. She was, by every measure that mattered to the Crown, a charm-seller of no particular gift, a woman of no consequence, a person whose name would be forgotten the moment she left a room.

She'd gotten very good at it. Some mornings she worried she was getting too good.

* * *

Midmorning brought the customers.

First came the Widow Marsh, tiny and sharp-eyed, buying her weekly fish from the cart down the row and stopping at Gideon's stall out of ritual rather than need.

"Any luck today, dear?"

"There's always luck," Gideon said. "Question is whose." She handed over a fresh knot. "Tie it to the bedpost. Left side."

"Why the left side?"

"Because the right side is where you keep your grudges, and grudges strangle luck in its sleep."

The Widow Marsh cackled, paid her copper, and shuffled off delighted. It wasn't magic. But it was care, of a kind, and Gideon had come to believe care counted for something even when the gods weren't listening.

Then the cooper's boy, wanting a charm for his mother's bad hip. Then a dockhand who bought a mended-bone token before his shift on the rigging and didn't meet Gideon's eyes, because dockhands didn't like to admit they believed in things. Then a long dry stretch where nobody came at all, and Gideon re-tied fraying cords and listened to the city breathe.

And under the breathing, the other conversation. Always the other conversation.

The rats under the bridge were complaining about poison in the granary district—third cellar this month, said the big female with the scarred nose, and the grain man's boy is getting clever with traps. The pigeons on the Tanners' Bridge roof reported a wedding on Copper Lane and much spilled cake, which pigeons considered the highest form of news. And the gray tom, having failed to extract fish through negotiation, had settled onto the warm boards beside her stall like a sentry who'd decided the post was his and she was merely permitted to share it.

"You're crowding my customers," Gideon whispered.

*I'm attracting them,* the tom replied, in that way cats have—not words exactly, but meaning arriving whole, like warmth through a wall. *Old women buy more when I sit here. I've done the sums.*

"You've done no such thing."

*Copper. Fish. Copper buys fish.* He began washing one paw with tremendous dignity. *I am a business partner.*

"You're a freeloader with good timing."

*In this city,* the tom said, *those are the same thing.*

Gideon laughed—quietly, behind her hand, the way she laughed at everything—and felt the familiar ache underneath it. This was the part nobody warned you about, being what she was. Not the fear. Fear you got used to, the way you got used to cold water. It was this: having a hundred conversations a day and being able to keep none of them. Knowing the name of every rat in the Underveil and the griefs of every dog on Tanner Street, and having to fold all of it up small and hide it behind a face that said *nothing here, move along.* Being full to bursting with company and lonely as a lighthouse keeper anyway.

The tom bumped his head against her wrist. Cats always knew, somehow, even when they pretended not to.

"I'm all right," she told him, which was true the way a cracked bell is still a bell.

* * *

The trouble came at midday, as trouble preferred to do.

She heard it first from the horses—of course she did; the street heard everything through the horses first. Down Copper Lane, past the chandler's, a carter's mare had gone down between the shafts. Not the ordinary down of a beast resting wrong, but the bad kind: the wet, rattling breathing that meant colic turned deadly, or something torn inside. Gideon felt it in her own chest the way you feel a door slam in a house you grew up in.

By the time she reached the corner—drawn despite herself, telling herself she was only curious—the crowd had already gathered. Crowds always did. The carter knelt in the muck with his big hands pressed uselessly against the mare's heaving flank, and the horse's eye rolled white, and she was calling out—not words, horses don't have words, but meaning, raw and enormous:

*hurt hurt hurt dark coming help help help*

Gideon stood at the edge of the crowd with her hands in her pockets and her heart going like a hammer, and she knew—she knew with the absolute certainty of her own blood—what would fix it. Not surely. Not gently. But the old knowledge rose in her like water finding its level: walk to her, put your hands here, speak low, tell her where the pain is caught and how to let go of it. Half the time it worked. Her grandmother had pulled six horses back from that dark door in the years Gideon had known her, and every farmer within ten miles had loved her for it.

Every farmer, and then, in the end, someone had reported her. That was how the story ended. That was how all those stories ended now.

*help*, the mare called again, and her eye found Gideon through the crowd, the way animal eyes always found her, and held on. *you. you know.*

"I don't," Gideon whispered. "I'm sorry. I don't."

But the mare couldn't hear lies, and neither, in that moment, could Gideon herself.

A witch-finder was already pushing through the crowd—not for the horse, not yet, but because crowds drew them like gulls draw fish guts. Ash-gray coat, ledger under his arm, the flat patient face of a man whose profession was suspicion. He looked at the dying mare. He looked at the weeping carter. He looked, unhurried, at every face in the crowd, one by one, cataloguing.

Gideon stepped backward. Stepped again. Melted into the lane like water into sand, and walked away with her spine straight and her pace unhurried, while behind her the mare called her name—she didn't know how the horse knew her name, but she did, they always somehow did—

*Winterbourne, help, please*

—until the sound of it was swallowed by the noise of the city, and then swallowed by the walls Gideon kept around herself, and then swallowed by nothing at all.

She got four streets away before she stopped walking. She leaned against a wall in an alley that smelled of tallow and old rain, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth, and she breathed.

She had refused. Again. Third time in three years, and she could name every one: the ewe on the Dyer's slope, the child's canary, and now a carter's mare who had trusted her. Each time, the same arithmetic, quick and merciless as a moneylender: one life weighed against her own, and against the others—because if they took her, they'd take whoever had seen her do it, and whoever that one talked to, and the net spread outward from any single act of kindness until it caught half a street.

That was the Edict's real genius, she thought bitterly. Not the witch-finders. Not the ropes. It was making kindness itself into the crime, so that the punished and the saved were the same people, so that a woman couldn't kneel beside a dying animal without doing treason. They hadn't outlawed magic so much as they'd outlawed being useful with it, and usefulness was the soul of the thing.

"They'd hang me," she told the alley quietly. "You understand that, don't you? They'd hang me in the square and call it mercy."

The alley, being an alley, said nothing. But somewhere above her, on the rooftiles, a bird had landed—one of the black ones, a crow, and it was looking down at her with an attention that felt less like a bird's idle curiosity and more like a listener leaning in.

Gideon frowned at it. Crows watched everyone; that was crow nature. But this one watched her the way the mare had. Like it knew something.

"Shoo," she said.

The crow did not shoo. It tilted its head, and its voice—when it finally spoke, rough and strange, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years—was not the voice of any pigeon or sparrow or common crow reporting crumbs and cats. It was older than that. Deliberate.

*"The crown is hungry,"* it rasped.

Gideon went very still.

Around her, Vaelmoor carried on: carts and vendors, the chandler's boy shouting prices, the ordinary machinery of a city pretending it had never heard of gods. Somewhere down the lane, faintly, the carter's grief had gone quiet—one way or another.

The crow ruffled its scarred feathers and kept its eyes on hers, unblinking, waiting, as if it had been waiting a very long time and could wait considerably longer.

"Well," said Gideon Winterbourne, invisibility and all, "that's not something a normal bird says."
