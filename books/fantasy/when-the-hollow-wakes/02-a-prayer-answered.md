## 2. A Prayer Answered

Gideon Winterbourne had three rules for surviving Vaelmoor, and she recited them most mornings while knotting luck-charms at her stall in Tanners' Row.

One: never look a witch-finder in the eye, because they mistook eye contact for challenge and challenge for confession.

Two: never sell a charm that actually worked.

Three: never, under any circumstances, answer when the animals spoke first.

She was minding rule two with particular diligence that morning. The charm in her hands was a luck-knot of braided hemp and river-clay, and it was precisely as magical as a bootlace, which was to say not at all. But old Marta Copperkettle believed in it, and belief was the only magic left legal in the city, and Marta's arthritis responded to placebos almost as well as it would have to real craft.

"For the fishing boats," Gideon said, tying off the last loop. "Hang it by the till and don't let anyone else touch it."

"You're a treasure, girl." Marta pressed two coppers into her palm and leaned close, lowering her voice as though the cobbles themselves might inform on her. "You hear about the Hollow Shrine? Last night?"

"I hear about everything on this street eventually."

"The beggar woman. The one with the ruined hand — Sela, they call her, sleeps under the shrine eaves." Marta's eyes had gone bright and frightened, the way eyes did lately all over Vaelmoor. "Her hand's whole. Healed overnight, clean as a babe's."

Gideon kept her face pleasant and empty, the expression she'd spent three years polishing. "Old wounds do strange things. Bodies mend when they've a mind to."

"Not like this. Not *whole*." Marta crossed herself with the old sign, the one that predated the Edict, then glanced around guiltily for having done it. "They say she went to the Shrine and prayed. Actually prayed. Out loud. To nothing."

"To the hollow," Gideon corrected gently. "That's what everyone prays to now. An empty alcove where a god used to sit."

"Aye, well." Marta clutched her luck-knot. "Maybe it isn't empty anymore."

She shuffled off toward the fish market, leaving Gideon standing in the gray morning light with her heart doing something complicated and unwelcome against her ribs.

Because here was the thing Gideon hadn't told Marta, or anyone: she'd felt it happen.

* * *

It had been near midnight, and Gideon had been awake because the rats of Tanners' Row had been restless — not afraid, exactly, but *attentive*, which was worse. Rats were gossips and cowards by turns, but they were never attentive without reason. She'd lain listening to them through the floorboards, their voices a dry rustle at the edge of her mind the way other people heard wind.

*The stone is drinking,* one of them had said. *The old stone under the eaves. It is drinking something.*

And then — Gideon had no better word for it, though she'd turned the moment over forty times since — the city had *exhaled*. A pressure she hadn't known she was carrying had shifted, the way you only notice a weight when it moves. Something vast and slow had turned over in its sleep beneath Vaelmoor, and every animal within the walls had felt it, and so had she, deep in her marrow, like a second heartbeat waking up after years of stillness.

She'd sat bolt upright in bed with her hand pressed flat against her own sternum, counting. One heartbeat. Two. Hers, and underneath it, faint as a drum across a valley: something else.

*No,* she'd told herself. *You imagined it. You want it too much, and wanting makes liars of us all.*

But the rats had kept murmuring until dawn — *the stone is warm, the stone is drinking, the shining thing is turning* — and now Marta Copperkettle was telling her that a withered hand had bloomed back to life in the night, and Gideon's second heartbeat was keeping time against her ribs like an eager guest.

She wanted it. That was the shameful truth of it, the thing she'd have denied under torture. For eleven years — since the Godfall, since the Edict of Ash, since the witch-finders had burned the speaker-circles and silenced everything — she had been starving in a room full of food. Her gift was a language, and the Edict had outlawed conversation. Every day she swallowed the cats' chatter, the pigeons' complaints, the horses' sorrows, and said nothing, did nothing, was nobody. And if the gods were waking — if the silence were ending —

Then she could stop hiding.

And then, said the colder voice, the one that sounded uncomfortably like her grandmother, they will come for you with iron hooks and a bonfire, little Winterbourne, and your last word will be a scream.

Gideon tied off another bootlace-masquerading-as-a-luck-knot and tried very hard to think about nothing at all.

* * *

By midday, Tanners' Row had stopped being a market and started being a congregation.

They came in twos and threes at first, then in streams — the lame leaning on the halt, mothers hauling children, old men who hadn't walked past the tanneries in a decade. All of them drifting, sooner or later, toward the Hollow Shrine at the end of the row, where Sela the beggar woman sat holding up her healed hand like a lantern.

Gideon watched from behind her stall, selling nothing, hearing everything.

"It was the Waking Mother," insisted a dyer with blue-stained fingers. "She who keeps the loom of flesh. Of course it was her. Who else mends bodies?"

"There's no such person," snapped a salt merchant. "There's no *anyone*. The gods are dead, the Edict says so, and I'll thank you not to get us all ashed for saying different."

"Then explain the hand."

"Bodies mend."

"*Whole*, I tell you—"

Gideon's stall cat, a scarred tortoiseshell named Duchess whom Gideon fed in exchange for intelligence rather than affection, wound between the customers' ankles and reported in her usual tone of weary contempt.

*The whole city is mad,* Duchess observed. *The woman with the hand is telling everyone who'll listen. She's saying the hollow spoke back to her. She's saying it has a voice like warm bread.*

"What does it actually sound like?" Gideon murmured under her breath, lips barely moving.

*A door opening in a house that's been shut for years,* Duchess said, and then, with feline precision: *Why do you smell afraid? You smell like fear and honey. It's confusing.*

"I'm not afraid."

*Liar,* said the cat comfortably, and went to sleep in the sun.

By late afternoon the crowd at the Shrine had grown thick enough that Gideon could see it from her stall: dozens of people kneeling before the empty alcove, praying out loud, some of them weeping. A witch-finder stood at the row's end in his gray-and-iron coat, arms folded, watching with the patient expression of a man watching a kettle he intended to confiscate.

And through it all, threading under the human noise like a bass note, Gideon felt the second heartbeat. Stronger now. Or perhaps she was simply listening harder. It pulsed in time with nothing — not her blood, not the tide, not any rhythm a body ought to keep — and every time it swelled, the animals of the row lifted their heads and stared east, toward the palace on its hill, in perfect unison, like a field of flowers turning toward a sun that wasn't there yet.

Duchess opened one eye.

*They're all looking at the crown,* she said.

"I know," Gideon whispered. "I wish they wouldn't."

*So do I,* said the cat. *The last time everyone looked at the crown, my mother got burned.*

* * *

Sela came to find her at dusk.

Gideon knew her by sight, of course — everyone on the Row knew the beggar woman with the ruined hand, who'd slept under the Shrine eaves for six years and asked alms with the quiet dignity of someone who'd decided begging was honest work and refused to be ashamed of it. But Sela had never come to Gideon's stall before, and the way she approached now — hesitant, half-turned, like a deer deciding whether the meadow was safe — made Gideon's stomach drop.

"You're the charm-seller," Sela said. "Winterbourne."

"Luck-knots and mended-bone charms. Harmless as soup." Gideon smiled her empty smile. "Can I sell you something?"

Sela didn't smile back. She looked over her shoulder — at the witch-finder, at the gathering dusk, at the crowd still murmuring around the Shrine — and then leaned close.

"They told me you were one," she whispered. "The old ones, before they died, they said there was a family on this row with the old ears. The beast-ears."

Gideon's blood went cold and pleasant at once, dread and longing tangled together like rope. "I sell knots," she said, louder than necessary. "Hemp and clay. Nothing more."

"I'm not asking you to fix anything." Sela held up her healed hand — whole, unscarred, the fingers straight and pink and terrible. "It's already fixed. That's what frightens me. I prayed at the hollow because I had nothing left to lose, and something *answered*. And now every prayer in this city is going to be aimed at that empty alcove like arrows at a target, and I need to know—" her voice cracked, steadied— "I need to know what I woke up. Because it felt *hungry*, charm-seller. It felt grateful. And I've known men who were grateful like that, and none of them were safe."

For a long moment Gideon said nothing. The second heartbeat pulsed in her bones. Somewhere above them, on the rooftops, hundreds of birds sat facing east.

"If I were such a person," Gideon said carefully, "which I'm not, I'd tell you to pray somewhere else for a while. Quietly. To nothing in particular. And I'd tell you that gratitude is the most expensive gift there is, and the thing under the Shrine has been poor a long, long time."

Sela searched her face. Whatever she found there, it seemed to satisfy her, or at least to resign her. "Thank you," she said. "For the soup advice."

"Charm-soup," Gideon said. "Very harmless."

Sela pressed a copper into her hand — payment, Gideon realized, for a service neither of them would ever name — and walked away into the darkening row, her healed hand flexing open and closed at her side, like something newly born learning what fingers were for.

Gideon stood alone at her stall as the lamps came up along Tanners' Row, one by one, each flame a small act of defiance against the dark.

*Well?* said Duchess, materializing on the counter beside her. *Are we going to talk about the second heartbeat, or are we going to pretend it's indigestion again?*

"You knew?"

*I'm a cat. I know everything worth knowing.* Duchess groomed one paw with elaborate unconcern. *It's getting stronger. By the next full moon, every beast in this city will hear it plain. And so will whatever wears the gray coats.*

Gideon looked down the row, toward the Shrine, where the candlelight flickered over bowed heads and upturned faces. Toward the witch-finder, who hadn't moved all day, whose patience was a kind of promise. Toward the palace on its hill, dark against the stars, where something wore a king's body the way a thief wears a stolen coat.

Eleven years of silence, and in one night the world had cracked open, and everything she'd starved for was seeping through the gap — warm, and vast, and hungry, and hers, almost, nearly, if she dared.

She wanted it so badly her teeth ached.

She feared it so badly her hands shook.

Both things were true. Both things stayed true all the way home, all through her cold supper, all through the long dark hours while the rats below her floor murmured their gossip — *the stone is warm, the shining thing is turning, the crown is listening* — and somewhere across the sleeping city, a bell tolled once, though no ringer pulled the rope.

In the morning, when she opened her shutters, the notice-board at the corner of Tanners' Row bore fresh paper, the wax seal of the Office of Ash glinting red in the early light.

**BY ORDER OF THE CROWN:** reports of unlawful devotion will be investigated. Informants rewarded. The gods are dead. Let them stay buried.

And beneath the notice, on the cobblestones, in a scatter of black feathers that no one had swept away, a crow was watching her stall with one intelligent, scarred eye — and when their gazes met, it said, quite clearly, in a voice like gravel and old grief:

*"You feel it too. Don't you, little speaker?"*
