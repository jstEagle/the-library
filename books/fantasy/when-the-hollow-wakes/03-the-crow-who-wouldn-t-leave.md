## 3. The Crow Who Wouldn't Leave

The crow was waiting for her when she left the market.

Gideon noticed it the way you notice a splinter—small at first, then impossible to ignore. It sat on the eave of the tanner's shop with its head cocked at an angle that suggested either deep interest or a broken neck, and when she turned down Tanner's Row toward home, it lifted off and glided after her.

She told herself it was coincidence. Crows followed people. Crows followed carts, dogs, other crows, anything that might conceivably produce food. This one had probably watched her pack up her stall all afternoon and drawn the reasonable conclusion that a woman who sold luck-knots also carried bread.

Except she didn't carry bread. She carried charms, thread, a knife she wasn't licensed to own, and exactly four coppers, two of which were counterfeit.

She stopped at the corner by the chandler's and looked up.

The crow looked down.

It was an ugly thing, as crows went—not the sleek oil-black of the river birds but patched and ragged, with a scar running through the feathers of its left wing like someone had dragged it through a fire and it had mostly forgiven everyone involved. One eye had a pale rim to it, old damage healed crooked.

"Shoo," Gideon said quietly.

The crow did not shoo. The crow said, in a voice like gravel poured through a keyhole:

*"Hungry."*

Gideon's stomach dropped in a way that had nothing to do with dinner.

Now—here was the thing about speaking with beasts. She'd been doing it since before she could do arithmetic, and in thirty years she had learned precisely what animals sounded like. Cats thought in warmth and territory: *sun-here, mine-this, soft-soft-soft.* Rats thought in routes and crumbs. Dogs were a flood of *pack-pack-you-pack* that could drown you if you weren't careful. Even the horses, even the clever ones, even old Marrow at the livery who had opinions about everything—their speech was weather, not words. Feeling shaped into meaning. Never grammar.

This crow had used grammar.

*Hungry*, yes—but there had been something under it, a shape of intent, a wanting-to-be-understood that no animal bothered with because animals were never misunderstood by their own kind. Only a creature that had spent years trying to talk to things that couldn't hear it properly would learn to build sentences like bricks.

Gideon glanced up and down Tanner's Row. A boy was scrubbing the chandler's step. Two women argued cheerfully about the price of tallow. Nobody was watching her. Nobody was ever watching her—that was the whole architecture of her life, built brick by careful invisible brick over three years.

She walked on. The crow followed, flapping from shutter to signpost to washing line, keeping pace with the smugness of something that knew exactly what it was doing.

"I don't have food," she said under her breath.

*"Liar,"* said the crow.

Gideon stopped so abruptly that a man carrying a ladder nearly took out her shoulder. "Sorry—sorry," she muttered, sidestepping him, heart hammering, and ducked into the alley behind the cooper's where the light went greenish and nobody went if they could help it because of what the tanneries did to the groundwater.

The crow landed on a rain barrel six feet away and regarded her with one pale-rimmed eye.

"What are you?" she whispered.

*"Crow,"* said the crow, with tremendous dignity.

"That's not—" She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. Think. Think like a woman whose survival depended on being unremarkable. A talking bird was a death sentence with feathers. If anyone heard—if a witch-finder heard—there would be no luck-knots to sell her way out of it. The Edict of Ash didn't have categories for degrees of crime. Magic was magic. Ash was ash.

"Go away," she said firmly. "Find someone else. Someone stupid."

*"You're the only one who listens,"* said the crow, and there was something in it—not begging, nothing so soft—but a flat, tired certainty, the voice of a creature that had made this argument before, to other ears, and lost.

Gideon looked at the scarred wing. At the pale eye. At the general air of a thing that had survived things.

"No," she said, and meant it, and walked home without looking back, which was the first lie of the evening, because she looked back four times.

* * *

Her rooms were above a dyer's shop off Cordwainer Lane—a single room, a cot, a stove that smoked if you looked at it wrong, and a window that faced the canal wall, which meant privacy, which was worth more than the view, which was fortunate since the view was a wall.

She climbed the outside stair, checked the street both ways out of habit, let herself in, barred the door, and sat on the cot to eat half a loaf and think.

The beggar woman's hand.

That was what she should be thinking about, and the crow was making it difficult. Three days ago, at the Hollow Shrine, old Maren—with her fingers curled inward like dead ferns for as long as Gideon had known her—had prayed aloud, ugly and desperate, not caring who heard: *I know You're gone, I know it's foolish, but please, please, if anything is left—*

And in the morning Maren's hand had opened like a fist unclenching after years. Whole. Brown-knuckled and working. Gideon had seen it herself, had held Maren's fingers, had felt the bones knitting true beneath skin that still remembered being ruined.

The city had gone strange since. People prayed louder. People prayed quieter, in doorways, glancing over their shoulders—terrified hope, hope terrified, both wearing the same face. And underneath Gideon's ribs, in the place where her gift lived coiled and quiet, something had begun to stir. Not her gift. Something older that her gift seemed to be leaning toward, the way flowers lean at a window. Like a second heartbeat, slow and vast and patient, several streets away or several miles under the world, she couldn't tell which.

She wanted it. That was the shameful part, the part she kept folding smaller and smaller and stuffing down. Thirty years of silence, and now power moved in the world again like spring water under frost, and some traitor part of her sang toward it.

And every singing bone she owned said: *this is how they catch you.*

A knock against the window.

Gideon froze.

Another knock. Insistent. Rhythmic. *Knock-knock-knock. Knock.*

She crossed the room and pulled back the curtain and there, clinging to the canal-side ledge outside her window, three floors up, was the crow.

"How," she said, opening the casement, "did you find my window."

*"Followed your smell,"* the crow said, hopping inside with zero respect for boundaries. *"Bergamot and lies."*

"Bergamot and— I do not lie."

*"You lied to me in the alley. You have bread. I can smell the bread."*

Gideon closed the window. Drew the curtain. Stood in her own small room with a fire-scarred crow on her table, and felt three years of careful invisibility develop a crack straight down the middle.

"Listen to me very carefully," she said, kneeling so they were eye to eye. "If anyone hears you speak—if any person, anywhere, hears a single word come out of you—they will burn us both. Do you understand? There are men with white armbands and iron hooks who get paid by the head. You cannot talk in front of people. Ever."

The crow considered this. *"Then we talk here,"* it said.

"*We?*"

*"You feed me. I stay. We talk here."* It hopped twice, closer. *"My name is Bramble."*

"Crows don't have names."

*"Crows don't talk, either, and yet."*

Gideon sat back on her heels. Outside, somewhere down the lane, a watchman called the hour—two bells past dusk, the good dark coming on. In the stove the last coal ticked. She looked at this impossible, raggedy, presumptuous bird and felt something she hadn't felt in a long time, and it took her a moment to name it because it had been so thoroughly starved: company.

"Where did you come from, Bramble?"

The pale-rimmed eye fixed on her. For a moment the flippancy drained out of the creature entirely, and what was left underneath was old. Old like the stones of the Hollow Shrine.

*"From before,"* Bramble said. *"Before the Edict. Before the ash. When the gods still answered and the speakers still spoke and a witch named Ysolt Winterbourne kept me fed and scolded and loved, and then one day the sky broke and she didn't come back."*

The name went through Gideon like a hook through the gills.

Winterbourne.

Her name. Her grandmother's name—the grandmother who'd vanished from family story like a stone dropped in deep water, leaving only ripples of *she went away, child, don't ask.* The grandmother whose charm-book Gideon kept hidden under a floorboard and had never once dared show another living soul.

"You knew my grandmother," Gideon said. Her voice came out strange.

*"Knew her? Girl, I nested in her hair."* The crow shuffled its ragged wing. *"Thirty years I've been listening for the bloodline. Thirty years of hearing nothing but cats complaining about the rain. Then three days ago the whole city's beasts went mad at once—every rat in Vaelmoor screaming the same word—and I smelled bergamot on the wind and I thought: no. Couldn't be. And then I saw you give a luck-knot to a child for free and lie about the price afterward, and I thought: oh, *yes*. Couldn't be anyone else."*

"Every rat screaming what word?"

Bramble went still. When he spoke again, the humor was gone, and his voice was low, and it filled the little room the way cold fills a room when the wind finds the gap under the door.

*"'Waking.'"*

The second heartbeat under Gideon's ribs turned over in its sleep.

"The crown is hungry," Bramble said. "That's why I came. That's why I've been following you since the shrine, you stubborn, frightened, generous woman. The crown is hungry, and it has started to eat, and you are the only one in this city who can hear the menu."

* * *

They argued about it for most of an hour, in whispers, while Gideon pretended to mend a hem and Bramble ate the heel of her loaf with the manners of a small feathered aristocrat.

The argument was simple. Gideon said: no. Whatever was happening—at the palace, in the walls, under the world—it was happening to other people. Serah Vane's problem, the priests' problem, the Crown's problem, the rats' problem. Gideon Winterbourne's problem was staying alive, staying unnoticed, and getting enough sleep. She had a system. The system worked. The system did not include talking crows or waking gods or grandmothers' ghosts.

Bramble said: *"The system is a coffin with better lighting."*

Which was rude, and accurate, and Gideon threw a crust at him for it.

He caught it midair, naturally.

In the morning—she told herself, hanging the mended hem by the stove, banking the coals, performing the whole ritual of a woman going sensibly to bed—in the morning she would open the window and put him out and go back to being nobody. That was the plan. It was a good plan. She'd survived on plans like it for three years; they fit her the way her mother's coat had never quite fit, too big in the shoulders, but hers.

She woke before dawn to scratching at the shutter.

Bramble had gotten out onto the sill somehow—he'd found the gap where the frame warped, she'd seal it later—and he was pecking at the glass, urgent, insistent, and when she stumbled over and cracked the casement he thrust his head in, feathers puffed against the cold, and said:

*"Notices. They're posting notices. On the shrine doors, the guildhalls, the market posts. You need to see them before the crowd does. Get dressed. Wear the grey hood, not the blue one—you look like a widow in mourning in the blue one, and widows get sympathy, and sympathy gets you remembered."*

"It's not light yet," Gideon said.

*"Precisely. Empty streets. Come on."*

She should have shut the window on him. She knew that. Every instinct trained into her over three years of careful survival said *shut the window, bar the door, be nobody until the danger passes.*

Instead she put on the grey hood.

The notices were already up by the time they reached Cordwainer Square, pasted in rows on the notice boards and the shrine pillars, the paste still wet and shining in the lantern-light. Gideon read the nearest one twice, because the first time her eyes refused to cooperate.

BY ORDER OF THE CROWN AND THE OFFICE OF ASH:

Let it be known that reports of unlawful healing, prophecy, and wonder-working have increased within the city bounds. Such reports are crimes against the Edict, whether the deed be done by witchcraft, prayer, or fraud. Any citizen receiving or witnessing such a wonder is bound to report it within one day, to the Office of Ash, under penalty of law.

Furthermore: the bounty on confirmed practitioners is raised to forty crowns. Informants will be compensated. Informants will be protected. Silence is complicity.

Forty crowns. Gideon did the arithmetic without meaning to. Forty crowns was four years of luck-knots. Forty crowns was a new life in some coastal town with a different name. Forty crowns was every neighbor, every customer, every friendly face on Cordwainer Lane suddenly weighing her worth in coin.

"They're scared," she murmured. "Why post this unless they're scared?"

*"Because it's working,"* Bramble said from her shoulder—he'd ridden there openly, and no one was awake to see, and she hadn't the heart to make him hide. He nodded his scarred head toward the shrine doors across the square, where fresh candles burned in every niche, more than she'd seen in years, wax pooling and guttering, the little flames reflected in the dark windows like a congregation of stars.

"People are praying again," he said. "All of them. All at once. To whatever answered poor Maren. And prayers, girl, are not free."

"What do you mean, not free?"

Bramble's pale-rimmed eye caught the candlelight and held it, and for a moment he looked less like a scruffy market-bird and more like something that had watched an age end.

*"I mean,"* he said, *"that thirty years ago I watched a god eat a city that loved it. And the loving was the trap. The crown is hungry, Gideon Winterbourne. And somebody just set out a feast."*

Across the square, unseen in the pre-dawn dark between two guildhalls, a man in a white armband finished nailing up the last notice, stepped back, and looked directly—at her? at the crow? at nothing?—for one long moment.

Then he walked away, and Gideon stood very still among the candles, and understood that the system, the good plan, the careful invisible life—

was already over.
