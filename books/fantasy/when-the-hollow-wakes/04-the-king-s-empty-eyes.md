## 4. The King's Empty Eyes

Serah Vane had been summoned to the royal apartments eleven times in six years, and each time the summons came at an hour no respectable physician kept. Tonight it was half past two in the morning, and the rain was coming down on the palace of Vaelmoor like the sky had a grudge against slate roofs.

She dressed quickly and without complaint, which was itself a kind of discipline. Six years ago she would have complained. Six years ago she'd been Physician Royal, with a chain of office and a staff of nine and a standing invitation to argue anatomy with the Surgeon-General over brandy. Then old King Maren had died in his sleep — quite naturally, quite peacefully, quite inconveniently for everyone who'd wanted him to sign the succession papers first — and his son Aldric had ascended the throne, and within a year Aldric had begun to change.

And within two years of *that*, someone had decided that the physician who kept writing *no discernible cause* in her reports was more trouble than she was worth. The chain of office went back. The staff of nine became a staff of none. She was given a small office near the mews that smelled of hawk droppings and was told, in the gentlest possible language, that her services remained *available to the Crown as needed*.

As needed, it turned out, meant: when the king did something that frightened the servants.

Tonight it had been the candles. Or rather, the lack of them. The night watchman had found the king seated in the dark of his private chapel, forty unlit candles arranged around him in a perfect ring, and the king had said — and this was the part that made the steward's hands shake as he relayed it — *"They burn better on the inside."*

So here she was, at half past two, crossing the rain-slicked gallery with her medical bag and her professional face, past guards who wouldn't meet her eyes.

"Evening, m'lord," she said to the nearest one, mostly to be contrary.

He flinched. She noted it. Physicians are trained to note things; it is the whole trade. You note the color under the fingernails and the way the patient holds the left arm and whether the mother in the corner has been crying or praying, because the body lies constantly but everything around it tells the truth.

The guard told her the truth by not looking at her. Everyone in this palace had stopped looking at the king, and now they were extending the courtesy to anyone associated with him.

* * *

The royal bedchamber was warm — too warm, the fires banked high though it was the tail end of winter — and it smelled wrong. Not foul. Wrong the way a room smells after a storm, that faint green-metallic edge of the air having been *used*. Serah set her bag down on the table and made herself look at the king properly, which was more than anyone else in the building seemed willing to do.

King Aldric Vael sat by the window with the rain behind him. He was thirty-four years old and looked, depending on the light, anywhere between twenty-five and sixty. His hair had gone from brown to that strange pale ash-color that no barber could explain and no dye would take. He was still handsome, in the way a statue is handsome, which is to say: you admired the workmanship and felt vaguely guilty about it.

"Your Majesty," Serah said, and bowed. "I'm told there was some trouble with candles."

"They burn better on the inside."

"Yes. So I hear." She came closer, unhurried. "May I examine you? It's late, I know. I'll be quick."

He turned his head to look at her, and here was the thing — the thing she had been circling for six years and never once managed to say aloud, even to herself, even alone, even in the safety of her own skull:

When King Aldric looked at you, nothing looked back.

Not madness. She knew madness. Madness was *busy*. Madness filled the eyes like floodwater fills a cellar, churning, debris-ridden, full of terrible life. Serah had examined madmen in Bedlam Row during her training, and the mad were always *present*, even when they were present somewhere impossible — a battlefield twenty years gone, a conversation with their dead mother. There was always a person home, however badly the house was burning.

Aldric's eyes held no fire, no floodwater, no person. They held *patience*. That was the only word she'd ever found for it, and she hated the word, because patience implied waiting, and waiting implied something coming, and every time he turned those gray, depthless eyes on her she felt like a mouse who had realized the owl wasn't hunting yet — that the owl was simply watching, storing her up, saving her for later.

"You may examine me," the king said, and smiled.

The smile was perfect. That was the worst discovery of her career: the smile was *perfect*. Whoever — whatever — wore this face had studied human expression the way a foreign spy studies a language. Every component correct. Mouth corners raised, eyes crinkled exactly the right degree, duration precisely calibrated. And all of it assembled just slightly out of order inside, like a clock rebuilt by someone who'd only had the parts described to them.

Serah took his hand. The skin was cool but not corpse-cool. Pulse slow and strong, steady as a mill wheel. She pressed her fingers beneath his jaw.

"How is your appetite, Your Majesty?"

"Growing." A pause, delicate as a surgeon's knot. "I find I am hungry all the time lately. For so many things."

"And sleep?"

"I don't sleep. I listen." His head tilted, very slightly, toward the window. Toward the city below, spread out in the rain, ten thousand roofs and ten thousand sleeping people. "There is so much to hear, if one attends. All those little hearts. All that... *wanting*."

Serah wrote *sleep disturbance* in her notebook, because her hand needed something to do besides shake.

She worked through the examination with the flat competence that had once earned her a chair at the College of Physicians before they remembered women weren't supposed to have chairs. Pupils: equal, sluggish to light in a way that didn't match any lesion she knew. Tongue: clean. Heart: slow, regular, and — this was new — she kept her stethoscope pressed to his chest longer than strictly necessary, because she could have sworn the rhythm between beats wasn't silence. It was too soft to name. Like a word spoken just under hearing. Like a congregation murmuring in a distant room.

"Your Majesty," she said carefully, "do you ever feel as though there is someone else in your thoughts? Another voice?"

The king considered this with what appeared to be genuine, courteous interest — a scholar being asked about his specialty.

"There is no voice," he said at last. "That is what you're hoping to hear, isn't it? That I am ill. That there is a thing inside me we might cut out or bleed away, and then the man returns." He smiled again, that flawless, hollow smile. "You keep looking for the man, physician. You've been looking for six years. Isn't it time you asked yourself why you haven't found him?"

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere below, a dog began to bark, then stopped abruptly, as if corrected.

"The man is tired," Aldric said softly. "The man rests. And in his absence, I keep his kingdom warm for him. I am *very good* to his kingdom. Ask them. Ask how loved they feel." He rose, and moved to the window, and looked down at Vaelmoor with something that in a human face she would have called tenderness. "They pray, you know. Even now. Even with the gods silent and the Edict and all their little grey laws. They kneel at the Hollow Shrine and they ask for help, because wanting help is the one prayer they can't legislate away." His hand drifted up and touched the cold glass. "Every prayer is a knock on a door. And I am learning, physician, slowly, patiently, *deliciously*, how to answer."

Serah closed her notebook. Her fingers were steady. She was proud of that later, in the way you're proud of small survivals.

"I'll prescribe a tonic for restlessness," she heard herself say. "Chamomile, valerian. Take it before dawn."

"I will take whatever you give me," the king agreed pleasantly. "It changes nothing, but I know it comforts you to try, and I do so want my physicians comfortable."

* * *

The corridor outside was empty except for one person: Chancellor Hesse Morvane, standing beneath a lamp in his dove-grey robes, hands folded over his belly, wearing the expression of a man who has been waiting for a slow kettle.

"Physician Vane." He inclined his head. "Late night."

"Chancellor." She matched his courtesy ounce for ounce. "You're up early."

"I find I rarely sleep these days. Troubled times." He fell into step beside her, and they walked together down the gallery, two middle-aged insomniacs in a haunted house. "How did you find His Majesty?"

"Much the same. I've prescribed a tonic."

"Mm." They passed a tapestry of the Godfall — the old gods tumbling from heaven in silver flame, woven three generations ago by artisans who clearly enjoyed their work far too much. "You know, Serah — I may call you Serah? — I've read all eleven of your reports. Every one. *No discernible cause. No progression consistent with known pathology. Recommend continued observation.* Very thorough. Very honest." He stopped walking, and so, since stopping seemed politic, did she. "Do you know what I admire most about you? Your honesty. It's practically medieval."

"Thank you," said Serah, who had been called worse things by better men, and waited.

"The court," Morvane said, gazing up at the woven falling gods, "has reached certain... understandings. About His Majesty's condition. We are practical people, Serah. The realm is fed. The borders hold. The witch-finders keep the peace, such as it is. Whatever sits on that throne, it does not make war, it does not tax beyond bearing, and it has, if anything, a certain *genial* quality of late." He turned to her at last, and his eyes were kind, and that was the most frightening thing in the entire palace, kinder than the king's smile. "The understanding is simple. We serve the crown. We do not inquire what wears it. The last two people who inquired are farming turnips in the Fenmark, and I'd call that a merciful outcome, wouldn't you?"

"I would call it," Serah said, "a diagnosis deferred."

Morvane sighed, genuinely, wearily. "Yes. That's what I said. Six years ago. Word for word, almost — you'd think we'd rehearsed it." He patted her arm twice, gently, the way you'd pat a horse you liked but intended to sell. "Get some sleep, Serah. You look pale. And do write up tonight's visit. Full detail. Lord Castellan wants the reports going forward delivered directly to his office rather than the archive. Efficiency, you understand."

She understood. She understood perfectly. The reports would go to the thing's stewards now, and her name would go on a list, and somewhere a file with her name on it would grow a second page.

"Goodnight, Chancellor."

"Goodnight, physician. Oh—" he said, turning back, lamp-light pooling on his pleasant round face, "—one more thing. The guards will see you home. New policy. For the protection of Crown personnel, walking the city at odd hours. You'll hardly notice them."

She noticed them. Of course she noticed them. Two of them, trailing her through the rain-slick streets of pre-dawn Vaelmoor at a distance of thirty paces, wearing the livery of the palace watch instead of the black of the witch-finders, as if the livery were the disguise rather than the opposite. They were polite about it, at least. One of them sneezed when she paused under an awning, and she almost — almost — offered him a peppermint from her bag, because she had spent her whole life unable to stop being a physician even when the patient was following her.

She stood a while on the bridge over the Gullwater, watching the grey water carry the lamplight apart, and let herself finally think the thought she had been refusing for six years, eleven examinations, and one perfect, hollow smile.

*The court knows.*

Not suspects. Not fears. *Knows.* And has weighed the thing on the throne against the inconvenience of replacing it, and chosen the thing, and called the choice peace. And the city below her — the city where she'd delivered four hundred babies and set three thousand bones and buried her husband and learned to drink coffee black enough to strip varnish — that city knelt at the Hollow Shrine and prayed into the dark, and the dark had begun, patiently, deliciously, to pray back.

Serah Vane was fifty-one years old. She had survived disgrace, widowhood, and the College of Physicians. She was not brave, particularly. But she had spent six years writing *no discernible cause* in reports that went into an archive, and she was done.

She went home, pulled her father's old field ledger from beneath the floorboards, and began, by candlelight, to copy out eleven years of the king's examinations — dates, symptoms, the phases of the moon beside each entry, because her hand had started marking those months ago without her permission and she'd only just admitted to herself why.

Beside the eleventh entry she wrote a single line in her tight, unsentimental script:

*Whatever answers the prayers is keeping a schedule. Find the schedule.*

Outside her window, unseen, a rat paused on the sill in the rain, whiskers trembling, and looked at her for a long moment with bright, considering eyes — and ran, fast, east along the wall, toward the canals, toward the Underveil, carrying what it had seen the way rats carry everything:

carefully, and to whoever fed it first.
