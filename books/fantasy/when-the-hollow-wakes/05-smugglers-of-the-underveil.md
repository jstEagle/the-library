## 5. Smugglers of the Underveil

The morning the witch-finders came, Gideon was arguing with a rat about cheese.

"You had a whole rind yesterday," she said under her breath, crouched behind her stall in the Tanners' Row market. "I watched you eat it. You don't get to look wounded at me."

The rat—she'd named him Provost, because he had the self-important strut of a city official—groomed his whiskers with an air of profound injustice.

"Fine. One crust. And you tell the others I'm not a bakery."

Provost took the crust and vanished beneath a barrel of tanning brine, and Gideon straightened up to face another morning of selling harmless things to frightened people. Her stall was small even by Tanners' Row standards: a plank table, a striped awning gone grey from three years of rain, and a careful arrangement of charms that could not, by any stretch of the Edict of Ash, be called magic. Luck-knots braided from horsehair. Mended-bone pendants for bad joints. Little cloth sachets of lavender and salt labeled *for restful sleep*, which worked exactly as well as lavender and salt ought to work, which was to say not at all, but which people bought anyway because they needed to believe something was looking out for them.

Three years since the Godfall. Three years since the gods went silent and the king signed the Edict and the bonfires started. Three years of being very good at nothing.

"Morning, Giddy." Old Marta from the dye-works shuffled past with her basket, pressing a warm heel of bread into Gideon's hand without breaking stride. "You look thin."

"I always look thin."

"You look *thinner*. Eat."

Gideon ate. That was the thing about Vaelmoor's poor—they fed each other because no one else would, and they kept their voices down, and they didn't ask why the woman at the charm stall sometimes paused mid-sentence as though listening to something no one else could hear.

She was pausing now, in fact, because a sparrow had just landed on her awning pole and said, in the flat, unbothered way sparrows say everything:

*Black coats. Coming.*

Gideon's hands went still over her luck-knots.

"How many?" she breathed.

*Four. Dogs. Down the row.* The sparrow cocked its head. *Fast.*

Bramble was not there—Bramble had not been anywhere since the strange crow had vanished two days ago after delivering his cryptic warning about hungry crowns, and Gideon had told herself she wasn't worried, and had been lying. But the sparrow's report was enough. Witch-finders did not come to Tanners' Row for luck-knots. They came because someone had talked.

Someone had talked about *her*.

She began dismantling the stall with the practiced speed of three years of drills she'd hoped never to use. Charms into the false-bottomed basket. Awning folded. Table—leave the table, tables were innocent. She flipped her signboard to the little painted legend she'd prepared long ago: *CLOSED ON ACCOUNT OF FUNERALS*, which discouraged questions better than anything else she'd tried.

"Gideon?"

Tamsin was at the next stall over, arranging her own wares—candles, mostly, and small carved animals that were genuinely lovely and genuinely just carvings. Tamsin was nineteen, gap-toothed, loud, and possessed of a gift for flame that she expressed exclusively by lighting her own candles with a snap of her fingers when no one was looking, because she said it made her feel rich. Gideon had told her a hundred times to stop. Tamsin never listened. That was Tamsin all over: a candle burning at both ends and grinning about it.

"Go home," Gideon said quietly.

"What? It's barely nine."

"Tamsin." She gripped her friend's wrist. "Witch-finders. Four of them. Dogs. They're coming down the row right now, and if they find your candles lit—"

"They won't be lit, I'm not stupid—"

"They'll take you for the *carvings*, Tam. A girl who whittles wolves this good? They'll invent a reason. They don't need a real one."

Something flickered across Tamsin's freckled face—not fear exactly, but the particular stubborn brightness she wore instead of fear, like armor made of jokes. "Come with me, then. My mum's got the cellar off Cordwainer Lane, nobody—"

"I can't lead them to your mother's house."

"Then where?"

The dogs were barking now, two streets over, and voices were rising along the row—the particular hush-shout of a market realizing what was happening, baskets clutched, eyes down. Gideon looked at Tamsin's stall, at the unlit candles, at the little carved wolf with its absurdly noble expression, and felt the cold slide of certainty in her gut.

They weren't coming for her carvings.

*Black coat at your neighbor's stall,* the sparrow reported helpfully. *Pointing.*

"Tamsin," Gideon whispered. "Listen to me. Whatever happens—you never saw me. You don't know my name. You bought lavender once. That's all."

"Gideon, what—"

And then the black coats arrived.

There were four, as promised, and two hounds on heavy chains, and a captain with a face like a closed ledger. The market contracted around them the way flesh contracts around a splinter. The captain walked past six stalls without slowing and stopped directly in front of Tamsin's, and Gideon understood, with sick clarity, that the informant hadn't described Gideon Winterbourne at all.

They'd described the girl who snapped her fingers and made fire.

"Witch-finder work," the captain announced to no one and everyone. "By order of the Edict. No one moves. No one speaks."

One of the black coats swept Tamsin's candles off the table. Another seized her wrist. And Tamsin—foolish, brave, blazing Tamsin—looked the captain dead in the eye and said, "Those cost four coppers each, you absolute—"

Her free hand twitched toward the fallen candles.

Gideon saw it happen the way you see a cart accident: slowly, horribly, with time to know it couldn't be stopped. The nearest candlewick curled alight. Just a curl, just a thread of smoke—but the captain saw it, and smiled, and it was the worst smile Gideon had ever seen because there was no triumph in it at all. Only paperwork.

"Well," he said. "That's convenient."

"Let go of me!" Fire kindled along Tamsin's forearm now, real fire, frightened fire, and the market screamed and scattered, and the hounds lunged, and Gideon stood frozen behind her CLOSED ON ACCOUNT OF FUNERALS sign with her heart trying to climb out through her teeth.

Run to her. Help her. Burn them. Do *something*—

But her feet wouldn't move, because her feet knew what three years of invisibility had taught them: that one woman against four witch-finders was not rescue, it was a second arrest, a second name on the ledger, and Tamsin's sacrifice would mean nothing except doubled.

*Tamsin,* Gideon thought, pouring everything she had into the word, knowing the girl couldn't hear her the way beasts could, sending it anyway. *I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.*

Tamsin's eyes found hers across the chaos—one instant, one look—and Gideon saw her understand, and nod, almost imperceptibly, and mouth: *Run.*

So Gideon ran.

She hated herself for every step of it. She ran through the dyer's yard, vaulted the stinking drainage channel, cut through the cooperage where she owed six weeks of credit to a man who pretended not to know what she was, and ran down the long stone stair to the canal district where the city kept its secrets and its smells in equal measure.

Behind her, faintly, she heard Tamsin shouting something defiant and probably obscene, and then she heard the crackle of real fire, and then she heard nothing but her own breath and the slap of her boots on wet stone.

* * *

The Underveil canals were Vaelmoor's basement—black water, older arches, the drowned foundations of the city that had stood here before Vaelmoor was Vaelmoor. Officially, they drained runoff. Unofficially, they carried everything the Crown preferred not to see: untaxed brandy, unsanctioned pamphlets, unregistered persons.

Gideon knew the water was watched. She knew it was cold enough in autumn to stop a heart. She went in anyway, because the alternative was the street above, where the dogs already had her scent off her abandoned stall.

The cold took her like a debt collector.

She'd meant to wade, to keep to the shallows under the arches, but the shelf stone crumbled and the canal took her whole, and suddenly the world was green-black roar and her lungs were full of knives. She thrashed. Something wooden—a barge pole, a broken beam—slid past her fingers. Above her, distorted by water, lantern light swayed along the towpath, and she thought, distantly, absurdly: *the witch-finders will never find me now, and neither will anyone else, and isn't that what I wanted, isn't this the whole point of being no one—*

Then her head broke surface, or half of it did, wedged against stone, and she heard voices.

"—told you I heard a splash, and I heard a splash, so haul her out before she drowns, because dead cargo pays nothing."

"That's cold talk, Finch."

"That's *arithmetic*, Pell."

A hook on a rope snaked past her nose. Gideon, whose options at this point consisted of drowning or trusting strangers, grabbed it.

They hauled her out the way fishermen land a particularly indignant eel, over the gunwale of a flat dark barge and onto planking slick with fish scales and something she chose not to identify. She lay gasping, streaming, alive, surrounded by legs. When her vision cleared, she counted five of them, and a boy of perhaps twelve holding a lantern, and a dog-sized creature that turned out to be a capybara wearing a tiny oilskin hat, which she decided she must have imagined.

At the center stood a woman built like a ship's anchor, grey-streaked hair bound back with tarred cord, sleeves rolled over the stump of her right arm with the brisk neatness of old habit. Her remaining hand held a boat hook, and her face held the expression of a person who has been interrupted during dinner and is deciding whether to be annoyed or murderous.

"You," the woman said, "are either a spy or an idiot. Possibly both. The Veil's been hot for a week—witch-finders walking the towpaths like they pay rent—and you come swimming down the middle of it making noise like a sack of church bells."

"I didn't—" Gideon coughed up half the canal. "They took my friend. I was running."

"Running from what?"

"Witch-finders."

The word changed the air. The crew exchanged glances. The boy lowered the lantern slightly, and in its light Gideon saw the woman's face do something complicated—hardness rearranging itself around an old wound.

"Huh," the woman said finally. She planted the boat hook and looked Gideon over, top to bottom, with the frank assessment of someone pricing livestock. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The hunted look. I know it. Had it myself, years back." She jerked her chin at her missing arm, casual as mentioning weather. "Got this in the last purge. Lost more than the arm." Something crossed her face and was gone. "Odessa Finch. This is the *Saltcellar*. We move things the Crown doesn't approve of, and occasionally we move people. You're dripping on my cargo, so let's make this quick. Who are you, and who's hunting you?"

"Gideon. Gideon Winterbourne. Charm-seller, Tanners' Row. Nobody important."

"Mm." Odessa's eyes narrowed. "Nobody important doesn't get four finders and a pair of hounds. I've done this a while, girl. Nobody important gets ignored. Somebody thinks you're worth taking."

Gideon said nothing, which was its own answer, and Odessa read it fluently.

"Right. Well. Here's how it goes aboard the Saltcellar. Everybody pulls weight. Everybody keeps quiet topside. And everybody—" she leaned closer, and her voice dropped into something quieter and far more dangerous than her earlier gruffness— "everybody is what they are, and we don't ask, and we don't tell. You follow?"

"I—yes?"

"No. You don't. Not yet." Odessa straightened. "Pell, get her a blanket before she rattles apart. Nix, tea. The awful tea. She's earned the awful tea."

"She hasn't earned anything," muttered a wiry man with a beard full of net fragments.

"She survived the Veil on a Tuesday with finders on the path," Odessa said. "That earns the awful tea. Move."

* * *

They put ashore an hour later in a flooded boathouse that smelled of pitch and old rope, and someone gave her dry clothes that fit badly and a tin cup of tea that tasted the way Gideon imagined pond scum dreamed of tasting, and she sat wrapped in a blanket by a stove the size of a bread loaf and shook.

Not from the cold. From the other thing.

Every time she closed her eyes she saw Tamsin's face across the screaming market. *Run.* Tamsin had known. Had known Gideon wouldn't—couldn't—come for her, and had spent her one look telling her friend to save herself, and Gideon had taken the offer, because she was a coward, because she was smart, because those were the same thing in Vaelmoor and had been for three years.

"You didn't do wrong," said a voice near her elbow.

The boy—Nix—sat cross-legged nearby, pretending to mend a net and failing at it comprehensively.

"You don't know what happened."

"Nah. But I know Finch. She does this face when someone new comes in all cracked open. Says the same thing every time." He shrugged. "'Surviving's not a betrayal. Dying's just dying.' She says it to everybody."

"Does it help?"

"Nah." He grinned, gap where a tooth should be, so like Tamsin that Gideon's chest seized. "But it's true, which is better than helpful."

From the deck above, footsteps. Odessa came down the ladder one-handed with the ease of long practice, carrying a plate of bread and hard cheese, which she set in front of Gideon with zero ceremony.

"Eat. Then talk. The rats on the towpath have been chatty tonight—don't look at me like that, half my crew has its oddities, the capybara reads weather, don't ask—and they say the finders took a candle-girl to the Graylock holding. Alive. Interrogation wing." She watched Gideon carefully. "That your friend?"

"Yes."

"Then here's what I know about Graylock. People go in. Sometimes people come out. What comes out usually isn't all there." Odessa crouched, bringing her level, and for the first time her voice lost its anchor-weight and became simply human. "I'm not going to promise you a rescue tonight. Tonight we're laying low, because the whole canal's crawling. But the Saltcellar doesn't forget names, girl, and it doesn't leave debts unpaid. Your friend's name goes on the list. Understand?"

Gideon nodded. Then, because some part of her had apparently decided the hiding was over whether she liked it or not, she heard herself say:

"Her name's Tamsin. She lights candles with her fingers because it makes her feel rich." Her voice cracked. "That's all she ever did. That's all any of us ever did."

Odessa was quiet a moment. Then she reached out and, with her single hand, briefly squeezed Gideon's shoulder—an awkward, iron-hard gesture that somehow landed gentler than any kindness Gideon had been shown in years.

"Aye," she said. "That's how it always starts."

She climbed back up the ladder. Gideon sat with her terrible tea and her grief and the strange, terrifying realization that she was, at this moment, safer than she had been in three years—surrounded by strangers who asked no questions, on a barge named for a salt box, in the dark underneath everything.

Above her, through the boathouse roof, she heard the night birds of the Veil going about their business, and among them—cutting through the murmur of gulls and moorhens like a knife through water—one voice she knew.

*Found you,* said Bramble.

Gideon was on her feet before she'd decided to stand, blanket falling, staring up at the roof beams.

"Crow," she said aloud, and the whole boathouse went still, and she realized too late that she'd just answered a bird in front of witnesses.

Nix's eyes went wide. Somewhere, the capybara made a noise that sounded distinctly like *finally*.

And from the gap under the eaves, feathers rustling, Bramble dropped down into the lantern light—scarred, ragged, unmistakably smug—and fixed Gideon with one glittering eye.

*"The crown,"* he said, *"is coming for you next. And girl—we need to talk about what you actually are."*
