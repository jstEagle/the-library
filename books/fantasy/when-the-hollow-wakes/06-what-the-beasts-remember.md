## 6. What the Beasts Remember

Gideon woke to the smell of salt, tar, and someone frying onions, and for three full seconds she could not remember where she was.

Then the memory arrived all at once: the raid, Tamsin's face going white as the witch-finders closed in, Bramble's shriek of *run, girl, run*, and the cold green slap of the Underveil canals. The hand that had hauled her out of the water. The one-armed woman who had looked at her dripping, shivering, half-drowned self and said, "Well. You're a mess. Come on, then."

The Saltcellar barge rocked gently beneath her, moored somewhere in the labyrinth of forgotten canals beneath Vaelmoor's eastern quarter. Through the porthole above her bunk—actually just a hammock strung between two cargo hooks—she could see the underside of a bridge and the pale bellies of fish drifting past.

A rat sat on the barrel beside her hammock, washing its whiskers.

*You snore,* it observed.

"I do not," Gideon said, before she remembered she wasn't supposed to talk to rats anymore. Old habits. Three years of silence, and one night of nearly drowning had washed them away like mud off a cobblestone.

*You do,* the rat insisted. *Like a little pig. Pip snores too, but he's fat, so it's expected.*

"Who's Pip?"

*My brother. He's below decks stealing cheese. Don't tell Odessa.*

Gideon sat up slowly, wincing at the bruise blooming along her ribs where she'd struck a canal ladder. The rat watched her with the frank, unbothered interest of its kind.

*The tall one with the missing arm says come eat,* it added. *She says if you don't come up on your own, she'll send Juniper down, and Juniper talks more than I do, which is saying something.*

"Does everyone on this boat talk to animals?"

*No. Just you. That's why you're interesting.*

* * *

The main deck of the *Saltcellar* had been converted, with smuggler's pragmatism, into a kitchen, a map room, and a home. Lanterns hung from the rigging. A stove squatted in the corner, presided over by a broad, cheerful man called Doss who was indeed frying onions. A young woman with ink-stained fingers—Juniper, presumably—was arguing with a gull perched on the rail, and losing.

"It's not *stealing* if they were going to throw them out," Juniper was saying.

*It is absolutely stealing,* the gull replied. *I know theft when I commit it.*

"That's not how theft works."

"That's exactly how theft works. That's why it's good."

"Gideon." Odessa Finch stood by the stove, and up close, in daylight, Gideon could see the burn scars climbing out of her collar where her left arm used to be. She was maybe fifty, gray-shot hair tied back with what appeared to be fishing line, and she had the eyes of a woman who had done arithmetic on people before and hadn't always liked the totals. "Sit. Eat. You look like something the river coughed up."

"The river did cough me up," Gideon said, taking the offered bowl.

"And yet here you are. Sit."

She sat. The stew was good—better than anything Gideon had eaten in months—and she realized halfway through that she was eating it too fast, the way you do when you've been hungry longer than you admitted.

Bramble landed on the rail beside her, feathers ruffled, dignity intact. *They gave me sardines,* he announced. *Acceptable. Not generous, but acceptable.*

"You're welcome," said Doss.

*I wasn't thanking you.*

"He does that," Gideon murmured.

"We noticed," Odessa said dryly. She lowered herself onto a crate across from Gideon, studying her with that arithmetic gaze. "Here's where we are, girl. Last night my crew pulled a stranger out of the Underveil because Bramble screamed at us to. Witch-finders took your neighbor. You're marked—you might not know it yet, but you are. So. Who are you, and what exactly did they see?"

Gideon set down her spoon. Every instinct she had, honed over three years of careful invisibility, told her to lie. To be nobody. To be the charm-seller with the luck-knots and nothing else.

But Tamsin's face was still behind her eyelids. And the rat had already told half the crew she talked in her sleep—to whom, they hadn't asked.

"They saw a woman talking to a crow," Gideon said quietly. "And the crow talking back."

Silence around the deck. Juniper had stopped arguing with the gull. Even the stove seemed quieter.

"A speaker," Odessa said. Not a question.

"My grandmother's word for it. I never knew there was a word until—" She stopped. "Until recently."

"Say the rest."

So Gideon said it. All of it, or most of it: the charms, the cats and rats and horses she'd spoken with since she was small enough to think everyone could; the Edict of Ash and the informants and the careful, grinding work of being no one; the beggar woman's healed hand at the Hollow Shrine; the way the old power had started humming in her bones like a second heartbeat ever since. And then, because it seemed pointless to stop halfway: "Your rats have been telling me things since I woke up. Things about the palace."

Odessa's expression didn't change, but something behind it did—a door opening a crack.

"Go on," she said.

* * *

They brought her below, to the hold, where the cargo had been cleared away and replaced with maps, ledgers, and a long table scarred by knife-points and candle wax. And then, at Odessa's nod, the crew began bringing in the witnesses.

Rats first. Six of them, ferried down in a covered basket by a wiry young man called Fen, who set it down with the tenderness of a man handling eggs. When the lid came off, the rats poured out onto the table and arranged themselves with surprising orderliness, and Gideon understood that this was practiced. This was routine. These people had been listening to beasts for years—not with a gift, but with patience: patterns of behavior, trails of crumbs, messages carried in ways she couldn't parse. But now they had her.

"Tell them what you told me," Odessa said.

Gideon crouched so she was eye-level with the table. It felt strange and enormous, like kneeling at an altar. "What have you seen? Near the palace. At night."

The eldest rat, a grizzled female with a torn ear, stepped forward.

*We do not go near the palace,* she said. *The palace is wrong. But the drains run under everything, and the drains touch the foundations, and sometimes things come down the drains.*

"What things?"

*A procession.* The rat's whiskers trembled. *On moonless nights. Men in silver masks, walking in step, carrying lamps that burn without flickering even in the wind from the water. They sing. Not with their mouths—with their throats, deep, like bells underwater. And ahead of them walks—*

The rat stopped. The other rats had gone very still.

*Ahead of them walks the shining hungry thing,* the eldest said at last. *It looks like a man wearing light the way other men wear clothes. It has the king's face. It walks through the dark and the dark leans toward it like wheat toward sun, and where it passes, we feel—* she searched for the word, ears flat *—*hollowed*. Like something drank the place and left the shape of it. We do not go back to those tunnels. Rats that follow it come back empty. Some don't come back at all."

The hold was silent except for the creak of the hull.

"Gulls," Odessa said.

Juniper whistled, and the thieving gull from the rail came flapping down the hatch stairs with zero grace and total confidence, landing on the table among the rats, who scattered with offended squeaks.

*Fine, fine,* the gull said. *Everyone's so delicate.*

"You fly over the palace district," Gideon said. "What do you see on moonless nights?"

*Meat,* said the gull. *Sorry—what was the question?*

"The palace. Night. No moon."

*Ah.* The gull settled, and its black eyes went oddly serious, which Gideon hadn't known gull eyes could do. *The palace breathes. You laugh, but it does. On dark nights the whole roof exhales warm air, like a sleeping thing, and every bird in the city knows not to land there. But two turns ago—the very darkest night—I flew high, higher than I should, and I saw them come out onto the eastern terrace. The masked ones. And the shining one stood at the edge, looking down at the city, and it raised its arms like a priest, and every bird in Vaelmoor went silent at once. Every bird. Sparrows, crows, pigeons, my own foul cousins. We all felt it pass through us like cold water. It was listening to us. Counting us.* The gull ruffled its feathers. *I have stolen from widows and orphans, lady. I am not easily frightened. I did not fly over that palace again for a month.*

Bramble, who had been silent on Gideon's shoulder through all of it, spoke quietly.

*It's gathering,* he said. *That's what the crown is doing. Feeding. Growing. My speaker used to say the old gods weren't men and weren't beasts—they were hungers that learned to wear shapes. And a hunger with a shape and worshippers and a calendar—* his feathers flattened *—that's not a god waking. That's a god setting a table.*

Nobody laughed. Nobody moved. In the lamplight, Gideon looked around the hold—at the rats with their torn ears and their terrible courage, at the gull, at Juniper with ink on her fingers and fear in her eyes, at Doss standing very still by the stairs, at Odessa Finch with her one arm and her arithmetic gaze fixed on Gideon.

"Well," Odessa said finally. "That confirms it, then."

"Confirms what?"

Odessa reached under the table and came up with a ledger—old, swollen with damp, its cover stamped with a sigil Gideon almost recognized. She opened it to a page dense with cramped handwriting.

"Three years ago, before the last purge, my brother Tomas kept records. He was a speaker too—small gift, birds mostly, nothing like yours. He tracked the same procession. Moonless nights. The masked singers. He wrote that the king hadn't been seen eating, sleeping, or bleeding in a year, and that the palace's grain stores went out but nothing went in." Her voice was level, but her hand on the ledger wasn't quite. "He sent his findings to a friend in the Edict's own archive, thinking the law would want to know its king was hollow. Two weeks later the witch-finders burned our quarter. Tomas didn't make it out. Neither did my arm." She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin. "So when your rats describe a shining hungry thing walking under the palace, girl, I don't need convincing. I need to know what you're going to do about it."

Every eye in the hold was on her. Gideon felt the old reflex rise—to shrink, to deflect, to become the charm-seller again, harmless, invisible, alive.

But the second heartbeat was there too, steady under her ribs. And Tamsin's face. And the rats who had walked into a room full of humans to describe the thing that hollowed their kin.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "I don't know what I can do. But I know I'm done pretending I'm no one. If that's not enough—"

"It's enough," Odessa said, before Gideon could finish bracing for rejection. "It's more than most people ever offer. Here's the shape of it, then. You stay aboard the *Saltcellar*. You're crew now—or near enough. In exchange, you stop hiding. Not reckless—but done. No more flinching when the beasts talk to you. No more pretending you can't hear what this city is whispering. Because the thing under the palace is listening too, and I'd rather it hear us coming than catch us hiding."

Gideon's throat was tight. Three years of careful silence, and this one-armed smuggler had just dismantled it in a sentence, kindly, like pulling a splinter.

"Why?" she managed. "Why help me? You don't know me."

Odessa looked at her for a long moment, and something old and tired moved behind her eyes.

"Because the last time I stayed quiet to survive, I lost everything worth surviving for," she said. "Never again. Welcome aboard, Winterbourne."

*Winterbourne.* Gideon went still. "I never told you my name."

"No," Odessa agreed. "But Bramble did. Loudly. Repeatedly. While eating our sardines." She almost smiled. "Also, my brother kept records on the old families. Yours is in there. Page forty-one. We'll get to it."

From Gideon's shoulder, Bramble made a sound that was suspiciously close to a smug chuckle.

*Page forty-one,* he repeated. *I told you I was useful.*

"You ate their sardines."

*Which made me useful AND beloved. It's called diplomacy.*

Around the hold, the tension broke into motion—Doss back to his stove, Juniper scribbling notes, Fen returning the rats to their basket with whispered apologies. Found, Gideon thought dizzily. Not found family, not yet, maybe not ever—but found. Seen. Named. It terrified her more than the witch-finders had.

That night, in her hammock, she listened to the barge settle and the water lap, and the rat from that morning crept up the rope to sit near her head.

*Pip says welcome,* it offered. *He also says the cheese was Odessa's, and if she asks, it was my idea.*

Gideon laughed—really laughed, for the first time in longer than she could measure—and the sound felt strange in her own chest, like a door unbarred.

She was still smiling when Bramble dropped through the porthole and landed hard on her stomach.

*Ow—*

*Listen,* he said, and all the smugness was gone from him, and that frightened her more than anything the rats had described. *The gulls are screaming at the harbor mouth. Something's coming up the Underveil tonight. A boat with no lights. And the rats on the eastern wall say the men aboard wear silver masks.*

Gideon sat up in the dark, the second heartbeat pounding now, loud as drums.

The procession had found the canals.
