## 7. Thirteen Turns

The archives of Vaelmoor Palace smelled of dust, tallow, and the particular despair of clerks who had been told to organize something by men who would never check.

Serah Vane had spent eleven years in that smell. She knew the archive the way other physicians knew their own hands—every drawer of ledgers, every mislabeled box of coroner's reports, every shelf where the really old things went to be forgotten. She had come down here a thousand times to look up a dosage, a death record, a precedent. She had never once come down here looking for the truth.

Tonight she told herself it was only insomnia. Insomnia was respectable. A disgraced royal physician with insomnia could wander her own corridor at midnight without anyone marking it. A disgraced royal physician hunting through sealed records—that was another creature entirely, and witch-finders could smell the difference from across a courtyard.

So: insomnia. She carried a candle and her grandmother's shawl against the cellar chill, and she let her feet take her to the restricted stacks as though they'd simply decided on their own.

*You're lying to yourself,* said the part of her mind that sounded annoyingly like her old anatomy tutor. *You've been lying to yourself since you knelt beside that bed and felt nothing looking back at you.*

She pulled the first ledger anyway.

* * *

It had been nine days since she examined the king.

Nine days since she had stood in the royal bedchamber with her little case of instruments and watched Aldric Winterbourne—no relation to any Winterbourne that mattered, the heralds assured everyone—sit perfectly still while she pressed his eyelids back and looked into eyes like windows of an empty house.

No madness. That was what had unsettled her most, at first. Madness had texture. Madness wandered, fixated, wept, raged. Serah had spent thirty years learning the geography of broken minds, and there was always *someone* home, even behind ruin.

There was no one home in King Aldric.

But there was something else. Something patient. When she held his wrist and counted his pulse—steady, slow, *wrong*, beating once for every four of hers, like a heart that had learned to wait—she had felt a sensation she still had no clinical term for. The closest she could come, scribbling in her private journal afterward, was this: *like being read by something that has all the time in the world and finds me mildly interesting.*

And then the court had smiled at her. That was the part that kept her awake. Not horror. Not alarm. Chamberlain Corvelis had thanked her warmly for her thoroughness, pressed a purse into her hand heavier than her fee, and said, "His Majesty is much improved under our care, don't you think?"

*Our* care. As if she were being folded into something. As if she were being asked—not asked, *assumed*—to be part of the care.

She had been dismissed that afternoon. Dismissed with honors, with a pension, with a new title: *Physician Emerita of the Royal Household.* She'd gone home to her narrow house near the tanners' row and poured herself three fingers of plum brandy and understood, with the clarity that only brandy provides, that she had not been dismissed at all.

She had been marked. Watched. Filed away as harmless—or as useful—and either way, they intended for her to keep her mouth shut about whatever lived behind the king's eyes.

The court knew. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. Corvelis knew. The Lord Protector's people knew. Half the chamber staff moved around the king with the careful choreography of people who had long ago stopped seeing him as a man. They served it. They fed it its meals and dressed it in ermine and carried its decrees to the Edict-keepers, and somewhere along the line someone had decided that a hollow king was better than no king, and everyone had simply... agreed.

Serah had spent one full day being furious, and then one full night being afraid, and then she had done what she always did when fear and fury got too loud.

She had gone to work.

* * *

The restricted stacks were organized by reign, which meant organized by lie. Every monarch's archive told the story that monarch wished remembered, and the truth—if it survived at all—lived in the gaps, the misfilings, the documents too embarrassing to destroy but too damning to display.

Aldric's father, King Osric, had reigned twenty-two years. His archive filled six shelves and was boring beyond description. Aldric himself had reigned fourteen years, but his section was thin—thin in a way that made Serah's professional instincts prickle. Fourteen years of rule should have generated mountains of paper. Tax rolls. Edict enforcement reports. Correspondence.

What it generated instead was a small locked chest, and a gap of exactly five years' worth of records that simply didn't exist.

Five years. Serah sat down on the clerk's stool and did the math with her fingers against her knee, the way she did when counting a pulse. Five years ago, give or take a season, was when the court physicians' logs went strange. She remembered those years. She had been junior then, running the fever ward, but she remembered how the senior men had begun taking turns attending the king personally—turns taken in pairs, never alone, and never speaking of what they saw.

One of those senior men had been her mentor, old Physician Halloway. And Halloway, before he died, had drunk too much one festival night and gripped her arm and said, "Girl, if they ever put you in that room, count the pulse. Count it twice. And whatever you find, *pretend you found nothing.*"

She had thought it senility. She had thought it brandy.

She had spent nine days not thinking about it at all, which was its own kind of discipline.

Now she set her candle beside the chest and took out the two hairpins holding her bun together, and went to work on the lock, because a woman who had spent thirty years extracting splinters from screaming children could pick a palace lock in under a minute, and she'd thank the gods for that skill except the gods weren't answering, were they.

The chest opened with a click that sounded obscenely loud.

Inside: perhaps forty documents. She spread them on the reading table with hands that were almost steady and began, physician-fashion, with the oldest.

Most of it was administrative drivel—rotation schedules for the king's attendants, supply requisitions for quantities of incense that made no sense for one man's chambers, a receipt from a Chandlery for "nine hundred weight black tallow candles, unmarked." But beneath those, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord, was a folder labeled in Halloway's cramped handwriting: *Observations, His Majesty, Years One Through Five.*

Her mentor's notes. Her dead mentor's secret life's work.

Serah untied the cord and began to read, and somewhere around the third page her tea—which she hadn't brought any tea, but her throat performed the swallowing motion anyway—her throat performed the swallowing motion, and she realized she had been standing up for some time without noticing.

Halloway had been a better observer than she had ever given him credit for. He had charted the king's decline the way he'd have charted a disease: onset, progression, remission. And the disease, whatever it was, had a *calendar*.

*"Full moon,"* Halloway wrote, in the entry dated the first winter of Aldric's strangeness. *"HM slept 19 hours following the turning. Pulse at 1:4. On waking, did not recognize the Lord Protector. Recognized the chaplain immediately—addressed him as 'old friend' in a voice HM does not possess. Voice was deeper. Older. The chaplain did not seem surprised."*

The next entry:

*"Second turn. I observed the renewal directly tonight, God forgive my curiosity. At moonrise HM rose from bed unassisted, walked to the window, and knelt facing the palace shrine. For the space of a hundred breaths he did not breathe at all. I counted. Then the pulse resumed at its wrong rhythm, and HM turned to me and smiled, and I have been a physician forty years and I have never seen a smile arranged like that. It started at the edges and worked inward, like frost crossing glass. He said: 'Another month, doctor. You are keeping me very well.'*

*I checked the shrine afterward. The offering plate was empty. It is never empty. Whatever is left there at dusk is gone by morning, and no servant will speak of it."*

Page after page. Month after month. Halloway had documented twelve turns before his entries became frightened, then fragmentary, then stopped altogether. The last coherent page was a single line, underlined twice:

*"I have found references to a prior binding. The court knows. The court has ALWAYS known. There is a number involved. Thirteen. I do not know what happens at thirteen. I intend to find out."*

He hadn't found out. Or he had, and it had killed him, or worse—it had gotten him promoted, which in Vaelmoor amounted to the same thing these days.

Serah sat back. The candle had burned down two fingers. Somewhere above her, the palace settled and creaked, and she thought she heard footsteps in the corridor—the measured pace of the night watch—but they passed, and the silence came back thicker.

Thirteen turns. Full moons. She counted backward from Halloway's records through the missing five years, through the present. If the binding renewed each full moon, and the thirteenth completed something—

She was not a mathematician. She was a physician. So she did what a physician does: she looked for the pattern in the body of the thing, and the body of the thing was the city itself.

Because she'd seen it. Everyone had seen it, these past months, and pretended not to. The beggar woman at the Hollow Shrine whose withered hand had healed overnight. The well water that tasted sweeter on certain streets. The prayer flags that sold out of every market stall within hours. The old gods were silent no more, and the prayers were being *answered*, and answered prayers in Vaelmoor had exactly one source now, and it wore a crown and beat its heart once every four beats like something conserving itself for an occasion.

Something was feeding. Something was growing. And Halloway's number—thirteen—was not a warning.

It was a schedule.

* * *

She worked until the candle drowned in its own wax. By gray dawn light she had assembled the whole shape of it on the reading table: Halloway's observations, the rotation schedules proving the court's complicity, a scrap of older parchment—pre-Godfall, by the script—that spoke of "the Winterbourne binding" and "the vessel-line" in language so formalized it might have been liturgy.

*The binding shall renew upon each turning of the moon,* the old parchment read, *and at the thirteenth turn the vessel shall be no vessel but a door entire, and that which waits shall wake in fullness, sustained by the devotion of the faithful.*

Sustained by devotion. Fed on prayer.

Every answered prayer in Vaelmoor was a meal. Every terrified, hopeful soul lighting a candle at the Hollow Shrine was carrying food to the thing in the palace. The Edict had outlawed magic and burned speakers for fourteen years, and all the while the largest working in the city's history had been sitting on the throne, fattening itself on faith, one full moon at a time.

Serah gathered the papers with great care. She was surprised to find her hands quite steady now. Fear, she had learned long ago, was like pain: unbearable in anticipation, manageable in fact, and best handled with clean technique.

She thought about burning them. She thought about burying them. Both options left the knowledge in exactly one skull—hers—and her skull, she suspected, had recently been added to somebody's list.

Which left the third option. The stupid one. The one that made her grandmother's shawl feel suddenly very heavy on her shoulders.

The smugglers.

Everyone in Vaelmoor knew the Underveil existed the way everyone knew rats existed—you didn't discuss it in polite company, but you knew which drains they came out of. The Saltcellar crews ran the canals below the city, moving contraband charms and unsanctioned medicines and, if rumor held, the occasional fugitive from the witch-finders. Rumor also held that they'd feed you to the eels if you crossed them, but Serah had spent thirty years among courtiers, and eels struck her as refreshingly straightforward by comparison.

She needed allies who were not the court. She needed people who knew the veins of the city, who could hide a woman and a stack of stolen papers, who had no love for witch-finders and no reason to trust anything wearing a crown.

She needed, in short, criminals. She dressed in her plainest wool, wrapped Halloway's folder in oilcloth against the canal damp, and tucked the pre-Godfall parchment inside her bodice where it lay against her heart like a coal.

At the door of the archive she paused. Behind her: eleven years of respectable service, a pension she would never collect, a title that had already begun to rot. Ahead: the dark water, and whatever waited in it.

"You should have seen him, Hal," she said quietly, to a dead man, in an empty room. "Fourteen years I patched the fevers and the gout and the poisoned thumb, and I never once looked in his eyes. You tried to tell me. You tried to tell everybody."

She blew out the stub of candle.

"I'm done pretending I found nothing."

* * *

Getting out of the palace district was the easy part; nobody watches a gray-haired woman in a gray shawl, which was precisely why she'd worn one. Getting into the Underveil was harder. The canal entrances were watched—lightly, lazily watched, because the witch-finders considered the smugglers vermin rather than threat—but watched nonetheless, and Serah Vane had never in her life climbed down a drain grate, and her knees filed formal complaints about it.

She followed the sound of water. Old storm-drains under Tanner's Row, down stairs slick with moss, into air that smelled of river weed and lamp oil and, faintly, of salt. The Underveil announced itself gradually: first a light ahead, warm and yellow, then voices—low, argumentative, alive—then the extraordinary sight of a canal-side market operating at midnight, stalls strung with lanterns, boats sliding past loaded with crates that officially did not exist.

Nobody looked at her. Everybody looked at her. She felt the difference between being ignored and being *catalogued*, and it raised every hair on her neck.

At a stall selling what appeared to be luck-knots—actual ones, working ones, and wasn't *that* a small blasphemy—she cleared her throat and addressed the vendor, a wiry woman with a burn scar along one jaw.

"I'm looking for passage."

The vendor didn't look up. "Passage where?"

"Away. From the Crown."

"Mm." The woman tied off a knot with her teeth. "Crown's got long arms lately. Witch-finder trouble?"

"Worse." Serah lowered her voice. "I have proof the king is dead. What sits on the throne is something else, and it's eating this city's prayers, and in—" she calculated, quickly, against Halloway's calendar—"in roughly two moons, it finishes waking. Permanently."

That got the woman's attention. She looked up, and her eyes were sharp and unhurried, taking Serah's measure the way one takes the measure of a horse offered cheaply.

"That's a mad story, gran."

"I'm a physician. I examined him. I've read his own court's records. And I'm offering the only complete account of what's happening to him that exists outside the palace." She patted the oilcloth bundle. "I imagine your people would find that worth a boat ride."

The vendor was quiet a moment. Then she whistled—a low, two-note signal—and further down the canal, a lantern swung in answer.

"Saltcellars run cargo," the woman said. "Not crusades."

"Then run me as cargo. Destination doesn't matter. Just get me to whoever decides things."

"And why would they decide anything about you?"

"Because," Serah said, and heard her own voice go flat and cold with the guilt that had carried her here, "because I treated that man for eleven years, and I never once saw what was happening to him. Because his own physician died trying to say it out loud and I called it senility. Because I am *done* being the kind of person who pretends she found nothing."

Somewhere behind the sharp eyes, something shifted—recognition, maybe. The look of a person who had lost things to the same machinery.

The vendor jerked her chin toward the lantern. "Finch'll see you. Odessa Finch. Fair warning—she's lost an arm to the Crown and she doesn't waste sympathy on strangers. Tell her Tamsin sent you."

"Tamsin," Serah repeated, filing the name. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me." The vendor was already tying another knot, eyes back down, conversation over. "If Finch throws you in the canal, I never saw you."

* * *

Odessa Finch turned out to be a broad, weathered woman with one sleeve pinned neatly at the shoulder and a voice like wet gravel, and she listened to Serah's entire account without interrupting once—which Serah would learn, later, was either courtesy or triage.

They talked in the cabin of a canal barge that smelled of tar and dried fish. Serah laid out Halloway's folder on the scarred table, page by page, the rotations, the pulse charts, the thirteen turns, the old parchment with its liturgy of the door. Odessa read slowly, lips moving, one hand flat on the table as if steadying it.

When Serah finished, the silence stretched long enough that she could hear the water lapping the hull.

"The thirteenth turn," Odessa said finally. "Two moons."

"Give or take. The next full moon makes twelve. The one after makes thirteen."

"And you stole all this out from under the witch-finders' noses."

"I stole it out from under the *court's* nose. The witch-finders haven't seen it yet. Give them time."

Odessa grunted. It might have been a laugh. She turned the last page over, read Halloway's final line again—*the court has ALWAYS known*—and tapped it once with one blunt finger.

"My brother used to say the same thing," she said. "About the purges. That the ones giving the orders knew exactly what they were burning." Her jaw tightened. "They hanged him for saying less than what's in this folder."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." Odessa looked up, and her eyes were the flat gray of canal water, and behind them something moved that was not softness exactly, but was adjacent to it. "You understand what you've handed me, physician? This says the Crown is a hollow shell with a god inside it. This says every prayer in this city is feeding it. This says in two moons it's done cooking. You're not asking for passage. You're asking us to make war on the throne of Vaelmoor."

"I'm asking you to help me stop it. The war may follow regardless."

"Hm." Odessa rolled the parchment closed. "There's a girl works the Underveil stalls. Charm-seller. Little knots, mended-bone trinkets, harmless stuff." Something flickered across her face—curiosity, or caution. "My people say she talks to rats. And my people say a crow's been following her around like a debt collector. And my people say the god's attention landed on her, one full moon back, and she survived it."

Serah went very still. "Survived it how?"

"Walked away from it. Which is more than the witch-finder captain managed." Odessa slid Halloway's folder into a waterproof satchel and lashed it shut. "If there's a binding on that throne, physician, there's someone who made it. And if there's someone who made it, there's blood that carries the making. And if your papers are right, that blood is currently selling luck-knots three stalls down from a woman named Tamsin—who, incidentally, the witch-finders took last week."

The lantern swayed. The water slapped the hull. Serah felt the whole shape of the thing assemble itself in her chest: the charm-seller, the crow, the god's attention, the binding, the thirteenth turn. Guilt sat in her stomach like swallowed stone—eleven years of pulses counted and eyes avoided—and she found that the stone, for once, was useful. Ballast. Something to keep her steady in moving water.

"So," Odessa said, watching her. "Still want that boat ride, gran?"

"No," Serah said. "I want the war."

Odessa Finch grinned, and it was not a comforting sight.

"Good," she said. "Because the tide turns in an hour, and you're going to meet a crow."
