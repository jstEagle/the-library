## 8. The Beast-Speaker's Blood

The hedge-witch lived where the city forgot to look.

Gideon had heard of her the way you hear of anything in Vaelmoor's underside—half rumor, half dare. *Old Maghda, up past the tanneries. Old Maghda who talks to bees. Old Maghda who'll curse your teeth loose if you steal her rosemary.* Half of it was surely invention. But Bramble had gone quiet and serious when she'd said the name aloud, which for a crow was as good as a signed testimony.

"She smells like smoke and secrets," he'd told her, preening one ragged wing. "And she throws things."

"Charming."

"I'm just warning you. I don't make the hedge-witch. I only report on her."

So on a grey morning that smelled like rain deciding whether to commit, Gideon climbed the crooked stair beyond the tannery district, past walls furred with moss, to a house that seemed less built than accumulated—leaning against its neighbors like a drunk leaning on old friends. Dried herbs hung from every eave in bundles so thick the windows were mostly suggestion. Bees droned lazily around a box nailed above the door, and as Gideon approached they turned toward her—all of them, at once, the way cats did—and hummed something low and curious.

*She carries the old blood,* the bees said, in their round, humming way. *She carries it and doesn't know her own pockets.*

Gideon stopped with her fist raised to knock.

"You could have mentioned," she muttered to Bramble.

"I mentioned the throwing things," Bramble said. "Priorities."

Before she could knock, the door swung open on its own, revealing a woman small enough that Gideon might have mistaken her for a child from behind—a hunched spine, hands like knotted tree roots, and eyes the pale gold of old honey that missed absolutely nothing. She looked Gideon up and down once, then looked longer at Bramble on her shoulder.

"Well," the woman said. "Thirty years, and it sends me a crow and a girl who flinches when bees talk about her." She stepped back. "Come in before someone sees you standing there looking guilty. You've the face for it. Guilty. Like a cat caught near cream it didn't even want."

Inside smelled of woodsmoke, dried yarrow, and something older underneath—stone and iron, like a cellar that remembered being a tomb. The single room was crowded but not cluttered; everything had a place, even if the place was "somewhere under that pile." A fire ticked in a squat hearth. A kettle already hung over it, which struck Gideon as either hospitality or prophecy.

"Sit," said the hedge-witch. "I'm Maghda. You're the Winterbourne girl. Don't argue, it wastes tea."

"I haven't told anyone my name here—"

"You told the rats. Rats tell bees. Bees tell me." She poured two cups without asking how many. "Also you walk like her."

Gideon's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "Like who?"

Maghda sat down across from her, folding her root-knuckled hands, and studied her with those honey-gold eyes until Gideon felt catalogued down to her bones.

"Like Maren," she said. "Your grandmother walked like she was listening through her feet. So do you."

* * *

The tea was bitter and good, tasting of pine and something Gideon couldn't name—something that made the second heartbeat in her bones, the one that had been stirring since the shrine, sit up and pay attention.

"Three years ago," Gideon said carefully, "a woman came through my market stall. Older than you, maybe. She bought a luck-knot, looked at my hands while I tied it, and went white as milk. She said—" Gideon hesitated. The words still stung, three years later. "She said I should be careful who I let see me work. And then she left, fast."

"And you thought: what did I do wrong?"

"I thought I'd been careless."

"You weren't careless." Maghda set down her cup. "You were recognized. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole reason you grew up hiding." She leaned forward. "Tell me what you can do. Not the parlor tricks—the true thing."

So Gideon told her. Speaking with beasts—not commanding, never commanding; the beasts spoke first, always, and she only answered. Feeling their pain through her palms when she touched them. Knowing where rats planned to run before they ran it. And lately, since the shrine, since the beggar's withered hand had healed overnight while the city prayed itself hoarse—knowing the god's attention when it passed over the city like a searchlight, feeling it press against her skin like a thumb testing fruit.

Maghda listened without blinking. When Gideon finished, the old woman nodded slowly, as if confirming something she'd long suspected and taken no joy in.

"That's not hedge-craft," she said. "That's not charm-work or herb-sense or any of the little gifts that got people burned in the purges. What you have is older than the Edict. Older than Vaelmoor." She reached beneath her chair, into a gap Gideon would have sworn was solid wall, and drew out a book—leather gone dark and soft with age, corners worn round as river stones. "Your family didn't sell charms, girl. Your family *made* the cage."

* * *

The book opened to pages dense with a hand Gideon somehow knew before Maghda said it—her grandmother's hand, the same slanted script that addressed the letters Gideon kept in a box under her floorboards, the last three letters Maren Winterbourne had ever sent before the silence began.

"The binding rite," Maghda said, tapping a page covered in interlocking circles and a name written in letters that hurt to read directly, like staring at the sun through a crack. "When the Godfall came and the gods went silent—or seemed to—there was one that wouldn't go quiet. It fed differently than the others. Not on sacrifice. On *devotion*. On being needed. On prayers answered just enough to keep the pray-ers coming back." Her mouth twisted. "Sound familiar?"

Gideon's stomach dropped. "The king."

"King Aldric's great-grandfather. A pious man, a beloved man, a man whose prayers got answered a little too well, a little too often. By the time anyone noticed what wore his smile, half the court prayed to him instead of the heavens." Maghda turned the page. Diagrams. Warnings in margins cramped with urgency. "A Winterbourne stopped it. Not killed it—you can't kill a thing like that, you can only cage it. Bound it into the royal line itself, into the blood, so it would sleep as long as the binding held and no heir ever willingly fed it again. It's held four generations." She looked up. "It's failing now."

"Why now? Why my grandmother's time, why mine—"

"Because bindings aren't stone, girl, they're knots. Knots loosen. Knots need retying by hands that know the pattern. And the last hands that knew the pattern—" Maghda's voice went flat and careful, the way voices do around wounds, "—were hunted out of this city thirty years ago. Before the Godfall purges even properly started. Before the Edict. Someone at court understood what a living Winterbourne meant: proof the cage existed, proof it could be maintained, proof the king's grandfather hadn't simply been mad. Living proof is dangerous to liars."

Gideon set down her cup because her hands had begun to shake.

"My grandmother didn't flee."

"No."

"She wrote me letters saying she'd gone south, that the air was better for her lungs, that I'd understand when I was older—"

"Yes."

"She was *hunted*." The word came out of Gideon strange and hot, a coal she hadn't known she was carrying. "She was hunted out of her own city, out of her own life, and she sent her granddaughter letters full of lies so the girl wouldn't come looking, so the girl would grow up small and quiet and *invisible*, and I spent three years thinking that was wisdom. Thinking she taught me caution because caution was virtue." Gideon laughed, and hated the sound of it. "She taught me to hide because they were still hunting. Because I'm not a survivor, I'm *evidence*."

Maghda let the fury burn. She was clearly a woman who knew better than to smother a fire mid-flare; you let it take the wet wood, and then you cooked on it.

"There's more," she said finally. "There's always more, and it's worse. The binding needs a speaker of the line to renew it—each renewal, each generation. Your grandmother was meant to renew it the year she was driven out. Which means the knot has been held these thirty years by nothing but friction and habit and the fact that the thing inside the king is patient." She met Gideon's eyes. "And it has stopped being patient. The healing at the shrine. The prayers getting answered. That's not the cage weakening by wear, child. That's the cage being *picked*, from inside, by something that has had thirty years to study the lock."

Bramble shifted on Gideon's shoulder. He had been silent through all of this, which was unlike him, and when he finally spoke his voice was stripped of its usual sardonic gloss.

"The crown is hungry," he said quietly. "I told you. I told you before I knew why I knew."

Gideon looked at him—at the scarred wings, the eye clouded with some old injury, the fragments of speech no ordinary bird should carry—and something cold moved through her. "How did you know, Bramble?"

The crow ruffled his feathers and did not answer, and Maghda's honey-gold gaze moved between them with the expression of a woman adding a note to a very long ledger.

"Later," she said. "Everything in its order. First—" she tapped the book again, "—you need to decide what you are going to do with what you are. Because the witch-finders have notices up with your description on them, girl. Did you know that? A sketch. They're calling you 'the charm-seller of Tanner's Row.' They took your neighbor Tamsin because they think her gift connects to yours. And the thing in the palace knows a Winterbourne lives, because it felt you feel it, that night at the shrine. You're not hiding anymore. Hiding ended the moment you answered that beggar's prayer-echo with your own heart instead of walking away."

"I didn't heal her."

"You *listened* to her. In this city, right now, that's worse."

* * *

Gideon walked home along the canal in a fury that tasted like copper and grief.

Not the clean fury of injustice—she'd carried that for years, banked, useful, the way you carry a coal in ash. This was worse. This was the fury of discovering that the shape of your life had been drawn by someone else's fear. Every lesson in smallness. Every practiced slump of the shoulders. Every charm sold slightly wrong on purpose so no one would look too closely at her hands. Three years of careful, exhausting invisibility—and it had never been wisdom. It had been inheritance. A hunted woman's last letter, teaching her granddaughter to be prey.

*They will not find you if you are small,* her grandmother's letters had said. *Be small, little wren. Be small and live.*

*I lived,* Gideon said to the water, silently, furiously. *I lived so small I nearly disappeared.*

A rat on the towpath paused to look at her—an old rat, grey-muzzled, with the unhurried confidence of a creature who had survived every trap this city set.

"You're angry," it observed.

"I'm furious."

"Good. Furious travels better than frightened." The rat washed its whiskers. "The gulls say the shining hungry thing walked the palace cellars last night, moonless, looking at maps. Looking for doors. Looking for *you-shaped* doors."

"I know."

"It also says the crown is hungry."

"Everyone keeps saying that," Gideon snapped, "as though hunger were news."

The rat blinked at her, unbothered. "Hunger is never news, walker-of-two-worlds. Hunger is the oldest story there is. The only question is what gets eaten, and whether anyone thinks to ask the meal." It scurried off along the stones, tail high, leaving Gideon standing in the wind with her grandmother's fury in her chest and her grandmother's book—Maghda had pressed it into her hands at the door, refusing refusal—weighing her pack down like a second spine.

*Do not carry it alone,* one marginal note had said, in Maren's hand, underlined twice. Gideon hadn't asked Maghda what *it* was yet. Some part of her had understood that the answer would change the shape of everything, the way learning her grandmother had been hunted had changed the shape of everything, and some stubborn, exhausted part of her wanted one more day of not knowing.

But the day was not offering her the option.

She smelled the smoke before she rounded the bend to Tanner's Row—woodsmoke and something sharper underneath, oil and paper burning, the smell of notices being burned in a brazier, which was a strange thing to burn notices in unless—

Unless someone was clearing the way for new ones.

Witch-finders stood at both ends of her street. Four of them at the far end, grey cloaks, iron-tipped staves, and between them, being marched toward a waiting cart with her wrists bound in front of her, was Tamsin.

Tamsin, who mended pots and sang badly and had once spent an entire afternoon helping Gideon tie forty luck-knots for a wedding party, complaining the whole time and refusing payment. Tamsin, who was struggling now—not uselessly, but hopelessly, twisting against the bindings with the desperate strength of someone who knew exactly what happened in witch-finder cells.

"Tamsin—" Gideon started forward without deciding to.

Bramble's claws bit her shoulder. "*Don't.*"

One of the witch-finders turned. Not all the way—just his head, just his eyes, sweeping the street with the flat professional patience of a man fishing with a wide net. He was looking for exactly what she almost gave him: a face, a movement, a reason.

Gideon went still behind a stack of barrels. Her heart hammered. Bramble pressed close against her neck, warm as a coal, whispering too low for human ears: "Small, wren. Be small. Live."

Her grandmother's words, in a crow's mouth.

*Later,* she promised herself, and the promise had teeth. *Later I will learn what you know, and why you know it, and what you were before you were a scarred bird who quotes dead women.*

The cart creaked away with Tamsin in it. The witch-finders nailed fresh notices to the post outside her burned stall—her face, her description, a reward—and Gideon watched from cover as the ink dried, understanding with terrible clarity that the choice Maghda had named was no longer a choice at all.

She could not be small anymore. Small had gotten Tamsin taken anyway.

She turned toward the canals, toward the Underveil, toward the smugglers Odessa Finch had told her about with a grunt that might have been an invitation—and behind her, on the notice board, her own face stared out into the rain, no longer invisible at all.
