## 9. Salt and Oath

The Saltcellar's hold smelled of tar, brine, and the particular sourness of men who had been arguing since sundown. Odessa Finch sat at the head of the upturned crate they used for a table, her one hand wrapped around a mug of tea gone cold an hour ago, and listened.

She had learned long ago that listening was the better part of command. Her brother Tam had never learned it. Tam had talked his way into the purge, talked his way past three warnings, talked himself onto a witch-finder's scaffold with his mouth still running. She thought about him more when the crew argued, because arguing was what he'd loved best, and because half of them sounded like him when they got going.

"—I'm just saying," Rook was saying now, leaning over the crate with his elbows planted like he owned it, "that if the witch-finders want her that badly, she's worth something. Worth a lot. We've all seen the notices. *Beast-speaker, reward of fifty crowns.* Fifty crowns is a new boat. Fifty crowns is winter done right for every soul on this crew."

"Fifty crowns is a rope around our necks too, you great barnacle," said Marisol, who ran the rigging and most of the arguments that mattered. She had a way of speaking as though everyone else in the room were slightly slow, which — Odessa would admit privately, in the privacy of her own skull where no one could dock her wages for it — was often fair. "You sell her to the finders, they ask how we found her, then they ask what else we know, and then they burn it out of us one finger at a time. I've only got ten fingers and I've grown attached."

"You'd grow attached to anything, Marisol. You named a rat once."

"I named *two* rats. The second was called Revenge, and he earned it."

Odessa let them run. Across the hold, the girl herself sat near the lamp with the crow hunched on her shoulder like a small feathered judgment, mending a net she clearly did not know how to mend. Gideon Winterbourne had been pulled out of the Underveil canals two nights ago, soaked to the ribs and shaking with grief for a neighbor taken by the finders, and ever since she had been trying very hard to be furniture. Quiet hands. Downcast eyes. That particular stillness of people who have spent years learning not to be noticed.

Odessa knew that stillness. She'd worn it herself, for about a year, after the arm and after Tam. Then she'd decided that hiding was just dying slowly with extra steps, and she'd gotten loud instead.

The crow, Bramble, cocked its scarred head at Rook and croaked, with terrible clarity: "*Fool.*"

Rook pointed at it without looking away from Marisol. "And another thing, that bird is a menace."

"That bird has better sense than you," Marisol said.

"That bird ate my bootlace."

"He apologized," Gideon said softly, not looking up from the net.

"He *croaked* at me."

"He's a crow. That's an apology. It's practically poetry, from him."

There was a beat of silence, and then the hold laughed — even Rook, grudgingly, even the twins who mostly communicated in elbow-nudges. And Odessa watched the girl flinch at the laughter before understanding, a heartbeat later, that it wasn't aimed at her. Watched her shoulders come down half an inch. Watched her almost-smile at the netting in her hands like it had surprised her.

Well, thought Odessa. There it is.

"All right." She set down the mug. The sound of her stump rapping the crate cut through everything; it always did. Twenty years on the water and the crews still went quiet for a knuckle-tap like schoolchildren. "Rook. You're wrong, and I'll tell you why in a way that even you can follow. Sit."

Rook sat.

"We don't sell her because selling her gets us all burned, yes, Marisol covered that. But there's a second reason, and it's the real one." Odessa looked around the hold — at Marisol, at the twins Pip and Palla, at old Hesper who cooked and eavesdropped with equal skill, at young Fen by the door pretending not to hang on every word. At Gideon, watching her now with those careful grey eyes, the crow still as a carved thing on her shoulder. "The reason is that we're the Saltcellars, and the Saltcellars don't sell people."

"We smuggle people," Pip said.

"Different trade entirely. Smuggling's moving folk from trouble toward safety. Selling's the reverse. Anybody confused?"

Nobody was confused. Rook looked sulky but convinced, which was his natural resting state.

"But," Odessa went on, because the harder part was coming and she wanted it done clean, "there's a third thing, and this one isn't a vote. This is me talking, and when I'm done, anyone who doesn't like it can take their share and walk, no hard feelings, door's that way."

The hold went properly quiet now. Even the crow shifted.

"The finders aren't hunting her because she's worth fifty crowns. They're hunting her because of what she is. Beast-speaker. Winterbourne blood, if Hesper's hedge-witch friend is right, and Hesper's usually righter than any of us enjoy." She nodded at Hesper, who inclined her head with the modest air of a woman who had been right about many regrettable things. "You all heard what the rats told us. Something under the palace. A shining hungry thing walking on moonless nights. The king's prayers getting answered, and answered *wrong*. That's not a bounty, that's a war coming. And when wars come, folk like us get to choose which side we stood on, afterward, when everybody asks."

"And you've chosen?" Marisol asked quietly.

"I chose eleven years ago, on a scaffold, watching them take my brother apart for a gift smaller than hers." Odessa kept her voice level. She'd had practice. "Tam could talk to moths. Moths! Harmless as anything the gods ever made, and they burned him for it while the crowd sang hymns. I lost the arm same night, reaching for him." She lifted the empty sleeve, folded it once, neat, and set it on the table between them like evidence. "So yes. I've chosen. She stays. We keep her. And if the Crown comes for her, the Crown learns what the Saltcellars cost."

Silence. Somewhere above, water slapped the hull. The lamp guttered and steadied.

It was Fen, youngest of them, who broke it — voice cracking halfway: "They say the new captain of finders, Vess, took Tamsin's gift out of her with questions. Just... questions. For hours." His eyes went to Gideon. "Is that true? Can they do that to anyone?"

Gideon's hands had stopped on the netting. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "Yes."

"Then I'm in," Fen said. "Whatever we're doing. I'm in."

"Me too," said Palla. Pip elbowed her and added, "Obviously both of us. We're a matched set."

Hesper simply said, "I've been in since the canal, dear. Do keep up."

Marisol looked at Odessa for a long moment, and something passed between them that needed no words — the look of two women who had both buried people the Edict took and had agreed, silently, over years of shared rum and grief, that the next time someone needed standing in front of, there'd be standing. "In," she said. "Obviously."

All eyes went to Rook. Rook sighed the sigh of a man surrendering a fortune in imaginary crowns. "Fine. In. But I want it noted that I raised the economic argument and was shouted down by sentiment."

"Noted," Odessa said. "Sentiment wins. It usually does on this boat, which is why we're poor and happy."

That got the laugh again, easier this time, and Odessa let it run its course before she stood. Her knees cracked. Everything cracked these days except, apparently, the Edict of Ash.

"Gideon," she said. "Come here."

The girl rose slowly, setting down the ruined net. The crow rode her shoulder, and up close Odessa could see the old scars on its beak, the notched ear of some long-ago violence survived. A survivor's bird for a survivor's girl.

"I'm not going to make you swear anything," Odessa said. "You've spent three years swearing nothing to nobody, and it's kept you breathing. But this crew has decided you're ours, and I don't mean as cargo or bait or coin. I mean as family. Which means we eat together, we grieve together, and when the finders come — and they will come, love, sooner than any of us wants — we stand together. That's the whole oath. There's no magic in it. There's barely any grammar."

A sound escaped Gideon then, half laugh, half sob, quickly smothered. Her eyes were bright. "Why?" she asked, and it came out raw. "You don't know me. I'm trouble. I'm *worth* trouble. Every sensible instinct you have should be telling you to put me back in the canal."

"Every sensible instinct I have has been wrong since the Godfall," Odessa said. "Besides." She reached out with her one hand and turned the girl's chin up gently, the way she'd once done for Tam when he was small and frightened and certain he was a monster. "My brother could talk to moths. He used to tell them where to sleep so they wouldn't get eaten by bats. That was the whole of his wicked sorcery — kindness to small things. And they killed him for it." She let go. "I see a woman who talks to rats and cats and crows, and I think: there but for the grace of a bad night's swimming goes Tam. So no, I won't put you back in the canal. The canal's full enough."

For a moment Gideon couldn't speak. The crow leaned against her cheek, and she raised a hand to touch its feathers absently, anchoring herself.

Then she straightened, and something in her face changed — not the careful furniture-face, not the flinch, but something older and steadier underneath, surfacing like a keel finding water. "Then I'm yours too," she said. "Fair warning: I bring witch-finders, hungry gods, and one extremely rude bird."

"Bramble," Odessa said solemnly, "welcome aboard."

"*About time,*" said the crow.

* * *

Later, when the hold had emptied and the watch had settled, Odessa climbed topside to breathe air that didn't smell of argument. The moon was a thin paring over the rooftops of Vaelmoor, and the city beyond the canal glowed faintly gold at its heart — the palace district, where prayers were being answered by something patient and vast, and where fifty-crown notices were printed by lamplight.

Gideon was already there, at the rail, the crow a dark knot on her shoulder. Odessa joined her without asking permission. Family, she was discovering, involved a lot of unannounced standing next to people.

"They'll come for me," Gideon said. Not a question. "Maybe not tomorrow. But they will. And when they do, everything you just swore to them — it burns with me. You understand that. You're trading your crew's safety for a stranger."

"Eleven years ago I traded an arm for a stranger," Odessa said. "Worst bargain of my life, and I'd make it again before breakfast. That's not arithmetic, girl. That's just what you are, after. You spend what you've got on what matters, and you don't haggle."

Gideon was quiet a while. Below them, in the black water of the canal, something moved — a pale flicker, there and gone. A fish, probably. Probably.

"The rats say it's getting stronger," Gideon said at last. "Under the palace. On moonless nights it walks. Bramble says—" she glanced at the crow, "—Bramble says the crown is hungry, and crows don't lie, they just editorialize."

"Crowns are always hungry," Odessa said. "Difference is, this one's got teeth." She turned, leaning her back against the rail so she could watch the girl's face. "Here's what happens next. Hesper sends word to her hedge-witch about your bloodline. Meanwhile we move quiet, we move dark, and you stop looking over your shoulder, because looking over your shoulder is *my* job now. That's what family means. Division of labor."

A small, startled laugh. "You've thought about this a lot."

"I've had eleven years and one sleeve to think with." Odessa pushed off the rail and headed for the hatch, then paused. "Get some sleep, Winterbourne. Tomorrow you start earning your keep. First lesson's at dawn."

"What's the first lesson?"

"How to talk to gulls without insulting them. They remember. Grudge-holding little thieves, the lot of them — worse than Rook."

Behind her, she heard the girl laugh properly this time, and the crow cackle along, and below decks someone groan and demand quiet. Odessa smiled to herself in the dark and went down the ladder with her usual economy, one hand on the rail, the missing arm aching its old ghost-ache the way it always did when something felt like hope.

By morning, the answer would already be moving toward them: a stolen bundle of palace records, a disgraced physician with a guilty conscience and a fast horse, riding hard for the Underveil with the thirteenth turn written in her own careful hand.

But that was tomorrow's weather. Tonight there was salt on the wind, a full hold, and a crew sworn to something bigger than cargo — and Odessa Finch slept, for the first time in years, without dreaming of scaffolds.
