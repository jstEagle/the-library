## 10. The First Turning Moon

The moon rose over Vaelmoor like a coin someone had polished obsessively and then decided to show off.

Gideon felt it before she saw it. She was below decks on the *Saltcellar's Sorrow*, wedged between a crate of unlicensed candlewax and Odessa Finch's entire collection of extremely illegal tea, when the second heartbeat in her bones picked up its pace.

"It's starting," she said, to no one in particular, and then, because the ship had opinions about everything: "Bramble, wake up."

The crow was asleep in the rigging above, one foot tucked up, looking like a scrap of burnt cloth that had achieved tenure. One black eye opened. *"Moon,"* he croaked. *"Big one. Told you."*

"You tell me lots of things. Half of them are about bread."

*"Good bread,"* Bramble said, with dignity, and ruffled his feathers into a shape that meant *I am awake now and also I expect bread.*

By the time Gideon climbed topside, the whole crew was at the rail. Not because anyone had called them—because the river had gone quiet. The Underveil canals were never quiet. They sloshed and gurgled and muttered about the weather the way old sailors did. Tonight they had stopped entirely, held still as poured glass, and the full moon lay on their surface so perfectly that Odessa kept glancing down as if she expected her reflection to do something unauthorized.

"That's not right," said Pinch, the youngest of the crew, who was seventeen and said that about most things, including soup. "Rivers don't just—" 

He didn't finish, because the river began to flow backward.

Not violently. That was the horrible part. It simply reversed itself with the unhurried confidence of something that had been meaning to get around to it for three hundred years, sliding upstream past the moored barges, carrying leaves and a surprised-looking duck back the way it had come. The duck did not seem to enjoy this.

"Right," Odessa said flatly. Her missing arm made her balance a study in permanent negotiation; she leaned against the rail with her one hand and stared at the water like it owed her money. "Nobody touches the water. Nobody drinks the water. Gideon—ask it what it thinks it's doing."

"You want me to *negotiate* with a river."

"I want you to ask it politely whether we're all about to die, yes. That's the job now. Congratulations."

Gideon crouched at the rail and let her awareness slip down into the current—not the water itself but the things living in it, the fish and the eels and one enormous, ancient catfish who lived under the weir gate and whom she'd spoken to exactly once, an experience she described to herself as *humbling*.

The catfish was frightened. That came through first, before any words: a broad, muddy terror, the kind of fear a creature has when the ground moves. Then, reluctantly, images. The moon too bright. The water forgetting which way was downhill. And beneath everything, beneath the mud and the stones and the roots of the city itself—

*a shining hunger turning over in its sleep, opening one eye*

Gideon snatched her mind back so hard she sat down on the deck.

"Gideon?" Pinch was beside her instantly, all jokes gone. "What is it? What did it say?"

She found her voice somewhere near her boots. "It didn't say anything. That's the problem." She looked up at the palace on its hill, pale in the moonlight, every window dark except one. "Everything else is saying something. Every rat, every gull, every fish in this river—they're all looking at the same thing. They've been looking for days. I thought they were just nervous."

"And now?"

"Now I think they're watching."

Odessa pushed off the rail. "Then we watch too. All hands stay aboard. Gideon—" and here her voice did the thing it almost never did, the thing where the gravel softened into something that was nearly gentleness, "—you don't reach for anything tonight. You hear me? Whatever's out there, whatever's stirring—you keep that gift of yours tucked in tight as a purse in Cutpurse Alley. Tonight the whole city is shouting, and I'll not have you answering."

Gideon wanted to argue. She was, she reflected later, getting quite good at wanting to argue with Odessa Finch and then not doing it. "Aye, Captain," she said.

Bramble hopped down onto her shoulder and pressed his scarred head briefly against her cheek, which was crow for *I am worried about you and will never admit it again.* 

*"Small hours,"* he murmured. *"That's when it turns. Sleep with your boots on."*

* * *

Serah Vane watched the moon from the physician's gallery above the throne room, and she did not sleep either, though not for lack of trying.

She had told herself she was there to monitor the king. His pulse had been erratic for three days—erratic in a way she had no word for, because it wasn't fast or slow, it was *synchronized*, beating in time with something she couldn't hear. She had told herself a great many things over the past weeks. It was remarkable, she thought, how much nonsense a person could tell themselves and still function.

The records were sewn into the lining of her coat. Thirteen turnings. Thirteen full moons from the first answered prayer, and on the thirteenth, the binding would finish dissolving—not break, dissolve, the way sugar dissolves, leaving nothing behind but sweetness and the thing that had been hidden in it. She had read the ledger entries four times. She had checked the arithmetic twice, because she was the sort of person who checked arithmetic while committing treason, and both times the answer had been the same: eleven moons gone. Two left.

Below her, through the floor grate, she could see the throne room.

King Aldric was praying.

He did that, some nights. She had documented it for years in her clinical notes: *His Majesty exhibits nocturnal devotional behavior, origin unclear, duration variable.* She had assumed it was a tic, a fragment of the man he'd been before the hollowing, some ghostly muscle memory of faith. She had written it down in her neat physician's hand and prescribed him camomile.

Tonight she could hear the words, and they were not prayers any god of Vaelmoor had ever received.

They weren't even words, quite. They were shapes of sound, syllables arranged the way a mimic arranges birdsong—correct in every particular and wrong in total. Aldric knelt beneath the empty altar of the Hollow Shrine with his head bowed and his hands open at his sides, and out of his mouth came the sound of something practicing being devout. Learning it. Getting better.

And around him, kneeling in the shadows where no courtiers were permitted to stand, were the others. Lord Chancellor Merrick. The Captain of the Inner Guard. Three bishops of the Edict—the Edict, which had burned the old gods' names out of books, whose founding law was *there is nothing left to pray to*. They knelt on cold stone in their fine clothes, faces lifted, mouths moving in silence, following along.

Serah's hands were steady on the rail. She noticed this with detached professional interest, the way she'd once noted a patient's steady hands during a botched amputation and understood, distantly, that the steadiness was hers and the screaming was everyone else's.

The court knew. Of course the court knew. They hadn't chosen ignorance; they'd chosen service. Somewhere in those first terrified nights after the beggar woman's hand healed, the powerful men of Vaelmoor had done their sums and concluded that a god in the palace was not a catastrophe but an opportunity, provided one got in early with the kneeling.

Something changed in the room below. The air pressure shifted—she felt it pop softly in her ears—and the king raised his head.

His eyes found the grate. Found her. Thirty feet of stone and iron between them, and his eyes found her the way a needle finds north.

He smiled. Aldric's smile, the gentle, tired smile she'd known for nine years of treating him—but wider at the corners than a face should allow, held a half-second too long, like a portrait that had learned to move.

Then he turned back to his prayer, and Serah discovered her hands were no longer steady after all.

She backed away from the grate on legs that belonged to someone else, gathered her coat with its stolen lining, and walked very calmly out of the physician's gallery, down the servants' stair, past two guards who did not stop her because no one had yet told them to, and out into the moon-drowned streets of a city whose river was running the wrong way.

Two moons, she thought. One, if the god got impatient. She needed to find the smugglers. She needed, absurdly, to find a charm-seller named Gideon Winterbourne, because a witch-finder informant had mentioned the name in her hearing three days ago and she had spent every hour since turning it over like a stone.

The moon climbed. Behind her, in the throne room, something practiced being devout, and got better.

* * *

The beasts came at midnight.

Gideon heard them before she saw them—paws on cobbles, hooves on the canal bridges, the soft industrial hum of ten thousand small bodies moving with purpose. She went up to the deck and stopped breathing.

They were coming down every street that led toward the palace hill. Rats in rivers along the gutters. Cats walking shoulder to shoulder, feral and tame alike, the butcher's fat tom alongside wild things with torn ears. Dogs. Foxes come down from the northern hills, which had not happened in living memory. A stag, actually a stag, antlers like a chandelier, picking its way daintily through Fishmarket Square as though it had an appointment. And overhead, the sky was black with birds—not circling, not crying out. Flying in a slow, deliberate spiral above the palace, like water going down a drain, only upward.

None of them made a sound.

"They're not being driven," Gideon whispered. Her breath fogged although the night wasn't cold. "There's nothing behind them. They're just... going. Like pilgrims."

Pinch had his hand on the hilt of a knife he clearly had no idea how to use. "Should we stop them?"

"With what? Strongly worded feelings?" Odessa shoved him gently toward the hold. "Below decks, all of you. Gideon, that includes—"

But Gideon wasn't listening, because a rat had come aboard.

It trotted up the mooring line, past Odessa, straight across the deck as if it owned shares in the ship, and sat down in front of Gideon's boot. It was a dock rat, big and battered, one ear notched. She had spoken to its cousin once about grain shipments. And it looked up at her with small wet eyes, and she felt the pull of it—not her reaching out, but something reaching *in*, using the rat as a door, and through the door:

attention.

There was no other word for it. A vast, patient, delighted attention, turning toward her the way the birds turned over the palace, and inside the attention a voice like honey poured over broken glass:

***there you are***

It was not angry. That was the worst of it. It was pleased. It had been looking for her the way a diner looks through a menu, and now it had found the dish it wanted, and its attention closed around her mind like a hand around a moth—

*the last of the binding blood, come home at last, I remember your grandmother, I remember her hands, I could give them back to you, I could give you everything, little speaker, all you must do is—*

Somewhere very far away, a crow screamed.

Bramble hit the rat at full diving speed—a black blur off the rigging, talons first, knocking it skidding across the deck. The connection snapped. Gideon came back into her body all at once, on her knees, nose bleeding freely onto the planks, ears ringing, every joint feeling unpacked and repacked by someone in a hurry.

The rat shook itself, gave Bramble a look of profound affront, and scampered away unharmed. That somehow made it worse. It hadn't been an attack. It had been a *greeting*.

"Gideon!" Odessa was on her knees too, one hand gripping Gideon's shoulder hard enough to bruise. "Hey. Eyes here. What happened, what did it do?"

"It said hello," Gideon managed. Blood dripped off her chin. She started laughing, which was probably shock, and the laugh came out wrong, high and thin. "It said hello and it offered me my grandmother. It knows things it shouldn't know, Odessa, it looked at me like—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Like I was dinner it had pre-ordered."

Bramble landed on her other shoulder, breathing hard, feathers askew. He pressed his beak against her temple, gently, once.

*"Mine,"* he said to the departing rat, to the night, to the palace on the hill, in a voice like a knife wrapped in velvet. *"She's mine, you great shining glutton. Find your own."*

"Bramble," Gideon wheezed, "did you just threaten a god on my behalf?"

*"Someone had to. You were bleeding on the deck."*

Odessa hauled Gideon upright, bracing most of her weight without comment or ceremony. Up close, Gideon could see the fear in the captain's face, carefully filed down flat. "Can it reach you again? Through any animal?"

"Any animal in Vaelmoor, probably. Maybe farther." Gideon wiped her nose with the back of her wrist. "Captain, listen to me—it isn't the king. I thought we had until the thirteenth moon, I thought we had time, but it's not waiting for some rite. It doesn't need a ritual. It needs *prayers*. Every person in this city who's hoping the old power will fix their lives is feeding it, and there are thousands of them, and they're praying *right now*—"

Across the water, a bell began to ring. Then another. Then a dozen, the alarm bells of the witch-finders' citadel, clanging out across the backward-running river.

From the top of Palace Hill came light—white, sourceless, rising like heat-shimmer off the great dome—and with it a sound that none of them ever managed to describe afterward, because it was less a sound than a *sensation*, the acoustic equivalent of standing too close to a fire. Screaming threaded through it. Human screaming.

"The shrine garrison," Odessa said quietly. "Witch-finder barracks are next to the shrine."

They stood at the rail, the whole crew having crept back up despite orders, and watched the white light bloom and gutter and bloom again. Through the beasts' silence, through the ringing bells, Gideon could feel the edge of what was happening on the hill—could feel, sickeningly, the *satisfaction*.

Later, survivors would tell it in fragments: how Witch-finder Captain Hollis Arden, a man famous throughout Vaelmoor for the fervor of his anti-magic sermons, had walked out into the shrine courtyard at the height of the moon, fallen to his knees, and begun to pray—not to the silenced gods, but loudly, desperately, to the *Edict itself*, to law and ash and absence, *begging the void to stay void*. How the light had come down on him like a struck match coming down on a wick. How when it faded there was nothing left of him at all, not ash, not bone—only his boots, standing upright, and his badge, and a smell like incense and copper. How the other witch-finders had knelt, to a man, and prayed to nothing, and been spared.

The god had eaten the loudest denier first. As a demonstration. As an *advertisement*.

"He's not a man at all," Gideon said. Her voice came out small. "I keep thinking of him as a man wearing a crown. He's not. He's a hunger, and it has a calendar, and we're on it."

Odessa turned from the rail. When she spoke, her voice was the flat, hard thing it became when she was making decisions instead of having feelings. "Two moons to the thirteenth turning. Maybe less, after tonight. So here's what happens: nobody prays on this ship—we'll whistle, we'll swear, we'll sing filthy songs, but nobody hopes out loud. Gideon gets guarded when she sleeps. And come morning, we find the physician."

Gideon blinked. "The—what?"

"Serah Vane. Disgraced royal physician, marked for surveillance, vanished from the palace tonight with something stuffed in her coat worth killing for. My contacts say she's asking after smugglers." Odessa's mouth twitched, which for her was a grin. "Desperate people always find us eventually. It's practically our business model."

"Why do we want her?"

"Because she spent nine years with her hands on that king's chest," Odessa said, "and she's the only soul in Vaelmoor who might know what's growing in there. And because, lass—" she nodded at the palace, at the spiral of silent birds against the bloated moon, at the boots standing empty in the shrine courtyard, "—tonight taught us the size of what we're fighting. We're going to need everyone who's willing. Even the ones who took their time about it."

Above them, the last of the birds settled onto the palace roof, fold upon fold of black wings, a congregation with nowhere left to kneel. The river slid backward in the moonlight. And far away, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, Gideon felt the shining hunger settle back into its long patience, satisfied, fed, and—she was certain of this, certain down in her rearranged bones—already counting.

One moon left. Perhaps not even that.

Bramble tightened his grip on her shoulder. *"Boots on,"* he reminded her. *"Sleep sitting up. And Gideon?"*

"Yes?"

*"It knows your name now. So we'd best go make sure it regrets learning it."*
