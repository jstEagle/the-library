## 11. Tamsin's Cell

The witch-finder holding at Candlewick Row had once been a granary. Gideon knew it from her old life—she'd bought rye flour there on market days, haggling with a fat-cheerful miller who gave extra to children. Now the windows were barred with iron lattice and the miller was gone, and the smell that drifted from the loading doors at night was not grain.

"It smells like fear," said the rat on her shoulder, whose name was Pib and whose opinion of most things was low. "Fear and bad soup. They feed them badly on purpose. I heard it from a mouse who got in through the drains."

"Mice will say anything," Gideon murmured.

"Mice are honest," Pib sniffed. "It's crows you can't trust. No offense meant to your feathered associate."

"None taken," said Bramble, from his perch in the crook of her elbow. "Though for accuracy's sake, I'd like the record to show that mice are liars and cowards, and this one is currently eating a crumb you dropped while pretending to scout."

Pib froze mid-crumb.

Gideon almost smiled. Almost. It was hard to find smiles tonight, crouched in the reek of the Candlewick canal with nine smugglers around her and the holding's lanterns burning behind its iron lattice like eyes that never blinked.

*Three years,* she thought, and the thought had teeth. *Three years I walked past buildings like this and told myself there was nothing I could do.*

Odessa Finch crouched beside her at the waterline, her single sleeve pinned and knotted at the shoulder, studying the loading doors the way she studied everything: as an opponent to be outwaited. "Two guards on the dock door," she said quietly. "One walks the roof. The rest are inside, and inside is where it gets stupid."

"When does it not get stupid?" asked Wick, a wiry young man whose entire contribution to planning meetings so far had been pessimism delivered cheerfully.

"When we stop being clever and start being fast," Odessa said. "Clever is for people with time. We've got until the tide turns, then the canal drops and every boat down here scrapes bottom loud enough to wake the garrison. So." She looked around the boat, at each face in turn. "We go in the way the rats say the drains go. Gideon?"

Every eye turned to her. Three weeks ago that would have been the worst moment of any night. Now it only made her stomach clench instead of seize.

"The drain mouth is under the east pilings," she said. "Rats use it. The grate's rusted through at the bottom left corner—big enough for a person if they don't mind the eels."

"I mind the eels," said Wick.

"Noted," said Odessa. "You'll be first in, then. Motivation."

There was a scatter of soft laughter, quickly smothered—the kind of laughter crews make before violence, because the alternative is being sick over the gunwale. Gideon felt Bramble shift against her arm, and beneath the joke and the jostle she felt what he was actually doing: watching her. He'd been watching her since the plan was laid, silent in a way he almost never was, and when their eyes met he said, very quietly, in the voice no one else could hear:

"You're looking for her."

"Yes," Gideon said aloud, because hiding things from the crew had gotten harder and she'd stopped trying.

"Then let's go get her," Bramble said, "and try not to set anything on fire that isn't load-bearing."

* * *

The drains were exactly as advertised: rusted, eel-haunted, and narrow enough that Gideon spent forty yards with her cheek pressed to wet stone, breathing through her mouth and apologizing silently to whatever god hadn't answered yet for every unkind thing she'd ever thought about rats. Pib rode her hood and narrated the whole way, which was either comforting or maddening depending on how closely she listened.

"Left here. Mind the trap wire—they string bells across the low passages now, clever monkeys. Up through the floor gap. There's a guard asleep above you; he snores in B-flat and dreams about his mother's kitchen, poor lamb."

The floor gap came up behind stacked grain sacks in what had been the mill's weighing room. Gideon rose through it into lamplight and the smell again—that smell, fear-sweat and carbolic soap and something scorched underneath, something that made her gift recoil in her chest like a hand pulled back from a hot stove.

She knew what burned like that. She'd smelled it once before, years ago, when the witch-finders took the tanner's daughter on Cooper's Lane and did whatever they did in wagons with black curtains.

The weighing room was empty except for the sleeping guard and rows of small iron cages along the wall, each barely large enough for a grown person to sit upright. Most were empty. Two were not.

In the nearest, a boy of maybe fifteen pressed himself against the bars, and when he saw Gideon his face did something terrible—it tried to hope and couldn't quite remember how. In the second cage, an older man sat perfectly still with his hands open in his lap, staring at nothing, and Gideon understood without being told that he had gone somewhere inside himself and locked the door.

"Hey," she whispered to the boy, kneeling. "Hey. We're getting you out. Can you walk?"

His mouth worked. "They ask," he said, in a cracked whisper. "Every day they ask the same question and if I answer wrong—" He held up his hands. His fingers were fine and clever-looking, a craftsman's fingers, and three of the nails were gone.

"We know the questions," Gideon said, and hated how gentle her voice could be, how practiced. She'd used that voice on frightened animals for years. It worked the same. That was the part that shamed her—that it worked the same. "Wick. Through here. Take them down to the boats, slow and quiet."

Wick came up through the floor gap, saw the cages, and lost all his cheerfulness at once. He didn't say anything clever. He just got to work on the locks, jaw tight.

Bramble landed on Gideon's shoulder and pressed his scarred beak briefly to her ear. Not words. Just weight.

"Where?" she asked him softly. "Can you smell her? Smell me—you've spent enough time on my shoulder. Find the thread."

The crow tilted his head, one dark eye catching the lamp. Then he lifted off, gliding low along the corridor beyond the weighing room, and paused at a door at the far end—a heavy door, oak-banded, with a slot for passing trays and a lock the size of a fist.

"No," Gideon breathed. "No, they wouldn't put her in solitary, she's not dangerous, she mends *bones*, she couldn't hex a—"

But even as she said it she remembered the notices posted after Tamsin was taken. *Unlicensed practitioner, bone-craft, believed capable of more.* The Crown always inflated what you could do. It was cheaper than admitting they were afraid of what anyone could do.

The corridor was quiet. The guard rotation wouldn't come round again for six minutes; Odessa had timed it off a bribed laundry-woman and a bored sentry's habits. Six minutes. Gideon crossed to the door, and her hands were shaking, and she made herself look at that shaking honestly, because lying to herself was how she'd wasted three years.

This was not going to be the reunion she'd rehearsed on the boat. She had rehearsed it anyway—everyone does. In the rehearsal Tamsin was thin but grinning, and said something sharp about Gideon's hair, and hit her arm, and forgave her, or at least agreed to be angry later, in safety, over stew.

She put her palm flat against the oak.

"Tamsin?" she whispered. "Tamsin, it's Gid. It's Gideon Winterbourne."

Nothing. Then, faintly, from inside: a sound like someone trying to speak through a mouth full of broken glass.

Gideon's heart went sideways. She wrenched at the lock with the picks Odessa had drilled her on—her fingers clumsy, her breath too fast—and it seemed to take a year, though it was perhaps ninety seconds, before the mechanism surrendered with a click that sounded obscene in the silence.

The door swung open on darkness and that smell, stronger now, the scorched thing, and Gideon's eyes adjusted and she saw Tamsin sitting against the far wall with her knees drawn up, and she stopped moving altogether.

They'd kept her in the dark. That was plain. They'd kept her in the dark and cold, and she was thinner than memory allowed, hair lank, wrists ringed with healing shackle-sores. But none of that was the thing. The thing was her hands.

Tamsin's hands had always moved. Even sitting still, even asleep, they'd twitched with little craftsman's rhythms—thumbing invisible joints, testing unseen splints. Now they lay in her lap like something unloaded from a cart. And when Gideon stepped into the cell, Tamsin flinched from the light, and looked up, and her eyes found Gideon's face—

—and kept going past it. Searching. The way a person searches a crowd for someone who isn't coming.

"Gid?" Tamsin's voice was gravel and rust. "Is that—why can't I hear you? Why is everyone quiet?"

Gideon's mouth opened. Nothing came out.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. Tamsin's gift wasn't bone-craft alone. It had always had a listening edge to it—she could feel the hum of a fracture setting true, feel the little song of marrow knitting. She used to joke that she could hear skeletons gossiping. She'd heard Gideon's heartbeat quicken whenever Gideon lied about where she'd been, and had teased her mercilessly for it.

And now the room was silence to her. The interrogation hadn't just hurt her. It had burned the listening out, the way you burn a field so nothing grows back.

"They asked me questions," Tamsin said, in that ruined whisper, still searching Gideon's face like it might explain itself. "For a long time. Weeks? I think it was weeks. And there was a machine, or—or a man who was like a machine, and he kept asking where the speakers were, and I didn't know, I swear I didn't know anything, and then one day I woke up and—" She lifted her hands. Stared at them. "It's quiet, Gid. Everything's quiet. Did you bring my kit? I can't—I can't feel whether bones are set right anymore, I need my kit—"

"Tamsin." Gideon knelt. Her knees hit stone and she barely felt it. "Look at me."

Tamsin looked. And in her eyes Gideon saw the truth arrive ahead of her words—the way it does, the way the body knows before the mind consents—and she watched Tamsin begin, very slowly, to understand why her friend had come down a drain at midnight with iron picks and strangers' footsteps behind her.

"Oh," Tamsin said.

"Tamsin, I'm so sorry. I'm so sorry, I should have—"

"Don't." It wasn't harsh. It was worse than harsh; it was tired. Tamsin closed her eyes. "Don't do the speech. Please. I haven't got anything left to forgive you *with*."

* * *

Getting her out was the easy part. Gideon would remember that with a strange, useless gratitude forever: that Tamsin could still walk, that she leaned on Gideon's shoulder through the drain without complaint, that the worst of the night's physical work was done by other people—Wick carrying the boy, Odessa's crew sweeping the corridors with a competence that looked, from the inside, like anger wearing discipline as a coat.

The hard part came later, on the Saltcellar's broad barge, in the lamplit hold among crates of contraband tea, when the rescued were wrapped in blankets and fed, and the crew's usual post-raid noise—boasting, recounting, Wick reliving his eel ordeal with embellishments—went carefully quiet around the corner of the hold where Gideon sat with Tamsin.

Because the crew knew. They'd known since Gideon had come aboard three weeks ago saying Tamsin's name like a wound she kept touching. And they were giving the two women space the way sailors give space: loudly, by being elsewhere, except for Bramble, who perched on a tea crate six feet away with the studied innocence of a creature pretending not to listen.

Tamsin held a cup of broth in both hands and didn't drink it. Her hands were still. Gideon kept catching herself waiting for them to move.

"The resistance," Tamsin said at last. "That's real, then. It's not just—stories people tell in cells to keep each other sane. There's really people. Boats. Plans." Her voice scraped. "I thought I was going mad in there, making it up. A whole network under the city, and nobody ever came for me."

"We didn't know where you were," Gideon said. "We looked—"

"For three weeks." Tamsin nodded slowly. "And before that? Before I was taken?" She turned her head. Her eyes weren't accusing. That was the unbearable part. They were just asking, genuinely asking, with the flat honesty of someone who has been emptied of everything softer and harder alike. "You knew, Gid. You've always known. You hear the cats talk about the raids. You hear the rats describe the wagon routes. You've had ears in every gutter in Vaelmoor for three years, and you sold luck-knots and kept your head down and told yourself—what did you tell yourself?"

"That I'd get us both killed," Gideon said. It came out raw. "That one wrong word in front of the wrong cat—one informant's window cracked open—and they'd take us both, and no one would come for either of us, because there *was* no one. That surviving was the job. Keeping us alive until things changed."

"Things changed," Tamsin said. She looked down at her hands. "For you."

The hold was very quiet. Somewhere above, water slapped the hull. Bramble hadn't moved, but Gideon could feel him listening with his whole body, the way birds do—not hearing, attending.

"I'm not going to apologize my way out of it," Gideon said finally. "I've tried writing the speech. There isn't one. All I've got is: I was a coward, and I dressed it up as caution, and you paid for it. And I'm not going to be a coward anymore, and I can't give you back what they took, and I hate that. I hate it so much I can hardly see straight."

Tamsin was silent a long time. When she spoke, her voice had found a fraction of its old dry edge, worn thin but recognizable, like a coin rubbed nearly smooth.

"You always did hate owing people," she said. "Remember when I fixed your wrist, that winter? And you turned up the next day with a pie you'd clearly spent your last coppers on, and pretended you'd baked two."

"You cried over that pie."

"I did not cry. My eyes sweated." But the corner of her mouth moved—the ghost of a ghost—and then faded. She set the untouched broth down. "Here's the thing, Gid. Here's what I figured out in the dark, when I was trying to decide whether to keep existing." She flexed her fingers, watching them not move. "They didn't take my magic to punish me. Punishment ends. They took it so I couldn't help anyone ever again. So the question isn't whether I forgive you. The question is whether I'm still worth anything with empty hands. And I don't—" Her breath hitched, steadied. "I don't know the answer yet. Ask me again when I do."

Gideon reached over and took those still hands in hers. They were cold. They stayed still. But Tamsin's grip tightened, weakly, and held on.

"Then stay," Gideon said. "Stay until you know. The Saltcellars don't ask what you're worth. Odessa keeps a ledger and your name's already in it, and the entry says 'crew,' and that's the whole entry."

From the tea crate, Bramble said, "There's also a footnote about snoring," and Tamsin laughed—one short, cracked, astonished laugh, as if surprised to find the machinery still worked—and Gideon felt something in her chest crack and run warm, which was as close to absolution as she supposed she'd get, and closer than she deserved.

* * *

Odessa found her at the rail near dawn, when the barge was sliding home through the grey Underveil mist and Vaelmoor's rooftops stood above them like the backs of sleeping animals. The captain leaned her one good elbow on the rail and said nothing for a while, which was her way of talking.

"She'll eat eventually," Odessa said at last. "The empty ones always fight the food first. Like it's surrender."

"How many empty ones have you seen?"

"More than I want to count." Odessa's gaze stayed on the water. "After the last purge, my brother's crew. Two of them came out of the holdings alive. One of them was empty. He ferried for me eleven years. Never spoke of it. Died in his bunk with a full net and a calm face, and I choose to believe that counts as winning."

Gideon watched the mist curl. "The hedge-witch," she said quietly. "Maren. She said gifts burn back sometimes. If the root isn't dead. She said she'd seen it twice."

"She say how long it takes?"

"Years. Maybe never." Gideon turned the words over like stones, looking for the shape underneath. "Odessa, she also said something else, the night she taught me the first part of the rite. She said the binding on the king renews at every full moon, and the thirteenth turn completes it. Eleven moons have passed." She looked at the captain. "We have two moons left. Less, now, counting travel and preparation and every way this can go wrong. And Maren won't teach me the rest of the rite until I answer a question she says only I can answer honestly. She wouldn't tell me what the question is. She said I'd know it when it found me."

Odessa was quiet. Then, in the tone of a woman adding a line to a ledger she intended to balance personally: "Then I suppose we'd best get you home before it finds you somewhere inconvenient."

Above them, out of the thinning mist, Bramble wheeled once, twice—and then flew suddenly toward the city wall, screaming a single word that Gideon had never heard him scream before, a word from the old tongue that raised every hair on her arms:

"*Winterbourne.*"

Down the canal, in the direction of the walls, every rat on every rooftop had risen to stand facing the palace.
