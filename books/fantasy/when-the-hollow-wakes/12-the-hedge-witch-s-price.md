## 12. The Hedge-Witch's Price

The hedge-witch's cottage sat where the Underveil forgot to be a city—a lean-to of driftwood and old boat planks wedged between two leaning tenements, roofed in river reeds, with a chimney that smoked sideways as if even the fire were trying not to be noticed. A sign hung above the door: *No cures. No curses. No questions.*

"She's charming," Bramble muttered from Gideon's shoulder. "I can see why she's beloved by all."

"Be polite."

"I am always polite. I simply narrate honestly."

Gideon knocked twice, then—remembering Maren's instructions—twice more and once softly, like a heartbeat losing its nerve.

The door opened on a chain, one eye visible in the gap. The eye was pale green and deeply unimpressed. "You're late."

"You said dusk."

"Dusk was an hour ago. Dusk is a mood, girl, not a schedule. It comes when it comes, and so should you." The door shut. The chain rattled. It opened again, whole. "Well? Come in before the dark gets ideas."

Inside, the cottage smelled of tallow and dried yarrow and something older underneath—the way cellars smell, the way caves smell, the way the world smells when it's been holding its breath for thirty years. Bundles of herbs hung from every beam. Jars lined the walls, labeled in a spidery hand: *foxfire moss*, *grave-soot*, *do not open, Odessa*.

The hedge-witch herself was small and bent as a question mark, wrapped in three shawls despite the mild night, her white hair braided tight against her skull. Her hands were the surprise—steady, unspotted, young-looking, as if they'd made a separate arrangement with time than the rest of her had.

"Maren," she said, offering no handshake. "And you're the Winterbourne. Sit. Don't touch the jars. Especially not the one marked for Odessa—that woman has the self-restraint of a gull."

Bramble hopped from Gideon's shoulder to a rafter beam and settled there like a gargoyle with opinions. Maren looked up at him, and something flickered across her face—not recognition exactly, but its cousin.

"That bird's been dead longer than you've been alive," she said.

"He mentions it constantly."

"Do I," said Bramble.

Maren snorted, which Gideon would learn was the closest thing she offered to warmth, and shuffled to the hearth, where a kettle already waited over coals banked low. She poured three cups—one for herself, one for Gideon, and set the third on a shelf near the window without explanation. Steam rose from it into empty air.

Gideon didn't ask. Three years in Vaelmoor had taught her that some hospitality was extended to guests she couldn't see.

* * *

"Now." Maren lowered herself into a chair that seemed to have grown around her rather than been built. "Tell me what you know about your grandmother."

"Not much." Gideon wrapped both hands around her cup. "Elowen Winterbourne. She left Vaelmoor before I was born—or was taken, or fled, depending on who tells it. My mother never spoke of her except to say we don't use the gift where anyone can see. That was the whole inheritance. One sentence and a lifetime of flinching."

"Mm." Maren drank. "And what do you know of the binding?"

"That there is one. In the king. My line made it." Gideon hesitated. "That's all, really. That's why I came to you. You knew her, Odessa says. You knew what she knew."

"What she knew." Maren turned the phrase over like a coin she suspected was clipped. "Girl, what Elowen Winterbourne *knew* could fill this room twice over, and half of it would curdle your blood, and the other half would make you wish you'd stayed a charm-seller hawking luck-knots to fools until your fingers gave out. But fine. Fine." She set down her cup. "You want the shape of it. So we start at the shape of it."

She rose, crossed to a chest beneath the window shelf, and drew out a bundle of oilcloth that crackled with age. Inside lay pages—not paper, but thin sheets of bark-parchment covered in dense, looping script, and among them a diagram drawn in faded iron-gall ink: two circles interlocked, one filled with names, one left deliberately blank.

"This is the rite," Maren said. "The unbinding. Written down by your great-great-grandmother, corrected by your grandmother, and cursed by every soul who read it after."

Gideon leaned close. The two circles were joined at a single point, and at that point the ink had been pressed so hard it had nearly torn through.

"The god in the king—it isn't a demon, whatever the Edict's preachers scream. It isn't a sickness. It's a *weight*. Something vast folded very small and tied into a man the way you'd tie a knot in a rope. Your ancestors didn't banish it. They couldn't. You cannot banish hunger; hunger only waits. So they bound it—took the knot of it and tied it around a willing vessel, and tied themselves around the vessel, and the vessel's line around that, generation upon generation, each Winterbourne adding their thread to keep it cinched." Her finger traced the outer circle. "The binding renews. Every full moon, the vessel's flesh tries to shed the weight—any body would—and every full moon, the knot must be pulled tight again. By a speaker. Of the line."

"The thirteenth turn," Gideon said quietly. "Serah's records. The renewal completes something."

"It completes *him*." Maren's voice dropped. "Thirteen turns, thirteen tightenings, and the knot becomes the rope becomes the thing itself. The god stops being bound and starts being *installed*. After the thirteenth turn there is no unbinding. There is only worship, or ash."

Outside, somewhere in the dark, a dog began barking—then stopped mid-cry, as if it had thought better of it.

"And the rite undoes it," Gideon said. "Before the thirteenth turn."

"The rite *transfers* it." Maren held up one steady young hand. "Understand me, child, because everyone before you got this part wrong, or got it right and lied about it. You cannot destroy the binding. You can only move it. Unhook it from the king's bones and hook it into—" she tapped the blank circle in the diagram, "—a new vessel. A speaker, willing, of the line. Someone who takes the knot into their own body and holds it. Forever."

The fire ticked. The third cup steamed on its shelf.

"Forever," Gideon repeated.

"Forever," Maren agreed, mercilessly pleasant. "It's not a cage you lock and walk away from. It's a cage you climb inside of and lock behind yourself. The god will live in you as it lived in him—whispering, hungry, patient. And you will hold it. Until your body gives out. And here's the mercy, such as it is—" she smiled, and it did not reach her eyes, "—your body will give out quickly. The vessel must be emptied to make room. What you are now, your memories, your stubbornness, that ridiculous bird's worth of hope you carry around—you pour it out to take the weight in. The last three who tried lasted days. Hours, one of them."

Gideon heard her own voice from very far away. "Three?"

"Your great-aunt Sable. Your uncle Corren. And Elowen's sister, Wren, who went first and wrote most of these notes in the margins because she wanted the next one to understand what they'd gotten wrong." Maren gathered the pages gently, the way one handles a dead bird. "Three Winterbournes attempted the transfer during the Godfall purges, when the god was loose and screaming through the streets. Three failures. The binding snapped back each time, wounded and furious, and finally your grandmother did the only thing left. She found a hollow man—a soldier, brainless since Marrow Field—and bound it into him instead, imperfectly, temporarily, and spent the rest of her life keeping the knot tight from outside. Then the witch-finders came for her, and the temporary became permanent, and the permanent became your king."

The words settled over Gideon like cold water. Somewhere in them was her whole life explained: her mother's flinching, the sentence-shaped inheritance, the design of her invisibility.

"Aldric," she said. "He was the hollow man."

"He was. And he has been paying for our family's arithmetic for twenty years." Maren's pale eyes fixed on her. "So now you know the price. Ask me your questions, girl. Ask them properly. Because if you do this, you don't get to be surprised by any of it."

* * *

They worked until well past midnight.

Maren taught the way rivers cut stone—slowly, relentlessly, without any apparent interest in whether you enjoyed it. The rite was a language before it was a ritual: nine phrases in Old Vaelic, each of which had to be shaped exactly, because the binding understood intent the way a dog understands tone, and a sloppy phrase was an invitation.

"No," Maren said, for perhaps the fortieth time. "Again. You're rounding the third syllable. The binding will hear *release* instead of *unhook*, and it will let go of everything, including the parts keeping the king alive, and you'll have a corpse and a free god and a very short remainder of a very bad evening."

"It's a hard word."

"It's a precise word. Precision is the entire trade. Beasts don't speak in sentences, girl—they speak in *meanings*, whole and exact, which is why your gift works and why the rite will work for you and no one else. You've spent your life saying things precisely to animals. Now say them precisely to a god."

From the rafters, Bramble said, "She's enjoying this."

"I am," Maren agreed. "Thirty years I've waited to boss a Winterbourne around. Again."

But between corrections, something else happened—something Gideon hadn't expected and didn't know how to name. Maren told stories. Small ones, tossed off like scraps, between drills: how Wren Winterbourne hummed while she worked and got the third syllable wrong exactly once; how Sable had argued with the god for six hours before the transfer took, buying the city time with sheer insolence; how Elowen, at the end, had written letters to a granddaughter she'd never meet and burned them one by one, because each one was evidence.

"She burned them?" Gideon asked.

"All but one. The last. Which said—" Maren closed her eyes and recited it with the weariness of long memorization, "*'If you're reading this, you're the one, and I'm sorry, and I'm proud, and do not carry it alone.'*"

Gideon's throat tightened. "Carry what alone? The rite needs one vessel. One person in the circle."

"So the circle says." Maren opened her eyes. "But Wren wrote something else, in a margin, in the last month of her life. We never tested it. There was never time, and then there was never anyone left brave enough or stupid enough." She slid a bark-page across the table. At its edge, in handwriting smaller and shakier than the rest, ran a single annotation:

*the knot is one weight but grief is many hands — try sharing it, E. says impossible, I say nothing about the gods has ever been impossible except being kind*

Gideon read it three times. The candle guttered. On the shelf, the untouched third cup had gone cold, though no one had touched it, and somehow that felt less like neglect than like company that had stepped out briefly and meant to return.

"It probably means nothing," Maren said gruffly, watching her face. "Dying women write poetry. It doesn't make it physics."

"No," Gideon said slowly. "It probably doesn't."

But she copied the margin note anyway, word for word, into the little waterproof journal she kept strapped under her coat, next to the Saltcellar tide-tables and Bramble's list of grievances against pigeons. And when Maren wasn't looking, she pressed her palm flat against the page for a moment, the way she did when greeting a nervous animal—as if the words might calm under her hand.

They didn't. Words like these never calmed. But they steadied, which was different, and possibly better.

* * *

She left the cottage in the grey hour before dawn, when the Underveil was at its most honest—lamplighters dousing their flames, barges groaning against their moorings, the canals breathing mist. Bramble rode her shoulder in silence for three streets, which for Bramble constituted a eulogy.

"You're going to do it," he said finally.

"Eleven moons down. Two left. Yes."

"And you're going to die."

"That's the standard outcome, yes."

"Hm." He preened once, hard, the way he did when arranging his feelings. "You know, when I met you, I sized you up as a coward with good posture. I'd like the record to show I've revised my assessment."

"Thank you, Bramble."

"Don't thank me yet. I haven't finished revising." He hopped to her other shoulder, nearer her ear. "The margin note. The sharing. You believe it."

"I believe Wren died believing it. That's not proof. But it's more than nothing."

"And if it's nothing? If the knot goes into you alone, and the pouring-out begins, and there's no one to reach for?" His voice, for once, had no mockery in it at all. "What's your plan then, Gideon Winterbourne?"

She walked a while before answering, boots quiet on wet cobbles, past shuttered stalls and sleeping cats who stirred at her passing and murmured half-dreams she chose not to listen to. *Don't carry it alone.* Her grandmother's letter. Wren's margin. Two dead women, agreeing across thirty years, and her the punchline of their joke.

"My plan," she said at last, "is that I tell no one."

Bramble's feathers went still. "That's not a plan. That's the opposite of several plans."

"It's the kindest version. If I tell Odessa, she'll forbid it, and then I'll have to lie to her anyway, and I'll do it badly, because I've watched you lie and apparently it's genetic. If I tell Tamsin, it'll break her—she's lost enough of herself to the finders without losing me too. If I tell the crew, they'll either stop me or follow me into something they can't survive." She shrugged, and felt the small weight of him shift with it. "So. No one. I learn the rite, I wait for the twelfth turn, and when the thirteenth moon rises, I walk into the palace alone and I pay our family's bill."

For a long moment the only sound was water lapping stone.

"You know," Bramble said at last, conversationally terrible, "your grandmother tried that too. The going-alone part. Want to guess how that worked out for her?"

Gideon stopped walking.

The mist drifted between them and the lamplighter's last lamp, and somewhere above the rooftops, far off toward the palace hill, a bell rang once—an hour bell, ordinary, meaningless—except that every rat along the canal wall lifted its head at the same instant, and every gull on the mooring posts went silent, and Gideon felt, faint and cold beneath her ribs, the second heartbeat stir in answer to something stirring on the hill.

Two turns left. And the thing in the crown was beginning, at last, to count down too.

"Bramble," she said quietly. "How much do you remember about the palace's beast-ways?"

He ruffled his scarred wings, and when he spoke, she could hear the grin in it, sharp as a talon. "Enough to get us killed. Why?"
