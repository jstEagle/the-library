## 13. Crown and Cage

They came for her at the fourth bell of the morning, which Serah Vane considered deeply rude. She had been awake since the second bell, packing.

She had known this day would come. You did not steal eleven years of royal medical records from the palace archive and walk out through the Weir Gate whistling. You did not spend three nights copying the binding ledgers into your own hand and then take a room at the Drowned Gull under the name *Mistress Puddle*, as though anonymity were a garment one could simply purchase at market. She had done all of those things anyway, because the alternative was to sit in her small cold house and know what she knew and do nothing, and Serah had discovered, somewhere between the king's empty eyes and the ledgers' patient arithmetic — *one renewal per moon; the thirteenth turn completes it* — that doing nothing had become physically impossible. It sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. It made her burp dread.

So when the knock came — three measured raps, the knock of men who expected doors to open themselves — she set down her bag, straightened her collar, and opened it before they could break it.

"Physician Vane," said the taller of the two witch-finders. He had the particular pallor of a man who worked indoors and enjoyed it. "Captain Merrow would like a word."

"At this hour?"

"The Captain keeps unusual hours."

"So I've heard. Tell him I keep ordinary ones." But she was already reaching for her coat, because there was no version of this conversation that ended with her going back to bed, and both of them knew it, and pretending otherwise was the last small dignity she owned. She intended to spend it lavishly.

The walk to the palace took forty minutes. Nobody spoke. The witch-finders flanked her the way bookends flank a book they intend to burn.

* * *

They did not take her to the witch-finder barracks, or to an interrogation room, or anywhere sensible. They took her up. Through the servants' corridors, past the kitchens where the bread smelled wrong — too sweet, she thought distantly, everything in this palace had begun to smell faintly too sweet, like fruit going over — up the east stair, through doors she remembered from her years as royal physician, until they stood in the antechamber of the king's private audience room.

Captain Merrow was waiting. He was not a large man, but he had the gift of seeming larger than whatever space contained him, like water poured into a jug. His witch-finder grey was immaculate. His smile was the smile of a man who has read your file and found it adequate.

"Serah Vane," he said warmly. "The prodigal physician."

"I prefer *the recently promoted*. I hear there's a vacancy at court."

Something flickered behind his eyes — appreciation, perhaps, or its taxidermied cousin. "You've been busy."

"I've been retired."

"You've been *thorough*. Eleven years of attendance records. Renewal schedules. Names of every physician who examined His Majesty and every one who stopped being scheduled afterward." He tilted his head. "You know what I admire about you, Vane? Other doctors looked at the king and saw a madman to be managed. You looked at him and saw a *question*."

"I saw a patient," Serah said. "That used to be the same thing as seeing a question."

"And what did your question tell you?"

"That I should have asked it sooner."

Merrow studied her for a long moment. Then he laughed — a real laugh, brief and genuine, which was somehow worse than anything else he might have done. "Oh, you're exactly what He hoped you'd be. Come in. He'll want to see you."

"He?"

Merrow opened the door.

* * *

The audience room had been the king's solar, once. Serah remembered it from Aldric's early reign: sun-warmed, cluttered, smelling of ink and the candied ginger he sucked when his stomach troubled him. There had been maps on the tables and a bad-tempered spaniel under them and a king who laughed too loudly at his own jokes because no one else dared to first.

The spaniel was long dead. So, in every way that mattered, was the king.

Aldric sat by the window with the light full on his face, and Serah's physician's heart did the thing it always did — catalogued, measured, grieved. He had lost more weight. His skin had the translucence of parchment held up to a flame. His hands lay in his lap with terrible stillness, fingers laced, the posture of a man posing for a portrait he could not leave.

But it was the eyes that stopped her, even now, even prepared. Not madness. Madness has weather in it — storms, fogs, sudden suns. These eyes had nothing. They were windows in a house where someone had turned off every lamp and was standing very quietly behind the glass, watching you watch the house.

"Doctor Vane," the king said.

His voice was Aldric's voice. That was the horror of it, she thought. It had kept the voice the way a stolen coat keeps its buttons. The cadence was almost right — warm, a little tired, faintly amused. Almost. Something underneath it ran a half-beat late, like music played by a musician listening to another musician through a wall.

"Your Majesty," she said, and bowed, because her knees were smarter than her pride.

"You left us." He said it gently. Reproachfully. A husband greeting a wife returned from a long errand. "Eleven years, and then you came back only to take things that weren't yours."

"The records were mine to keep. I wrote half of them."

"You wrote down what you saw." The king smiled, and the smile arrived a moment after the words, like thunder after lightning, and Serah felt her stomach drop through the floor and keep dropping. "You saw so little. That was never your fault. We were — careful. With you."

*We.* Her skin crawled. She thought of the ledgers, the neat columns, the renewals timed to the full moons, the physicians rotated out one by one whenever their notes grew too curious. *The court already knows,* she had written in her own journal, the night she decided to steal the records. *The court has always known. The court is feeding it.*

"We?" she said aloud.

The king's head tilted. For just a moment the warmth drained out of the face entirely, the way water drains from a basin, and what looked out through Aldric's eyes was old — old and vast and *patient*, a hunger that had learned to wear patience as a mask because masks got it fed faster. Then the warmth poured back in, and the king laughed softly, apologetic.

"Forgive me. Old habits. The court, the crown, the kingdom — we say *we* now. It simplifies things." He gestured to the chair across from him. "Sit, Doctor. Please. I have an offer, and I would rather you heard it sitting down. You look tired."

*I look tired because I have spent three weeks discovering that my king is a glove and something wears him,* she did not say. She sat.

* * *

"You are afraid," the king said. "Good. Fear is honest. I get so little honesty these days — everyone at court loves me, and love, Doctor, is the least honest substance in the world. It flows around a thing instead of through it." He leaned forward slightly. "You do not love me. You never did. You *examined* me. Do you know how rare that is? How precious?"

"I examined a man," Serah said carefully. "I'm not sure what I'm examining now."

"A fair question. Deserves a fair answer." And then — she would remember this all her life, the way one remembers the exact shape of a scar — the king closed his eyes, and his shoulders dropped, and for three seconds the whole architecture of the possession seemed to sag, like a puppet whose handler had stepped away to sneeze.

When the eyes opened, they were wet.

"Serah," Aldric whispered. His own voice, frayed and small. "It's still — I can still —"

The warmth snapped back like a door slamming. The king straightened, smoothed his sleeves, and continued in the pleasant borrowed tone as if nothing had happened at all.

"As I was saying," he said. "An offer."

Serah's hands were shaking in her lap. She pressed them flat against her knees and made herself breathe the way she taught apprentices to breathe before setting a bone: in for four, hold, out for four. *He's in there.* The thought arrived white-hot and refused to leave. *He is in there and he reached for me and it put him away like a toy on a shelf.*

"The beast-speaker," the king said. "You've been asking about her. In the archives, in the Underveil, in the taverns where respectable women drink watered wine and pretend not to listen. The Winterbourne girl."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Serah." The king chided her gently, shaking his head, and for a moment he sounded so much like a disappointed uncle that she nearly apologized. Nearly. "You stole my records. You found the thirteenth turn. You are many things, but stupid is not among them, and neither is forgetful. Let us not insult each other. It wastes the morning, and I have a kingdom to pray at."

*Pray at.* Not rule. Not serve. Pray at. As though the kingdom were a shrine and he the god deciding whether to answer.

"What's the offer?" she said.

"Restoration." The king spread his hands, magnanimous, the portrait of a reasonable monarch. "Your name cleared. Your position returned — chief physician of the realm, with a seat at council. Your research funded. Your sister's debts—" he paused delicately, watching her, and Serah went very still, "—quietly resolved. All of it. In exchange for one small service."

"Which is?"

"Find her. The Winterbourne girl. You have already begun; finish. Learn where she sleeps, who shelters her, what she intends. Bring me that knowledge, and let my witch-finders do the rest." He smiled again — that delayed smile, lightning and thunder in the wrong order. "You would not even need to touch her. You are a healer, Serah. I would not ask a healer to harm. Only to *observe*. To write things down." A pause, soft as snowfall. "You're so very good at writing things down."

And there it was. The trap dressed as kindness, the knife offered handle-first because the blade was understood. She thought of Tamsin the charm-seller, taken in a raid; she thought of the burned-out speakers the smugglers spoke of in low voices; she thought of the ledger column marked *renewals* and what happened to kings who missed one.

She thought, most of all, of those three seconds. *I can still—*

Revulsion rose in her like floodwater, hot and total, filling her throat, her ears, the spaces behind her eyes. She wanted to stand up and call the thing by its true name in front of all its grey-clad priests. She wanted to spit on the offer and the hand that offered it and the stolen mouth that shaped it.

Instead, Serah Vane — disgraced, surveilled, fifty-one years old, and tired down to the marrow — lowered her gaze the way a beaten woman does, and let the floodwater freeze into ice, and said:

"If I refuse, you'll take me apart to find out what I know anyway."

The king sighed, sorrowful. "I would hate that. Truly. You are the only person in this palace who ever touched me without flinching." Which was, she realized, the most honest sentence it had spoken yet, and possibly the most obscene.

"Then I'll do it," she said.

The warmth brightened. The room seemed, absurdly, to grow warmer with it, like a hearth newly fed. "I hoped you would."

"But I work alone. No handlers, no minders, no grey coats following me through the fish market. If your finders frighten her before I've earned her trust, you lose your prize and I lose my usefulness. Agreed?"

A pause. The smallest pause. Somewhere behind the borrowed face, she imagined, something vast turned something vaster over, considering.

"Agreed," the king said. "Observe. Report. Nothing more." He rose, and Merrow opened the door, and the interview was clearly over. But at the threshold Serah paused, because some stubborn physician's reflex in her demanded one last examination, and she turned back.

"Your Majesty," she said. "Your color is poor. You should eat more."

The king looked at her. And for a heartbeat — less than a heartbeat, a sliver of a heartbeat — something moved behind the eyes that was neither the hunger nor the warmth nor poor hollow Aldric, but all three at once, crowded together like passengers at a window, and the mouth said, in a voice with no delay in it at all, perfectly synchronized, perfectly smooth:

"*Eat more.* Yes. We intend to."

* * *

They walked her out through the east stair, alone, unhandled, exactly as agreed. Merrow peeled off at the gate with a courteous nod, and Serah walked the forty minutes home through streets loud with morning, past shrines hung with new ribbons, past beggars praying with their faces turned toward the palace like flowers toward a poisoned sun.

At her door she stopped. Checked the street. Went inside. Bolted it.

Then she took out the copy of the records she had sewn into her coat lining months ago — the copy they had never found, because they had searched her house twice and found only the decoy pages she'd left in the flour bin, because even a cornered woman plans ahead — and she sat down at her table with fresh paper and began to write.

*To the crew of the Saltcellar, or whoever in the Underveil carries word to the Winterbourne girl:*

Her pen hovered. How to warn them without the warning falling into grey hands? How to say *I met the thing wearing our king and it offered me thirty pieces of silver and I took them, and every word I speak to you now may be a coin it is counting*?

She wrote: *It speaks well but half a beat late. It kept his voice. It cannot keep his timing. Remember that — when someone offers you exactly what you most want, count the beat before you believe them.*

And then, because her hand was steady enough now and her disgust had cooled into something harder and more useful, she wrote the rest: the audience, the offer, the terms she had bargained — *no handlers, they agreed, use that window, it will not stay open long* — and the three seconds.

*He is still in there. He reached for me. Write back quickly. The twelfth moon is nearly full, and I do not think the thing suspects me yet, but I am a poor liar and getting worse, and if I am taken, tell the girl the ledgers are in the flour bin and the truth is in my coat.*

She sealed it, melted wax, pressed her thumbprint deep. Then she opened the shutter and whistled — badly; she had never learned properly — at the gull that had been loitering on her roofline for two days, the one with the notched wing that Odessa Finch swore was reliable.

The gull cocked its head at her with an expression of profound professional skepticism.

"Yes, yes," Serah muttered, tying the packet to its leg. "Tell your mistress the disgraced doctor says: the crown made me an offer." She lifted the bird to the window, out into the grey-gold morning air, toward the canals, toward the Underveil, toward the girl everyone was hunting.

"Tell her the cage has a key," she said, "and I know where it's hidden — and so does the thing that wants us all on our knees."

The gull wheeled once over the rooftops and was gone.
