## 14. Bramble's Secret

The Saltcellar safehouse smelled of tallow smoke, wet rope, and the particular sourness of men who had been drinking since midday and intended to see it through to dark. Gideon sat in the corner with a bowl of barley soup she was mostly using as a hand-warmer, and watched the crow watch her back.

Bramble had claimed the beam above the hearth as his own three nights ago, displacing two gulls and one extremely offended cat who now lived outside out of spite. He sat there now, feathers ruffled against the draft, head cocked at an angle that suggested he was either thinking deep thoughts or judging her posture. With Bramble it was usually both.

"You're staring," Gideon said.

"I am *considering*," Bramble said. His voice came out like gravel poured through a keyhole—rough, unhurried, faintly amused. "There is a difference. Staring is for pigeons."

"Then consider somewhere else."

"No." He hopped down the beam, closer. The firelight caught the old scar across his breast, the pale seam where feathers refused to grow. "Consideration works better close. Also your soup smells terrible and I want you to feel guilty about it."

"It's barley."

"It's *punishment*."

She smiled despite herself. That was the thing about Bramble she hadn't yet gotten used to—the way he made her smile without permission. Three years of keeping her face smooth as pondwater, and this bird had cracked it open in a week.

Around them the safehouse went about its evening. Odessa argued freight weights with a smuggler named Pike; Serah sat apart, reading stolen palace records by candle stub with the focused hunger of a woman trying to outrun her own guilt. Tamsin slept in the loft above, exhausted by nothing, because there was nothing left in her to exhaust. Gideon still couldn't look up at that loft without something tightening behind her ribs.

Bramble dropped from the beam onto the table beside her bowl. Up close, in good light, his eyes were not quite crow-black. There was a brown depth to them, like river water over stones.

"Gideon Winterbourne," he said.

Her spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. In all their weeks together he'd called her *girl*, *charmseller*, *you*, and once, memorably, *walking disaster*. Never her name. Never her whole name.

"How do you know that name?"

"Because I have been waiting thirty years to say it," Bramble said, "and I'll be damned if I rush it now that I finally can."

* * *

He made her follow him.

Out the back door, past the coal store, down the crooked stair to the canal wall where the tide muttered to itself and nobody could stand close enough to listen without getting their boots wet. The moon was a fingernail paring over Vaelmoor's rooftops. Somewhere across the water, the palace sat black and patient on its hill, and Gideon did not look at it. She was getting better at not looking at it. She was not sure that was a victory.

"Talk," she said. "And if you say 'the crown is hungry' again, I'm going to drop you in the canal."

"The crown is always hungry. That hasn't changed." Bramble hopped along the wall's coping, wings half-spread for balance. "What has changed is that you asked me last week why I stayed. When every crow in this city flies south in winter, why does one ragged scarred fool stay in the coldest city on the coast, following a charm seller who feeds him scraps and insults?"

"Because you're fond of me."

"I am *tolerant* of you. It's different." He stopped. Faced her. And when he spoke again, the gravel had gone out of his voice, and what remained underneath was older and quieter and very tired. "I stayed because I promised."

Gideon felt the cold come off the canal and into her chest. "Promised whom?"

"A witch named Maren Winterbourne."

The world went very still. The tide kept moving, the wind kept working, but Gideon stood inside a silence of her own making, hearing a name she had not spoken aloud since she was nine years old.

"My grandmother," she said. Her voice sounded far away even to herself.

"Your grandmother," Bramble agreed. "Speaker of beasts. Binder of the Hollow God. The finest mind and the worst singing voice I have known in forty years of knowing minds." He tilted his head. "I was hers. Familiar, they called it. Bonded. Not pet, not servant—more like... the other half of a conversation that never ended. Thirty years ago, when the purges came, she hid me in her coat and told me to be quiet and small. I have never been quiet or small in my life, but for her I managed it. We ran north along the coast. We almost made the marshes."

"What happened?"

"Fire arrows," Bramble said simply. "Witch-finders were thorough even then. She threw me into the reeds and stood up so they would chase her and not search for a bird." A pause. The tide filled it. "They chased her. I waited in the reeds for three days. Then I flew back toward Vaelmoor, because that was where she had been going, and I found the city, and I found no Maren. So I did what she told me to do instead of what I wanted. I waited. I watched the Winterbournes dwindle—one daughter gone west, one son taken in the second purge, one granddaughter born who smelled of the gift like woodsmoke—and I waited for the granddaughter to grow."

"Thirty years," Gideon whispered. "You've been waiting thirty years for—"

"For someone too stubborn to leave Vaelmoor, yes. For someone who talks to cats in alleyways and pretends she doesn't. For someone whose grandmother taught her mother a lullaby that was actually a binding-verse, hoping some scrap of it would pass down the blood like eye color." Bramble's eyes caught the moonlight. "It passed down, girl. It reached you. I've heard you humming it when you think no one is listening. You hum badly too. It's in the blood."

A laugh broke out of her, wet and sudden, and she pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. It wasn't funny. None of it was funny. But grief and laughter live in neighboring rooms and share a wall, and sometimes you can't knock on one door without the other opening too.

"She's dead," Gideon said. "You're telling me she's dead."

"I'm telling you she died so that you could live long enough to be standing here asking stupid questions." But Bramble hopped closer, and his voice gentled into something she had never heard from him—something almost tender. "I'm also telling you she left you more than a lullaby. Maren planned the way she planned everything. Thoroughly. Annoyingly. Before the end, she cached everything that mattered. Notes. Diagrams. Thirty years of watching the binding fray, written in her own hand, hidden where only a beast-speaker could ever find it. Because only a beast-speaker could ever reach it."

"Where?"

"Where do you think?" Bramble spread one wing toward the city, toward the black hill above it. "She hid it under the nose of the thing that killed her. Under the shrine district. Under the Hollow Shrine itself, girl—in the crypts beneath it, in the old god-stone that everyone thinks is just a foundation rock. The witch-finders walk over her work every day. They've walked over it for thirty years."

* * *

They went at dawn, because Bramble insisted that crows understood dawn the way fish understood water, and because Gideon had learned that arguing with Bramble was like arguing with weather—you got wet and the weather didn't care.

The Hollow Shrine at first light was empty in the way only holy places can be empty: swept, silent, full of the absence of everyone who used to come. Since the beggar woman's healing, crowds had come and gone in waves, praying loudly, lighting candles, terrified and hopeful in equal measure. At dawn the waves receded, and what remained was old stone worn smooth by knees, and the smell of cold ash.

Gideon walked the aisles with her hood up, feeling like a burglar in her own inheritance. Bramble rode her shoulder, tucked under the hood, muttering directions in a voice pitched below human hearing—which was fine for him, and meant Gideon spent the whole walk nodding at nothing like a madwoman.

"Left at the third pillar. No, your other left. Honestly, how did your line survive as long as it did."

"We were very good at running."

"Evidently not that good," Bramble said, and then, immediately, quieter: "That was unkind. Strike it. Grief makes my beak sharp."

Behind the shrine's apse, where the oldest stonework sank into the hillside, stood the god-stone—a squat grey block older than the Edict, older than the Godfall, that the faithful touched for luck and scholars ignored entirely. Bramble directed her to kneel and press her palm flat against its surface, and speak.

"Speak what?"

"The lullaby. All of it. The parts your mother never finished teaching you, because she didn't know them—but your blood knows them. Blood remembers verses the mind forgets. Just start, and let the rest rise."

So Gideon knelt on cold stone in an empty shrine, feeling like the greatest fool in Vaelmoor, and began to sing.

The first lines were easy—the ones her mother had sung until the words wore smooth as sea glass. Then she reached the edge of what she knew, and her voice faltered—

And rose again without her.

It was the strangest sensation of her life, stranger than speaking through a rat, stranger than touching the god's attention on the turning moon. Her mouth kept moving, but the words coming out were not words she knew; they were older, heavier, shaped for a tongue her throat barely remembered how to make. The melody descended beneath her voice like roots going down. The stone under her palm grew warm.

The god-stone opened.

Not with grinding or thunder—with a soft sigh, like a door in a dream, a section of the block swinging inward on hinges that had waited thirty years for oiling. Inside: a shallow cavity lined in oilcloth, and within that, a bundle wrapped in waxed linen tied with cord, and atop the bundle, a single crow feather gone silver-grey with age.

"That's mine," Bramble said softly. "She took it the day we bonded. Sentimental old woman. Don't tell her I said that. Don't tell myself either."

Gideon lifted the bundle with both hands, the way you lift something sleeping. Inside the linen: a leather journal, swollen with damp and years; loose pages covered in diagrams of circles and interlocking rings; a strip of birch bark with a verse burned into it; and a small tin box that rattled.

"Take it home," Bramble said. "All of it. And Gideon—" He said her name plainly this time, no ceremony, and it landed harder than the formal version had. "Whatever you find in those pages, remember that Maren Winterbourne wrote them for the granddaughter she hoped would exist, in the years she feared wouldn't. Read them the way she wrote them. Slowly. With breakfast. She believed everything went better with breakfast."

* * *

Back at the safehouse, while the crew slept off the night's work, Gideon sat by the banked fire with the journal open on her knees and Bramble perched on the chair-back beside her ear like a small judgemental conscience.

Maren's handwriting marched across the pages, cramped and certain, correcting itself in margins, occasionally insulting its own author ("*foolish, Maren, foolish, you nearly frayed the third ring doing it that way*"). Gideon read about the binding as she had never understood it—not a chain but a knot, thirteen interlocking turns, each turn a moon, each moon a renewal. Not cast once but maintained forever. A cage that had to be re-woven by hand, generation after generation, until the Winterbournes were hunted and the weaving stopped and the knot began, slowly, patiently, over thirty years, to loosen.

The god wasn't escaping the binding, she realized, heart slowing with dread. It was *outlasting* it.

Page after page. Diagrams of the rite of renewal. Diagrams of the rite of *unbinding*—and here Maren's handwriting changed, grew larger, pressed harder into the page:

*"The unbinding cannot be done from outside. The binding must be carried to be loosed. One speaker must take the whole of it into themselves—the weight of thirteen turns, the hunger of thirteen moons—or the god slips free into whatever is nearest. There is no way around this. I looked for twenty years. There is no way around.*

*But hear me, whoever you are, whoever of my blood is reading this: the carrying need not be solitary. The knot holds because one soul bears it alone. It can be loosened the same way it was tied—together. Do not carry it alone. Whatever you are, whoever loves you—do not carry it alone. Alone it kills. Shared, it only changes.*

*I did not understand that in time. Perhaps you will."*

Gideon sat for a long time after she finished, the journal closed on her lap, looking into the embers.

"Do not carry it alone," she repeated.

"Maren never could write a short sentence," Bramble said, but his voice had gone rough, and he preened his scarred breast twice before he could continue. "She wrote that page last. I watched her write it. She knew what the rite would cost her if she tried it alone, and she tried to solve it, and she ran out of time. That is the shape of our family, girl: brilliant, out of time, and alone at the end." He hopped down onto her knee, talons light as thought. "So don't be. Alone. It's the one instruction I have left of hers, and I intend to enforce it."

Gideon looked at him—at the scarred breast, the river-water eyes, the thirty years of patient watching folded into one ragged bird—and felt something shift in her chest that had been locked since Tamsin was taken. Not the grief. The grief could stay; grief was honest. But under the grief, something warm put down roots.

"Inheritance," she said quietly. "That's what this is. Everyone keeps telling me about bloodlines and destiny and the Winterbourne burden. But this—" she touched the journal, the birch bark, the tin box that rattled with who-knew-what, "—this is just my grandmother leaving notes on the table. Like any mother does. 'Eat breakfast. Don't go alone.' Thirty years late, but she left them."

"She'd be insufferable about being right," Bramble agreed. "She once predicted a storm three days out and mentioned it every day for a year."

The tin box rattled when Gideon shook it. She worked the lid free—and stopped breathing.

Inside, nested in wool gone grey with age, lay three things: a ring of iron keys stamped with the palace sigil, old enough that the sigil was the *old* sigil, pre-Godfall; a folded map drawn on silk, marked in faded ink with tunnels and passages beneath the Hollow Court labeled in Maren's hand—*the beasts' forgotten ways*; and a slip of paper, newer than the rest, ink barely faded at all, written in a different hand entirely. Small, careful letters.

*"If the thirteenth moon comes before you do, use the east gate. Trust the physician. —S.V."*

Gideon stared at it. Beside her ear, Bramble went very still.

"Bramble," she said slowly, "who is S.V.?"

And from the doorway behind them, pale as candlewax, still holding the stolen palace records she'd been reading all week, Serah Vane said: "That would be me."
