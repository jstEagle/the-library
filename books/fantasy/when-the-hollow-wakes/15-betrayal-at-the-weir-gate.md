## 15. Betrayal at the Weir Gate

Serah Vane had learned, in her years at court, that a lie told well begins as a truth you refuse to finish.

So she let them believe it. She sat in the witch-finders' barge as it slid down the black water of the Underveil canal, lanterns hooded, thirty armed men and women around her in silence, and she let Captain Hesse believe that Serah Vane—disgraced physician, traitor, thief of palace records—had been broken in her cell and now led them willingly to the beast-speaker's den.

It was almost true. That was the horror of it.

They had not tortured her. That was the first thing she'd noticed, back in the holding cell beneath the Weir Gate barracks, when they'd brought her bread and watered wine and asked, gently, if she was comfortable. Torture was for people whose cooperation might fail. Serah had understood immediately what she was instead: an investment.

The thing wearing the king's face had been very courteous at their second meeting. It had poured her tea itself, with Aldric's long physician's hands, hands she had once taken a pulse with, and it had said, *You treated this body well for eleven years, Doctor Vane. You kept it alive through fevers that should have ended it. I remember loyalty.*

*He remembers nothing,* she had wanted to say. *I checked. There is nothing left in there to remember.* But she had drunk the tea, because refusing would have told them something too.

And then it had made its offer—not threats, never threats, only arithmetic. The beast-speaker would be found. The question was only whether Serah stood beside the finders or in front of them. And if she helped—if she gave them the Underveil, the Saltcellars, the girl who spoke with rats—then perhaps, when the world settled into its new shape, there would be a place in it for a woman of her talents. A title, even. Restoration.

*I could give you back your name,* it had said, smiling with Aldric's mouth, and the smile had been wrong in a way she still couldn't describe, like watching a portrait try to blink.

She had said yes. She had watched herself say yes from somewhere slightly above her own shoulder, the way she used to watch her own hands during surgery, detached and precise, and she had thought: *good. Now you're inside. Now you can see the gears.*

That was the truth she refused to finish. She had said yes because yes was the only door out of the cell, and a door was all she needed. What came after the door—that part she had not finished thinking. She had let them believe she would.

Now the barge slipped past the drowned chapel of Saint Ossian, past rope bridges sagging with wet laundry no one dared hang by daylight, and Serah counted the turns of the canal against the map she had drawn for them. Two more bends, and the Weir Gate—the rusted sluice-gate where the Saltcellars moved their cargo, where Odessa Finch ran her people like a tide chart come to life.

Hesse crouched beside her, a lean woman with a burn-scarred jaw and eyes like a fisherman reading weather. "The gatehouse," she said. "You're certain."

"Certain enough." Serah kept her voice flat, clinical. "Finch trusts routine. She'll have four on watch, no more. The speaker will be inland, at the salt-house."

"And you want us to know you're telling the truth," Hesse said. Not a question. An observation with teeth.

"I want this over," Serah said. Which was also true. Let them weigh which truths were load-bearing.

Two more bends. The canal narrowed. Somewhere ahead, water dripped from the vaulted ceiling in a rhythm Serah had already memorized without meaning to—eleven years of taking pulses had given her a clockwork heart, always counting.

She had spent three nights in that cell doing arithmetic of a different kind. If she simply fled at the first opportunity, they would catch her; she was fifty-three and had the knees to prove it, and witch-finders hunted people for a living. If she led them true, everyone in the Underveil died—the Finch woman, the smugglers, the burned-out girl Tamsin they'd already pulled from a cell once, the beast-speaker most of all, and with her whatever thin hope the city had of seeing spring again.

There was a third sum. She had turned it over all night, the way you turn a stone to see what lives underneath.

A warning, delivered late enough to be believed but early enough to matter. A betrayal that looked like betrayal until the last possible moment. It required her to walk them nearly to the gate. It required her to stand among them while they loaded crossbows. It required—

"There." Hesse pointed.

The Weir Gate rose out of the dark ahead, a wall of green-slick iron across the canal, and beside it, built into the stone abutment, the squat gatehouse with its single lit window. Serah's stomach did something cold and surgical.

On the window ledge sat a crow.

Her heart stopped counting.

She knew the bird by reputation—Bramble, the beast-speaker's shadow, the scarred crow that half the Underveil swore talked back. He was looking directly at the barge. At her. And his head tilted, one bright eye catching the hooded lantern light, and he made a sound no crow makes—a low, questioning crook, almost human in its grammar.

*Well, Doctor?* it seemed to say. *Which way does this go?*

"Signal positions," Hesse murmured behind her. "Doctor Vane. Walk to the gatehouse door and knock. You know the password."

Serah stood. Her legs obeyed, which felt like treachery of their own. She stepped onto the abutment's narrow ledge, boots finding the grooves worn by decades of smuggler traffic, and walked toward the lit window and the crow upon it, thirty crossbows trained on her back, and inside her chest something finished the sentence she had left unfinished in her cell three nights ago.

She knocked. Twice, then once—the pattern Odessa Finch had drilled into her own people, the pattern Serah had pried out of a captured Saltcellar boy with kindness and soup rather than irons, back when she still believed the Crown deserved her cleverness used gently.

The door cracked. A young man's face, wary, flour on his jaw.

"Bread for the ferryman," Serah said, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her, "and tell Odessa Finch that the tide is wrong tonight. Tell her—" and here she raised her voice, deliberately, carelessly, loudly enough to carry over the drip of the vaulted ceiling and into the listening dark of the canal—"tell her the whole tide is coming, every wave at once, and the doctor says run."

Behind her, Hesse shouted an order. Crossbows snapped up. The crow burst off the windowsill in a clatter of black wings, shrieking now, pure alarm, and the young man vanished, and Serah Vane stood very still in the doorway of the enemy with her hands empty and her heart, at last, beating honestly.

"Well," she said, to no one, to the crow vanishing eastward, to the girl she'd never met who spoke with rats. "Better late."

They took her arms roughly, but gently enough. Investments, after all.

* * *

Gideon heard it before Bramble reached her—a sound like the whole night tearing along one seam.

She was on the salt-house roof keeping the third watch, wrapped in Odessa's spare coat, when the crow came out of the dark overhead screaming words instead of cries: *"Finders! Weir Gate! All of them, all of them, DOCTOR SAYS RUN!"*

She was moving before he landed, sliding down the drainpipe, hitting the courtyard, shouting the alarm. The Saltcellars woke the way smugglers wake—fast, silent, furious. Within a hundred heartbeats the yard was organized chaos: crates dragged over trapdoors, children handed down into the cellar ways, Odessa Finch standing in the middle of it with her one arm directing traffic like the harbor master she'd been born to be before the world got ideas.

"How many?" Odessa demanded of Bramble, who landed on her shoulder—he alone of all living things was permitted—and said, "Thirty. Boats. They had the doctor."

Gideon went cold. "Serah's with them?"

"Serah *led* them," Bramble said. Then, quickly, feathers flattening: "But she warned. Loud. On purpose. Gideon, she *warned*, she used the knock, she gave the signal, she made sure I saw her face while she did it—she wanted me to see."

"That doesn't mean anything," Gideon said. "It could be a trick to make us trust her—"

"It could," Odessa agreed grimly. "But tricks cost the same either way, and we haven't time to audit 'em. Move!"

They moved. The plan was old and simple and hated by everyone: scatter along the escape channels, break into small boats, meet at the second junction if any of them lived to make it. Gideon was shoved toward a skiff with Tamsin—the poor hollowed-out girl, who flinched at shouts but knew these waters better than anyone now that her gift was ash—and they pushed off into the current just as the first lanterns bloomed around the Weir Gate like a wound opening.

What happened next, Gideon watched from the water, and she would watch it again behind her eyelids for a long time after.

Odessa Finch did not get in a boat.

"Someone has to close the weir," she called out, wading to the great sluice-wheel set into the channel, calm as Sunday. "Gate comes down, their barges ground out, we slip past in the shallows. Standard tide-craft. Go on, now."

"You're mad," someone shouted.

"Been saying so for years. Keeps the price of my opinions low." And she put her one good hand on the wheel and began to turn it, the great iron mechanism groaning awake, and Gideon understood with sickening clarity exactly what closing the weir meant: whoever worked the wheel stood in full lantern light, alone, on the one piece of ground in the entire Underveil the witch-finders could not ignore.

"ODESSA!" Gideon screamed.

"Go on, love." Odessa glanced back at her, and even at this distance, even in that terrible light, she grinned. "Told you once I don't lose people twice. Get my crew home. That's an order from a captain with one arm and all the sense."

The wheel turned. The gate began to descend. The first barge rounded the bend at speed and grounded on the shallowing channel with a crunch that Gideon felt in her teeth, and then the night filled with shouting, and bolts, and fire.

She saw Odessa take three bolts before the wheel locked. She saw her sit down against it, deliberate, unhurried, like a woman resting after honest work. She saw her raise her remaining hand—not in surrender, Gideon realized later, but in the Saltcellar farewell, the two-fingered salute they gave departing ships: *fair tides, see you at the next port.*

Then the lanterns closed over her like water, and Gideon was dragging the skiff into the side-channel with Tamsin pale and shaking beside her, and the Underveil swallowed them, and the last thing she heard above the waterline was Odessa Finch laughing.

* * *

Odessa Finch had a theory about pain, developed over forty-one years and one arm's worth of experience: pain was just information arriving rudely. So she catalogued it. Bolt in the shoulder, rude. Bolt in the thigh, ruder. Bolt through the meat of her forearm—the one that remained—downright discourteous, and she filed all three under *later* and concentrated on the present, which was Captain Hesse crouching in front of her with a lantern held high, theatrical as a bad play.

"The beast-speaker," Hesse said. "Where."

"Gone," Odessa said. "Like my patience. Like my brother, actually—you wouldn't remember him, small fellow, big laugh, hanged in the Ashfield purge for teaching his dog to count. Funny thing, grief. Makes a person particular about who they hand over to the likes of you."

"We can do this kindly or otherwise."

"Oh, I hope otherwise, genuinely." Odessa leaned her head back against the weir wheel and smiled through the blood on her teeth. "Kindly means quick, and quick means I miss the show."

"What show?"

"The one coming, love." And here Odessa Finch, beaten, bleeding, bound at the wrists with her crew scattered and her life's work burning somewhere behind her, laughed—really laughed, deep and unbothered, because it was true, all of it was true, she'd bet her brother's memory on it. "The thirteenth moon's near. Your master's getting hungry. And there's a girl out there in the dark right now with rat-eyes and gull-wings and the blood of the people who caged your god the first time—and every hour you spend fishing old women out of canals is an hour she spends learning how to end it. So." She tipped her head toward the burning skyline. "Tick-tock."

They took her to the Weir Gate barracks. They were rougher after that, once the laughter started getting under their skin. She didn't mind. Pain was information, and the information was: *they're afraid of her, and they should be.*

In her cell, in the dark, she did what Finch captains did when the tide turned against them. She waited for slack water, and she trusted her crew to read the chart.

Somewhere out there, Gideon Winterbourne was learning to be more than bait.

Somewhere out there, Bramble was carrying word east, wingbeat by wingbeat.

And somewhere out there—Odessa smiled at the ceiling, and it hurt, and she catalogued it—a disgraced doctor sat in the cell next door, and through the wall, very softly, Serah Vane was whispering the layout of the barracks to anyone who might be listening. Rats, probably. Rats were everywhere, if you knew how to ask.

Odessa closed her eyes.

"Hold fast, Doctor," she murmured. "Fair tides. See you at the next port."

Above the city, unseen behind cloud, the moon swelled toward its thirteenth turning, and in the palace on the hill, something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, hungrily, to dream of dawn.
