## 16. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The eleventh moon since the First Turning rose over Vaelmoor the color of an old bruise, and Gideon watched it from the Saltcellars' kitchen roof with a bowl of barley soup going cold in her hands.

Below her, through the smoke-hole, she could hear the household arguing about turnips. It was, she reflected, a very particular kind of argument—the kind that only happened when a family had been hungry long enough to develop strong opinions about root vegetables.

"They're *soft*," Odessa was saying. "Soft means rotting. Soft means we eat them tonight."

"Soft means *soaking*," Tamsin countered, her voice flat and careful the way it always was now, every word placed like a stone across a stream. "You soak them and they firm right up. My gran did it through the Famine Winter."

"Your gran also thought eels were government spies."

"She wasn't wrong about everything."

Gideon smiled despite herself, despite the bruise-colored sky, despite the rat sitting on the chimney beside her who had come all the way up from the grain wharves to tell her something she didn't want to hear.

"Say it again," she said quietly. "Slowly."

The rat's whiskers twitched. Its voice arrived in her head the way rats' voices always did—quick, sly, threaded with the smell-sense of things rather than words proper, so that what she received was less a sentence than a picture of dark water moving wrong.

*The river. Last night. The river ran uphill past the tanneries for the space of eleven heartbeats. The fish came with it, gasping, looking at the palace. All of them looking at the palace.*

"Eleven," Gideon repeated. "Eleven heartbeats. Eleven moons."

The rat shrugged, which is a thing rats can do with their whole spines. *Numbers are your business,* it seemed to say. *Fish is mine.* Then it flowed back down the chimney into the dark, and Gideon sat alone with her cold soup and the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for two months.

Two more turns. Two full moons until the thirteenth, and the binding would complete itself like a knot pulled home.

She ate the soup. It was soft. Odessa was right about the turnips after all.

* * *

Vaelmoor had split down its seams, and everyone pretended not to see the seams because pretending was cheaper than looking.

In the mornings the Edict loyalists still swept their doorsteps and pinned their little leaden charms above their lintels—harmless things now, dead things, metal that no longer meant anything—and glared across the lanes at anyone who lingered too long near a shrine. In the evenings the cults came out. They didn't call themselves cults; they called themselves congregations, hearth-circles, the Waking Kind. They gathered at the Hollow Shrine where the beggar woman's hand had healed three years ago, and they knelt on the frost-bitten cobbles, and they prayed with their faces turned toward the palace the way flowers turn toward light.

And the prayers were being answered. That was the horror of it. Not always, not grandly—but often enough. A fever broke. A lame man walked to the shrine gates on his own feet. A child lost in the Underveil tunnels wandered out unharmed after two days, telling anyone who'd listen that a warm voice had sung to her in the dark.

Gideon had gone once, early on, wrapped in a hood, telling herself she was gathering intelligence. She had stood among the kneeling faithful and felt the prayer rise off them like heat off bread—not words, exactly, but a great low hum of wanting, thousands of hearts all leaning the same direction at once. And beneath it she had felt something lean *back*. Patient. Pleased. Hungry the way a well is hungry: endlessly, and without malice, and without ever being full.

She hadn't gone again. She didn't trust herself to stand in that crowd anymore. Some nights she woke with the hum still in her teeth, her own heart trying to fall into its rhythm, and she lay in the dark listening to Bramble breathing on the rafter above her bed just to have one heartbeat in the room that was only hers.

"The city's going to tear itself apart before your god even has to lift a finger," Serah said. She'd taken to visiting the Underveil safehouse twice a week, coming down through the weirs with medical supplies disguised as pickling brine, her court robes traded for a physician's plain gray. She sat at the kitchen table now, cataloguing wounds the way other people catalogued groceries. "Three riots this month. The witch-finders burned the Lantern District congregation out on Thirdday, and by Sateday the same people were back kneeling on the same cobbles with half of the district watching. You can't arrest faith. Believe me, the Crown tried arresting mine."

"You had faith?" Tamsin asked, surprised. Tamsin asked few questions these days, so when she did, the whole table went quiet to let it breathe.

"I believed in the king," Serah said simply. "For nine years I bled him, dosed him, charted his sleep, told myself the hollow look was grief. Faith doesn't need altars. It needs only somewhere to put itself down." She looked up at Gideon. "That's what frightens me. Not the cultists. The ordinary ones. The baker who leaves a loaf on his windowsill 'for whoever wakes.' The guardsman who salutes the throne a beat too long. Everyone is praying a little, now, just in case. And every prayer is a thread, and every thread runs to the same spool."

Gideon turned her spoon in the soup. "I know."

"You know." Serah's eyes were sharp as scalpels. "Then say it aloud. Say it where the others can hear. Because you've been carrying it alone since the rat came up the chimney, and I've watched you carry things alone before, girl, and it nearly got us all killed at the Weir Gate."

Odessa snorted from the stove. "Tell her, physician."

Gideon set down the spoon. Around the table sat her family: Odessa with her sleeve pinned and rolled, salt-crusted hands folded; Tamsin with her burned-out gift and her careful voice; Serah with her guilt worn smooth as sea glass; and overhead on the rafter, preening one scarred wing, a crow who had been waiting thirty years for this family and would never admit how much he liked it.

"All right," Gideon said. "Here's what the beasts keep telling me, and here's what I've been too much of a coward to say. The fields are emptying. Every farm within a day's walk—cows, geese, dogs, rats, everything with legs—they gather at night on the hills facing the palace and they *watch*, and in the morning they've drifted another mile closer, and by spring there won't be a beast left working a plow or guarding a flock in Vaelmoor. The fish run backward. The birds circle the palace towers instead of migrating. Food's short because the beasts have stopped being ours."

"We know that part," Odessa said. "Half the crew's gone vegetarian by necessity and the other half's gone vegetarian by despair."

"And the city prays harder because of it," Gideon went on. "Hungry people pray for bread. Frightened people pray for safety. Every empty field, every backward river, every riot—it all makes them *need* it more. Don't you see? The god doesn't need the rite finished. The rite was just the key turning in the lock. What wakes it—" she pressed her palm flat against the table, against the wood that had once been a living thing, feeling faintly, terribly, the memory of sap still sleeping in the grain— "what wakes it is *us*. Is the praying. Eleven moons of it, thickening like cream. We could storm the throne room tomorrow and cut Aldric's throat and it wouldn't matter, because by then the god will be less in the king than in ten thousand open hearts. It built itself a congregation. It made the whole city into the thirteenth turn."

Silence. Somewhere above, a beam settled with a groan, and Gideon heard—she couldn't help hearing, not anymore—the old wood dreaming of forests.

Tamsin spoke first, very quietly. "Then what do you do against a weapon made of hope?"

"That," said Gideon, "is the question I've been hiding under the floorboards for two months."

* * *

Bramble found her that night on the roof again, which was becoming their place, though neither of them had ever said so. He landed on her knee with the delicacy of a bird who had once belonged to a witch with standards.

"You told them," he said. Not a question. Crows disapproved of questions.

"I told them."

"And?"

"And Odessa started listing ways to burn down the Hollow Shrine, and Serah pointed out that martyrs are the best fertilizer for faith there is, and Tamsin—" Gideon's throat caught unexpectedly. "Tamsin said maybe the answer isn't stopping the praying. Maybe it's giving people something better to pray *to*. Or someone. And then she looked straight at me, Bramble, and I pretended I had to check the eel traps."

Bramble ruffled his feathers in the way that meant he was laughing at her, a soundless, contemptuous little shiver. "You are the worst coward I have served in thirty years, and I served Maren Ashvale, who once hid inside a bear for a week to avoid a difficult conversation."

"She sounds wonderful."

"She was terrible. You'd have loved her." He hopped up onto her shoulder, talons finding the old familiar purchase. Below, across the black rooftops, the palace glowed with festival-light—Aldric's court celebrating the Feast of the Unconquered Dawn, some Edict holiday nobody believed in anymore but everybody observed, because observance was safe. From here Gideon could hear it all if she let herself: the fiddlers, the laughter, and underneath, woven through every note like a worm in fruit, the great low hum of ten thousand small prayers rising from ten thousand houses. *Keep my son safe. Let the price of flour drop. Let me see my mother again. Let me matter. Let me matter. Let me matter.*

"It's using them," she whispered. "It's wearing their hoping like a coat."

"It was worshipped willingly once," Bramble said. "You know that now. Your grandmother wrote it plainly enough. Perhaps it never learned any other way to be fed. Perhaps it genuinely thinks it's *helping*." A pause, and then, gentler than crows usually manage: "Maren used to say that most monsters are just hungers that grew in the shape of the feeding."

Gideon stared at the palace lights. Somewhere behind those walls was a man mouthing *help me* between a god's sentences, and somewhere below was a city begging the thing that held him to please, please make the hunger stop, not understanding that the hunger was drinking straight from the asking.

Despair came over her like high tide, slow and total, and she let it—for a moment, only a moment—because fighting it all the time was its own exhaustion. Eleven moons. Two left. An enemy with a calendar and a congregation and infinite patience, and her side had one burned-out smuggler, one disgraced physician, one one-armed captain, a crow, and a charm-seller who talked to rats.

Then the tide went out, the way it did lately, and left something hard and bright behind on the sand.

"Bramble," she said slowly. "When Maren fed you. Treats, scraps—whatever witches fed familiars. Did you ever pray to her?"

He tilted his head. "Crows don't pray."

"But if you had. If you'd wanted something from her badly enough. How would you have gotten it?"

"By asking *her*," he said promptly. "Directly. Not into the air like a fool. To her face, with an offering." His beak clicked as the thought assembled. "Prayer goes to whatever's listening. But a speaker—" 

"A speaker can *answer*," Gideon finished. Her heart had begun to hammer, and around her, faintly, she could feel the night-city stir: cats lifting their heads on windowsills, dogs raising their ears, moths turning on the wind. Everything in Vaelmoor that had a voice had been waiting eleven moons for hers. "The god answers prayers because it's the only one answering. But it's not the only one *hearing* anymore, is it? There's me. There's the whole waking world. If the people of Vaelmoor prayed to something that actually listened back—if even a handful of them got an answer that wasn't a hook in the mouth—"

"—the threads start running to a different spool," Bramble breathed. "Oh, you mad, marvelous creature. Maren would have kissed your forehead and then locked you in a cellar for suggesting it."

"She'd have been right to do both." Gideon stood, knees cracking, and looked down through the smoke-hole at the lamplight where her family argued about turnips and kept each other alive. It was a mad plan. It was probably a doomed plan. It required her to do the one thing three years of hiding had taught her never to do—to stand in front of a frightened, hungry, praying city and speak.

But Tamsin had already seen it. Had named it, gently, over supper, and Gideon had run to check eels that didn't exist.

"Get Odessa," she said. "Get all of them. Tell them the charm-seller's opening shop again—and this time the stock is miracles."

Far away, deep beneath the palace, in the dark where a crown sat on a hollowed head, something vast and patient paused mid-drink—and, for the first time in three hundred years, listened.
