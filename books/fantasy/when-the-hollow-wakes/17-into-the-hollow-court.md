## 17. Into the Hollow Court

The rats told them about the door.

Not a door in the way that doors are usually meant—no hinges, no handle, nothing a sensible architect would sign his name to—but a gap beneath the old granary on Weft Street where the foundation stones had shifted after the Godfall and never been set right again. The rats had been using it for three generations. They were, Gideon had learned, extremely proprietary about it.

"The gray one says we owe him for the information," she reported, crouched at the granary's edge while the last light died. "He's asking for bread. Specifically the crusts. He has opinions about crust."

"Tell the gray one," said Serah Vane, kneeling beside her in the mud with all the dignity of a woman who had once held a physician's chair at court and now had rat-bargaining on her résumé, "that he is receiving medical attention for free and can consider us even."

"He says medicine is not bread."

"He says—" Serah pinched the bridge of her nose. "Fine. Crusts after. If we live."

"That's what worries him," Gideon said. "He'd like payment first."

Bramble, perched on her shoulder with his one good eye fixed on the darkening city, made a sound that was either laughter or a death rattle. With Bramble it was often both. "Pay the man," he croaked. "Cheap at twice the price. In my day a familiar negotiated these things, you know. Now everyone wants a cut. Rats, gulls, moths. There's a moth asking for a fee. A *moth*, Gideon."

"What does a moth even want?"

"Lamplight and tragedy. It's very dramatic." He ruffled his feathers. "I like him."

Gideon dug the crusts from her coat pocket—she had started carrying them days ago, once she understood how much of this plan rested on rodent goodwill—and scattered them along the wall. The gray rat emerged from the shadow of the granary with the unhurried confidence of a landlord collecting rent, took a crust in both paws, and regarded her with small black eyes that held, she thought, more sense than half the Crown's council.

Then he chattered something quick and urgent, and her stomach dropped.

*They've doubled the watch at the river gates. Dogs on the walls tonight. Big ones.*

She passed it along in a whisper. Serah's face went pale and set. "Then we go now, or not at all. Once the moon rises—"

"Once the moon rises, it won't matter what we do," Gideon finished. Eleven turns gone. One left. Tomorrow night the thirteenth moon would crest, and whatever was wearing King Aldric's face would wake all the way, fed fat on a year of answered prayers, and no rat in Vaelmoor would be able to tell her anything useful ever again because there would be no *her* to tell it to.

Tonight was the only door. Even if the door was under a granary and smelled like it.

She pressed two fingers to her collarbone, where the hedge-witch's chalk mark still sat beneath her shirt—a spiral, worn almost away by sweat and fear—and thought, as she had thought every hour for eleven moons: *I should have told them.* Odessa. Tamsin. Even Serah, standing right here, breathing her nervous little physician breaths. The rite needed a speaker willing to carry the binding. Three Winterbournes had tried it before her and none had come back whole. She had read her grandmother's warning a hundred times—*do not carry it alone*—and had decided, quietly, in the stubborn cellar of herself, that carrying it alone was exactly what Winterbournes did. That it was practically the family trade.

Bramble's claw tightened on her shoulder. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Not tonight," he murmured, too soft for anyone else to hear. "Whatever you're deciding in there. Not tonight."

"I'm not deciding anything," she lied.

"You have your grandmother's exact face when you lie. I knew her, girl. I know the face."

* * *

The beasts' way began as a crack and became a throat.

Gideon went first, because the rats insisted, because she was the smallest, and because—though nobody said this aloud—if something down there ate people, it ought to eat the one whose gift could at least negotiate. The passage was older than the Edict, older than the Godfall, maybe older than the city itself: a seam of darkness where the palace foundations met the bones of the hill, carved long ago by hands that had wanted animals to come and go unseen. Beast-speakers' ways, the hedge-witch had called them. Her grandmother's people had built them so the bound king's watchers could never seal every ear of the city shut. The Edict had walled up the doors and forgotten the walls forget too.

Serah came behind her, breathing through her mouth and reciting anatomical terms under her breath like a rosary. "Femur, tibia, this is definitely some enormous femur—"

"It's a support pillar," Gideon said.

"I'm choosing to believe it's a pillar."

Bramble flew ahead as best he could in the low dark, a scrap of muttering shadow. Every hundred feet or so the passage opened into pockets where roots had broken through, and in those pockets lived things: bats who gave directions in clicks and echoes, a family of foxes who had moved in from the hills during the famine years and charged toll in eggs, an ancient blind badger whom everyone simply called Grandmother, including, apparently, the foxes. Grandmother the badger lay across the final junction like a fur rug that had opinions, and she spoke in a voice Gideon felt more than heard, slow as continental drift.

*You go to the hollow one,* the badger said.

"Yes," Gideon whispered.

*My mother's mother remembered the first binding. The screaming lasted nine days. You could hear it in the eggshells.* A long pause, filled with the sound of the badger breathing, which was like listening to a cellar settle. *The thing they bound was not evil, little speaker. That was the mistake they all made. It was hungry. Evil knows better than to be hungry where everyone can see.*

"Thank you," Gideon managed, which felt inadequate, and offered the dried fish she'd brought. The badger accepted it with the gravity of a queen receiving tribute and rolled aside just enough to let them pass.

Beyond her, the passage climbed, and the air changed—stone dust giving way to candle smoke, lilies, incense, the particular sweetish smell of a great deal of wealth trying very hard to smell holy. Somewhere above them, muffled through fathoms of stone, the city was beginning to burn.

That had been Odessa's part of it. Gideon tried not to think about it and failed completely.

* * *

Odessa Finch had laid out the plan three nights ago on the Saltcellar's chart table, using salt, spoons, and Tamsin's cup, because the crew owned no map accurate enough for sedition and Odessa refused on principle to draw one.

"They want a distraction," she'd said, tapping the spoon that stood in for the palace. "Witch-finders pulled off the walls, guards staring the wrong way, the whole garrison scratching its collective head. Fine. Vaelmoor's got forty thousand people and most of them haven't eaten proper in a month, the god's cults are brawling the Edict loyalists every night, and the grain barges stopped coming Tuesday. I don't have to make a riot. I just have to aim one."

"And you'll be at the front of it," Gideon had said. It hadn't been a question.

Odessa had looked at her with that flat grey stare, the one with her brother's ghost standing behind it. "Someone has to hold the tiller."

"Odessa—"

"Don't." But then, quieter, in the voice she used maybe twice a year: "Eleven months ago I fished you out of a canal because you were worth losing things for. Turns out the list got longer. That's how it goes. You don't get to choose the size of what you love." She'd swept the salt back into its crock, meeting adjourned. "Riot starts at dusk bells. You'll have until the second bell after midnight. After that I stop aiming and start surviving, and so had you better."

Now, in the dark throat under the palace, Gideon heard dusk bells roll down through the stone like coins dropped down a well, and a moment later—faint, far, unmistakable—the sound of a city deciding it was done.

It didn't sound like violence, at first. It sounded like cheering.

* * *

They came up through a beast-door behind the old falconry, into air that tasted of smoke and lilies, and Gideon stood a moment in the courtyard weeds with her heart slamming, listening.

Listening was different lately. Since the first turning moon it had been getting harder to close the gift off, and tonight she didn't try. The palace opened around her like a struck bell. Horses in the royal stable, sweating panic at the smoke-smell. Falcons on their perches, wild-eyed, screaming one word over and over: *hungry hungry hungry hungry*. Dogs on the walls pulling their handlers toward the riot with the simple joy of dogs who have finally been given permission. And underneath it all, everywhere, seeping up from the throne room at the palace's heart like cold from a cellar floor:

*A hunger with a calendar. Patient. Pleased. Setting the table.*

"This way," she whispered, and led them through service corridors Serah half-remembered and the mice fully remembered, past kitchens stripped near bare, past a chapel where a lone priest prayed with the desperate speed of a man doing sums he knows will come out wrong. Twice they froze for patrols; once a witch-finder's hound turned its head and looked directly at Gideon, and Gideon looked back and said, silently, in the oldest grammar of her gift: *not prey. Not tonight. Sleep.* The hound yawned enormously and sat down, and its handler cursed it roundly for laziness, and they slipped past.

The doors of the great hall were guarded, but the hall had a hundred other mouths. The beasts' ways threaded the palace like veins in a wrist, and one of them opened—of course it did, her grandmother's people had planned it that way—high in the wall behind the throne, behind a screen of carved stonework worn smooth by centuries of wings.

From there, Gideon looked down on the court of the Waking God.

* * *

Candles by the thousand. Courtiers in white, kneeling in ranks on the marble, faces upturned, eyes shining with the terrible clean joy of the truly converted. Incense thick enough to swim in. And on the dais, beneath a canopy worked with sunbursts, the throne.

King Aldric sat upon it.

Or rather: the shape of King Aldric sat upon it, robed in gold, crowned, hands folded on the arms of the throne with the stillness of something that had learned stillness the way water learns the shape of a bowl. From a distance—from the distance of the kneeling court—he might have been a king at prayer. He might have been a saint.

Gideon had seen him before, from farther off, at processions, through crowds. She had expected a monster. Some part of her had braced for horns, for a shadow too big for the body, for the obvious machinery of evil.

What she saw instead was a tired man in his forties sitting very straight because if he slumped, something would notice.

And between the god's words—because the god was speaking; the whole hall rang faintly with it, a voice like honey poured over iron, promising the kneeling court harvest and healing and a new age where no prayer would go unanswered—between those golden syllables, the man's lips moved.

Small movements. Easy to miss. Gideon saw them because she had spent three years learning to read mouths across noisy markets, because she watched everything, because watching was how you stayed alive.

*Help me.*

The god said: *I have heard your hunger, my children, and I am not full.*

The man's lips shaped: *help me.*

The god said: *Bring me the last of the speakers, and the thirteenth moon shall make you all forever.*

The man closed his eyes. Just for a moment. The way a drowning man closes his eyes when the light on the surface gets too far away to bear looking at.

"Oh," Gideon said. It came out very small. "Oh, you're still *in there*."

Beside her, Serah made a sound like a wound. "I examined him," she whispered. "For years. Fevers, joints, his headaches—I held his wrist and counted his pulse and I never—how did I never see—"

"Because you weren't listening," Gideon said, and meant no cruelty by it. "Nobody was. That's how it feeds, Serah. It eats the prayers and leaves the man. He's been starving in front of the whole court for six years, and they're all kneeling to the thing that's eating him."

Below, the god raised Aldric's hand in blessing, and the court sighed as one body, and somewhere in that sigh was Tamsin's burned-out gift and Odessa's lost arm and thirty years of ash from the purges, and Gideon felt her fury rise hot and ready—

—and then the king's eyes lifted.

Not the god's eyes. His. For a heartbeat the glaze cracked, and what looked out of that face across the candles and the incense and the kneeling thousands was exhausted, and terrified, and achingly ordinary. His gaze wandered up the wall, past the carved screen, and found hers.

He couldn't have seen her. It was impossible. The screen was deep shadow, the hall was bright.

But his lips moved anyway, barely, barely, and this time she didn't need the gift to read them, because she already knew the words.

*Help me.*

And Gideon Winterbourne—who had spent three years perfecting invisibility, who had walked past a dying horse, who had kept silent through purges and raids while Tamsin paid for her silence—felt something in her chest break open in a way that had nothing to do with fear.

*I'm coming,* she said—not aloud, but in the deep grammar, the one that reached beasts and badgers and, apparently, the buried remnant of a hollowed king. *Hold on. I'm coming.*

His breath hitched. The god noticed—it paused mid-blessing, Aldric's head tilting like a bird's, attention sliding up the wall toward the screen with the smooth terrible curiosity of a thing that has just heard a mouse in the pantry—

"Gideon," Bramble breathed against her ear. "Move. *Now.*"

The thirteenth moon was tomorrow. The god's voice poured gold over the kneeling court below. And high in the dark of the wall, behind a screen carved by her grandmother's people, Gideon stepped backward into the beasts' way—knowing, with absolute clarity, that whatever happened next, she could not carry this binding alone.

She just didn't yet know how to say so.
