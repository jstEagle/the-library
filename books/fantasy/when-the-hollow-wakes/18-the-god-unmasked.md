## 18. The God Unmasked

The throne room of Vaelmoor had been built to make a person feel small.

Gideon had always assumed that was the point — kings liked their visitors humbled. But standing now on the dais steps, with witch-finder helmets rolling across the marble behind her and Odessa's riot roaring distantly through the palace like weather, she understood that the room's true architecture was older than any king. The pillars were too tall for human masons. The windows were shaped like eyes. And every stone sang, faintly, with a hunger she recognized from her bones outward.

*You came.*

It did not speak aloud. That was the first horror of it. The voice arrived the way her gift arrived — inside, intimate, familiar as her own heartbeat. Which, she realized, was precisely what it had been doing for eleven months: beating in time with hers, learning the rhythm, waiting.

"I came for the king," Gideon said.

*He is here.* Aldric sat on the throne, and up close he was worse and better than Serah had described. Worse, because his skin had gone translucent at the temples, veins showing like roots through snow. Better, because his eyes — those famous empty eyes — were not empty. Something flickered behind them, deep down, the way a candle gutters behind shuttered glass. His lips moved between the god's words, shaping syllables that made no sound.

Gideon read them easily. She'd spent three years reading mouths through market noise.

*Help me. Help me. Help me.*

Her chest did something painful. She filed it away. There would be time to feel things later, if there was a later.

Bramble shifted on her shoulder, his claws tightening through her coat. *Careful,* he murmured, that gravel-and-ash voice of his. *It talks pretty. It always talked pretty. That's how it got fed in the first place.*

"The crow," said the god, and now it did use Aldric's mouth, and the sound was wrong — warm and rich and layered, like a choir squeezed through one throat. "Thirty years I have felt him grieving. Such small, fierce grief. Crows grieve beautifully, do you know that? They hold funerals for their dead." Aldric's head tilted. "Your speaker died screaming, little shadow. Would you like me to tell you her last words? I remember everything anyone ever prayed."

"Don't," Gideon said quietly.

Bramble's feathers flattened against her neck. He said nothing. She put a hand up and touched his breastbone, felt the rapid tick of his heart, and something in her settled into a decision she hadn't known she was making: *no more hiding, no more hedges, no more running.* She stepped forward onto the dais.

"Then let's skip the threats," she said. "I know why you're talking instead of taking. You can't reach me while I'm awake and angry. So you're going to try the other thing."

Aldric smiled. It was a good smile. It was an *awful* smile, because somewhere under it the king was still mouthing *help me*, and the mouth doing the smiling belonged to both of them at once.

*The other thing,* the god agreed, dropping back into her skull, gentle as a hand on her hair. *Truth. You deserve truth, Winterbourne. You have been lied to since before you were born.*

* * *

The throne room went away.

Not faded — went, like a page turned, and Gideon stood ankle-deep in warm grass under a sky the color of unpolished silver, and the city of Vaelmoor was around her but *wrong*: smaller, wooden, ringed by standing stones that no longer existed anywhere but this memory. Beasts moved through the streets openly. A bear argued cheerfully with a fishmonger. Children rode a boar who was complaining about it in a voice exactly like Tamsin's.

And the people were praying. Not the frightened, furtive prayers of the Hollow Shrine — these prayers rose like birdsong, unashamed, and every one of them *landed*. Gideon could feel them landing, could feel the answering warmth pour back down, and it was —

It was the second heartbeat. The thing she'd felt stirring since the beggar woman's healing. Except here it wasn't a whisper. Here it was a *chorus*, and it sounded like home.

"This is before the Godfall," she whispered.

*Before the Edict. Before the fear. Look closer.*

She looked. And because her gift had always been listening rather than seeing, she listened — and heard what the memory wanted her to hear. Not worship, exactly. Not only. Under the hymns ran something else: bargains. Contracts. Farmers trading first-fruits for rain. Mothers promising years of service for sick children's lives. A young man on his knees before the standing stones, offering *everything*, because the girl he loved had died and the god had promised — had *promised* — to weigh her soul gently.

"They didn't just love you," Gideon said slowly. "They paid you."

*They gave gladly,* the god corrected, and its voice had lost none of its warmth, which somehow made it worse. *Is a gift not still a gift when given gladly? I never took what was not offered, Gideon Winterbourne. I fed on devotion the way you feed on bread, and in exchange I healed, and blessed, and answered. Your Edict calls that hunger. Your histories call it the Fall. But ask the beasts what happened. Ask them who drew the circle.*

The grassland shivered. Now she saw the binding — saw it as the beasts must have seen it, done from below, from the ground where the beast-speakers had stood. The Winterbournes. Her blood. Figures in grey with antler-shadow horns, singing a circle around the standing stones, and the song was beautiful and it was also a *cage*, and the god's vast attention folded down into it like a storm forced into a bottle.

*They bound me,* it said, without heat. *Not because I broke faith. Because they feared what faith could build. One man offered me his whole heart, and your ancestors looked at that and saw slavery. Perhaps they were right. Perhaps they were afraid. It has been so long I no longer know which story is true. But I know this:* the silver sky leaned close, *your grandmother knew it too. She found my notes in my own cage. She learned what I was before the circle. And she chose the circle anyway, and then she ran, and then she burned, and her last thought was that she should have read further.*

Gideon's breath caught. "What's the rest of it say?"

*Ah.* Warmth again, terrible and patient. *Now we arrive at the offer.*

* * *

The vision changed. This time it did not bother being subtle.

She stood in the kitchen of the house above the harbor — a house she had not seen since she was nine years old — and her grandmother sat at the table mending a net, humming, alive, whole, the burn scars that Gideon had never actually witnessed absent from hands that were only hands. Maren Winterbourne looked up and smiled, and said, "There you are, goose. You're late. The gulls have been gossiping about you all morning."

Gideon did not move. Every part of her wanted to move. She gripped the doorframe until the wood creaked, because she knew — she *knew* — this was bait, and knowing did not matter at all, because her grandmother smelled like cedar smoke and lamp oil and the specific soap she used to buy from the chandler on Rope Street, and grief is not rational, grief is a beast, and beasts can be spoken to but rarely reasoned with.

"You're not her," Gideon managed.

*No,* the god admitted, and the kitchen stayed anyway, kind and cruel. *But I remember her perfectly. I remember everyone who ever prayed within a hundred miles of my prison, and she prayed constantly, mostly complaints about you. 'That child talks to rats,' she told me once, 'and lies about the mending.' I could give you thirty years of her. Accurate to the last sigh. Is that nothing?*

"It's a puppet wearing my grandmother's voice."

*A puppet,* the god agreed pleasantly, *that remembers more of her than you do. Shall we go on?*

The kitchen dissolved into the Saltcellars' low hall. Tamsin sat by the fire where she always sat, wrapped in a blanket, staring at her own hands — and then her head came up, and her eyes focused, really focused, for the first time since the rescue, and she said, "Gid? Gid, I can hear the gulls again. Gid, they're saying the harbor's full of herring, they're saying—" and she laughed, and the laugh cracked Gideon open along seams she hadn't known she had.

*Her gift was not destroyed,* the god said softly. *Nothing is ever destroyed. It is folded away, and I can unfold it. I can unfold every speaker the finders burned hollow. I can give this city back its voice — all of you, every rat-whisperer and bird-caller and horse-mender, out of the cellars and the hedges and into the light. No more Edict. No more burning. Speakers at the right hand of the crown, because the crown will be* — Aldric's face surfaced briefly in the firelight, serene — *occupied.*

"And the price," Gideon said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "There's always a price. You taught this city that. First-fruits. Firstborns. Whole hearts."

*Thirteenth moons,* said the god. *Devotion freely renewed. Prayer without shame. Nothing you were not already made of, Gideon. You have been starving yourself for three years to avoid exactly this bargain, and look what it bought you. A dead neighbor. A broken friend. A city that informs on itself. Hiding was your sacrifice, and it purchased nothing but loneliness.*

The words went in deep, because they were *true*, or true enough, and that was the worst discovery of the night: the villain's best argument was built almost entirely out of honest timber.

"I watched you consume a witch-finder captain mid-prayer," Gideon said. "On the Turning Moon. Was that devotion?"

*He prayed to me with a knife in his hand and hate in his mouth. I am not obliged to enjoy every meal.* The god's warmth flickered — the first crack she'd seen in it, brief as a blink. *Yes. I am hungry. Hunger is not evil; hunger is hunger. Your Edict starved me for twenty years and called the starvation peace. I am offering you a world where no one starves — not me, not Tamsin, not the beasts whose tongues your law cut out. I am offering you your grandmother's laugh and your friend's sight and a seat beside a throne instead of beneath a knife. Name one thing in that list that is wicked.*

Gideon opened her mouth.

And Bramble said, from her shoulder, very quietly: *"Me."*

* * *

The vision snapped taut around them — kitchen, hall, silver sky, all of it holding its breath.

"Grief is the price," Bramble said. His voice was steady in the way that cost him visibly, feather by feather. "You want us fed. Fed things get fat. Fat things get *kept*. I watched it happen, bird and woman alike, thirty years ago. My speaker started with luck-charms and ended kneeling on cold stone giving you her winters, her summers, her name, until there was nothing left to kneel *with*. You didn't take it. You never take. You just make the giving feel like flying right up until the ground." He looked up at Gideon, one eye scarred and bright. "And you, girl. Ask it what happened to the last three Winterbournes who tried the other road. Ask it about the unbinding. Ask it why it's *talking*."

The god was silent for a long moment.

When it spoke again, the warmth remained, but something vast moved underneath it, the way a whale moves under calm water.

*Because I am tired,* it said. *Twenty years of ash and silence. Eleven months of borrowed flesh. I have worn kings and I have worn beggars and I am old, older than your stones, and there is a third road that neither of you has named. Bind me into a willing vessel — not a cage, a *communion* — and I will show you what your bloodline was meant to be, before the fear. Or refuse, and in nine nights the thirteenth moon crests and I wake regardless, through a thousand throats that love me, and we will do this the old way. Either way, Gideon Winterbourne, you choose tonight. The moon does not wait. The king cannot hold me much longer — see how he frays.*

She looked at Aldric. The flicker behind his eyes was guttering now, the candle nearly drowned. His lips still moved. *Help me.* But beneath it, fainter, almost lost, new words:

*Don't listen. Don't—* and then his jaw stilled, seized, and the god poured back in like tide reclaiming sand, and Aldric's spine straightened on the throne, and the smile returned.

*Nine nights,* said the god, gentle as cedar smoke. *Choose.*

* * *

The throne room came back. Real torchlight, real smoke, the distant thunder of Odessa's crew breaking down some outer gate. Gideon's legs were shaking. Bramble pressed against her cheek, small and solid and real in a way no vision had been.

"Gid," he murmured. "Whatever it showed you — the kitchen, the girl —"

"I know."

"It lied about the parts that mattered."

"No," Gideon said, and her voice came out strange and level, and somewhere beneath it the second heartbeat kept its patient time, and she understood suddenly that she had already chosen, had perhaps chosen the moment her grandmother's ghost had smiled at her and she had refused to cross the room. "It didn't lie. That's what frightens me. Everything it offered was real." She looked up at the throne, at the fraying man inside the hunger, and lifted her chin.

"So I'm going to give it something real back."

Behind the god's eyes, for half a heartbeat, Aldric stopped mouthing *help me* — and began, instead, to nod.
