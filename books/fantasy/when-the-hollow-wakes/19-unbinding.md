## 19. Unbinding

The throne room of Vaelmoor had been built to make a person feel small.

Gideon had always assumed that was the point—architecture as intimidation, all those pillars like petrified trees and that ceiling lost somewhere in shadow above. But standing there now, with the thirteenth moon cresting through the great eastern window and pouring silver down onto the dais, she understood it differently. The room wasn't built to make people feel small. It was built to make them feel *heard*. Every surface curved inward, every angle funneled sound toward the throne, so that when a petitioner spoke their plea, it arrived at the king's ear wrapped in the room's own attention.

It was a listening room. And something in it was listening very carefully right now.

"Talk to me," she murmured, and every rat in the rafters, every moth beating against the glass, every mouse in the palace walls turned its small face toward her at once. "What do you feel?"

*Hungry,* said the mice. *Hungry,* said the moths. And beneath it, older and colder, a voice that did not come from any throat: *Come closer, little speaker.*

"Not yet," Gideon said. "I'm not finished being afraid."

She knelt on the cold flagstones and began unpacking her grandmother's things from the satchel: three candles made of tallow and grave-wax, a bowl of salt water, a knot of braided hair that Bramble had guarded for thirty years without telling her what it was. The notes were spread across her knees—the hedge-witch's careful transcription of her grandmother's hand, the unbinding rite written out in ink that had faded to the color of old bruises.

Eleven steps. Eleven knots. She'd practiced them until her fingers could tie them blind, drunk, weeping, or all three.

The problem was step twelve.

*The binding must go somewhere,* the hedge-witch had said, turning her cup of tea in her ruined hands. *That is the cruelty of it. You cannot unmake a cage; you can only change what it holds. Three Winterbournes tried. All three carried it alone. All three burned out like lamps with no oil.* She had looked at Gideon then with an expression Gideon still hadn't decided whether to call pity or warning. *Find oil enough for two lamps, girl. Or find a hundred.*

Gideon had smiled and thanked her and told no one.

The moon crested higher. Somewhere below, muffled by stone, the Saltcellar riot was beginning—she could hear it through the gulls wheeling over the harbor district, through the rats along the canal walls: Odessa's crew setting fire to the tithe-barges, the witch-finders' horns sounding, the city waking up angry in all directions at once. A distraction bought with arrest warrants and broken bones. She had hugged Odessa goodbye and Odessa had gripped her with the one arm and said, "Come back or I'll dig you up just to yell at you."

"Gideon." Serah's voice, low, from the shadow of the pillar where she kept watch. "Movement. The god's attention is shifting—it knows something's wrong."

"It's known since I walked in."

"Then hurry."

"You can't hurry a rite." She struck the first match. "Believe me. I've thought about it."

* * *

The candles caught. The salt water trembled in its bowl though nothing touched it. And King Aldric lifted his head.

For eleven moons, Gideon had seen him only at distance, through borrowed eyes—a rat in a wall, a gull on a roofline. Always the same: the beautiful hollow face, the crown sitting on him like a lid on a jar, the eyes with nothing home behind them but patience. Now, ten feet away, she saw the truth the beasts had been trying to tell her all year.

He was flickering.

Like a candle in wind, the man guttered up through the thing wearing him—and for one heartbeat his eyes were not patient, not hungry, but *terrified*, and his mouth shaped words around the god's silence: *help me.*

"I know," Gideon whispered. Her hands shook on the second knot. "I'm trying. Hold on."

The god noticed. Of course it noticed. The temperature of the room changed—not colder, exactly, but *thinner*, as if the air had been wrung out. The shadows under the pillars deepened and leaned toward her like tall grass toward a door.

*You came alone,* it said, and its voice was kind, which was the worst thing about it. It had spent a year learning kindness from ten thousand prayers. *Alone, small, mortal. I could give you your grandmother back. I have her last winter, still, folded here with everything they prayed for and never received. I could—*

"No." Third knot. Fourth. "You could offer. That's different from giving. You've never given anything. You just leave the plate out and let people starve toward it."

*They love me,* the god said, and there was something almost wounded in it, almost true. *No one bound me because I was cruel. They bound me because I was loved too much. Do you understand the difference, little speaker?*

"Actually," Gideon said, tying the fifth knot, "I think I do. And I think that's why my family caged you instead of killing you. Because even then, somebody loved you."

The god went quiet.

And Aldric screamed.

Not aloud—his mouth didn't move—but through every beast in the palace at once, a sound like a hinge tearing free after thirty years rusted shut. Gideon felt him rise inside himself, felt the man take hold of the god's edges from within the way a drowning sailor takes hold of a spar, and she understood what he was doing and what it would cost and she tied the sixth knot faster.

*Go,* said every mouse in Vaelmoor, in his cracked and borrowed voice. *While I hold the door.*

"Aldric—" she said, uselessly, absurdly. "Thank you."

*Don't thank me,* he said, and now she could hear how thin he was, how little of him was left, a man holding a door against a tide with his shoulder and his teeth. *Finish it. My name was Aldric Winterbourne's—no. No, wait. That's wrong. My name was—*

The god slammed down on him like a lid.

The seventh knot slipped. Gideon's hands were slick with sweat and shaking so badly she couldn't feel the cord, and the god rose off the throne in a shape that had no business existing—a vast slow unfurling of attention, of *hunger given geometry*, filling the space between the pillars like smoke fills a lung—and it looked at her with Aldric's beautiful empty face stretched across its whole impossible front, and it said:

*You cannot carry me alone. No single vessel holds a god. That is the first law, older than your Edict, older than your gods. You will break, little speaker. Break beautifully, if you like. But you will break.*

"I know," Gideon said.

Her voice came out steadier than she expected. Steadier than she felt. She thought of the hedge-witch's hundred lamps. She thought of three Winterbournes burning out alone because they were proud, or frightened, or—most likely—because they loved their families too much to ask them to share the dying.

She thought about how tired she was of doing things alone.

"Bramble," she said. "Get everyone."

*Already gone,* said the crow.

* * *

He went out the high window and into the dark, and Gideon knelt alone before a god with four knots left to tie and waited, and the waiting was the longest minute of her life.

The god talked while she waited. It was good at talking. It showed her her grandmother's kitchen, bread on the board, steam on the window. It showed her Tamsin's stall with the luck-knots hanging in rows, both of them laughing over nothing. It offered her a Vaelmoor where the Edict had never fallen, where speakers walked in daylight, where nobody had ever had to hide anything ever again. And underneath all of it, sweet and reasonable, ran the real argument, the honest one: *I was only ever asking to be loved. Is that a crime? Your whole species prays. You built rooms like this one to carry your voices to someone. I am simply the someone who answered.*

"That's the trap, isn't it," Gideon murmured, tying the eighth knot. "You're not lying. That's what makes you a god and not a devil. Everything you say is true. It's just also a mouth."

The window banged open.

They came in the way no army ever had—through the forgotten ways, the beasts' ways, up the outer wall and along the ledges that only pigeons used. Odessa first, one-armed and swearing, hauling herself over the sill with Serah pushing behind her. Then the crew: Pike with his smuggler's rope still coiled on his shoulder, young Fen with a kitchen knife he clearly had no idea how to use, old Marta who'd once ferried fugitives through the Underveil and hadn't stopped moving since. And last, half-carried, wrapped in a stolen cloak, gray-faced and hollow-eyed—

Tamsin.

"They wouldn't leave her at the safehouse," Odessa said, breathing hard. "Wouldn't hear it. Said—" her voice cracked, and she steadied it with visible effort, "—said family doesn't get left out of things."

Tamsin looked at Gideon kneeling on the stones with her cords and candles, and her ruined gift read the room the way burned-out speakers still can, the way you can read a fire's history in ash. Her eyes filled.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you stupid brave girl. It's tonight, isn't it. It's been tonight for weeks and you weren't going to tell us."

"There wasn't time—"

"There's never time!" Tamsin's voice broke into something fierce. "There's never time and there's never enough of us and that's exactly why you tell people, Gideon. That's the entire point of people!"

The god watched them all arrive with something that might have been curiosity. *More lamps,* it observed. *Still too few.*

"We'll see," Gideon said, and tied the ninth knot, and held the cord out flat across the flagstones between the candles, an open line, an unfinished braid. "Listen. Everyone. Quickly, because this gets bad fast. The binding has to land somewhere. In me, unless—I need hands. Not magic. Most of you don't have any, and it doesn't matter. The rite runs on willingness. On choosing to hold something that isn't yours. Touch the cord. All of you. And whatever you feel when the moon crests—and it will feel like drowning, I won't lie to you—don't let go."

Silence. One breath. Two.

Pike spat on his palm—for luck, the old canal custom—and put it on the cord. Marta next. Fen, white-knuckled, whispering an apology to no one in particular. Odessa laid her forearm along the braided line like a woman laying claim to a deck. Serah knelt last, the disgraced physician, the woman who had treated the king for years and never once looked in his eyes, and pressed her fingers down beside Gideon's own.

"He held the door for you," Serah said quietly. "Least we can do is hold it for you."

"Tamsin?" Gideon said. "You don't have to. Your gift is—"

"My gift is burned. My *hands* work fine." Tamsin took the cord with both of them.

Bramble hopped down the center of the line, crow-footed and solemn as a funeral bird, and stood on the knot itself.

*We are the oil,* he said. *All of us. Tie the tenth knot, Winterbourne. We're done hiding.*

Gideon laughed—or sobbed, hard to say—and tied the tenth knot, and the eleventh, and the room went silver as the moon reached its crest, and the god rose up in its full and terrible attention, and she pulled the final turn tight and spoke the word her grandmother had died rather than surrender:

"*Unbind.*"

* * *

Later, Gideon would try to describe it and fail. The closest she ever got was this: imagine holding a river in your arms. Imagine it agrees to be held.

The binding came off Aldric like ice breaking off a cliff-face, and it came *into* her, and it was every prayer ever prayed in Vaelmoor, ten thousand voices wanting things, and the weight of want itself, the whole hungerscape of a city, landing on her chest all at once. She heard herself scream from very far away. She felt the cord go taut through seven pairs of hands and felt each of them take weight—Pike gasping, Marta praying, Fen crying without letting go, Odessa snarling like a storm at sea, Serah steady as a scalpel, Tamsin holding with hands that would never work magic again and working the oldest magic there was anyway, which is staying.

And through Bramble—through the scarred old familiar who had waited thirty years for exactly this—something passed down the line that was not weight at all. It was warmth. It was the sense of being many hands on one rope, of a burden redistributed until it was not a burden but a *shared thing*, the way grief is bearable when it's witnessed, the way joy is doubled when it's divided.

*This,* said the god, and for the first time it sounded small. *This is what they feared. Not power. This.*

"Yes," Gideon said, from somewhere at the bottom of the silver light. "Power you have to carry alone eats you. Power you carry together—" she tightened her grip on the cord, and seven grips tightened with hers, "—power you carry together is just *people*. Welcome to the worst fate imaginable. Being one of us."

The binding settled. The god folded down, and down, and down, smaller and smaller, furious and diminished and—she felt this with perfect clarity, and would spend a year making peace with it—*relieved*. A hunger put away. A plate finally cleared from a table where no one should have been asked to eat.

The moon passed its crest and began, slowly, to fall.

* * *

When Gideon opened her eyes, she was on the floor of the throne room with her head in Tamsin's lap, and everyone was alive, and the king was weeping.

King Aldric lay crumpled at the base of his own throne like a coat dropped off a hook, thirty years older than his portraits, hollowed and shaking and unmistakably, heartbreakingly *present*. He was looking at his hands. Turning them over. As though checking whether they still belonged to him.

"It's quiet," he kept saying. His voice was rusty, a well-pulley unused for years. "It's quiet in here. What did you—what did you do? There was something in me, there was something, and now it's—"

"Caged," Gideon said. "Not dead. I'm sorry. It couldn't be dead. It lives in the binding now, and the binding lives in—"

She stopped. Because she could hear it. Not faintly, not through beasts—with her own new ears, the ones she hadn't known she had until this moment, ears that could hear every living thing in Vaelmoor at once: the gulls over the harbor, the rats in the Underveil cheering in their chittering way, the horses in the witch-finders' stables stamping with something like hope, and beneath all of it, deep in her own chest, a murmur like a prisoner turning over in sleep.

Bramble landed on her shoulder. He looked at her sideways, crow-fashion, and whatever he saw made him go very still.

"Gideon," he said softly. "Your eyes."

"What about them?"

"And the—on your head. There's—"

She reached up. Her fingers found bone. Living bone, warm, growing from her skull in smooth curves that branched at the tips like a young tree, like antlers in shadow-form, delicate as frost on a window.

In the corner of her vision, reflected in the polished floor among seven exhausted faces, Gideon Winterborne saw a figure with eyes like a winter sky and a crown of shadowed horn—and heard, rising through the broken doors below, the roar of a city whose god had suddenly gone silent, and whose silence was louder than any miracle.

Odessa hauled herself upright, looked down at her, and managed a crooked, terrified grin.

"Well," she said. "Vaelmoor's going to have *questions*."
