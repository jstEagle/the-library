## 20. What Wakes in Her Place

The first thing Gideon heard was everything.

Not the ringing silence after a bell, not the soft hush of her own heartbeat—*everything*. The river speaking to its stones in the long slow grammar of water. Forty thousand lungs breathing in Vaelmoor, each one a small tide. A moth somewhere in the rafters above the throne room, beating out its brief frantic prayer against the dark. Rats beneath the palace dreaming of bread crusts and their grandmothers. A gull on the seawall cursing another gull with impressive fluency.

And underneath it all, at the very bottom of the world like a stone at the bottom of a well: the god, caged now inside her own ribs, whispering.

*What have you done,* it said. Not angry. Almost curious. That was somehow worse.

Gideon opened her eyes.

She was lying on the dais steps of the throne room, and the first thing she saw was Odessa Finch's face, one-eyed and grimy and closer than any face had ever been, peering down at her with an expression Gideon had never seen on her before and never expected to: pure, undiluted terror.

"She's breathing," Odessa announced to the room behind her, in the tone of a woman who had not entirely believed it would be true. "Bramble, get off her chest, you great feathered anchor, let her breathe."

"I'm *guarding* her," Bramble said from somewhere near Gideon's collarbone. His voice sounded strange—smaller, rougher, as though he were speaking through a throat full of ash. "There's a difference."

Gideon tried to sit up and discovered that her body had opinions about this. Her bones felt newly made, like someone had taken them apart overnight and reassembled them without the instruction leaflet. She managed half-way up, propped on her elbows, and looked down at herself.

Her hands were still her hands. That was a relief. Callused palms, the old burn scar on her left thumb from the charm-kiln, dirt in every crease because apparently even apotheosis didn't include a bath. But when she moved her fingers, she felt them move twice—once as flesh, once as something else, some vast slow awareness that extended past her fingertips into the floor, into the foundations, into the sleeping earth beneath Vaelmoor that murmured in its sleep like an old dog dreaming.

And there—she raised her arm and felt it before she saw it—a shadow rising from her brow. Not bone. Not quite antler either. Something between: a darkness shaped like a stag's crown, made of the same stuff as the space between tree branches in winter. When she turned her head, it moved with her, casting no shadow on the marble even though there was light enough for one.

"Oh," she said, very quietly. "Oh, that's going to be a problem."

"It's *beautiful*," said Serah Vane, who was sitting against the far wall with her physician's satchel clutched to her chest like a shield, watching Gideon with eyes red-rimmed from crying or laughing or both. "I'm sorry—I know I shouldn't say—it's just, medically speaking, you should be dead. You were dead. Your heart stopped for—" She stopped, swallowed. "For four minutes. I counted."

"Four minutes," Gideon repeated. "That's not so bad."

"Gideon." Serah's voice cracked. "You were *dead*. And then the whole city screamed."

That, Gideon thought, explained the headache. Somewhere beyond the walls, beyond her hearing-that-was-everything, forty thousand people had felt the moment the binding settled, the moment the thirteenth moon crested and did not find what it was looking for. She could feel the echo of it still, rolling away over the hills like thunder learning manners.

"The king," she said. "Where's—"

"Here."

Aldric stood at the edge of the dais, and Gideon's new senses flinched away from him before her mind caught up. For eleven moons he had been, to her gift, a hollow place—a silence shaped like a man, a doorway with nothing behind it. Now—

Now he was *thin*. That was the only word for it. Thin as winter ice, thin as paper held to a candle, but *there*, and trembling, and alive, and looking at her with eyes that were finally, unmistakably, his own. They were ordinary eyes. Brown. Tired. Wet.

"You held him," Gideon said. "At the end. From inside. You bought me the seconds."

Aldric's mouth worked. He was a tall man; he had been a tall man even hollowed, wearing his emptiness like a well-tailored coat. Now the coat hung wrong, because the man inside it had spent eleven years being worn by something else and had forgotten how to stand as himself. He gripped the back of the throne—the empty throne, the god's cage gone, its occupant evicted—and Gideon realized he was holding himself up by it.

"I heard myself," Aldric said. His voice was rust and disuse. "All that time. Locked in a small dark room in my own skull, listening to it wear me like a glove. Praying, if you can believe it. Praying to anyone who might be listening, which is a particular kind of joke when the thing wearing your face is what everyone's praying *to*." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, and then it became something else, something rawer. "And then you came through my walls like spring coming through a frozen window, and you *asked* me. Do you understand? Eleven years, and you're the first person who asked me anything. You said, *hold him, hold him, help me*, and I could have wept for joy at being *commanded* by someone who meant me no harm—"

He did weep then. It was not dignified. Kings are not permitted much dignity at moments like this anyway, and Aldric had already surrendered every other possession he had; he gave up this last one standing at his own throne, gripping it like a drowning man grips flotsam, while a smuggler's crew and a disgraced physician and a scarred crow watched.

Nobody rushed to comfort him. Nobody looked away either, which was perhaps the kinder gift.

*He will abdicate,* whispered the god, from its cage in her ribs. Its voice was different now. Smaller. Muffled, the way a voice is muffled through a wall—or through bark, or through horn. Before, it had spoken with the confidence of a thing that owned the air it moved through. Now it spoke like a prisoner who has learned the thickness of the door. *They always do. They always kneel to me after. Give it a season.*

"We'll see," Gideon said aloud, and then realized everyone was staring at her.

"Talking to it?" Bramble asked, hopping up onto her shoulder properly now. His talons found purchase on something that was not quite flesh, and Gideon felt it as a faint pressure, a bird perching on a branch that happened to be shaped like her soul. He didn't seem bothered. Crows, she suspected, were constitutionally incapable of being bothered by anything except missed meals and insults.

"It talks," she said. "It's going to keep talking. I'm going to keep not listening." She paused. "Mostly."

"That's how it gets you," Bramble said darkly. "Mostly. My speaker used to say mostly. Mostly the fire's out. Mostly I can handle it. Then the cottage burned down and I ate her ashes, which sounds worse than it was, grief does strange things to a bird." He preened once, briskly. "Point is. Don't do mostly."

"Noted."

* *

Serah insisted on examining her, because Serah was Serah, and the fact that the laws of nature had been visibly violated did not exempt Gideon from a basic physical assessment. They did it in a side chamber off the throne room, by lamplight, while the city outside slowly remembered how to make noise.

"Hold still."

"I am holding still."

"Your heart is doing something I don't have a word for." Serah pressed her stethoscope's little trumpet against Gideon's chest, listened, frowned, moved it, frowned harder. "It beats. Then it pauses. Then during the pause something *else* beats—not in your chest, more… under everything. Like an echo arriving before the sound." She sat back. "I'm going to write this down, and in thirty years some poor medical student is going to fail an exam because of it."

"Happy to ruin lives."

"Your temperature is low. Your pupils—" Serah leaned close, lamplight catching them, and went quiet.

"What?"

"They're grey," Serah said softly. "Winter-sky grey. Yesterday they were brown. I remember your eyes, Gideon, they were the first thing I noticed about you, brown as river mud, very unremarkable, I mean that kindly." She reached up, hesitated, touched the air just beside the antler-shadow crowning Gideon's brow. Her fingers passed through it as though it were smoke. "Does it hurt?"

"No. It doesn't feel like anything. It feels like—" Gideon searched. "Like remembering something. Constantly. Like a memory of antlers that I've always had and only now noticed."

"And the hearing?"

Gideon closed her eyes, and the whole of Vaelmoor opened like a book.

The docks, where Odessa's crew—scattered, exhausted, triumphant—were telling the story of the riot at the Weir Gate to anyone who'd buy them a drink, and the story grew a shipwreck and two duels with every retelling. The shrine districts, where people knelt in the ruins of the Waking God's altars and did not know yet whether to pray or apologize. The witch-finders' barracks, silent, their captains' offices dark, the whole apparatus of the Edict suddenly a machine whose purpose had evaporated overnight. A cat on a chimney across the city, washing her paw, thinking in a loose drift of images: *warm stones, the grey-eyed one, the grey-eyed one smells like the forest before men, tell the others.* A child waking from a nightmare and being held. Ten thousand small kindnesses and cruelties, all of it audible, all of it *hers* to hear whether she wanted to or not.

"It's loud," Gideon said, opening her eyes. "It's so loud, Serah. Every living thing in the city, all at once, forever. How do I sleep? How do I ever be alone again?" Her voice climbed despite herself. "How do I know which thoughts are mine?"

Serah took her hand. Simple as that. Warm human fingers around warm human fingers, calluses against calluses, and in the flood of the city's noise it rang clear as a single bell: *one person, present, choosing her.*

"There," Serah said. "Can you hear me?"

"You're not thinking anything. You're just… here."

"I'm not a beast-speaker. There's nothing for you to hear but my hand." She squeezed. "When it's too loud, find hands. Find Bramble. Find Odessa complaining about the state of the palace kitchens, which she has been doing loudly and continuously for the last hour. Start with what you love and work outward. That's how you learn a new sense. Same as learning to walk on a ship's deck—you hold the rail until the sea makes sense."

Gideon breathed. Held the rail. Worked outward.

It helped. Not fixed—she suspected nothing would fix this, that this was simply what she was now—but helped. Enough.

"Bramble," she called. "Fetch the others. And if Odessa tries to bring the palace silverware, stop her."

"I make no promises on her behalf," said Bramble, and flew out the window into the dawn.

* *

They gathered in the throne room as the sun came up, because it seemed right that the thing end where it began. The Saltcellars arrived dusty and bloodied and grinning—Pike with his arm in a sling he absolutely did not need, Marlow carrying an entire wheel of cheese he claimed to have liberated from the palace larder "for morale," young Fen who kept looking at Gideon and looking away and looking back, like the sun, like something you weren't supposed to stare at directly.

Tamsin came too.

She walked slowly, leaning on Pike, and her eyes went to the antler-shadow and the winter-grey eyes and she did not flinch. Burned-out witches, Gideon had learned, were the least afraid of magic of anyone in Vaelmoor. There was nothing left in them for fear to burn.

"So that's what it looks like," Tamsin said. "When it works."

"Tamsin—"

"Don't." But gently. "Don't apologize. I've had three weeks in a cell to do the arithmetic, Gideon. My gift was small. Little warmth-charms, finding lost buttons, making milk keep an extra day. It's gone, and it's not coming back, and I mourned it in that cell like mourning a sister." She looked at her own hands, flexed them. "But I'm alive, and the woman who broke the god is standing in front of me wearing a crown of shadows she didn't ask for, and if I catch you apologizing for that one more time I'm going to hit you with this cheese wheel."

Marlow clutched the cheese protectively.

"All right," Gideon said, and laughed, and the laugh hurt and healed in the same breath. "No apologies. Deal."

Aldric approached them then. Someone—Serah, probably—had gotten him washed and into plain clothes, and without the crown and the court robes he looked startlingly ordinary: a tall, gaunt man in his forties with the posture of someone who has forgotten how his own shoulders work. He stopped an appropriate distance away, as though aware he was something people might not want to touch.

"I owe you my life," he said. "All of you. And I owe Vaelmoor eleven years of a king who wasn't in it." He drew breath. "I am going to abdicate. Today, if the paperwork permits; I'm told there's always paperwork. I'll name a regency council. Serah—" he turned to her, and Serah went rigid, "—you've seen what power does when no one watches it. I'd have you watch it. Counsel the regents. Keep them honest. Refuse, and I'll ask again tomorrow, and the day after; I'm told I can be very stubborn."

"He can," Serah said faintly. "Eleven years of clinical notes. The man fought a god's sedatives like a champion." She pressed her lips together. "Yes. All right. Yes."

"And you." Aldric faced Gideon, and here he faltered, because what do you say, what ceremony exists for *thank you for reaching into the prison of my own head and asking me to help save myself.* In the end he did the only honest thing available to him.

He knelt.

"Don't—" Gideon started.

"Let me." His voice was steady. "Let me do this one thing as a free man. Not to a queen, not to a god—to the woman who pulled a drowning stranger out of deep water." He bowed his head. "Whatever you become, Vaelmoor should know it owes you. And whatever you choose next—" he rose, awkward, joints cracking, almost smiling, "—I hope it involves letting us feed you breakfast. You've been dead four minutes and unconscious six hours. Medically speaking, you're a scandal."

Behind him, the god stirred in its cage and whispered, soft as ash settling:

*A king knelt. It starts the same way every time. Kneeling. Gratitude. Temples. Wait.*

"Watch me," Gideon murmured.

"What?" said Odessa.

"Nothing. Breakfast sounds excellent."

* *

Later—much later, when the cheese wheel had been reduced by two-thirds and Pike had fallen asleep sitting upright and Fen had worked up the courage to ask if the antlers were load-bearing (they were not) and Odessa had formally, gruffly, in front of witnesses, informed Gideon that she was Saltcellar crew now and crew didn't get a vote in the matter—Gideon slipped away to the high balcony above the palace gardens.

Bramble found her there, of course. He always did.

The sun was fully up now. Below, Vaelmoor was arguing. She could hear it in fragments even filtered through the rail she was mentally holding: the Edict's notices being torn down in one district and defended in another; someone proposing, absurdly, that the shrines be reopened as libraries; a hundred conversations about what magic *should* be allowed to be, now that the question was no longer academic. The whole city, awake and unfinished, deciding itself.

"It's very loud," she said to Bramble. "In my head. Everything alive, all at once."

"Mm. And what does it sound like, overall? The whole symphony."

Gideon listened. Really listened, past the arguments and the gulls and the dreaming rats and the river, to the great tangled chord of a living city at dawn.

"Hopeful," she said, surprised. "Mostly hopeful. Some of it's terrified. Some of it's furious. But underneath…" She shook her head. "Underneath it sounds like morning."

"Good." Bramble shuffled along the balustrade to press against her jaw, crow-warm. "Because that thing in your chest is going to spend years telling you it sounds like worship, and you'll want something true to measure it against." A pause. His feathers ruffled. "Also your grandmother says hello."

Gideon went very still. "Bramble."

"In the cache. Her notes. Page forty-one, margin, tiny handwriting: *if you're reading this, Bramble found you, hug the wretched bird, he waited long enough.*" The crow's voice wobbled, just slightly, on a note no ordinary bird could produce. "I may have read ahead. I regret nothing."

Below them the city went on arguing, and healing, and waking. Inside her ribs, the god whispered its patient poison, and Gideon Winterbourne—charm-seller, beast-speaker, neither woman nor beast, something new under the sun—held the rail, and breathed, and answered it with patience instead of fear.

But page forty-one. Her grandmother's handwriting, waiting for her all these years.

"Bramble," she said, standing, "where exactly is this cache?"
