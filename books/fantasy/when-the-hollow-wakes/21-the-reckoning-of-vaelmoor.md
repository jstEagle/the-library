## 21. The Reckoning of Vaelmoor

### I. Gideon

The Edict of Ash did not collapse so much as it quietly fell over, the way an old fence does after a hard winter—no dramatic breaking, just a slow lean and then a morning when everyone agreed it had been lying in the mud for some time.

Gideon watched it happen from the steps of the Hollow Shrine, of all places, three days after the thirteenth moon, while a crowd argued about what to do with the witch-finders' headquarters.

"Burn it," someone shouted.

"Turn it into a bathhouse," shouted someone else, to general applause, because Vaelmoor had strong opinions about public bathing and weak ones about arson now that fire no longer felt like the government's favorite tool.

"The archives are inside," said a thin man with ink-stained fingers. "The records. Every name they ever took. Every confession they extracted. If you burn it, you burn the proof of what was done to us."

That quieted them. Gideon understood why. She had spent three years being careful not to be remembered by anything official, and she knew how much a person could want the record of their suffering kept somewhere safe—even if they never looked at it. Even if looking hurt.

Bramble shifted on her shoulder. "Burn the *chairs*," he suggested, at full crow volume. "Keep the paper. The chairs are where they sat while doing crimes. Everyone knows chairs remember."

"They do not," Gideon murmured.

"You would know if you listened to more furniture."

A woman near the front turned and saw her—the antler-shadow horns, the eyes like a winter sky—and the crowd's murmur changed key. Gideon had been dreading this for three days: the moment the city remembered who had walked into the palace and walked out again with something caged behind her ribs.

Someone knelt.

Then two someones. Then a dozen, spreading through the crowd like frost across a windowpane, and Gideon's stomach dropped with a horror that was entirely different from the kind she'd felt facing the god. This was worse. This was people deciding she was something other than tired.

"No," she said loudly. "No. Stand up. All of you. I have stood in line for bread; I am not going to be bowed to by people who've smelled my cooking."

A ripple of startled laughter. The kneeling faltered.

An old woman stayed down anyway, gripping her cane, glaring up with wet furious eyes. "My son was taken by the witch-finders in the second purge," she said. "I prayed every night for eleven years. You're the answer. Let me kneel."

Gideon crouched down to her level, which put her face-to-face and let her feel, faintly, through the new sense that lived under her skin like a second heartbeat, the ache in the woman's knees and the grief in her chest, both of them old, both of them worn smooth as river stones.

"I'm not the answer," Gideon said gently. "But I'll tell you what I can be. Come to Saltcellar Row on the first of next month. We're opening a hearth. Anyone who lost someone to the purges, anyone whose gift got burned out of them, anyone who never had one but always wondered—that door will be open. Bring your son's name if you have it. We're keeping records too." She glanced at the ink-stained man. "Better ones."

The old woman rose slowly, using Gideon's offered arm. "And if we bring offerings?"

"Bring bread. I'm told the hearth will need feeding."

By evening the crowd had dispersed into arguments about the bathhouse question, which Bramble declared "the true victory of civilization," and Gideon walked home along the canal feeling the whole city humming against her senses—not prayers anymore, not exactly, but attention, curiosity, the raw material out of which gods were made. It frightened her more than hunger ever had. Hunger she understood. Worship she was going to have to learn to survive.

*They made me out of your wanting,* the thing behind her ribs whispered, sly as ever. *You could be made of theirs.*

"Not tonight," Gideon said, and meant it, and the whisper subsided, sulking, into the cage that was also her.

### II. Serah

Serah Vane had served four years as royal physician to a king who was not there, and she found that the work of regent-counselor to a king who was only partly there required, remarkably, the same skills: patience, careful observation, and the ability to conduct an entire conversation with someone who might vanish mid-sentence.

Aldric sat by the window of his own council chamber because he could not bear the throne. He had abdicated in everything but paperwork—he still signed the documents, because the realm needed signatures and there was no one else the factions would accept, but he refused the chair itself. He said the chair remembered. Serah had begun, privately, to wonder if he was right.

"The northern provinces want autonomy," she reported. "The southern temples want the gods formally restored. the guilds want the Edict repealed and also want to keep all the laws it funded. And the Council of Voices"—she paused—"wants to speak with you personally."

"Which voices?" Aldric asked. His voice had come back in pieces, like a house reopening room by room. Some days he sounded nearly like a man.

"The beasts'. Through Gideon Winterbourne. They've petitioned for representation in matters concerning rivers, forests, and—this is verbatim—'the proper management of cats.'"

Something moved across Aldric's face that might once have been a smile. "Cats."

"Cats. Specifically the ones in the Fishmarket district, who have apparently organized."

He was quiet a moment. When he spoke again his voice was smaller. "Serah. Do you remember when I stopped being here? Truly. Was there a day? Did I say anything?"

She had asked herself that question every night since the throne room. She owed him honesty; she had spent too many years giving hollow men comfortable lies.

"There was a winter, six years ago. You complained of headaches. Then you complained of nothing at all, which worried me more, and I said so, and the court reassured me you were 'at peace.'" Her hands tightened on the ledger. "I should have fought harder. I treated the peace instead of questioning it. That failure is mine, and I'll carry it."

"You carried enough," Aldric said. "Carry the city instead. You're better at institutions than I ever was." He looked out at the rooftops. "I used to think ruling was listening. Then something wore my crown and called its hunger listening, and now I don't trust the word. But you—you actually do it. Listen. So listen well. And when the Voices come about the cats, agree to whatever they ask. I have it on excellent authority the Fishmarket cats are not to be crossed."

Serah wrote it down. *His Majesty recommends capitulation re: cats.* It was, she reflected, the most sensible policy advice she'd recorded in four years.

### III. Odessa

Odessa Finch stood on the deck of the *Saltcellar*, one hand on the rail and the empty sleeve of the other pinned up neat, and watched her crew argue about the future the way sailors argue about everything: loudly, in circles, with occasional breaks for drinking.

"We were smugglers," said Pim, who had been with her nine years. "It's a good word. Short. Honest."

"It was a cover," said Marisol. "Half our cargo was medicine and letters. We were ferries that didn't pay taxes."

"Taxes are the taxman's opinion," said Pim.

"And smuggling is a crime with better weather," said Marisol, "which we can now afford legally, thanks to the amnesty, so stop being romantic about it."

Odessa let them go on. She'd learned long ago that a crew arguing freely was a crew that trusted you to let them. Her brother Tam had argued with her exactly that way, the last morning before the purge took him, about whether to run one more shipment or lie low. She had won the argument. She would give anything to lose it.

"Grief makes her stare at the water," Pim observed, not unkindly.

"Grief makes *you* talk," Marisol returned.

"All right." Odessa pushed off the rail. "Here's the shape of it. The canals need moving. People, goods, letters, news—Vaelmoor's roads are mud half the year and the river gates are wrecked from the riots. There's coin in ferrying and more in being trusted, and trust is cheaper than bribes, which is fortunate, because bribes cost an arm." She raised the empty sleeve slightly. "Ask me how I know."

Nobody asked. They never did.

"So. The Saltcellar Company. Licensed, honest, dull as ditchwater." She held up a finger before Pim could protest. "*Mostly* honest. If a package doesn't declare itself and pays extra for discretion, we are a company with a flexible interpretation of manifests. A body needs a little sin left or the respectability goes to the head."

Pim brightened visibly. "So we're ferries with a criminal hobby."

"We're ferries with a *hobby*," Odessa corrected. "The criminal part stays retired. Mostly." She looked down the canal toward the burned-out stretch where the witch-finders' barges used to dock, and her voice went quieter without her permission. "We run one route free. Forever. Any exile, any burned-out speaker, any soul the old regime broke—free passage to the hearth on Saltcellar Row and back. That's not negotiable and it's not charity. That's debt."

The crew was silent. Then Pim said, gruffly, "Whose debt?"

"Mine," Odessa said. "To everyone I didn't save. Now get below and start repainting the name. *Saltcellar Company.* And paint a small crown underneath it."

Marisol frowned. "Why a crown?"

"Upside-down," Odessa said, and walked away before they could see her face.

### IV. Gideon

The hearth opened on the first of the month, in a former tannery on Saltcellar Row with good bones, bad smells that took three scrubbings to leave, and a courtyard big enough for the goats, who arrived uninvited on day one and were granted tenancy by popular acclaim.

Gideon had expected a handful of visitors. She got forty, then eighty, then a queue that Odessa's ferries had to stagger across the week.

There was Tamsin, of course, who came every day though her magic was gone—burned out of her in a witch-finder cell until all that remained was the memory of it, like a limb that ached in cold weather. She couldn't speak to beasts anymore. But she discovered she could *teach*, and it turned out that knowing exactly what had been taken from her made her fierce about protecting it in others. She ran the beginners' lessons with a wooden pointer and a temper, and small children adored her, and Gideon never once told her that watching her teach felt like watching a wound learn to sing.

There was a boy of maybe ten who could calm dogs, and a grandmother who'd hidden her green-thumb gift for sixty years and cried the first time she let a seed sprout in front of witnesses. There were exiles from the border towns, and a defrocked priestess who wanted, she said, to learn what worship felt like when nobody was hungry underneath it.

And there were the burned-out ones, like Tamsin, who came mostly to sit near the fire and be where the language of gifts was spoken aloud without shame. Gideon couldn't restore what the finders took. She had asked the god, once, in a moment of weakness, whether it could.

*I could,* it whispered, *if you loosened the cage.*

"That's a no, then," Gideon had said, and gone to make tea, and hated it, and survived it.

One evening near midwinter, the old woman from the shrine steps came with her son's name written on a slip of paper. Gideon read it and entered it into the hearth's great book, alongside hundreds of others, in a hand steadier than she expected.

"He'd have liked this place," the woman said, looking around at the chaos—a goat eating a lesson plan, Bramble supervising from a beam, children practicing mouse-speech in giggling whispers. "All this noise."

"Noise is the point," Gideon said. "Quiet is where they got us. One person alone, afraid, sure that no one else is like her." She closed the book. "Never again quiet."

### V. Bramble

On the high beam above the hearth's main room, Bramble held court, as was his right, his due, and frankly his nature.

Below him, the small humans practiced. Above him, the rafters hosted sparrows, two pigeons of dubious character, and a bat named Hesper who only attended for the gossip. Word of the hearth had spread through the beast-roads faster than through any human channel, and now animals came from three districts over just to look—to see the place where the speaking was legal, where a crow could testify in a human court about which merchant's grain carts had been dumping slops in the river (a case Bramble had personally brought, and won, and never stopped mentioning).

"You're preening," Hesper accused.

"I am *maintaining*," Bramble said. "There's a difference. Maintenance has dignity."

"You've maintained the same feather for twenty minutes."

"Some feathers require sustained attention."

Below, a child laughed, and Tamsin's voice rose over the din—"*Wren, we do NOT promise the goat things*"—and Bramble felt the old ache shift in his chest, the ache that had lived there thirty years, since the Godfall purges took Mira and left him a familiar with no one to be familiar *to*. For thirty years he had waited by her cold hearthstone, telling himself he was free, telling himself a crow needed no one.

Then a charm-seller with careful eyes had fed him cheese in an alley, and he had followed her home, and it had turned out he had simply been waiting for a Winterbourne. Or perhaps—though he'd have denied this under oath—for permission to belong to somebody again.

Mira's cache was safe in the hearth's back room now, her notes on the binding studied by candlelight, her warning copied onto the wall above the fireplace in letters Gideon had painted herself:

**DO NOT CARRY IT ALONE.**

Bramble looked at the words, then at the crowded room beneath him, then at Gideon, laughing at something Odessa had shouted from the doorway, one-armed, flour-dusted, holding a loaf aloft like a standard.

"Well," Bramble told Hesper, "she listened. Eventually. After significant crow intervention."

"You take credit for everything."

"Yes," said Bramble, "it's called leadership," and flew down to steal bread.

### VI. Gideon

Late that night, after the fires banked low and the last visitors gone home, Gideon climbed to the roof with tea she was technically supposed to share and sat under the stars, Bramble a warm weight on her shoulder.

Below, Vaelmoor glimmered—still arguing, still rebuilding, still lit by windows that no longer shuttered themselves in fear of what knocked. Somewhere a cat sang to the moon. Somewhere the river ran forward again, obedient and cheerful, as if it had never once considered running backward.

"How does it sound tonight?" Bramble asked quietly. He always asked. He was the only one who did.

Gideon listened past the city's noise to the thing behind her ribs, turning over in its cage like a sleeper, muttering its old sweet poison. *They love you. Take it. Become it.*

"Louder than yesterday," she said. "Quieter than last month." She sipped her tea. "It's learning patience. So am I."

"And the horns?"

She touched the antler-shadow at her brow—still more suggestion than substance, visible only when the light hit wrong, or when she forgot to be ashamed of it, which happened more often lately.

"Growing," she admitted. "Slowly. Like everything worth having."

From the streets below rose the sound of the night watch calling the hour, and beneath it, faintly, a new sound the city was still getting used to: the Voices' bell at the river gate, ringing once for every petition the beasts had filed that day. Tonight it rang seven times. The cats of Fishmarket, Gideon sensed through the dark, had submitted a formal complaint regarding the new ferry schedule.

Tomorrow she would sit in a room with Serah and Odessa and a pigeon delegation and try to build a world where power was shared or it was nothing. Tonight she just sat, and the city breathed around her, and the god muttered, and she answered with patience, not fear.

"One year," Bramble said, settling deeper against her neck. "Hard to believe what one year can hold."

"Hold on tight," Gideon said. "I have a feeling the next one's going to be louder."

Far off across the rooftops, where the palace stood dark and kingless and waiting to become something else, a single light burned in a window—and Gideon felt, for just a heartbeat, something stir in the deep places beneath the city that was not the god in her ribs, was not any voice she knew, and was very softly, very patiently, waking up.
