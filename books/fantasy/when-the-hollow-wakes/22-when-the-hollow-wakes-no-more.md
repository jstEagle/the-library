## 22. When the Hollow Wakes No More

The dawn came up over Vaelmoor the color of new copper, and Gideon Winterbourne walked the old walls to meet it, as she had done every morning for a year.

Bramble rode her shoulder with the gravity of a much larger bird, which was his habit and his right. He had groomed each feather to an oily shine for the occasion—*the occasion* being, as far as she could tell, the anniversary of the day he hadn't died, which in his accounting included everyone else in the city as a supporting detail.

*"Sunrise is late,"* he said, in that voice like a drawer of odds and ends rattling open. *"Late sunrise. Suspicious."*

"The sun keeps its own hours, Bramble."

*"Kept you waiting once. I remember. Thirty years, I kept waiting too, and then—"* He did what he always did with the ends of sentences that hurt: he fluffed up until he looked twice his size and said something practical instead. *"—you walk too slow. Walls won't fall if we stroll them."*

"They're not going to fall."

*"Walls say otherwise. Everything says otherwise if you ask it properly."*

She smiled and let him have it. Below, Vaelmoor was waking in its new way—not the furtive, hunched waking of the Edict years, when shutters opened an inch at a time and every second glance was someone checking whether they were seen, but a whole-throated, unguarded racket. The fishwives at the Weir Gate were arguing about prices in voices that carried half a mile. Someone on Chandler's Row was singing, badly and early. A dog was barking at gulls who barked back, and under all of it, faint as silt in river water, she could hear the city's older residents waking too: rats stretching in their warm nests, cats on windowsills opening one eye apiece, pigeons muttering their endless pigeon grievances from the eaves of the Shrine of Ashes, which nobody called the Shrine of Ashes anymore because there was nothing to be ashes of.

One year since the thirteenth moon. One year since she had stood in the throne room with a god's hunger pouring through her ribs and her family's hands reaching through a crow to hold her together.

She still didn't sleep well on the anniversary. But she slept.

* * *

The walls themselves had opinions this morning, which was normal; stone remembered everything. As she passed the North Bastion the old masonry hummed against her awareness like a struck bell dying slowly—the mortar remembering the mason's hands, the stones remembering the siege of two hundred years ago, one particular block above the water gate remembering, with something close to smugness, the exact weight of the witch-finder captain who had been dragged up there by his own men after the god consumed him mid-prayer, back when consumption mid-prayer was still a thing that happened.

It didn't happen anymore. That was the point of everything below her, wasn't it? The whole slow, noisy, imperfect project of the last twelve months, distilled: nothing in Vaelmoor got eaten anymore. Not people, not gifts, not gods' patience.

Mostly.

Because there it was again, right on schedule, arriving with the light: the murmur.

It came up through her feet the way cold comes through boot leather, patient and vast and old. It did not use words, exactly. Words were for creatures with mouths. It used pressure, and longing, and a texture like the moment before a storm breaks—a pressure that a year ago would have sent her heart hammering and her hand reaching for Bramble. Now she stopped walking, leaned her forearms on the parapet, and listened to it the way you listen to a cranky elder relative who has been saying the same thing for a very long time and mostly wants to be heard.

*I am still here,* said the murmur.

"I know," Gideon said aloud. A gull wheeling past the bastion gave her an odd look, then decided this was normal; in Vaelmoor, these days, it was. "Good morning to you too."

*It is dark in here.*

"It is. You made it dark. Well—" She considered being fair about it, because fairness was another thing she'd had a year to practice. "We made it dark together. Your hunger. Our fear."

*They still pray.* There was something almost wounded in it, or the shape of wounded, the way a hollow log holds the shape of fire. *Small prayers. For rain. For mothers. I can hear them even here, in your dark. They taste like—*

"Like the old days," Gideon finished quietly. "I know. And no. You're not getting any of it. Rain goes to the rain, and mothers get comforted by other mothers, and you get me, sitting on your lid, listening."

A long silence, tectonic in scale. Somewhere beneath the palace foundations, in the cage that was no longer Aldric's body but a binding woven of beast-speech and shared weight and one woman's stubborn refusal to do things alone, something shifted and settled.

*You are not afraid of me,* the god observed. It said it the way Serah might note an interesting symptom.

"No," Gideon agreed. Then, because honesty was the load-bearing wall of the whole arrangement: "Not today. Some days I am. Days like yesterday, a little. It comes and goes, like weather. You'll have noticed I don't let the weather run the city either."

*Your grandmother was afraid every day of her life.*

"My grandmother," said Gideon, "was alone. That was her mistake and it nearly became mine and it will not become anyone's again. That's the whole lesson, if you're taking notes down there."

Another silence. Then, so faintly she almost missed it, in a register that was almost—almost—playful:

*You have grown horns, little speaker.*

"I have grown antler-shadows, thank you, and they are none of your business." She touched the place above her left ear where, if the light hit correctly, and if you believed hard, there was a branching darkness that cast no shadow but somehow made one anyway. Children pointed at them. Old women touched them for luck, which she hated and permitted. Odessa claimed they meant Gideon owed her a drink, an argument Odessa advanced roughly weekly.

The god subsided into its long mineral sulking, and Gideon straightened, and Bramble, who had been silent throughout the exchange with the elaborate patience of a creature pretending not to eavesdrop, finally commented:

*"You talk to it nicer than you talk to me."*

"I've known you longer."

*"Thirty years I waited for a Winterbourne and this is the respect I get. Nicer than the god. I want that noted. Witnessed. Notarized—"*

"Noted," Gideon said, scratching the scarred feathers at his neck where he leaned into it, eyes half-closed, ruined dignity entirely forgotten. Above them the sun cleared the eastern hills at last, and the whole wall went gold, and far below in the harbor the *Saltcellar* —rechristened, repurposed, repainted a cheerful and smuggler-scandalizing white—was loading the morning ferry crowd to Saltrow, Odessa's voice carrying across the water as she ordered everyone aboard with the tenderness of a landslide.

* * *

The descent into the city took her past the shrine, and at the shrine, as most mornings, someone was waiting for her.

This morning it was Tamsin, sitting on the low wall with a basket of bread rolls and her face turned up to the sun. Her gift had never come back—the witch-finders had burned it out of her with questions and hot iron, and no rite, no god, no Winterbourne had been able to find where it had gone. She'd spent the first months after the rescue somewhere behind her own eyes. But Tamsin had always been stubborn in the specific way of people who sell bread for a living, and stubbornness had brought her back by degrees: first the bakery, then the jokes, then the mornings on the shrine wall, watching for Gideon.

"You're late," Tamsin said, handing her a roll without looking.

"The walls were talkative."

"The walls are always talkative. You indulge them." Tamsin studied her face with the frankness of long friendship. "Bad night?"

"Some night."

"The whispering kind or the memory kind?"

"Both. They take turns." Gideon broke the roll in half and gave the smaller piece to Bramble, who accepted it as tribute rather than charity. "It asked about my horns again."

"It's obsessed with your horns. It's like a toddler." Tamsin grinned, and for a moment the burn scars on her wrists caught the light, and neither of them flinched, which had taken months to manage and was worth more to Gideon than any rite she'd ever worked. "Odessa wants you at the hearth by noon. Something about the Saltrow children and a drowned gull and a formal hearing."

"A *what*?"

"Tamsin's telling it wrong," said a voice from the alley, and Sera—no, *Serah*, physician, regent-counselor, and the only person in Vaelmoor who could make a formal accusation sound like an apology—stepped out with her medical bag and her records ledger and an expression of profound administrative suffering. "There is no hearing. There is a *gull*. Possibly drowned, possibly faking, currently refusing to speak to anyone but you, and the children have formed a committee."

"Refusing to speak—" Gideon closed her eyes. "It's talking. Isn't it. It's fine, and it's talking, and it wants something."

"It wants," Serah said gravely, "a pension."

Bramble choked on his bread.

* * *

The morning unspooled from there in the way her mornings now did: in pieces, in company, in requests. The gull was indeed fine and indeed wanted a pension, or at least guaranteed herring rights at the harbor mouth, and Gideon negotiated it down to first pickings on Tuesdays while six children watched solemnly from the pier as though witnessing a treaty between nations, which, in a sense, they were.

That was the part no one had warned her about—the sheer *administration* of a world where everything could speak. When her gift had been a crime, it had at least been simple. Now there were complaints to arbitrate (cats versus pigeons, ongoing, generational), contracts to witness (the miller's mule had terms), petitions from beasts whose people had moved away, and a standing monthly assembly at her hearth where speakers and their creatures hashed out the city's oldest grievances with more shouting than wisdom, though somehow, meeting by meeting, more wisdom than shouting.

She had refused the crown. She had refused the sainthood, harder, because the sainthood came with candles and candles came with people kneeling, and she had seen what happened when something in Vaelmoor started collecting devotion. But the hearth—the big ramshackle house on Ember Lane with the blue door and the room out back for the burned-out ones like Tamsin, where a child who heard her dog thinking could come and learn that it was a door and not a curse—that she had said yes to, and never regretted, not even on the nights when the god murmured and the memories came and she sat up alone with antler-shadows flickering above her ears.

Never regretted. Only sometimes frightened. Those were different things; Serah had drawn her a diagram.

By afternoon she was on the wall again—not the old fortification walls but the low warm wall of the hearth garden, where the vegetables grew in defiant abundance because she'd negotiated a truce between the rabbits and the lettuce involving designated sacrifice rows—and it was there, shelling peas with Bramble supervising from a barrel, that the child found her.

She'd seen him around the neighborhood: maybe seven, gap-toothed, son of the cooper on Ember Lane, one of the quiet ones. He'd been standing at the gate for a while. She let him take his time. You learned that, working with the burned-out and the frightened: the gate mattered more than the greeting.

Finally he came and stood before her, hands clasped behind his back in the manner of someone who had rehearsed.

"Mistress Winterbourne."

"Gideon's fine."

"Mistress Gideon." He glanced at her head, quickly, then away, then back, courage gathering. "The other boys say you're a god."

"Ah," said Gideon.

"Tom Whitlow says you ate the Waking God and it lives in your shadow and that's why shadows follow you in the street."

"Tom Whitlow," said Bramble from the barrel, *"is a rumor-monger and a coward and also that's technically true, the shadow part."*

"Bramble."

"What?" said Bramble, with enormous innocence.

The boy's eyes had gone very wide. Gideon set down the peas and considered him seriously, because children asking this question deserved serious answers; she had been a child who overheard the wrong answers about herself, once, in a city that had decided what she was without asking.

"Do I look like a god to you?" she asked.

He studied her. The antler-shadows. The winter-colored eyes that saw too much. The pea shells scattered on the wall, the flour on her apron from Tamsin's morning delivery, the crow with crumbs in his feathers arguing with a sparrow over jurisdiction.

"No," he said at last, honestly.

"Good lad. Because I'm not." She patted the wall beside her, and after a moment he climbed up, careful of the peas. "I'm just someone who came home. That's all it ever was, in the end. Somebody who belonged here, doing the work in front of her, with people beside her." She handed him a pea pod to shell, because hands helped thinking. "Gods don't shell peas."

"What do gods do?"

"Lots of listening," Gideon said, "and not enough asking. Which is backwards, when you think about it." She nodded toward the hearth with its blue door, where light was coming on in the kitchen window and the smell of Tamsin's supper bread was beginning its evening campaign. "Come inside. If you're going to help shell peas, you're going to help eat supper, those are the rules."

The boy hesitated, glancing at the door, at the sounds within—the clatter of the hearth household, Serah debating dosage with Odessa, somebody laughing, a cat complaining about seating arrangements.

"They say the house talks," he whispered.

"The house *mumbles*," said Bramble. "It has opinions about the roof. Everyone here talks, small human. That's rather the point."

And the boy smiled—the wobbly, deciding smile of a child stepping through a gate—and went inside ahead of them.

Gideon stayed on the wall one moment longer. Below, Vaelmoor was lighting its lamps street by street, a constellation assembling itself out of ordinary courage, and beneath her feet, deep and dark and patient, the old hunger stirred and murmured its one endless sentence.

*I am still here,* it said.

"Yes," Gideon answered softly, warmly, the way you answer something that has nowhere else to go and never will. "And I'm still here. And tomorrow we'll do it all again."

From inside, Tamsin called her name. From the harbor, Odessa's ferry bell rang the hour. From the rafters, forty sparrows argued bedtime. From everywhere at once, gently, insistently, life went on speaking to her—and she rose, brushed the pea shells from her apron, and went in to answer it, leaving the door open behind her.
