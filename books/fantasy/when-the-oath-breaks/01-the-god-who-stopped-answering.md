## 1. The God Who Stopped Answering

The rain in Thornfell came down like an apology—too late, too much, and from someone who would not meet your eye. Juniper Okafor stood at the mouth of the pass with his hood back and let it hit him, because the ritual required it, and the ritual was the only thing left that still worked.

*Vess of the Nine Roads, Vess who walks where the map ends, Vess whose hand is the hand I put mine in.*

He spoke the words under his breath, the way his grandmother had taught him, thumb pressed to the oath-mark on his inner wrist—a small scar like a closed eye, inked and branded in the same motion when he was nineteen and stupid with certainty. The old rite demanded three things: water on the face, so the god could see you clearly; the mark bared to the sky; and the words spoken aloud, not whispered, because a god should never have to lean in.

Juniper whispered.

Behind him, ten paces up the trail, the company waited with their mules and their silence. He could feel them watching the way you watch a man at a graveside—not with pity exactly, but with the particular embarrassment of people who have outlived something together and don't know how to talk about it anymore.

He finished the gestures anyway. Knuckles to the earth, forehead to knuckles, rise and turn east, where no one knew what was east anymore because the maps had a hole in them the size of a kingdom. Then he wiped his face, pulled his hood up, and climbed back onto the trail wearing the expression he had spent ten years perfecting: the look of a man who had just been answered.

"Road's open," he said.

Maren fell into step beside him, which was her way of saying she'd watched, and her way of saying she hadn't believed a word of it, both at once. She was good at that. Ten years of being his second had taught her to carry two meanings in every gesture like a knife carried flat against the forearm.

"Heard anything?" she asked.

"The road's clear," Juniper said. "That's what I heard."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," he agreed. "It wasn't."

She didn't push. That was the arrangement they'd made without ever making it: she would not ask him to admit the god was gone, and he would not ask her to pretend the company wasn't dying. It was, he thought grimly as his boots sucked at the mud, a very mercenary kind of marriage. All terms, no tenderness, held together by mutual debts neither party wanted called in.

Thornfell swallowed them the way it always did—the pines closing overhead, black trunks running with water, the light going green and submarine. The smuggler roads here were old before anyone now living was born, cut into the mountainside in switchbacks that the Crown's surveyors had never bothered to chart, because charting them would mean admitting the Crown couldn't close them. Juniper had been running this route since he was fifteen, first with his grandmother's caravan, then with Vess's blessing, and lately with nothing but habit and the memory of both.

Habit was heavier than either.

They made four miles before the first mule went lame, which by Thornfell standards was practically a miracle. Doss hobbled it and cursed it with equal fluency. Pell walked the lead rope and complained about the rain in the tone of a man who had been complaining about weather for so long that the complaint had become a kind of prayer itself, addressed to no one, answered by nothing—which, Pell would have told you if you asked, was also how praying to Vess had gone these last ten years. Nobody laughed. The joke had stopped being funny around year six, and now it just sat there among them like an old dog that kept sleeping in the spot where the fire used to be.

They were eleven, when they had once been sixty. Sixty sworn swords under the blue-and-iron banner of the Okafor Free Company, contracted through Vess himself, oaths signed in the god's own script on vellum that smelled like lightning. Kings had paid their tariffs. Guilds had paid their bribes. And the god—the god had *answered*. Not with thunder. Vess was not that kind of god. Vess answered the way a good quartermaster answers: quietly, precisely, on time. A road that should have been closed would be open. A patrol that should have caught them would turn the wrong way at the ford. A fever moving through camp would break overnight, and in the morning there would be nine crows sitting on the picket line, looking pleased with themselves, and everyone would touch the marks on their wrists and say *thank you, Road-Father* without a trace of irony.

Ten years ago, on the third night of autumn, all of that had stopped. No warning. No sign of anger. Just silence—the particular silence of a door closed gently by someone who does not want to wake the house.

Companies broke over less. Most of them had. But the Okafors had contracts outstanding, and a contract signed under Vess's seal was, by the old law, binding until fulfilled or forgiven—and since forgiveness required the god's voice and the god wasn't speaking, the debt simply hung there, over all of them, like smoke that wouldn't rise or fall.

So they kept walking. Eleven mercenaries, four sound mules, one lame one, a cargo of untaxed ironbark resin worth maybe two hundred crowns, and a god-shaped hole where their purpose used to be.

* *

They made camp in the hollow below the Widow's Tooth, where the rock overhang kept off the worst of the rain and the fire could be built small enough not to show. Juniper took first watch, because he always took first watch, because sleep had become a country he could visit but not live in.

From the hollow he could see the pass they'd come through, and beyond it, far off, the grey shoulder of the mountains where the maps gave up. His grandmother had called that region *the Hem*—the stitched-off edge of the world. She'd grown up in the last generation that remembered Old Thornic spoken openly, in a village that no longer appeared on any chart, and she had taught him fragments of it the way you teach a child a lullaby: not for meaning, but for keeping. *This word means bread. This word means home. This word means the god's name, and you will never need it, little juniper-berry, because the god is everywhere the road goes.*

Then the kingdom vanished. Not conquered—erased. One spring the maps were redrawn, the names scraped off, the villages reassigned or unassigned, and the Crown's historians produced, with suspicious speed, a learned consensus that there had never been any such place. Grandmother had burned her papers and kept only a box, and had never once, in all Juniper's childhood, looked north without flinching.

He was turning his wrist toward the firelight, studying the closed-eye scar, when Maren came and sat beside him without asking. She brought two cups and poured from the flask he pretended not to know she'd taken from his pack.

"To the Road-Father," she said, "wherever he's walking."

"You don't believe he's walking anywhere."

"I believe he owes us an explanation." She drank. "I believe we're owed wages, Juniper. That's what this is. We're owed ten years of wages by a god, and instead of collecting, we stand out in the rain doing his hand-signs."

"It's not hand-signs. It's the rite."

"It's the rite the way a widow setting two plates is a marriage."

Juniper looked at her. Firelight moved on her face, and beneath the weathering he could see the girl she'd been at twenty-two, signing on in a river town because Vess's companies were the only ones that never sold their wounded. She'd been the fourth-youngest of sixty. Now she was second-oldest of eleven, and the arithmetic of that loss lived behind her eyes like a tenant who never slept.

"Say what you came to say," he said.

"I came to say nothing. I came to sit." She turned the cup in her hands. "But since you ask. Pell wants out. You know that. He's got a cousin in Greywater running barges, honest work, no oaths. And Doss has been talking to the Corvane brothers, and the Corvanes are talking to whoever pays. We're down to eleven, and half of us are already halfway down the road."

"And the other half?"

"The other half are waiting for you to tell them what we are." She said it evenly, without accusation, which was somehow worse. "Because here's the thing, Juniper. I've watched you do the morning rite for ten years. I've watched you bare the mark and speak the words and turn east. And every single time, you come back wearing that face. The answered face." Her voice stayed low, so the others wouldn't hear. "And every single time, I think: either he hears something I can't, or he's the finest liar I ever soldiered with. And I've decided I don't want to know which. Because if it's the first, then the god speaks to you and not to me, and I'll resent you until I die. And if it's the second—if you're standing out there every morning lying to your god and your company at the same time—then I'm following a man who lies better than he believes. So I don't ask. Do you understand? I am *choosing*, daily, not to ask."

The rain ticked against the stone above them. Somewhere down the valley, water found its way over rocks with the patience of something that had all the time in the world.

"What do you want me to say, Mare?"

"I want you to say we're done. I want you to stand up tomorrow and tell them the contract's dead, the god's deaf or gone or never was, and we're free men selling our swords to whoever pays. Let them grieve it properly and go. Pell goes to his barges. Doss drinks himself rich. I—" She stopped. Started again. "I'd follow you anyway. You know that. That's my whole problem. But some of them need permission to stop hoping, and you're the one holding the permission."

"And if I give it?"

"Then we're a real company again. Small. Honest. Alive."

"And if I don't?"

She looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her sigh and finish her cup.

"Then we're what we are now," she said. "Eleven ghosts keeping the books of a firm whose owner skipped town." She rose, squeezed his shoulder once—harder than affection, gentler than rebuke—and crawled back under the overhang to sleep.

Juniper stayed up. He took out the mark and looked at it again. The closed eye. Sometimes, in the worst years, he had dreamed it opened.

* *

It was near dawn, in that grey hour when the rain finally thinned to mist, that Juniper performed the rite properly for the first time in months. He didn't decide to. His body decided for him—knees in the wet moss, hood back, the cold clean water finding the channels of his face. Maybe it was Maren's words working in him like a splinter working toward skin. Maybe it was the hour, the thin place between dark and light, when his grandmother had always said the Road-Father did his accounting.

He spoke the words aloud. Full voice. The whole litany, the one for the start of a road, the one that ended *and walk with me, Vess, as I walk for you.*

Nothing answered.

Of course nothing answered. Nothing had answered for ten years. He knew the shape of that silence the way a man knows the shape of his own missing tooth—by tongue, constantly, helplessly, mapping the absence. It was not mystery. Mystery would have been easier; mystery at least implied a question worth asking. This was wound. This was the specific grief of speaking into a room you once shared, knowing the furniture is gone, knowing the hearth is cold, saying *good morning* anyway because the alternative is admitting you live alone.

He finished the gestures. Rose. Turned east.

And stopped.

Because east—down the long throat of the valley, past the switchbacks, past the Hem where the maps surrendered—there was a light. Faint, blue-grey, wrong for dawn, wrong for torchlight, wrong for anything. It pulsed once, twice, like something breathing under cloth.

Then it went out, and the ordinary grey morning came pouring in behind it as if embarrassed.

Juniper stood with his arm still raised from the final gesture, heart hammering against the closed-eye scar, feeling—for the first time in ten years—like a man who had spoken into an empty room and heard the floorboards shift.

Behind him, from the camp, came a sound that did not fit the morning at all.

A child's voice. High, clear, unafraid, speaking words in a tongue that had been erased from every map in the world—and Juniper, without understanding how, understood every one of them:

*I knocked. Will someone open the door?*
