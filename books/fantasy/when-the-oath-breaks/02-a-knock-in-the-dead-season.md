## 2. A Knock in the Dead Season

The waystation had no name that Juniper knew, only a reputation: the last roof before the high pass closed for winter, and a poor one at that. Its stones sweated cold. Its hearth smoked sideways when the wind came off the shoulder of the mountain, which was always. The company called it the Coffin, not because anyone had died there—though the ledgers scratched into its doorframe suggested otherwise—but because staying felt like something you'd regret in the morning.

Juniper stood watch because he couldn't sleep and wouldn't pretend to try.

Outside, the dead season had settled over Thornfell like a lid. Snow fell without wind, straight down, patient, as if the sky were emptying itself of everything it owed. The pines wore it on their shoulders. The road below the station had already gone white and featureless, and by morning it would be as if no one had ever walked it—as if the whole country were being slowly erased from the top down, one snowfall at a time.

He thought about that too often lately. Erasure. His grandmother used to say that mountains kept grudges and rivers kept accounts, but countries kept nothing at all; they forgot faster than men could carve their names into them.

"Third watch," he told himself, though no one had assigned him one. There were eleven of them now. Eleven, where once there had been forty and then thirty and then twenty-two. Eleven didn't need watches. Eleven needed a miracle with a ledger attached.

The hearth behind him ticked and spat. He could hear Maren's breathing through the thin wall, even and deliberate, the breathing of someone who slept the way she did everything else—on schedule, without complaint, ready to stop at the first wrong sound. He envied her that. He envied everyone who still believed the night held nothing worse than cold.

His hands ached. They always ached now, in the old places where the oath-marks sat beneath the skin like buried coins: the heel of each palm, the hollow of the throat. Ten years since Vess had stopped answering, and the marks hadn't faded. That was the cruelty no one talked about. A god who dies releases his people. A god who *stops* holds them forever, the way a door held shut from the far side still holds the room dark.

Juniper pressed his thumb against the mark on his palm—an old habit, half prayer, half checking whether the coin was still there—and looked out at the falling snow.

That was when he heard the knock.

Three strikes, spaced evenly, delivered low. Not on the door. On the *doorframe*, near the bottom, at the height of a knee or a small hip. He knew the sound of knuckles on wood; he'd heard every variety of it across twenty years of mercenary work—the desperate knock, the lying knock, the knock that meant soldiers. This was none of those. This was polite. Almost apologetic. As if whoever knocked understood they were interrupting something important and wished to be brief about it.

There should have been nothing out there but snow, pine, and the long drop into the valley. Nothing for eight miles in any direction, unless you counted wolves, and wolves did not knock.

Juniper took the knife from his belt before he touched the bar. Old lessons die hard; some don't die at all. He lifted the bar, cracked the door open two inches, and let the night look at him.

A child stood on the threshold.

She was perhaps seven years old, maybe eight, small enough that the drift beside the step came up past her waist. She was caked head to foot in mud—not the brown mud of riverbanks or roads but a strange grey clay, pale as ash mixed with water, and it had dried in ridges along her arms and cheeks so that she seemed built out of the mountain itself, layered like sediment, like something excavated rather than born. Her hair was dark and heavy with it. Her eyes were very wide and very dark and entirely calm, and that calm frightened Juniper more than tears would have. Children who had crossed eight miles of high country in the dead season should not be calm. Children who had survived it should not exist.

Her feet were bare. There was no blood on them. No frost either, though the snow lay thick enough around her to swallow a grown man to the thigh.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Snowflakes drifted between them through the gap in the door and melted on the warm stone of the threshold, and Juniper became absurdly aware that he was standing in his shirt with a knife held low against his leg, looking down at a child made of grey clay, and that somewhere in the last few seconds the world had quietly changed shape.

"You're letting the cold in," the child said.

Not in trade-tongue. Not in any tongue Juniper had ever heard spoken aloud. And yet—

The words went through him like a hook through soft meat. He knew the sound of them. Not the meaning—he couldn't have said what she'd said, not exactly—but the *sound*. The vowels that turned downward like roots. The consonants that caught at the back of the throat like thorns catching wool. His grandmother's voice rose up in him from wherever dead voices go, thirty years deep, saying the same words over a cookfire in a language she would only use when she thought he was asleep, or when she was angry, or once, memorably, when a tinker tried to cheat them and she cursed him with such fluency that the man dropped his scales and left without his money.

*Old Thornic,* she had called it. The old speech. The crooked tongue. And once, when Juniper was small and had asked why she never taught it to him, she had gripped his wrist hard enough to leave marks and said:

*"Because it's cursed, boy. Because the people who spoke it aren't on any map anymore, and the maps are right to leave them off. Some names get taken down for a reason."*

The child on the threshold said it again, patiently, the way you speak to someone slow. *You're letting the cold in.*

"I don't understand you," Juniper said, in trade-tongue, and hated how his voice shook.

The child considered this. Then she reached up—mud-cracked fingers, nails rimmed with pale clay—and touched her own chest.

"Sable," she said. That word, at least, needed no translation. A name offered across the gap like a plank laid over a chasm.

Then she pointed past him, into the station, at the hearth-light, and said something longer. He caught nothing in it except—there, at the end—one word that landed in him like a stone dropped down a well. He knew it. He knew it the way you know your own name shouted across a field: instantly, bodily, before thought has any say in the matter.

*Oath.*

The word hung between them. Juniper's mouth had gone dry.

"What did you say?" he asked. "Say it again."

She said it again. The same sentence, unhurried, and again that word at the end of it, round and final as a door closing: *oath, oath.* She pointed at him—at his hands, specifically—at the heels of his palms where the marks lay sleeping under the skin—and repeated herself a third time, and now her tone had changed. It wasn't a request anymore. It sounded almost like an accounting. Like something read aloud from a ledger.

*What the oath owes. What is owed to the oath.* He couldn't parse it, but he could feel its grammar pressing against him, patient and immense, the way you feel the weight of a mountain through the soles of your boots.

Behind him, a floorboard groaned. Maren's voice, sleep-rough and blade-flat: "Juniper. Who's at the door?"

"A child," he said. It sounded insane even as he said it. "Alone. On foot."

Silence from the other room. Then the sound of Maren dressing quickly, which was its own kind of alarm.

Juniper made the decision the way you make all decisions in the mountains: fast, imperfectly, with the weather deciding most of it for you. He stepped back and opened the door wide.

The child walked in as if she owned the debt on the place. She left no wet prints on the stone. Juniper noticed that and noticed himself noticing it, and filed both away in the part of his mind where he kept things he wasn't ready to think about.

* * *

By the time the others stumbled into the main room—Doss first, knife already drawn, because Doss slept armed and woke angry; then the twins, Halla and Brek, blinking and matching each other's confusion like always—Sable was sitting cross-legged on the hearthstone, mud drying and flaking off her in little grey scales, warming her hands at a fire she had not yet looked at directly.

"She just walked in?" Doss said.

"She knocked," Juniper said.

"On the frame?"

"At the bottom. Low down."

Doss looked at the door, then at the child, then at Juniper, and Juniper watched him decide not to ask the obvious question, which was *how does anything that small get here*, because the answer was going to be worse than the question. That was Doss's particular talent: knowing which doors to leave shut. It was also why Juniper trusted him.

Maren crouched at the edge of the hearthlight with a bowl of barley broth that had been meant for tomorrow's breakfast. She set it down in front of Sable the way you'd feed a stray dog you suspected of being something else—carefully, at arm's length, watching the teeth.

The child ate. She ate neatly and completely and with a strangeness that Juniper couldn't at first name, until he realized what it was: she ate like someone performing eating. Like someone remembering how it was done. Every spoonful lifted, paused, tasted. Not disgust. *Re-familiarization.* As if food itself were a language she was relearning.

Nobody spoke while she ate. The fire popped. Wind tested the shutter hinges. Outside, the snow went on falling with that terrible patience.

When the bowl was empty, Sable set it down, wiped her mouth with the back of her hand—a normal gesture, heartbreakingly ordinary—and looked up at Juniper. She had chosen him, he realized. Out of the eleven of them, she had knocked where he stood watch, and she had eaten under Maren's guard, but her eyes came back to him again and again like a compass needle finding its north. She had known whose door to knock on. Eight miles of dead-season wilderness, barefoot, and she had known.

She spoke again. The crooked vowels, the thorn-caught consonants. And this time she didn't point at his hands.

She pointed at the floor.

At first Juniper saw nothing. Rough planks of mountain pine, grey with age, scarred by boot-heels and spilled pitch and forty years of camp-knives whittling boredom into splinters. Then Sable crawled forward on her knees—Maren tensing, Doss's knife whispering an inch out of its sheath—and dipped one finger into the cooling dregs of broth, and began to draw.

She drew a rectangle. Tall, narrow, with a lintel line across the top and a threshold line at the bottom. Then, within the rectangle, she drew lines radiating outward from a point at its center—like rays, like the spokes of a wheel seen edge-on, like light forced through a crack.

It was a door. Anyone could see it was a door. Children drew doors; children drew houses and suns and stick-figure families holding hands. But there was something wrong with this door, something Juniper's eye snagged on and couldn't free. The proportions were exact. Not child-exact—*draughtsman*-exact, the lintel precisely twice the width of the jamb, the radiating lines evenly spaced as if measured with a cord. And the whole figure sat on the floorboards with a terrible rightness, positioned not where a child happened to kneel but where the boards themselves seemed to want it, aligned with the grain, with the seams, with the station's bones.

And the floorboards beneath the drawing were warm.

Juniper knew it before he touched them. Knew it the way he'd known the word *oath*—in the body, before the mind. He crouched, ignoring Maren's warning murmur, and laid his palm flat on the planks inside the drawn rectangle.

Warm. Not fire-warm, not body-warm. Warm the way a wall is warm behind which something enormous is sleeping. The warmth had depth. It came up from underneath the station, from underneath the mountain, from somewhere the cold of the dead season had never reached and never would.

Beneath his palm, faint as a pulse through a wrist, the floorboards thrummed.

He snatched his hand back. The motion dislodged the mark on his palm—or woke it—and pain lanced up his arm, bright and specific, the pain of an old wound remembered all at once. The oath-mark flared hot under his skin, and across the room Sable's eyes went to his hand as if she'd heard it. She nodded, slowly, gravely, the way a priest nods. Then she said the word again, pointing now at the drawing, at the warm floor, at him, connecting all three with her finger like threads drawn tight:

*Oath. Door. You.*

He didn't know the second word. He knew it was the second word anyway. Something in the structure of her sentence told him, some grammar older than any grammar he'd been taught, and the knowing terrified him more than the knocking had. His grandmother's voice was louder now, thirty years dead and still lecturing: *some names get taken down for a reason, boy.* And underneath it another voice, older than hers, saying the thing the company never said aloud, the thing that lived under ten years of silence like the warmth lived under these boards:

*Vess did not answer. Vess did not answer. But did Vess ever actually leave?*

"Juniper." Maren's hand on his shoulder. "Your nose is bleeding."

He touched his upper lip and his fingers came away red. He hadn't felt it start. Around the hearth, the others had gone very still. Even the twins had stopped matching each other's expressions and arrived, independently, at the same one: fear.

Sable watched the blood fall from his chin onto the warm floorboards, onto the corner of her drawn door, and she did not look alarmed. She looked satisfied, in the solemn, terrible way of a child watching a seed she planted finally break soil. The blood sank into the grain of the wood and vanished, and where it vanished, Juniper would have sworn—would swear later, when he finally allowed himself to remember—that the drawn lines darkened. Settled. Became less like broth and more like something that had always been carved there and merely needed filling.

The child folded her small clay-cracked hands in her lap and spoke one more time. Slowly. Clearly. A teacher to a student who was finally, finally beginning to listen.

He understood nothing. He understood everything. Somewhere in the middle of the sentence, wrapped in those crooked, cursed, mapless syllables, sat that single word again, placed at the end like a signature on a contract:

*Oath.*

Outside, the snow stopped falling all at once, mid-flake, the way a singer stops mid-note—which no natural snowfall has ever done—and in the ringing silence that followed, every member of the company heard it, distinctly, from everywhere and nowhere, from under the floor and behind the walls and inside their own marked skin:

a sound like an enormous breath, held for ten years, finally beginning to release.
