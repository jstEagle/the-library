## 3. What the Company Owes

Maren Voss counted heads the way other people counted coins: out of habit, out of hunger, and with the growing certainty that there were never enough.

Fourteen, at the last tally. Fourteen of what had once been sixty shields sworn to Vess of the Long Watch. She stood in the doorway of the waystation's common room with a chipped clay mug in one hand and did the arithmetic she did every morning, the arithmetic that was slowly eating her alive. Two had wintered in Redford and never come back. One had taken a farm wife in the lowlands—taken her honestly, taken vows, taken a name that wasn't the company's. Three were dead under cairns along the Thornfell road, and none of them had gotten the burial rites because nobody alive remembered all the verses. The god who was supposed to receive them had stopped listening ten years ago, and the verses had gone out of fashion the way everything went out of fashion eventually.

Fourteen. And now fifteen, if you counted the child.

Maren looked at the child.

Sable sat on the bench nearest the hearth, wrapped in Juniper's spare cloak, which swallowed her whole. She had been scrubbed—Dessa had seen to that, with the fierce efficiency of a woman who could not abide mud on anything, including miracles—and beneath the grime she was just a kid. Thin wrists. A scab on her chin. Eyes the color of river ice that watched everything and gave back nothing, except when they watched Juniper, and then something moved behind them, quick as fish under frost.

She was speaking to Dessa now, or trying to. Her mouth made sounds like wind through briars, like nothing human, like the language Maren's own grandmother had spat salt against. Old Thornic. The tongue of the erased kingdom. The child drew in the ash at the hearth's edge while she talked—a circle, a line through it, small patient strokes—and Dessa nodded along as though understanding, which she didn't, which none of them did, which was precisely the problem.

*What do we owe her?*

That was the question, and it had teeth.

Maren drank her tea. It was terrible, bark-scrapings and snowmelt, but it was hot, and heat was one of the three pleasures left to her. The other two were a clean blade and the sound of Juniper laughing, and the second one had grown rare enough to be precious.

The common room smelled of wet wool and old smoke and the tallow stink of cheap candles. The waystation had been a toll house once, back when there was traffic worth taxing; now it was a shell of stone rooms stacked into the mountain's shoulder, half-swallowed by pine. They'd been using it as a bolt-hole for six years. It had good sightlines down both approaches and a cellar deep enough to hide cargo that preferred not to be declared. It also had a floorboard in the corner of this room where a child had drawn a door two nights ago, and Maren had caught herself looking at it eleven times since dawn. Twelve. The lines hadn't faded. That was the thing about the door. Ash should have smeared. Ash always smeared. This hadn't. It sat in the wood grain like it had been burned there, thin and black and exact, and every time Maren's eyes passed over it her skin crawled in a way she refused to name in front of the others, because a second-in-command who named her fears gave everyone else permission to have them.

The company gathered by degrees, the way water gathers in the low places of a floor. Brannic first, because Brannic was always first, big and gray-bearded and smelling of the pipe he wasn't allowed indoors. Then the twins, Osk and Ilse, moving together the way they always did, finishing each other's silences. Then Halvard with his bad knee and his ledger-brain, and Petra with her quiet hands, and the rest filtering in until fourteen faces turned toward the hearth and the child and the question hanging over all of it like smoke.

Juniper came in last, as he always did. Not because he liked making an entrance—Maren knew better than anyone alive that he hated entrances—but because he liked to see how they arranged themselves before he joined them. A commander's habit. He leaned against the doorframe opposite hers, arms crossed, and for a moment their eyes met across the room, and what passed between them was the old fluent silence: *I don't want to do this either. Do it anyway.*

So she did.

"Company council," Maren said, and her voice carried the way voices carry in cold rooms, flat and clear. "You know what about."

"The girl," said Brannic.

"The girl," Maren agreed. "We need to decide what she is. Cargo, guest, or problem. Because right now we're treating her like all three, and that's how companies die. Slowly. By indecision."

"She speaks the dead tongue," said Ilse. "That's not a problem, that's a *price tag*. There are collectors in Corvale who'd pay a year's wages for a living speaker of pre-kingdom speech. Scholars. Worse than scholars." Her twin nodded beside her, identical and grim.

"And there's the Crown," said Halvard. He said it quietly, the way you say the names of dangerous animals. "Inquisitors have been sniffing up the mountain passes since midsummer. Grey's warrant-men, someone said. If the Crown learns we're sitting on a child who speaks the language they spent four hundred years burning out of the world—" He spread his hands. "They won't send a bill. They'll send fire."

"Then we sell her fast and far," said Osk.

"We kill her and bury her deep," said Petra, and the room went still, because Petra almost never spoke, and when she spoke it was the truth, unvarnished, the way weather is true. "I've thought about it three days. I hate it. But hear me. Every hour she's ours, she's a debt. You want to know what the company owes? Look at the ledger. We owe the brewer in Redford forty marks. We owe the ferryman. We owe back-winter rations to nine people in this room. We are fourteen soldiers held together by habit and a smuggling route that dries up a little more every year. We cannot afford a war. We cannot afford an Inquisitor's attention. And most of all—" Petra's voice dropped, and it was gentle, which made it worse, "—we cannot afford *hope*. Hope is the most expensive thing there is. I've watched it bankrupt better companies than ours."

Maren let the silence sit. She'd learned that from Juniper too: let them hear themselves. Then she set down her mug and walked to the hearth and crouched, so that she was level with the child.

"Sable," she said. The girl's eyes came up. Ice-color, ancient, watching. Maren pointed at herself. "Maren." Pointed at the girl. "Sable." Then she pointed, slowly, around the room—at Brannic, at the twins, at each face in turn—and then back to Sable, and drew a line in the air connecting them all.

*Ours. You're ours. Or you could be.*

It was clumsy. It was the kind of gesture that would have embarrassed her in any other room. But the girl tilted her head, and something flickered across her face—not trust, nothing so generous as trust, but maybe the recognition of a shape. Family-shaped. She'd seen it somewhere before, even if it had been two hundred years ago or in a country that no longer existed.

Maren straightened and turned to the company.

"Petra's right about one thing," she said. "Hope is expensive. So let me tell you what hopelessness has cost us instead, because somebody in this room ought to keep honest books." She began to walk, slow, past the benches, the way Juniper did when he meant business, and she hated herself a little for borrowing his walk. "Ten years ago we were sixty strong. We were *Vessmen*. People left offerings on our boundary stones. Farmers paid us in bread and cider just to have us pass their fields, because a sworn shield's shadow was worth more than a fence. And then the god went quiet, and here's what happened next, in case anyone's forgotten the order of events."

She held up fingers.

"First year: we told ourselves he was testing us. We doubled the rituals. We prayed until our knees bled on the flagstones. Cost: nothing but dignity. Second year: the contracts dried up. Nobody hires a godless shield company when there are godly ones to be had for the same price. Cost: twenty-two of us. Third year through tenth: we became smugglers. Good ones—I'll say that, we're the best on this side of the mountains—but ask yourselves why. Why does a sworn company of Vess end up running untaxed brandy through holes in the world?" She stopped walking. "Because we stopped believing we were owed anything. And once you stop believing you're owed anything, you start taking whatever's offered. That's the rot. Not the god's silence. The rot is what grew in the silence, and every one of us has it in us, me included. I count heads every morning like a miser, and I haven't prayed in four years, and I told myself it was practicality." She looked at her own hands. "It was grief. Grief that got tired of being grief and put on work clothes."

Nobody spoke. The fire ticked. Somewhere above, wind worried the eaves.

"So," Maren said. "Here's my position. We keep her."

"Maren—" Halvard started.

"Let me finish, old man, you'll get your turn." She turned to face them all. "We keep her. Not because she's a price tag, though she is. Not because she might be useful, though gods know she might be. We keep her because of what we were. Listen to me. Listen." Her voice cracked, just slightly, and she let it. "There used to be a rule older than any contract. Older than pay. *No one under our oath goes hungry.* No orphan left at a gate we passed. No widow whose man died under our shields. Half this company is here because of that rule—Brannic, you were an orphan at a gate. Dessa, your mother was a camp follower we took in. Juniper himself was a foundling off a wreck on the coast road. We were the place lost things ended up, and that was the *whole point*, that was what the god actually wanted from us, and I think—I think some of us have forgotten that we ever stood for anything except staying alive."

"That's easy to say warm by a fire," Osk said, but without much force.

"It's easy because it's true," Maren said. "Hard is selling a child to collectors who'll take her apart word by word to learn what she knows. Hard is putting a knife in her while she sleeps after she fell asleep trusting us. You want to talk about what we can afford? We can't afford those. Those cost more than money. Those are the kind of debts that follow a company into the ground and wait for it there."

"And the Crown?" Halvard asked. "Grey?"

"Grey isn't here yet. Rumors aren't warrants. And if the day comes when Inquisitors ride up that pass—" Maren shrugged, and it was a soldier's shrug, light and hard at once. "Then we fight, or we run, or we hide her, same as we've hidden cargo for years. But we decide that on the day. Today the only decision is whether we're the kind of people who sell children, and I'm telling you plainly: I will not be that. I'd rather the company die honest than live rich and rotten. We've been dying for ten years either way. At least this way we die as *Vessmen*."

"Vess doesn't answer," Brannic said softly. It wasn't an argument. It was a wound, shown.

"No," Maren agreed. "He doesn't. And I don't know why. Maybe he's dead. Maybe he never was. But the oath was never really about him answering, was it? It was about *us* answering. To each other. Everything else was decoration." She looked around the room, face by face, and finished quietly: "The company owes her shelter. That's the whole of the law. It always was."

Silence again. Longer this time. Maren felt the room tipping, felt the argument balanced on its edge, and understood with sudden clarity that whatever way it tipped would be the company's soul decided for another year, maybe forever.

It was Sable who tipped it.

The girl had been watching them all this while, silent, the way she was always silent, and now she slid off the bench, crossed the floor on bare feet, and stopped in front of Petra—the one who'd voted to kill her. She looked up at the scarred, weathered woman who could break a man's arm without raising her voice. Then Sable reached into the folds of Juniper's cloak and produced something: a crust of bread, saved from breakfast, hoarded the way hungry children hoard. She held it out to Petra with both hands, solemn as a priest making offering.

*Take it. Even from me. Even knowing what I am to you.*

Petra stared down at the bread for a long moment. Her jaw worked. Then she knelt—one knee, the old formal kneel, the kneel of sworn shields—and took the crust as gently as if it were an egg, and said something in a rough murmur that Maren couldn't catch, and Sable patted her head twice, the way you'd pat a big dog, and walked back to the hearth as if nothing in the world had happened.

Petra stayed kneeling a moment longer. When she rose, her eyes were wet and furious about being wet, and she said, to no one and everyone: "Keep her, then. Damn it. Keep her."

One by one the others came around—it was never unanimous, Maren knew, it would never be unanimous again, unanimity was another thing the silence had eaten—but it was enough. Halvard sighed and opened his ledger to a fresh page, muttering about ration shares. The twins exchanged a look that meant they'd already priced the girl's value both ways and filed the numbers away. Brannic knocked out his pipe against the wall, smiling into his beard.

And Juniper pushed off from the doorframe, and crossed the room, and crouched by the hearth next to Sable, and said something to her—soft, in that low voice he kept for wounds—and the girl answered in her wind-through-briars language, and for a moment the two of them bent together over the ash-drawn door like conspirators, like family.

Maren picked up her terrible tea and drank it and let herself feel, for exactly three breaths, something dangerously close to peace.

Then the knock came at the outer gate. Three blows, spaced evenly, unhurried—the knock of someone who had never once needed to hurry.

Every hand in the room went to steel. Juniper rose. Through the arrow-slit window Maren saw the road below, and the riders coming up it through the pines, and at their head a figure in travel-gray with a warrant-case slung across the saddle like a coffin.

"Everyone calm," Juniper said quietly. "Maren—get the girl below."

But Sable had already gone rigid, staring at the window, staring at the riders, and the sound she made was small and terrible, and Maren recognized it in her bones, because she'd heard it once before—from a dying man who'd seen the thing that killed him.

"*Many hands,*" the child whispered, in Juniper's grandmother's cursed tongue. "*Many hands are coming.*"
