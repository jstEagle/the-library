## 4. Old Thornic

The door on the floorboards had not faded by morning.

Juniper had expected it to. Doors drawn in mud, in ash, in charcoal—doors were things children made and morning erased. But this one remained, dark lines sunk into the grain of the waystation planks as though the wood itself had grown them, and when he crouched over it with the first grey light coming through the shutters, he saw that the lines were not pigment at all. They went *down*. The floor was scored to the depth of a fingernail, smooth-edged, as if someone had traced the shape with a hot wire two hundred years ago and left it cooling ever since.

He didn't touch it. He'd learned that much from his grandmother: never touch a threshold until you know which side of it you're standing on.

Behind him, Sable slept curled against the cold hearth like a comma of mud and bone, breathing in the small, stubborn rhythm of the very young. She had not stirred all night. Whatever she had run from, it had not followed her here—or if it had, it was patient enough to wait outside the reach of lamplight, and Juniper decided, deliberately, not to think about that.

Instead he climbed to the loft, dragged down his pack, and dug past the smuggling goods, past the spare knife and the waxed canvas and the letters of passage he kept forged for three different names, until his fingers found the oilcloth bundle at the bottom that he had carried for twenty-two years without once opening it sober.

* * *

His grandmother's name had been Ilsa Okafor, and she had been, depending on who asked, either a widow, a herbalist, or a witch. She had died when Juniper was nine, and what she had left him was a voice—the sound of Old Thornic spoken low over a cookfire, words that curved like river water—and a box he was forbidden to open until he "heard the language again from a mouth that wasn't mine."

For thirty years he had assumed that mouth would never come. Old Thornic was a dead tongue attached to a dead country attached to a dead god. Scholars called it proto-Thornfellish at best, fabrication at worst; priests of the Concordat called it cursed, and said so loudly enough that Juniper had spent his childhood learning not to flinch when other children's parents crossed the street.

Now there was a child downstairs speaking it in her sleep.

He unwrapped the bundle on the loft boards. Inside: a prayer book bound in hide gone stiff as bark, its spine stitched with sinew that had dried to wire. A handful of river stones, each painted with a single glyph. And a strip of leather, soft with age, inked along its length in a hand Juniper recognized as his grandmother's—though the writing itself was not any alphabet he could read.

He opened the book.

The pages smelled of smoke and tallow and something older underneath, something mineral, like the air in deep caves. The script ran right to left, dense, angular, every letter built of strokes that looked less written than *carved*, and beneath each line, in a different, shakier hand, his grandmother had pencilled translations in Common. Not all of them. Some lines she had left blank. Some she had crossed out so hard she'd torn the paper.

Juniper read the first prayer aloud, quietly, testing his tongue against thirty years of forgetting.

*"Vess-ah torren kil—"*

The word broke apart in his mouth, but the shape of it came back the way a scar comes back: not the wound, only the memory of where it had been. His grandmother's kitchen. Steam off the pot. Her voice dropping into that other register, the one that made the fire sit up straighter.

He tried again. Slower. *"Vess-ah torren kil, vash orenna sul."*

Below the line, in pencil: *God-of-oaths, hear what is owed.*

Downstairs, the child stopped sleeping.

It wasn't a sound that woke her—or rather, it was, but not one Juniper heard. One moment she was a comma of mud and bone against the hearthstone; the next she was sitting upright, eyes open, fixed on the ceiling, on him, through him, listening the way a deer listens—not with ears but with the whole architecture of its body.

Juniper closed the book. Went down the ladder slowly, the way you approach a snared animal.

"Good morning," he said in Common. Useless. Then, feeling foolish, feeling nine years old and caught at witchery, he knelt at the far end of the room and said, in Old Thornic: *"Sul veth. Kil na torren."*

*I am here. Nothing is owed.*

Her whole face changed.

He had seen that look before, once, on a man in a border camp who had spent six years as a prisoner of war and heard his own mother's dialect spoken by a stranger across the fire. It is a terrible thing, that expression—joy and grief arriving in the same breath, too entangled to feel separately. Sable's mouth worked. No sound came out. Then she scrambled up, mud cracking off her in flakes, and crossed the floorboards in four steps and stopped just short of him, trembling, and said something long and urgent and broken, and this time Juniper understood perhaps one word in ten.

But ten out of a hundred was more than nothing. Ten out of a hundred was a rope thrown across a gap.

"Slowly," he said—in Common, then remembered, then again in Thornic, dredging the word up from somewhere below memory: *"Shenna."* Slowly. His grandmother saying it over the pot, over his childish hands, *shenna, shenna,* patience, patience.

She knelt opposite him, mirroring, close but not touching. Her eyes went to the book in his hands and widened—she reached for it, stopped herself, looked at him with a question shaped so plainly it needed no translation.

"You can read this?" he asked, then hated himself, because obviously she couldn't understand the question. Instead he held up the book, tapped the cover, pointed at her, raised his eyebrows.

Sable nodded so hard her whole body nodded with her.

* * *

They spent the morning building a bridge out of wreckage.

It went like this: Juniper would find a page with a translation he half-trusted, read the Thornic aloud, mangle it, watch her face for recognition or wince. When she recognized it, she would repeat it correctly—the difference was astonishing, his words sounding like stones dropped down a well and hers like the water they fell into—and then she would point at the thing the word meant. The hearth. The shutter. The light moving on the wall. His hand. Her own chest.

By midmorning he had recovered perhaps forty words. By noon, sixty. His grandmother's ghost sat over the whole exercise, correcting his vowels, and twice he caught himself glancing up as though the loft ladder might produce her, disapproving, arms folded, alive.

The words came back in clusters, and the clusters told their own story. There were many for stone. Many for doors, thresholds, hinges, locks—a suspicious abundance, a language built by people obsessed with passages. There were few for sea. None, as far as he could find in the whole book, for *forgive.*

There were dozens for *oath.* Sworn-oath, blood-oath, oath-of-road, oath-of-hearth, oath-broken, oath-redeemed, oath-abandoned—each a separate word, each with its own weight, and when Juniper read the list aloud, slowly, Sable touched the last one—*vess-kil,* the abandoned oath—and then pressed her palm flat over his heart, where, under twenty-two years of muscle and scar, the oath-mark sat.

He hadn't shown her the mark. He hadn't told her anything.

"How did you know that?" he asked in Common, and then, uselessly, again in Thornic, and she cocked her head at the question-shape of it and answered at length, earnestly, in a flood he couldn't follow, except—

Except for the middle of it. In the middle of the flood she made a gesture: both hands rising from her own shoulders, branching, dividing, dividing again, fingers splaying into more fingers, an impossible arithmetic of limbs. Then she hugged herself and shivered, and said a word he didn't know, and said it again, pointing back the way she had come, south and east, toward the mountains' blind spine, and repeated the branching-hands gesture slower, and her voice cracked on the third repetition and became simply crying, and some things are the same in every tongue.

Many hands.

Something with many hands had chased her here.

* * *

In the afternoon, Maren came back from the ridge line, and the temperature of the waystation dropped ten degrees.

She stood in the doorway with snow in her hair and her crossbow slung easy, the way she carried it when she'd seen nothing and wanted everyone to know she'd seen nothing, and she watched Juniper sitting on the floorboards—an officer of the Broken Vess company, forty-one years old, kneeling in the dust playing a finger-pointing game with a mud-child—and her eyebrow performed an entire speech.

"Don't," Juniper said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're about to say several things."

Maren came in, stamped the snow off, set water boiling. She moved around the two of them with the elaborate care of someone pretending the scene was normal, and Sable watched her the whole time with the stillness of a creature deciding whether the new animal was predator. Finally Maren crouched at the edge of the firelight, well inside arm's reach of her belt knife, and said, "Grey's scouts are in the valley. Two days out, maybe three. Burned the ferry archive at Lowbridge on the way through—Ash says the smoke was visible from the pass." She let that land. "Whatever she is, someone important wants her found. Or wants whatever she *says* found. So we need to know what she knows, Juniper. Fast."

"I'm working on it."

"You're working on it with your dead grandmother's witch-book and a vocabulary of fifty words."

"Sixty."

"*Juniper.*"

He looked at the child—at Sable, he was already thinking of her as Sable, when had that happened, sometime between the finger-pointing and the crying, the name had simply installed itself—and he looked at the door scored into the floorboards, and he made a decision he did not examine too closely, because examining it would have meant admitting how badly he wanted it to work.

"Maren," he said. "Watch the door."

"The one on the floor?"

"No." He took Sable's hand—it was the first time he'd touched her, and her grip closed on his fingers with a desperate strength that hurt something old in his chest—and led her to the other one. The real door. The front door of the waystation, oak and iron, older than the company, older than the maps. "Watch *this* door. We're going to ask her about doors."

* * *

It took another hour, and it worked better than it had any right to.

Doors turned out to be the one subject where his recovered vocabulary was almost sufficient, because his grandmother had been obsessed with them too, and because the prayer book's margins were full of them. Threshold. Lintel. Hinge. That which opens. That which must not. He laid the words out one at a time like cards, and Sable answered with her whole body—yes, no, *close*, *farther*—and gradually, painfully, a picture assembled itself out of gestures and fragments:

There was more than one kind of door. There were the ordinary ones—she dismissed these with a flick of her fingers, contemptuous, a child dismissing the alphabet after learning to read. And there were others. Doors that were places. Doors that were *people*, sometimes—here she hesitated, circled her own chest with her hand, uncertain whether the word applied to herself, and Juniper's skin crawled and he let it go. And the ones who tended the doors, who guarded them, who *made* them—

The branching hands again.

Here her speech stopped being fragments and became recitation. She straightened. Her voice changed register, dropped into something formal, rhythmic—a catechism, Juniper realized, a thing memorized, taught to children the way Concordat children learned the Nine Names—and though he understood almost none of it, the cadence did the work. Rhythm carries meaning across any gap. He caught *hands* three times, *faces-no* twice, and once a phrase his grandmother had pencilled a translation beside and then crossed out so hard the paper tore, and he fumbled the book open to it with suddenly cold fingers.

Beneath the torn line, barely legible in the ravaged margin, his grandmother had written, years ago, in a moment of weakness or courage:

*The Sealers keep what sleeps asleep. Pray it is not our sleep they keep.*

"What are they keeping?" Juniper whispered, in Common, to no one, to the room.

And Sable—whether she understood the tone, the shape of a question, or whether the recitation had simply arrived at its ending—answered in Thornic, in a single word, and pointed at the floor.

At the drawn door.

Juniper looked down at it. All day it had sat at the edge of his attention, familiar as furniture, and now he looked at it properly for the first time and felt the vertigo arrive late, the way it does after a fall. The door was drawn in the old style—he knew that now, after a day of the book, knew the conventions of it, the lintel-stroke, the hinge-marks. It was drawn *correctly*. Drawn the way a mason draws, or a priest. And in the center of it, where a handle should be, there was a mark he now recognized from page after page of his grandmother's book, the glyph that headed nearly every prayer:

The sigil of Vess. God-of-oaths. The god who had stopped answering.

"It's a door to him," Juniper said. His voice sounded strange in his own ears. "You drew a door to my god."

Sable shook her head. Pointed at the door. Pointed at herself. Shook her head again—but it wasn't denial, he understood suddenly; it was *correction*, frustration, the agony of a thought too large for her sixty shared words. She knelt at the door's edge. Looked up at him. Held out her hand, palm up.

*Give me your hand.*

Every instinct he owned, every smuggler's caution, every scrap of his grandmother's warnings, voted no. He gave her his hand anyway—told himself afterward it was because Maren was watching, because they needed answers before Grey's scouts came up the valley, because strategy. He was lying and part of him knew it. The larger part was simply the child's face, trusting, asking, and the unbearable fact that he had already begun, without meaning to, without permission from any part of himself that kept the books, to love her.

She pressed his palm flat against the center of the door. Against the sigil of Vess.

The wood was warm.

Not sun-warm. Blood-warm. And under his hand, the scored groove of the sigil's lowest stroke was *wet*, and he lifted his palm away and his skin came away red, and the line beneath it welled slow and thick, welling *upward* against everything sensible, beading along the carved stroke like dew along a blade, and the smell hit him a heartbeat later—iron and salt and something older underneath, mineral, the air in deep caves, the smell of the prayer book's pages.

Maren swore. Sable didn't flinch. She watched the door bleed with an expression Juniper had no word for in either language—grief, maybe, or recognition, the look of someone seeing an old wound reopened on a body they loved.

Then she said one word, quiet, and pointed at the blood, and then at the mountains to the south and east, and Juniper, following her finger, understanding finally, horribly, what she had been trying to tell him all day, translated it aloud with lips gone numb:

"She didn't draw the door." His voice cracked. "She *is* the door. And they're coming to close it."

Outside, far down the valley, thin on the wind, a horn sounded. Once. Patient. Like something that had all the time in the world, and many, many hands.
