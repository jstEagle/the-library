## 5. Inquisitor Grey Arrives

The snow came early to the Thornfell highlands that year, and Inquisitor Grey counted it a mercy. Snow simplified things. It erased the tracks of the guilty as readily as the innocent, which meant it erased nothing at all—only made every set of prints equally suspect, equally worth following.

Grey did not believe in mercy. Grey believed in procedure.

He rode at the head of six Crown troopers through the last pass before the valley villages, his warrant case strapped behind his saddle like a coffin too small for its occupant. Inside were forty-one documents in three alphabets: Old Thornic angular script, the pre-Union knotwork of the coastal septs, and the older thing still—the handless cursive the archivists called Rootscript because no one had ever seen it written by a living man. Each document bore the seal of the Office of Precedents. Each one authorized him, in language of increasing bluntness, to find speech that should not exist, and to end it.

Grey was fifty-one years old and had held his commission for nineteen years, and in all that time he had burned eleven archives, silenced four speakers, and buried one child whose only crime had been a lullaby her grandmother taught her before the grandmother died. He kept no ledger of these things. The Office kept it for him. That was what an office was for—to hold what a man could not carry and remain upright.

He thought about the child sometimes. Not with guilt; guilt was a luxury of people who believed the world could be arranged otherwise. He thought about her the way a mason thinks about a wall he had to take down to find the crack beneath. Necessary work. Ugly work. Work someone would have to do again if he didn't do it thoroughly now.

*Precaution*, the Office called it. Grey preferred the word *maintenance*. Kingdoms were not founded; they were maintained, daily, against the pressure of everything they had been built on top of. The Crown's founding was a story told so often it had worn smooth as a river stone, and stories wear smooth precisely where hands keep reaching for the truth underneath. His job was to stand between the hands and the stone.

The rumor had reached the capital in autumn, as rumors did, wrapped in three layers of deniability: a peddler who'd heard from a shepherd who'd heard from a dying woman that up in the high valleys past Thornfell, there was speech older than speech. Words from before the Union. Words that had been dead two hundred years, spoken alive.

Most such reports dissolved under investigation. Grey investigated all of them anyway. This was the discipline no one understood about his work: ninety-nine parts tedium to one part fire. He read the shepherd's testimony twice on the road, then the peddler's, then the marginal note some junior clerk had appended—*likely folk nonsense*—and felt the small cold pleasure of disagreeing with a colleague. Folk nonsense did not make grown shepherds weep when they repeated it. Folk nonsense did not cause a woman on her deathbed to refuse the rites out of fear of being overheard.

Someone up there was speaking Old Thornic. And Old Thornic, in the mountains, near the old border, could mean only one category of thing: remnant. A survivor. Or worse—a door.

Grey had spent nineteen years learning what doors meant, and none of it comforted him.

They came down into the first village at dusk, a huddle of slate roofs and peat smoke called Harrow's End, population sixty-one according to the census rolls that were nine years stale. The troopers took the inn's back room without ceremony. Grey took the front table, ordered soup he did not eat, and asked the innkeeper ordinary questions in an ordinary voice: trade routes, weather, births and deaths, whether anyone had passed through lately.

It was in answer to the last question that the innkeeper hesitated. Half a breath. The kind of hesitation that is itself an answer.

"There was a company," the man said. "Mercenaries. Down on their luck, by the look. Wintering at the old waystation up the mountain road."

"How many?"

"Fewer than there used to be, I'd say. They come down for salt and lamp oil." Another hesitation, smaller. "There's a child with them."

Grey set down his spoon with great care, as though it might go off.

"A child."

"Girl. Eight, maybe nine. Dark-skinned, doesn't look like anyone local. Doesn't talk—or talks, but nobody can follow it." The innkeeper wiped his hands on his apron, a gesture Grey catalogued automatically: nervousness performing innocence. "Folk say she's simple."

"Folk say," Grey repeated mildly. "And what do you say?"

The innkeeper looked at the soup Grey hadn't touched, then at Grey's face, which was the face of a patient schoolmaster, which was the face Grey had practiced for nineteen years until it fit like scar tissue. "I say I've got a wife and three daughters and this inn is my whole life, Your Honor."

"Yes," Grey said. "That's what I thought you'd say."

He paid for the soup he hadn't eaten. Generosity loosened tongues better than terror, and cost less to administer.

* * *

The village archive of Harrow's End occupied the ground floor of the tithe barn, and it consisted of one iron-banded chest, one shelf of parish registers going back to the Union, and—this was what interested Grey—a crate of loose papers the parish priest called "the grandmother box," though it belonged to no single grandmother. For generations the mountain women had deposited in it whatever their own grandmothers left them: recipes, charms, letters in hands no one could read anymore, scraps of cloth worked with patterns whose meanings had worn away. Every village in the highlands had one. The Office had records of four hundred and eleven such collections. The Office had destroyed three hundred and eighty-eight of them.

Grey knelt before the crate in the light of two storm lanterns while the priest stood wringing his hands at a decorous distance, protesting in advance a destruction he could not name yet because Grey had not named it.

"You understand I'm required to examine anything that might bear on Crown precedents," Grey said. He said it gently. Sincerity was the sharpest tool he owned; men forgave sincerity things they never forgave cruelty.

"Of course, Your Honor. It's only—these are just old women's things. Charms against milk-souring. A blessing for the bees. Nothing political."

"Nothing is political until it is," Grey said, and lifted out the first paper.

It was a letter, or the fragment of one, written in the angular alphabet he knew best. He read it by lantern light, translating slowly, the way a man wades a cold river: *...and do not teach the little ones the names, whatever your mother says. Names are how it holds on. My mother taught me and see what I am...*

The rest was water-damaged. It didn't matter. Grey sat back on his heels and let the cold settle into him, the professional cold, the one that arrived precisely when other men would have begun to feel excitement.

Because this was not a remnant. Remnants were accidents—a word here, a lullaby there, surviving like weeds in a wall. This letter was doctrine. Someone, two centuries ago, had been *suppressing deliberately*. Which meant there had been something to suppress. Which meant the silence the Office maintained was not the natural quiet of a dead tongue but the maintained quiet of a buried one.

Which confirmed the thing Grey had believed his entire career and had never once been permitted to write in an official report: the Union had not found empty land. It had *made* empty land. And somewhere in these mountains, the making was coming undone.

"Your Honor?" the priest ventured. "Is something wrong?"

"No," Grey said truthfully. "Something is right. That's rarer."

He examined the crate for three hours. Most of it was exactly what the priest promised—charms, recipes, the domestic archaeology of women who kept a culture alive the only way women are ever permitted to keep anything alive: by disguising it as housekeeping. Grey read blessings for bees that were not blessings for bees. He read a birthing song whose rhythm, tapped out on his knee, matched the meter of a Thornic war chant recorded in the Office's oldest sealed folio. He read, twice, a child's copybook in which someone long dead had practiced letters in two alphabets side by side, Thornic and Crown common script, the same words in each, as if teaching herself to be a bridge between worlds—and then, on the final page, in a shakier hand: *burned the rest. forgive me.*

Grey understood that page better than anyone living. He had written its descendant a hundred times in his own reports.

At midnight he stood, brushed straw from his knees, and addressed the priest, who had waited faithfully in the cold all those hours because hope makes men patient.

"I'm going to burn this," Grey said. "I want you to understand why, so you can tell your successor, and she can tell hers."

"Your Honor—"

"The Crown protects you," Grey said. "You've heard that your whole life, and it's true, and you've never had to ask what it protects you *from*. I'm asking it for you, every day. There are things in this box older than the Crown, and things older than the Crown do not coexist with it peacefully. They don't compete. They *precede*. And whatever precedes the law is a hole under the law, and holes open. I have watched them open. I have stood in villages where they opened, and I will tell you honestly: the burning of this box will be the kindest thing done in Harrow's End this winter."

The priest was crying, quietly, the way men cry when they have already lost. "My grandmother wrote some of these."

"I know," Grey said. "So did mine."

He gave the order. The troopers carried the chest, the crate, and the shelf of registers—all of it, precaution being indivisible—out into the snow of the barnyard, and Grey himself struck the flint, because he had never once delegated a burning, and he never would. The fire caught the dry paper hungrily. Ash rose straight up in the windless dark like a column of grey moths, and Grey watched it with the expression of a man doing arithmetic.

Sixty-one people in Harrow's End. Four hundred and eleven collections once. Somewhere above them, up the mountain road, a child who spoke a dead tongue was wintering with a mercenary company that was itself a kind of remnant—a company sworn to a god whose name the Office had also tried to bury, though that file was sealed even above Grey's clearance, and he had only ever glimpsed the cover: *VESSEL PROJECT — DISCONTINUED.*

A discontinued project. Like a well filled in. But wells fill in badly. Things come up through them.

The child, he thought. The child was either the weed or the crack, and either way the procedure was the same: find it, isolate it, and cut it out cleanly, before the wall came down on everyone standing beneath it. He took no pleasure in imagining it. He took less pleasure in failing to imagine it. Both failures were dereliction.

"Your Honor," the priest said behind him, hoarse. "What was in it? What was in the box that frightens the Crown so much?"

Grey considered lying, but lies were for people with something to protect, and his protection had been spent long ago, poured out like everything else into the maintenance of the smooth stone.

"Grief," he said. "Two hundred years of it, kept in a box by women who weren't allowed to mourn out loud. Grief keeps, Father. That's the trouble with it. It keeps, and it waits, and one day someone opens the box and all of it stands up at once."

He turned to his troopers. "Saddle up. We ride for the waystation at first light."

Above the black ridgeline, the stars wheeled indifferent and ancient, and Grey stood a moment longer in the ash-flecked snow, listening—not for hoofbeats or voices, but for the particular silence of a country holding its breath around a grave.

Somewhere up that mountain, the silence had started answering questions.

He meant to stop it. God help him, he sincerely meant to stop it.
