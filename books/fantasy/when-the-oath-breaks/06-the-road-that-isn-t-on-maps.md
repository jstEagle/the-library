## 6. The Road That Isn't on Maps

They left the waystation before dawn, in that grey hour when the mountains hold their breath and even the crows have not yet found their voices. Juniper went first with Sable's small hand wrapped in his, and behind them came what remained of the company—nine souls where there had once been ninety, their boots crunching frost, their breath pluming white against the dark.

He did not look back at the waystation. He did not need to. He could smell it burning in his memory already: the scorch of Inquisitor Grey's lantern-oil, the way paper blackens and curls like a living thing in pain. Grey had burned a village archive two valleys over just to warm up. Juniper had no doubt about what he would do to a child who spoke a dead tongue.

"Left," Sable said.

Juniper stopped. The trail forked ahead of them in the pre-dawn murk—the left-hand branch plunging down into a ravine choked with deadfall, the right climbing toward the high pasture where smugglers had run contraband since before his grandfather's grandfather. Every instinct honed by twenty years of illegal roads said *right*. The left was a deer track at best. It wasn't on any map he'd ever bought, stolen, or memorized.

"Left," Sable said again, and this time she said it in Old Thornic, one word rolling out soft and round as a river stone: *vessu*. Left. He knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat, from prayers his grandmother had whispered when she thought no one listened. *Vessu* meant left. *Vessu* also meant something else—he could feel the other meaning folded inside the word like a letter inside an envelope—but he couldn't open it yet.

"The ravine?" Maren said at his shoulder. Her voice was flat with exhaustion and disbelief. "Juniper, there's nothing down there but rockfall and old snow."

"Sable says left."

"And if Sable says jump off the Widow's Tooth, do we do that too?"

"She hasn't been wrong yet." He turned left. Behind him he heard the low grumble of the company rearranging itself around his decision—Hesk muttering, Odile's sharp laugh, the wet click of someone loading a crossbow they probably wouldn't use. Nine people following him because there was nowhere else to go. Because Vess was silent and the Crown was not, and silence, it turned out, was the more dangerous of the two.

The ravine swallowed them. And the moment it did, Juniper felt something shift beneath his feet—not the ground itself, but something under the ground, some vast attention turning over in its sleep. The air changed. It tasted different: green where it should have tasted of stone, sweet where it should have tasted of frost. Pine resin and cut hay and woodsmoke from hearths that had not been lit in this valley for two hundred years.

He knew this smell.

He knew it the way you know a face in a dream—intimately, impossibly, with a certainty that has no evidence behind it. He knew it from dreams he'd had since he was small enough to sleep in his grandmother's bed in the smuggler's warren below Carrowgate. Dreams of a road running beside water so clear you could count the coins thrown into it by travelers making wishes. Dreams of orchards where the apples hung heavy and gold and no one ever picked them because no one was ever hungry there. Dreams he had never told anyone about, because boys who dreamed of places that didn't exist got looked at sideways in a company sworn to a god who no longer answered.

"Juniper." Maren caught his arm. "Your face. What's wrong with your face?"

"Nothing."

"You look like you've seen a ghost wearing your own skin."

He kept walking. The ravine floor, which should have been a jumble of ice-slick boulders, resolved under his boots into something smoother—a paved way, half-buried, its fitted stones furred with moss but still unmistakably *made*. Not laid by smugglers. Smugglers didn't cut stone to fit; they took the shortest line between profit and escape. This had been built to last. Built to be walked for generations.

No one had walked it for generations. The moss told him so. Two centuries of moss, undisturbed until this morning, until his boots.

Sable trotted beside him, mud still dried in the creases of her neck, her breath quick and visible. She glanced up at him with those dark eyes that held too much—too much fear, too much age, too much of whatever it was she carried—and then she looked at the road, and her expression did something he hadn't seen before. It softened. She smiled. A small, wondering smile, the first he'd seen on her, and it broke his heart a little, because he understood it immediately.

She was home. Or close to it. And she was a child walking toward home through a country that wanted her dead.

* * *

By midmorning the ravine had widened into a gorge, and the gorge had begun to lie to them.

That was the only word Juniper could find for it. The light came through the pines at angles that didn't match the sun. The stream that ran beside the old paved road babbled in a rhythm too regular, almost like speech, almost like a song sung very softly by someone who didn't want to be overheard. Twice he saw things at the edge of vision—smoke rising where no chimney stood, a fence line running into the trees where no fence could be—and twice, when he turned to look directly, the ordinary forest snapped back into place like a door closing.

"Company halt," he called, and the nine of them gathered in the lee of a granite outcrop, passing water skins and hard bread while the strange stream sang to them.

"We're being followed." Hesk said it around a mouthful of bread. "I've felt it since the waystation. Something behind us on the trail."

"It's Grey," Odile said. "Has to be. He burned the archive at Millbrook on the fourth. Waystation yesterday. He's moving faster than he should be, which means he's got help, which means we stop pretending we can outrun a Crown Inquisitor with a company of nine and a child who—"

"A child who is the fastest thing we've got," Maren cut in. "Have you watched her walk? She doesn't get tired. She doesn't eat much. She sleeps like a stone and wakes knowing things she shouldn't know. If Grey catches us, it won't be because she slowed us down. It'll be because we argued too long."

The argument went where arguments among mercenaries always went—to the question of cost. What was the girl worth? Who would buy her? Could they sell her to Grey himself and buy their pardons with her? Juniper stood apart and let them say it, because saying it aloud made it smaller somehow, and because he'd learned long ago that you cannot forbid men from thinking a thing; you can only make sure they know exactly what the thing costs when it comes due.

Sable sat beside him on the outcrop, drawing in the dirt with a stick. He watched over her shoulder. She drew a road—of course she drew a road, they were all drowning in roads now—and along the road she drew small marks, and beside the marks she wrote words in Old Thornic, her letters careful and upright, taught by hands long dead.

"What does it say?" he asked, quietly, in the fragments of Thornic his grandmother had left him. He knew perhaps forty words. Half of them were prayer-words.

Sable considered him for a long moment. Then she pointed at the first mark on the road, and said, slowly, the way you speak to someone learning: "*Ondra.*" Bridge. He knew that one—*ondra*, bridge, it appeared in a blessing for safe crossing. Then the next mark. "*Sel.*" Well? Spring? Water of some kind. Then the next, and this one he did not know, and she saw him not know it, and she frowned in concentration, searching for another way to say it. Finally she pressed her palm flat against the dirt, over the mark, and closed her eyes, and shuddered once, delicately, like a bird shaking off rain.

Cold. The mark meant cold. Or something that made you cold.

"How many of these are on the road ahead?" he asked.

She held up both hands, all ten fingers, then folded three down.

Seven. Seven marks between here and wherever the road ended. One of them cold enough that a child who had fled something *with many hands* flinched to touch its picture.

"Mount up," Juniper said, and the company groaned and obeyed, because that was the last habit the god's silence had left them: obedience without faith, motion without meaning.

Except that today, for the first time in ten years, the motion felt like meaning. He hated how good it felt.

* * *

The second turn came at noon, and this time Sable corrected him before he'd even chosen wrong.

They had climbed out of the gorge onto a ridge, and the ridge offered three ways down: a scree chute to the west, a game trail east, and—almost invisible, a seam in the hillside rather than a path—a track leading straight down through a stand of birches gone silver-white in the winter light. Juniper was reading the wind and calculating grades when Sable tugged his sleeve and pointed at the seam.

"*Dorath vennu,*" she said.

He shook his head. Unknown words. She tried again, slower, pointing: *dorath*, and then she made a gesture with both hands cupped, like holding water, or holding something precious, or—

"Dreams," he said, before he knew he was going to say it. His mouth had known the word his mind didn't. "*Dorath.* Dreams." His grandmother had used it. *Sleep well, little dreamer. Dorath vennu.* The dreaming road. The road of dreams.

Because he had dreamed this road. That was the terrible thing, the thing that made his palms sweat inside his gloves. He had dreamed this exact ridge, these exact birches, the seam of the path cutting down between them like a scar. In the dream he had been older, and alone, and the birches had been in leaf, and someone had waited for him at the bottom—someone whose face he could never hold onto past waking, only the feeling of being awaited, of being *expected*, of coming home to people who had been keeping his place at the table for a very long time.

"Juniper." Maren's voice, careful. "You've stopped walking."

"I know this place."

"Sure. We're in the Thornfell backcountry, you've run goods through here for twenty years—"

"No." He heard his own voice come out strange, and he didn't correct it. "Not like that. I dreamed this ridge when I was six years old, Mare. I dreamed it before I ever crossed the mountains. I dreamed the birches. I dreamed—" He stopped. He almost said *I dreamed you waiting at the bottom*, but that wasn't true either; the person in the dream had never been Maren. The person in the dream had worn robes, he thought, or maybe it had been light shaped like a person, and it had spoken in Old Thornic, and he had understood every word, and waking had been like drowning.

Maren studied him the way she studied terrain before committing the company to it: thoroughly, unsentimentally, looking for the weakness that would kill them. Whatever she found in his face made her jaw tighten.

"And the girl put it there?" she asked. "In your head? When she drew the door? Is that what you're telling me?"

"I don't know."

"That's not an answer that keeps me warm at night."

"It's the only one I have." He started down the seam of the path, and the birches closed around them, and the feeling of being expected settled over his shoulders like a mantle he had never agreed to wear.

But here was the thing he did not say to Maren, the thing he kept folded away in the envelope inside himself where he kept everything that frightened him most: the dreams had started years before Sable was born. Years before the waystation, before the door drawn on floorboards, before any of it. Whatever was reaching for him through these roads had been reaching for a long time. The child hadn't put anything in his head.

The country had remembered him first.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow off the dreaming road, a hollow that should have been open to the sky and somehow wasn't—no stars showed above them though the clouds had broken, and the fire they built burned strangely quiet, its smoke rising straight as a plumb line and simply stopping, as if it reached a ceiling no one could see. Odile complained about it until Hesk threatened to stuff a sock in her, and then everyone laughed, briefly, the brittle laughter of people whistling past something enormous.

Juniper took first watch, which was a lie, because he gave the watch away in pieces and spent the night sitting beside the sleeping Sable, watching her breathe.

In the firelight she looked younger than she had in daylight. Younger and more breakable. He thought about the door she had drawn on the waystation floorboards—the lines that had appeared from nowhere, the wood that had bled when touched—and he thought about what Maren had asked, and he found he was no longer sure of the shape of the question. Was Sable calling to the country? Or was the country calling to her, using her small voice the way a trapped man uses a knock on a pipe?

Or—third possibility, the one that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone—were they calling to each other, child and country, seal and sealed, and had been calling all along, and he was only the fool standing in the middle of the conversation with a sword he no longer believed in?

His oath-mark ached. It had been aching since the gorge—the old brand on his inner forearm, the knot-and-circle sigil of Vess, pressed into his skin at thirteen by a priest whose name he'd forgotten. Ten years of silence, and now, suddenly, it ached like the day it was made. Like something pressing on the other side of a wall.

He pushed back his sleeve and looked at it in the firelight.

The sigil had changed.

Not much. Anyone else would have missed it. But Juniper had lived with this mark for thirty years; he knew its lines the way he knew the scars on his knuckles. The circle that enclosed the knot was no longer closed. There was a gap in it now, thin as a hair, wide as a door left ajar.

Beside him, without opening her eyes, Sable said, in Old Thornic, softly, the way you tell someone news you've known for a long time:

"*Thurath dorath. Ondra vessu.*"

He knew two of the four words. *Dreaming road. Bridge left.*

The road of dreams runs left. The bridge is on the left.

And then, after a silence long enough that he thought she'd gone back to sleep, she opened her eyes and looked at him across the fire, and added a fifth word—one he didn't know, one that made the ache in his oath-mark spike sudden and hot, one she repeated gently, patiently, twice more, until he understood that she wasn't teaching him a vocabulary lesson.

She was telling him what was coming. She was giving him the name of the thing at the end of the road, and the name was:

"*Vessu-anneth.*"

The left hand of the god. The hidden hand. The door.

Far behind them, down the dark of the dreaming road, a lantern-flare kindled in the trees—small, distant, deliberate. Grey had found the seam in the hillside. Grey was reading the same road they were.

Juniper woke Maren with a hand over her mouth, and by the time the company was moving, they were moving fast, downhill through the silver birches, nine mercenaries and one child, fleeing along a road that existed in no book and no ledger and no law, toward a door that a sleeping girl had named with a god's own hand.
