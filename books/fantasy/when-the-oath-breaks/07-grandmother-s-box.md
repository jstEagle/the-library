## 7. Grandmother's Box

The cache was where dreams said it would be.

That was the part Juniper could not stop turning over as he climbed the scree above the drowned valley: not that the road had felt familiar, not that Sable had corrected his turns in a dead tongue and been right every time—but that he had known, without knowing how, to look for the cairn shaped like a kneeling man. He had dreamed it a dozen times over the years. He had always assumed the dream was memory, some half-remembered story his grandmother told him by the peat fire when he was small enough to fit inside the crook of her arm. *The kneeling man watches the water that isn't there,* she used to say, in Thornic first and then again in Common so the words would stick. *When you need what I kept, kneel beside him.*

He had thought it was nonsense. Grandmothers were full of nonsense. Cursed tongues, gods who signed their names, doors under hills—all of it the same warm nonsense, told to keep a child still through the long dark months when the snow came sideways off the fells.

Now the child was walking behind him in the cursed tongue, singing under her breath, and the water that wasn't there was a valley of grey stones and old silt below him, dry for two hundred years or more, and Juniper Okafor knelt beside a cairn in the shape of a kneeling man because he no longer had any other idea left to kneel at.

Sable stopped singing. She crouched a few paces off, mud still dried in the seams of her clothes from the night she'd knocked on the waystation door, and watched him with those eyes that seemed to hold more weather than a child's face should be asked to carry.

"The stone," she said—she'd been teaching him, or he'd been letting her teach him, word by word, prayer by prayer. *Elath.* Stone. Then she touched her own chest. *Vess-kin.* It was one of the phrases she used often, pointing at things: the standing stones on the ridge, the door she'd drawn on the floorboards, once, terrifyingly, at Juniper himself.

"I know," he said in Common, because his Thornic was still a beggar's Thornic, stitched together from fragments. "I'm looking."

*Elath* meant stone, but the kneeling man was not stone. That was the trick of the cairn, and he only understood it now, up close, with his palms flat against it. The shape had been built from river cobbles, yes, but beneath the cobbles—under a skin of lichen and two centuries of frost-heave—there was iron. A box, maybe. Something man-shaped, or nearly. His fingers found a seam where no seam should be in a pile of rocks, and when he traced it the lichen came away in wet green sheets like skin off an onion, and underneath was bronze gone the brown-black of old blood, worked into the shape of a man kneeling with his hands cupped before him.

Cupped. Offering. Or receiving.

Juniper's mouth had gone dry. He was aware of his own heartbeat, slow and enormous, like something wading through him. "Grandmother," he said aloud, though she had been twenty years dead and had never once, in all her life, mentioned this place by name. Only in riddle. Only in the language everyone else called cursed, and which she had called *ours.*

The kneeling figure's cupped hands were a lid. Of course they were. He pressed them the way you press anything in Thornic stories—*with grief, not force*, his grandmother always said; *grief opens what force cannot*—and the hands folded back on hidden hinges with a sound like a held breath released after two hundred years, and cold clean air breathed out of the ground, smelling of cedar oil and tallow and something faintly sweet, like honey left too long in a dark room.

Inside was a box.

It was small enough to carry against the chest with both arms. Black oak banded in the same brown-black bronze, unadorned except for a single line of characters burned into the lid, and Juniper's breath caught because he could read them now—or almost. Three weeks ago they would have been pretty squiggles. Now, with Sable's patient voice and his grandmother's stubborn ghost behind his eyes, he made out: *Okafor.* Just that. A name. His family name, sitting on a buried box in a country that had been erased so thoroughly that even the maps pretended it had never existed.

"You knew," he whispered—to the box, to the woman, to whoever was listening. Nobody answered. Gods didn't answer anymore; that was the whole wound he carried. But the silence here felt different from the silence of the last ten years. Less empty. More like the silence of a room where someone is sleeping.

He lifted the box out. It was heavier than its size explained, weighted low, and whatever lay within shifted once—a soft, dense sliding, like paper, or like sand obeying a law of its own.

Sable crept closer. She put one small hand flat on the lid, right over the name, and looked up at him with an expression he was beginning to dread on her face: recognition. She knew this object, or the language of it, or the weight of it. "Open," she said in Common, carefully, the way she said all her Common—each word set down like a cup she was afraid of spilling. Then, softer, in Thornic: *Elaveth su vane.* Open what is given.

"That's the trouble," Juniper muttered. "Everything's given lately. Nothing's asked."

But his hands were already working the bronze clasps, and they gave the way old things give—reluctantly, then all at once.

* * *

The top layer was cloth. Wool, dyed a deep madder red that had faded to the color of embers, embroidered along one edge in white thread: more Thornic, tiny and exact, a border of text running around the whole panel like a river around an island. Juniper lifted it and understood it had been folded around everything else, a wrapping, a keeping-cloth. He would take it out later, he told himself. He would sit with Sable and they would unpick the letters together, the way they unpicked everything now, his rough Common questions and her careful answers, building a bridge between languages plank by plank.

Under the cloth: a book.

It was small, palm-sized, bound in leather gone soft and dark as chestnuts, its corners worn round by decades of a hand returning to it. The spine was cracked in three places from use. Someone had read this book to death and loved it anyway. On the cover, stamped in dull silver, was the sign of Vess—the god's mark, a closed hand with the thumb crossed over the fingers, the gesture of a promise being kept—and beneath it, in the same silver, worn nearly smooth: *Prayers of the Sealed Country, Being the Offices of Vess as Kept in Thornfell.*

His grandmother's prayer book. He was almost sure of it before he opened it. When he did open it, the smell rose up and hit him like a fist: tallow and cedar and woodsmoke and, underneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, the smell of her—the rosemary oil she rubbed into her hands, the same oil she'd rubbed into his scalp when he was feverish as a boy. Ten years of godless mercenary work fell away from him all at once. He was six years old, wrapped in a red blanket, watching her lips move over these pages while the storm tried the shutters.

The pages were dense with script in two columns—Thornic on the left, an older hand; a translation in Common on the right, added later, the ink a different shade, the handwriting familiar down to the little hook she made of her *v*'s. Marginal notes everywhere. Little asterisks. One passage near the front had been underlined so hard the nib had torn the paper:

*What is sworn in silence is kept in silence. What is kept in silence is owed in silence. The debt does not die when the debtor forgets it. Remember, little ones: the oath sleeps in the blood, not in the mouth.*

Below that, in her handwriting, squeezed into the margin: *J. will understand when he is older. Pray he never has to.*

*J.* Juniper. His throat closed. He sat very still on the cold stones with a dead woman's book open on his knees and let the wind comb through his hair, and Sable, bless her, said nothing at all, just sat down beside him and leaned her slight weight against his arm as if she'd been told about grief and recognized its posture.

There was more under the book. A bundle of letters, tied with wire, water-stained but legible—correspondence in Common with a bookseller in Lowmark, a chandler, someone signing themselves only as *K.*, the last letter dated the year Juniper was born. And under the letters, flat and sealed in a sleeve of oiled leather, a document.

He knew what it was before he unfolded it. Some part of him had known since the cairn. You spend ten years carrying a mercenary company's contracts—the real ones, the binding ones, the ones written in blood-oath formulae because in the old days companies swore their commissions to gods and gods enforced them—you learn the shape of such papers the way a swordsman learns the weight of a blade in the dark.

But nothing had prepared him for the seal.

Contracts sworn to Vess were sealed with a thumbprint taken in the god's own presence, pressed into red wax by the priest officiating. Every soldier in Juniper's company carried a copy of theirs somewhere—in a boot, in a breast pocket, against the heart. Juniper's had burned with everything else the night the waystation at Harrow Ford went up, and he had told himself it didn't matter, because the god who backed it had stopped answering, and a contract with a silent guarantor was just paper with ambitions.

This seal was not wax. It was gold, thin as foil, laid into the parchment itself, and it bore not a thumbprint but a full hand—an entire hand, long-fingered, spread wide, as if the god had laid his palm flat on the page and the metal had risen to meet it like frost on glass. And the hand was warm. Not metaphorically. Juniper's fingertips registered it before his mind caught up: the gold held a heat like a stone left too long in the sun, two hundred years underground, and it warmed further under his touch, the way a living thing warms.

"Oh," said Sable softly, in Thornic, and then, translating herself with visible effort: "It knows you."

"It doesn't know me." His voice came out wrong. "I'm nobody. I'm a smuggler with nine hungry soldiers and a bad winter coming."

He read the contract.

The script was formal Old Thornic in the body—he could parse perhaps one line in five—but the crucial parts had been duplicated in Common, in that beautiful old formal hand they must have taught in chanceries before the erasure. He read it twice. Three times. The wind moved over the dry valley and the light shifted and he read it a fourth time, because the words refused to stay still, refused to mean what they plainly said.

*Commission of Service, Sworn Under Hand of Vess, God of the Kept Word.*

*Let it be known to all generations: the Company of the Red Standard, being eleven souls in number, hath entered service of the God Vess for the defense of the Sealed Country, term perpetual, wage the keeping of the oath itself...*

Eleven souls. His grandmother's stories had always named eleven. Eleven founders, eleven names recited at every funeral, eleven cups poured at the winter feast though only four people ever sat at the table. He had thought it ritual. He found the roster now, appended at the foot of the commission, eleven lines in Old Thornic, and beside each name in the margin, in his grandmother's later handwriting, translations—and descent-lines. She had traced them. Line by line, generation by generation, down two hundred years, through exiles and renamings and marriages into Common-speaking families, through the quiet smuggling clans of Thornfell who kept odd customs and never said why.

Eight of the lines simply ended. Crossed through, dates beside them, the note *line failed* in her cramped script.

Two lines trailed off into illegibility, lost in the wars, in the plagues, in the general erasure of the world.

One line ran unbroken down the margin like a vein, and terminated, in ink barely twenty-five years old, in a single word written in a young woman's careful hand:

*Okafor. Juniper. Born under the mark. Let him choose when he is grown—or let the choice wait for him, and find him anyway.*

The oath was always his.

That was the sentence his mind kept returning to, circling it like a bird over a kill it couldn't land on. All those years he had believed the oath was chosen—that he had stood in a temple at nineteen with his blood on a priest's knife and *chosen* Vess, freely, the way the doctrine demanded, the way every recruit was told they were choosing. He had carried the god's silence for a decade believing the silence was aimed at a decision he himself had made. Grief for his own foolish faith. Punishment for his own presumption.

And all the while it had been inheritance. Blood-debt. A thread spun before his birth, wound through grandmothers and great-grandmothers who poured eleven cups at a table for four, who taught a cursed language to children in whispers, who buried a box under a kneeling man beside water that wasn't there and trusted the country itself to remember the way.

*The oath sleeps in the blood, not in the mouth.*

He laughed. It came out cracked and awful, one syllable, and Sable flinched, and he pulled her in against his side because he was sorry and because holding onto her was the only thing in the world he currently understood. "They never told me," he said to the valley, to the ancestors, to the god with the warm golden hand. "Not one of them ever just *told* me."

But even as he said it, he knew it wasn't true, or wasn't entirely. His grandmother had told him, constantly, in the only way that wouldn't get a Thornic-speaking family in a Crown province dragged into a square and questioned. *The kneeling man watches the water that isn't there. When you need what I kept, kneel beside him.* She had told him the location of his own soul in the form of a fireside rhyme, and he had carried it in his head for thirty years, and here he was. The telling had worked. It had just taken a silent god, a hunted child, and the end of everything he pretended his life was to make him listen.

Sable was touching the gold hand-print now, gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "Vess-kin," she murmured. Then her finger moved to the roster, to the crossed-through names, the failed lines, and something in her face changed—tightened. She looked up at him and said a long sentence in Old Thornic, fast, urgent, the most words at once he'd ever heard from her, and caught only fragments: *given*, *kept*, *owed*, and a word she'd never used before, a word she repeated twice, pressing her palm flat to her sternum each time as if the word lived there.

*Sevvaneth.*

He didn't know it. He reached for his grandmother's marginal glosses, flipping pages with a thumb gone clumsy, and found it—of course he found it; the book had been waiting for him to need it—near the middle, beside a prayer titled *For Those Who Keep the Keeping:* *sevvaneth — the custodians. They who maintain the sealing. Neither priests nor jailers. Do not speak their true number. Do not meet their hands.*

Do not meet their hands.

Something with many hands, Sable had said, back at the waystation, in the first broken hours of her arrival. Something she had fled across a mountain range in the dead season, alone, a child carrying a language like contraband.

Juniper looked up from the page. The light had gone strange over the dry valley—thinner, the grey of pewter, though noon was still an hour off. The wind had stopped. In the total quiet he could hear, very far away and very high, a sound like many feet on scree, or like many hands opening and closing, patiently, in rhythm.

Sable had gone rigid against his side. Her small hand closed over the prayer book as if it could be barricaded behind.

"They've found the box," she whispered in Common—the clearest thing she'd ever said to him. "We have to run."
