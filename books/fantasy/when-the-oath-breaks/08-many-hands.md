## 8. Many Hands

*This is how Sable tells it. The telling is not a story; the telling is a wall she builds brick by brick out of the pieces she can carry. Some of the words come in Old Thornic and Juniper writes them down. Some of the words come as pictures, charcoal on the backs of Maren's supply ledgers. What follows is both, laid side by side, the way she laid them out on the floor of the waystation while the storm chewed at the shutters.*

---

Small.

I was small.

Before this — *this* meaning her own two hands, held up, turned over, examined like tools someone else left behind — before hands, there was no I. There was a room. There were many rooms. There was a dark that was not empty but full, full the way a cup is full, and Sable does not have the word for what filled it so she draws it instead: a great shape made of folded grief, wings like collapsed tents, an eye that is not an eye but a wound that learned to look.

She colors the whole page black around it.

Then, at the edge, very small, very careful: a stick figure with yellow hair. Herself.

*I,* she says, tapping the stick figure. *I was small. I was made small so I could fit through.*

Fit through what, Juniper asks, and she looks at him with the patience of someone who has already answered this question many times to people who cannot hear it.

Through, she says. She draws a door. Of course she draws a door. Every answer she has is a door.

---

The ones who kept us had no faces.

Not *lost* their faces. Not *hid* their faces. Had none, the way a stone has no face, the way a duty has no face. Sable draws them: tall shapes, smooth where features should be, robes that are not robes but the idea of robes rendered by someone who has only ever seen robes from far away. She gives them no mouths because they did not speak. She gives them no eyes because they did not need to see. They knew where everything was the way a lock knows its key.

Hands, though. Hands they had. So many hands.

She fills a whole ledger page with hands. Hands folded, hands raised, hands pressed flat against stone. Dozens of figures, each with too many arms branching off them like winter trees, and every hand doing something small and endless: turning a wheel, holding shut a lid, pressing a seam closed, stitching, stitching, stitching.

*They hold it together,* she says. *All of it. They hold the seams.*

Juniper asks what seams. She looks at him the way you look at a fish that has asked about water.

*The world has seams,* she says, in her broken half of a language that used to be whole. *Where the country was cut away. Someone cut it out — cut us out — and the cutting leaves edges, and edges leak, and the Sealers press the edges so nothing leaks out and nothing leaks in.* Her voice does something complicated around the word Sealers, half reverence and half the specific terror children save for the things that love them wrongly. *They are not cruel. That is the worst thing. They are not cruel. They are only certain.*

---

Here the account becomes difficult, and Juniper's notes grow sparse, because Sable stops narrating and starts showing.

She takes his hand — the first time she has initiated touch — and presses it flat against the floorboards of the waystation, over the door she drew days ago, the one that wasn't there before and hasn't faded. Under his palm he feels something he will never be able to explain properly to Maren or to anyone: a rhythm. Slow. Enormous. Like feeling a heartbeat through the hull of a ship too large to see.

*That is Vess sleeping,* she says. *Under everything. He is not dead. Dead would be kinder. He dreams, and when he dreams badly, the Sealers must work faster, and when they work faster, the seams pull, and the pulling makes holes, and the holes make—*

She stops. She goes to the window. She watches the snow for a long time.

*Holes make me,* she finishes, without turning around.

---

She was born from a hole. She says it plainly, the way children say terrible things plainly, because they do not yet know that adults require these things wrapped in cloth.

*A hole opened near the last prayer. The last prayer was my mother's — no. Not mother. Not-mother. The one who prayed me.* Charcoal scratches: a woman kneeling, hands raised, mouth open, drawn with more skill than anything else Sable has made, because this image she has carried whole. *When the kingdom went under the fold, some of us stayed awake inside it. Frozen mid-life — you saw them, you saw the market, the man with the bread, they are not dead, they are *held* — and the ones who stayed awake could not stay awake forever. It costs. Waking costs. My not-mother spent her waking on one prayer, all of it, every year she had, poured into words.*

*What did she pray for?*

Sable turns around. Her face in the firelight is very old for a moment and then very young again.

*She prayed for a key that could walk out. Because keys cannot walk. Doors cannot walk. But a child can walk, and if the child *is* the door—* She shrugs, a small sharp motion. *So they made me. Not born. Made. Out of prayer and out of hole-leak and out of two hundred years of everyone dreaming the same dream at once, which is a heavy thing to build a girl out of. I am heavy,* she adds, conversationally, patting her own chest. *You cannot tell. But I am very heavy.*

---

And then the Sealers noticed.

This part she tells fastest, the way you tear off a bandage, and Juniper's transcription falls behind, and later Maren will read his scrawled fragments and understand why his handwriting shakes.

The Sealers are custodians. Custodians do not permit exits. A key that walks is an exit with legs. When Sable came out through the fold — small enough, made small enough, folded up like paper into the shape of an ordinary mud-caked orphan — the Sealers could not follow. Too big. Too bound. Every hand they have is committed to holding the prison closed. To leave their post is to let the whole thing unravel; the god wakes; the grief-rage wakes with him; the new kingdoms that were built on the erased ground burn the way the old one burned, except this time there is no one faithful enough, foolish enough, desperate enough to seal him again.

So they sent something smaller.

Sable draws it and then, halfway through, stops drawing and sits on her hands.

It has no face either, she says. But its facelessness is different. The Sealers' blankness is peace. This thing's blankness is hunger wearing a mask it found somewhere. It has hands too — everything in the old country has hands — but its hands end in points, and it does not hold, it *takes*. It followed her out of the hole before the seam closed. It has been following her since. It walks roads the way water walks downhill, patient, always patient, and it knows her the way a hook knows the shape of a mouth.

*How long has it been following you?*

*Since the mountain pass. Before the waystation. Since—* She counts on her fingers, loses count, frowns. *A long time. It is slow because I am heavy. Carrying me is hard. It wants to carry me home.*

Home, in her mouth, means the dark. Means the fold. Means being a door instead of a girl.

*And if it catches you?*

She considers this honestly, weighing it, a scholar of her own doom.

*If it catches me, it carries me back and puts me in the wall where I fit, and the door closes, and everyone stays frozen forever, and Vess keeps sleeping, and the Crown keeps its clean maps, and nobody is happy but everybody continues.* A pause. Fire pops. Snow ticks against the shutter like fingers. *Or it breaks me, because I have been walking too long and doors crack when they walk. If I break, everything opens at once. Him. All of it. No one holding the seams.*

*Which is worse?*

*Both are the same thing,* Sable says, *wearing different coats.*

---

Near dawn she does the last drawing, and this one she does facing away from the others, hunched over it like a animal guarding food, and Juniper only sees it because he rises quietly and she is too deep in the work to notice.

It is a picture of the waystation. Their waystation — she has gotten the crooked chimney right, and Maren's axe leaning by the door, and even the mule, unflatteringly. Around it she has drawn the mountains. And threaded through the mountains, in lines of charcoal pressed so hard the paper shines, she has drawn roads.

Roads Juniper knows.

Roads he has dreamed since he was small enough to believe dreams were instructions. Roads his grandmother hummed about. Roads that are not on any map the Crown prints, because the Crown does not print maps of places it says never existed.

And on each road, coming from every direction, walking with the terrible patience of water going downhill: hands. Dozens of hands. Hundreds. Small at first, distant, weeks away perhaps. But all of them pointing here. All of them closing.

At the bottom of the page, in her fractured, hard-won trade-tongue, she has written the longest sentence Juniper has yet heard from her, each word set down like a stone across a stream:

THEY ARE NOT ANGRY. THEY ARE NOT CRUEL. THEY ONLY WANT THE WORLD TO STAY CLOSED. THAT IS WHY YOU MUST CHOOSE FAST. CLOSED IS EASY. OPEN COSTS EVERYTHING AND EVERYONE AND IT COSTS ME MOST OF ALL BUT I CAME ANYWAY BECAUSE MY NOT-MOTHER PRAYED FOR A KEY THAT COULD WALK AND I AM WALKING.

Below that, smaller:

I am sorry about your friends. More of them will sell you. That is what fear does. It is not their fault. Fear is also a kind of many hands.

* * *

Juniper stands in the doorway of the common room with the pages in his fist and finds Maren already awake, already dressed, already watching him with an expression he has learned, over ten silent years, to dread — the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't want to finish.

"Outside," she says quietly. "Now. We have a problem, and it isn't the one the child drew."
