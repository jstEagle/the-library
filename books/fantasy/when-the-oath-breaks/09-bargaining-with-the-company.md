## 9. Bargaining with the Company

Maren counted them by firelight the way a shepherd counts a dwindling flock: not with hope, but with the grim arithmetic of loss.

Fourteen. There had been forty-two when Vess went silent, ten years and one season past. Forty-two sworn blades, forty2 oath-marks inked on forty-2 forearms, all of them warm once with the low hum of a god's attention—like standing near a hearth you couldn't see but could feel on your face. Now fourteen sat or stood around the hollowed-out shell of an old watchtower in the Greymaws, and the fire was small because Halvard had said woodsmoke carried, and Halvard was usually right about the things that got you killed.

She knew what they were going to do before anyone said it. You spend enough years reading mercenaries across a campfire and you learn to read the weather of their faces. Tonight the weather had turned.

Dree spoke first, because Dree always spoke first when the thing needed saying and no one else wanted to sully their mouth with it. She was the youngest of the veterans, sharp-faced, her oath-mark scarred over with a knife-wound she'd gotten falling down a mountain two winters back—a wound that would have closed in a breath, once, if Vess had still been listening.

"We have to talk about the girl."

Maren didn't move. She kept her hands where they were, turning a whetstone along the flat of her sword in long, even strokes, because that was what you did when you wanted your body to say something your voice wasn't ready to.

"There's nothing to talk about," said Tammas. He was older than the mountains felt, and he'd stopped believing in anything around the same time the rest of them had. "Juniper's made his decision."

"Juniper's made *a* decision." Dree spat into the fire. "He's made the decision of a man who's been talking to his grandmother's ghost for twenty years. The Inquisitor knows where we are. Or close enough. You've all seen the riders in the valley. That's Crown steel down there, and we're sitting up here like crows on a dead tree, guarding a child who draws doors on floors."

"She saved us," Tammas said. "The pass. When Grey's outriders came up the goat trail, she—"

"She did something." Dree's voice dropped, and that was worse than shouting. Maren marked it. "We don't know what she is. We don't know what she *did*. And I know what I saw in the village before we ran: a man in grey burning books that were three hundred years old and calling it mercy. That man has a warrant with our names on it now. All of ours. And he also has coin."

The word landed on the fire like a stone on a bird's nest. Everything scattered inside Maren.

"Coin," she said. Her voice came out flatter than she intended.

"Coin." Dree turned to face her, and Maren gave her the courtesy of looking back. Around the fire, the other eleven had gone very still, the way men do when the thing they've all been thinking finally crawls out into the light and everyone pretends to be surprised. "Grey will pay for route knowledge. Everyone knows the company holds the smuggler roads through Thornfell—the passes, the caches, the ways that aren't on any map. We could sell him the roads and walk away rich. Walk away *alive*. Or we can sit here until he brings enough soldiers to take what he wants off our corpses."

"That knowledge isn't ours to sell," Maren said.

"It stopped being anyone's when our god went deaf!" Dree was on her feet now. Firelight jumped along her jaw. "Ten years, Maren. Ten years of eating rats in the winter and taking contracts that scrape the bottom of shame, and for what? An oath to a god who doesn't answer? You've done the ritual a thousand times. Don't tell me your arm doesn't feel like a lie every time you raise it."

It did. God help her, it did. Maren looked down at her forearm, at the oath-mark that had faded from silver-grey to the color of old milk, and she hated Dree a little for saying it out loud, and loved her a little too, because someone had to say it.

"The roads aren't ours," Maren said again, quieter. "They're Juniper's. His grandmother cut half of them herself. If we sell them out from under him—"

"If we *don't*, we die under him." Dree looked around the fire, gathering the others' eyes the way a potter gathers clay. "Half of us already think so. Brannick. The Ostler twins. Kell and Sorrel. They've already been down the mountain once this week. You think they were picking herbs?"

Nobody answered. The fire popped. Somewhere far below, wind moved through the valley like a long exhalation, the sound the Greymaws made when they were deciding something about the people clinging to their bones.

Maren set down her whetstone. Very carefully, the way her mother had taught her to set down anything that might become a weapon.

"Then say it plain," she said. "Here, in front of everyone. Say you want to sell the child."

"I never said sell the *child*."

"You didn't have to. Selling the roads sells her. Grey follows our routes straight to whatever door she's leading Juniper toward, and whatever happens to her then happens because we drew him the map." Maren rose. Not fast. She'd learned long ago that standing up slowly frightened people more than standing up quickly; it gave them time to understand what was coming. "So say it. Say you'll trade a child's life for fourteen purses of Crown silver, and let's see how many of us can hold our heads up on the road after."

Dree's jaw worked. For a moment—one long heartbeat—Maren thought the girl would say it, would commit to it in front of witnesses and force the split into the open. It might have been kinder. A wound opened cleanly bleeds less than one left to fester.

But Dree looked away first.

"We should vote," someone said from the dark edge of the firelight. Kell. Of course it was Kell.

"There's nothing to vote on," Maren said. "Not yet. Not while Juniper lives and the oath still stands in his name."

"The oath doesn't *stand* anywhere," Tammas murmured, not unkindly. "That's the whole trouble, girl. It's a bridge with no river under it. We keep crossing it out of habit."

Maren looked at the twelve faces ringed in firelight—fourteen counting herself and the boy on watch above—and understood, with the particular clarity that comes only when you've been dreading a thing so long that its arrival feels almost like relief, that the company was already broken. What happened next was just paperwork. Just ceremony. The rot had gone through the beam years ago; tonight was merely the day the roof noticed.

"Then go," she said. "Anyone who wants to ride down and bargain with the Inquisitor—go. I won't stop you. I won't even hate you. But you'll go knowing you're selling more than roads. And those who stay—" she looked at Tammas, at Halvard's empty place on the wall above, at old Wren who hadn't spoken since the waystation— "those who stay stay whole. No half-oaths. No riding down to sell and riding back up to share the purse. Choose."

Silence, except the fire.

Then Brannick stood, big and slow as a bear waking, and wouldn't meet her eyes, and that told her everything. Then the Ostler twins stood together, because even in betrayal they moved as one creature. Kell and Sorrel followed, and then Dree, last, chin up, eyes wet and furious about being wet.

"Seven," Dree said. "And you?"

Maren looked at the fire for a long moment. She thought about the first time she'd seen Juniper Okafor, nineteen years old and trying very hard to look like he wasn't terrified, holding his new-sworn contract with both hands like it was a live coal. She thought about ten years of shared rations and shared graves. She thought about a thing she had never said and would never say, which had no name she was willing to give it, which lived in her chest the way the oath-mark lived on her arm—faded, maybe, but never gone.

"I stay," she said.

Tammas stayed. Wren stayed. Little Ferro, who was seventeen and worshipped the ground Maren walked on and would have followed her into the sea. Five. Five of them against seven walking into the dark with torches, down toward the valley where the Crown's banners hung limp in the windless cold.

Dree paused at the edge of the firelight. Whatever she meant to say—and Maren saw the shape of several sentences cross her face and die—in the end she only said, "I hope he's worth it," and was gone.

Maren sat back down and picked up her whetstone and did not cry, because the remaining four were watching her, and a second does not cry in front of the remnants of a company. She ran the stone along the blade. Along the blade. Along the blade.

*Worth it.* The words turned over in her like stones in a riverbed, wearing smooth.

He wasn't. That was the honest answer, the one she'd never given anyone: no man was worth seven lives and a company and whatever passed for her soul these days. But worth wasn't the shape of the thing. Worth was merchant's math, and what she felt about Juniper Okafor had never once balanced a ledger. It was closer to duty, if duty were a fever. Closer to faith, if faith had a face and laughed at its own jokes and carried grief around like a coat it refused to take off even in summer.

She had watched him perform the dead rituals for ten years, hands moving through gestures to a god who had stopped listening, hiding the hollowness behind that careful novelist's calm he wore like armor, and every single time, some traitorous part of her had thought: *he is doing this alone so the rest of us don't have to see it fail.* She had loved him for that. Quietly. Without signature or seal, the way you love something you're certain you'll lose.

*You already lost the company tonight,* she told the fire. *Whatever's left, you hold together with your two hands and nothing else.*

Above her, boots on the wall. Halvard came down the ladder, folded himself beside her, and handed her a strip of dried meat without a word, which was his version of a eulogy.

"They're gone," he said.

"They're gone."

"Grey will use them within three days. Sooner, if he's smart."

"He's smart," Maren said. "That's the whole problem with him."

Halvard chewed. Wren slept sitting up, as she always did, hands folded like a woman in church. Ferro had fallen asleep mid-sentence somewhere around midnight, the way the young did, as if the world were safe simply because they were tired.

"Five of us," Halvard said at last. "Against an Inquisitor, seven traitors who know every cache we own, and whatever the Crown sends behind him."

"Five," Maren agreed.

"And yet here we sit, chewing jerky."

"Here we sit."

Halvard studied her with those flat grey eyes that missed nothing and commented on less. "You could have gone with them," he said. "No one would have blamed you. *I* nearly went."

"I know."

"So why didn't you?"

Because there is a man up a smugglers' road right now, she did not say, holding a child's hand and following a dream he's had since he was a boy, and he is walking into the one place in the world the Crown wants buried more than it wants us dead. Because someone has to stand between him and what's coming, and I have spent nineteen years being that someone, and I don't know how to stop being it without stopping everything. Because there is a word for what he is to me and I have never once let myself say it, and I am not going to start saying it now, to you, over jerky, the night my family walked out.

"Someone has to carry the whetstone," she said instead.

Halvard almost smiled. It was the closest he came to laughter, and she'd learned to treasure it the way misers treasure bent coins.

They kept the fire small. Maren took first watch because sleep was a country she couldn't reach tonight. She climbed to the wall and looked north, up the hidden road, the road that wasn't on maps, where Juniper and Sable had vanished three days ago into the high passes with Wren's blessing and none of hers.

The stars over the Greymaws were hard and close. Below, in the valley, she could see the far glimmer of the Crown camp—and, nearer, a single lantern moving up the switchbacks ahead of schedule. Too soon. Days too soon. Grey wasn't waiting to consolidate; he was moving *now*, and moving fast, and Maren felt the truth of it land in her stomach like swallowed ice:

The seven hadn't sold route knowledge.

They'd sold urgency. They'd told him where the door was.

Maren was already sliding down the ladder, already kicking Ferro awake, already hearing her own voice come out in the flat, hard register she reserved for things that could not be survived twice.

"Get up. All of you. We ride for the standing stone tonight—and gods forgive us, I think we're already late."
