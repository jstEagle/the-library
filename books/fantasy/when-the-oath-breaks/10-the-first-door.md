## 10. The First Door

They walked out of the high country the way ghosts leave a house—through walls no one had bothered to build.

For three days Sable had been leading them, and for three days Juniper had let her, which was its own kind of madness. He was a smuggler. He had spent twenty years learning every goat track, every collapsed mine adit, every smuggler's stair cut into the Thornfell scree by hands that predated the Crown's surveyors. He knew these mountains the way a man knows his own mouth—by touch, by dark, by the ache in old teeth before weather turned. And none of it explained how a mud-caked child who could not have seen her ninth winter kept turning left at forks he would have sworn led nowhere.

"Left," she said again now, in her broken trade-tongue, and pointed with a stick she carried like a scepter.

"There's nothing left of that path but a memory," Maren said behind him. Her voice was flat the way it went flat when she was frightened and refusing to be. "I've run this ridge twice. It ends in a washout."

"It doesn't end," Sable said, and then said something in Old Thornic that slid past understanding and lodged somewhere under his ribs, warm as a swallowed coal.

Juniper looked at the place she was pointing. The trail did end—he could see it, the scar of an old switchback crumbling into a gully choked with alder and grey stone. But looking at it made his eyes water slightly, the way looking at the sun through closed lids does, and beneath the watering there was something else. A tug. The same sensation he'd had since childhood, the dream-feeling: walking a road at dusk with the smell of bread and cut hay on the wind, and someone ahead calling a name that was almost his name, and never, in thirty years of dreaming, arriving.

He dreamed those roads. He had told no one but his grandmother, dead now twenty years, and she had gone very still when he described them, and then she had prayed over him in the cursed tongue until dawn.

"Juniper." Maren's hand on his arm. "The Inquisitor's outriders are two valleys back. We don't have time to follow a child's fancy down a washout."

"We don't have time *not* to," he said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it a little—the certainty in his own voice, borrowed from a god who hadn't spoken to him in ten years. But his feet were already moving, carrying him down the crumbled switchback with the sureness of a man descending stairs he'd climbed ten thousand times, though his eyes told him each step should break his ankle.

The steps didn't break. They held. Each one held exactly where his foot expected stone, and where his foot found nothing, there was suddenly something—a ledge widening out of loose shale, a root thick as a wrist where the alders grew too thin to make one. Behind him he heard Maren swear softly, and heard the company follow: Doss breathing hard, old Wren muttering the soldier's psalm to no god in particular, the twins silent as they'd been silent since Grey burned the archive village and half their number with it in spirit.

Sable walked beside him without hurrying. She didn't look at the trail. She looked *through* it, the way you look through a window at something on the other side, and once she reached out and touched empty air the way you'd steady yourself on a doorframe.

"What do you see?" he asked her.

She considered the question with terrible seriousness. "The road is sleeping," she said. "Not gone. Sleeping things can wake."

The gully bottom was cold and smelled of wet fern and older things underneath, a sweetness like apples going soft in a cellar. The light changed. That was the first wrongness he could name afterward: the light came down through the canopy green-gold and slanting, honeyed, the light of late afternoon in high summer—and it was morning, and the season was three weeks from first frost. Maren noticed it too; he saw her stop and turn her face up into it, and her expression was the expression of a woman tasting wine she'd been told was water.

Then the standing stone.

It rose out of the gully wall like a tooth out of a gum—no, not rising; it had always been there, and everything else had grown around it and failed to mention it. Twice a man's height, leaning slightly east, furred with lichen the color of old brass. Its face had been carved once. Weather had eaten the figures down to suggestions: a row of kneeling shapes, and above them a shape standing with arms wide, and above that a circle with rays—or chains?—falling from it. His oath-mark, worn smooth and huge, the mark he bore on his inner forearm rendered in stone the size of a cart wheel.

Sable pressed both palms against it.

"Maren," Juniper said quietly, "get everyone behind me."

"Why?"

"Because I think we're about to do something the Crown hanged men for even mentioning."

Maren looked at the stone, at the kneeling figures, at the circle-and-chains. She'd grown up south of the Thorn, where they still whispered about the Erasure the way you whisper about plague. She moved the company back without another word.

Sable spoke into the stone. Not loudly. The way you speak to someone sleeping, close to the ear, careful not to startle. Old Thornic poured out of her, fluent now in a way it never was in trade-speech, as if the language were her mother tongue and everything else the translation. Juniper caught only fragments—his grandmother's prayers had given him maybe forty words of the tongue—but he caught *vess*, and he caught *dor*, and he caught a word he didn't know that made the hair on his forearms stand up, because he somehow knew what it meant anyway: *remember*.

The stone remembered.

Light didn't pour out so much as arrive—there was no seam, no flash, no thunderclap. One moment the gully wall stood behind the stone, fern-grey and dripping; the next, the wall was a valley.

That is the only honest way to say it. The wall became a valley. Sunlight fell across a floor of deep grass starred with white flowers, running down between orchard rows heavy with fruit out of season, toward rooftops—thatch and timber and slate, smoke rising from chimneys, real smoke, woodsmoke—and beyond them a river the color of hammered tin, and beyond the river terraced fields climbing a hillside to a watchtower flying a banner Juniper knew from his grandmother's prayer book, the circle-and-rays stitched in gold on green.

No map showed this valley. No map *could* show it; Juniper understood that viscerally, the way you understand a trapdoor in your own floor. The Crown's surveyors had walked within miles of this place, had drawn the gully, had named the ridge after some minor noble, and the valley had sat inside the stone like a seed inside a hull, patient for two hundred years.

Nobody moved. Even the wind seemed to hold itself.

Then the smell reached them. Bread. Cut hay. Woodsmoke and ripening apples and river-water and, faintly, incense—the temple-smell of his childhood, myrrh and something resinous, the smell that meant Vess's altar had been tended. His knees nearly went. Thirty years of dreams had prepared him for the sight of it and not at all for the smell.

"It's real," Doss whispered. "Saints' teeth, it's actually—it's *real*—"

"Don't touch anything yet," Maren said, but her voice shook, and her hand had gone to the medallion at her throat, the little tin Vess-disc every mercenary of the company wore, dull with ten years of unanswered polishings.

Juniper stepped forward. The threshold was a line in the grass, invisible except that he could feel it against his shins the way you feel the change from flagstone to earth underfoot in the dark. He stood with one boot in the gully and one boot in the valley and waited for something to happen—alarm bells, a curse, the world folding shut. Nothing happened. The sunlight lay on his boot. An apple tree dropped a ripe fruit somewhere to his left with a sound like a sigh.

"The country remembers me," he said, not meaning to say it aloud.

"You've never been here," Maren said.

"I know." He looked down at the grass under his planted foot. It bent away from his sole and sprang back. Ordinary grass. Impossible grass. "I've never been here, and it knows my weight."

Sable slipped past him through the threshold without hesitation, the way children cross any doorway, and stood in the tall flowers with the light all around her, and for the first time since she'd knocked on the waystation door in the dead season she looked—Juniper searched for the word and could only find it in Old Thornic—*home*. She turned in a slow circle, arms out, face lifted, and laughed. It was the first time he'd heard her laugh. It went through the company like a blade goes through silk.

Wren was crying. The old soldier didn't seem to know it; tears just ran down the seams of his weathered face while he stared at the watchtower and the banner. "My grandfather," he said stupidly. "My grandfather used to sing songs about—about the harvest festival, about the river fair, we thought they were just songs, we thought—" He stopped, because there was nothing past that thought that could be said standing up.

The vertigo came for Juniper then, and it came all at once.

It arrived not as dizziness but as scale. He stood in a doorway and understood, suddenly and completely, that the world was not the shape the Crown said it was—that the maps in the royal cartographers' hall, the maps taught to schoolchildren, the borders paid for in blood for six generations, were drawings laid over a body whose actual anatomy no living person knew. If this valley fit inside a standing stone in a nameless gully, what else fit where? How much of the world was sleeping? How many stones stood in how many gullies, holding whole countries like breath held in a chest? And if the kingdom had been erased—not conquered, not destroyed, *erased*, unwritten from every record and every mind—then what did that make the Crown? What did it make every oath of fealty Juniper had ever watched men swear on altars built over the grave of something older?

His hand found the oath-mark on his forearm through his sleeve. Under the cloth, the ink-wrought sigil felt warm. Warmer than skin. Warmer than it had ever felt in ten silent years, as if proximity to the god's country were waking something in it, or in him.

"Captain." Maren, low, at his shoulder. She'd come through the threshold too; they all had, drawn across one by one like iron filings after a magnet. "Tell me you're thinking what I'm thinking so I don't have to say it."

"I'm thinking we're standing in the single greatest crime ever committed by a crown," Juniper said, "and that nobody has ever been punished for it because officially it never happened."

"I'm thinking about the price on our heads," she said. "And the fact that Grey is two valleys back. And that this—" she gestured, and her gesture took in the orchards, the rooftops, the smoke, the banner, everything, "—this is worth more than every warrant in the Chancellery. Kings have gone to war over less than a valley that appears on no map. Empires have fallen for less. And we're eight tired mercenaries with a child who opens doors."

"Nine," said Doss. "Tolliver's watching the horses."

"Tolliver's horses are dead weight if Grey finds our camp," Maren said, and Juniper heard what lived under her practicality: fear. Not of Grey. Of this. Of hope, maybe. Hope was the most dangerous thing a mercenary could carry; it got men killed faster than bad luck or bad wine.

But Sable had stopped turning. She was standing very still now, head cocked, listening to something in the valley's sound—the river, perhaps, or the wind in the orchards—and her laughter had drained away, replaced by an expression Juniper had last seen on the face of a man reading his own death-warrant.

"What is it?" he asked, crouching to her level. "What's wrong?"

She pointed down the valley, past the orchards, past the rooftops, to where the smoke rose.

"People," she said. Then, in Old Thornic, a longer phrase, and though he couldn't parse it, the tone needed no translation. It was the tone of a child saying: *but they aren't moving.*

Juniper straightened. He shaded his eyes against the impossible afternoon light and looked where she pointed, really looked, past the prettiness to the particulars. The smoke rose straight and unmoving, plume-frozen, like smoke painted on glass. The figure in the nearest field—yes, there was a figure, a woman bent over a vegetable row with a basket—had not shifted her weight between one blink and the next. On the road by the river, a cart stood mid-turn, its ox mid-step, its driver's whip raised forever at the top of its arc.

Two hundred years, the whispers said. Two hundred years since the Erasure. Whatever lived here had not lived through it. It had been *stopped*—paused mid-life like a candle flame photographed at its brightest, waiting for a wind that had never come.

"They're alive," Sable said softly, in trade-tongue, translating herself now, choosing words with care. "Sleeping-things. Like the road." She looked up at Juniper, and her eyes were ancient and nine years old at once. "You woke the door. Now they will start waking too. Slowly. Like ice." A pause, and then, with the terrible directness of children: "Some of them are going to be very confused."

Behind them, back through the threshold, the gully wind moved the ferns with a dry rattle, and somewhere far off—two valleys off, maybe three, or closer than that now, closer than comfort—Juniper thought he heard a horn. One long note. The Crown's outriders' signal for *found something*.

Or perhaps for *found them*.

He stepped fully into the valley, drew his knife, and cut the air at the threshold the way smugglers sealed a door behind them—an old gesture, a warding, half habit and half prayer. The light of the standing stone dimmed to a shimmer, like heat-haze, like a held breath.

"Company," he said. "Form up. We're going down to meet the neighbors."
