## 12. Grey's Bargain

The interrogations were held in the tithe-barn at Hollowmere, because it was the only building in the village large enough to hold a table, and Inquisitor Grey believed that all honest work began with a table. A surface on which documents could be laid flat. A plane of order imposed upon a world that had, of late, shown an unseemly tendency toward folds and hidden seams.

He had four of them now. Three mercenaries and a farrier who had once shod their horses and talked too much over the anvil. The mercenaries sat in a row on grain sacks with their wrists bound in front of them—not behind; Grey had learned long ago that a man whose hands hung useless before him felt more like a debtor than a prisoner, and debtors answered questions—while Corporal Vess-keep-it-away-from-me Illich took notes in the Crown's cipher, his pen scratching like a mouse in a wall.

Outside, the snow came down in the fat, indifferent flakes of late winter, filling the wheel ruts, erasing the road by which Grey's warrant-party had arrived. He found this faintly offensive. Roads should persist. That was their purpose.

"Name," he said to the first of them.

"Bren Aldous."

"Rank in the Okafor company."

"Was spear-second. Now I mostly cook."

Grey laid a document flat on the table. It was one of three warrants he carried, each written in a different dead alphabet—the Old Thornic angular script, its cursive descendant, and the seal-hand of the pre-Conquest chancery—and beneath each script, in careful modern gloss, the same sentence: *Let no door be opened; let no name be spoken; let the silence be kept.* He had copied the gloss himself, twice, because the first time his hand had shaken, and Grey did not permit his hand to shake.

"You were paid," he said, "in coin struck before the Conquest. Coin that has not circulated in two hundred years. Explain."

Bren Aldous looked at the coin Grey set beside the warrant—a square-cut piece, black with age, showing a face worn nearly smooth—and laughed, short and unhappy. "We didn't get paid, sir. That's rather the point of us. Ten years back the god stopped answering, and the pay stopped with Him. We keep the contracts anyway. Habit." He turned the coin over with bound fingers, gently, the way you'd turn a dead bird. "Where'd you get this?"

"A village archive. I burned it."

The laugh died. Something moved through the barn then, through all four prisoners at once—a flinch, a settling, the way a flock goes still when the hawk's shadow crosses. Grey noted it without satisfaction. Fear was data. He took no pleasure in it, any more than a surveyor takes pleasure in the fall of a plumb line.

"Corporal," he said, "read back what the farrier told us about the child."

Illich read: *"Small girl, dark-skinned, maybe eight years, speaks a language none of them knew except Okafor, who claims his grandmother taught him bits. She drew a door on the floorboards of the waystation at Cold Saddle. The door bled when touched. Okafor took her south along the drover roads after your lordship burned Marrowfield, and half the company sold you the routes for pardons, which is why we're all sitting here, and may every god who's still listening forgive me, because Maren stayed, and Maren would have stayed for anyone."*

"That last part is editorial," Grey observed.

"Yes, my lord."

"Leave it. Editorial content is often where the truth hides." He rose, went to the barn's one window, and watched the snow bury the road. Behind him the prisoners shifted. He let them. Silence was also a tool, though they used the word differently in his trade than anyone else used it anywhere else in the world.

Here was what Grey knew, assembled from burned archives and frightened villagers and a decade of quiet correspondence with men who owed him favors: that before the Conquest there had been a kingdom called Thornfell, and that it was not conquered but *removed*—every map scraped, every chronicle excised, every stone boundary-marker toppled into bogs—in a single season, by decree of the first King Aurel, in the year the histories simply stopped pretending to count. That the god Vess, whose worship had been the kingdom's spine, had gone silent at the same hour. That the Crown called this the Founding Peace, and that Grey had spent eleven years learning what the word *peace* was doing in that phrase.

And now a child spoke the erased tongue as her mother-tongue, and doors were bleeding out of floors, and a mercenary with a smuggler's memory for unmapped roads was walking her south through country that, according to every chart in the royal cartography office, did not exist.

Grey had burned Marrowfield's archive as precaution. He thought about that fire sometimes—not with guilt, exactly; the books had been lists of names, and names were how the old thing remembered itself—but with the particular weariness of a man sweeping a floor that keeps growing dust. You could burn every record in the Marches and it would buy a generation, perhaps two. The past was patient in a way institutions were not. Institutions wanted results within a fiscal quarter. The past could wait under a mountain for two hundred years and then send up a child.

"My lord," said Bren Aldous, into the silence. "You're going to kill her, aren't you. The girl."

"No," Grey said, and turned around, and made sure they saw his face do nothing at all. "I am going to offer to save her. Whether she survives the saving depends largely on Juniper Okafor, and on whether he loves that child more than he loves being right."

* * *

They brought Okafor in at dusk.

Not dragged—he walked, hands bound, between two of Grey's troopers, and Grey watched him take inventory of the barn the way a professional does: exits, numbers, the corporal's pen, the brazier, the sacks holding his people. His eyes passed over Bren Aldous and the others with something that was not quite surprise and not quite grief. He had known, then. Half his company had sold him, and he had known, and he had come anyway, or been taken anyway, and either way he stood in the doorway of the tithe-barn like a man arriving at a funeral he'd helped plan.

"Sit," Grey said.

Okafor sat. Up close he was younger than his reputation—thirty, perhaps thirty-two—with the weathered look of a man who had spent a decade outdoors and a longer decade indoors, grieving. There was a mark on the inside of his left wrist, half-hidden by the binding: a knot-work sigil, inked or branded, that Grey recognized from the third warrant's marginalia. An oath-mark. A contract-signature written on flesh.

"Inquisitor," Okafor said. His voice was level. "You burned Marrowfield."

"I did."

"There were children's primers in that archive. Thornic primers. The last ones in the world."

"There are primers in this barn," Grey said, and tapped the table. "In my head. I have spent eleven years memorizing a language whose last native speaker was supposed to be two centuries dead, so that if it ever surfaced again I would know what was surfacing. Do not lecture me about preservation, Captain. I am the only institution in the Crown currently practicing it."

Something flickered in Okafor's face—recalculation. Grey had seen it before, in better liars: the moment a man realizes the cold official across the table has done more homework than he has.

"The girl," Grey continued. "Her name is Sable. She is approximately eight years old. She fled something she calls the many-handed ones, which my sources call Sealers, which the third warrant calls *custodians of the silence*. She draws doors that bleed. And she is walking, or being walked, toward the standing stones above the Vale of Erren—which appears on no map because there is no Vale of Erren anymore, and yet your grandmother left you prayers addressed to it." He folded his hands. "Tell me where I'm wrong."

For a long moment Okafor said nothing. The brazier popped. Outside, the snow went on falling with the patience of something that had been falling since before the Conquest and intended to go on falling after.

"She's just a child," Okafor said finally.

"Yes," Grey said. "That is precisely the horror of it, and I am glad you see it, because it means we can skip past the part where you pretend I'm a monster and I pretend you're a fool, and arrive at the actual matter." He drew a second document from his case and laid it beside the warrants. This one was fresh ink, Crown seal, blank in the particulars. "Amnesty. Full pardon for you, for Maren Kestrel, for any of your people who didn't sell you—which, incidentally, was a filthy thing done to you, and I say that as the man who bought it. Pardon, back-pay computed against your original contract with—" he touched the oath-mark's image in the margin, "—Him. And the girl walks out of this under Crown wardship. Not a cell. Wardship. Tutors, physicians, a household in the capital. She grows up warm and fed and *ignorant*, Captain, which is the kindest thing anyone will ever be able to give her."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange you stop walking." Grey leaned forward. "You tell me where the stones are—or better, you never tell me, you simply never go—and you help me close what's open. There are procedures. Old ones. The first King's chancery wrote them down before it wrote everything else down, and I have them. Sealing the doors permanently. The god stays buried. The kingdom stays dead. The girl stays alive."

"You think she's the key."

"I think she's the *lock*, and locks exist to be locked." Grey heard his own voice flatten further, the way it did when he was near the center of the thing, and he let it. "Captain. I have read the sealed correspondence of King Aurel. I know why your god went silent, and I know it wasn't death. And I know—if you've gotten as far as I think you've gotten—that what's behind those doors isn't a sleeping king waiting to be woken with a kiss. It is a grief the size of a nation, and grief that size does not wake up grateful. It wakes up *owed*."

Okafor's jaw tightened. "She told me things. About what's keeping it shut."

"Then you know the cost of opening it isn't paid by kings."

The silence stretched. In it, Grey did something he almost never did: he spoke off the document. He set down the amnesty, set down the warrants, and put his own hands flat on the table where the mercenary could see them, empty.

"I am not cruel, Captain. I want you to understand that, because everyone in this barn thinks it and no one will say it. Cruelty is inefficient. I have burned one archive in eleven years and I remember the smell of it every day, and I would burn a hundred more tomorrow if the arithmetic demanded it, and neither of those facts makes me a monster—they make me a man who has read what happens when the arithmetic is ignored." He paused. "Two hundred years ago, faithful men sealed their own god into a box with a whole country, because the alternative was watching Him unmake the world that had failed Him. They were not villains. They were the only adults in the room. I intend to be the next one."

"And the girl? When she's sealed again. If she's the lock." Okafor's voice cracked on it, just barely, and Grey marked the crack and did not exploit it. Not yet. "What happens to her?"

Grey looked at the fire for a moment. When he answered, he made himself answer truly, because a lie here would unravel later, and Grey did not build with rotten timber.

"I don't know. The procedures don't say. They were written by men who sealed a god and never had to seal a child, and I have spent three nights trying to find a version of this that ends with her whole, and I haven't found it yet." He met Okafor's eyes. "But I will keep looking. That is my offer, Captain. Not certainty. Effort. The Crown's entire weight bent toward finding a mercy that may not exist—versus a smuggler's hope that the door opens onto something kind. Weigh them honestly."

Okafor looked down at his bound hands, at the oath-mark showing beneath the rope like a scar deciding whether to speak. When he raised his head, his eyes were wet and furious and terribly awake.

"You're offering me a bargain," he said slowly, "where the price is that everything my grandmother prayed to stays dead forever. And you're calling it mercy."

"I'm calling it the only mercy on the table," Grey said. "Which is how most mercy arrives."

Somewhere outside, down the buried road, a horse screamed—short, cut off—and one of the sentries shouted, and the barn door banged open on snow and torchlight and Maren Kestrel standing in it with a crossbow and blood on her sleeve, saying, "Juniper. Love. We need to go *now*."

Grey did not reach for a weapon. He reached, instead, for the amnesty, and held it out across the table into all that wind and noise—an offer, still open, still flat, still orderly—as the world came apart around it.

"Captain," he said. "The door is open. Isn't it."
