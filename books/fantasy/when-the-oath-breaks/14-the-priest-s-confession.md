## 14. The Priest's Confession

The priest was dying the way old ice dies—slowly, from within, in a silence you could hear if you pressed your ear to it.

Juniper found him in the chapel behind the temple of the frozen square, where the survivors of the pocket-kingdom stood mid-motion like figures in amber: a woman with a jug half-tilted toward a trough, two boys frozen in the middle of a game of knucklebones, an old man forever reaching for a door that had been shut on him for two hundred years. The chapel still smelled faintly of incense, though the incense had burned out before Juniper's grandmother's grandmother drew her first breath.

Brother Aldous—for that was his name, he'd said it twice already and would say it again, because forgetting things had become his life's work whether he willed it or not—lay on a pallet of folded vestments beneath the empty altar niche where Vess's statue should have been. There was only a smooth scar of stone where the god's likeness had been chiseled away, and Juniper understood now that the chiseling had come first, before the sealing, before the silence. You did not carve away your god's face unless you were preparing to do something worse.

"You came back," Aldous said. His voice was a dry leaf dragged over stone. "I hoped you would. Hoping is the one prayer left to me."

"I brought water." Juniper knelt and held the cup to the old man's lips. The priest drank in small, careful sips, as if even swallowing were a rite he no longer trusted himself to perform correctly.

"And the child?"

"Asleep. Maren's watching her."

"Good. Good." A pause. His clouded eyes fixed on some point past Juniper's shoulder, or past the wall, or past two centuries. "Then we can talk about the thing you have not asked me. The thing you have been circling since I called you heir."

Outside, through the chapel's narrow window, Juniper could see the pass where Maren had gone at dawn with a crossbow, three barrels of oil, and a face that had told him everything and nothing. He could see the smoke of Grey's vanguard fires, thin gray threads against the white peaks. One night. Maybe two. He sat down on the cold floor beside the dying man because standing suddenly felt dishonest.

"Tell me," he said.

Aldous smiled. It transformed his ruined face into something almost young. "Ah," he breathed. "There it is. The question under all your questions. *Why?* Why would a god let himself be buried. Why would his faithful turn the key. You carry his mark on your wrist, Okafor. You've spent ten years believing he was stolen from you. Murdered by enemies. Sealed by tyrants." The smile faded. "And every year the belief has rotted something in you a little more, hasn't it? Because grief without a villain curdles into doubt, and doubt into shame, and shame into—" he gestured vaguely at Juniper's whole existence— "*that.*"

"Don't," Juniper said quietly.

"I am a priest. Lying to the grieving was never my trade. Comforting them with lies was, and there is a difference, though I took thirty years to learn it." He struggled up onto one elbow; Juniper moved to help him and was waved off with surprising strength. "The massacre happened in the spring of the last true year. You know this much. Every Thornic child knew it—the way children everywhere know the shape of their own doom before they can name it."

"I don't know anything," Juniper said. "My grandmother gave me prayers and a warning and died before she could give me reasons. Everything else I learned from maps that pretend your country never existed."

"Then listen, and I will give you the only inheritance I have left, which is the truth, which is always the heaviest thing anyone leaves behind."

* * *

"The Crown did not destroy us in a day," Aldous began. "They starved us first. Embargoes dressed as tariffs. Treaties written to be broken. And when our borders finally burned, when the armies came down out of the north with their new alphabet and their new god of crowns and contracts—it took eleven days. Eleven days to do what I will spend my eternity failing to describe."

His hands trembled in his lap. Juniper watched them and thought of Sable's drawings: the figure with many hands, rendered in charcoal with the terrible economy of a child who has seen too much and cannot yet say it.

"We were Vess's people. All of us. Not the way kingdoms are a king's—we were *his*, marked at birth, oathed in blood, every hearth in Thornfell lit with a prayer to him. And he heard us. That was the horror of it, boy. He heard every single one."

Juniper's mouth had gone dry. "What do you mean—"

"A god who answers is a god who feels," Aldous said. "You know this. You have spent ten years screaming into his silence and calling it abandonment. But silence is not absence. Silence is what a wound looks like from the outside." He closed his eyes. "On the eleventh day, Vess heard eleven thousand of his people die at once. Do you understand? Not over years. Not spread across a war. In a single day, on a single plain, the Crown herded them together and burned them, precisely because they knew—a god's power lives in his faithful, and a god in agony is a weapon pointed at whoever stands nearest."

The chapel was very quiet. Somewhere in the frozen square outside, wind moved through streets that hadn't known weather in two hundred years.

"What did he do?" Juniper asked. His voice sounded far away to himself.

"He grieved." Aldous opened his eyes, and for a moment they were not clouded at all but bright and wet and ancient. "He grieved the way only a god can. Not with tears, boy. With *unmaking*. We felt it beginning—the mountains to the east softening like wax. The river running backward into its source. The new towns across the border, full of people who had never prayed to him, whose only crime was being built on the far side of our graves—he began to unmake them. Not in rage. That's the lie the Crown tells, the lie that justifies everything. Not rage. Grief. Grief so vast it could not tell the guilty from the innocent, because nothing looked innocent anymore. Nothing looked like anything. He was drowning in the sound of us dying, and he reached out to stop the sound, and what his hand touched began to stop existing."

Juniper thought of Sable asleep in the granary, small hands curled, breathing slow. He thought of the word he'd understood in her first hours among them. *Oath.*

"So we stopped him."

"So we stopped him," Aldous echoed. "His own priests. His own sworn. Every surviving soul in Thornfell gathered in this valley—you are kneeling in the place where it happened—and we did the only thing left to people who loved a god too much to let him destroy the world in our name."

He lifted his hand. It shook as it rose, and then it steadied, and he pressed his fingers to Juniper's wrist—to the oath-mark, the whorled scar every member of Juniper's company carried, the brand of a contract signed by Vess himself.

"This," Aldous said. "Do you know what this truly is? Your company thinks it is a mercenary's bond. A god's protection, purchased with service. Your grandmother thought so too, when she fled south with a handful of others and a prayer book and a child's memory of fire." His fingers tightened, surprisingly strong. "It is a *seal-anchor*. Each oath-mark is a nail holding the lid. Vess did not design the seal—we did, his priests, in the last desperate weeks—but he consented to it. Do you hear me? He *consented*. Because even drowning, even mad with grief, some part of him knew what his hand was doing, and he could not bear that either. So his faithful bound him—not as captors. As physicians binding a man who has begged them, between convulsions, to hold him down."

Juniper could not speak. Something in his chest had cracked open along a fault line he hadn't known was there, and everything he'd believed for ten years was sliding through the crack into darkness.

Ten years of ritual gestures performed into silence. Ten years of feeling like a man praying to an empty chair. Ten years of the slow rot Maren had named aloud around a dying fire—the purposelessness, the dissolution, the company eating itself because its god had stopped answering.

He hadn't stopped answering.

He had been *held down* by the ones he loved, screaming, while they nailed the lid shut—and every oath-mark in the world was a nail, and Juniper's family had carried theirs south for generations like a splinter working its way toward the heart.

"The seal needed keepers," he managed at last. "The Sealers. The things with many hands."

"Our custodians," Aldous said. "Made from the seal itself, from the combined faith of everyone in this valley—they are not men and they are not monsters, whatever they have become in the dark. They maintain the prison. They hunt anything that might crack it. And they have been hunting your child since the day she was grown from the kingdom's last prayer, because she *is* the lock, and locks that walk are dangerous to their makers." He coughed, long and wet, and Juniper held him until it passed. "But you must understand the cruelest part, Okafor. The part I have carried longest."

"There's more."

"There is always more. Truth is a well; you only ever draw up the bucket you can lift." He settled back against the vestments. "We did not seal him to save the world. Or rather—we did, but that was the second reason. The first reason was smaller and more human and more shameful." His eyes found Juniper's. "We could not bear his grief. Do you understand? Eleven days of hearing him weep inside our skulls, and the sound was going to kill the survivors faster than the Crown's soldiers could. When we sealed him, we were not saving the new kingdoms." His voice dropped to a whisper. "We were covering our ears."

The words landed in Juniper like stones dropped down a well, falling a long time before they struck bottom.

Faith complicated. That was the phrase forming itself somewhere in him, though he had no name for it yet. His god was not a tyrant stolen by villains. His god was not a corpse betrayed by cowards. His god was a father smothering in the smoke of his murdered children, begging to be restrained, restrained, and then—left alone. Left in the dark for two hundred years with his grief intact and undiminished, because grief does not decay the way flesh does. Grief waits.

And someday the lid would come off, and everything the seal had postponed would arrive at once.

"That's why the second door matters," Juniper said slowly. "That's why breaking the oath freely—if it works—matters. Not to free him. To stand *outside* the grief. To open the door without waking—"

"—without becoming another voice crying out inside his skull, yes." Aldous smiled again, tired and fierce. "You see it. Two hundred years, and the priests of this valley never dared. To break an oath to Vess was unthinkable—it was the one blasphemy with no rite of absolution. So the lid stayed nailed. So the grief stayed fresh." He reached up with trembling fingers and touched Juniper's cheek, the way you touch someone you are saying goodbye to. "And then a smuggler's grandson walks into my valley carrying the oldest mark in the world and a child made of prayer, and he asks me why, and I finally get to tell someone the truth before I die. Hope is a strange mercy, boy. It arrives late and charges everything."

* * *

Afterward, Juniper sat for a long time on the chapel steps with his head in his hands.

The frozen square lay before him in the late light: the woman with her jug, the boys with their knucklebones, the old man reaching for his door. Two hundred years ago they had chosen this. Had stood together in this valley and done the hardest loving thing anyone had ever done, and then covered their ears, and then—frozen, preserved, sealed alongside their god like a wound kept from healing so it could be remembered.

He tried to be angry and found he couldn't hold it. He tried to forgive and found that wasn't his to give. What remained was something rawer and less useful: understanding. Understanding that love, at scale, becomes indistinguishable from violence to the ones doing it. That his grandmother's warnings, her cursed tongue, her hidden prayer book, her family contract signed by a god's own hand—all of it was not inheritance of duty.

It was inheritance of *complicity*. Generations of nails, carried south in exile, waiting.

"Juniper."

Maren's voice. He looked up. She stood at the edge of the square with Sable's hand in hers, and her face was wrong—pale in a way that had nothing to do with the mountain cold, and her eyes went past him to the eastern sky.

"They're not waiting for morning," she said. "Grey's found another route down—I don't know how, maybe the deserters, maybe worse. Torches on the ridge. Hundreds of them." Her jaw tightened. "An hour. Maybe less."

Sable pulled free of her hand and crossed the frozen square to him, small boots crunching on snow that hadn't fallen in two centuries. She took his face in both her hands, the way she sometimes did when she wanted him to listen with more than his ears, and spoke in Old Thornic—one clear sentence, unhurried, certain.

He didn't need the translation. He'd begun dreaming in the language days ago.

*The god is listening now,* she said. *He heard everything.*

Behind them, deep beneath the chapel, beneath the valley, beneath two hundred years of deliberate silence—something turned over in its sleep, and the frozen woman's jug tilted one degree further, and the first drop of water fell from lips that had been dry since before the Crown existed.

From the pass above, Maren's voice rang out sharp and clear: *"Light the oil!"*
