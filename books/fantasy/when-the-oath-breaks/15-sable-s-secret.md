## 15. Sable's Secret

The priest's chamber smells of dust and candle-tallow and something older underneath, like the inside of a stone that has never seen sun. Sable sits on the edge of a bed that is too big for her, in a room that is too quiet for anyone, and practices being brave.

She has gotten good at it.

Being brave, she has learned, does not feel the way the stories say. It does not feel like fire or like singing. It feels like holding your breath underwater and counting, except you never come up, and the counting never stops, and eventually you forget that breathing was ever something you were allowed to do.

Outside the shuttered window, Juniper Okafor's voice rises and falls with the priest's, two men arguing softly about doors and gods and what may be unmade. She can pick out his words now without trying. The language of the kingdom lives in her mouth the way water lives in a well — it was there before she was, filling all the empty places, and everything else she says is drawn up bucket by bucket from its depth. But the other tongue, the mercenary tongue, the tongue of roads and rations and *duck down, girl* — that one she had to learn the slow way, word by word, listening at firesides for three seasons while the company forgot she was listening.

She is not sure they ever forgot. Maren watches her too sharply for that. Maren watches everyone the way a woman watches a bridge she does not trust.

Sable pulls her knees up to her chin and counts her breaths and waits for the men to finish so she can do the thing she came here to do, which is the hardest thing, harder than running, harder than the many-handed dark, harder even than knowing.

Telling.

* * *

Here is what Sable remembers, and remembering it is like pressing her thumb into a bruise to prove it still hurts:

There was no before. That is the truth no one will like. Everyone wants her to have had a mother, a village, a burning, a flight across the mountains. The Inquisitor's people want it because it makes her legible — a refugee, a remnant, a child to be filed under *pitiable*. The company wants it because it makes her safe — an orphan, an obligation, someone small enough to protect. Even the priest wants it; she has watched him pray over her when he thinks she sleeps, asking Vess to guard *this last lamb of Thornfell*, and his voice goes tender and broken in the middle, and she cannot bear to correct him, so she lets him have his lamb.

But there was no village. There were no soldiers at her back. There was no mother's hand slipping from hers.

There was a prayer.

That is the first thing. Before anything else — before hands, before hunger, before the cold of the high passes and the taste of pine bark she chewed when there was nothing else — there was a prayer, and she was inside it the way a seed is inside a fruit, and the prayer was saying: *let there be someone left to remember us. Let there be someone small enough that the Sealers will not look twice. Let there be a door that walks.*

And then there was light, which was really just the absence of stone, and then there was grass, wet and astonishing, and then there was the sky, which she did not have a word for yet but understood immediately, completely, the way you understand falling.

She opened her eyes on a hillside above a valley that had been folded shut like a letter, and the last thought of the last priest of Vess was still dissolving in her mouth, and she was four years old, or she had been alive for four minutes and would always be four years old, and both of these things were true.

The Sealers found her footprints within the hour. Of course they did. They find everything. They are not cruel — she knows this now, though it took her a long time to understand, because their faces are smooth as eggs and their hands are many and their patience is the patience of masonry. They are not cruel. They are *dutiful*. They keep the seal the way a wall keeps a roof, without opinion, without end. And a walking door is a crack in the wall, and cracks must be closed.

So she ran. Not from them, exactly. From the closing.

She ran through the folded country where the years pool like standing water, and where the people stand mid-motion, mid-laugh, mid-farewell, two hundred years deep and none of them breathing. She ran past a wedding frozen at the kiss. Past a boy with a ball that would never land. Past an old woman feeding birds that hung in the air like ornaments. She did not cry, because she had been made already knowing what crying cost, and besides — this is the part she has never told anyone, not even in drawings, especially not in drawings —

Besides, they were beautiful. The frozen ones. She loved them before she ever met a single person who could move.

* * *

Juniper comes in smelling of woodsmoke and argument, and the door clicks shut behind him, and the noise of the others — Brannick's low grumble, Tess's laugh like a snapped twig — cuts off all at once, the way it does around her, the way it has since the valley.

He crouches in front of her the way he always does, forearms on his knees, making himself small. He is very good at making himself small for such a large man. She thinks that is what soldiers learn instead of prayers.

"Hey, sprout." His voice is rough-soft, banked-fire soft. "Priest says you wanted me."

She nods. Then, because nodding is a coward's answer and tonight she has resolved not to be one: "I need to tell you something true."

Something moves behind his eyes — quick, hunted, gone. She has learned to read the mercenaries' faces the way she reads weather. He sits down on the floor entirely, cross-legged, like a boy, so that they are level. "All right," he says. "Truths are in short supply lately. Let's hear yours."

She takes a breath. Holds it. Counts, the way she counts. One. Two. Three. The candle flame leans and rights itself. Somewhere below them, in the heart of the folded kingdom, the great door waits under its standing stone, humming the note that only she can hear, the note she has heard every day of her life because she *is* the note, she is the hum made into a girl, and she has known this for longer than she has known her own name.

"I wasn't running away," she says. "I was running *toward*."

His brow creases. "Toward what?"

"Toward you." The words come easier now, in either language, and that frightens her a little, how easy. "Not you-you. The company. The oath." She touches the mark on his wrist without asking, because she stopped asking about that weeks ago and he stopped flinching, and the mark is warm under her fingers like a stone kept near a hearth. "This. I grew from this."

"You grew from—" He almost laughs, the polite kind adults use on children who say charming nonsense. "From an oath-mark?"

"No." She keeps her thumb where it is. She makes herself keep looking at his face, because if she looks away now she will lose the thread and the thread is everything. "From the *prayer*. The last prayer. Do you know what the priest said? When they sealed it? When they sealed *him*?"

"The priest has said many things—"

"He said: *let there be a key small enough to hide, and let the key be loved, so that no one will spend it.*" Her voice comes out steady and that surprises her most of all. "That was me. I am the key. The kingdom prayed one more time, right at the end, right before the silence closed over everything, and the prayer didn't go up. Prayers have to go somewhere, Juniper. Grandmother-tongue rule." She uses his grandmother's phrase deliberately, and sees it land, sees him go still as the frozen wedding-party. "It went into me. I am what's left when you pour a whole country's last hope into a shape small enough to walk through the world without being noticed."

The candle gutters. Neither of them moves to fix it.

"That's not possible," he says finally, but his voice has gone flat and careful, the voice he uses when he is lying to himself about how bad a contract is. She has heard it across a dozen campfires. "You're a child. You got lost. Someone hurt you, and you—"

"A man with many hands touched my shadow once, in the second winter." She says it plainly, because plainly is all she has. "Where he touched, the ground went grey for a year. Grass won't grow there. If they catch me, they don't kill me. They put me back. Back into the door, back into the seal, back into being a *thing* instead of a girl. And the door stays shut forever, and everyone in here stays asleep forever, and Vess stays buried under his own grief forever, and that is the ending the Sealers want, and it is a fine ending, and I would let them do it, I think, if it weren't for the other thing."

He has gone very pale. She wishes he wouldn't. It makes the next part worse, and the next part is the worst part, and she needs him to hear it with a whole face.

"The other thing?"

"The door opens from the inside, Juniper. That's why I could walk out and they couldn't stop me. The seal is a knot, and I'm the loose end of it, and a loose end can pull." She draws a breath. Counts. One. Two. Three. "If the door opens all the way — if the knot pulls free — the kingdom comes back. All of it. The sleeping people wake. Vess wakes. Everything that was taken gets given back." She swallows. "And I stop."

"*Stop.*"

"I stop." She spreads her small hands, palms up, showing him they are just hands, ten fingers, bitten nails, a scab on the left knee from the scree slope. "There isn't a girl under the seal, Juniper. There's a seal shaped like a girl. The prayer made me to be spent. That was always the plan. That was the *whole* plan. A key that gets used and then doesn't need to exist anymore."

The silence afterward is enormous. It fills the little stone room the way water fills a cup, to the brim and trembling.

Then Juniper Okafor, celebrated nowhere, sworn to nothing that answers, reaches out and takes both her hands in his two large scarred ones, and holds them like something rescued from a fire.

"No," he says.

"It's all right—"

"No." Louder, and the candle jumps. "No, sprout, listen to me. Listen. Nobody is spending you. Not a god, not a country, not a prayer, not a *priest*, not — nobody. You hear me? I've spent my whole life keeping a bargain I never signed. I know exactly what it costs to be the thing other people's promises hang on. I will cut the knot off my own arm before I let them do that to you."

She looks at their hands together. His are shaking. Hers are not, and that seems important somehow, and sad.

"That's why I waited to tell you," she says quietly. "Because I knew you'd say that. Because I knew it would make you do something stupid and brave, and stupid-brave people die, and I've only just gotten a family, and I would like to keep it a little longer. Even if I can't keep it long." She lifts her eyes to his. "I knew, Juniper. I knew what I was before I ever knocked on your waystation door. I knew in the valley. I knew on the mountain, drawing shapes on your floorboards hoping you'd understand. I came anyway. Do you know why?"

He doesn't answer. His jaw works. His eyes are wet, and mercenary men hate that, so she pretends not to see, because that is also a kindness, and kindnesses are the only currency she has ever had much of.

"Because a key that's never used is just metal," she says. "A seal that never opens is just a cage with better manners. I was made from a prayer that said *remember us* — not *entomb us*, not *forget us politely*. Remember. And remembering hurts, and remembering ends things, and I chose it. I walked out of a perfect, safe, sealed forever because somebody in this world ought to choose the ending instead of just enduring it." She smiles, and it wobbles, and she lets it wobble, because he should see that she is afraid, that she has always been afraid, and came anyway, that is the whole point, that is the entire point of her. "You taught me that. You and Maren and Brannick and Tess. You spend yourselves every day for each other. I just — I'm the same. Only bigger. Only older. Only sadder."

"Don't," he whispers. "*Don't.*"

"Hush." She squeezes his hands, small against huge. "I didn't tell you so you'd grieve early. Grieving early wastes the time you've got. I told you so you'd know. So that whatever you choose at the door, you choose it *knowing*, the way I chose. No one gets to make you spend me by trickery. Least of all me." She tilts her head. "That's the difference between us and the Sealers, you know. They keep things by force. We keep them by choosing. Every day. Even when choosing costs."

He laughs then — a cracked, terrible sound, half sob, the laugh of a man whose god went silent and whose heart just went loud. He pulls her in and she lets him, folding into the hollow of his chest where his heartbeat is fast and frightened and stubbornly, gloriously alive.

"Sprout," he says into her hair, "if there's a third option—"

"There might be." She says it into his collarbone, muffled. "The priest keeps circling it and won't land. Something about oaths. About what a freely-broken oath means to a god who's been held prisoner by faithfulness." She pulls back and looks up at him, and for a moment — just a moment — she feels what she truly is underneath the girl-shape: old, old, older than the mountains, patient as sediment, hopeful as a seed. "Find it. Find the third option. I'll hold the door as long as I can. But Juniper —"

Somewhere far below them, deeper than the priest's crypts, deeper than the roots of the standing stones, the great door changes its note.

They both hear it. A hum through the floorboards, through the bones, through the seal-shaped girl and the oath-marked man alike — and threaded through the hum, faint but unmistakable, comes another sound. Boot-heels. Many. Marching in step. Coming up the pass that only Maren's sold maps could show them.

Grey is in the valley.

Sable closes her eyes and listens to her own beginning approach, and grips Juniper's hands tighter, and does not let go.
