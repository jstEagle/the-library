## 1. The Smuggler's Ledger

The mountain did not care that it was the last night of the harvest month. It did not care that Tamsin Okafor had not slept properly in nine days, or that the cold had found the seam under their left arm where the oilskin had worn through, or that somewhere below them thirty-one people and forty-two mules were trusting a single lantern and a single guide to keep them from falling off the face of the world. The mountain only cared about its own long patience — snow load on the eastern cornices, meltwater gnawing at the roots of the switchbacks, wind coming down off the high shoulder of Veyra's Tooth with the smell of old ice and older stone.

Tamsin cared about all of it for the mountain.

They stood at the head of the column where the path narrowed to the width of a coffin, one hand flat against the rock face, feeling the stone the way a physician feels a wrist. Cold, but not the deep cold that meant ice beneath. Dry. The rock was talking to them in its small voice — grit shifting under their boots, the faint tick of frost letting go somewhere above — and it said what it always said this time of year: *passable, if you go gently.*

"Rope line," Tamsin called back down the dark, pitching their voice to carry without echoing. "Hands on the line, no talking, no torches past the marker stone. Della — count them through. If you lose more than two mules to the drop, tell me after, not during."

A breath of laughter moved down the column like wind through wheat, quickly stilled. Della Voss, who had been counting mules through bad passes since before Tamsin could shave, muttered something that was probably profane and probably affectionate, and the rope went taut.

Tamsin turned to the gap.

It was true, what they said in the lowland taverns — that there was no way over the Spine of Aldair anymore, that King Aldric's surveyors had mapped every pass worth the name and bricked up the ones he couldn't garrison, that Thornfell was a word out of songs and nothing else. All of it true, and none of it mattered, because the mountains had never belonged to kings. They belonged to the people who read them, and Tamsin Okafor read them the way priests once read scripture — fluently, reverently, and with a professional awareness of everything that had been edited out.

There were eleven ways across the Spine between Watcher's Gap and the sea. Aldric's maps showed three. The other eight were written nowhere except in Tamsin's feet, and in the feet of perhaps six other people alive, and two of those were dead this year.

They took the narrow stretch at a walker's pace, placing each step as deliberately as a signature. Below, to the left, the drop sheared away into a darkness so complete it felt less like distance than like substance — a black country lying against the mountain's flank. Wind came up out of it carrying pine resin and, faintly, woodsmoke from some valley that believed itself hidden.

*You are the wall,* Tamsin thought, in the old cadence, without meaning to. *You are the roof. You are the road that does not break.*

The prayer of the passes. Their grandmother's formula — no. Not their grandmother. Old Maret, who had taught them the high trails when they were eleven and all elbows and hunger, who had claimed every year to be dying and every spring come back up the mountain thinner and tougher, like wire. Maret had been born in Thornfell before the annexation, back when the country had borders instead of memories, and she had taught Tamsin the trail-prayers the way she taught everything: as if refusing to teach them would be one more small surrender.

*You are the wall. You are the roof.* And then the closing line, the one every child of the high country had once known:

*And Veyra walks with you, though her voice is quiet.*

Her voice was quiet. That was one way to put it.

Twelve years ago — Tamsin did the arithmetic the way you probe a tooth, briefly and with dread; thirteen now, nearly fourteen — the god had stopped answering. Not gradually. There had been no long twilight of diminishing miracles, no gentle fading such as the lowland clergy described when they spoke of other, lesser shrines going dark. One winter solstice the offering-fires on the peaks had lit green, as they always did, and the god had been *there*, the way a presence in a room is there, vast and attentive and near. And by spring thaw the presence was gone. No explanation. No last word. Just absence, total and specific, the way a missing tooth is specific.

The church of Aldric said Veyra had never existed at all — that the silent god was Thornfell superstition, a mountain spirit inflated by provincial grief into divinity, and that the annexation had mercifully freed the highlanders from their ignorance. Tamsin had heard a fat canon explain this once in a lowland tavern, in the warm confident tones of a man who had never been higher than a cathedral bell tower. Tamsin had bought him another drink, smiled, and gone to bed early rather than do something that would cost the caravan its guide.

Because here was the thing the canon would never understand: Tamsin still prayed. Every crossing, every season, the same trail-prayer, mouthed into a silence that had not softened in fourteen years. They didn't know what the word for that was. Stubbornness, probably. Sera would have called it — 

Tamsin stopped the thought. Sera did not exist yet. Sera was a name they hadn't met, a shape the future was keeping. But the reflex of imagining someone to say things to — that was real, and it was getting worse each year, and it was the loneliest fact of their life, lonelier than the cold, lonelier than the drop beside them. Competence kept you alive on the mountain. It did not keep you company.

*You are the road that does not break,* they finished, silently, and stepped off the narrows onto the broad shelf beyond, and held the lantern high so the light would run back down the rope line like a promise.

* * *

They made the cache by the second hour past midnight: a cleft in the mountainside behind a curtain of hanging roots, invisible from three paces, dry as a tomb and almost as old. Smuggler's work, this one — not Tamsin's own provisioning hole but one of the ancient ones, pre-annexation, cut into the rock with tools nobody used anymore. The high country was stitched with them. Most held nothing but rusted lamp-hinges and the ghosts of contraband. Some held grain when the valleys starved. One, which Tamsin had found at nineteen and told no one about, held a child's shoe, a bronze bell, and a bundle of letters in an alphabet that had officially stopped existing thirty years before the letters were written.

Tonight the cache was theirs by right of schedule — a waystation on the long slide down toward the lowlands, a place to get the column out of the wind and let the mules drink from the seep that ran cold and iron-tasting from the back wall.

"Two hours," Tamsin told Della. "Then the descent to the tree line before dawn. We sleep in the pines or we don't sleep."

"You always say that." Della was sixty and built like a door, and she lowered herself onto her bedroll with the sigh of a woman who had made peace with every ache she owned. "You ever notice you never sleep first? Fourteen years I've walked with you. You never sleep while the watch is yours."

"The watch is always mine."

"That's what I'm saying." She pulled her blanket to her chin. "Whatever god you keep muttering to up front — you should ask it for a partner."

Tamsin almost smiled. "He doesn't take requests."

"No," Della said, already half asleep. "They never do."

The column settled by degrees — mules stamping, the young ones (there were three hired hands under twenty this trip, and one of them, a lowland boy called Piet, had taken the crossing with his eyes shut for the entire narrows) wrapping themselves in the cargo tarps among the bales of uncut garnet and tins of quinine syrup that paid everyone's wages. Contraband, all of it, though Tamsin had long ago stopped thinking of it that way. Aldric's tariffs made criminals of anyone who moved goods across a border he had drawn with a ruler through the middle of living communities. The border existed on paper. The mountain had never signed it.

Tamsin took the first watch at the cache mouth, as always, back to the rock, facing the wind.

This was the hour they hated most and would have traded nothing for. The column breathing. The stars in their brutal clarity, the whole sky scoured by altitude until it seemed you could see the bones of heaven. Somewhere south and east, beyond the shoulders of the massif, lay the drowned valleys of Thornfell — villages with Aldric's garrisons in them now, boundary stones pulled up and melted for cannon, a country erased so thoroughly that children in the lowlands learned the word only as a warning. *Thornfell happened. Let it stay happened.* That was the schoolroom catechism.

Tamsin had never lived there. Had never seen it. And still some traitor instinct in the chest turned toward it every time, the way a compass needle turns, useless and faithful.

To occupy their hands they checked the cache walls — habit, ritual, the guide's superstition that a shelter must be inspected or it will inspect you. The left wall: sound. The seep: running clean. The back wall —

The back wall had changed.

Not fallen, not cracked. *Changed.* Where the rock had always been raw and wet-dark, tonight — in the lantern light, at an angle Tamsin must have looked at a hundred times — there was a seam. A joint so fine it might have been masonry, except no mason working this far back in the hills had ever cut stone that cleanly. Tamsin put their palm against it. Dry. Warm, very slightly, in the way a hearthstone stays warm after the fire dies.

They should have woken Della. Later, they would think about how easily they didn't.

The knife came out first — a guide learns to open unknown things with a blade between themselves and the surprise — and then both hands, and the seam gave with a sound like a held breath released, and a slab of fitted stone swung inward on hidden pivots that had not seized in a century because, apparently, they had been *waiting*.

Inside: a niche, dry as the rest, lined with lead sheeting gone white with age. And on the lead, wrapped in waxed cloth that cracked like autumn leaves when Tamsin touched it, a book.

Leather. Board-stitched. Heavy in the hand the way only old books are heavy, as if the weight were mostly time. On the cover, tooled in a script Tamsin knew from boundary stones and grave markers — the Thornfell hand, the one that officially no longer existed — a single word:

*LEDGER.*

They unwrapped it fully, meaning only to look, meaning to wake Della any moment now, certainly, in just a moment. The spine opened with a sigh to the first page, and the handwriting that filled it was fine and clerkish and utterly certain of itself, and Tamsin — who read four languages and had buried two of them — began, standing alone at the mouth of a smuggler's cache with the whole sleeping world at their back, to translate.

It was a ledger, at first. Records. Shipments and dates and sums, a century dead, the commerce of a smuggling ring that had operated out of these very hills when Thornfell still had a customs office to defy. Tamsin skimmed, disappointed and relieved in equal measure, and turned pages faster, and faster—

And then the handwriting changed.

Later hand. Shakier. The same script but pressed hard, urgent, a person writing against something — against doubt, maybe, or against time. The entries stopped being accounts. They became sentences. Then paragraphs. Then something that made the hair rise along Tamsin's arms in the freezing dark, because the shivering hand had begun to *predict*. Small things first — a rockfall, a birth, the year the green fires would fail to light. Then larger. And then, near the end of the book, on a page whose margin was scorched as though the volume had passed close to a flame and been pulled back, the writing steadied, as if the writer had stopped being afraid of what they were seeing:

*In the days when the Door is shut and the god is silent, one will come who carries the oath without knowing why. A guide of roads that are not on any map. They will walk the high paths and pray to a god who does not answer, and they will keep praying.*

Beneath it, in the same hand but inked separately, deliberately, as if underlined by history itself, a date and a name.

The date was tonight. The feast of the last harvest, by the old reckoning — the night the green fires once burned on every peak. The night it currently was.

The name was *Okafor.*

Tamsin did not remember sitting down. They were on the floor of the cache with the book open across their knees, and the lantern had guttered low, and their heart was doing something strange and slow, like a mule refusing a bridge it has crossed all its life.

There were other Okafors in the world. Dozens. It was a common enough name in the high country, a smith's name, a ferryman's name. Coincidence was a thing. Coincidence was most things.

They turned the page. Only a few lines remained, the final entry, the letters large and deliberate as a signature:

*The Oathbearer of the Last Door. May Veyra forgive us for what we ask of them.*

Behind them, out in the night, on every peak of the Spine as far north and as far south as the eye could reach, the old offering-fires — unlit for fourteen years, unlit because there had been no one to light them, unlit because the god had gone silent and the high country had learned to look away — flickered green.

Tamsin sat very still in the dark with a century-dead book naming them by name, and listened to thirty-one people breathe, and understood, in the marrow-first way one understands falling, that the ground they had stood on all their life was not ground at all.

The green fires burned on. They did not go out.

Neither, quite, did Tamsin's hands stop shaking — not while they wrapped the book, not while they hid it inside their coat against the beating heart, not while they finally woke Della with a voice they were proud stayed level, saying only: "Change of plan. We're not going down to the lowlands tomorrow."

Della blinked at them, sleep-heavy and sharp underneath. "Where then?"

Tamsin looked south, toward the drowned valleys of Thornfell, toward a country that had apparently been waiting for them the way the cache had waited, patient as stone.

"Up," they said.
