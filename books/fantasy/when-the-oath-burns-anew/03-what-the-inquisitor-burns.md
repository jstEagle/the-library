## 3. What the Inquisitor Burns

Magistrate Corvel arrived in Lowmark on the fourth day of the rains, and the town knew it before they saw him, because the birds went quiet.

Not all at once. It began with the crows abandoning the eaves of the tithe-house, lifting in a ragged black sheet that wheeled once over the market square and scattered toward the fens. Then the sparrows withdrew from the gutters of the chandlery, from the dovecotes, from the warm crevices of every chimney on Cordwainer Street, until by midmorning Lowmark's roofs stood bare as a skull stripped of hair. The townsfolk noticed. Townsfolk always notice what vanishes from their periphery long before they attend to what arrives within it. They murmured about omens over their beer, and their wives said it was only the weather, and both were wrong.

Corvel came up the coast road in a plain grey coach drawn by four dun horses, escorted by six riders of the King's Second Inquiry who wore no livery at all — which was itself a kind of livery, in the way that an empty chair is a kind of throne. The coach bore neither crest nor sigil. Only a small brass plate above the door, polished to a mirror shine, reading: *THE CROWN ASKS QUESTIONS.*

He had chosen Lowmark deliberately. He chose every place deliberately; deliberation was the whole architecture of the man. Lowmark sat at the mouth of the mountain roads like a cork in a bottle, the last fat lowland town before the passes narrowed into Thornfell's high country — or what the maps now called simply *the King's Northern Reaches*, the ink having been instructed, some thirty years ago, to forget otherwise. Anything smuggled out of the mountains passed through Lowmark. Anything burned in the mountains had first been bought or stolen or prayed over in Lowmark. If prophetic texts were moving — and they were moving; three burnings in two months, each fire hotter than the last — then the current of them flowed through this town.

Corvel stepped down from the coach onto wet cobblestones and stood for a moment in the rain without hood or hat, letting it strike his face. He was fifty-one years old and looked younger in the way that men look younger when they have surrendered nothing to pleasure. Grey at the temples, precise at the jaw, eyes the flat brown of river stones — the kind of eyes that gave back your own reflection and kept nothing of yours. He carried one leather satchel. He required nothing else. Everything else would be provided, because everything else always was: rooms, confessions, lists.

The harbormaster met him at the customs house, sweating despite the cold, bowing too often.

"Magistrate. We — we weren't told to expect—"

"You were not told," Corvel agreed pleasantly. "That is how expectation works. It can be watched." He smiled, and the smile reached every part of his face except its purpose. "Show me your registry of goods. Ten years back. And send for your night-soil men, your dock-wives, your toll-keepers. I'll want a room with one window facing the mountain road, and I will want it understood that no one in this town speaks of my arrival until I permit it."

"And if they do speak?"

Corvel set his satchel on the customs desk and unlaced it with the care of a surgeon. Inside lay his instruments: a set of steel pens ground to needles; a stoppered vial of ash-salts that flared white when breathed upon by fresh ink; a folding brass frame for pressing pages flat; and a small book bound in grey calfskin, unmarked, in which he recorded names.

"The ones who speak," he said, "will be the ones I thank."

* * *

By evening he had thanked eleven people.

This was how it worked, and Corvel permitted himself a small satisfaction in the elegance of it, the way another man might savor a well-built bridge. You did not arrive with soldiers and break doors. Doors broken were doors that taught a town to bar itself. Instead you arrived alone-ish and mild, you asked for records, you listened — really listened, head tilted, pen ready, the very portrait of a bureaucrat worn down by distance and weather — and you made yourself so easy to talk to that talking became a habit before anyone thought to examine it. The dock-wife who mentioned, laughing, that her cousin's husband ran wool where wool had no business being. The toll-keeper who let slip that certain caravans paid their fees in old coin, coin struck before the Reunification, coin with dead kings' faces. The tithe-clerk whose ledgers showed charcoal purchased in quantities suited to smelting ore, though no ore came down those roads.

Old coin. Charcoal beyond need. Wool routes that bent toward the passes. Corvel wrote the names in his grey book in his beautiful, merciless hand, and beside each name he wrote not accusations but questions, because questions were patient and accusations were merely loud.

Then he opened the second ledger — the one he kept folded inside his coat, against his heart, as his enemies would have said mockingly and he would have corrected them: against his *duty* — and reviewed the list of burned things.

A granary loft outside Harrow Ford, eight years past: forty-one books destroyed by a farmer who claimed rats. Among them, by the farmer's own later testimony, wrung gently, regretfully, over the course of a long afternoon — a psalter of Veyra's silent order, annotated in a hand that predated the Reunification.

A miller's cellar in Dunmore, five years past: a cache of letters and one bound manuscript, already half-rotted, burned by the miller's son. The son had described the manuscript's cover as *dark leather, with a door pressed into it in gold*. A door. Corvel had underlined that word twice.

And now, this season: smoke seen on the high trails by shepherds who sold mutton to Lowmark and gossip with it. Smoke in places where there were no hearths. Someone was burning things *up there*, in the old country, which meant someone up there had found something worth destroying — or worth keeping, which was worse.

Because that was the truth Corvel had built his life upon, brick by brick, testimony by testimony, ash sample by ash sample: Thornfell was not erased. Erasure was a word for the lazy. Thornfell had been *archived*. Every song suppressed had survived in a throat somewhere; every boundary stone toppled lay face-down in the moss still bearing its carved words; every book the Crown burned had first passed through hands that could recite it. A country did not die when you killed its king. A country died when you killed its memory, and memory was the most stubborn organism the crown ever prosecuted, regrowing from fragments, from half-lines of verse, from a child asking why grandmother turned her cup upside down at supper.

So the Crown had learned patience. Thirty years of patience. And Corvel was the instrument of that patience, and he loved his function the way a scalpel might love, if a scalpel could love, the steadiness of the hand that held it.

He believed — and this must be understood plainly, because history prefers its monsters simple and Corvel was not simple — he believed he was a good man doing necessary work. He had seen what prophecy did to the lowborn when it took root. He had watched a fishing village tear itself apart over a girl of nine who dreamed of drowned bells; he had read the inquiry transcripts, the neighbors' testimonies, the final count of the dead. Faith unregulated was a fire in a thatched city. The King's church of the Unified Rite offered order, hierarchy, accountability — a god with office hours. What the old texts offered was older and hungrier: gods who spoke directly, oaths that bound bloodlines, doors that opened onto lands outside all lawful maps. Chaos, dressed in holiness.

Someone had to hold the lantern steady. Corvel held it. If the work left stains, then the stain was the cost of the clean world, and he had long ago stopped counting costs, because counting them had become a cruelty to himself.

There was only one thing he permitted himself to want, in the way of wanting — one thing beyond function. Somewhere in the mountains, intact, was a text the archivists called the *Veyran Codex*: a century-old prophecy, author unknown, contents unknown, known only through a single confiscated fragment — a torn flyleaf in the Royal Archive bearing three lines and the notation *remainder consigned to smugglers, disposition unknown.* Three lines that spoke of a Door. Of an Oathbearer. Of a date.

Dates, Corvel knew, were the bones of prophecy. Strip away the poetry and every true prediction came down eventually to a day. And the flyleaf's date was close now. Close enough that the King's own astrologers had begun lighting lamps late.

He intended to find the Codex before that date arrived, read it entire, and burn it entire — after transcription. The Crown archived even what it destroyed. That was the difference between the Crown and the mob: the Crown remembered why it forgot.

* * *

On the third night, the answers began arriving, as answers did, once a town understood that speaking to the magistrate was safer than silence.

It was the toll-keeper first, a soft man named Brack with damp eyes and the smell of cheap gin, ushered into Corvel's rented room — the one window facing the mountain road — where Corvel received informants seated, unhurried, with wine he never drank.

"They come down mostly in autumn," Brack said, wringing his cap. "The mountain folk. Smugglers, we call them, though it's wool and cheese and suchlike, mostly harmless—"

"Names."

"Well, there's the Vane brothers, and old Hesk with the limping mule, and—" Brack hesitated. Corvel waited. Waiting was also an instrument; he had tuned it over decades until it sang. "There's one called Okafor. Tamsin Okafor. Guides the hard passes, the high ones nobody else will take. Good with the mountains, they say. Quiet. Pays fair."

"Okafor." Corvel wrote it. The name meant nothing yet. Names were seeds; most never sprouted. "Thornfell-born?"

Brack blinked. "Never thought on it. Accent's not lowland. Could be."

Could be. Corvel noted the *could be* and moved on, and Brack, relieved, babbled on about tolls and seasons, and left richer by three silver pieces and poorer by something he couldn't name, and would drink about it for years.

But it was the fourth night that mattered.

A woman came after midnight, hooded, and would not give her name, and Corvel did not press, because the nameless were usually the most useful. She was lowland-born but had married a stonecutter who worked the high quarries in summer, and she talked the way frightened people talk, in floods.

"It's my husband's brother," she said. "He runs with the mountain caravans sometimes. And he came down week before last, white as whey, and he'd taken the pledge — you know, the old pledge, the one they're not supposed to say anymore — and he broke it to warn us. He said there was a chest found. A smuggler's cache, old one, up past the tree line. And in the chest a book, and in the book—" Her voice dropped to a whisper that the rain nearly stole. "He said the book had a name in it. Written down a hundred year gone. And it was the name of someone walking the passes right now."

Corvel felt the sensation he always felt at such moments, and had trained himself never to show: a cold, bright settling, like ice forming on still water. The moment the archive became a hunt.

"A name," he repeated mildly. "Whose?"

"I don't know. He wouldn't say. Said saying it aloud made it truer. But he said the date too, Magistrate, the date written next to the name, and it was — it was near. Days near. And he said whoever it named, the book called them—" she swallowed— "*Oathbearer.*"

Outside, the rain intensified, drumming on the shutters like a thousand small urgent fingers. Corvel rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the mountain road gleaming wet under the clouds, running up into darkness toward a country that officially did not exist.

Oathbearer. The word from the flyleaf. The word from thirty years of files. Not a smuggler's fancy, then — the Codex itself, found, opened, read. And read by superstitious men who would now do the worst possible thing with it: believe it.

Belief made carriers of people. A man who believed he'd read a true prophecy would guard it, or sell it, or flee with it, or kneel to it — and in every case he would move, and movement left tracks, and tracks were Corvel's native country.

"Your husband's brother," Corvel said, turning back with his gentlest expression. "Does he come down to Lowmark again? Might he be persuaded to describe this book? Its binding, perhaps. Its condition."

"He's gone back up. Yesterday. With the Okafor caravan." She twisted her hands. "They're taking the high road. Past Ash Pass."

Ash Pass. Corvel returned to his desk, opened the grey book, and beneath *Okafor, Tamsin — guide, high passes, possible Thornfell origin* he added a line in his needle-fine script:

*Ash Pass. The book travels with the caravan.*

He sat for a while after the woman left, listening to the rain, feeling the particular clarity that came when a shape emerged from fog — not triumph, triumph was for smaller men, but *alignment*, the click of a mechanism engaging. Somewhere up in that wet dark, a smuggler was carrying a book that named them, unaware that the naming worked both ways. A prophecy that marked its bearer marked them also for every instrument of the Crown. The book was a lantern in the mountains. Corvel needed only to climb toward the light.

He lit his lamp, drew the mountain charts of the old survey — the forbidden survey, the one of Thornfell entire, kept in sealed drawers for three decades — and began, with a ruler and red ink, to plan a journey of his own.

Behind him, unnoticed on the sill of the single window, a bedraggled sparrow landed, shivered once, and flew back down into the lowlands, carrying nothing, remembering everything.
