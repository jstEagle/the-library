## 5. Borders That No Longer Exist

The riders came at dusk, and the mountains answered before Tamsin did.

They had made camp in the hollow beneath a leaning shelf of granite — a poor camp, a cold one, because Sera had insisted on no fire and Tamsin had not argued. Three days out from the shrine where the defector priestess had found them, three days of hard climbing and harder silence, and Tamsin had almost convinced themselves that Corvel's hounds had lost the scent.

Then the dogs began to sing down in the valley, a thin high chorus threading up through the pines, and every hair on Tamsin's arms stood like the hackles of an animal that knows the hunt has turned its way.

"Riders," they said, already rolling their bedroll. "Six, maybe eight. Coming up the shepherd's road."

Sera was awake instantly — she slept the way soldiers did, or perhaps the way fugitives did, which was worse: never fully, always listening. She knelt over her pack with quick hands, stowing the little tin icon of Veyra she still carried despite everything, despite the church that had cast her out and the god who had, as far as anyone could prove, gone quiet.

"You said this road was safe," she said. There was no accusation in it. That was somehow more irritating than accusation would have been.

"I said it was *empty*," Tamsin corrected, cinching a strap. "Empty roads are only safe until someone remembers they exist." They paused, one hand flat against the cold stone of the shelf above them, and listened past the dogs — to the creak of tack, the wet slap of hooves in mud, the clipped lowland vowels of men shouting orders they didn't understand. "Corvel's people. Not local levy. He's brought his own."

"How can you tell?"

"The horses complain too much. Levies ride whatever the villages give them. These are war-mounts, shod heavy." Tamsin shouldered their pack. "And they're moving wrong. They think we'll run for the low passes, back toward Thornhollow and the river towns. So we won't."

"Where, then?"

Tamsin looked north, into the blue-black wall of the mountains, where the last light lay like a blade's edge along the peaks. Somewhere up there, past the tree line, past the glaciers, past the places even smugglers went reluctantly, there were roads that led nowhere anymore — roads whose destinations had been struck from the world thirty years ago by decree and ink.

"Up," they said. "We go up, and then we go *back*. Back along the old boundary."

Sera's breath caught audibly. "You mean—"

"I mean," Tamsin said, and heard their own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when they stepped near something that might collapse underfoot, "that if Magistrate Corvel wants us, he can freeze chasing ghosts. We cross into Thornfell tonight."

* * *

There was no gate. There had never been a gate, not even when Thornfell was a country instead of a memory. Borders in these mountains were lines drawn by rivers and ridgelines, marked by stones older than any living kingdom — squat grey pillars carved with a spiral that meant, depending on which scholar you asked, either *the mountain watches* or simply *here*, in the oldest sense of the word: this place, this ground, this side.

Tamsin knew where three such stones stood within a night's climb. They chose the one at the head of the Greywater fork, because the approach was steep enough to discourage pursuit and because — though they would not have admitted this to Sera, or to themselves — because it was the one their mother's people had used, according to a story told once, drunk, by an old caravan hand who claimed to have crossed the border the year before it stopped existing.

They climbed for four hours through pine and then through nothing but wind-scoured rock, the dogs' voices fading below them into the ordinary noise of the world, and all the while Sera followed without complaint, without stumbling, without asking how much farther. Tamsin kept waiting for her to ask. Priests, in Tamsin's experience, were talkers. This one moved like someone who had learned silence the hard way, in rooms where the wrong word got people burned.

It was nearly midnight when they reached the stone.

The moon was up, three-quarters full and hard-edged, and it laid the boundary pillar bare: taller than a man, broader than two, its spiral worn shallow by centuries of weather but still legible, still turning inward, drawing the eye down and down into the center of itself. On the far side of it the trail continued northward, descending gently into a broad hanging valley that the maps in Aldric's capital called, when they bothered to call it anything, *District Seven, Northern Reaches, Unincorporated.*

Tamsin stood with one boot on each side of the line and felt — nothing. That was what they had expected. Stone was stone. A border was a line some dead king had argued over with another dead king, and both of them were dust, and the mountain did not care, and neither did Tamsin Okafor, mercenary of no nation, oath-sworn to a god who had stopped listening years ago.

But beside them, Sera went down to her knees in the frost-stiff grass.

Tamsin looked away, embarrassed, as though they'd walked in on prayer. Which, in a sense, they had. The priestess pressed her palm flat against the boundary stone and stayed there a long moment, lips moving, though no sound came out. When she rose, her eyes were wet, and she didn't wipe them.

"It's warm," she said.

"It's rock. Rock holds the day's heat. It's summer at the lower elevations—"

"Tamsin." Her voice was very quiet. "I have touched a hundred of these stones. In the seminary they made us memorize the border provinces, and I walked the survey roads for six years, and I touched these stones in winter and summer, in sun and snow, and they were always exactly as cold as stone ought to be." She looked at her palm as if it belonged to someone else. "This one is warm. Like a hand. Like—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and Tamsin understood she'd been about to say *like the god*, and had decided against saying it aloud, here, on the edge of a country that wasn't supposed to exist.

Tamsin crouched and put their own hand against the pillar.

Warm. Faintly, unreasonably warm, the way a hearthstone stays warm hours after the fire dies. And beneath the warmth — this was the part that made Tamsin's stomach turn over slowly, the way a boat turns over, without drama and without hope of righting — beneath the warmth there was a rhythm. Slow. Deep. Far slower than any heart.

Like breathing, if the breather were the size of a mountain range.

They pulled their hand back and stood up fast, and the vertigo hit them a half-second later, so that they swayed, and Sera reached out to steady them, and Tamsin shook her off harder than they needed to.

"Don't," they said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to say it's a sign. An omen. That your silent god is waking up, or the land is holy, or the prophecy knows what it's talking about." Tamsin's voice came out rougher than intended. "Don't. Because if you're right about that, then you're right about other things too, and I'm not ready to be right alongside you yet."

Sera studied them in the moonlight. She had a talent for studying people without making them feel flayed, which Tamsin suspected had made her an excellent priestess and a terrible inquisitor, and which was now being deployed, patiently, mercilessly, on them.

"What things?" she asked softly.

"That I belong here," Tamsin said, and immediately regretted it, and covered it the only way they knew how, which was motion: they stepped across the line, both boots on the northern side, into District Seven, Unincorporated, and started walking. "Come on. We've got miles of dark left and I want the treeline behind us before dawn."

Behind them the boundary stone stood in the moonlight, warm as a sleeping thing, and neither of them looked back at it, and both of them thought about it for a long time.

* * *

The strangeness began gently, the way rot begins in timber — invisibly, structurally.

At first Tamsin attributed it all to exhaustion. They had slept maybe five hours in two days, and fear burns fuel like fever does, and high altitude plays tricks. So when the trail they were following proved better-kept than any abandoned road had a right to be — when the drainage cuts were clear of silt and the retaining walls stood whole, as though mended recently, as though tended — Tamsin filed it under *mountain magic, i.e., luck* and kept walking.

When they passed the first shrine — a small stone alcove off the track, roofed with slabs, holding a carved figure whose face had been worn to featurelessness — they noted that someone had left an offering. Fresh bread, by the smell of it. A day old, perhaps two. On a road that officially terminated in nothing, in a country that had ceased to exist before Tamsin was born.

"People live out here?" Sera murmured, staring at the bread.

"No one lives out here. That's rather the point of the annexation. Aldric emptied the valleys — resettled the population in the lowlands, scattered the clans, burned the records." Tamsin kept walking, faster now. "There's nobody to bake bread."

They walked another mile. Neither of them spoke. Then, around a shoulder of rock where the valley opened wide under the stars, they came upon the first village.

Or what should have been the ruin of one. Tamsin knew what thirty years of abandonment did to a mountain village: roofs fallen, walls tumbled, doorways grown shut with birch saplings, the whole settlement dissolving back into the hillside like sugar into tea. They had seen dozens. They had slept in dozens.

This village had no roofs. But its walls stood straight. Its chimneys stood straighter. And in the windows of the houses — empty houses, houses that could not possibly shelter anyone — there was light. Not firelight. Something softer, gold-grey, like the glow of coals remembered rather than seen, and it flickered in the windows in patterns that were almost, almost movement. Almost figures passing lamps from room to room. A village going about its evening, thirty years after its evening ended.

Tamsin stopped walking.

Beside them, Sera made a small sound — not fear. Tamsin knew fear sounds; they'd catalogued them across a decade of bad nights. This was something else, closer to the sound a person makes when music catches them unaware.

"They're not ghosts," Sera whispered. "Look again. Look properly."

Tamsin looked properly, which meant standing still long enough for the eyes to stop flinching away, and what they saw made their chest ache in a way they had no name for. The lights weren't wandering randomly. They moved in patterns — a light rising to a high window and settling, a cluster of them gathering low in what must have been a common hall, two lights lingering close together at a doorway as if in the long unhurried conversation of neighbors. Domestic light. Evening light. The light of people who expected tomorrow.

"There's no smoke," Tamsin said. Their voice sounded strange to them, flattened. "No smell of cooking. No sound. It's just — the shape of it. The memory of the shape."

"Yes." Sera's voice had gone reverent and ragged at once. "The land remembers. I was taught this, in the seminary, and I thought it was doctrine, poetry. *The mountains hold what is given to them.* But I never — I never dreamed it was literal. The annexation took the people, took the name off the maps, took the king and the laws and the songs from the schools. But it couldn't take—" she gestured helplessly at the lit windows, "—it couldn't take *this*. The country is still here, Tamsin. Underneath. It's been lying here under Aldric's district names for thirty years, breathing, keeping the hearths warm, waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

Sera looked at them. In the strange gold-grey light her face was unreadable and entirely readable at once.

"You know what," she said gently.

Tamsin did know. The book in their pack seemed suddenly heavier, the leather cover pressing against their spine like a hand. *Oathbearer of the Last Door.* A century-old prophecy with their name written in ink dried decades before their birth. They had spent three days refusing to open the book again, refusing to reread the line, refusing to let the words settle into them like a splinter working toward the heart. Now the splinter twisted.

"I never lived here," they said. The words came out low and fierce, aimed at the lit windows, at the warm stone, at the whole patient breathing country. "You understand that? I grew up in caravan camps and garrison towns and the stinking back quarters of border taverns. I don't know the songs. I don't know the clan names. My mother never taught me a single word of the old tongue, if she even knew it. Whatever this land thinks it's remembering—" their hand cut through the air toward the village, "—it's not remembering me. I'm not its grief. I'm just a smuggler who happened to walk through the wrong boundary stone."

Sera said nothing for a moment. Then, quietly: "Is that what you want it to think?"

"That's what's *true*."

"Is it?" She turned to face them fully, and the ghost-light of the village caught in her eyes. "Because I watched you find that boundary stone in the dark, Tamsin. In terrain you claim you've never crossed. You walked to it like a man walking home in fog — knowing every turning before he takes it. I watched you read the spiral on the pillar and your hand traced the second half of it, the answering mark, without your noticing. Your body knows this country even if your mouth denies it." She paused. "Someone sang to you as a child. Someone told you stories. Someone carried this place inside them and gave it to you without telling you what it was, the way parents give children everything they can't say."

Tamsin wanted to argue. They opened their mouth to argue.

What came out instead, barely above a whisper, was: "My mother hummed. All the time. Songs in no language I ever learned. The other caravan kids used to laugh at it — said she was singing gibberish, making it up." Their throat closed. They forced the words through it like forcing a door in a flood. "She hummed one the night she died. Over and over. Slower and slower. I was nine. I thought she was just — tired."

The village lights flickered, all together, like a held breath released.

And faintly — so faintly that later Tamsin would spend years deciding whether they had truly heard it or whether grief had composed it on the spot — a melody drifted across the valley. Wordless. Hummed. Rising from the lit windows the way woodsmoke rises, threading up into the cold air, and it was the song. It was the same song, the same seven falling notes their mother had died on, sung now by an entire remembered village at its remembered evening fires.

Tamsin Okafor, who had not wept since they were eleven years old and had sworn off it as a luxury of people with homes, stood in a country that did not exist and shook with the effort of not weeping in it.

Sera did not touch them. She stood beside them, close enough that their shoulders nearly brushed, and let the song pass over them both, and after a long time she said, "We should keep moving. This valley will be light soon, and light is Corvel's ally."

Tamsin nodded. They could not speak yet. They walked.

* * *

They climbed out of the haunted valley as the stars thinned, up a switchback trail toward a saddle between two peaks that Tamsin knew from a smuggler's map twenty years old — except that the map had shown the trail washed out, impassable, and the trail beneath their boots was whole and graded and lined at its curves with short white posts, the kind road-wardens set, the kind that had not been set in these mountains in thirty years.

The land was remembering forward, too. Mending itself. Preparing.

"For what?" Tamsin muttered, kicking one of the white posts half-accidentally, finding it solid as bone. "For whom? There's no one left to come home."

"Isn't there?" Sera said, and pointed ahead through the pre-dawn grey.

The saddle narrowed to a throat of rock, and across the throat of rock — impossibly, absurdly, magnificently — someone had built a toll-gate. Rough timber and iron-banded stone, a barricade across the whole width of the pass, and before it a fire burned in an iron basket, real fire, casting real warmth and real shadow, and around the fire stood figures in mismatched mail and cloaks stitched with a spiral badge.

Not ghosts. Tamsin could tell at this distance, with this light. Ghosts didn't stamp their feet against the cold. Ghosts didn't pass a wineskin hand to hand. These were living men and women, armed, watchful, stationed at a gate across a road into a country that had been erased from every ledger in the world.

"Veterans," Sera breathed. "Exiles. The ones who never accepted the annexation — they've been holding the passes all along, haven't they? All these years. Keeping the borders."

Tamsin's hand had gone to their sword without their deciding anything. Old instinct: unknown armed men at a chokepoint equals toll, tax, or ambush, and all three end in blood unless handled carefully. But some newer, stranger instinct held the other hand flat against their chest, over the pack strap, over the buried book, over the heartbeat that had not steadied since the valley.

One of the figures by the fire raised a horn to her lips. The note that came out rolled down the saddle and echoed off the flanks of the peaks, and from somewhere further up the pass, deeper into the country that wasn't supposed to exist, another horn answered it.

Then a third. And a fourth. A chain of signal fires and horn-calls running away northward into the heart of Thornfell, announcing — Tamsin understood with a lurch — announcing *them.* Two travelers crossing a border in the dark. The whole country being told, post by post, pass by pass, in the old warning-tongue of a land that refused to forget its own name:

*Someone is coming. Someone is coming home.*

The woman at the fire lowered her horn and looked directly at Tamsin across fifty yards of broken morning light, and even at that distance Tamsin could see the scarred, weathered, absolutely unimpressed face of a soldier who had been waiting a very long time.

"Hail the road!" she called, in the lowland trade-tongue first, and then — deliberately, testingly — in another language entirely, one Tamsin's mother had hummed and Tamsin's blood apparently spoke: "*Who walks the King's-road, and by what right?*"
