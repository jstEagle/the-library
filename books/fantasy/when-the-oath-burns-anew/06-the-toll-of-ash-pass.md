## 6. The Toll of Ash Pass

Sera had learned to read Tamsin Okafor the way she had once learned to read scripture: slowly, hungrily, and with the constant suspicion that the text was hiding something from her.

Three days on the mountain roads, and the mercenary still hadn't decided whether to trust her. Sera could see it in every small economy of movement — the way Tamsin walked half a pace ahead rather than beside her, the way questions about Thornfell were answered in the flat tone of a person reciting freight manifests. But there were other things too, things Tamsin probably didn't know they were showing. The way their hand drifted to their chest whenever a boundary stone passed, as if checking for a wound. The way they went silent at the old cairns and did not resume speaking until the cairns were well behind them.

They were climbing toward Ash Pass now, the last high crossing before the deep country — the country that was not supposed to exist anymore. The air had gone thin and mineral-sharp, tasting of old snow and older stone. Pines gave way to scrub pine, scrub pine to bare granite furred with lichen the color of rust and bone. Somewhere below, far behind, Sera imagined she could still smell the smoke of Corvel's riders working through the lowland villages, though she knew it was only memory performing its usual theater.

"You keep looking back," Tamsin said, without turning.

"I'm keeping watch."

"For inquisitors."

"For whatever you're not telling me."

That earned her a glance over one broad shoulder — dark eyes, weathered face, an expression that might have been amusement if it weren't so tired. "You're a defector priestess asking a smuggler what they're not telling *you*. That's a rich vein, that is."

"I never said I was a good priestess."

"No," Tamsin agreed, and turned forward again. "You didn't."

The path narrowed where it cut between two shoulders of rock, forcing them to walk single file, and Sera used the silence to study the land itself, because the land was doing something it should not have been doing.

She had felt it since yesterday morning, faint as a pulse under a wristband. Thornfell had been erased from maps for thirty years — Aldric's cartographers had redrawn the borders, burned the archives, renamed the towns with good honest imperial syllables. On paper, this was all the Marchland of Vell now, grazing territory, strategically unremarkable. And yet. The stones here remembered. She could feel it in her teeth, in the soles of her feet: a low harmonic hum, like a cathedral organ heard from three streets away. Every boundary marker they passed sang a little louder. Every cairn. Every rusted iron post where a gate had once hung.

The land was holding its breath. Or perhaps counting them in.

"Tamsin," she said quietly. "When you were a child — where did you grow up?"

"Lowlands. Saltmarsh side."

"And your mother?"

"I never met her." A beat. "My captain bought me off a debt-slaver when I was nine. Everything before that is mud and hunger and other people's names for me."

*And yet you carry the blood of kings in your veins like a splinter,* Sera thought, and did not say it. Not yet. Not on this road. There would be a moment for that revelation, and she dreaded it more than she had ever dreaded anything in her years serving Aldric's church, because she knew exactly what it would cost both of them.

The pass opened ahead without warning, the way mountain passes do — one switchback and suddenly the world fell away on either side, and they stood in a high saddle between two black peaks, a stony amphitheater open to a sky gone the color of hammered pewter. Wind came down the funnel of it with a sound like distant singing.

Tamsin stopped so abruptly that Sera nearly walked into their back.

"What—" she began.

Then she saw it too.

A wall across the saddle. Not built — *arranged*. Boulders stacked with deliberate care, old wagon axles lashed upright, iron spikes crowned with the bleached skulls of goats. And strung along the top of it, catching the gray light, banners: faded, rain-rotted, but unmistakable even in ruin. The white stag of Thornfell on a field of green, stitched by hands long dead.

"The toll-gang," Tamsin murmured. There was something strange in their voice. Something Sera had not heard there before. "I've crossed this pass eleven times. Eleven times in six years. There's never been—"

"There's never been anyone who could raise *that*," Sera finished.

Figures rose behind the barricade. Seven of them, then nine, then a dozen — hard-moving, wrapped in patchwork cloaks, and every one of them armed with the particular competence of people who had once been paid to kill for a living. Veterans. Sera marked the drill-ground precision of how they spread along the wall, the economy of it, the way the oldest among them took the center without being told.

The leader came down alone. An old woman, tall despite the stoop the mountains had bent into her, gray hair braided with strips of faded green cloth, a scar that ran from temple to jaw like a lightning-strike fossilized. She carried a spear more out of habit than need; her real weapon was her face, which had been carved by forty winters of altitude into something patient and implacable.

She stopped ten paces away and looked at Tamsin for a long time.

"Well," she said at last. Her voice was gravel poured slow. "The mountains said someone was coming. I told the others it was traders. They said no — the stones don't sing for traders." She turned her head slightly, addressing Sera without looking at her. "And a crow of Aldric's church walking free beside a stranger. That's new weather."

"I left the church," Sera said.

"They all say that." The old woman smiled without warmth. "Right up until the knives come out."

"We're not here for trouble," Tamsin said. "We're crossing to the high valleys. We'll pay the toll. Coin, salt, powder — name it."

"Oh, we'll take coin." The old woman's smile widened fractionally. "Coin keeps us in arrowheads. But coin's not the toll. Not today. Not on this pass, for you." She planted the butt of her spear against the stone, and the sound rang oddly — too resonant, as though the whole saddle were a struck bell. "You're Okafor. The caravan-master who knows these passes better than the birds do. The one who pays the shrine-tolls in the low country even though nobody's tended those shrines in thirty years. Word travels, even up here. Especially up here."

Tamsin said nothing. Sera watched the mercenary's jaw tighten, watched the small muscles around the eyes do their careful work.

"So here's the toll," the old woman said. "Not coin. Words. You'll give us the border-oath. The old one. The oath of the marches, what we swore when we were soldiers of a country that isn't allowed to have a name anymore." Around her, along the barricade, the veterans had gone very still. "Say it, and you pass. Refuse, and you can climb round by the scree slopes and lose three days to the cold, and maybe your fingers while you're at it."

"That's it?" Tamsin's voice was carefully light. Carefully, deliberately, insultingly light. "Some words? You're robbing travelers with poetry now?"

"It's not poetry," the old woman said, and for just a moment the patience in her face cracked, and underneath it Sera saw grief, raw and enormous, held up by nothing but discipline. "It's the last thing we own. Say it or climb, smuggler. Those are your choices."

Sera touched Tamsin's arm — lightly, barely a pressure through the wool sleeve. *Careful*, she meant. *Something is happening here. Can't you feel it? The whole pass is listening.*

But Tamsin shook her off with a flick of the shoulder, and Sera understood then what the mercenary was doing, because she had seen soldiers do it before: armor going on. Mockery as a shield. If the words meant nothing, then saying them could cost nothing. Tamsin was going to perform the oath as an insult precisely so that it couldn't touch them.

"Fine," Tamsin said, spreading their hands, voice pitched to carry with a busker's cynical warmth. "By the stones, then. Since everyone's so fond of stones."

They drew a breath, and recited:

*"Stone remembers what the king forgets.*
*I stand upon the boundary and I am the boundary.*
*From this height to this river, from snowmelt to saltmarsh,*
*what is Thornfell I hold, and what holds me*
*I will not forsake — not for crown, not for coin,*
*not for fear, not for fire.*
*Let the pass know my name and let the pass answer:*
***the border holds where the heart holds it.***

There it ends. There is nothing after the fires."

For one heartbeat, the wind filled the silence, and nothing happened, and Sera felt the beginning of relief—

Then the pass answered.

It began underfoot. A glow in the cracks between the flagstones, faint as embers breathed upon — gold-white, shot through with green, the exact green of the ruined banners. It crept outward in branching lines, following seams in the rock no human eye could have mapped, and where it went, the air itself kindled: ghost-fires rising knee-high across the whole saddle, hundreds of them, thousands, standing in ranks like a congregation, burning without heat, without smoke, without consuming anything at all. The pewter sky caught the light and turned to hammered bronze. The black peaks wore crowns of flame.

Along the barricade, the veterans had sunk to their knees. The old woman had dropped her spear. She was staring at Tamsin with an expression that broke Sera's heart to witness — awe and terror and a joy so naked it was almost obscene, the look of a starving woman set before a feast she doesn't dare believe in.

"The fires," the old woman whispered. "Thirty years. Thirty years and the fires remember the words. *Maren* — Maren, get down here, you have to see—"

But Sera wasn't watching the veterans. She was watching Tamsin.

The mercenary hadn't moved. Hadn't knelt, hadn't flinched. They stood in the middle of a burning amphitheater with the ghost-light gilding their face, and their expression was worse than awe. It was recognition. The look of a person hearing a song they didn't know they knew, note for note, and understanding with horrible clarity that some part of them had always known it — that the mockery had been a lie before it ever left their mouth, that the words had come out *right*, in the old cadence, with the old stresses, in an accent they'd never been taught.

"No," Tamsin said softly. To the fires. To themselves. "No, that's not— I made that up as I went, I got half of it wrong—"

"You got none of it wrong," Sera said. Her voice shook. She let it. "Every word. In the march-cadence. Tamsin — the fires don't rise for liars. They don't rise for strangers. They rose for *you*."

"I don't want this." The mercenary's composure was cracking now, and what showed underneath was not anger but something closer to panic, clean and animal. "Whatever this is — the book, the Door, now this — I'm a caravan-guide. I move cargo. I don't belong to dead countries and burning passes, I *belong to myself*—"

"Do you?" It came out harsher than Sera intended, and she watched it land, watched it wound. She pressed on anyway, because some truths are knives that must go all the way in or not at all. "Then why did your hand go to your chest at every boundary stone? Why did you pay the shrine-tolls? Why does the ground sing under your feet and not mine?"

Tamsin looked at her, and for a moment Sera saw the child behind the mercenary's eyes — the nine-year-old bought off a slaver, mud and hunger and other people's names — and she almost reached out. Almost.

But the old woman was rising, unsteady, and coming toward Tamsin with her arms slightly open, the way one approaches something sacred and easily startled.

"Child," she said, and her gravel voice had gone soft. "What's your mother's name?"

"I told you. I never—"

"Your *mother's*. The woman who bore you. Think. Was there a name? On a tag, a ledger, anything?"

And Sera saw it hit — saw the mercenary go rigid, because there *was* a name, there always is, some scrap of paper in a debt-slaver's inventory that a nine-year-old memorized and buried and never spoke of again for twenty-five years.

Tamsin's lips parted.

Whatever name was about to be spoken — whether truth or refusal, surrender or defiance — the mountain took the choice away. Because at that moment the ghost-fires all across the pass leaned, all together, all in the same direction, like wheat before wind — leaning east, toward the deep country, toward the place the old maps called the Last Door — and in the sudden hush the wind carried down the funnel of the saddle a sound that froze the marrow of every soul present.

Horns. Many horns, far below and closing fast. Imperial signal-horns, in the triple-call pattern Sera knew from her church years: *heretic sighted. seal the passes. burn the high country.*

Corvel had entered the mountains.

The old woman's face transformed in an instant — grief shoved down, the soldier hauled up. She snatched up her spear and turned to her kneeling veterans, and her voice cracked across the saddle like a whip.

"To arms! Break out the war-arrows! Anyone who ever swore to Thornfell — the oath is awake, and the empire is coming to put it back to sleep!" She spun to Tamsin, and her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "You wanted to belong to yourself, smuggler? Too late. The pass knows your name now. So does the man hunting you. Choose fast — the fire-road through the deep country opens behind our lines, and it closes at dusk."

Behind the barricade, the veterans were already moving, and above them the ghost-fires burned on, rank upon rank upon rank, waiting — Sera could not shake the feeling — for an order only one person in the pass had the right to give.
