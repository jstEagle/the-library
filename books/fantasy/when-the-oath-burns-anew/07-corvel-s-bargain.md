## 7. Corvel's Bargain

The letter came wrapped in oilcloth and tied with red cord, the kind of knot a soldier ties when he wants you to know he was trained by someone who mattered. It had been waiting at the waystation at Cold Ford for three days, wedged under the loose stone where smugglers left messages and inquisitors, apparently, left invitations.

Tamsin read it twice by the light of a shuttered lantern while Sera watched from across the stable loft, her face half-shadowed, her hands very still in her lap.

*To the one who carries what should have burned,* it began.

*You know me now, though we have not met. I am Magistrate Corvel, servant of King Aldric, keeper of the King's peace in these mountains. I hold a woman called Brannagh Voss, once captain of the Grey Company, once your commander, once — if rumor serves — something closer to family.*

*She is alive. She will remain so until moonset three nights hence. Bring the book to the chapel at Hollowmere. Come alone. Come unarmed. Do this, and she walks free with my word upon it. Fail, and I will burn her as I burned the texts at Marrowgate — slowly, publicly, and with every courtesy of law.*

*I am not a cruel man. I am a thorough one. There is a difference, though I understand the distinction may be lost on you at present.*

*Corvel.*

The paper was good stock, cream-colored, watermarked with Aldric's stag. Even his threats were official.

Tamsin set the letter down on their knee and looked at their hands. They were steady. That surprised them. Inside, something was moving like water finding cracks — not panic exactly, but the cold spreading knowledge that the world had just narrowed to a single corridor with walls made of other people's lives.

"You read it," Sera said. Not a question. She'd been watching their face the whole time.

"Brannagh." The name came out rougher than Tamsin intended. They cleared their throat. "My captain. Twenty-two years in the Grey Company, and she carried me out of the Saltmarsh campaign when I was nineteen and stupid and bleeding from places I didn't know could bleed. She taught me how to read terrain, how to read men, how to—" They stopped. "She taught me everything I am that isn't in that book."

Sera rose and crossed the loft, careful of the boards that creaked. She'd learned the building's voice in two days; she learned everything fast, which was one of the things about her that frightened Tamsin most. She sat down beside them, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and did not take the letter.

"What will you do?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Tamsin laughed, without any humor in it. "The obvious answer is go. Walk into his chapel, hand over the book, walk out with Brannagh. He gets his ash-burning, I get my captain, everyone goes home."

"And the Door stays shut."

"And Thornfell stays dead." Tamsin said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "And every refugee in the lowlands keeps running from Aldric's riders with nowhere to run *to*. And the god stays silent. And the prophecy sits in an inquisitor's fire doing what all prophecies do when they're burned, which is nothing."

"But you're going to consider it anyway."

"I'm going to consider it because it's *Brannagh*, Sera." The words cracked out of them louder than they meant, and somewhere below, a horse shifted in its stall. Tamsin lowered their voice. "You've been a priestess. You've been a defector. You've lost things, I can see that. But you haven't lost—"

"Don't tell me what I've lost." Sera's voice stayed soft, which somehow made it worse. "I lost a god, Tamsin. I sat in her temple for eleven years and poured out my heart to silence. Then I heard her whisper, once, and I threw away everything — my vows, my rank, my name in the church rolls — to follow a whisper. You think I don't understand choosing between what you love and what you're *for*?" She shook her head. "I understand it better than you do. That's why I'm asking. What will you do?"

Tamsin looked at the letter again. *Come alone.* Corvel would know they wouldn't come alone. Corvel would be counting on it, probably — counting on Sera, counting on ambushes and counter-ambushes, playing the game three moves deep the way institutional men always did. But there was another possibility, colder and more likely: that Corvel genuinely wanted only the book, because the book was evidence, and evidence was how a magistrate built a case, and a case was how a magistrate built a career, and a career was how a man who believed himself righteous kept believing it.

"He'll keep his word," Tamsin said slowly. "That's the worst part. If I give him the book, he'll let her go. Men like him always keep the small promises so they can break the large ones with clean hands."

"Then give him the book."

"And then steal it back."

Sera went still.

Tamsin turned to look at her fully. In the lantern light their face must have shown something, because Sera's expression shifted from wariness to alarm. "No," she said.

"It's what I'm thinking."

"It's a *terrible* plan."

"It's not a plan yet. It's a direction." Tamsin stood, pacing the length of the loft, four steps and turn, four steps and turn — an old habit, Brannagh's habit, drilled into them during a winter siege when pacing had been the only exercise available. "Think it through. Corvel wants the book because it's proof. Proof of prophecy, proof of sedition, proof that the Oathbearer exists. He needs it intact — burning it costs him his case. Which means when I hand it over, it goes somewhere safe first. A strongbox. A locked chest. Somewhere he can produce it for witnesses before he destroys it, because a man like Corvel doesn't destroy anything without an audience. He'll want to burn it publicly, at Hollowmere or back in the lowlands, with the town assembled, with a sermon. That gives us time. A day, maybe two."

"And between the handing-over and the burning, you propose to rob a magistrate guarded by the King's own riders."

"I propose to try."

"*Try.*" Sera pressed her fingers to her eyes. "Do you hear yourself? This isn't a toll-gang or a rival crew. These are inquisitors. They don't sleep, they don't drink, and they don't leave loose ends. If you fail, you lose the book *and* Brannagh *and* your life, and I lose—" She stopped herself. The unfinished sentence hung in the air between them like smoke.

"You lose what?" Tamsin asked quietly.

"Nothing. Forget it." She dropped her hands. "If you succeed, you teach him that you negotiate in bad faith. He'll never trade again. Every hostage he takes after this dies, because you've shown him hostages are worthless against you. Have you thought about that? Have you thought about what happens to the *next* person he takes?"

The knife went in exactly where she aimed it. Tamsin felt it turn.

"No," they admitted. "I hadn't."

"Good. Now think about this too." Sera stood, and for a moment she seemed taller than she was, the way she did when the old priestess surfaced through the fugitive. "The book names you Oathbearer. It doesn't name Brannagh. Corvel took her because she's leverage on *you* — which means as long as you carry the mission, everyone you love becomes a lever. That's not a bargain you can win, Tamsin. That's a rack he can turn forever. Unless you stop being someone who can be leveraged. Unless you become someone who walks away."

"Walk away from Brannagh."

"Walk toward the Door."

They faced each other across the small space, and the lantern guttered, and outside the wind came down off the high passes carrying the smell of snow and old stone, the smell Tamsin had known all their life without knowing it was the smell of a country that had been erased.

"I can't," Tamsin said. It wasn't defiance. It was simpler and more terrible than that — a plain statement of fact, the way you'd say *the bridge is out* or *the rope won't hold*. "I can't walk away from her. Whatever I am to Thornfell, whatever the book says, I was hers first. She made me. And if I let her burn to save a prophecy, then everything I'd be saving the country *for* is already gone. You'd have an Oathbearer with no oath left worth the name."

Sera looked at them for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes — grief, maybe, or recognition, or the memory of a choice she'd made differently and regretted ever since.

"All right," she said finally. "Then we do it properly. Not a robbery. An exchange with teeth."

* * *

They spent the next day preparing, and preparation, for Tamsin, was a form of prayer — the only kind that had ever answered.

They scouted Hollowmere from the ridge above it, lying flat under gorse while the wind covered their scent. The chapel was small and ugly, a converted tithe-barn with a new steeple bolted on like an apology, ringed by a low wall. Six riders visible, which meant ten, which meant fourteen. Corvel's tent was pitched in the churchyard among the graves, because of course it was; the man slept among the dead the way other men slept among books.

Brannagh was in the chapel. They saw her brought out at midday for water — gray-faced, one arm bound against her chest, walking upright anyway, because Brannagh Voss walked upright at her own funeral. She scanned the ridgeline as they marched her back inside, slow and deliberate, the way she'd taught Tamsin to scan a horizon twenty years ago. Looking for signs. Looking, maybe, for them.

*Don't come,* her posture said. *Don't you dare come.*

"I'm coming," Tamsin murmured into the gorse.

At dusk they went over it one final time in the hollow below the ridge, scratching lines in the dirt with a stick. Sera would take the eastern wall an hour before moonset, where the drainage culvert ran under the foundation — old smuggler's architecture, older than the chapel, and Tamsin knew it because Tamsin knew every vein in these mountains. Tamsin would walk in the front, alone and unarmed as instructed, and make the trade, and walk out with Brannagh, and the moment the chapel door closed behind them, Sera would be inside the tithe-barn's hidden cellar, where Corvel's people — lowland men, not mountain men — would never think to look.

"The book goes in the culvert with me," Sera said. "Not a strongbox, not a chest. The moment you hand it over, I have it, and we're gone before his sermon's even drafted."

"And if he searches me on the way in and finds I'm not carrying it?"

"Then we're past negotiating anyway, aren't we?" Sera's mouth twisted. "Say it plainly, Tamsin. Are you going to hand him the real book or a forgery?"

Tamsin had spent the night before copying what pages they could — the name, the date, the title of Oathbearer — onto matching vellum, aging it with tea and ash. It would fool a glance. It might fool an hour. It would not fool a magistrate who had spent his career authenticating heretical texts.

"The real book," Tamsin said. "He keeps his word, I keep mine. We steal it back clean or not at all. If I cheat him in the chapel, he burns Brannagh out of spite before we clear the wall."

"So we give a magistrate the actual prophecy, trust his honor, and bet everything on a culvert and a cellar." Sera stared into the dirt map. "My order used to call this presumption. We had forty words for it."

"What did your order call it when it worked?"

"A miracle." She looked up, and for the first time in two days, she smiled — thin and crooked and real. "Let's go commit one."

* * *

Moonset came gray and cold, and Tamsin walked down into Hollowmere with empty hands and a full heart, which was, they reflected, exactly backwards from how a sensible person would arrange it.

The sentries took their knives, their boot-blades, the garrote wire in their collar — all of it, professionally, without comment. Corvel received them in the chapel itself, standing beneath the fresh-painted icon of Aldric's stag where the altar of Veyra had been torn out; you could still see the pale rectangle on the floor where the old stone had rested, like a scar, like a grave-marker for something that couldn't die.

Magistrate Corvel was smaller than Tamsin expected. Neat, gray-templed, with ink-stained fingers and the patient eyes of a man who had read every horror he'd ever inflicted and found it justified. He looked less like an executioner than a schoolmaster, which was somehow far worse.

"Tamsin Okafor." He said the name carefully, tasting it. "Or should I say Oathbearer?"

"Should you?"

"I'd rather not. Titles complicate things." He gestured, and two soldiers brought Brannagh forward from the shadow of the vestry. She was worse than she'd looked at distance — lips split, ribs strapped, one eye swollen nearly shut. But when she saw Tamsin her jaw set in the old familiar line, and she said, clearly enough to carry:

"You absolute idiot. You came."

"Someone has to keep you from dying stupid, Captain."

"That was supposed to be *my* line, all those years ago." Her voice caught on the last word, just slightly, and Tamsin's chest tightened like a fist.

Corvel watched the exchange with clinical interest. "You love each other. Good. That makes this honest." He lifted a strongbox onto the altar rail — iron-banded, locked, exactly as predicted. "The book, please."

Tamsin drew it from inside their coat — they'd been allowed to carry it, because Corvel understood that some things had to be surrendered freely to mean anything — and held it a moment. A century of waiting. A nation's last testament. Their own name in old ink beside tonight's date.

*Oathbearer of the Last Door.*

"Your word first," Tamsin said. "She walks out of this valley free. No riders following. Your word on it."

"On my office, on the King's peace, and on whatever I still believe in," Corvel said, and the frightening thing was that he sounded sincere. "I told you. I am not a cruel man."

"No," Tamsin agreed, setting the book in his ink-stained hands. "You're a thorough one."

He opened it immediately — couldn't help himself — and for a moment his composure flickered as he read the date beside the name, and Tamsin saw something pass behind his eyes that looked almost like fear, quickly filed away. He closed the book. Nodded to the soldiers.

They cut Brannagh's bonds. She swayed; Tamsin caught her arm and took her weight without making a show of it, the way you carry a wounded friend past enemies, daring anyone to comment.

"Captain," Tamsin murmured, turning her toward the door. "Don't look back. Don't run. Walk."

"Where am I walking *to*?"

"Cold Ford. There's a horse waiting in the third stall, gray mare, lame on the near side but sound enough. Ride south to the coast road and don't stop for anyone wearing white." A pause. "There'll be coin in the saddlebag."

"You planned this." It wasn't a question. Brannagh limped beside them toward the chapel door, and her voice dropped to a rasp only Tamsin could hear. "Which means the book isn't really his for long. Which means you're staying."

"Captain—"

"Twenty-two years I commanded you, and I know every trick in that stubborn skull." At the threshold she stopped, gripped Tamsin's shoulder with what strength she had left, and looked at them with her one good eye — fierce, furious, proud. "Whatever you're about to do, do it fast and do it mean. And Tamsin —" her grip tightened — "whatever they told you in that book, whatever name they put on you — you were mine before you were anybody's prophecy. Remember whose you are."

Then she was through the door, into the gray pre-dawn, walking — not running, walking, because she was Brannagh Voss — toward the tree line and the horse and the south.

Corvel stood at the altar rail with the prophecy in his hands, watching them both with those patient schoolmaster's eyes, and Tamsin understood, with sudden terrible clarity, that he had seen all of it. The plan. The culvert. The mare. Perhaps not the details, but the shape of it — the shape of a person who would always, always come back for their people.

"You'll try to take it back," he said quietly. It wasn't an accusation. It was almost gentle.

"Yes," Tamsin said.

He nodded slowly, as if confirming a calculation. "Then I find myself in an interesting position, Oathbearer. Because I gave my word that *she* goes free — and she does. But I made no promise about you." He turned the book over in his hands. "And I begin to suspect that you are the text that truly matters. Books can be rewritten. Believers are so much harder to edit."

Behind the chapel wall, faint as breath, Tamsin heard the culvert grate shift — Sera, in position, waiting for the signal that would never be safe to give.

Outside, the sky was going the color of ash, and somewhere down the valley, horns were sounding — not Corvel's horns. Deeper ones. Slower. The horns of an army coming up the passes, and Magistrate Corvel turned his head toward the sound with the first true uncertainty Tamsin had yet seen on his careful face.

"Well," he said softly. "It seems the King has decided to attend to matters himself."
