## 8. The Book Given Up

The message came at dusk, tied to the leg of a gull that should never have flown this high into the mountains. Tamsin knew the knot before they cut it loose — mercenary cordwork, the kind drilled into every hand in the Company of the Broken Standard. The paper inside smelled of tallow smoke and something sweeter beneath it, like flowers laid on a corpse.

*Come alone to the Widow's Span by moonrise. Bring the book, or Captain Vess dies at dawn. She has been very brave, which is to say she has not yet told us anything useful. — C.*

Tamsin read it three times by the light of a shielded candle, while Sera sat across the cache's low ceiling of stone, sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. That was what Sera did with her hands when her mind was running too fast — Tamsin had learned that much about her in the weeks since the shrine, and hated how well they'd learned it.

"Corvel," Sera said, reading Tamsin's face rather than the letter.

"He has Vess."

Sera set down the knife. "You don't know that for certain—"

"The knot's hers. She taught me to tie it." Tamsin folded the letter along its creases, once, twice, until it was small enough to disappear inside a fist. "He wants the book. He'll trade her for it."

"And you believe him? A man who burns villages over ink?"

"I believe he keeps his bargains." Tamsin looked up. "It's the only thing about him I do believe. Corvel doesn't lie. He doesn't need to. He just tells you exactly enough truth to make the trap feel like mercy."

Outside, wind combed through the pines with a sound like distant water, and somewhere below them the world went on being the country-that-wasn't, Thornfell, all its boundary stones and buried songs. Tamsin felt the book's weight against their ribs where it rode in an oilcloth wrap under their coat. A century old and heavier than any blade they'd ever carried.

"You're thinking of giving it up," Sera said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm thinking about what happens to Vess if I don't."

"She's one woman. The Door is a nation."

Tamsin stood, slow, joints aching from three days of climbing. When they spoke, their voice came out flat as slate. "When I was nineteen, Vess pulled me out of a river with a rope around my own neck because I'd botched a crossing and panicked. She held the line while everyone else shouted advice. She didn't let go even when the current nearly took us both. You want me to weigh her against a *nation*, ask her what she'd say." They paused. "She'd say leave me, take the bridge, keep moving. And then she'd call me a fool for listening."

"So you won't listen."

"So I won't listen."

Sera rose too. In the candlelight her face was all planes and shadow, beautiful the way winter is beautiful, and there was something in her expression Tamsin couldn't name — anger, or fear wearing anger's clothes. "That book may be the only map to the Last Door. Not a copy. Not a memory. *The* map. If Corvel burns it—"

"He won't burn it. He needs it more than we do." Tamsin pulled the oilcloth bundle free and held it between them, unopened. "Which is why I'm going to give it to him. And why tonight is going to be a very long night."

* * *

The Widow's Span was an old smuggler's bridge, thrown across a gorge so deep the river at its bottom was only a rumor of white noise. Its timbers were black with sixty years of weather, and its name came from a story no two tellers agreed on, except that every version ended with someone falling. Moonrise turned the mist rising from the gorge into slow silver ghosts.

Corvel was already there. Of course he was. He stood at the far end of the span with four riders behind him and a lantern held at hip height, and he looked exactly as he had in the lowland town: neat, gray-eyed, patient, a man whose cruelty was indistinguishable from his diligence. Beside him, kneeling on the frost-stiff planks with her wrists bound, was Captain Vess.

She looked terrible. One eye was swollen shut, and there was dried blood in a long ribbon down her chin, but when she saw Tamsin she grinned with the half of her mouth that still worked. "Took you long enough," she called. "I've been bored out of my skull."

"Shut up, Vess," Tamsin said, and walked out onto the bridge.

Each step rang hollow underfoot. Halfway across, Corvel raised a hand and the riders stopped; he came forward alone, boots deliberate on the boards, and stopped an arm's length away. Up close he smelled of ink and horse and clean cold air. No incense. Tamsin had expected incense — inquisitors in the songs always reeked of it.

"You came," Corvel said. "Good. I confess I gave it perhaps even odds."

"You have something of mine. I have something of yours."

"A fair framing." His gaze dropped to the bundle under Tamsin's arm. "May I?"

Tamsin unwrapped the oilcloth slowly, letting the book sit bare in their hands — old leather, cracked spine, the title stamped in faded gilt: *The Thornfell Cycle, Being Certain Prophecies of the Last Door.* Their name was written inside it in a dead man's careful script. Their own name. Tonight's date had come and gone two nights past, and the world had kept turning, which was either comfort or horror depending on the hour.

"Vess first," Tamsin said. "Untie her and send her to this side. Then you get the book."

"Inquisitors are famously mistrustful people, Okafor." But Corvel gestured, and one of the riders led Vess forward across the span, cutting her wrist-cords at the midpoint before withdrawing. She staggered the last few steps and caught Tamsin's arm with fingers gone clumsy from cold and worse.

"Okafor," she murmured, low, under the wind. "Whatever you're planning — don't be clever. Be alive."

"That's the whole plan."

"Liar." But she squeezed their arm, and let herself be pushed gently toward the near shore, where she sat down hard on a boundary stone older than either nation and began, grimly, to laugh or cry or both.

Tamsin held out the book.

Corvel took it with both hands, reverent as a man receiving a relic, and for one unguarded moment his face opened into something almost young — hunger, awe, the look of a scholar who has finally held the text he'd built his life around secondhand. Then it closed again, and he was the magistrate, and he inclined his head.

"Our bargain is kept. You have my word no further action will be taken against you or your companions."

"Your word."

"I am many things, Okafor, but apostate to my own promises isn't one of them." He tucked the book into his saddlebag with care. "Go home. Whatever Thornfell was, it's dust. Let it stay dust."

He turned back toward his riders. And Tamsin stood on the Widow's Span in the moonlight, empty-handed, watching the man who would erase the last map of a dead country ride away with it slung at his saddle, and felt nothing at all for the length of three heartbeats.

Then the fourth heartbeat came, and with it the thought that had been waiting patiently all evening, dressed in guilt and resolve and the smell of tallow smoke:

*He said nothing about the bridge.*

* * *

They waited until the riders' fires burned low in the valley below — Corvel made camp at the span's foot, cautious, unwilling to travel the mountain road by night. Tamsin watched the camp from the ridge above, counting sentries by the way the shadows moved: four awake, rotating, predictable. Mercenaries' habits. Inquisitors borrowed their methods from mercenaries; Tamsin had spent a decade being the thing they copied.

Sera crouched beside them, and her silence had teeth.

"No," she said, before Tamsin had spoken a word.

"You haven't heard the plan."

"I don't need to. Your face is doing it. The answer is no. We got Vess back — that was the price, it's paid, it's done. If you go down there and he catches you, everything ends tonight. The Door. Thornfell. All of it. For a *book*."

"For the map," Tamsin corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is whether anyone ever gets out of this country alive." They checked the knife at their belt, the flint in their pocket, the coil of waxed cord looped over their shoulder. "Corvel sleeps fifty feet from a timber bridge that's the only crossing over that gorge for twenty miles. He thinks the bargain bought him safety. Bargains don't buy safety. They buy time."

"Tamsin."

"Sera." They turned and found her face close, moonlit, furious, and beneath the fury — they were learning to read her, gods help them — afraid. Not of Corvel. Of this. Of the shape Tamsin's loyalty took when it closed its fist. "I gave my word to a captain once that I'd never leave a debt unpaid. Corvel has my book. My book has my name in it. I'm not leaving both in the same saddlebag overnight."

"If he wakes—"

"He won't. And if he does—" Tamsin smiled, and knew from Sera's expression that it wasn't a kind smile. "Then he learns what Thornfell does to men who steal from it."

* * *

Descending into the camp took longer than the deed itself. Tamsin went down the scree slope on their belly, slow as growing things, matching breath to wind, while Sera held the ridge with a bow she claimed to have renounced along with Aldric's church and had evidently lied about renouncing. Two sentries at the fire, one walking the perimeter, one asleep sitting up — Vess's training would have failed him. Tamsin slid past the walker in the shadow of a supply wagon, counted to thirty, and reached the horses.

Corvel's bay gelding stood at the picket line, ears flicking at the strange night sounds. The saddlebag hung where saddlebags hang. Tamsin's hands, steady as prayer, drew out the oilcloth bundle — heavier now, absurdly, as though the book had eaten the day's guilt and grown fat on it —

And the gelding screamed.

Not whinnied. Screamed, wheeling, and the picket line jerked, and the sleeping sentry woke with a shout, and the fire scattered sparks upward like startled birds. Tamsin ran. There was no art left in it, no craft, just legs and terror and the book crushed against their chest, boots hammering frozen ground while shouts bloomed behind them and a crossbow bolt hissed past close enough to part the hair.

The bridge.

They hit the Widow's Span at a dead sprint, planks booming under their feet like a war drum, and behind them torches were lighting, and Corvel's voice rose above the chaos — not screaming, worse, *calm*: "Let him run. Cut the approaches. He can't cross back."

But Tamsin wasn't running to cross back.

Halfway along the span, they stopped, spun, and knelt, and pulled the flint from their pocket, and set the waxed cord alight where they'd soaked it into the bridge's central beams an hour before moonrise — while Sera was still arguing, while the plan had already been halfway built. Old timber. Old pitch. Sixty years of dry rot. It caught like it had been waiting its whole life for this, flame roaring up through the gaps in the planks with a sound like applause.

Tamsin stood on the burning bridge with the book in their arms and watched Corvel's riders reach the near approach and pull up short, horses shying from the heat, the gorge full of orange light and drifting embers that the wind carried out over the void like a handful of thrown stars.

For a moment, through the smoke, Tamsin met Corvel's eyes across the fire.

The magistrate did not rage. He stood very still among his men, lantern-light and firelight fighting on his face, and he looked at Tamsin the way a scholar looks at a page that has contradicted everything he believed he understood. Then, without a word, he turned away — and Tamsin would spend many nights afterward wondering what it cost him to do it.

* * *

Sera was waiting at the treeline when Tamsin came off the far slope, smoke-stinking, singed, alive. Vess leaned against a pine behind her, wrapped in every blanket they owned, watching with her one good eye.

"You absolute—" Sera started, and stopped, and pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth, and Tamsin realized with distant surprise that she was shaking. Not with rage. With the specific fury of someone who had spent the whole descent convinced they were watching a death and could do nothing to stop it. "The plan was suicide. The *bridge* — you lit it with yourself standing on it—"

"It was wet on the north third. I checked."

"You *checked*—"

"Sera." Tamsin held up the book, oilcloth scorched at one corner but intact. "I got it back. We have the map. We move at first light, deeper into the passes, before Corvel finds another way across."

"We?" Sera laughed, and it came out cracked. "You set a bridge on fire while standing on it, and you say *we move at first light* like it's a normal thing to say. Like I could watch you do that and simply — continue." Her voice dropped, roughened. "Do you know what I thought, on that ridge? I thought, there it is. There's the Oathbearer. The one the prophecy chose. And it doesn't matter what I — it doesn't matter what—" She cut herself off, jaw tightening, and looked away toward the dark mass of the mountains.

Behind her, Vess snorted. "For the record," the captain rasped, "I told her not to be clever too. Nobody listens to me."

"Nobody's listened to you for years, Vess. It's why you're captain."

Tamsin wanted to say something to Sera — something true, something about the fourth heartbeat, about how the guilt had arrived on schedule and been immediately, shamefully outweighed by the relief of the book's weight against their ribs. About how they were beginning to suspect that everything they'd ever called duty was just love wearing armor, and that this terrified them more than Corvel ever could.

Instead they said, "First light. Sleep."

Sera looked at them a long moment, firelight dying in her eyes, and whatever she saw in Tamsin's face made her exhale — not forgiveness, not yet, but a truce. "Fine. First light." She turned toward the trees, then paused. "For what it's worth — the bridge was a good idea. I hate that it was a good idea."

From the ridge above, far off across the gorge, Tamsin thought they saw a single lantern still burning at the edge of Corvel's camp — motionless, facing the burnt ruin of the Widow's Span, keeping its vigil over ash and empty air.

Watching. Waiting. Learning.

And in the book against Tamsin's chest, beneath their own name, beneath the words *Oathbearer of the Last Door*, they could not shake the feeling that some new line of ink was drying somewhere in that century-old text — a line none of them had read yet, and all of them were now walking toward.
