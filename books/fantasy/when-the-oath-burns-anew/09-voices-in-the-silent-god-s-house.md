## 9. Voices in the Silent God's House

The temple of Veyra had been dying for thirty years, and it wore its dying like a queen wears mourning — slowly, deliberately, with terrible dignity.

Sera stood in the entrance hall and felt the cold of it settle into her bones, a cold she remembered from her novitiate, though she had never set foot in this particular house. Every temple of the silent god was cold. That was doctrine. Veyra did not warm Her faithful; She watched them. The warmth was supposed to come from within, from the certainty that someone vast and patient stood behind your shoulder, attending to every breath you drew.

For eleven years Sera had believed that attention was real. For four years since her defection she had believed it was not. Now she stood on the threshold between those two beliefs, and both of them were shaking.

"Tamsin." She said the name to the dark ahead of them, where the nave opened like the mouth of something swallowed. "We should make camp outside. The roof—"

"The roof held for a hundred years before Aldric's men stopped paying for candles," Tamsin said behind her. Their voice was flat, mercenary-flat, the voice they used when fear was being converted into logistics. "It'll hold one more night. And Corvel's riders won't look for us in a god's graveyard."

That was true, and Sera hated how much of their recent life had become a sequence of true things she wished were otherwise.

They went in together. The great doors had long since rotted off their hinges; what remained were two stumps of black oak carved with the sigil of the listening ear, worn smooth by centuries of pilgrims' hands. Sera touched one as she passed, an old reflex, and was ashamed of the relief she felt when the wood was only wood.

The nave stretched away under a vault of cracked stone. Moonlight fell through the shattered clerestory in long silver blades, cutting across rows of pews furred with dust and moss. At the far end, the altar stood bare — no statue, no icon, because Veyra had never permitted one. The silent god was represented by absence: an empty alcove, a space shaped like a person, so that worshipers could stand in the god's place and understand what it meant to attend without speaking.

Sera had spent eleven years standing in that space. In seminary they called it *the Vigil*, and the eldest priestesses said that if you held the vigil truly — if you emptied yourself completely — you might, once in a lifetime, feel the god lean close.

She had never felt it. Not once. Not in eleven years of vigils, not through the deaths of two abbesses, not through the night she had first read Aldric's edicts aloud in a Thornfell chapel while the congregation wept. Nothing had ever leaned close. And then, four months ago, in the crypt beneath the cathedral in Carreth, scrubbing soot from the memorial stones of a country that officially did not exist —

*Serah.*

Her name, spoken into the marrow of her skull by no throat she could find. One word. It had unmade her faster than any argument ever could.

She had told Tamsin it was a whisper. It had been a shout. It had been the loudest thing she had ever heard, and she had run from it for four months, and now she was walking back toward it voluntarily, and she could not have explained why if Tamsin asked. Tamsin did not ask. Tamsin had stopped asking questions about the god somewhere after Ash Pass, after the ghost-fires, after the boundary stone and the blood that would not close. Tamsin asked instead: *How far? How cold? Can we burn the pews?*

Practical questions. Sera answered them and was grateful.

"We'll sleep in the transept," she said. "Away from the door. Stone holds the wind less than wood does."

Tamsin grunted acknowledgment and began unpacking with the economy of someone who had slept in worse places and expected to again. Sera watched them work — the careful way they favored the wounded side, the wound that refused to knit, bound now in linen gone brown at the edges. Three days. Any cut Tamsin had ever taken before the boundary stone had closed in three days. This one sat in their flesh like a sentence waiting to be finished, and Sera knew what finished it: the old oath, sworn in infancy by parents Tamsin had never known, binding a child of Thornfell's last royal line to a god who had been silenced. The oath was failing because the god was failing, or the god was failing because the oath was, and either way Tamsin Okafor was bleeding out of history at a rate no physician could stop.

Only the Door could stop it. Only the prophecy. Only the thing Sera had known since Carreth and concealed until the fork in the pass, where the concealment had cost her more than confession ever would have.

Tamsin had forgiven nothing. But they had let her walk beside them again, and forgiveness, Sera thought, was a country you entered in stages.

"I'll take first watch," Tamsin said.

"You should sleep. The wound—"

"The wound will still be there in four hours. So will I." They settled against a pillar, sword across their knees, and closed their eyes in the way of soldiers — lightly, with one ear open. "Say whatever you came here to say, priestess. I'll be listening. Apparently that's contagious."

Something almost like humor. Sera took it as permission, which perhaps it was.

* * *

She made her vigil alone in the moonlit nave, kneeling on stone that had absorbed a century of knees before hers, and found that she did not know how to begin.

Prayer had once been as natural to her as walking. Then it became performance — first for superiors, then for herself, then for the version of her that needed to believe defection had been principle rather than terror. Now, kneeling in the house of the god who had broken her life open with a single syllable, she discovered that prayer was none of those things. Prayer was a door. And she had nailed hers shut from the inside.

*Veyra.* She tried the name silently first. The silence did not shift. *Veyra, I'm here. I came back. I don't know if that's courage or cowardice and I don't suppose You care about the difference.*

Nothing. Dust settled. Moonlight moved by increments no eye could track.

*You spoke to me once.* Her hands tightened on her thighs. *Once in forty years of Your church's existence, as far as anyone knows. Why me? I was nobody. I was mopping soot off the names of dead people You didn't save. If You wanted an instrument, there were better ones. There were braver ones.*

The silence continued, vast and indifferent, and Sera felt the old despair rise in her like water in a well — the despair of the abandoned child, the despair that had driven her half-mad in the months after Carreth, that had made her chase rumors of prophetic texts down smuggler roads until she'd found a mercenary with a name written in a century-old book.

*I hate You.* She thought it clearly, deliberately, the way she had never dared think it in the cathedral. *I want You to know that. I defected, and I lost everything, and I've been hunted for four years, and I did all of it because of one word in the dark. One word. Do You understand what that costs? Do You count costs at all, or is silence free for You the way it has never been free for us?*

Still nothing. Of course nothing.

And yet — she knelt there, hating, and found that the hatred had a strange texture to it, a heat, an aliveness. You could not hate a stone this thoroughly. You could not address a void this precisely. Hatred, she realized with a lurch that was almost laughter, was a form of prayer. It assumed a listener. It bent the knee even while cursing the knee-bending. She had been praying furiously for four years and calling it apostasy.

*Fine,* she thought. *Fine. Then hear this too: I'm afraid. I've been afraid since the day You said my name. Afraid I imagined it. Afraid I didn't. Afraid of what You'll ask, because gods don't chat, Veyra — gods ask, and what You're going to ask for, I already know. It's in the book. It needs her blood and my faith and probably my life, and I would rather die than—*

The candle-flame of her anger guttered.

Because the silence had changed.

Not broken — Sera would have known, would have leapt up screaming — but *changed*, the way the air changes before a storm, pressure shifting in the ears. The nave felt suddenly smaller and infinitely larger at once. The moonlight thickened. The dust hung motionless in the silver blades of light, each mote suspended as though the whole world had paused to listen, and Sera understood with animal certainty that she was no longer speaking into emptiness.

Something was attending.

Every instinct she had acquired in four years of exile screamed at her to run. She stayed on her knees. Her heart slammed against her ribs hard enough to hurt. The cold of the temple deepened, gathered, pressed down upon her shoulders like a hand laid there — not cruel, not kind, simply *present*, the weight of attention that doctrine had promised and reality had withheld for eleven years, and now that it finally arrived it was so much larger than she had imagined, so much older, a presence like the underside of a glacier, and she heard herself whimper, actually whimper, a priestess of twelve years' training reduced to a child's sound in the dark.

Then the voice came.

It did not come through her ears. It came up through the stone, through her knees, through the marrow of her, faint and fractured, like speech heard through deep water — syllables arriving damaged, half-drowned, each one fought for as though the speaker were hauling them up from a very great depth.

*Sera.*

Just her name. But the saying of it took what felt like a minute, each letter surfacing separately, and the effort behind it was so immense, so costly, that Sera felt tears slide hot down her frozen face without deciding to cry. The god was not silent because She was absent. The god was silent because She was *drowning*. Strangled by the usurper's edicts, starved of Thornfell's prayers, sealed behind a country that had been erased — and yet fighting, century after century, to push single words up through the fathomless weight of that silencing.

*I'm here,* Sera thought, and the thought itself seemed to travel, seemed to be received. *I'm here, I'm here, I'm sorry it took me so long—*

The presence shifted. Turned. Sera felt it turn, the way you feel someone's gaze cross a room and find you, except the gaze kept moving, sliding past her shoulder, searching the shadows of the transept, the pillar where a mercenary slept with a sword across their knees and blood that would not clot.

And the voice rose again, weaker now, nearly spent, dragging itself up through the deep:

*Tamsin.*

One word. Two syllables. An entire nation's hope compressed into a name, spoken with the last strength of a drowning god, and Sera heard in it — beneath the strain, beneath the fracture — something she had never heard in any liturgy, anything formal, anything doctrinal.

She heard tenderness.

The presence withdrew like a tide going out. The cold eased. The dust resumed falling, mote by mote, and the moonlight was only moonlight, and the nave was only a ruined room, and Sera knelt alone on the stone with wet cheeks and her hands pressed over her mouth.

Behind her, in the transept, she heard Tamsin stir. Not wake — soldiers didn't wake for grief — but shift, mutter, the wounded side twinging. Alive. Bleeding. Named.

"Did you hear that?" Sera whispered to the empty alcove, to the space shaped like a person. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Was that — please, was that real?"

No answer. Of course no answer. One word per lifetime, perhaps; one word per drowning god. But real. Undeniably, catastrophically real. Everything she had built her exile on — the comfortable atheism of the betrayed, the belief that she had thrown away her life for an auditory hallucination in a soot-filled crypt — lay in pieces around her, and what remained was worse and better than faith: it was knowledge.

Veyra lived. Veyra listened. Veyra was drowning, and had just spent a fortune of strength She could not afford to say a dying woman's daughter's name.

Which meant the Door was real. Which meant the prophecy was true. Which meant everything Tamsin feared and everything Sera had lied about converged now on a single threshold in the high country, and there was no longer any path that led anywhere else.

Sera rose on legs that shook. She looked toward the transept, toward the shape of the sleeping mercenary who was also an heir, also an oathbearer, also — though they did not know it yet, though the knowing would land like a knife — beloved of a god who had just paid dearly to say so.

*Tamsin,* the deep had said. As though summoning them home.

She woke them gently, hand on the good shoulder, and Tamsin came up fast, blade half-drawn, eyes finding hers in the dark.

"What."

"You need to hear something," Sera said. Her voice steadied as she said it, and she marveled at that, at the way terror and purpose could occupy the same breath. "Or rather — you need to know that Someone said something. To me. Just now."

Tamsin stared at her. Beyond the pillar, through the broken clerestory, the sky was turning the grey that comes before dawn, and somewhere far below in the lowlands, Sera knew, Magistrate Corvel was rising too, and King Aldric's torches were moving into the passes, and the country that did not exist was running out of time to be reborn.

"It said your name," Sera told them.

Tamsin went very still. When they spoke, their voice was rougher than the mountain wind.

"Then it can damn well tell me what it wants," they said, "to my face."

Outside, far to the south, the horizon glowed — not with sunrise.
