## 10. The Usurper's Court

The throne room of Aldric the First smelled of beeswax and lilies, because King Aldric could not abide the smell of stone. Stone reminded him of cellars, and cellars reminded him of the winter his father had died in one — a minor lordling of the eastern marches, frozen out of his own keep by creditors, buried under six feet of snow with nothing to his name but a sword and a son who swore, kneeling in that drift, that he would never again be small enough for anyone to bury.

Thirty years later, the smell of stone still frightened him, and so the masons of the capital had built him a court that breathed like a garden: marble veined green as new leaves, fountains murmuring at each corner of the dais, climbing roses trained along pillars carved to look like living wood. It was beautiful. It was warm. And it was, every inch of it, a lie he had commissioned with public funds, which made it honest by the only measure Aldric trusted.

He sat upon the Thornfell throne.

That was the detail no chronicler would ever write, though three of them had tried and now rested in unmarked graves beneath the palace orchard. The throne had not been built for him. It had been carried south from the fallen capital of Thornfell on fourteen ox-carts, its high back crowned with the antlered silhouette of Veyra's silent stag, its seat worn smooth by kings whose names Aldric had ordered struck from every record his soldiers could burn. He kept it. Everyone assumed it was triumph — a conqueror seated daily upon the trophy of his conquest.

The truth was simpler and uglier. He kept it because he was afraid to destroy it.

"Your Majesty." Magistrate Corvel stood at the foot of the dais, grey as slate, hands folded behind his back in the posture of a man who had never once needed to gesture to make a point. Behind him, two of the King's Own held a third man upright between them — or what remained of a man. The prisoner's feet dragged twin furrows through the spilled fountain-water.

Aldric did not rise. Kings did not rise. But his hand found the arm of the throne and gripped it, and the old antler-carving bit into his palm the way it always did, as if the chair itself were taking toll.

"You interrupted my morning," Aldric said mildly. Mildness was his weapon of first resort; men relaxed around mildness, and relaxed men told him things. "This had better justify itself."

"The prophecy book has surfaced, Majesty." Corvel's voice never varied in pitch. It was the most unsettling thing about him, Aldric had long decided — more than the ash smell that clung to his coat, more than the ledgers of names he carried everywhere like a psalter. "In the Thornfell passes. A smuggler's cache above the Greywater road."

"A book." Aldric let the word sit in the air like something dead. "You've burned four hundred books this year, Magistrate. I signed the warrants myself. Why does this one walk into my throne room wearing a prisoner?"

"Because of what is written inside it." Corvel nodded, and the guards dropped the prisoner onto the wet marble. The man groaned — a courier's uniform, half-burned, one eye swollen shut. "Tell His Majesty what you carried."

The courier lifted his good eye. Aldric watched him take in the roses, the fountains, the golden light pouring through windows tall enough to admit God, and relax, just slightly, exactly as intended. Men always relaxed here. That was why he'd built it this way.

"It's an old text," the courier rasped. "Century-old, maybe older. My master bought it off a digger in the pass country. Thought it might be worth something to collectors — old Thornfell liturgies fetch a price in the free cities—"

"What is written inside it," Corvel said again, without heat, without hurry.

The courier swallowed. "Names. A list of names down through the years, like a... like a ledger of saints. And the last page—" His voice cracked. He started again. "The last page gives a date. Tomorrow's date, when we read it. And beside it, a name. Tamsin Okafor. A caravan-guide out of the lowlands. The text calls this person the Oathbearer." The courier's breath came fast now. "It says this Oathbearer will open the Last Door."

The fountains murmured. Somewhere in the rafters, a dove that had nested against all protocol shifted and resettled. Aldric found that he had stopped breathing, and resumed it carefully, one slow pull of air, the way he'd taught himself decades ago when panic came visiting — because panic, indulged, became visible, and visible fear in a king was blood in water.

"There is no Last Door," he said. "There is no Door at all. There are passes, and roads, and mountains full of sheep-thieves. I have closed them all myself."

"Yes, Majesty," said Corvel.

"I burned the boundary stones."

"Yes, Majesty."

"I burned the *songs*." Aldric rose now — let them see him rise, let them feel the height of him — and descended the three steps of the dais to stand over the courier. Up close, the man smelled of road dust and terror-sweat, and beneath it something else: the cold mineral scent of the high country. Thornfell's scent. Aldric had spent thirty years scrubbing it out of his own memory and still knew it instantly, the way a man knows the smell of a house that burned down with people inside it. "Every ballad, every genealogy, every parish record east of the Serpent River. I have spent a reign making Thornfell into a story children don't believe. And you bring me a book that says otherwise?"

"The book says worse than otherwise, Majesty," Corvel said quietly. "It names a living person. And my agents in the lowlands confirm the name is real. A guide. Competent. Well-traveled in exactly those passes where the Door is said to stand." He paused — the only pause Aldric had ever heard him use, deployed like a surgeon's knife laid neatly on cloth. "And there is another matter. One of my defectors has gone missing. Sera Vane. Formerly of the Temple of Veyra. She was seen on the Greywater road three days ago. Moving toward the same person."

The dove moved again overhead. Aldric looked at it — fat, white, complacent, nesting where it pleased in the roof of his kingdom — and felt the old cold climb his spine, the cellar cold, the creditor's cold, the cold of being small.

"Sera Vane," he repeated.

"She heard something in her years of service, Majesty. Something she should not have heard, given that your god — forgive me — given that *Veyra* has been silent since before your coronation. We assumed she fled out of ordinary apostasy. We may have assumed wrong."

Aldric turned away from them both and walked to the window.

Below, the capital went about its morning in the order he had decreed: market-cries, cart-wheels, the bell of the Reformed Temple tolling the hour in a rhythm designed by committee to resemble no prayer ever actually spoken. His city. His country, if a country could be said to belong to the man who had erased its predecessor. And eastward, past the smoke and the rooftops, the mountains stood on the horizon like a wall of teeth — the Thornfell ranges, unnamed on every map his surveyors drew, marked instead with the flat bureaucratic label *Eastern Protectorate*. As if protection were something you could impose by renaming.

His father had been small. Aldric had promised himself largeness. And largeness, he had learned, was mostly subtraction — you became vast by cutting away everything that might contradict you. He had cut away a country. He had cut away a god, or rather he had starved one, because gods lived on oaths and he had outlawed the speaking of them. Thirty years of careful, patient erasure. And now a book, older than his whole dynasty, claimed that somewhere in his subtracted mountains a door remained that he had failed to close — a door with his erasures written on the far side of it.

"Magistrate," he said, without turning. "Do you believe in the Last Door?"

Silence, of the particular quality Corvel produced when he wished a question to feel heavier than its answer. Then: "I believe in texts, Majesty. Texts do not require faith. They require handling." A rustle of paper. "I have secured the original. I have copies in transcription. If the Door exists, it exists because the text insists on it, and I intend to be present with sufficient fire when anyone attempts to prove the point."

"And if it opens?"

Another silence. Longer. When Corvel spoke again there was something in his voice Aldric had never heard there before, and it took him a moment to identify it, because he had built a court where no one dared carry such a thing openly.

Doubt.

"Then, Majesty," Corvel said, "we will learn whether the stories are true. The ones my grandmother told. About what stands on the other side."

* * *

They convened the war council at dusk, in the map room, because Aldric did not hold councils of state among the roses. Roses were for display. The map room was for truth, and the truth hung on its walls in ink and vellum: the whole of his kingdom rendered in meticulous lines, and along its eastern edge, the mountains drawn in the surveyors' deliberately vague hatching, labeled and cross-hatched and annotated until the actual shape of Thornfell had vanished beneath administrative accuracy.

He had done that too, Aldric remembered. Had personally instructed the chief cartographer to soften the passes, widen the rivers, round the peaks — to make the mountains look tame. A map was a promise, he had told the man. People believed maps the way they once believed gods. Make the land look governable and they will believe it is governed.

Around the long table sat the machinery of his largeness: the Lord Marshal, grey-whiskered, solid; the Chancellor of the Eastern Protectorate, a soft man named Halloway who had never seen the protectorate and grew visibly pale whenever it was mentioned; the Master of Fires, whose office title was officially *Master of Archives* but whose true function everyone understood; and Corvel, standing, as he always stood, because chairs implied rest and Corvel did not rest.

"Recommendations," Aldric said.

"Seal the passes," the Lord Marshal said at once. "All of them. Not garrisoned — *sealed*. Collapsed. We have the engineers; the deep-shaft charges from the southern mines would bring down half the high road in an afternoon. Nothing crosses. Nothing comes out."

"Burn the approaches," said the Master of Fires. "The villages in the foothills shelter smugglers by habit if not conviction. Clear them. Burn what won't move."

"Clear them," Halloway echoed faintly. "Your Majesty, the harvest is — some of those communities have been there since — I mean to say, the displacement alone—"

"Since when, Chancellor?" Aldric asked pleasantly. "Since when have they been there? Give me the date. Give me the charter. Show me the record of their founding." He let the silence work. Halloway had none, and they both knew why; the records were ash, by royal warrant, in a year Halloway had spent safely south. "They are squatters on protectorate land with no documented history. The Crown owes them what any landlord owes squatters. Notice, and fire."

Halloway bowed his head. Around the table, no one else objected. They never did. That was the bargain Aldric had struck with every soul in his court: complicity in exchange for proximity. They stood near the throne because the alternative was distance, and distance from Aldric the First had historically proven fatal to careers, and occasionally to persons. He knew what they said about him in the servants' corridors. Tyrant. Usurper. He had heard the words so long they had lost their edges, worn smooth as river stones, smooth as the seat of a stolen throne.

"Corvel," he said. "You're quiet."

"I am considering the Door, Majesty."

"There is no Door."

"No, Majesty." Corvel unfolded one of his ledgers — the man treated paper the way priests treated relics — and laid it flat on the map, across the hatched emptiness of the eastern mountains. On it, in his small precise hand, was a rendering of the old Thornfell songs, transcribed from informants, from confiscated letters, from the memories of exiles interrogated over twenty years. Aldric had read fragments before. He had forbidden himself to read them whole.

"The Door appears in nine distinct sources," Corvel said. "Peasant variants differ in detail. All agree on three points. First: it stands in the high country, past Ash Pass, in the temple district of old Thornfell. Second: it opens only for the Oathbearer — a figure bound to Veyra by oath, of the old royal line. Third." Corvel's finger came to rest on the ledger's final line. Aldric read it despite himself, despite thirty years of discipline.

*A door once opened cannot be shut from either side.*

"That last is consistent across every source," Corvel said. "Even the ones that disagree about everything else. Even the ones recorded from dying men who had every reason to lie." He looked up, and his slate eyes met the King's, and for the second time that day Aldric saw the thing he had outlawed in his own court looking back at him without shame. "Majesty, if this person opens it, we cannot close it. Not with fire, not with armies, not with thirty more years of erasure. Whatever is on the other side of that door will remain reachable. Forever."

The map room was very quiet. The candles guttered in a draft from the sealed windows — Aldric kept them sealed; open windows admitted the smell of the city, and the city smelled of people, and people smelled of the kinds of loyalty that changed hands.

"Then we ensure," Aldric said slowly, "that no Oathbearer reaches it."

"We kill the guide," the Lord Marshal said. "Simple. One rider, one blade, done before the week's out. I can have—"

"No." Aldric surprised himself with the sharpness of it. He smoothed his tone. "No. Think, Marshal. A dead guide is a martyr, and martyrs make better texts than living ones. Burn a prophet and you print him. No — if the book is right, then killing changes nothing. There will be another name on the next page, and the next. Prophecy is a hydra of paperwork." He walked the length of the table, trailing his fingers along the mountain ranges he had renamed. "We do not fight the text. We *outlast* it. Seal the passes — yes, Marshal, collapse them, I want the roads gone so thoroughly that even the memory of roads is gone. Burn the approaches — yes, clear the foothills, every village, every shrine. If the Door needs an Oathbearer, then we remove everything an Oathbearer could need: roads, guides, believers, witnesses. Let them open their door onto a wilderness. Let them find no one left to save."

"And the guide?" Corvel asked.

Aldric stopped at the head of the table, beneath the great map, beneath the hatched and tamed and falsified mountains, and felt the old cold in his spine and recognized it at last for what it had always been. Not ambition's chill. Not the creditor's winter. Fear. Plain, animal, undressed fear — the fear of a man who had built an empire of erasure and had just been informed that somewhere in the dark of it, a door stood ajar.

"The guide travels with a priestess," he said. "Sera Vane. Find her. Find what she knows and whom she loves and what she cannot bear to lose." He smiled, and was aware, distantly, of how much practice the smile had required. "We do not kill the Oathbearer, gentlemen. We make certain that by the time they reach their door, there is nothing left in them that wants to open it. Bring me their heart, whatever it's made of. I have broken larger things."

Corvel inclined his head. "There is one difficulty, Majesty. The sources agree on one further point." He turned the ledger toward the council, and read aloud, in his level, pitchless, terrible voice:

"*When the Oath burns anew, the silent god shall speak.*"

Aldric laughed. It came out correctly — rich, unhurried, the laugh of a man amused by superstition. He had rehearsed that laugh for thirty years in front of mirrors, and it worked, as it always worked; the Lord Marshal chuckled obediently, Halloway managed a nervous smile.

Only Corvel did not laugh. He stood with his hand flat on the ledger, on the old words, and watched Aldric with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he did not like the sum of.

"Majesty," Corvel said. "If Veyra speaks — even once — then every oath ever sworn in this kingdom's hidden history becomes audible again. Every family that whispers the old names at night. Every exile who remembers. Every stone you did not think to burn." He closed the ledger softly. "I will ride east tomorrow. I will find this Oathbearer. But I counsel you, sire, as I have never counseled you before: pray that the book is wrong."

"I don't pray," Aldric said.

"No," Corvel agreed. "That is rather the point."

He bowed and left, and his footsteps faded down the corridor, and Aldric stood alone in the map room with the candles burning down and the falsified mountains looming over the table, and after a long time — after the Lord Marshal and the Chancellor and the Master of Fires had all withdrawn and the servants had been dismissed and the doors barred from within — he did the thing he had not done since the night of his coronation, when he had knelt on the stones of the captured temple and sworn, silently, to a god he was in the act of starving, that he feared nothing.

He knelt.

Not to Veyra. Never to Veyra. He knelt because his knees had decided for him, the way bodies decide things the mind has forbidden, and he stayed there in the guttering candlelight, a king alone on the floor of his own map room, staring east at the hatched and lying mountains.

Somewhere out there, a smuggler-guide with an impossible name was walking toward a door he had spent a lifetime pretending did not exist.

"Stay silent," Aldric whispered — to the mountains, to the dark beyond them, to the god whose name he could not stop himself from shaping. "Whatever you are. Whoever listens. Stay silent one year longer, and I will give you anything."

The candles went out, all seven, in a single breath of wind from a room with sealed windows.

And in the morning, the orders went east: seal the passes, burn the foothills, and bring the Oathbearer's heart to the throne — whatever it was made of, and by whatever means remained.
