## 11. Blood on the Boundary Stone

The high road out of Thornfell was not a road at all anymore — that was the first lie of it. It was a scar, a pale seam of gravel and old paving stones stitched across the shoulder of the mountains, and it remembered wheels that had not rolled on it in forty years. Tamsin had walked worse paths for worse pay, but they had never walked one that felt like this: like walking down the spine of something sleeping.

They kept Sera ahead of them and the book against their chest, wrapped in oilcloth beneath their coat, and they counted the boundary stones as they passed. That was an old caravan habit, counting things — bridges, cairns, the breaths between lightning and thunder — because a thing you could count was a thing you could survive. The stones were squat granite fingers, each carved with a sigil worn nearly smooth: a door with no frame around it. Thornfell's mark. Every third stone still stood. The rest had been toppled, dragged to the roadside, or in two cases blown apart with sapper's charges so thoroughly that even the fragments had been hauled away, as though Aldric's engineers had feared the rubble itself might remember what country it belonged to.

"You keep touching your ribs," Sera said without turning around.

"I'm checking my coin."

"Your coin is in your left boot. You told me so when we divided the watch last night." She walked on, her priestess's stride unhurried despite the altitude, her gray cloak the exact color of the scree slopes. "You're touching where the book sits."

Tamsin let their hand fall. "It's heavy."

"It's a book."

"It's a hundred-year-old book with my name written in it like a debt ledger." They said it lightly, the way you said such things lightly, because saying them any other way invited the vertigo back, and the vertigo was a poor companion on a cliff path. "Every mile east feels less like traveling and more like walking into someone else's sentence."

Sera glanced back at them. In the flat mountain light her face was all angles and patience. "The sentence was written before either of us was born. We're only choosing how to read it aloud."

"That," Tamsin said, "is exactly the sort of thing a defector would say."

"And exactly the sort of thing you'd want to hear, Oathbearer." She smiled — small, dry, gone again — and pointed ahead. "There. Do you see it?"

Tamsin saw it. Rising from a bend in the road where the mountains pinched close stood a boundary stone taller than the rest, untoppled, untouched, its carved door-sign sharp as if cut yesterday. And around its base, impossible and unmistakable, grew a ring of flowers. White ones. Out of season. Out of reason, this far above the treeline, in soil that should have been frost-locked until spring.

They both stopped. The wind moved through the pass and made a sound that was almost — almost — like a crowd holding its breath.

"Don't touch it," Tamsin said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were absolutely going to."

Sera's smile came back and stayed a moment longer. "The land remembers," she said softly, kneeling at the ring's edge but not crossing it. "Aldric burned the maps, renamed the towns, salted the archives. But he couldn't reach the stones. Look how many are still standing. Someone has been raising them again. Secretly. For years."

Tamsin looked up the road, and once they knew to look, they saw it everywhere: stones righted and mortared with river clay; a fallen fragment set upright in a shepherd's cairn; a doorway symbol scratched fresh into rock beside older, weathered marks, the hand of some nameless farmer or exile who had risked the magistrate's fire to carve a wordless *we were here*. A country erased from every map, insisting on itself in granite.

Something moved in Tamsin's chest that had no name and therefore no permission to exist. They stamped it down and checked the sky instead. Clouds building over the western peaks, the wrong color, the wrong speed. Weather coming — or cover for something worse.

"We camp short tonight," they said. "Off the road. I don't like the light up there, and I like the quiet even less."

* * *

The quiet broke an hour past dusk, and it broke the way Tamsin had known it would: not with a sound but with a silence. The crickets along the riverbank stopped all at once, as if a hand had closed over the valley's throat.

Tamsin was moving before thought caught up — thirty years of ambushes lived in the body, below the mind, and the body already knew. They killed the small fire with a sweep of a boot, pulled Sera down behind a shelf of granite, and put an arrow on the string in the same motion.

"How many?" Sera whispered.

"Listen. Boots on the switchback above us — two men, trying to be quiet, failing. More below by the water. At least six. Corvel doesn't send six for a smuggler."

"No," she breathed. "He sends six for a prophecy."

The first crossbow bolt buried itself in the granite an inch from Tamsin's cheekbone, and then the night came apart.

They fought the way they always fought — economically, viciously, with the economy of someone who had never once been able to afford waste. Tamsin's arrows took two shapes out of the dark before the dark learned to stay behind rocks. Then steel, close work, the bad work. A soldier in Aldric's black-and-silver came over the boulder line swinging a shortsword, and Tamsin met him blade to blade, turned his weight, opened his thigh, put him down into the shallows. Another behind him. Another. Sera fought too — she fought like a liturgy, precise and strange, a knife in each hand and prayers under her breath, and Tamsin had time for one absurd flash of thought: *she really was a priestess after all* —

— and then the world went white-hot along their left side.

The blade slid under their arm, between the leather plates, deep enough to matter. Tamsin felt it happen the way you feel ice, distantly, and then they felt nothing at all, which was worse. They killed the man who'd done it — drove a dagger up under his jaw without looking, the old animal trick — and heard shouting, retreat-shouting, and then the soldiers were falling back down the switchback with their wounded dragging between them, and the valley was silent again except for the river and Tamsin's own breathing, ragged, too loud.

"Tamsin." Sera was beside them, hands pressing the wound, and Tamsin saw her face change.

"What?"

"Let me see."

"It's a sword cut. I've had—"

"*Let me see.*"

They got the coat open. The moon had come out from behind the clouds, helpfully, cruelly, and in its light they both looked down at the gash along Tamsin's ribs.

It wasn't closing.

That meant nothing to anyone who hadn't grown up on battlefields, so let it mean this: a wound bleeds, clots, seals. This one bled, and kept bleeding, and at its edges the flesh did not knit but *frayed*, like rope end, like cloth, like something unraveling rather than injured. And in the blood itself — Tamsin saw it, couldn't unsee it — there was a faint luminescence, a dim gold shimmer, fading even as they watched. Fading the way coals fade. Fading the way a voice fades when it stops being answered.

"Oh," Sera said. Very quietly. As if she'd just read something.

"What. *What.*"

"The oath," she said. "Veyra's oath. It lives in the blood of those who swear it — I told you the old rites held that the god's protection was carried literally, in the body, in the—" She stopped herself, pressed harder on the wound, and Tamsin hissed. "I hoped I was wrong. I prayed I was wrong about this part."

"About which part? Say the whole sentence, Sera."

"The oath is failing in you. Not weakening. *Failing.* Like a lamp running out of oil. But oaths don't run out, Tamsin — they're renewed at shrine, at feast-day, at every dawn prayer. Yours hasn't been renewed in years, and yet it's lasted, it's *held*, far longer than any sworn blood has a right to hold unless—" Her hands stilled on the wound. "Unless it isn't really yours. Unless you inherited it."

The word went through Tamsin colder than the blade had.

"Inherited."

"Blood-oaths pass down bloodlines. That's the oldest doctrine, the one the church burned out of the books after Aldric took the throne, because it led to one conclusion nobody in power wanted spoken." She looked up at them, and her eyes in the moonlight were terrible with pity. "Only one lineage in the world carried Veyra's oath in its blood. Only one family ever swore it at the founding, when the Last Door was first shut. Tamsin — the protection in you is royal. It's the old king's line. It's *Thornfell's* line."

The river went on talking to itself. Somewhere upslope a soldier groaned and was silenced by his comrades. Tamsin sat very still, one hand pressed to their own unraveling side, and felt the ground open under everything.

"No," they said.

"Tamsin—"

"No. I'm a foundling. I'm a caravan rat. My mother died on the Salt Road when I was four; Captain Vess pulled me out of a ditch. There's no lineage in a ditch."

"There's lineage anywhere people flee to," Sera said. "Where do you think foundlings come from, Tamsin? Where do you think a fleeing queen would leave a child, if she wanted the child beyond a usurper's reach? With strangers. On a road. In a ditch, if need be — somewhere no herald would ever look." She reached out, hesitated, touched their wrist. "You've been carrying the proof since before you could walk. You've been carrying it in your *blood*. And now the oath is dying in you because it was never renewed, never tended, because everyone who should have taught you who you were decided you were safer ignorant."

"Safer." Tamsin laughed, and the laugh came out broken in the middle. "Someone decided for me. Again. Everyone decides for me. The prophet writes my name. Corvel hunts my shadow. The god goes silent for twenty years and lets me bleed faith into empty shrines. And now you tell me the whole story was written on my body before I could read, and *no one told me*—"

Their voice cracked. They hated that it cracked.

"They didn't tell you," Sera said gently, "because telling you was a death sentence. Don't you see? Every secret was a shield. Your captain, whoever hid you, whoever raised you — they weren't stealing your history, they were burying it to keep you alive. Ignorance was the only armor Aldric's magistrates couldn't burn through."

"And it's failing anyway." Tamsin looked down at the fraying gold-edged wound. "Because I'm supposed to do something. Because the blood knows what I'm for even if I don't." They pressed their palm flat against the cut, and the shimmer answered faintly, weakly, like a candle cupped against wind. "How long do I have? If it keeps unraveling?"

"I don't know. Days? Weeks? The Door may renew it. Or opening the Door may require—" She didn't finish. Tamsin watched her decide not to finish, and filed that away, another secret banked behind her eyes, another debt to collect later.

Above them, the boundary stone stood against the stars, its carved door catching the moonlight, its ring of impossible white flowers nodding in the wind. Tamsin looked at it for a long time.

"My mother," they said at last, and their voice was flat and strange, a stranger's voice, "was a ditch on the Salt Road. That's what I've believed for thirty years. It kept me fed and it kept me moving and it asked nothing of me but the next mile." They laughed again, quieter. "And now there's a kingdom in my veins rotting through a sword-cut, and a god in my blood going out like a spent lamp, and I never got to choose a single piece of it."

"No," Sera said. "But you get to choose what happens next. That's the one thing no prophecy can write for you."

Tamsin wanted to strike her for that — for the comfort of it, for the truth of it, for how much easier rage was than the alternative. Instead they bound the wound with strips of coat-lining and Sera's prayer-knots, and the bleeding slowed to a seep that did not stop, and they climbed back toward the high road as the sky went gray with false dawn.

At the boundary stone, Tamsin paused. Their blood had dripped along the way — small dark spots on the gravel, each one faintly, fadingly gold. One drop had landed near the stone's base, at the edge of the flower-ring.

Where it fell, the grass had straightened. The nearest bloom had turned, impossibly, toward it.

Tamsin stared, and the cold that went through them then was not fear of Corvel, or of death, or even of the unraveling oath. It was the colder thing underneath all three: the understanding that the land knew them. That it had always known them. That every step east was a homecoming arranged by dead people without their consent.

"Sera," they said, not looking away from the flower. "Who else knew? About the bloodline. Who besides you, besides whoever hid me?"

Sera was quiet long enough that the wind filled the pause.

"Tamsin—"

"*Who else.*"

"There's a reason Corvel stopped burning books and started taking prisoners," she said finally. "There's a reason he wants you breathing. He's known what you are longer than I have. And there's someone he serves who wants to meet you before the oath runs out entirely."

Far to the west, down in the lowlands, a bell began to toll — one bell, then ten, then all of them, town after town, the alarm passing along the valleys like fire along a fuse.

The road east lay open. The blood ran thin and golden and slow.

Tamsin picked up their bow and started walking, because it was the only choice left that had ever truly been theirs.
