## 12. You Never Told Me

Sera had lied to kings and lied to gods, and both had forgiven her — the kings because they never noticed, the god because it had no mouth with which to accuse her. But she had never learned how to lie to someone she loved while looking them in the eye.

She was learning now.

Tamsin stood at the fork in the pass with their back half-turned, and that was the worst of it. Not rage. Not the cold silence of betrayal. Just Tamsin, standing very still the way they did before a fight, watching the two roads divide below like a man reading a wound.

The wound was Sera's making. She had known that from the first night at the shrine, when she'd seen the prophecy's ink catch firelight on Tamsin's name and felt the old guilt stir in her chest like something waking. She had known it through Ash Pass, through the temple where Veyra's broken voice had spoken *Okafor* into the dark. She had carried the knowledge the way a soldier carries a blade too heavy for her hand — knowing she would have to use it, dreading the moment.

The moment had come at dawn, three days after the boundary stone, when Tamsin's blood had refused to close its own doors.

"You're going to say something," Tamsin said quietly, not turning. "I can hear you deciding how to say it."

"I—"

"Don't." Tamsin held up a hand. The bandage beneath their sleeve had soaked through again overnight; Sera could see the rust-brown blooming at the cuff, patient as frost. "Don't soften it. Don't manage me. I've spent my whole life being managed by people who thought they knew what was best for me. Captain Ysolde. The caravan masters. Every priest who ever told me my prayers were too loud or too quiet." They turned then, and their eyes were not angry, which frightened Sera more than anger would have. "You knew."

It was not a question. Questions could be answered. This was a door already open, showing her everything inside.

"Yes," Sera said. "I knew."

The wind came down off the high snowfields and moved between them, lifting the ash-grey hair from her face, carrying away whatever small mercy might otherwise have lived in the pause. Below the fork, the western road dropped toward the lowlands and Aldric's fires; the eastern road climbed toward the Door and whatever waited past it. Between the two roads stood the boundary stone, older than either kingdom, its carved letters worn to suggestions.

"How long?" Tamsin asked.

Sera closed her eyes. There was no version of honesty left that would spare her, so she chose the whole of it, laid out flat like a body on a table. "Since before I met you. Since before I left the church. The archives under the Basilica keep genealogies of every house Aldric burned. Thornfell's last royal line didn't end at the annexation — one daughter survived, smuggled north with a mercenary company. Her name was Okafor. The line thinned and hid and forgot itself, but it didn't end." She opened her eyes. "When Corvel began burning prophetic texts, I went looking for the ones he hadn't found yet. I found the lineage first. I found you second."

"Found me." Tamsin laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Like a thing in a drawer."

"Tamsin—"

"The shrine." Their voice stayed level, which took work; Sera could see the seams of it, the effort holding their face together like mortar. "You were waiting at the shrine. You told me you heard Veyra whisper your name. Was any of that true?"

*All of it,* Sera wanted to say. *Every word, except the order in which it happened.* But she had promised herself no softening, no managing, so she gave him the truth in its ugly sequence instead.

"The whisper came first. Years ago, when I still wore the grey. A voice through deep water, saying *find the Oathbearer.* I told myself it was grief, or fasting, or madness. Then I read the genealogies and the prophecy together, and I understood what the voice wanted, and I was afraid of it. So I ran. I defected. I told myself I'd abandoned the god entirely." Her hands were shaking. She let them. "And then I found you, and I followed you anyway, and I let you believe we met by chance because if you'd known I came hunting you — that the church trained me to hunt people like you —"

"Like me." Tamsin's jaw tightened. "A bloodline. An instrument. A key."

"A person," Sera said. "I promise you, by the time I understood anything else, I already knew you were a person. That's why I couldn't say it. Every day I waited made the lie heavier, and every day the mission mattered more than my courage, and I kept choosing —"

"The mission." Tamsin said the word slowly, tasting it. "So that's what I am. The mission."

"No."

"That's what you're saying."

"That is *not* what I'm saying." Her voice cracked at last, and she hated the crack, hated that her composure broke exactly when she needed it most. "Do you want to know what I was choosing? I was choosing between telling you the truth and losing you — losing you before the Door, before any of it, losing you to fear or fury or flight — and keeping silent long enough that maybe, *maybe*, you'd reach the Door alive and hate me after instead of dying free of me. I chose your survival over your trust. I knew it was wrong. I did it anyway. That's the confession. There isn't a cleaner one."

The words hung in the cold air. Somewhere above them, a hawk turned on the thermals, indifferent.

Tamsin looked at her for a long time. When they spoke again, their voice had gone quiet in a new way — not controlled now but emptied.

"When I was a child," they said, "I used to ask my mother where our family came from. Before the roads, before the caravans. And she'd smile and say, *from everywhere and nowhere, little guide — that's what makes a good road-walker.* I thought it was a joke. I built myself on top of it. No homeland, no name that meant anything, just the oath and the road and the god who stopped answering. And it turns out—" Their breath caught. "It turns out I come from a country. From a *house.* From a line of kings who died screaming under Aldric's boots. It turns out my blood has been failing to heal since the day I touched that boundary stone because it's been waiting a hundred years to be claimed and I never even knew it existed. Everyone knew but me."

"Not everyone."

"My captain?"

Sera hesitated. "I don't know."

"You do know. You just don't know if telling me will make this worse." Tamsin shook their head slowly. "That's the disease, Sera. That's the whole disease. Everyone who loved me decided what I could bear. My mother. Ysolde. Now you. Did it occur to any of you that I've borne everything else? That I might have been able to bear the truth?"

"It occurred to me every single day," Sera whispered. "And every single day I imagined your face when you heard it, and every single day I flinched. That's the truth. I wasn't protecting the mission. I was protecting myself from this. From your face right now."

Something flickered across Tamsin's expression — not forgiveness, not yet, but the recognition of a fellow coward, a fellow liar, the two liars recognizing each other one last time before the recognition curdled. For a heartbeat Sera thought they might reach for her.

Then Tamsin stepped back.

* * *

They tended the wound in silence first, because whatever else had broken between them, the body kept its own appointments. Sera knelt in the scree with her kit open, cleaning the gash along Tamsin's forearm, and the flesh beneath her hands behaved like stranger's flesh — slow to knit, sullen, as though the blood itself had grown tired of pretending to belong to a mere mercenary. The old oath ran in that blood. She could feel it now, faintly, the way you feel weather coming: Veyra's covenant fraying thread by thread, stretched past the shape it was cut for.

"You should have told me at the shrine," Tamsin said at last, eyes fixed on the middle distance. "Or on the road. Or in the temple, when the god said my name and you went white as salt. Any of those. I'd have been angry. I'd have gotten over it."

"I know."

"So why the boundary stone? Why wait until the wound wouldn't close?"

"Because until then I could still pretend I was wrong." Sera tied off the bandage, her fingers steady now that steadiness no longer mattered. "As long as there was no proof, I could tell myself the genealogies were corrupted, the prophecy metaphorical, that I'd dragged an innocent road-guide into a war of signs. The stone settled it. Your blood settling it. Once I saw that, staying silent became theft. I was stealing your own history out of your hands and calling it kindness."

Tamsin flexed the bandaged arm, testing. Above the tree line the sky was doing what mountain skies did in late autumn — stacking blue and iron in equal measure, promising weather it might or might not deliver. The fork waited below them, patient as all roads are.

"Where does the eastern road go?" Tamsin asked.

"To the Door. Eventually. Through the high country where Aldric can't easily follow." Sera paused. "And the western goes down to the lowland towns, and from there to the coast, and from the coast anywhere in the world that doesn't know your face."

"And you want me to take the eastern road."

"I want you to live." She said it plainly, without dressing. "The Door may be the only way out for anyone. But it wants something from you, Tamsin. Something I don't fully understand yet, and the prophecy's language turns to riddles exactly where it matters most. I've watched you bleed since that stone. Whatever the Oathbearer's part costs, it's already collecting."

"So you want me to walk away. Down the west road. Forget the Door, forget the refugees Aldric's going to burn out of the valleys, forget the country in my blood." Tamsin's mouth twisted. "Forget the name in a book written a hundred years ago, waiting for me specifically, on tonight's date, in a smuggler's cache I just happened to shelter in."

"Fate is a story liars tell," Sera said. "I was a priestess. I know."

"Do you?" Tamsin turned to face her fully, and the wind pulled at their cloak, and for a moment they looked less like a mercenary than like something older standing in the pass — a figure out of the songs Sera had sung in the basilica choir, back when Thornfell was only a melody in a lament. "Because here's what I know. I know that god of yours — *our* god, apparently — has been silent since I was a child. I know I swore an oath to that silence anyway, because someone had to keep faith with something. I know that when I finally hear that voice again — broken, drowning, barely a whisper — it says *my name*. And I know that everyone I have ever trusted decided, out of love, to make my choices for me." They swallowed. "I won't survive being chosen for much longer, Sera. Do you understand? It's not the lies. Lies I can carry. It's that each one takes another piece of the map."

The image struck Sera somewhere beneath the ribs. *A piece of the map.* Of course. For a road-guide, a stolen truth was a missing mile, a washed-out bridge, a pass that ended in cliff. She had not taken a secret from him. She had taken terrain.

"I'm sorry," she said. "For what it's worth — and I know what it's worth, which is nothing — I am sorry."

"It's worth something." Tamsin looked at her, and their eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "That's the hell of it. It's worth everything, and it changes nothing."

* * *

They ate the last of the hard bread sitting on opposite sides of the boundary stone, because some habits outlive every rupture. Sera chewed mechanically and watched Tamsin watch the roads, and she understood, with the terrible clarity that only comes after a loss is already complete, that she had done this before. In the basilica. With her own reflection in the dark glass of the reliquary, the night she decided to defect: she had sat across from herself and eaten bread and planned a departure, and the woman in the glass had looked at her exactly the way Tamsin looked at the eastern road now.

Love against duty. She had always believed duty would win, because duty was heavier and love was only air. But watching Tamsin sit with their back straight and their bleeding arm cradled, she understood that duty had lost in her long ago — that everything she'd done since the basilica, the defection, the pursuit, the silence, all of it, had been love wearing duty's uniform, love marching under false colors, love too ashamed to show its face. And look what shame had built. Look at the fork. Look at the two roads.

"Tamsin." She set down the bread. "Ask me anything. One question, any question, and I'll answer it true. Call it the last payment on a debt."

Tamsin considered for a long moment. The hawk cried once, far up, and was gone behind the ridge.

"Do you believe in the Door?" they asked finally. "Not the prophecy. Not the mission. The actual Door. Do you believe there's something on the other side worth all this?"

"Yes." No hesitation. This, at least, had never been a lie. "I served Veyra for twenty years in a church that had already hollowed the god out and filled the shell with Aldric's politics. And still — even hollow, even silent — something was *there.* Like heat in a dead coal. I believe the Door leads to wherever the god went when the country was erased. I believe the refugees can be saved. And I believe the price will be worse than either of us knows." She smiled, thin and joyless. "There. True to the last word, including the ugly ones."

"Thank you." Tamsin rose, wincing, and shouldered their pack with the economy of thirty years on the road. They looked east a long time. Then west. Then, briefly, at her — and Sera saw in that brief look everything she would carry for the rest of her life: affection with nowhere to go, trust with a hole burned through it, and underneath both, stubborn as bedrock, the person she had betrayed precisely because she loved them.

"I'm taking the east road," Tamsin said.

Her heart lurched — relief and dread in the same instant, twins she could no longer tell apart. "Tamsin—"

"Don't." They raised the bandaged arm, almost a salute. "I'm not doing this for your prophecy or your god or my dead kings. I'm doing it because Aldric is going to burn the lowland villages this winter, and I've guided enough caravans to know what a road looks like when ten thousand people need it and nobody's walked it ahead of them. Someone has to go first. That's the job. That's always been the job." A ghost of the old crooked smile, there and gone. "The rest — the bloodline, the Door, the voice in the water — I'll deal with what's real when it's in front of me. Same as always."

"And me?" The question escaped before she could cage it. "What am I to you now?"

Tamsin was quiet. The wind moved. Far below, the boundary stone threw a long shadow across the fork, pointing neither way and both.

"Right now?" they said. "Someone I walked with. Someone I might have—" They stopped, jaw working. "I don't know what you are, Sera. Ask me again on the other side of the Door. If there is an other side. If there's a me to ask."

They stepped onto the eastern road.

Sera stood at the stone and watched them descend — that familiar figure growing smaller against the vast grey shoulder of the mountain, the stride she knew better than her own prayers, the pack riding slightly left the way it always did because of the old shoulder scar they'd never explained to her. She watched until the road bent around a spur of rock. She watched a moment longer than that, at nothing.

Then she turned west.

It was the only choice left that made sense. If Tamsin would not run, someone had to slow what chased them — and Sera knew Corvel, knew his methods, knew his copy of the prophecy sat locked in his baggage train with the passages Tamsin had never read. The passages about the price. The passages she had translated herself, years ago, kneeling in the basilica archive, and had prayed even then she would never need to speak aloud.

She went down the western road alone, toward the smoke of the lowlands, toward the inquisitor's camp, rehearsing under her breath the lie that would get her inside his tents.

Behind her, the boundary stone stood at the fork with its worn letters facing the sky, and the wind moving over it sounded, if you were foolish or faithful enough to listen, almost like a voice remembering how to speak.
