## 13. The Captain's Choice

The safehouse smelled of tallow smoke and rotting sweetgrass, and beneath both — faint but unmistakable to a nose trained on twenty years of contraband — the sweet iron stink of blood that had gone bad in a wound.

Tamsin knew it before they crossed the threshold. They had smelled it on men dragged off the passes at Graywater, on the smuggler Hesk whose gut opened like a purse at Ashford Bridge, on their own thigh the winter a Thornfell arrow found them in the dark and some stranger's prayer kept them breathing until dawn. It was the smell of a body that had decided something.

Brannick stood aside when they came in, and Brannick's face told them the rest. The big man could lie about ledgers and tolls and the whereabouts of a magistrate's riders, but his face was a boundary stone: it said what it said.

"She's asking for you," he said. "Been asking since midnight."

The room beyond was low-ceilinged, an old wool-store with one window shuttered against the light. Captain Ilsa Vane lay on a pallet that was too short for her, her grey-streaked hair spread out on the bare boards like something spilled. She had been a large woman once — broad through the shoulders, built for hauling men out of rivers and arguments — but Corvel's people had spent eleven days teaching her flesh new shapes, and what remained of her looked borrowed from someone smaller.

Tamsin stopped in the doorway. It was not sentiment. It was the old mercenary arithmetic, the counting of a thing before you committed to carrying it: *how heavy is this going to be.*

"Tamsin." Her voice was gravel rolled in honey, still, even now. "Close the door. Not that it matters. Whole mountain probably heard me screaming."

"I'm sorry." The words came out flat and useless, coins of no currency.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful. Sit down where I can see your face without turning my head, because my neck has opinions now."

Tamsin sat. Up close there were things they wished they hadn't seen: the burn patterns on her forearms, precise as script; the way her left hand wouldn't close; the bruise-bloom at her throat where someone had asked questions in the old way, the patient way. Corvel did not use torturers who enjoyed it. That was the horror of him. He used ones who took notes.

"They wanted the book," Ilsa said. "And they wanted to know where you'd gone to ground. I gave them the first day free, because everyone breaks eventually and it's kinder to start the clock yourself. Then I gave them nothing, because—" she smiled, and her teeth were brown and whole and stubborn— "because I owed a debt."

"Captain—"

"Ilsa." Her hand twitched toward them, an order older than either of them. "Not captain. Not anymore. Listen to me now, because I've been rehearsing this speech for three days and the pain keeps eating my drafts, and if you interrupt me I'll lose the thread and we'll both die embarrassed."

Tamsin folded their hands and waited. Outside, wind moved down from the high snowfields and worried the shutters. Somewhere below, Brannick was being very loud about organizing supplies, loud in the particular way men are loud when they are trying to drown out a room they cannot bear.

* * *

"You were nineteen," Ilsa began. "Nineteen, starving, standing in the rain outside the Broken Tally with a sword you clearly didn't know how to hold and a look on your face like the world had already signed your death warrant and lost the paperwork. Do you remember what you said to me?"

"No."

"'I work hard and I ask no questions.'" She laughed, and the laugh cost her; Tamsin watched her ride it out with the same discipline she'd once used on a broken ambush line. "That was the whole interview. I hired you before you finished the sentence. Not because I needed another blade. Because I recognized the look. It was my mother's look. It's the look of people who come from somewhere that got erased."

The wind gusted. The shutter knocked twice against its frame, soft as a knuckle.

"Everyone in my band has a story like that," Ilsa went on. "Hesk's village burned in the annexation wars. Brannick's grandmother sang songs in a language Aldric banned, taught them to him anyway. We're all refugees who never filed the paperwork, all of us carrying countries in our mouths. But yours—" she stopped. Breathed. "Yours was different. Yours had a seal on it."

Tamsin felt the cold arrive ahead of the understanding, the way frost comes into a valley before the night itself does.

"What did you know?"

"I knew your mother." Ilsa turned her head, slowly, wincing, until her eyes found theirs — dark eyes, shrewd, unafraid even here, even now. "Or rather, I knew *of* her. Everyone in the old trade knew of her. There was a woman moving through the borderlands in the years after the annexation. Quiet. Careful. Carrying papers nobody should have had — genealogies, oath-records, the old rolls of Thornfell's houses. She was gathering up the country's bones before Aldric's bone-collectors finished the job. And every smuggler, every fence, every toll-gang between here and the coast knew two things about her. First: she paid gold. Second: if Aldric's men ever learned what she carried, she'd burn, and so would anyone who'd helped her. So we helped her, and we forgot her name, deliberately, the way you forget a password."

"And she was—"

"Your mother. Yes." Ilsa's voice gentled, which was somehow worse than if she'd shouted it. "She came through the passes the year you were born. Going the other way. Out of Thornfell, into exile, with a bundle that cried. I saw her at the Toll of Ash Pass. She asked us to forget her too. Asked it like a woman asking you to carry something heavy across a river and never set it down. So we did. Twenty-three years, we set it down every morning and picked it up again every night."

"Why?" The word tore out of Tamsin rougher than intended. "Why would you keep that from me? You let me walk blind into—Corvel, the prophecy, all of it—"

"Because knowing is a brand, child." For the first time her voice sharpened, the old command voice, and pain be damned the room snapped to attention around it. "You think I didn't want to tell you? You think there weren't nights around a fire when you'd say something — some turn of phrase, some way of reading weather off a mountain — and I'd have to bite my own tongue bloody? I watched you swear your oath to Veyra because it was the only inheritance anyone offered you, and I let you, because a soldier's oath keeps you small enough to hide. A mercenary with no name and no nation can walk through any gate in the world. An heir—" she coughed, wet and deep— "an heir gets hunted from the cradle. Your mother didn't bleed across half the borderlands so her child could grow up as a target. She bled so you could grow up as *nobody*. Free. And then you had to go find a century-old book with your name in it, because apparently nobody in this family knows how to stay hidden."

The words landed one by one, each with its own weight, and Tamsin sat under them and let them. Anger arrived, looked around for a place to live, and found the ground already occupied by something else — grief, maybe, or its twin.

"You could have told me after the book. After Corvel."

"After Corvel had you, he'd have peeled the truth out of you in an afternoon, same as he peeled me, and then he'd have known exactly what he held. Ignorance was armor. I'd have kept you ignorant till my last breath if the world had let me." She paused. "It hasn't let me. So here we are."

* * *

Tamsin rose and found the water jug, poured, and held it to Ilsa's lips. She drank like a bird, small sips, eyes never leaving their face. When Tamsin set the jug down, their hand was not quite steady, and Ilsa noticed — she noticed everything, she had always noticed everything, and Tamsin understood suddenly that twenty years of competence had been performed before the one audience guaranteed to see through it.

"The shrine at Coldmere," Ilsa said quietly. "You prayed there every time we passed. Don't deny it, Brannick told me years ago. You'd slip off alone and kneel at that broken altar to a god who never answered, and you'd come back looking like a man who'd written a letter and mailed it to a burnt house." She drew a breath. "I let you have that god because a person needs somewhere to put the ache. But you should know — the ache was never Veyra's absence. It was home's absence. You were praying to the wrong silence."

"Then what do I do now?" Tamsin heard their own voice crack open, young-sounding, nineteen again in the rain. "The book says Oathbearer. Sera says the Door opens only for the royal line. My blood is a map to a country that doesn't exist and a door that kills whoever holds it open. Every road ends in fire, Ilsa. What do I *do*?"

"You do what soldiers have always done with impossible orders." She reached up with her ruined right hand, and Tamsin took it, careful of the burns, and she gripped as hard as she could, which was barely at all, which was everything. "You stop waiting for permission."

"From whom?"

"From the dead. From the god. From me." Her thumb moved against their knuckles, clumsy, tender. "Tamsin Okafor. By right of hire, by right of twenty years' service, by the authority vested in me by a band that's mostly dead or scattered — I release you. From contract, from code, from obedience. Whatever you choose next, you don't choose it as my soldier. You choose it as a free man. Or a free woman. Or whatever in the hells you are — you always were more geography than gender, don't argue. You choose it as *yourself*."

Something broke in Tamsin's chest — not painfully. Like ice going out of a river in spring, the groan and give of a thing that had held too long. They bowed their head over her hand.

"The band was never a chain," they managed. "It was the only home I—"

"I know." Her voice frayed. "That's why it had to be me who cut it. Nobody else loved you enough to do it right."

They stayed like that while the shutter knocked and the tallow guttered. Then Ilsa pulled her hand free and fumbled at her throat, and Tamsin helped her draw out the cord she'd worn since before they'd met — a plain iron ring on a leather string, unremarkable, the kind of ring a caravan guard wears to look married and avoid questions.

"Her signet," Ilsa whispered. "Your mother's. She sold everything else to buy her road. Kept this. Gave it to me at the pass — 'hold this until my child comes looking,' she said, and I told her children never come looking, and she said, 'mine will.'" A ghost of a smile. "Arrogant woman. She was right."

Tamsin took the ring. It was warm from her skin, lighter than iron had any right to be, and stamped on the seal-face with a device they knew from the prophecy's margins, from the boundary stones, from the ghost-fires at Ash Pass: the thorn-crown of Thornfell.

"It won't fit," they said stupidly.

"It's not for your finger. It's for wax, when there's finally something worth sealing. Letters. Treaties. A door." She sank back against the pallet, spent, and her breath had begun to rattle at the bottom of each inhale, the sound Tamsin had heard too many times and never learned to bear. "One more thing. Last thing. Then you can go be dramatic at somebody else."

"I'm listening."

"When you open that Door — and you will, you stubborn mule, you'll open it if it costs both your hands and Sera's patience besides — when you open it, don't go through as an heir reclaiming a kingdom." Her eyes were dimming, but they fixed on Tamsin with the last of their ferocity. "Go through as a guide. Find the road. That was always your true trade. Kingdoms get burned; roads just get walked again." Her lips moved around the shape of a smile. "Walk it well, child. Walk it—"

The sentence didn't end. It just stopped arriving, the way a road stops at a collapsed bridge, and the rattle in her breath stretched long, and long, and then was quiet.

Outside, Brannick's loud inventory-making had gone silent. Through the door's gap, lamplight showed the big man standing with his hat in his hands, and Tamsin realized he had heard everything — had perhaps been posted there to hear it, by a dying woman who trusted her sergeant to witness the release.

Tamsin closed Ilsa's eyes. Straightened her limbs with the care owed to any fallen comrade, though no comrade had ever fallen farther or fought longer. And then, kneeling beside the pallet, they slipped the leather cord over their own head and let the iron ring settle against their sternum, where it lay cool and certain as a compass needle finding north.

* * *

They buried her at dawn above the tree line, where the wind scrubbed everything clean and the mountains could keep watch — the only priests Ilsa had ever respected. Brannick dug; Tamsin read, from memory, the mercenary's committal, the plain brutal words about roads ended and debts paid. Sera, when Tamsin glanced back, stood apart with her hood up, murmuring something in the old liturgy of Veyra, and for once Tamsin did not resent the faith in it. Even gods should have to attend funerals.

Afterward, on the ridge, Brannick cleared his throat.

"The men are asking what happens now. Contract died with her, near as they figure it. Half want to scatter south. Other half—" he shifted, embarrassed— "other half are looking at you."

"At me?"

"Heir of Thornfell, they're calling you. Word travels. Word always travels faster than we'd like." He squinted eastward, and Tamsin followed his gaze, and their stomach dropped.

Smoke. Not one plume but many, rising from the valley settlements along the lowland road — the villages that had sheltered them, fed them, lied to them at risk of ash. Too far to make out details, too near to pretend ignorance. And on the high road beyond them, a dark ribbon was crawling upward into the passes, unhurried, inevitable.

"That's not a patrol," Tamsin said.

"No," Brannick agreed grimly. "Patrols don't need supply wagons. That's an army, or the teeth of one. Aldric's burning the lowlands and driving folk uphill — and there's nowhere uphill *to*, except—" He stopped himself. Looked at Tamsin. "Except the legend. Except the Door. Except you."

The wind came off the snowfields and pressed against them, cold and clean, smelling of stone and distance. Against Tamsin's chest, beneath their shirt, the iron ring seemed to hum — or perhaps that was only their own blood, remembering what it was for.

Below, on the switchback road, the first refugees had appeared: small figures with bundles and children, climbing away from the smoke toward the only promise left in the world, which was a dead god's door and a living stranger's blood.

Tamsin settled their pack, checked the straps out of twenty years of habit, and started downhill to meet them.

"Get the men together," they said. "We're going to need every guide we've got. The pass to the temple runs through country nobody's mapped in a hundred years — and I intend to lead several thousand people across it before Aldric's army finishes its breakfast."
