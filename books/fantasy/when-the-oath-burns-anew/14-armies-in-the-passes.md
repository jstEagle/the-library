## 14. Armies in the Passes

Maren had buried two husbands and one king, and she had learned from all three funerals that smoke was always the first messenger of grief.

She smelled it before dawn, standing in her doorway in the village of Hallowmere with a shawl pulled tight against the autumn cold, and for a long moment she simply breathed it in — pine resin and thatch and something sweeter underneath, like burning grain, like a harvest given to fire instead of the threshing floor. The wind came down off the Thornfell passes carrying it, and Maren, who was seventy-one years old and had survived annexation, famine, and the slow erosion of everything Aldric's crown had not thought worth stealing, felt her old heart turn over like a stone in a plowed field.

"Up," she said to the dark house behind her. "Everyone up. Wake them all."

Her granddaughter Bramble, fourteen and stubborn as a mule in August, appeared at the window with sleep still creasing her face. "Gran, it's not even light—"

"Bramble." Maren said the name once, quietly, the way you strike a bell. "Look east."

The girl looked. Beyond the terraced fields, beyond the orchards stripped half-bare by frost, the sky above the lowland road glowed a deep, wrong orange. Not sunrise. Sunrise came from the other direction, and it did not flicker.

By the time the sun actually rose, they could see the columns of smoke properly — six of them, seven, rising from the villages along the river valley like black trees growing out of the earth. And with the smoke came the people.

* * *

They came up the shepherd tracks in twos and threes at first, then in rivers. A miller with his arms burned to the elbow, cradling nothing, having carried nothing out because there had been no time to carry anything but himself and his children. A widow leading a goat whose milk would keep three infants alive. Two brothers hauling their father on a door torn from its hinges, the old man's breathing wet and rattling. A girl of perhaps ten walking alone, soot-streaked, who would not say where her family was and would not stop walking, and whom Maren finally caught by the collar and folded into the procession because some children needed to be made to stop before they walked themselves off a mountain.

The soldiers had come at night, the survivors said. Aldric's men, in the king's gray-and-crimson, with white hoods over their armor and braziers of pitch they rolled into the granaries. They had not asked questions. They had not taken prisoners worth taking. They had nailed a proclamation to the well-post of every village before they burned it: *By order of His Majesty Aldric, Second of That Name, the mountain provinces are closed. All persons found in the high country after the Feast of Falling Leaves will be judged as rebels.*

"The Feast of Falling Leaves," Maren repeated, when the miller told her. She laughed — a short, terrible sound, more cough than laughter. "That's four days away."

"They gave us four days," the miller agreed. His voice had gone strange and flat, the voice of a man describing weather in a country he no longer lived in. "Four days to walk out of our own homes. He might as well have told the fish to leave the river."

"Why?" Bramble demanded. She had appointed herself Maren's shadow since dawn, carrying the elder's bundle without being asked, as if work could hold fear at arm's length. "Why burn the villages? What did we do?"

"You were born in the wrong place," Maren said, "and remembered the wrong songs." She looked north, up the valley toward the wall of peaks that the maps no longer named. "He isn't punishing us, child. He's erasing us. You can't burn a memory out of people while they're still sitting in the houses their grandmothers built. So he takes the houses first."

Word moved faster than feet, the way word does among the desperate. By the second evening, refugees were arriving from valleys Maren had never visited, speaking dialects she had to lean close to understand, and they all carried the same story in different clothes: the armies were coming up every pass at once. Not raiding. Coming. Supply wagons and siege engines and companies of hooded infantry, moving into the mountains the way water moves into a cracked foundation — patiently, everywhere, meaning to stay until the whole structure came down.

And threaded through all of it, passed mouth to mouth like contraband, was the other story.

The Door. The Last Door. The old tale every Thornfell grandmother kept in the same cupboard as the funeral linens — that when the kingdom was erased from the maps, the land itself had been left one way out, hidden in the high country, sealed against the day it was needed. That a god who had gone silent still kept watch behind it. That an Oathbearer would come.

"I heard it from a drover," said a tanner woman, crouched beside Maren at the third night's fire, her hands too blistered to hold her cup steady. "From the western valleys. He says the shrines have been lighting up. Little fires on the old altars, nobody lighting them. He says the silent god is waking."

"He says," Maren replied. But she said it gently, and she did not say what she was thinking, which was: *I lit a candle at the Veyra shrine in Hallowmere every week for forty years and never once felt an answer, and I would give what teeth I have left to be wrong about the silence now.*

Bramble, wrapped in a borrowed blanket, spoke from the darkness beside her. "Gran. Is it true? About the Door?"

Maren was quiet long enough that the fire had time to spit twice. Around them, three hundred people slept in the open on a shelf of mountain pasture, huddled against the cold that was sharpening nightly, and somewhere below, torchlight moved along the lowland road like a slow infection climbing toward the heart.

"I'll tell you what I know is true," Maren said at last. "I know my mother sang me a song about a Door before I could walk. I know her mother sang it to her, and hers before that, back past anyone's counting, which means somebody, sometime, believed it enough to bother keeping it alive. People don't carry useless things that far." She touched the girl's head, smoothing the soot-stiff hair. "Whether it's true doesn't matter yet. What matters is that three hundred people just climbed a mountain because of it. Hope doesn't need to be proven, Bramble. It needs to be *carried*. That's the whole job of the old — we carry it until someone younger can use it."

"What if nobody can?"

"Then we set it down gently," Maren said, "and die near it. There are worse deaths than dying close to hope." She had seen most of them.

* * *

On the fourth morning, the Feast of Falling Leaves, the army reached the foothills, and Maren watched the end of the world arrive with supply trains.

They came up the river valley in good order, banners snapping, and even at five miles' distance she could read the shape of them the way she'd once read weather: vanguard, main body, baggage train, rear guard. Professional. Patient. Behind the lead companies, the first captured village was already burning — not for tactics, this time, but for instruction. A lesson written in smoke for everyone watching from the heights: *this is what staying costs.*

"We can't outrun them," the miller said. He stood beside Maren at the pasture's edge, and he had stopped being flat-voiced; now he was worse, which was hopeful, and hope made men shake. "Not with the children. Not with the wounded. We've got maybe two days before the passes close, and then—" He didn't finish it. Nobody finished sentences anymore. Everyone knew how they ended.

"Then we don't run," Maren said. "We climb."

"To where? There's nothing up there but stone and the—"

He stopped. Maren watched him stop, watched the word *legend* die in his mouth, and she finished it for him, loud enough for the nearest fifty refugees to hear, because a leader's job — she had decided this somewhere around the second night, without asking anyone's permission, the way leaders always decide — was sometimes just to say the unsayable thing first.

"The Last Door," she said. "Yes. That's exactly where."

A silence went around the pasture like wind through wheat. Then a woman's voice, ragged: "It's a story."

"It's the only thing between us and those fires that isn't a sword," Maren answered. "So we treat it as true. If it's true, we live. If it's false—" she shrugged, and the shrug cost her more than she let show, "—we die higher up, colder, looking at something beautiful instead of something burning. I've made worse trades."

She turned to the crowd gathering around her, three hundred faces and more straggling in hourly, and she raised her voice the way she had once raised it over a market square, the way her mother had raised it over a funeral crowd, the old Thornfell way, from the chest, from before the annexation, from a country that existed only in throats now.

"Listen to me! The roads are closed. The valleys are burning. King Aldric has decided we are a story he wants ended, so we will become a different story — one he cannot burn, because it lives in us and walks on our own two feet. Tonight we take the high track toward the Veyran temple ruins. From there, the old songs say, the way opens for those the god remembers. I don't know if the god remembers us. But I remember *him*, and I remember every prayer I ever wasted on his silent ears, and if he's got any hearing left at all, he knows there are three hundred people coming up his mountain with nowhere else in the world to go!"

A sound went through the crowd — not quite cheering, not yet. Something rawer. The sound of despair turning over in its sleep and beginning, painfully, to wake.

They moved within the hour. Maren walked at the front because no one argued with a seventy-one-year-old woman with a walking staff and forty years of burying people, and Bramble walked beside her holding the banner — not a war banner, nothing so foolish. Maren's mother's tablecloth, embroidered with the boundary-stone pattern of old Thornfell, the interlocking rings that meant *here ends one country and begins another*. The miller carried the two weakest children in shifts. The widow's goat walked in dignified protest. Behind them the column stretched back along the switchbacks farther than Maren could count, a river of the erased flowing uphill, toward stone and snow and a story.

Near dusk, with the burning valleys glowing below them like coals raked from a hearth, the drover came scrambling down the trail from ahead, breathless, waving both arms.

"Elder! Elder Maren!" He caught his breath against his knees. "There's people ahead. On the high track. A caravan — armed ones, smugglers by the look, and a priestess in gray, and—" he gulped air, eyes wide with something Maren hadn't seen on a human face in thirty years, "—and they're saying there's a man with them. An oath-sworn mercenary. They're saying he's got a book a hundred years old with tomorrow's date in it. They're saying—" the drover's voice dropped to a whisper that somehow carried further than his shouting, "—they're saying he's the one. The Oathbearer. And he's asking for us. He's *waiting* for us."

Maren stood very still. Below, the world burned. Above, the first stars were coming out over the pass, cold and patient and impossibly old, and somewhere between the fire and the stars, on a trail of broken stone, a stranger with an impossible book was holding a place open for three hundred people who had nowhere else to go.

"Well," she said at last, to Bramble, to the goat, to whatever silent god might still be listening after all. "Then we'd best not keep him waiting."

She tightened her grip on her staff and started up the last stretch of the pass.
