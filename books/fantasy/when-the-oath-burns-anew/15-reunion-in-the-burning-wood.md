## 15. Reunion in the Burning Wood

The wood burned for two days before Tamsin understood it was following them.

Not chasing — following. The fires Aldric's soldiers had set in the lowland villages crept uphill at the pace of the exiles, as though the flames had been given their orders and meant to see them carried out: drive them high, drive them hungry, drive them to the snowline where a thousand people could freeze quietly without troubling anyone's ledgers.

Tamsin walked at the head of that river of people and did not let themselves look back at the smoke, because looking back was a luxury they could no longer afford. Ahead of them was a goat track that Maren swore her grandmother had danced on as a girl; behind them was everything else.

"Guide." A boy tugged at their sleeve — Pellin, eight years old, soot in his eyebrows, carrying a chicken under one arm like a stolen loaf. The chicken had adopted him somewhere outside Harrowmere and now went where he went, clucking its opinions into his ear. "The old ones are stopping again."

Tamsin turned. Halfway up the switchback, the column had snagged on itself, the way rivers snag on stones. Maren knelt beside an old man named Dorrel who had sat down on a boundary stone — an actual boundary stone, moss-eaten, its carved sigil worn to a suggestion — and was weeping with the particular silence of men who believed crying was a debt they owed no one.

Tamsin went down the line, past the carts that weren't really carts anymore but collections of doors torn from doorframes and fitted with rough wheels, past the woman leading three goats on a rope of plaited bedsheets, past children who had stopped asking questions days ago because the answers were all smoke.

Dorrel looked up at them with wet, furious eyes. "This stone," he said. "It says *Thornfell*. You can still feel the letters."

"I know," Tamsin said.

"My father taught me the words that go with these stones. I thought I'd forgotten them." His hand lay flat on the moss like a man warming himself at a small fire. "I hadn't forgotten them. I just had nowhere to put them."

Tamsin crouched. Up close, Dorrel smelled of ash and sheep's milk and the specific sourness of someone who had walked too far on too little. "Put them here," Tamsin said, tapping the stone. "Then get up, because the light's going and this track turns ugly after dark."

Something in the old man's face shifted — not comfort, nothing so kind, but recognition. He had spent six days watching this mercenary read the mountain the way he used to read his father's face, and whatever he saw in it made him rise, groaning, and take his place in the column again.

That was the thing Tamsin could not say aloud, not to Maren, not to anyone: they had never belonged to Thornfell, and yet the mountain answered them anyway. Every switchback they chose was right. Every dead-end they steered the people around was, in fact, a dead-end, confirmed by some instinct older than their own memory. The country knew them. It was the knowing that terrified them, because knowledge implied expectation, and expectation implied a bill that would come due.

They climbed.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in a hollow below the treeline, where a rock overhang gave shelter from the wind that came off the heights smelling of ice and, beneath the ice, smoke. Nine hundred souls, give or take, arranged in the dark like spilled beads. Fires lit small and shielded. The chicken roosted on Pellin's chest while the boy slept sitting up, upright against a pack, refusing on principle to lie down before the adults did.

Maren found Tamsin at the edge of the overhang, checking the backtrail out of habit rather than fear. Fear required believing there was something left behind worth catching them.

"The rearguard says the riders stopped at Ash Pass," she said. "They're burning the toll-house."

"Corvel doesn't burn things he's finished with. He burns things he wants remembered as gone." Tamsin squinted into the dark. Below, far below, the valley floor glowed in patches, like embers raked across black cloth. "He knows where we're going. Or he guesses. There's only one legend left worth chasing us toward."

"The Door."

"The Door."

Maren was quiet a moment. She was a compact woman, grey-haired, with the forearms of someone who had wrung out laundry for sixty years, and she had a way of standing very still when she was deciding how much truth to spend. "You told us you don't know if the Door is real."

"I told you I know where the book says it is. That's not the same thing."

"And Sera? The priestess?" Maren's voice didn't sharpen — she was too old for sharpening — but it found its mark anyway. "The one who left you at the fork?"

"Gone," Tamsin said. "With the mission. Which is what she always—" They stopped. Swallowed the rest of it, because bitterness was a coin they couldn't afford to spend in front of nine hundred people who needed a guide, not a wound. "Gone."

"Mm," said Maren, which could have meant anything, and left.

Tamsin stayed at the edge of the camp long after the fires had settled to red eyes in the dark. The mountain rose above them, moonlit, and the burned wood below crackled and sighed, and Tamsin thought about the fork in the pass — the actual physical fork, two gray tracks diverging under a cairn — and the way Sera had stood at the point of divergence with her priestess's hands folded and her defector's eyes, saying *I chose the mission, Tamsin, I chose it before I ever met you*, and the way Tamsin had said nothing at all, because anything said in that moment would have been either a lie or a knife.

They should have said the knife. Knives heal cleaner than silences.

They were still thinking about forks when the dog began to bark.

There was exactly one dog in the whole exile column, a brindled, half-wild thing belonging to the goatherd, and it never barked — it had come through six days of fire and flight with the philosophical silence of everything else from Thornfell. When its voice cracked out across the hollow, ragged and astonished, Tamsin was moving before conscious thought caught up, knife already in hand, shouting the watch-word, and then—

—and then the shape came up the backtrail into the last ember-light, hooded, limping slightly, leading a horse whose flanks were lathered white — and pulled back its hood.

Sera.

She looked like a woman who had ridden through a burning country and thought about dying at least twice along the way. Her hair had come loose from its knot. There was a burn-scald along her jaw, fresh, shiny. And slung against her back, wrapped in oilcloth, was a rectangular weight that Tamsin's body recognized before their mind did, the way you recognize a heartbeat.

"You're supposed to be ahead of us," Tamsin said. It came out flat, almost rude. It was the only sentence they trusted.

"Hello to you too." Sera's voice was raw. She looked past Tamsin at the camp — at the hundreds of sleeping shapes, the doors on wheels, the child with the chicken — and something crossed her face that Tamsin had seen once before, in the ruined shrine, when she'd first spoken Veyra's name aloud and flinched. "Nine hundred. You got nine hundred people up a mountain."

"They got themselves. I just picked the track." Tamsin crossed the distance between them, slowly, the way you approach a deer or a landmine. "Whose horse is that?"

"Corvel's. Whose book do you think this is?"

The word *book* went through the camp like wind through dry grass. Heads lifting. Maren appearing out of the dark with a lantern, uninvited, unashamed.

Sera unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of someone handling a relic or a bomb. Corvel's copy — his scribes' copy, the transcription made from the original before the original had gone into the magistrate's evidence-locker in Harrowmere, complete with marginalia in Corvel's own narrow, righteous hand. Tamsin stared at it. The leather was the same age-browned calf. The same impossible ink.

"You stole this from a magistrate's baggage train," Tamsin said.

"I stole this from a magistrate's *tent*," Sera said. "While he was holding evening prayers with his interrogators. There's a difference, and the difference is that if I'm caught, it's not theft they'll hang me for. It's heresy." She held Tamsin's gaze. "Which I suppose makes it official. I've finally defected all the way."

Behind them, the dog lay down with a satisfied huff. Somewhere in the camp, a baby started crying and was hushed. The chicken, offended by the commotion, buried its head under its wing.

Tamsin looked at the book, and then at Sera, and found that everything they had rehearsed at the fork in the pass — every accusation, every cold and correct sentence — had evaporated somewhere between the rehearsal and the woman standing in front of them smelling of horse-sweat and smoke and, faintly, of consecrated oil.

"You left," Tamsin said.

"Yes."

"You chose it. The mission. Before me. You said—"

"I said all of it, and all of it was true, and I would say most of it again." Sera's voice didn't waver, and Tamsin hated her a little for that, and loved her considerably more for what it cost her. "But Tamsin — I've had six days of riding through a burning country to find out which parts of it were lies. Here's what I learned." She took a breath. "I didn't leave *you*. I left because I thought the mission needed me more than you did, and I thought those were the only two options. They weren't. I know that now. I'm not asking you to pretend the fork didn't happen. I'm asking for a different fork."

The lantern hissed. Maren, magnificently, said nothing, though her stillness had the quality of a woman memorizing.

Tamsin wanted to say *you broke it*. They wanted to say *I bled on the boundary stone because of you* — because it was true, the wound below the collarbone that wouldn't close, the old oath failing in their blood like a tide going out, and Sera had known why, had known since the shrine, and had let Tamsin walk toward the ambush without telling them whose blood it actually was, whose throne, whose country, whose dead.

Instead they heard themselves say: "Your jaw's burnt. Sit down before you fall down."

Sera blinked. Then, carefully, as if accepting terms she hadn't dared draft: "All right."

* * *

They treated the burn by the smallest fire, away from the sleepers, with honey from Maren's dwindling stores and a clean strip off Tamsin's own shirt. Sera sat on a stone with the book across her knees and submitted to being bandaged the way soldiers submit to field medicine — eyes elsewhere, enduring touch.

"It's deeper than it looks," Tamsin said.

"It looks deep."

"Good. Then you'll believe me when I tell you not to talk."

They worked in silence for a while. Around them the camp breathed — snores, the goatherd's dog dreaming, the small sounds of nine hundred people doing the bravest, dullest thing in the world, which is continuing. Above the overhang the sky had cleared, and the stars over Thornfell's heights stood in their ancient arrangements, indifferent and exact.

"The margins are Corvel's," Sera said finally, ignoring the instruction. "That's why I took this copy instead of just running. He's been studying it for months, Tamsin. Longer than us. His notes cross-reference the prophecy with church archives, Aldric's private correspondence, survey records from the annexation. He knows things about the Door that we guessed at." Her fingers rested on the narrow script. "And there's a passage he's underlined three times. About the price."

Tamsin tied off the bandage. Their hands were steady. Everything under the hands was not. "The price of opening it."

"The price of being able to open it." Sera turned to look at them, and in the ember-light her face was stripped of everything defector-priestess, everything clever, down to the bare warning underneath. "It's bad, Tamsin. What it asks. What it asks *of you specifically*. I want to tell you tonight, all of it, but—" she glanced toward the sleepers, the listening dark, "—not here. Tomorrow. On the move."

"So I get one more night of not knowing."

"You get one more night of sleep. Those aren't the same gift, but I'm giving both."

Despite everything — despite the fork and the blood and the six days of walking with a hole in their chest shaped like her absence — Tamsin felt their mouth threaten something that, in a better-lit life, might have been a smile. "Still lying by omission. Still calling it mercy."

"Yes," Sera said simply. "Some things don't change."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "Apparently some things come back."

They sat shoulder to shoulder then, not touching, which was its own kind of touching, and watched the burned valley glow below. Neither apologized. The fork stayed in the past where it had happened, unforgiven and unhidden, a scar acknowledged rather than explained away. But Sera did not stand and leave when the fire burned low. And Tamsin did not walk to the edge of the camp and find reasons to be elsewhere. They stayed, each of them, in the other's reach, which was the whole apology either of them knew how to make and perhaps the only honest one available.

Near midnight, Maren brought them both tea made from pine needles, tasted like punishment, and set it down without comment. As she turned to go, she paused.

"There's water coming," she said.

Tamsin frowned. "Rain?"

"Melting." Maren nodded uphill, toward the black mass of the heights. "Warm wind out of the south tonight. Happens sometimes, early season. The snow comes down fast in the high passes when it does." Her eyes moved from Tamsin to Sera and back, and held the particular patience of a woman who had outlived three kings' worth of bad news. "Whatever's written in that book about where you're taking us — you'll want to get there before the water does."

She shuffled off into the dark. Far above them, invisible in the night, the snowfields of the Last Door's mountain had already begun, silently, inch by inch, to move.
