## 16. The Price Written Down

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Sera found the cipher.

She had been turning the pages of the prophecy for three days — turning them the way a woman turns a stone in her palm, looking for the seam. The book was old enough that its leather had gone soft as skin, and it smelled of tallow and cedar and something older beneath, something like the inside of a temple shut for a hundred years. Tamsin slept across the cave mouth with their sword across their knees even now, even wounded, even after everything. The refugees camped below them in the valley, cook-fires scattered like fallen stars, and above them the mountain climbed into cloud toward a Door that had no business existing.

Sera had read the prophecy aloud twice, once in trade-tongue and once stumbling through the Old Thornfell script she'd been taught in seminary and never thought she'd use again. Both times, the text had ended the same way: *the Oathbearer shall open what was closed, and the people shall pass.* Clean. Simple. A door, a key, a walking-through.

It was too clean. Prophecy was never clean. She had spent eleven years in Aldric's church learning how scripture lied by omission, how the comfortable reading was always the one someone wanted you to find. And so on the third night, instead of reading forward, she read backward — and found that the last quire of the book was written in a different hand, smaller, cramped, the ink browned almost to invisibility, and that the letters were not letters at all until you held the page near flame.

Heat-writing. The oldest kind. Words that only surrendered themselves to fire.

Her hands shook as she tilted the page toward the coals. Not close enough to scorch — never that, she would sooner cut off her own fingers than scar this book again — but close enough that the hidden ink woke, browning letter by letter, rising out of the paper like drowned things surfacing.

*To the one who reads this in the last days,* the hidden text began, *know that we did not hide these words out of cowardice but out of mercy.*

"Sera." Tamsin's voice, low, from across the fire. "You've been at that book since dusk. What is it?"

"Come here," she said. "I need you to see this. I need—" Her throat closed. "I need you to hear it before I can make myself say it."

They came. They always came when she called, and she hated how much she loved that, hated the way trust looked on a person who had been lied to by everyone who claimed to love them. They sat beside her, close enough that their shoulder touched hers, and the warmth of them was a small unbearable thing against what she was about to do.

She turned the page toward the fire again, and together they watched the words surface.

*We who wrote this were priests of Veyra in the year the Door was sealed,* the hidden hand continued. *We sealed it ourselves. Understand this first, before anything else: the Last Door was not closed by our enemies. It was closed by us, by choice, by oath — because what waits beyond it cannot be permitted to fall into the hands of conquerors, and because there was no other way to save what we could still save.*

Tamsin went very still. Sera felt it through the point where their shoulders touched, a stillness like deep water.

"The Door was locked from inside," Tamsin said slowly. "By Thornfell's own priests."

"And opened only from inside," Sera said. "Keep listening."

*The Door answers to one blood and one vow,* the text ran on. *It opens to the Oathbearer — the last of the royal line, bound to Veyra by the old border-oath, spoken whole and unbroken. But hear the price, and do not soften it for whoever comes after us, though mercy begs us to. The Door does not open to an Oathbearer under protection. It opens to an Oathbearer made naked. To pass through, the Oathbearer must willingly burn their oath — must renounce, before the threshold, every bond between themselves and the god. Veyra's protection, Veyra's guidance, Veyra's presence: all of it, surrendered freely, irrevocably, forever.*

The fire popped. Somewhere down the valley a child cried and was hushed.

*And know the worst of it,* the hidden hand wrote, pressing harder now, the letters deeper, as if the writer had gouged them. *Outside the Door there is no god. We do not mean the god is far. We mean the god is nothing. The lands beyond lie past the reach of every power, every prayer, every listening ear. Those who pass through leave Veyra behind as surely as the dead do — more surely, for the dead at least are mourned by heaven. The Oathbearer who burns their oath will walk into a country where no prayer has ever been answered and none ever will be. They will spend the rest of their life unheard.*

Sera stopped tilting the page. Her eyes were burning, and she could not tell if it was the smoke or something else.

"There's more," she said, because there was. There was always more. She tilted the page one final time.

*This is why the god fell silent,* the text said. *Understand — Veyra did not abandon us. Veyra went quiet the day the Door sealed, because a god who speaks must be present, and presence beyond the Door is impossible. The silence you have suffered, all of you, all these years — it is not wrath. It is distance. It is a god standing at the edge of what it can reach, saying everything it can say, which is nothing, which is only the shape of its absence. When the Door reopens and the people pass beyond, the god will remain here, on this side, alone with the conquerors, holding the silence like a wound. The Oathbearer does not lead the people to salvation. The Oathbearer leads them out of the story. And stays outside it with them.*

*We write this so that whoever comes after will choose with open eyes. An oath burned unwillingly is only broken. An oath burned willingly is a gift. Make certain, Oathbearer, whichever you are, whenever you are, that you know which one you are carrying.*

The ink faded as the page cooled, sinking back beneath the surface of the paper, and the cave was very quiet.

Tamsin did not move for a long time. Sera watched their face the way she had learned to watch weather over the passes — the small changes that meant everything. Their jaw worked once. Their thumb traced the edge of the sword-hilt at their knee, back and forth, back and forth, the way they did when they were counting something invisible.

"So," they said at last, and their voice was level in the way that Sera knew cost them everything. "Let me say it back to you. To open the Door, I have to give up the god. Permanently. Not just for me — everyone who walks through. Every refugee down in that valley. Every child. They don't just leave Thornfell. They leave *Veyra*. Forever. And the god—" Their breath caught. "The god stays here. Alone. With him."

"With Aldric," Sera said. "Yes."

"And the silence — all those years I knelt in shrine-ruins and got nothing back, all those years I thought I'd done something wrong, that the oath had rotted in my mouth or the god had spat me out—"

"It wasn't you." Sera heard her own voice crack and let it. "Tamsin, it was never you. You were praying across an ocean of distance. The god has been screaming at us for a century and the scream just doesn't carry."

"That's supposed to comfort me?" But there was no anger in it. There was something worse — there was grief arriving all at once, decades of it, the grief of a soldier learning the war they'd fought alone had never actually required them to fight alone. Tamsin put a hand flat on the stone floor, steadying themselves against the mountain itself. "All right. All right. Say the rest of it. Say the part you've been circling for three days."

Sera took a breath. This was the part. This was the knife she had to hand them handle-first, because anything less would be Corvel's method, Aldric's method, the church's method — truth used as a hook.

"The renunciation has to be willing," she said. "Freely chosen. That's not decoration in the text; it's the mechanism. The Door reads the heart the way a lock reads a key. If you burn the oath out of duty — because the prophecy says to, because the refugees need you to, because I need you to — it won't open. Or worse, it will open wrong." She turned to face them fully. "Everything that's driven you since the smuggler's cache — the oath, the code, the mission, the inheritance — none of it can be the reason. You have to walk to that threshold wanting to give up the only thing that's ever kept you alive, for no reason except that you *choose* it. Choose it the way you'd choose a person. Not a country. Not a prophecy. A person."

"A person," Tamsin repeated.

"You know who I mean."

"I know who you mean." Their eyes came up, dark in the coal-light, and for a moment Sera saw in them the mercenary from the passes, the one who trusted nothing, and then she saw the person underneath, the one who had knelt at ruined shrines for years whispering prayers to a silence, and her chest hurt. "And if I can't? If I get to the threshold and I'm doing it for the mission, for Maren's people, for the dead captain whose grave I haven't had time to dig properly — if it's duty dressed up as choice, the way it's been dressed up my whole life?"

"Then the Door stays shut," Sera said simply. "And Aldric's army reaches the valley in two days."

* * *

They told the others at dawn.

Not everything — Sera argued for everything and lost, and understood losing, because Tamsin was right: a thousand frightened people did not need to hear that salvation meant exile from heaven. They needed to hear that the Door was real and the road existed. The theology could come later, on the far side, in a country where no one would ever pray again and would have to learn to live without it.

Maren took the news the way she took everything, standing straight with her hands folded, an old woman built out of stubbornness and funeral clothes. "So the god stays behind," she said. "With *him*."

"With him," Tamsin confirmed.

Maren was silent a long moment. Down the slope, the refugee camp was waking — tarps being shaken out, a mule complaining, someone singing half a bar of a Thornfell planting song before catching themselves, because songs about home were dangerous now, they made people stop walking.

"My grandmother prayed to Veyra every morning of her life," Maren said finally. "My mother too. I taught my daughters the morning prayer and they taught theirs. And the god never answered any of us, not once, not in sixty years, and we prayed anyway." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and fierce. "Do you know what that means, Oathbearer? It means we already know how to live without an answer. We've been practicing for a hundred years. Whatever's past that Door — silence or worse — we'll carry it fine. Don't you dare carry it for us."

She walked away before anyone could answer, which Sera suspected was the point.

But it was Tam's old sergeant, Brannoc — one-armed, gray-bearded, a man who had followed the captain for thirty years and Tamsin for five — who caught Sera afterward, away from the fires, in the shadow of the boundary stone they'd raised at the camp's edge, a piece of Thornfell planted in alien soil.

"You're the priestess," he said. No accusation in it. Just fact, the way soldiers stated facts.

"I was."

"You were." He worked his jaw. "Then tell me true, priestess, because I've watched our Oathbearer since the cache, and I've seen what that book costs them, and now there's a new weight on them that they won't name. What's the price?"

Sera weighed lying. She was good at lying; she'd been trained in it by professionals. But Brannoc had the eyes of a man who had buried friends, and lies slid off eyes like that.

"They have to give up the god," she said. "Forever. Willingly. And the god gives up *them* — stays behind, alone, when the Door closes."

Brannoc stared at her. Then he laughed, short and terrible, a sound with no humor in it at all. "A soldier's bargain," he said. "Stay behind so the rest of us get out. Hold the line nobody thanks you for." He shook his head. "That god of yours sounds like the best soldier I never served under."

He walked back down toward the fires, and Sera stood alone at the boundary stone and let herself feel, for one unguarded minute, the full shape of what they had learned. A god that had gone silent out of love, not absence. A people who had practiced faithfulness without reward for a century without knowing it. And an Oathbearer who was going to stand at a threshold in a few days' time and be asked to burn the only promise that had ever held their life together — and to mean it, truly mean it, or everyone died.

*An oath burned unwillingly is only broken. An oath burned willingly is a gift.*

She thought of the night at the fork in the pass, when she had chosen the mission over honesty and Tamsin had walked away from her into the snow, and how they had begun again anyway, without apology, without erasure, choosing each other fresh each morning since. Maybe that was the only rehearsal that mattered. Maybe loving someone after a betrayal was the same muscle as burning an oath willingly — the same terrible freedom, the same refusal to let the old terms govern the new choice.

Or maybe she was rationalizing, the way she had rationalized in the church for eleven years, dressing up what she wanted in the language of what was holy.

Below her, Tamsin stood at the head of the column, checking harness knots, murmuring to the mule-drivers, doing the thousand small competent things that kept a thousand people alive. In a few days they would climb into the cloud toward the antechamber, and somewhere above them Corvel was climbing too, and behind them Aldric's torches were eating the lowland villages one by one.

Tamsin looked up and caught her watching. Held her gaze across the distance. Didn't smile — they weren't people who smiled easily anymore — but lifted one hand, briefly, the way travelers do.

Sera lifted hers back, and made herself a promise she had no right to make: whatever Tamsin chose at the threshold, she would not make it for them. She would stand beside them, and she would let the choice be free, even if freedom killed them both.

That was when the scout came down from the heights at a run, snow spraying, shouting a word that emptied the camp of breath:

"Smoke. Smoke on the northern ridge — the vanguard's found the pass."
