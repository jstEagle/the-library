## 17. Corvel at the Threshold

Magistrate Corvel had burned forty-one books in his career, and he could have named every one of them.

Not their titles — titles were nothing, titles were the dresses texts wore to market — but their contents. The heresy in them. The specific shape of the rot. He had read each one cover to cover before feeding it to the fire, because a man who condemned what he had not examined was not a servant of order but of appetite, and Corvel had never once in thirty years of service confused himself with an hungry man. The first book he'd burned, at nineteen, had been a Thornfell genealogy claiming descent for a shepherd family from the old royal line. He remembered the weight of it in his hands. He remembered thinking: *this is a weapon dressed as a ledger*, and he had been right. Weapons were why he did the work. Every text that named a dead country was a knife pointed at living people; every prophecy was an instruction manual for making orphans.

He told himself this now, climbing the mountain road in the vanguard's wake, and found the telling sat strangely in his mouth, like bread gone stale.

The pass above him was on fire. Aldric's orders had been simple and Corvel had executed such orders for three decades without once losing sleep: seal the passes, burn the high villages, leave nothing standing between the Oathbearer and the Door but ash. And yet. His horse picked its way past a burned shrine — a small one, a roadside thing, barely more than an alcove with a stone basin — and inside it, untouched by the flames that had taken everything around it, someone had left a clay cup of melted snow and a sprig of rowan. An offering. To a god who did not answer. Made within hours, probably, of the soldiers passing.

Corvel reined in and looked at it longer than he meant to.

"You want us to put the torch to it, Magistrate?" asked Sergeant Hale, riding behind.

"No." He heard his own voice come out flat, official. "It's a cup and a weed, Sergeant. Save your fire for something that matters."

But the words *something that matters* rang oddly in his ears all the way up the switchbacks, and by the time the vanguard reached the antechamber of the Last Door, Corvel had stopped listening to his own justifications entirely and started, for the first time in thirty years, merely hearing them.

* * *

The antechamber defied every expectation he had carried up the mountain.

He had imagined a cave mouth, perhaps, or a ruin — the usual squalor of the miraculous, all rubble and superstition. Instead the mountain simply opened. A hall of black stone, seamless, older than any masonry he knew, lit from no visible source by a pale gold light that lay along the floor like water. At its far end stood the Door itself: two leaves of grey metal, taller than the vantage of memory, carved with figures so worn they might have been people or might have been wind. It was closed. It looked as though it had always been closed, and would remain closed until the mountains themselves were sand.

And before it, small against that immensity, stood three figures.

The priestess — Sera, his files said, former hierodeacon of the Aldrican church, apostate, twice-escaped from custody — stood nearest the Door. Behind her, a ragged press of refugees crowded the antechamber's mouth: perhaps two hundred souls, the old and the young among them, carrying what they could. Children were crying. Someone was singing, low and tuneless, in a language Corvel recognized from interrogation transcripts and had never once heard spoken whole.

And in front of them all, alone, hand resting on the pommel of a plain mercenary's sword, stood Tamsin Okafor.

Corvel had studied the man for weeks. He knew the file: smuggler, caravan-master, oath-sworn to Veyra in the old rite, born — allegedly — of nobody, belonging to nowhere. He had expected a fanatic or a fool. Fanatics he understood; fools he pitied. What he saw instead was a tired person in a scorched coat, standing with the particular stillness of someone who has already decided everything there is to decide, watching Corvel come down the hall with eyes that held no hatred at all.

That absence of hatred unsettled him more than any drawn blade could have.

"Magistrate Corvel," Tamsin said, when the soldiers halted twenty paces off. Their voice carried strangely in the black stone, neither echoing nor dying. "You've climbed a long way to watch a door stay shut."

"I've climbed a long way," Corvel said, dismounting — one did not negotiate from horseback; that was theater, and he despised theater — "to make certain it does."

"It will open."

"It cannot. The Door is legend. Legends do not open; they are opened, by credulous people, into rooms." He walked forward, unhurried, boots silent on the stone. Up close the Oathbearer was younger than the reports suggested and more worn, both at once, the way rivers are young and ancient in the same bed. "I have read your book, Okafor. All of it. I burned my own copy last spring, after transcription."

Something moved in Tamsin's face. Not fear. Recognition, maybe. "Then you know how it ends."

"I know how books like that end. In graves. I have filled enough of them." Corvel clasped his hands behind his back, the posture of thirty years, the posture that had outlasted three kings' ministers and every rival who mistook conviction for stubbornness. "But I did not climb here to threaten you. Threats are for men who doubt their offer. Mine is genuine, and you will find it more generous than anything your prophet ever promised."

"Say it, then."

"The king offers you full pardon. All crimes dissolved — smuggling, sedition, the deaths at Ash Pass, all of it. Letters patent under seal. A commission in the king's own scouts; you know these mountains better than any officer he has, and he knows it too. Land. A house. Your comrades freed, those still living." Corvel let the silence do its work a moment. "And passage out. Yours and the woman's. You walk away from this door, tonight, before my soldiers' patience becomes my problem, and none of this needs to end in ash. Two hundred people downstairs in that valley will die tomorrow if you persist. They don't have to. That choice isn't prophecy, Okafor. It's arithmetic."

He watched the words land. He had delivered harder bargains to harder men and watched them weep with relief. But Tamsin only looked at him for a long moment with an expression Corvel could not catalogue, and then — absurdly, unbearably — asked:

"Do you believe it? Any of it? The Door. Veyra. Do you believe?"

"The question is irrelevant."

"It's the only question." Tamsin's voice stayed level, almost gentle, which was somehow worse than anger. "You've burned forty-one books. I know about you; smugglers trade in magistrate lore the way children trade in ghosts. They say you read every one before you burned it. Is that true?"

"It is true." He did not know why he admitted it.

"Forty-one books," Tamsin repeated softly. "Read, cover to cover, by a man who claims belief is irrelevant. Nobody reads that carefully to guard against lies, Magistrate. You read that way because some part of you was looking for the truth in them. And it frightened you every time you didn't find it where you expected — or worse, when you did."

Behind him, Corvel heard Sergeant Hale shift his weight. The hall's golden light lay on everyone equally, soldier and refugee alike, and there was something in that impartiality he found suddenly hard to bear.

"I believe in order," Corvel said. "I believe that countries erased from maps save lives, because half-dead countries kill by the thousand. Thornfell died in a week of war. If it rises now, it dies again in a year, and takes the free cities with it. I believe that every prophecy you open is a mass grave you fill later. I have spent my life choosing the smaller number of dead, and I will not apologize for the arithmetic."

"And how many dead since morning?" Tamsin asked quietly. "On the northern ridge. The villages your vanguard burned coming up."

"That was war."

"That was arithmetic," Tamsin said. "Yours. And it came due today, same as mine always comes due. I'm not here to pretend my hands are clean, Corvel. I've done things in these passes you'd have hanged me for, and I'd do half of them again. But I learned something on this road that I think you learned tonight and won't say aloud." They turned, slightly, and gestured — not at the Door, but at the crowd pressed into the antechamber's mouth. An old woman held a child asleep against her shoulder. A boy of maybe ten gripped a boundary stone, an actual boundary stone, torn from some ruined village and lashed to his back like a pack frame. "Look at them. Not one of them is fighting you. They're not an army, they're not a prophecy, they're not even a country anymore. They're just people who want to be somewhere the burning hasn't reached. The Door is the only somewhere left. You can call that credulity. I call it the smallest hope I've ever seen, and I've seen people eat their boots for less."

Corvel looked. He was, whatever else, a man who looked at things; it was the whole discipline of his life, examination before condemnation. He looked at the boy with the stone, and the stone had old letters on it, worn nearly smooth, marking a border that existed nowhere except in the loyalty of a ten-year-old's spine.

Something in his chest shifted — a hairline thing, a crack in fired clay — and he hated it, and he could not unfeel it.

"The Door may not even open," he said, and heard, to his horror, that his voice had lost its footing. "You understand that. You may stand here and speak your renunciation and die of nothing but cold air and disappointment."

"Then I'll have died honest," Tamsin said. "Which is more than the pardon buys me." They drew a slow breath. "No, Magistrate. I've had offers before. Better dressed than yours, some of them. This is the first one I've refused where refusing didn't feel like losing. Tell your king the Oathbearer declines. Tell him—" and here something almost like kindness crossed the exhausted face— "tell him a man who read forty-one books said the forty-second would have broken him."

* * *

Afterward, Corvel could never reconstruct the next quarter hour in proper sequence. Memory kept it as sensation: the shout from the hall's entrance as the main column arrived; the priestess beginning to speak in the old tongue, her voice thin and cracking and somehow still filling the black stone like water fills a cup; the Door — impossible, absolute — beginning, very faintly, to glow along its seam.

And Corvel, standing in the middle of it with a drawn sword he did not remember drawing, facing Tamsin Okafor across five paces of golden light.

"Last chance," he said. The words tasted like all his years. "Step aside. I will not ask again."

"I know," Tamsin said. They did not draw their sword.

Kill him. The thought arrived with the clean efficiency of doctrine: kill the Oathbearer, scatter the refugees, burn the antechamber, go home to a quiet report and a medal and the forty-second book, which he would not read, which he would throw into the fire unread, which he would never have to—

Corvel looked at the sword in his own hand. Then at the boy with the boundary stone, staring at him with wide, uncomprehending eyes. Then at the Door, glowing now like a hearth seen through a keyhole, like something patient beyond all his patience.

His arm lowered. Not all the way. Enough.

"Hale," he said, and his voice sounded foreign to him, scraped thin. "Bind him. Bind them both. Alive." He swallowed. "The king ordered the Oathbearer taken alive if practicable. We will proceed as though it is practicable."

"Magistrate—" Hale began.

"*Alive*, Sergeant."

The soldiers moved. Tamsin did not resist; Sera never stopped speaking, her voice rising now toward something that was either prayer or command, and through the black stone beneath Corvel's boots came a vibration, deep and rhythmic, like the pulse of an enormous heart waking from a long sleep.

He stood in the golden light with his prisoner's wrists being bound before him, and Corvel understood, with the terrible clarity he had spent thirty years earning, that he had just made his first arrest in three decades whose outcome he genuinely did not know — and that the fault line running through him, the hairline crack in all that fired certainty, was not going to close.

Behind him, in the old tongue, the priestess spoke a word he knew from his forty-one books.

The Door began to open.
