## 20. A Country Carried in Exile

Maren had buried three kings in her lifetime, and she had wept at none of the graves.

The first had died before she was born — old King Edvard the Lamented, whose name was already a song when she was a girl selling river-mussels in Thornfell's low markets. The second was the kingdom itself, killed quietly in an office in Aldric's capital by men with ink-stained fingers who redrew a border and called it peace. The third was every person who had ever said *I am from Thornfell* and been answered with a blank stare, or worse, with a correction: *You mean the Southern Reaches.*

She was seventy-one years old, her hands were ruined from fifty years of dye-work, and she had just watched a mercenary hold open a door made of mountain and silence while ten thousand people walked out of their own history into a country that did not exist yet.

Now the boundary stones were going into the ground, and Maren could not stop crying.

* * *

They had come through on the third morning after the Door opened, when the last of Aldric's rearguard had been turned back and the light that shielded the threshold had finally dimmed to something a body could look at without flinching. What lay beyond was not what anyone had imagined. There had been arguments about it all through the passes, in the refugee columns — some swore it would be a green country, others a city of glass, one dyer's boy insisted it would be nothing at all, just more mountain, and that Veyra's mercy was simply a longer road.

It was none of those things. It was a valley so wide its far edge dissolved into haze, ringed by peaks no map had ever troubled to name because no cartographer had ever stood where Maren now stood. Rivers ran silver through it, braiding and rebraiding like thread through a loom. The grass came up to a grown woman's waist. The air tasted of cold water and something sweeter underneath — blossoms from trees none of them could identify, white-flowered and heavy with bees.

Empty. That was the first thing everyone noticed, and the second thing everyone feared. Empty as a house before the furniture comes. Empty as a grave before the grief does.

But Tamsin Okafor had walked out ahead of them all, down into that grass, and planted a boot in the soil, and looked back up the slope at the ragged column of ten thousand souls, and said — Maren heard it herself, carried up the wind as if the valley wanted it heard:

"Well. Someone's got to tell me where we're putting the latrines."

And the terrible knot of fear in ten thousand chests had loosened, just slightly, just enough, because there is no fear so stubborn as the kind that survives a miracle, and there is no cure for it so reliable as a joke about latrines.

* * *

The boundary stones came down off the ox-carts two days later.

There were nine of them left in all the world. Maren knew this the way she knew the recipe for madder-red and the names of her eleven grandchildren: absolutely, without question. Nine boundary stones had marked Thornfell once, great granite pillars taller than a man, carved by hands older than any chronicle, set at the corners and crossings of the kingdom since before the usurper's grandfather drew breath. When Aldric annexed the country, his engineers had toppled seven of them within the year and ground them into road-fill. The eighth had been dynamited in front of a crowd as a lesson.

The ninth had been saved by Maren's mother.

Not the whole stone — a fragment, a piece the size of a bread loaf, pried loose the night before the demolition and carried forty miles in a flour sack. It had passed to Maren, and Maren had kept it under the floorboards of her dye-house for thirty years, wrapped in oilcloth, and when the burning started this last time — when Aldric's armies came into the mountains torching villages — she had dug it up and put it in her pack beside the dried fish and the baby clothes, and she had told no one why.

Because everyone would have thought her mad. Carrying a rock across a country, while soldiers burned behind them and the sky rained ash. An old woman with a rock.

But then the prophecy book had named an Oathbearer. Then a silent god had found its voice again in the mouth of a mountain. Then a Door had opened onto a valley that no king owned, and Maren had stood in the antechamber with the oilcloth parcel pressed against her chest, and she had known — with the same absolute certainty that she knew the madder recipe — exactly why her mother had saved it.

* * *

"You want to do *what*?"

Tamsin Okafor looked down at the fragment in Maren's arms the way people look at something they have been too tired to see clearly. They had lost weight in the passes; the hollows under their eyes had gone from shadow to structure. But their voice still had that dry gravel-temper that Maren had decided, somewhere on the fourth day of the exodus, was the most reassuring sound in either world.

"Boundary stones," Maren said. "A country isn't a place until someone draws its edges. That's how Aldric killed us — not with soldiers first, with surveyors. He erased the borders and let us fade at the edges like cloth left in the sun." She turned the fragment over. The carving on it was worn nearly smooth, but the old spiral-and-thorn sigil of the royal line still caught the morning light, the same sigil that had blazed above the Door when Tamsin spoke the new oath. "We plant these again. Here. New soil, old stones. And this land becomes Thornfell the way a scar becomes skin."

Tamsin crouched. Ran a thumb over the worn spiral, careful, the way one touches a wound that might be one's own.

"There are eight more?" they asked.

"Seven," Maren corrected, and watched something flicker across the mercenary's face — the arithmetic of loss done instantly, reflexively, the way soldiers count. "Two survived whole, hidden in cellars and shrines. Five are fragments like this one. Enough. You don't need the whole wall to know where the wall stood." She looked up at them. "You're the Oathbearer. The stones will answer you. The old songs say the founding stones woke for the first king's hand, and—" She stopped herself, gentled it. "I'm sorry. I keep forgetting you didn't get raised on the songs."

"No," Tamsin said quietly. "Tell me the songs. All of them. I've got thirty years of catching up to do and apparently only one lifetime to do it in."

Behind them, Sera stood with her priestess's staff planted in the new earth, watching the exchange with an expression Maren had learned to read over the long march: love wearing duty's clothes, or maybe the other way around. The defector had not left Tamsin's side since the Door. Whatever fracture had opened between them in the high passes, it had closed — not healed, Maren suspected, but closed the way a well-set bone closes, stronger at the break than anywhere else.

"We'll need to find where they go," Tamsin said at last, standing. "Nine points make a shape. If we plant them wrong, we build a cage instead of a country." They looked out over the valley — the rivers, the haze, the white-blooming trees — and Maren saw them doing it, saw the road-guide waking up inside the reluctant heir: reading land the way other people read scripture. "That ridge line runs north-south. Water table's high on the eastern flank — see how the grass changes color? Stone wants to sit on high ground near water but out of the flood plain. Give me a week and a good horse and I'll know every fold of this valley better than I know my own scars."

"And then you'll place the stones yourself," Maren said. It wasn't quite a question.

Tamsin went very still.

"I'll show your people where," they said carefully.

"That's not what I asked, child."

"I'm sixty years younger than you, grandmother, don't *child* me—"

"A country needs a head," Maren said, and her voice cracked on it despite everything, seventy-one years of dye-house discipline failing her at last. "The refugees are asking already. They ask me, and I'm nobody — I'm a dyer with a rock. They ask the captain's survivors, and those poor souls say *ask the Oathbearer*. They ask Priestess Sera, and she says *ask the Oathbearer*. Everyone is waiting for you to say the word, and every day you don't, the waiting curdles a little. Fear fills empty chairs faster than hope does. I've seen it happen. I watched it happen *home*."

The silence stretched. Somewhere downslope, children were shouting at each other over the rules of a game that required no equipment except a stick and a grudge, and the sound of it — ordinary, human, alive — filled the pause the way water fills a cup.

"Maren," Tamsin said at last, and their voice had gone quiet and rough in a way she hadn't heard before, "do you know what my life was, four months ago? I guided contraband through passes for money. My oath was to a god who'd gone quiet, and I kept it anyway because it was the only shape my life had. Then I found out I was a prophecy's puppet, a dead queen's blood, a stranger's heir. Every single thing I thought I chose about myself turned out to be written down somewhere a hundred years before I was born." They laughed, short and without much humor. "I burned my old oath to buy the right to choose anything at all. And the first choice anyone offers me is a crown-shaped hole with my name pre-written on it."

"It doesn't have to be a crown," Maren said.

"Everything is a crown. That's the problem with chairs." Tamsin looked away, out at the valley, and their hand drifted unconsciously to their side — where the old wound had been, Maren realized, the one that wouldn't close until Sera told them the truth of their blood. Some wounds you carry as maps of what you are. "Ask me for anything else."

* * *

So Maren asked for something else, and it took her three days to understand what she had asked for, because Tamsin Okafor was a slippery creature when cornered and had apparently decided that declining leadership counted as cornered.

What they gave her instead was mornings.

Every morning at first light, Tamsin rode out — Sera with them, always, the two of them falling into an easy rhythm of bickering that Maren privately classified as courtship conducted entirely through complaints about saddle-sores — and they read the land. And every evening Tamsin came back to the growing camp and taught. Not decrees. Lessons. Where to dig a well. Which slopes would grow barley and which would only disappoint. How to site a shrine — "small ones, open doors, no walls worth hiding behind, a god who spent a century learning to speak again shouldn't be asked to shout" — and where the roads should run, and which bend of which river would flood in spring, and how to build a bridge that would outlive everyone currently breathing.

The refugees called it *the Oathbearer's word*, and Tamsin called it *a survey*, and both were lying a little, and the country grew anyway.

Maren walked with them on the fifth such evening, up the eastern flank where the grass changed color, to the place where the first stone would stand. They had brought the fragment wrapped in oilcloth, and a spade, and a flask of the terrible plum spirit that the distiller families had begun making from fruit none of them could identify but which fermented enthusiastically regardless.

"Hold it a moment," Tamsin said, and handed Maren the fragment instead of setting it themselves.

Maren's ruined hands closed around the granite, around thirty years of floorboards and flour sacks, around her mother's fingerprints somewhere underneath the oilcloth if ghosts worked that way, and she understood suddenly what the mercenary was doing. Not refusing the chair. Refusing to sit in it alone. Making the first mark on the new country belong to a dyer's hands, not a queen's blood.

"You clever, stubborn wretch," Maren said, and Tamsin grinned, and for a moment looked twenty years younger and a hundred miles less haunted.

They dug together. The soil came up dark and rich and smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet. And when Maren set the fragment into the earth and tamped it down with the flat of the spade, the old spiral-and-thorn sigil caught the last of the sunset — and held it. Just for a heartbeat, longer than stone should hold light. Warmth rose through Maren's palms, faint as a memory of a fire, and somewhere in the valley below, she would swear until her dying day, every white-flowered tree released its blossoms at once, a slow snowfall of petals in the gold light.

Neither of them spoke for a long moment.

"It knows where it is," Maren whispered.

"It remembers being part of a wall," Tamsin said. Their voice was unsteady. "Walls remember. Gods remember. Apparently even countries remember, if you carry them far enough." They looked down at the small stone standing in the vast grass, absurd and undeniable, the first inch of a border in a land without maps. "Eight more to go. We ride north tomorrow — there's a shelf of high ground above the confluence that's practically begging for one. And after that..."

"After that," Maren said, "the council meets. Properly this time. Charters and arguments and somebody's uncle demanding a title. A country can't be founded on evenings and lessons forever, child, and you know it."

"I know it." Tamsin crouched, laid a palm flat against the warm granite, and Maren watched them take a breath that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than lungs. "Then here's what I'll give the council, and they can choke on it or celebrate it: I won't lead Thornfell. I never knew it. It wouldn't be mine to lead — it'd be the prophecy's, and I've had enough of being written down." They straightened, and there it was again, that road-guide's steadiness, the competence that had carried contraband and caravans and finally a nation through the mountains. "But I'll walk every road this country has. First one through every pass, every season, until the passes know my boots. I'll teach whoever wants to learn the land, and I'll guide whoever wants to go anywhere, and when the shrines start going up — and they will, Sera's already drawing plans on the back of Corvel's confiscated dispatch papers, which I consider poetic justice — I'll carry the first stone to each one myself."

"And what do we call a person like that?" Maren asked softly. "If not a queen?"

Tamsin considered it, looking north, where the peaks went on blue and nameless toward tomorrow's road.

"A guide," they said. "Call me a guide."

* * *

They rode out at dawn, as promised — Tamsin and Sera, two horses and a mule loaded with surveying line and the terrible plum spirit, silhouettes shrinking up the northern trail until the haze took them. Half the camp turned out to watch them go, and someone started singing one of the old border songs, the one about the ninth stone, and the singing spread down the hillside like fire through dry grass until ten thousand voices held the melody against the mountains.

Maren stayed at the planted stone a while after the song ended. Her knees complained. Everything complained, at seventy-one; she had stopped taking it personally.

She rested her hand on the granite one last time, feeling the faint warmth still living in it, and spoke aloud, because her mother had always said stones listen better than priests.

"Thirty years under the floorboards," she told it. "Worth every winter. You just wait — there'll be eight siblings for you by spring, and houses around you, and children climbing on you and grandparents scolding them off you, and in fifty years some fool scholar will write a treatise about the Founding Stones of New Thornfell and get half the details wrong, and that's how you'll know we made it. That's how you'll know we're *real*."

The warmth pulsed once under her palm, like a heartbeat, like a held breath released.

Then, from the north — faint, carried on the wind off the high passes — Maren heard hoofbeats stop, and Sera's voice rising sharp with alarm, and Tamsin's answer cutting through it, calm in the way that meant danger:

"Maren! Get the council! There's smoke past the ridge — and riders coming down the pass. A lot of them. And they're flying *our* colors."

Maren straightened, heart hammering, and turned to face the mountains where the future was coming down the trail — carrying, of all impossible things, the thorn-and-spiral banner of a kingdom everyone present had believed dead.

She ran for the council tents as fast as seventy-one-year-old legs would allow.
