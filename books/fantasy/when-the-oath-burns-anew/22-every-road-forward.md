## 22. Every Road Forward

The morning fog came off the new country's valleys the way it always did in the cold months — slow, unhurried, as if the land itself had learned patience from its people. Tamsin Okafor stood at the head of the caravan and watched it roll across the high meadow like a river deciding which way to run.

Twenty-three wagons. Forty-one walkers, most of them children or elders too stubborn to ride. A herd of nine goats that had opinions about everything. And at the rear, as always, old Maren with her boundary stone strapped to a handcart, refusing every offer of wagon space because, she said, *a stone that carried us across should carry itself the last mile.*

Tamsin had stopped arguing with Maren years ago. It was easier than losing.

"You're smiling," said Sera, coming up beside them with two cups of something hot and terrible that she insisted was tea.

"I'm not."

"You are. Your mouth is doing the thing it does when you pretend you aren't pleased." She pressed one cup into their hands. "It's very annoying. I've told you this."

"The tea is also very annoying."

"Then we match." She drank hers without flinching, which remained one of the small miracles Tamsin refused to examine too closely.

Below them, the lead wagons were picking their way down into the valley through a stand of birch gone gold and copper. There was no road here — not yet. That was the point of Tamsin's presence, the reason the caravan council had voted, seasons back, to hire them formally for the spring crossing: someone who could read the land the way smugglers read it, finding the paths that weren't written anywhere, the goat-trails and game-runs and old shepherd cuts that would become, in time, the roads of a country still learning to exist.

That had been Tamsin's whole life once, before Thornfell, before Veyra, before everything: finding ways where there were none. They had thought, in those first raw months on the far side of the Door, that they'd left that work behind. Instead they'd simply found a better use for it.

"Ledger," Sera said, and held out her hand.

Tamsin dug into the satchel at their hip and passed her the book.

It was smaller now. They'd had it rebound the second winter, when the original leather finally gave out — new covers of dun-colored hide stitched by a cobbler in the river settlement, brass corners to keep the pages from warping in the damp. The prophecy itself was still in there, somewhere in the middle, ink older than memory naming an Oathbearer and a date and a Door. But around it, page after page, in Tamsin's own hand, was everything else: water sources marked by season, passes rated by difficulty and rockfall risk, the places where the wind sang through the stone in a way that spooked goats, the meadow where the blue flowers grew that could be boiled for fever-tea. Distances. Slopes. Springs that froze early.

A road ledger. The book that had once named the end of one world now mapped the beginning of another.

Sera turned pages with the reverence she never quite managed to lose, though she pretended otherwise. "You wrote up the eastern fork?"

"Last week. It's passable but ugly — scree for half a mile, then a ledge with a bad drop. Fine for goats and fools. I wouldn't take wagons."

"And the shrine site?"

"That's what we're walking toward." Tamsin nodded down-valley, to where the birch thinned and a granite shoulder rose against the sky. "There's a spring under that outcrop. Fresh water year-round, from the taste of the rock. Sheltered from the north wind. And—" they hesitated, the way they still hesitated sometimes, even now, "—it feels right. You'll see what I mean when we get there. Or you won't. But I think you will."

Sera closed the ledger and handed it back. "You've gotten good at saying that kind of thing out loud."

"I had a good teacher. Terrible liar. Very patient."

"I recall being called worse."

"You were. Frequently. By me." Tamsin drank the terrible tea. "Mostly before the part where you were right about everything."

* * *

They made the valley floor by midday, and the shrine site by late afternoon, and Tamsin watched the caravan decide, collectively and without any single decision being made, that this was where they would stop.

Wagons circled. Fires bloomed. Children scattered toward the spring with the shrieking abandon of people who had walked all day and been promised water that didn't taste of iron. Maren parked her handcart beside the granite outcrop and stood looking at it for a long time, her weathered face unreadable, and then she said, to no one in particular, "Yes. This will do."

The shrines had started appearing three years ago, and no one ever seemed to know who built the first one. Small things — a stacked-stone cairn with a hollow at its heart, a carved lintel over a spring, a niche in a cliff face swept clean and left empty except for whatever a traveler chose to leave there. A button. A feather. A strip of cloth. Once, memorably, half a honeycomb, which attracted a bear and caused a great deal of discussion about theology.

But the doors were always open. That was the rule, unwritten and absolute, wherever the little shrines appeared. No doorposts without a gap between them. No lintel without light passing underneath. Tamsin had asked Sera once, early on, whether she'd written that rule into whatever instructions she gave the builders, and Sera had looked genuinely puzzled and said no — that people just seemed to know. That some things, apparently, survived the burning of every text that named them.

Veyra had not spoken since the Door closed. Not in words, not the way Sera had heard it in the temple, not the way it had come to Tamsin in the last burning moment — *not commanding but asking*, and Tamsin answering freely, and the answer holding the threshold while thousands poured through into lands beyond all maps.

But the silence now was different from the silence before. Tamsin knew silence the way a mercenary knows terrain, all its varieties and moods, and this one was — occupied. A held breath, Sera called it. A god leaning close. Some mornings, standing watch in the grey hour before dawn, Tamsin could almost feel it: attention, vast and gentle and patient, resting on the world like sunlight on stone.

They didn't pray anymore. Neither of them did, exactly. What they did was stranger and, Tamsin suspected, truer: they built things. They opened doors. They left gaps in walls so the light could get through, and trusted that was enough.

By evening the shrine-builders had found the spring. There were four of them traveling with the caravan this season — a stonemason named Pell who had lost his family in the burning of the lowland villages and spoken perhaps forty words total in the time Tamsin had known him; his sister Annet; a former inquisitor's clerk named Doss, whose defection story was long and painful and mostly involved ledgers, of all things; and a girl of maybe sixteen, Jena, who had been born on the far side of the Door and had never seen old Thornfell at all, and who was, everyone agreed, the best stone-layer of them all.

They worked by firelight, fitting the first stones of the lintel together, and Tamsin sat a little apart with the ledger open on their knees, recording the day: distance covered, water found, grazing quality, the shrine site confirmed.

*Spring: sweet, fast-flowing, does not freeze (verify in deep winter). Site: granite shelf, south-facing, wind-sheltered. Stone available within a quarter-mile. Good ground.*

Their pen hovered. Then, beneath it, in the smaller script they used for things that were not strictly road-notes:

*Feels right. Confirmed by others. Leave door open.*

* * *

Sera came to sit with them after full dark, when the fires had burned low and the singing had started — because there was always singing now, at the day's end, songs that had crossed the Door in the memories of grandmothers and were being relearned by grandchildren who clapped when they forgot the words. Songs of Thornfell. Songs of a country that was dead and, somehow, was not.

"They voted tonight," Sera said quietly. "While you were checking the tether lines. The council."

"I know. Maren told me."

"They want you to—"

"To lead. Yes. I know." Tamsin turned the pen between their fingers. "I'm going to say no."

"I told them you would."

"Did you tell them why?"

Sera was quiet a moment. The firelight moved on her face, on the scar along her jaw that she'd stopped hiding behind her hair sometime in the second year, a defector priestess learning to be only a woman among exiles. "I told them you'd say that belonging isn't a throne. But I don't think they understood. Half of them still think you refused Aldric's crown because of humility, and the other half think it's because you're afraid."

"It's neither."

"I know it's neither."

Tamsin looked out at the dark valley, at the shape of the half-built shrine against the stars, at the open gap where its door would be. "When I was a mercenary, I had a code. Keep the contract, protect the company, don't ask questions. It kept me alive and it kept me hollow. Then the oath to Veyra — same thing, really, just holier. Do the duty, keep the faith, don't ask why the god went quiet. Every version of my life, somebody else wrote the rules and I followed them well enough to survive." They tapped the ledger. "This is the first thing I've ever written myself. Roads nobody ordered me to find. Passes nobody commanded me to open. If I take a title, it becomes theirs again. Another oath. And I—" they stopped, and laughed softly, without much humor. "I burned one oath already. It nearly killed me. I'm not eager to forge another just because it comes with a chair."

"So you'll keep walking."

"I'll keep walking. Someone has to go ahead and find out if the eastern fork is passable. Someone has to fall through the ice on the frozen lake so the next caravan doesn't. It's honest work."

"It's lonely work."

Tamsin glanced at her. "Is it? I hadn't noticed."

Sera smiled — the real one, the rare one, the one that had taken three years and a burned bridge and a broken trust mended badly and slowly to earn. "No," she said. "I suppose it isn't. Not anymore."

They sat together while the songs wound down and the fires settled into embers. Somewhere past midnight, Tamsin took out the ledger again and wrote the last line of the day's entry, and then, because it was late and they were tired and no one was watching, one more line beneath it:

*Belonging is not a place on a map. It's the walking. We are here. It counts.*

* * *

Dawn came thin and gold. Tamsin was up before it, as always, standing at the edge of camp with the ledger under their arm, reading the eastern horizon the way other people read letters from home.

The route ahead climbed out of this valley and over the shoulder of the world into country no one from the caravan had yet seen — high passes, unmapped, unnamed, waiting. Beyond them, according to a fur-trapper's secondhand tale, another river system, another range, and somewhere past that, rumor said, smoke rising from hearths that belonged to no one anyone had met. Other survivors, maybe. Other travelers. People who had come through some other Door, or no Door at all, and were building their own small countries in the empty spaces of the world.

New roads. New names to give the mountains. New shrines to build with open doors.

Behind Tamsin, the caravan was waking — the creak of wagons, the complaint of goats, Maren's voice raised in cheerful tyranny as she organized the morning's loading. Sera emerged from the tent, hair unbound, squinting into the light, and came to stand at Tamsin's shoulder.

"East fork?" she said.

"East fork," Tamsin agreed, and opened the ledger to a fresh page, and began to write.
