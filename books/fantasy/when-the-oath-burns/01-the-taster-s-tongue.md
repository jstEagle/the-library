## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The arsenic arrives at half past eight, as arsenic always does at royal banquets—in the third course, wearing its best clothes.

I knew before the cup reached my lips, which is not a boast so much as a description of my job. The wine smelled of black cherries and ambition, and underneath both, faint as a whisper in a cathedral, of bitter almonds doing their best to be polite about it. Someone in the Duke of Marrow's kitchens had decided tonight was the night for King Aldous to meet his ancestors, and had chosen white arsenic for the errand because it dissolves beautifully and leaves no color, like a good lie.

I sipped.

Not enough to kill me—I have a relationship with arsenic the way other people have relationships with an old dog that has bitten them twice. My body keeps accounts. Every poison I've ever swallowed is written somewhere in my blood, in the slow calligraphy of survived things, and arsenic and I are on familiar terms by now. We nod across the room at each other. *You again,* it says. *Me again,* I say, and we both get on with the evening.

"Delightful," I announced to the table, loud enough for the steward to hear, and set the cup down with the little click that means *this one is clean* or, more precisely, means *this one will only kill you slowly over thirty years, which we've agreed not to count.*

Three seats down, Lord Cassel relaxed his shoulders by a fraction of an inch. He'd been watching the wine. They all watch the wine eventually—the ones who poisoned it and the ones who fear it, though it takes a certain education to tell those two groups apart. I have that education. It's the only inheritance I own outright.

The king drank. The duke smiled. Somewhere in the rafters of the great hall, the musicians sawed away at a pavane as if the world were a place where people simply danced after dinner and nobody's soup required professional attention.

I ate the fourth course without tasting it. This is the trick of my profession that no one warns you about: you spend your whole life tasting, and after ten years of it, food stops being food. Everything becomes a question. *Is there hemlock in the honey? Is the salt salt?* You eat answers all day until your tongue goes hoarse, metaphorically speaking, and you find yourself at midnight chewing bread and thinking, idly, *opium, probably not, but check the poppy seed supply chain anyway.*

My name is Ada Thackeray. I am the Crown's second taster, which sounds grander than it is. There used to be four of us. There used to be nine. The profession has what you might call a high turnover rate, and the Crown replaces us the way it replaces candles: quickly, quietly, and without mourning the ones that burned out.

That suits me fine. Mourning requires being known, and being known, in my experience, is the first step toward being dead. Invisibility is safety. I have built my entire life on that principle the way other architects build on bedrock, and I have never once regretted the foundation, except on nights like this one, when the hall roars with laughter I'm not part of and I catch myself memorizing the shape of other people's happiness like a thief casing a house I can never rob.

The banquet wound down around eleven. The king retired. The duke got very drunk and told the same story about a bear three times, each time with the bear getting larger, until by the final telling it was less a bear than a weather event. I slipped out through the servants' corridor while the court was busy admiring itself, changed out of my livery into a coat the color of rain, and left the palace by the gate that doesn't exist.

Every large building in this city has one. A door that isn't on any surveyor's map, guarded by no one, noticed by everyone and seen by none. Ours opens onto Ember Lane, which is also not on any map, and which smells always of woodsmoke and old paper, no matter the season.

I walked the long way. Old habit. You don't go from a poisoning to the Guild of Impossible Things by any route a tail could predict, even when you're fairly sure no one's following, especially when you're fairly sure no one's following. Certainty is just ignorance wearing a confident coat.

Ember Lane received me the way it receives everything: indifferently, with lamplight. The guild house squats at the lane's end, narrow and tall, with windows that glow amber from inside no matter how many lamps are actually lit. From the street it looks like a candlemaker's shop that has given up. From the inside it looks like the world's most patient bureaucracy, which is exactly what it is.

Corvane was waiting in the counting room. He was always waiting in the counting room; I sometimes suspected he slept standing up among the shelves like a heron, one eye open, keeping the accounts company.

"You're late," he said, without looking up from his own ledger. His pen scratched. It always scratches; I have never once heard him write a letter that didn't sound like a small animal digging.

"The duke told the bear story again."

"Ah. The bear grows?"

"It's a whale now, effectively."

"Then you're right on time." He looked up, finally. Corvane has one of those faces that seems assembled from spare parts belonging to several different kind uncles, which I have long believed is deliberate. Kindness, in his line of work—and mine—is a tool that must be maintained daily, like a blade. "And the banquet?"

"Arsenic. Third course. The wine." I hung my coat on the peg behind the door. "Marrows' kitchens. Probably the under-steward; he had new boots and old grudges."

"Reported?"

"Discreetly. The steward will find the under-steward's resignation letter on his desk tomorrow morning. The under-steward will write it himself and won't remember deciding to. Everyone keeps their dignity, the king keeps his life, and the Marrows keep their duchy. A tidy night's work."

"And you?"

"I'm fine. Arsenic and I have an understanding."

"Mm." He said it the way he said everything, as though filing it. "There's a trade tonight. Small one. You'll want to log it personally."

This was unusual. Normally I logged trades in batches, mornings, with tea. Corvane handing me a single entry at midnight meant either a test or a courtesy, and in my experience the two were often the same thing.

* * *

The trading floor of the Guild of Impossible Things looks, at a glance, like nothing at all—a parlor, really, worn chairs, a cold hearth, a window overlooking a garden that is not planted in anything that grows. Clients see the parlor. What the parlor contains depends entirely on what the client needs it to contain, and on what they can pay.

We do not deal in gold, mostly. Gold is the currency of possible things, and possible things are cheap and everywhere. Our clients pay in what they have too much of: memories, years, names, regrets, the last words they never said, the first words they wish they hadn't. The guild records every transaction, every debt, every impossible thing bought and sold, because—and here is the first law of the trade, drilled into me at age seven before I could even read properly—*every impossible thing must be recorded or it unravels.* An unrecorded stolen hour doesn't just vanish. It frays. It takes the edges of the day with it. People wake up missing Tuesday and blaming their neighbors.

Tonight's client was already seated when I came down: a woman of perhaps sixty, dressed in good black wool gone slightly shabby at the cuffs, the way clothes get when grief outlasts fashion. She held her hat in her lap like a small animal she'd rescued.

"This is Thackeray," Corvane said. "Our registrar. Whatever you tell the ledger, the ledger keeps."

She looked at me the way clients do—searching for the monster, finding instead a tired person with ink on their fingers, and not knowing which was worse. I sat across from her and opened the night-book.

"Madam," I said. "Tell me what you want to buy."

"My husband died in the spring," she said. Her voice was steady the way roofs are steady—fine until you look too hard at the weight they're carrying. "In March. The seventeenth. A Tuesday."

"Yes."

"There was an hour. Just an hour, at the end. The physician gave him something for the pain and it made him lucid, briefly, terribly lucid, and we talked. About everything. Things we'd been not-saying for forty years." Her hands tightened on the hat. "And then the medicine wore off and the fog came back and he went, two days later, without another clear word. And I have thought, every single day since, *I wasted it. I spent our last true hour talking about the garden.*"

"You want it back," I said. "The hour."

"I want to live it properly. I know what it costs. I've brought—" she touched the collar of her dress, where, beneath the wool, I saw the shape of something worn against skin for a long time, a locket or a key, "—what your letter asked for."

Corvane had written her a price. Of course he had. The guild always knows who is carrying too much time and who is starving for it; we are vultures of a very polite sort.

I turned to a fresh page and wrote, in the guild cipher, the terms as she spoke them: *One hour, borrowed backward. Purchaser: M—— W——, widow. Price: [sealed]. Term: once lived, once only. Witness: registrar.* The cipher is beautiful work, older than the city, older maybe than the language—it looks like ordinary shorthand to anyone who shouldn't read it, which is most of the point of shorthand.

"Understand the terms," I said, because the law requires me to say it and because some stubborn scrap of me insists on saying it honestly. "You will live the hour as it happened. You cannot change what you said. Knowing what you know now, you may say different things—but he will answer as the man he was, and the hour ends where it ended, and afterward it is spent, truly spent. No one buys the same hour twice."

"I understand."

"People come back angry. They think the guild cheated them. The guild cannot cheat you of a thing that never belonged to you. An hour is not a possession, madam. It's a loan from the future, and the future always collects."

Something flickered across her face—not anger. Recognition, maybe. "How much does an hour cost, registrar?" she asked softly. "Truly. Not the sealed price. Yours."

I kept my eyes on the page. "More than anyone can afford," I said. "Which is why we accept payment in kind. May I?"

She nodded. I took her hand—cool, dry, a working woman's hand—and pressed her thumb to the ledger beside her name. The ink took it faintly, a gray ghost of a print, and settled.

"That's done," I said. "Come Thursday, at dusk. Ask at the door with no handle. Wear comfortable shoes; the hour will put you on your feet in a garden in March, and March gardens are mud."

She almost laughed. It was the first almost-laugh of her visit and possibly of her month, and I found, to my mild irritation, that it lodged somewhere under my ribs and stayed there.

At the door she paused. "Do you do this often?" she asked. "Sell hours to old women?"

"Often enough."

"Does it ever make you sad?"

I considered lying, which is my native language, and found I didn't want to speak it just then. "It makes me careful," I said. "Sadness is for people who can afford the time."

She went out into the dark of Ember Lane, and Corvane appeared beside me with the silence of a man who has practiced appearing places for sixty years.

"Well logged?" he asked.

"Well logged."

"You were kind to her."

"I was accurate to her. Accuracy reads as kindness when people expect monsters."

"Hm." He took the night-book from my hands—or rather, I handed it to him, though with Corvane the distinction is academic—and leafed through the pages with his thumb, checking my cipher the way a priest checks a confession. Then he stopped.

Stopped entirely. Thumb resting on the page, eyes moving once, twice, along a line of text.

"Thackeray," he said. His voice was perfectly level, which with Corvane is the loudest alarm there is. "Tomorrow morning, first light, you'll begin cataloguing the Hallowmere seizure. The library we took off the drowned magister. Three hundred volumes, uncatalogued, some of them dangerous. I want your eye on every page."

"All right," I said. "Why first light?"

"Because," he said, closing the book gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child, "some books should only be read by daylight. And because I suspect—" and here he smiled his assembled-from-kind-uncles smile, and did not finish the sentence, and blew out the lamp.
