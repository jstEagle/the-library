## 2. Ledgers of the Impossible

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its books the way other institutions kept their secrets: badly, in my opinion, until you looked closer and realized the mess was the security.

I came to it through a door that wasn't there.

That sounds like a lie, or a boast, or the opening line of one of those penny dreadfuls the scullery maids trade back and forth under their pillows. It is none of those things. It is simply accurate, which in my experience is rarer than any of them. There was a wall at the back of Ember Lane with a bricked-up archway that had been bricked up so long the mortar had opinions about it, and if you pressed your palm against the third stone from the left — the loose one, the one that felt faintly warm even in January, like a cat had been sleeping on it — the wall thought better of being a wall.

The trick was not to hesitate. Doors can smell hesitation the way dogs smell fear and accountants smell an unbalanced column.

So I didn't. I stepped through the cold brick-smell into lamplight, and behind me the wall resumed its career as a wall, and ahead of me Corvane was waiting with two cups of tea and the particular expression he wore when I'd been late, which was no expression at all, which was worse.

"You smell of arsenic," he said.

"Good evening to you too."

"You *smell* of arsenic, Ada."

"It was in the wine. Third course. The ambassador from Serevan apparently feels that diplomacy is best conducted through his enemies' kidneys." I took the tea because refusing Corvane's tea was a ritual violation neither of us wanted to litigate. "I logged it. Half a grain per glass, distributed across six glasses so no single death could be traced. Someone in the Serevani kitchens has been reading the old manuals."

"Or writing them." He set his own cup down untouched, which I noted, because Corvane always drank his tea, because tea was warmth and warmth was trust and trust was the one currency he never let anyone else spend. Tonight he left it steaming. "Sit down. We have accounts to settle."

* * *

The guild's back rooms were the reason I stayed, if I'm honest, and I try to be honest at least once a week, usually here where no one could overhear.

Anyone who imagined the Guild of Impossible Things as smugglers with fancy inventory would have been disappointed by the front rooms: dusty ledgers, a counter, a sign advertising NOTARY WORK AND RARE BOOKS in letters chosen specifically to bore. The Crown's censors had walked through twice that I knew of. Both times they'd left with mild headaches and a conviction that nothing interesting had ever happened on Ember Lane. Boredom, properly maintained, is the strongest lock in the world.

But the back rooms —

The back rooms went further inward than the building went outward. That's not poetry; that's surveying. I had measured. I'd paced the corridor off against my own stride the way Corvane taught me in my first year — *count everything, girl, the uncounted thing is the thing that eats you* — and the corridor held four hundred and twelve paces in a building whose exterior dimensions permitted perhaps sixty. I stopped asking how around the same time I stopped asking why, which was the year I understood that the questions themselves were dangerous, that some doors are load-bearing.

Along that impossible corridor stood the shelves.

Not bookshelves. Well — bookshelves too, but the ledgers there were only the smallest denomination of the guild's holdings. The deep shelves held stranger stock. A jar of last words, stoppered and labeled in a hand that shook. A birdcage containing what appeared to be Tuesday, though which Tuesday was anyone's guess, and the cage was padlocked with three locks for a reason. A shoe. One shoe, polished to a mirror shine, sitting alone on velvet like a relic, its card reading ONLY EVER WORN ONCE, BY SOMEONE WHO WAS NEVER BORN.

"You're staring at the shoe again," Corvane said.

"I stare at it every time. Every time I decide it's a joke someone left to test me, and every time I look at the card and feel my skin crawl right up off my arms and go wandering."

"Good. Keep staring. The day the shoe stops bothering you is the day you've become the kind of clerk who shouldn't work here." He unlocked the counting room with a key from a ring that held more keys than I had ever successfully counted, and I want you to understand that this bothered me professionally. "Tonight's trade. Walk me through it."

"A stolen hour," I said, hanging my coat. "Sold to Widow Marsh, Weaver's Row. Seller was a debt-collector out of the Low Market who'd taken the hour off a gambler as collateral. Standard chain of custody, clean provenance, no residue." I recited it the way I recited poisons: flavor, dose, effect, antidote. "She paid eleven marks and her mother's recipe for feverfew tincture."

Corvane's eyebrows did something small and disapproving. "The recipe?"

"The memory of the recipe, rather. She sold the remembering, not the paper. She keeps the paper. She says she'll relearn it." I sat across the counting table from him and pulled the night's ledger toward me. "It seemed a fair price. She cried less than most sellers do."

"And what does she buy?"

"An hour. Any hour. To spend as she likes."

"On?"

I turned the ledger around so he could read my entry, because my entries were the good ones, tight and legible, and because I'd already written the answer and saw no reason to say aloud what ink said better. *Purchaser intends to spend one (1) hour seated beside her husband's grave, in company, the hour having been taken from a morning in which the seller did nothing but wait for a debt-collector who did not come.*

Corvane read it twice. Something moved behind his face and was escorted firmly back off the premises.

"Grief is our steadiest customer," he said finally. "Remember that. Joy buys impulsively and regrets it. Fear buys badly and blames us. But grief — grief saves up. Grief reads the contract twice." He tapped the page. "This entry is why I keep you."

"I assumed it was my palate."

"Your palate pays the rent. This pays for everything else." He leaned back. "Now. Why does the hour need recording at all? Humor me. You've asked me everything else over seven years; you've never once asked me this."

Because he was right, and because he knew he was right, and because Corvane's habit of knowing things I hadn't said was the closest thing I had to a chronic illness. I considered lying, decided he'd taste the lie the way I tasted arsenic, and gave him the true answer instead.

"Because it doesn't make sense. An hour spent is an hour spent. The widow sits by the grave, the hour passes, done. Nothing to record. Unless—" I turned my cup, watching the leaves settle. "Unless the hour isn't really gone. Unless it goes somewhere. And things that go somewhere can be followed."

For the first time all evening, Corvane drank his tea.

"There's my girl," he said, and the warmth in it was real, which is the worst thing about him, then and always: the warmth was always real. "Everything must be recorded, Ada. Everything. Not because we're sentimental. Because an unrecorded impossible thing unravels, and when it unravels, it takes its thread with it — backwards through everyone who touched it." His finger traced a slow circle on the tabletop. "The gambler who sold his hour. The collector who carried it. The widow who bought it. If we don't write down exactly what happened to that hour, then somewhere in the city a man wakes up tomorrow missing a morning and cannot say why, and his wife notices he's different and cannot say how, and the difference spreads. Impossibility is a stain, child. The ledger is the frame that keeps the picture from running."

"And if the ledger burns?"

He looked at me for a long moment, and the lamplight did something unkind to the planes of his face, hollowing them, aging him a decade in a second.

"Then the picture runs," he said quietly. "Which is why the ledgers live here, behind walls that aren't walls, and why you will die before you let a flame near them. Sworn?"

"Sworn," I said, because I had sworn it at fourteen, because I had meant it at fourteen, because at fourteen the guild had been the first place in my life where being nobody was a kind of armor instead of a sentence. I was twenty-three now and I still mostly meant it. Mostly was doing a lot of quiet work in that sentence, and I noticed, and filed the noticing away the way I filed everything: carefully, dated, awaiting audit.

"Good." He rose. "Come. There's something I want to show you, since you solved tonight's lesson faster than expected. Consider it a reward."

In seven years, Corvane had rewarded me with a new coat, a set of steel pens, and permission to catalog the third shelf unsupervised. Rewards from Corvane were never objects. They were access.

I should have known. On some level, in the place where I kept the ledgers of my own unease, I think I did know.

* * *

We walked deeper.

Past the shelf of confiscated tomorrows, past the drawer where the guild kept apologies that had arrived too late to be delivered (there were nine hundred of them; I had counted twice), past the alcove where the air itself was older than the air outside, thick and slow, the way air gets in churches and sickrooms. The lamps grew fewer and the light grew more deliberate, each flame trimmed and hooded and positioned as if illumination here were a courtesy extended grudgingly.

And then the corridor ended at a door.

Doors in the guild were a language, and I was fluent enough. Most of them were plain — deal wood, iron hinges, the honest hardware of trade. A few were ostentatious, and those were traps for visitors, locked and false, built to satisfy curiosity that ought to have killed it. This one was neither. It was tall, older than the corridor around it, made of a wood I couldn't name — grey as driftwood, dense as iron, and warm. Not sun-warm. Hearth-warm. The warmth of a fire tended by someone who loved tending it.

There was no lock. There was no handle. There was only a shallow depression at chest height, shaped — I realized with the specific vertigo I have learned to dread — like a handprint.

"Where does it go?" I asked, and hated how young my voice came out.

"You tell me. What do you observe?"

I made myself be a professional. "Wood I can't identify. Warmth without mechanism. No lock, which means either it's not locked, or locking is beneath it. The threshold's worn — see the dip in the sill? Foot traffic. Centuries of it, or years of very heavy traffic. And the air coming under the door—" I paused. That was the strange part. Air was coming *under* the door, steadily, like breath, and it smelled of woodsmoke and rain on hot stone and something green, something growing, which was wrong for this deep under Ember Lane where nothing had grown since before the lane existed.

"Well?" Corvane said.

"It smells lived-in."

He smiled, and put his own hand flat on the wood, not on the print, just resting it there, the way you'd rest a hand on the shoulder of an old friend or a sleeping animal.

"This," he said, "is the oldest thing we hold. Older than the guild. Older than the Crown it hides from. The guild didn't acquire the door, Ada. The guild accreted around it, the way a pearl forms around a grain of grit — every impossible thing we broker, every hour and name and borrowed year, traces back eventually to this, or to things that leaked through from the other side of it." His voice dropped into the register he used for scripture, low and certain. "It opens onto Vael."

The word landed in me strangely. I searched my memory — a professional reflex; I kept my own interior accounts with the same rigor as the guild's — and found nothing. Not ignorance. Ignorance is a shape; you can feel the edges of what you don't know. This was smooth. A word that sounded important, and behind it, a hole.

"I don't know that name," I said slowly. "Should I?"

"No one should. That's rather the point." Corvane took out a handkerchief, unfolded it with ceremony, and revealed a map — old, soft as cloth, drawn in faded ink. He spread it on the air somehow, or the air cooperated, and I stopped questioning that sort of thing around year three. "Here's the coast of the Middle Sea. Here's Serelle. Here's the Thornmark. And between them—" his finger rested on empty parchment, on space where a country should have been, where the coastline curved in a way that suggested a bay that suggested a harbor that suggested a city, and then simply *stopped suggesting*, the cartographer's pen lifting as if the ink itself had lost its nerve— "nothing. Nothing now. But a hundred and six years ago, there was Vael. Cities, songs, kings, a language with forty words for the color of evening. And the Crown erased it. Not conquered — erased. There was a war the histories don't mention, and the losing side didn't lose so much as become unwritten. Borders redrawn. Records burned. Names struck. People woke up in villages that had always belonged to Serelle, speaking accents no one could place, mourning relatives they could no longer name. And Vael itself—" he folded the handkerchief, and the map with it, "—went *elsewhere*. Through this door. The whole country, folded up small, hidden in the one vault no army can breach."

"Which is?"

"Oblivion. Mostly. Kept alive barely. Kept *warm*" — he touched the wood again — "by a single fire. But that's a story for another year, and I've already told you more tonight than prudence approves." He stepped back from the door. "You asked where it goes. Now you know. What you'll never do is open it. Not alone, not ever, not if it knocks. Some inventories are not for clerks."

"Yes, Guildmaster," I said, which was what I said when I agreed, and also what I said when I intended to find out anyway on my own schedule, and after seven years he still hadn't learned to tell the difference, or pretended he hadn't, which amounted to the same negligence.

But as he turned to lead us back toward the lamplight and the tea and the ordinary world, I did the thing I probably shouldn't have done, the thing I have replayed ten thousand times since with the benefit of hindsight and the wish for a different pair of hands: I put my palm against the door. Not on the print. Just beside it. Just to feel the warmth.

The door breathed.

And underneath the woodsmoke and the rain-wet stone and the green growing smell, faint as a fingerprint in dust, I caught something else — a scent I knew intimately, professionally, the way a priest knows incense. Ash. Not hearth-ash. Prophecy-ash, page-ash, the ash of a book burned in a hurry by someone who wanted it forgotten.

It lasted half a heartbeat. Then it was gone, and the door was only a warm old door, and Corvane was calling my name from the end of the corridor in a voice pitched to sound casual, and I went to him, because that is what I did, that is what I had always done.

But that night, home in my narrow bed above the palace kitchens, I opened my private ledger — the one I kept for myself, the one with no guild seal — and wrote a single line in my tightest hand:

*The door remembers burning. Ask it whose.*

I fell asleep before I could decide whether that was a question I truly wanted answered, which was, I would learn soon enough, the story of my entire life: falling asleep just before the part where it would have paid to stay awake.
