## 3. A Date Written in Ash

The library had been dead for eleven days before I got to it, and it smelled like it.

Dead libraries have a particular stink — vellum going sour, glue giving up, the sweetish rot of leather covers that spent a century being touched by hands that never washed. This one had all of that plus something else, something sharper underneath, like the whole room was holding its breath around a secret. Inquisitors don't notice smells like that. They notice heresy, sedition, and unpaid taxes, in roughly that order. Smells are beneath their pay grade.

Mine too, technically. My pay grade is wine and soup and whether the Duke of Marrow's third course has been improved by anyone with a grudge and a mortar. But Corvane had asked me — asked *me*, out of every taster and ledger-keeper in the guild — to come catalogue the seized collection before the Crown's burners got to it, and you do not say no to Corvane. You especially do not say no when he says it the way he did: softly, while pouring my tea himself, which he only does when he wants something or is sorry about something, and I hadn't yet worked out which.

"Old books," he'd said. "Boring work. You'll be done in three days."

Corvane has never once in my life described anything as boring unless it was going to change everything.

So here I was, on the second day, in the confiscated library of Lord Aldous Vane — executed for treason two weeks past, though nobody could ever quite say what the treason *was*, which in my experience means the treason was real and interesting — cataloguing books for the inquisition's bonfire. Title, author, date, condition, category: harmless, heretical, or *hold for further examination*. The last category was where the interesting things went, and interesting things, in my line of work, are just poison with a different delivery method.

I'd sorted four hundred and six volumes by noon. Three hundred and ninety were sermons. Vane had apparently bought theology the way other men buy turnips — in bulk, without much thought, and letting most of it rot. Forty-odd were histories, genealogies, a treatise on canal engineering that made me briefly wish I'd been born someone whose life involved canals. Eleven were flagged *hold*. A book of maps with countries on it that I was fairly sure didn't exist. A herbal with illustrations of plants drawn from the wrong side, as if the artist had seen them in a mirror. And a slim black volume with no title at all, its spine stitched in thread that wasn't thread — I know thread; I've sewn up enough of my own wounds after tasting accidents — this was hair, or wire, or something patient.

I put it in the hold pile and told myself I'd get to it after lunch.

I want it noted, for the record I would later burn, that I hesitated. That some small animal part of me looked at that book and said *no* with its whole body. But the larger part of me — the part that keeps accounts, that believes every impossible thing must be written down or it unravels, that has spent twenty-two years being invisible and therefore safe — that part picked it up, because an uncatalogued item is a loose thread, and loose threads are how tasters die.

The cover was cold. Not cool-from-the-cellar cold. Cold like a door left open in winter, cold like something that had been waiting a long time in a place without weather. I opened it.

The first page said: *Prophecies of the Burning Year, collected and set down by the hand that survives.*

Which is nonsense, obviously. Prophecy books are a genre, like pornography for people frightened of the future. Every noble house has one in the cellar, usually forged, always vague, conveniently fulfilled after the fact. I've read dozens. They all share the same grammar: *a king shall rise, a shadow shall fall, beware the ides of whatever.* Nothing you could check until it was too late to matter.

This book was different. This book had dates.

Specific ones. Day, month, year. Names beside them. Manner of death, recorded with the flat economy of someone who does this professionally. I recognized the format immediately because it was *my* format — the terse columns, the abbreviations, the little dash used for "cause withheld pending verification." Someone had written this book the way I write ledgers. Someone who thought in entries.

I started reading the way you read any forgery: looking for the trick. The first entry was dated a hundred years ago, give or take. *Maren Ostler, chandler. Fever, three days. Confirmed.* The next. *Tomas Breck, ferryman. Drowned in shallow water. Confirmed.* On and on, decade after decade, each name with its date and its manner and that same terrible word — *confirmed* — appended in what looked like different ink, different decades, but the same hand.

A hundred years of deaths, predicted to the day. And confirmed, over and over, by whoever kept adding to the book.

Here is the thing about being a royal food-taster: you develop a very specific relationship with certainty. Everything else in my life is hedged. Is the wine poisoned? Probably not. Will the guild protect me? In theory. Does Corvane love me? He says so. But the tongue knows. The tongue doesn't hedge. Bitter at the back, almond under the bitterness, metallic on the teeth — the tongue gives you facts, and facts are the only family I've ever trusted.

So when I tell you that my body knew, before my mind caught up, that this book was real — that it was no more a forgery than arsenic is a forgery of almonds — understand that I am telling you the way the tongue tells. Flatly. Without room for argument.

I did what any sensible person does with a cursed object. I put it down. I walked to the window. I breathed.

Then I picked it back up, because I am not sensible, I am thorough, and thoroughness is just fear wearing a good coat.

The entries marched forward through the century. Deaths of chandlers and ferrymen, then of merchants, then of minor courtiers, then — I noticed with a lurch — of people whose titles I recognized from guild records. A fence. A smuggler. A woman listed only as *doorwright*. All of them names I'd seen in Corvane's master ledger, entered there as clients or debts or both. The prophecy book and the guild's accounts had been describing the same people all along, from opposite ends of time, like two writers passing a story between them without either admitting it.

And then the handwriting changed.

Not much. A slight tightening of the letters. A dash placed a hair differently. Anyone else would have missed it. But I have spent years forging Corvane's signature for receipts we couldn't afford to have traced, and I know what a hand looks like when it's tired, when it's hurried, when it's trying to disguise itself. This new hand was mine.

I mean that literally. It was my handwriting. The specific, slightly cramped script I use, the one the guild scribes tease me for, the *t* crossed with an impatient slash, the numbers with their little serif feet. There, in a hundred-year-old book, in ink gone brown with age, was my own hand writing entries about deaths that hadn't happened yet.

My mind did the thing minds do: it offered explanations, politely, like a servant offering dishes. Coincidence. A teacher whose student copied them. A family hand passed down. I accepted each dish and found it empty.

Because the dates kept coming forward. Past the book's supposed age. Past last year. Into this one.

And then I saw tonight's date.

I didn't read it so much as fall into it. The letters arranged themselves and refused to rearrange. Tonight's date — the actual one, the one the palace almanac would confirm if I ran there screaming — sat in the left-hand column in my own cramped script, and beside it, in the right, a name.

*Ada Thackeray.*

Nobody writes my name. That's the entire architecture of my life. The palace rolls list me as *the Taster, position nine*. The guild ledger lists me as *keeper, internal*. Even Corvane, drunk once on traded years and sentimentality, called me *little bird* rather than say it aloud. I have spent two decades making myself a blank space in every record, and here was a book older than my grandmother filling that space in with ink.

I read the manner of death the way you read your own diagnosis. Once fast, hoping the words would blur. Once slow, hoping they wouldn't.

*Poison, self-administered. Unwitting.*

Unwitting. As in: I would pour it myself. As in: my own mouth, my own trained, careful, professional mouth — the one instrument I have ever trusted — would be the delivery method. Every poison I've survived is logged in my body like a scar-map, every tolerance built dose by dose since I was seven years old, and the book said none of it would matter. The book said the final poisoning would come from my own hand, and I would not even know.

The vertigo hit properly then. Not fainting — I've been faint-adjacent plenty of times, it's practically a job requirement — but the other kind, the kind where the floor stays perfectly still and the *world* tilts. Because here is what I had believed, all my life, with the devotion other people give to gods: invisibility is safety. Be the blank space. Be position nine. No one poisons a ghost; no one writes a ghost's name in a book. I had built my whole self inside that belief the way a snail builds inside its shell, and the shell was a lie, and had always been a lie, because somewhere, a hundred years ago, someone had looked directly at me — at *me*, Ada Thackeray, the invisible one — and written down not just my name but my death, to the day, in my own handwriting.

Seen. All along. By the one witness I could never bribe, flatter, or survive: myself.

I sat down on the floor among Lord Vane's dead sermons and laughed, once, a horrible bark of a sound that echoed wrong in the empty shelves. Then I did the only thing that has ever steadied me. I took out my notebook — my own, the little greasy one I keep for guild accounts — and I began to copy the entry. Title, author, date, condition, category. Except this time the title was my name and the category was *hold for further examination*, and my hand shook so badly the serif feet on my numbers came out crooked.

Halfway through copying, I stopped.

Because I noticed something I should have noticed at the start, something my trained eye had skipped over in its panic, the way a tongue skips the bitter note when it's expecting sweetness. Under tonight's entry, pressed hard enough to emboss the next page, there was another line. Erased. Scratched out with a knife or a thumbnail, deliberately, by whoever wrote the book — by *me*, presumably, a century ago, with a century of practice behind my hand.

But paper remembers pressure. Any taster knows that; half our craft is reading what a cup held before it held the wine. I tilted the page against the window light, the way I tilt a glass looking for lees, and read the ghost of the erased line in the dents:

*Unless the page is burned before the hour.*

Someone had tried to warn me. Someone with my handwriting and a hundred years of regret had written my death, then scratched out the escape clause — no. Wait. Turned it around. Had written the escape clause and scratched it out, so that no casual reader — no inquisitor flipping through a seized library, no Corvane doing his soft-voiced asking — would learn that the prophecy could be beaten. Had hidden the loophole inside the sentence, the way you hide poison in a sweet course, trusting that only someone with the right training, the right tongue, the right paranoid patience, would ever find it.

Trusting *me* to find it. Leaving it where only I would look.

I closed the book. The cold came off it like breath. Outside the window, the city went on being ordinary — carts, gulls, a bell counting the quarter-hour — and I stood there doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. If the book was real, then the guild's doors went further than Corvane had ever admitted, further than *borrowed years* and *vanished countries*. They went backward. Something had come through them from a hundred years away, carrying my handwriting and my death and one buried chance to refuse it.

And if that was true, then the question stopped being *how do I die tonight* and became something worse, something that made my scarred old stomach clench around nothing:

What had I done — what would I do — in a hundred years, that was worth sending a warning across a century to stop?

The bell finished striking. Somewhere below, in the ordinary street, a cart driver swore at his horse, and the sound was so blessedly mundane I nearly wept.

I wrapped the black book in my coat, against every rule of cataloguing, against the inquisition's inventory, against sense. Some things you don't log. Some things you carry out past the guards with your heart hammering your ribs and your face arranged into position-nine blankness, and you walk toward Ember Lane as fast as dignity allows, because there is exactly one person alive who knows what the guild's doors really do.

And I needed to ask Corvane why he'd sent *me* to catalogue a dead man's library — and why, when he'd poured my tea that morning, he'd watched me drink it like a man watching a candle he expected to gutter.
