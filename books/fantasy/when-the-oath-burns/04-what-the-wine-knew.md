## 4. What the Wine Knew

I did not sleep. Sleep is a currency I've never been able to afford on nights when a book has told me the date of my death, and besides, the guild's back rooms are at their most honest at four in the morning, when the lamps burn low and the impossible things stop pretending to be ordinary.

The prophecy book sat where I'd left it, on the reading table beneath the window that looks out onto Ember Lane, which is to say it looked like any other book, because that is what dangerous books do best. *A Concordance of Dates and Departures*, the spine said, in faded gilt. Somebody had loved it once. Somebody had pressed a sprig of some flower between its pages so long ago that the flower had become a brown ghost of itself, and I found myself thinking, absurdly, about who she'd been, and whether she'd known her date too, and whether she'd read it sitting exactly here with her hands exactly as cold as mine.

Enough. Sentiment before evidence is how tasters die.

I am a taster. Evidence first.

Here is what I know about poison: it leaves signatures. Arsenic thickens the tongue's memory of sweetness. Belladonna lingers behind the teeth like a lie you can't quite swallow. The body keeps accounts even when the mind declines to, and my body has been keeping accounts since I was six years old and Corvane set the first thimble of thallium under my nose and said, gently, *learn its name*.

So I brought the same discipline to the prophecy that I bring to a suspect vintage. I laid the book open beside the guild's death registry—the great leather tome where every taster's end is recorded, because the guild records everything or it unravels—and I began, very carefully, to cross-reference.

The Concordance was not one prophecy. It was many. Page after page of names, each with a date beside it, each date followed by a manner of dying written in that cramped, patient cipher I now recognized as tasters' shorthand. And the names—

The names were ours.

Not the royal household's tasters, though there were three of those scattered through the pages, including Master Ellery, who died two years before I was born and whose portrait still hangs in the palace corridor where nobody ever looks at it. Not just theirs. Ours. Guild tasters. The invisible ones. The ones Corvane keeps off every list the Crown maintains, the ones whose deaths would be recorded nowhere except in this room.

Tamsin Vale. Date: the ninth of Frostmoon, sixty-one years past. Manner of death: hemlock, administered in water.

My finger stopped on that line and stayed there while the lamp guttered.

Because Tamsin Vale wasn't a name to me. She was a story. One of Corvane's fireside stories from when I was small and poisoned and frightened: *the finest tongue the guild ever produced*, he'd say, *and careless at the last—drank untested well-water on a country errand.* A cautionary tale. A lesson wrapped in grief.

I pulled the registry toward me and turned to the ninth of Frostmoon, sixty-one years gone.

*Tamsin Vale. Hemlock. Well-water, untested.*

I sat back. The chair creaked. Outside, Ember Lane slept its narrow, honest sleep, and somewhere above the shops a baker was already awake, because bakers are the only people more suspicious of mornings than tasters.

Coincidence, said the careful voice in my head, the one Corvane trained into me alongside the poisons. Registries record what prophecies predict. That's their whole function. Of course they match. You're finding patterns in a ledger because ledgers are nothing but patterns.

Fine. Then let's find more patterns.

Brannoc Hale—no relation, thank all the bored gods, to the inquisitor, though the coincidence of the name used to make me laugh—Guild taster, retired. The Concordance gave him a date forty years past and a manner of death: *foxglove, taken willingly, in tea.* The registry agreed to the day. Foxglove, in tea, self-administered. He'd been ill, Corvane told me once. Old wounds. He chose his own hour.

Sera Ilves. Date: twenty-two years past. Manner: *cyanide, in honey.* Registry: cyanide, in honey. Sera Ilves had kept bees. Everyone knew the story—stung one time too many, some said; her own hives turned against her, others said, though honey doesn't work that way and everyone who said it knew better but preferred the story.

Do you see what I saw yet? Because it took me another hour, and three cups of cold chicory, and one small act of treachery against my own training, before I saw it.

Every death matched. Every single one. Twenty-three names in the Concordance that belonged to the guild's registry, and twenty-three matches, dates and manners both, precise as a jeweler's scale.

And here is the pattern underneath the pattern, the one that made my hands go cold around the chicory cup:

Twenty-two of those tasters died of poisons that were never served to them.

Hemlock in well-water—but Tamsin Vale was the guild's foremost expert on water-borne toxins. She tested everything she drank. It was her famous virtue, the other half of the cautionary tale. Brannoc Hale took foxglove in tea *willingly*, the registry says, which means someone handed him a cup and told him what was in it, which means no taster alive or dead would call that a poisoning. Sera Ilves's hives—cyanide doesn't come from bees. Cyanide comes from almond bitterness, from certain stones crushed fine, from a dozen sources, none of them winged. Someone had put it in her honey. Someone who knew she'd never test her own harvest, because trust begins at home, and that is precisely where we are taught to stop looking.

Poison never served. Poison that arrived by a door no taster guards: the well, the gift, the garden, the home.

I went back through the Concordance with new eyes, and the cipher confirmed it. Every predicted death, for a hundred years, had come true—not because the book foretold them, but because the book *described a method*. It said *hemlock in water*, and eventually, somewhere in the wide world, hemlock found its way into water near Tamsin Vale. Prophecy as instruction. Prophecy as recipe. The book didn't predict our deaths.

It scheduled them.

And tonight's date sat in that book beside my name, and the manner of death was written in tasters' shorthand in a hand a century old, and I understood—with the particular clarity that comes at four in the morning when the lamps are low—that whoever wrote that page had not merely seen my death.

They had ordered it.

* * *

Corvane found me at dawn. He always knows. Thirty years of raising me and I still don't know whether it's a sixth sense or someone watching the lane, and I have recently begun to wonder whether those are different things.

He came down the back stairs with two cups, which is our ritual, which has been our ritual since I was seven: morning tea, tasted first by him, then by me, then drunk by us both. A little ceremony of mutual trust performed daily between a man who buys children and a child who never asked to be bought. I used to find it sweet. Sitting there with twenty-three dead colleagues arranged on the table in front of me, I found myself tasting the tea and wondering—not for the first time, but for the first time *seriously*—whether the ritual was for my benefit or his.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"I worked."

"I can see that." He set his cup down and looked at the spread of books, and his face did something complicated and quick, the way a pond does when a fish turns under the surface. "The Concordance."

"You know it."

"I know of it." He lowered himself into the chair across from me, unhurried, a big man grown comfortable in his own weight, silver threading his beard the way frost threads a window. "Seized library stock. Catalogued?"

"Cross-referenced."

"Ada." My name in his mouth is always gentle. That's the trap of it. "What did you find?"

I could have lied. I want that recorded somewhere, wherever such things get recorded: I considered lying, and the lie was right there, ready, fluent, cheap. *Nothing. Numerology. A mad monk's datebook.* But he'd taught me the poisons and he'd taught me the ledgers and he'd taught me that a lie discovered costs ten times a truth confessed, and anyway—

Anyway, some part of me wanted him to explain it away. Wanted him to laugh and say the cipher was coincidence and the registry was public and the whole thing was dust and moonlight. I wanted my family back for one more morning before the world ended.

So I showed him.

I walked him through it the way I'd walk a novice through a tasting: the twenty-three matches, the precision of the dates, the method hidden inside each prediction, the poisons that arrived by doors no taster guards. I watched his face the whole time, because faces are texts too, and Corvane's face read like a ledger with a page torn out. There was recognition. There was calculation. And there was something else, something older and sadder, that surfaced once, briefly, when I said Sera Ilves's name—a flinch, quickly filed away.

He'd known her. Of course he'd known her. He'd known all of them. He'd raised some of them, the way he raised me.

"And my page?" I said. "Tonight's date."

"Is a hundred years old," Corvane said, "written in a cipher anyone with your training could forge, in a book of unverifiable provenance, catalogued among six hundred volumes of seized nonsense." He picked up his tea. "You've had a bad night and an active imagination. Both are occupational hazards."

"The registry matches. All twenty-three."

"The registry matches because a clever person read the registry first and wrote the book second." He said it kindly. That was the worst part. He said it the way you tell a child the dog has gone to live on a farm. "Forgers always work backward from fact, Ada. It's the first law of fraud. Whoever made this wanted the guild panicked, and here you are, awake at dawn, panicking beautifully."

It was a good answer. It was a *correct*-sounding answer. It explained everything except the thing I couldn't say aloud yet, the thing that sat under my ribs like a swallowed coal: if the book was forged backward from the registry, then why did the registry contain deaths by poison *never served*? Why did the methods fit the victims' blind spots like keys cut to locks? Why did a hundred-year-old book know that Sera Ilves trusted her own honey?

But I didn't say it. Because he was Corvane, and I was his, and loyalty is a habit like any other, and habits taste of nothing at all until suddenly they taste of arsenic.

"What do you want to do with the volume?" he asked.

"It should be studied. The cipher, the provenance—"

"It should be burned."

The words dropped into the room and lay there.

"Burned," I repeated.

"A book that predicts the deaths of every taster in this guild is not a mystery, Ada. It's a weapon. In the wrong hands—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the street, toward the palace, toward the gray spire where Inquisitor Hale kept whatever passed for his heart, "—it's a list. A kill-list with our names pre-checked. We don't study weapons aimed at us. We destroy them." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, mostly. "Besides. Your page says you die tonight of poison never served. Would you rather keep it on the shelf, checking the clock?"

I laughed. I actually laughed, because it was a good joke, the kind of joke you make to take the edge off fear, and he'd made a thousand of them over thirty years, and that's what family does.

Then he held out his hand for the book, and I gave it to him, and I watched him carry it to the hearth where the night's coals still glowed, and I watched him feed it in, page by page, pressing each one flat with the poker so the flames could reach the ink, and he did it thoroughly and without hurry, the way he did everything, and the fire ate a century of scheduled deaths in about four minutes.

My page went last. I watched my own name curl and blacken, the date beside it blooming orange at the edges, and I felt—nothing. Relief, maybe. Or its twin.

"There," Corvane said softly. "Now no one owns your ending but you." He touched my shoulder, brief and warm, the exact weight of touch he'd used since I was six years old and shaking after my first dose of thallium. "Get some sleep. Eat breakfast. Taste everything twice today, just to spite the book."

He went up the stairs. I heard his door close.

I sat in the ash-smelling room for a long time, looking at the hearth.

Here is the thing about being a taster: you learn that the most dangerous poison is the one you've already swallowed without knowing. Symptoms arrive late. By the time your tongue tells you, it's in the blood, it's in the bone, it's writing itself into you.

And here is what my tongue was telling me now, late, too late, in a voice I barely recognized as my own:

Corvane hadn't hesitated. Not for one breath. A book that might have been fraud, might have been madness, might have been the single greatest threat in the guild's history—he'd burned it within minutes, without sending for the archivist, without consulting the council of seniors, without doing any of the thousand careful things he does with everything else we keep. He'd burned it the way you burn a letter you wrote yourself.

And the Concordance had listed twenty-three guild tasters across a century, names the Crown doesn't have, names that exist in exactly two places: the registry in this room, and the mind of one man.

One man, who had just burned the only other copy of the list.

I raked the hearth with my fingers, careless of the heat, the way I haven't been careless of anything since I was six years old. Ash, ash, charred spine-gilt. And there, at the back of the grate, wedged where the poker couldn't reach: a scrap the size of a coin, half-burned, one corner surviving.

Tasters' shorthand. Four words in that cramped, patient cipher.

*I sent it back.*

I sat on the hearthstones with the scrap in my palm until the sun cleared the rooftops of Ember Lane and knocked on the shutters, and downstairs the shop-bells began their morning argument, and upstairs Corvane's floorboards creaked as he dressed for the day, unhurried, a man with nothing to hide.

Tonight's date sat in my chest where the burned page used to be.

And for the first time in my life, I caught myself rehearsing what I'd say to an inquisitor.
