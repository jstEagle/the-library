## 5. The Inquisitor's Invitation

I have interrogated forty-one people in my career, and I will tell you the truth that cost me a promotion: most of them want to confess.

They sit in the hard chair and they squirm and they sweat, and underneath all their careful lies there is a small exhausted voice saying *finally, someone asked*. Guilt is a debt, and debts want to be paid. I built my reputation on knowing this. I built my ledger on it too, though that part no one knows, because a man who keeps accounts of other men's secrets cannot afford to be an account himself.

The food-taster was the forty-second person to sit in my chair, and she did not want to confess anything at all.

* * *

The summons went out the morning after the banquet, delivered by a boy who didn't know what he carried, which is how I prefer my messages to travel. *Routine questioning of household staff following the incident at His Majesty's table.* Routine. There is nothing routine about a taster surviving arsenic in her wine, but "routine" is a word with soft edges, and soft edges get doors opened.

I read her file while I waited. Thin file. That was the first interesting thing about it. Ten years in royal service, three palaces, two changes of name — the second one unregistered, done quietly through some clerk who owed somebody a favor — and nothing else. No debts. No lovers. No letters home. A person who has been alive for thirty-odd years should leave more tracks than that. A person with no tracks is either dead or very good at walking.

Ada Thackeray. The name sat on the page like a coin on a table: something you could pick up and turn over and find blank on both sides.

She arrived at mid-morning, escorted by one of my clerks, and I made a point of not standing when she entered. Let her know where the power sat. She took the chair without being offered it, folded her hands in her lap, and looked at me with the mild patience of a woman waiting for a physician to finish writing a prescription she already knew the contents of.

"Master Thackeray," I said. "Thank you for coming."

"You sent four guards," she said. "It wasn't really an invitation."

"It was an invitation with a dress code."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, filing paperwork.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"The wine." She said it simply, as if reporting weather. "Arsenic, cut with something to slow its working. I tasted it in the third swallow. I drank enough to confirm and then I was sick on purpose, loudly, near the king's cousin, so the alarm would spread before anyone else poured." She paused. "You'll want to know why I didn't just announce it. Because announcements cause panic, and panic causes drinking. People drink when they're frightened. It's a terrible habit of ours."

I wrote nothing down. This is a trick of mine; the pen staying still makes people nervous, and nervous people elaborate. She didn't elaborate. She sat there with her folded hands and her ghost-smile and let the silence do what silences do.

Most people fill silence. She treated it like a courtesy extended to her, and declined to waste it.

"The poisoner was caught," I said finally. "A cellarman. He confessed."

"He confessed to the wrong thing," she said.

That got my attention, though I kept my face the way I keep everything: boring. "Explain."

"The cellarman is guilty of selling wine out the back gate for six years. He's guilty of watering the king's claret. He is not guilty of arsenic. Arsenic doesn't come from a cellar, Inquisitor. It comes from a glassblower's shop, or an apothecary, or a jeweler who cleans settings with it. Someone brought it to that banquet already dissolved." She tilted her head. "You know this. You caught him anyway, because a caught wrong man closes a case faster than an open right one, and open cases make ministers write letters, and letters make everyone miserable."

It was, roughly, my own assessment of the matter, delivered back to me by a woman whose official job description was *dies professionally if needed*. I felt something I rarely feel in that room: the sensation of looking into a mirror angled slightly wrong.

"And your theory," I said, "is that the true poisoner walks free."

"My theory," said Ada Thackeray, "is that I'm a taster, not an investigator, and theories are above my station. I taste. I report. I survive." Her hands stayed folded. "You summoned me to ask whether I poisoned the wine myself and survived my own poison, which would be clever, or whether I failed to warn the table sooner, which would be negligent, or whether I am simply very good at my work, which would be suspicious. Ask whichever one you actually mean."

For a moment — a long moment, long enough that the clerk by the door shifted his weight — neither of us spoke.

Then I laughed. Short, involuntary, and immediately regretted, because laughter in an interrogation room is a concession, and I had just handed her one.

"You've done this before," I said.

"I've been questioned before. There's a difference between being questioned and being interrogated. You're doing the second thing badly on purpose, hoping I'll relax into the first thing." She unfolded her hands, refolded them differently. A fidget, I thought, then corrected myself: a demonstration. *See? I show you my nerves. Therefore I have none.* "Ask your real questions, Inquisitor. It's nearly noon, and I have a palate to protect."

* * *

So I asked them. Not the ones in the file — those were theater, and we both knew it — but the ones I'd been carrying since the banquet, since before the banquet, since the night three years ago when a widow in Ember Lane paid off a debt she couldn't possibly have paid and described, drunk and grieving, a door where her front door should have been.

Here is what the Crown knows about the Guild of Impossible Things, officially: nothing. Here is what I know, unofficially, in a ledger bound in grey leather that lives behind a loose brick in my own hearth: eleven entries. A stolen hour. A borrowed year, returned late, with interest. Two names sold before their bearers had earned them. And always, always, the same faint signature at the bottom of the chain of custody — not a guild mark exactly, more a habit of bookkeeping. Impossible things, recorded. Debts, tracked. Whatever traded in doors and years and names, whatever had handed a childless widow one hour with her drowned son, it *kept accounts*.

And I am a man who believes that anything which keeps accounts can be audited.

"Where were you born?" I asked.

"Records say Coldharbour parish."

"And where does Ada Thackeray say?"

Silence. Not a flinch, not a dart of the eyes — I watched for both. Just silence, clean as swept stone, and behind it something almost like pity. "Records are often wrong about orphans," she said. "We're assigned beginnings the way houses are assigned numbers. It saves argument."

"You were bought. At age seven. By the palace, from a broker. Do you remember the broker?"

"No."

Lie. First clean lie of the interview, delivered with the same serenity as her truths, and I noted it in the place where my ledger lives, which is to say behind my ribs. She remembered. She remembered everything; you could see it in the way she held still, the stillness of a woman keeping a full cup level.

"What do you remember about that day?"

"Bread," she said, after a moment. "There was bread on the broker's table, and I remember thinking that if I was being sold, at least I'd been fed first. Children keep strange accounts, Inquisitor. We record what comforts us and lose the rest." Her eyes came up to mine. "Is that the question, or are you circling?"

"Circling," I admitted. "Habit."

"Hunting dogs circle too."

"Yes," I said. "They do."

I put the file down. I took a risk, and I want it recorded — in some ledger, somewhere, even if only this one — that I took it knowingly. I opened the drawer of my desk, took out a sheet of paper, and slid it across to her. On it, in my own hand, was a list. Dates. Nine dates over sixty years, and beside each date, a name and a title: *royal taster. royal taster. royal taster.*

Every taster who had died in service in the last six decades. Every one of them dead within a year of the date written next to their name — and here was the part that lived in my grey book and nowhere official — every one of them dead of a poison that had never appeared in any kitchen, any cellar, any glass. Poison never served. Deaths that matched no inventory anywhere in the kingdom. I had spent two years assembling that list from parish records and mortuary rolls, quietly, on my own time, and it was the single maddest document in my possession, and I had just shown it to suspect number one.

Ada Thackeray looked at the list for a long time.

When she looked up, everything had changed and nothing visible had changed. Her face was the same. Her hands were the same. But the quality of her attention altered the way a room alters when a window opens — the same room, suddenly with air in it.

"Where did you get this?" she asked, and her voice had dropped its performance entirely. Underneath it was something younger and much more afraid.

"Parish rolls. Mortuary records. The kind of reading nobody does because it's tedious." I leaned back. "Nine tasters in sixty years. All dead on schedule. All dead of poisons that don't match any purchase, theft, or stock in this kingdom. Tell me, Master Thackeray, as a professional: what kind of killer poisons a victim with a substance he never buys?"

She was quiet long enough that the clerk coughed. I waved him out; the door clicked shut.

"There are two answers," she said at last, carefully, each word set down like a foot on ice. "One is that your list is coincidence, and lists of nine coincidences exist only in ledgers, which is to say nowhere."

"And the second?"

"The second answer gets people killed." She pushed the paper back across the desk, aligned precisely to its original angle, which told me more than anything she'd said. "Inquisitor. May I ask you a question instead?"

"You may try."

"Why show me this? You suspect me. A smart man shows his evidence to no one, least of all a suspect." Her eyes were steady on mine. "Unless you wanted to see what I'd do. Unless you needed to know if I'd seen a list like this before. Which means somewhere, in whatever you keep that isn't in these files—" her gaze flicked, briefly, to my chest, to the space behind the ribs where a grey leather book might hide, "—you have an account of things that don't officially happen. And you showed it to me because you think I'm part of them. And you need to know which side of the ledger I'm on."

The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere below us, the city went about its honest dishonest morning.

"Two careful liars," I said, "in a room with one door."

"One door," she agreed softly, "that you believe opens onto more rooms than it should."

We regarded each other then, the two of us, across a desk covered in dead tasters' dates, and I understood that we had passed some threshold that had nothing to do with interrogation. She knew I kept a hidden book. I knew she knew. Neither of us would ever say so again in plain words. But a recognition had occurred, the way two pickpockets recognize each other in a crowd — not friendship, not trust, merely the mutual, wary respect of professionals who work the same street.

"Go home, Master Thackeray," I said, rising. "Drink nothing. Trust no deliveries. If anyone offers you a bargain that sounds impossible—"

"—I'll be sure to check the interest rate," she said, and for real this time, brief as a struck match, she smiled. Then it was gone, banked and hidden, and she was the mild taster again, thanking me for my time, apologizing for the mess at the banquet, promising to remain available to the Crown's inquiries.

At the door she stopped. She didn't turn around.

"Inquisitor. Your list. The ninth date on it — the last one. Whose is it?"

I had wondered whether she'd notice. I had hoped she wouldn't.

"Read it again tonight," I said. "Look at the final entry."

She left. I stood at the window and watched her cross the courtyard below, small and unhurried, a woman with no tracks, and I thought about the tenth line I had not yet inked into the grey book — the line I'd drafted two nights ago, after the banquet, after a taster sipped arsenic and lived:

*Tenth entry. Name: Thackeray, Ada. Date of projected death: [pending]. Manner of death: unknown. Note: subject survived a poison she was prophesied —*

No. Not prophesied. I don't deal in prophecy; I deal in records. But the fact remained, and the fact was this: three weeks ago, cataloguing seized materials from a burned house on Vellum Row, my clerks had logged a century-old book of verse and prognostication, valueless, water-damaged, destined for the furnace. And I had skimmed it, because I skim everything, and on a page near the end I had seen a date. A specific date, written in ash-brown ink a hundred years ago.

The date was ten days from now. Beside it, in the old book's cramped cipher, was a title: *the taster.*

I had told myself it was coincidence. I am a man who audits accounts; I know what coincidence weighs, and it weighed nothing.

Tomorrow at dawn, I decided, watching her disappear through the gate, I would go to Ember Lane. The widow. The stolen hour. Follow the debt to the door.

Behind my ribs, I turned to a fresh page, and began to write.
