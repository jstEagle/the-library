## 6. Doors That Remember

There is a rule among tasters: never eat anything you can't name. I broke it the night I was born, presumably, since no one has ever successfully named me, and I've spent my whole life since breaking it in smaller ways. The smallest way yet came three nights after I read my own death in a century-old book, when I stood in front of the Vael door and told myself I was only going to look.

The door lives at the end of the guild's longest corridor, behind a curtain of grey velvet gone soft as moth-wing from a hundred years of hands drawing it aside and letting it fall again, disappointed. Most of the guild's doors are showy things — gilded frames, hinges that sing, brass plates with the trade price engraved in script so ornate you need a second door just to read it. The Vael door has none of that. It's oak gone black with age, unvarnished, with an iron ring handle worn smooth on one side where everyone touches it and rough everywhere else, like the door flinched.

Corvane had said, more than once, *don't open it alone.* Corvane had also, more than once, ordered a page of prophecy burned in his own hearth while looking me in the eye and telling me it was for my protection, and I had learned what those two sentences meant together.

I went at the hour between third bell and fourth, when the guild's accountants sleep sitting up at their desks and even the doors doze. I took a lamp I did not light. There was light enough coming through the keyhole — a thin gold line, steady, the color of the last inch of a candle's life. Not lamplight. Lamplight flickers. This burned the way something burns when it has been burning a very long time and intends to keep doing it out of spite.

*You are only going to look,* I told myself again, because I have found that lies survive best with repetition. Tasters know this. We tell ourselves *the king likes this vintage* and *the chef would never* until the words wear smooth as the iron ring.

The handle was warm.

Doors aren't supposed to be warm. Doors are supposed to be indifferent — that's their entire profession. But this one gave heat into my palm like a hand held too long over a flame, not painful, not yet, the kind of warmth that says *I could become pain if you insist.*

I insisted.

* * *

The city on the other side was dying the way embers die: slowly, beautifully, and with full knowledge of what was coming.

I stepped out onto a street paved in stone the color of dried blood, and the air hit me first — warm, resinous, faintly sweet, like the inside of a bakery that has also caught fire. Overhead there should have been sky. There was instead a ceiling of dusk that never deepened and never lifted, pinned at the exact moment between sunset and dark, and hanging in it, far off at the center of everything, a single point of gold light.

The dragon-fire. Even before anyone told me its name I knew it, the way you know your own heartbeat — not by observing it but because it's the thing all other observations arrange themselves around. Every street ran toward it or away from it. Every roof angled to catch it. The whole city was built like a cupped hand around one small flame, and the hand was closing.

The buildings were beautiful in the way of things that have stopped being maintained and started being mourned. Tall houses with shuttered windows painted in colors that must once have been loud — saffron, madder-red, a green like pond water — all of them faded now to the shades of old bruises. Banners hung from balconies, sun-bleached to ghosts of whatever animals or kings they'd shown. And everywhere, the quiet. Not empty-quiet. *Held*-quiet. The quiet of a room where someone is sleeping and everyone else has agreed, without saying it, to whisper forever.

"Mind the step," said a voice near my ankle.

I looked down. A child sat on a doorstep, maybe eight years old, braiding straw into rings. She didn't look up. Her braid-fingers moved with the terrible fluency of someone who has done the same thing ten thousand times, which is a strange thing to see in someone so young, unless time works differently here — which, I would learn, is the entire problem.

"The step," she repeated, patient, "it remembers people who trip."

I stepped over it. It seemed wise to stay on good terms with local architecture. "Thank you," I said.

"You talk funny," she said, still not looking up. "Everyone talks funny at first." Then, finally, she raised her face, and I saw her eyes were the same gold as the fire on the horizon, both pupils lit from within like lamps behind paper. "You're new," she said. "But you're not new. That's odd."

"I get that a lot," I said, which was true, though usually from wine merchants reacting to my palate.

She went back to her braiding. I stood there feeling like a question that had wandered out of its book, and then, because standing still in a dying city felt like agreeing to become part of the scenery, I walked toward the fire.

* * *

The streets taught me things as I went, the way streets do if you're trained to read them. I read kitchens for kings; reading streets is easier, they don't lie about their ingredients.

This city had been rich once, and recently. Doorways still bore the marks of thresholds removed — Vael, I'd later understand, had no use for thresholds, a country that considered a door an invitation rather than a defense. Market squares appeared every few blocks, each with its fountain running dry or nearly, each fountain carved in the shape of a dragon curled nose-to-tail, and every dragon's mouth had once spouted water and now dripped, dripped, dripped into basins furred green with mineral bloom. Counting the fountains told me the population had been counted once, generously, and then counted again, less generously, and then not counted at all.

And the people. The people were the part I wasn't prepared for.

They were everywhere and nowhere. A man repainting a shutter that was already painted, stroke after stroke of red over red, contented as a monk. Two women playing a board game in a doorway, moving the same pieces back and forth across the same six squares, laughing at moves I suspect they'd made a thousand times before. An old man walking a dog that was more memory than dog, its edges soft, its tail wagging at a rhythm slightly out of sync with itself. Everyone busy. Nobody going anywhere.

They couldn't leave. I understood that within an hour, without being told, the way you understand the rules of a house you've been invited into. There were roads leading out of the city — proper roads, cobbled, signposted in a script I almost recognized, letters shaped like flames and fishhooks — and the roads simply stopped mattering a few miles out. Not blocked. *Uninteresting.* A person could walk them, I was sure. They'd just walk until the road ended and find themselves walking back into town without ever having decided to turn around, and somewhere on that walk they'd set down the reason they'd left, the way you set down a heavy basket, and by the time they got home they wouldn't remember having carried anything at all.

That was the price of the door. I knew it the way the child knew her straw: the erased country kept its people the way a ledger keeps debts. You could stay and remain yourself. You could leave and forget yourself. There was no third column.

A woman watering flowers that were already watered smiled at me as I passed. "Evening," she said. "Lovely evening." It was the same evening it had been for a hundred years, presumably, but I found I couldn't begrudge her the compliment. When every day is the same day, politeness becomes a form of courage.

The fire grew as I walked, and so did the wrongness in my chest, which I want to describe carefully because I've carried poisons my whole life and I know the difference between illness and recognition.

It started as warmth under my sternum. Not the door's borrowed warmth. Mine. It rose in me the way certain smells rise in a kitchen and grab you by the throat before your head knows why — bread baking in a house you've never entered, and suddenly you're crying and can't say for whom. My eyes stung. My step quickened without permission. Twice I stopped in the street and pressed my hand flat against my own chest like a woman checking whether she'd left the stove on, and twice the warmth pushed back against my palm, insistent, *familiar*, and I thought, with the specific vertigo of the prophecy page rising fresh in my memory:

*I have been homesick my whole life and never known for where.*

Which is a stupid sentence. Which is a sentence that belongs in the kind of book the Crown burns people for owning. And yet.

* * *

The fire lived in a plaza at the city's heart, in a bowl of black iron big as a mill wheel, mounted on a pillar of the same blood-colored stone as the streets. Around it, benches. Around the benches, people — dozens, maybe a hundred, all facing it, none of them speaking, in the posture of an audience waiting for a play that has already begun and may have been running for a century.

Up close, the fire was small. That was the horror of it. From the far streets it looked like a sun; from the plaza it was perhaps the size of a cooking flame, a tongue of gold lying low in the iron, and its light reached impossibly far anyway, bending around corners, sliding under doors, generous past all sense. It guttered as I watched. Rose. Guttered again. Each dip lasted a heartbeat longer than the last. I found I was counting, because counting is what I do when I'm frightened — counts of ten between the flame's rise and fall, poisoners' arithmetic, and the intervals were lengthening like a slowing pulse.

I know what a dying thing looks like. I've watched kings age. This was that, in fire form.

"You shouldn't count it," said a voice behind me. "It hates being counted."

I turned. An old woman sat on the nearest bench, wrapped in a shawl the faded saffron of the shutters, hands folded on a cane of twisted wood. She was tiny and vast at the same time, the way really old people manage, taking up no space and all of it. Her eyes were the gold of the child's, of the fire, of every lamp in every window I'd passed, and they were fixed on me with an attention that made my skin prickle the way it does when someone stands behind you in a crowd.

"It doesn't hate being counted," I said. "Nothing hates being counted. Things hate being *miscounted.*"

Something moved through her face — surprise, then delight, then a grief so sudden and total it looked like anger. She gripped her cane and stood, and stood, and kept standing, coming up and up like a banner unfurling, until she was taller than me, and her free hand rose and hovered an inch from my cheek without touching, trembling.

"Serevan," she said.

The word went through me like a fishhook through water. Not a word — a *name*. I knew that instantly, the way I knew the fire. It landed in some chamber of me that had been built to receive it and had stood empty my whole life, dust-free, waiting.

"That's not my name," I said. My voice came out wrong. Small. "My name is Ada."

"Serevan," she said again, softer, tasting it, a cook checking a seasoning she hasn't used in years. "Little ash-heart. You came back."

"I've never been here." I was backing up a step, two. The plaza tilted gently, the way a ship's deck tilts. "I'm a food-taster. I work on Ember Lane. I was born—" and here the sentence failed me, because I had never actually finished it in my life. Born *where*? To *whom*? Corvane's records said *acquired, age four, provenance unremarkable,* and I had always assumed provenance unremarkable was simply what orphans were made of. "I was born somewhere," I finished, uselessly.

"You were born *here,*" the old woman said, and her voice cracked down the middle like the fountain-dragons' voices must once have done. "In the house on the Ash Steps, in the year the maps burned. Your mother carried you out through the market with the soldiers coming and the fire already failing, and she gave you to the door because the door was the only thing that would hold you, and she stayed because someone had to feed the flame, and—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter, in the tone of a woman who has rehearsed a speech for a hundred years and is furious to be performing it badly. "She said you'd come back grown. She said the fire would call you home before it called you anywhere else."

Around us, the seated audience had begun to turn. One by one, faces lifted toward me — dozens of gold-lit eyes, the child from the doorstep now perched on the plaza's rim, the painter with his red brush dripping, the dog-memory whining at a frequency below sound. None of them spoke. Their attention was a weight, warm, enormous, and beneath it the fire in the iron bowl guttered hard, dipped, threw shadows, recovered — and in the instant of its recovery every face brightened, and every mouth formed the same silent syllable.

*Serevan.*

"No," I said, and heard how much it sounded like begging. "No, you've made a mistake. I'm nobody. That's the whole — that's the *job.* I taste poison for a living. I'm invisible on purpose. Invisible people don't have countries."

"Invisible," the old woman said, wonderingly, as if I'd confessed to being made of glass. "Oh, little one. You weren't hidden so no one would see you." She reached out, and this time her fingers touched my cheek, dry and light as ash, and the warmth under my sternum surged to meet them like a dog hearing its name. "You were hidden so *they* wouldn't. There's a difference, and it's the whole history of the world."

The fire guttered again. Longer this time. I counted without wanting to: a count of thirty, then forty, the darkness in the plaza stretching like a held breath before the gold dragged itself back up. And in that darkness, for half a heartbeat, I saw the audience clearly — not as they presented themselves, but thinner, translucent, people-shaped gaps in the world, held together by nothing but the light they were watching.

If the fire went out, I understood, they wouldn't die. Dying is something. They would *unravel* — the guildmaster's own word, from my lessons, applied at last to its original subject. Every impossible thing must be recorded or it unravels. These people had been recorded by nothing but one small flame for a hundred years, and the flame was losing the argument.

"Your mother fed it," the old woman said, following my eyes. "With years, at the end. With all of hers. It's why you were sent through — nothing feeds a fire like a life freely given, and hers bought you time. A century of it." Her thumb traced my cheekbone, once, like reading a line of text. "We've spent it. Can't you feel how we've spent it?"

I could. I had been counting it since the doorstep.

"And now the prophecy says you die in the spring," she said — gently, horribly, the way you'd tell someone their tea is ready — "and the fire dies with you, unless—"

"Unless what?" I grabbed her wrist. Her bones were small as a bird's under my fingers. "Unless what?"

Her expression did something I recognized, because I'd seen it in a mirror my whole life: the look of a careful liar deciding how much truth the listener can survive.

"Come inside," she said, nodding toward a house at the plaza's edge, saffron-shuttered, its windows gold-lit. "It's a long story, little ash-heart, and I've only waited a hundred years to tell it. Your mother's name was Mereth. And the first thing you should know—" she leaned close, conspiratorial, as if the dusk-ceiling itself might be eavesdropping, "—is that the book you found? The one with your death written in it?

"She didn't write that ending either."
