## 7. Corvane's Kindness

I did not sleep.

I want that on the record somewhere, in some ledger even I can't burn: on the night after I read my own death in a century-old book, Ada Thackeray lay on their narrow bed above Ember Lane with a candle guttering and their heart doing arithmetic it didn't know how to finish, and they did not sleep.

The prophecy page sat under my mattress like a second spine. *Thirteenth of Emberfall. The taster dies of poison never served.* I had turned the words over so many times they'd worn smooth as river stones, and still they refused to mean anything kinder. Poison never served. Not the wine at the banquet — that arsenic I'd tasted and survived, logged and forgotten. Something else. Something waiting.

Which meant someone was writing my death down ahead of me, and doing it with better penmanship than grief usually manages.

When the knock came at my door — three soft raps, the rhythm Corvane had used since I was six years old and afraid of thunder — I knew before I opened it that he knew. That's the thing about being raised by Guildmaster Corvane: you learn to read knocks like vintages. This one said *we need to talk, and I have already decided how the talk will go.*

"Ada." He stood in the corridor with a lamp in one hand and two cups in the other, steam curling off them, and he looked — there is no honest way to say this — he looked like a father. Silver at his temples, worry at his eyes, chamomile because he knew valerian made me dream of drowning. Forty years of small kindnesses distilled into one tray of tea at an indecent hour.

"You read it," he said.

"I read it."

"Then we should sit down."

* * *

His study at that hour looked like a man pretending to be modest. One desk, worn oak. Two chairs. Shelves of ledgers bound in grey leather, each spine unlettered, because a guild that officially did not exist could hardly letter its books. But behind the false panel by the hearth — I'd known about the panel since I was nine, another thing he thought he'd hidden and I'd catalogued — behind that panel were the real accounts, the ones kept in ink that only wrote true, and I felt their weight through the wall like a fever behind a forehead.

He poured the tea himself. He always poured the tea himself. I used to think it was humility. A man who ran a market in stolen hours and borrowed years, pouring chamomile for a frightened child, because power that cannot be gentle is just violence wearing a coat.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Corvane said, "and you're going to hate me for parts of it, and I'd ask you to hold your hatred until the end, because the end is the part where I explain why every cruel piece was also a kind one."

"That's not how stories work."

"No," he agreed, and something old crossed his face, something tired. "It isn't. But it's how mine goes."

So I sat, and I drank tea I no longer trusted — first time in my life, that; my tongue checked the cup out of pure reflex and found nothing but chamomile and honey, and still I hesitated, and *that* hesitation hurt worse than any poison ever had — and he told me.

* * *

"A hundred and four years ago," he began, "a woman walked into the guild carrying a book she couldn't read and a debt she couldn't pay. She wanted to buy passage back to Vael. She was Vaelish, you understand — one of the scattered, born after the erasure, raised on songs for a country that no longer appeared on maps. Her grandmother remembered it. She wanted her daughter to see it before the last door thinned too far to open."

"The door was already failing?"

"Failing. Doors to erased places are held open by belief, mostly, and dragon-fire where you can get it. Belief was thinning. Fewer people remembered Vael each year; fewer memories meant less door. We quoted her honestly. More than she had. So she offered collateral instead: the book. Said it came from her grandmother, who took it off a dying seer during the burning of the maps. Said the seer claimed it was written backward — that every word in it had already happened, or would."

My mouth had gone dry despite the tea. "The prophecy book."

"The prophecy book. And I told her what I'll tell you now: I don't deal in fortunes. Fortunes are vague enough to be anything and cost nothing to be right. But I opened it — because I open everything, Ada, that's the whole of my profession — and I found dates. Specific ones. Names. Manner of death, manner of life, all in cipher, all precise as a merchant's tally. And one entry near the front that stopped my hand."

He looked at me then the way he looked at ledgers whose sums wouldn't balance. Gently. Like something that might bite.

"It named a taster. Not by name — names weren't earned yet — but by trade and date. A royal food-taster who would die on the thirteenth of Emberfall, of poison never served. And beside the entry, in the margin, a second hand had added a note. *If the guild takes the child young, the death may be postponed indefinitely.*"

The candle flame leaned though there was no draft. Or perhaps there was and I'd simply stopped noticing drafts, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat until it stumbles.

"I don't understand," I said, which was a lie, because I understood with a horrible completeness, the way you understand falling before you hit the ground.

"Don't you?" Corvane said softly. "Ada. Twenty-three years ago, the Crown's household advertised for a foundling with a clean tongue. Standard practice — they take orphans young, before allegiances set, before anyone would miss them if the work went badly. I bought you before the Crown's agents finished their breakfast. Outbid everyone. I paid eleven times the going rate for an orphan child, and every colleague I have asked me why, and I told them I liked your eyes."

The room got very quiet. The kind of quiet that has a price attached.

"What's wrong with my eyes?"

"Nothing. They're fine eyes. I just needed a reason that wasn't the real one." He set down his cup with a click that sounded, in that silence, like a lock turning. "The real one was a date. Your date. You were born in the autumn of the year the prophecy named for the taster's death. The math admitted exactly one candidate window. So I went looking for a child of that season, and I found you in a foundling house in Saltmarsh, third from the window, chewing on a wooden spoon, and I paid more for you than I have ever paid for anything, including a door that opens onto tomorrow."

"Why?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Why spend that much? Why care whether some entry in a madwoman's book comes true?"

"Because of the note in the margin." He leaned forward. "Think, Ada. Think like a ledger. What does the guild sell?"

"Impossible things."

"And what is an impossible thing worth?"

"More when it's rarer—"

"*Everything,*" he said, "when it can't be replaced. You are a person the books have already killed, once, a hundred years before you were born. Which means either the book is wrong — in which case you are ordinary and I wasted a fortune — or the book is right, and you are the single most impossible object in my inventory: a living contradiction. A debt the universe has already collected and hasn't noticed it collected. Do you understand what I could do with that? What the guild could do? A man who cannot die on schedule is not a shield, Ada. He is a key."

The tea had gone cold in my hands. I hadn't drunk it. I set it down because my fingers had begun to shake and I didn't want him to see, and then realized he had already seen everything, had been seeing it since I was three years old and tasting pottage for a household that would have let me die of nightshade without filing a complaint.

"So the palace," I said. "The taster position. You placed me there."

"On purpose. Deliberately. The prophecy says you die of poison never served — so I put you where poison lives. If fate wants you, it has to walk through my arrangement to get you, and I have spent twenty-three years learning every route into the royal kitchens. Every supplier. Every grudge between cooks. Every window a bottle could come through." His voice took on the cadence he used for closing deals, warm and terrible. "You were never an accident, Ada. You were the best-designed thing I ever built."

* * *

Here is what I should have said: *you bought me.* Plain words, plain truth. Here is what I actually said, because grief makes cowards of us all and politeness is grief's favorite disguise:

"The thunder. When I was six. You sat up with me."

"I did."

"And when I burned my hand on the sealing wax — you carried me to the physicker yourself. Four streets. In the rain."

"I did."

"And you taught me the cipher. Nobody else in the guild knows the full tasters' shorthand but you and me." I heard my own voice climbing toward something I hated, pleading dressed as logic. "Corvane, fathers do those things. Transactions don't."

He had the grace — and I will grant him this, whatever else I burn of him later, he had the grace — to look away first.

"No," he said. "They don't. And here is the part where you hate me properly, so brace yourself." He drew a breath. "I didn't plan to love you. I planned to protect an investment. But you were four years old and you organized my spice cabinet by *flavor family* without being asked, and you cried for a week over a dead rat in the cellar, and you tasted my tea before I drank it — unprompted, Ada, nobody taught you — and somewhere in there the ledger stopped balancing and I stopped caring. Yes. I sat with you through storms. Yes, I carried you through rain. None of it was calculated. All of it was real."

"But the beginning wasn't."

"The beginning was arithmetic." He met my eyes again, and his were wet, and I discovered that this made it worse rather than better, because tears can be genuine and ownership can still stand behind them, the way a fire can be genuine and still burn down your house. "And the middle was love, and the end — the end is now, and now I am asking you to trust that both things are true at once. That a man can purchase a child and cherish one. That the transaction and the tenderness lived in the same chest without canceling each other out."

"They don't cancel out," I said quietly. "That's the problem. They compound."

He laughed — one short, startled note, the laugh of a man struck by a blow he'd been expecting from a direction he hadn't watched. "There it is. There's the mind I paid eleven times rate for."

"Don't." I stood. My legs held, barely. "Don't price me. Not now. Not while I'm standing in a room learning that my whole life was a hedge against a line in a book."

"Where are you going?"

"To check the records." I was moving toward the door, and the strange part was how calm my body was, how taster-calm, the calm of a person who has just identified arsenic in the wine and must finish the course smiling. "Every prior taster. Every entry. If they died on their dates — if that book has been collecting us like debts — then I want to see the column of names myself. All of them."

"Ada." He rose too, and for a moment he was not the guildmaster, not the buyer, just an old man with cold tea and silver temples, and I nearly stopped. Nearly. "Whatever you find — remember that knowing a thing early is the entire advantage. The prophecy is a wall built across your road. But walls have doors, and doors—" he smiled, thin and proud and awful, "*doors are my profession.*"

"I know," I said. "You taught me that too."

In the corridor, with his door shut behind me, I finally let my hands shake. Because here is the arithmetic I couldn't do in front of him, the sum that wouldn't close:

If he had never read that book, there would have been no bidding war. No eleven-times rate. No orphan from Saltmarsh carried four streets through the rain. Every kindness I had ever banked against loneliness traced back to a margin note in a dead woman's book — and I could not find, anywhere in the account, a way to separate the purchase from the parent. They shared a spine, like two ledgers bound together by mistake.

I loved him. That was the poison never served. It had been in my cup for twenty-three years, administered in doses of chamomile and thunder-watching, and I had swallowed every dose gladly, and there was no antidote, and the only question left — the one that sent me down the stairs toward the sealed records, candle in hand, past sleeping guildmates who still thought I belonged there —

was whether it had been slow-acting all along,

or whether it was timed, like everything else in my life,

for the thirteenth of Emberfall.

The records vault was dark. The lock was old. And as I knelt before it with my picks, I heard, from the floorboards overhead, Corvane's footsteps crossing his study toward the hidden panel — toward the true ledgers — moving fast.
