## 8. Poison Is a Language

I did not sleep.

This will surprise no one who has met me, but it surprised Corvane, who knocked at my door above Ember Lane at some grey hour when the candles had drowned in their own wax and found me still at the table with the prophecy book open like a wound I couldn't stop looking at.

"Ada." His voice through the wood was gentle, which was worse than shouting. Gentle was what he used when he wanted something and hadn't decided how much it would cost me. "You've been at that book all night."

"I've been *with* it," I said. "It's different."

A pause. Then, because he is the guildmaster and I am his taster and neither of us has ever been good at pretending the other doesn't exist: "Eat something. And Ada — don't burn anything until you've shown it to me first."

He said it lightly. He always says the important things lightly, the way you slip a knife through a seam so the cloth never shows where it was cut. Don't burn anything until you've shown it to me. As if he already knew there was something in my hands worth burning. As if he'd known for a hundred years.

I heard him go down the stairs. I ate half a heel of bread without tasting it, which for me is its own small blasphemy — my tongue is the one instrument I have never been able to lie to — and went back to the page.

Here is what a food-taster knows that nobody thinks to teach: poison is not a substance. It is a language. Arsenic speaks in almonds. Hemlock speaks in numbness climbing the tongue like frost on a windowpane, root to tip. Belladonna speaks softly, in dryness, in a throat gone quiet before the heart does. Every toxin has an accent, an idiom, a tell. The Crown pays me not because I can survive poisons — though my body's ledger of survived ones would fill a modest library — but because I can *read* them. I taste a wine and hear what it's trying to say.

And the cipher in that prophecy book — the looping marginal script that wrapped around the printed doom like ivy around a gallows — had been speaking to me all night in a language I knew.

Tasters' shorthand.

We are not taught it in any formal way. It grows in you, the way calluses grow, out of necessity. You cannot write down *the king's broth contained foxglove, dose sufficient to stop a heart* and expect to keep your post, or your head. So we invented a script of hooks and flourishes, a private alphabet passed mouth to hand from taster to taster, unrecorded anywhere official, deniable as gossip, legible only to someone who had spent years reading death off the tip of a spoon. Each of us develops our own variations. Our own hand. The way handwriting slants differently under fear, shorthand bends toward the writer's particular obsessions. Mine leans on dosage marks; I am precise about quantities the way other people are precise about grudges. Old Maren, who trained me, dotted her negations twice, always, a habit she said came from her own master who had once misread a single stroke and died of it.

The prophecy's cipher leaned on dosage marks.

I want you to understand what that did to me. It wasn't recognition, exactly. Recognition is clean — you see a face, you place it, done. This was the slow horror of watching a stranger's face resolve into your own reflection, feature by feature, while the stranger keeps talking. The loops of the letters bent left where most hands bend right. The negations were struck through with a single long stroke rather than dotted — a variation I had invented myself, three years ago, because Maren's double dots took too long when your fingers were shaking and a prince's life hung on your reading speed. Nobody else used it. Nobody else had ever seen it. I had shown it to exactly one person, and that person was me.

I copied a line out onto scrap paper with my left hand, badly, deliberately distorting it, and compared it against the book. The bones underneath matched. Stroke order matched. The little impatient tail I put on the sign for *delayed onset*, the one Maren scolded me for, the one I'd never managed to train out of myself — there. A century old, in iron-gall ink gone brown as dried blood, and wearing my impatience like a borrowed coat.

Someone had written this book in my hand.

Or I had written this book in mine, which was a sentence I refused to finish thinking for the better part of an hour, pacing the room, drinking water I didn't want, pressing my palms flat against the cold wall as if the building might confirm I was real. The Guild of Impossible Things trades in doors. Doors to vanished countries. Doors to borrowed years. I had logged, with my own careful invisible hands, the sale of a door that opened into yesterday. If a door could open onto yesterday, then somewhere on the far side of some door stood a me old enough to be dust, holding a pen, writing tonight's date beside my name and my manner of death, and sending the finished book backward through the guild's veins until it landed in a seized library and then in front of my eyes.

Which meant the prophecy was not a prophecy. Prophecies are things that happen *to* you. This was correspondence. This was a letter, addressed to me, by the only correspondent who could be trusted to know my address.

I sat back down. My legs made the decision before my pride agreed to it.

*Read it as a letter, then,* I told myself. *Stop flinching and read.*

The cipher wrapped the printed text the way a commentary wraps scripture — agreeing in places, arguing in others. The printer's block letters said ADA THACKERAY, TASTER TO THE CROWN, DIES OF POISON UNSERVED, ON THE [date]. But around those words, in the margin-language, ran a second text, denser, faster, the hand of someone writing against time — literally, God help me, writing against *time*. I began translating properly, marking each glyph against my own habits, building a key as I went, and the night's last candle guttered out and I didn't notice, and the grey light came up over Ember Lane and I didn't notice that either, because the letter was saying:

*Not unserved. Mis-served.* There is a difference, in our language, the difference between a dish that never reached you and a dish that reached you wearing the wrong name. The printed prophecy said I would die of a poison never served to me — a paradox, a cruelty, a riddle with no answer. The margins corrected it. *The poison will be served. The menu will lie.* Something intended for someone else, arriving at my table under a false label. Or something served to me *as* safety — and here the dosage marks clustered thick, urgent, underlined twice in that impatient left-leaning hand — something served to me as antidote that was neither.

And below that, smaller, almost squeezed into the gutter of the page as if the writer feared being interrupted:

*Corvane knows the date. Ask him why the shield is shaped like a girl.*

My blood did something complicated. I put both hands flat on the table and breathed the way Maren taught me for after bad tastings — four counts in, seven held, eight out — because the body panics ahead of the mind and if you let it lead you'll bolt. The body wanted to bolt. The body had read *ask him why the shield is shaped like a girl* and understood, in its dumb animal way, everything my mind was still refusing to itemize: that Corvane had raised me, fed me, taught me every letter of two alphabets, and had perhaps done all of it because a book told him to. That my invisibility might be architecture rather than luck. That the shape of my whole life might be somebody's answer to a written date.

I had believed invisibility was safety. It was the one article of faith I owned. Stand still, stay small, taste and record and never be recorded. And now a hundred-year-old book had looked directly at me — had known my name before I was born, had known my *handwriting* before I learned to write — and the faith cracked straight down the middle, and through the crack I could see the ugly possibility underneath: that I had never been invisible at all. That I had been *hidden*. There is a difference, and the difference is that hidden things are hidden by someone, for reasons, and the someone gets a vote in what happens next.

Four counts in. Seven held. Eight out.

Then, because terror is a poor fuel but it burns hot, and because I am my mother's daughter or somebody's daughter or nobody's, whichever the ledgers say — I got angry.

Anger is underrated in my profession. People imagine the ideal taster as calm, detached, a monk of the palate. But calm is what gets you killed on the third cup. What keeps you alive is the cold fury of *I have survived seventeen poisons and I will not be undone by the eighteenth out of politeness.* Anger steadies the hand. Anger reads fast.

I turned to the last pages of the cipher, the ones I'd been avoiding, the ones nearest the date. And here the letter changed register. Here the hand slowed — I could see it, the strokes lengthening, the impatience bled out of them — the way a hand slows when the writer knows they are near the end of what they can say. The final passage was short. In translation it ran:

*I wrote this to change the ending. If you are reading it as fate, I have failed. If you are reading it as instructions, begin. The fire is low. The country is nearly out of time. The door remembers you even though you do not remember it. Go through before they make the door into a weapon.*

*— A.T.*

*A.T.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, more air than sound, the laugh of someone falling. A.T. Ada Thackeray. Of course. Of course the author signed it. Who else signs a confession across a century? Who else could? The guild's doors carried cargo both directions if you paid enough, and I had logged the tariffs myself, and somewhere in the deep rooms there must be a receipt — a sending fee, a weight declaration, *one book, aged, fragile, do not bend* — filed in my own tidy script, because I file everything, because recording is the whole of my religion —

The receipt.

If the book had come through the doors, it had come through the ledgers. Nothing moves in the Guild of Impossible Things without an entry. Corvane's law, drilled into me since I could hold a pen: *every impossible thing must be recorded or it unravels.* He had taught me that rule like a catechism. And the same rule that kept the impossible from unraveling meant that his own miracle — a book posted backward through a hundred years — had to be sitting in his accounts, dated, priced, described.

He had ordered me to show him anything I meant to burn. He had said it gently. He had said it the way you say a thing when you are checking whether the trap you built is still sprung.

Very well, guildmaster. You keep accounts. So do I.

I closed the prophecy book and did not burn it. Instead I wrapped it in my spare shift, tucked it under the loose floorboard where I kept the things Corvane thought I didn't keep — the pressed sprig of Vaeli silverleaf from chapter six of my education, the widow's overpayment I'd never entered, the first pen Maren gave me — and I dressed, and I went downstairs into the guild's back rooms while the morning was still too young for anyone respectable to be awake.

The archive at that hour is a church. Dust hangs in the shafts of light like incense. The ledgers sleep in their alcoves, hundreds of them, spines stamped with years going back past the founding, and I moved among them quietly, an honest thief in the house of records, and pulled the volumes for the year the seized library was catalogued. If a book had arrived from a century away, there would be an entry. Acquisition. Provenance. Fee.

There was.

Third shelf, second volume, folio marked in Corvane's cramped upright hand — a hand as unlike mine as chalk is unlike ink, and yet I noticed, with a fresh small chill, that he ruled his negations with a single long stroke, same as me, same as the book, and I filed that observation in the drawer of suspicions that was getting dangerously full. The entry read:

*One volume, printed, annotated. Source: deep door, return freight. Sender's fee waived — house account. Note: recipient to be given access upon achieving competence. Do not accelerate. The text ripens.*

*Do not accelerate. The text ripens.* Like fruit. Like me.

I stood there in the dusty church of records with my heart going like a drum in a procession, and I understood two things with perfect clarity. First: Corvane had known about this book for years. Had received it, catalogued it, shelved it, and waited — waited for me to *ripen* — feeding me to the Crown's tables one banquet at a time, keeping me alive past the written date through means I was only beginning to guess at, all while letting me believe I was free.

Second: *return freight.* The entry said return freight. Freight goes both ways. Which meant the deep door the book had traveled through was still open, still charged, still sitting somewhere in the guild's deepest rooms — and a door that can carry a book backward through a century is a door that can carry a person.

Somewhere behind me, upstairs, the guild began to wake. I heard the kettle. I heard Tam whistling off-key, the way he does when he's nervous, which is always. I heard Corvane's tread cross the floor above my head — unhurried, familiar, the tread of the only father-shaped thing I had ever had — and beneath it, fainter, from the direction of the sealed stair, a sound I had never heard in ten years of living over these rooms.

The deep door, breathing.

Warm air moved through the archive like an exhale, smelling of cinders and rain and something older than both, and the ledger in my hands trembled very slightly on its shelf, as if the entries themselves had felt the draft of a country calling its own name.

Ada Thackeray dies of poison unserved. Unless, said the breathing dark at the bottom of the stairs, she serves herself to the fire instead.

I put the ledger back. I picked up the lamp. And I started down.
