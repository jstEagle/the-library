## 10. Raid on Ember Lane

The sun rose over Ember Lane the way it always did: indifferent, gilding the chimney pots, making the gutter water look briefly like something worth drinking. I was in the counting room with a pot of tea gone cold beside me and the widow's entry half-finished — *one hour, purchased fair, grief notarized* — when the first door in the guild broke.

I heard it before I understood it. That's the honest accounting. There is a particular sound a door makes when it is opened by someone who does not know it is an impossible door, who treats it as wood and hinges only, and that sound is wrong the way a false note is wrong. Every door in the Guild of Impossible Things has a voice. That morning, every one of them screamed.

* * *

Let me set down what happened, in order, because order is the only inheritance Corvane ever gave me that turned out to be worth anything.

The first sound was the front door — the plain one, the one that opens onto Ember Lane and nothing else, because even we needed a way to take deliveries of ordinary flour. It went in with a crack like a bone giving way, and then came boots, dozens of them, and the flat grey voices of inquisitors calling the litany of seizure: *In the name of the Crown, in the name of the Ledger Above All Ledgers, stand where you are.*

Stand where you are. As if any of us had ever stood anywhere at all. We were a guild of people the world had agreed not to see; standing still was the one thing none of us knew how to do.

I was three steps from the back stair when the second sound came, and this one stopped me cold on the landing. It was fire.

Not dragon-fire. Dragon-fire has a note in it, low and patient, like a held breath. This was ordinary fire, soldier's fire, lamp-oil and pitch and a torch thrown without ceremony into the archive room where the lesser ledgers lived. The ledgers of borrowed years. The ledgers of names not yet earned. The ledger, gods forgive us, of doors.

You cannot burn an impossible thing and have done with it. Corvane taught me that my first month, his hand heavy on my shoulder, his breath smelling of the plum wine he pretended he didn't drink. *Every impossible thing must be recorded or it unravels,* he said. He never told me what happens when you record an impossible thing and then burn the record. I learned it that morning, standing on the stairs while smoke crawled along the ceiling toward me like something curious.

What happens is this: it unravels *at you.*

* * *

The archive door burst open ahead of the flames, which should have been impossible, and everything that came through it was impossible too, briefly, gloriously, horribly. A stolen year got loose — I saw it go up the stairwell past me like a fox, all grey fur and greyer light, and somewhere below a young inquisitor began to weep for reasons he will never be able to name to his superiors. A shelf of unearned names caught alight and each burning page made a sound like a syllable being born and dying in the same breath. The debt-ledgers simply fell open, all of them at once, and every unpaid memory in the building tried to go home.

I felt one brush past me on its way out. An old man's memory of a daughter's wedding, owed to a moneylender forty years dead. For a half-second I was at that wedding. I could smell the bread. Then it was gone, finding whatever poor soul in the city had once sold it, and I stood on the stairs holding a taste of wedding cake I had no right to, grieving a family that was never mine.

That is what the inquisition burned that morning. Not papers. Debts. And when you cancel a debt by fire, the payment comes due somewhere else, immediately, in full, and the Crown's men had no idea what they'd just spent.

* * *

I found Sefa in the corridor outside the map room, coughing, dragging Wren by the collar. Wren is twelve and can walk through walls that believe in themselves, which is most walls, but a wall full of shouting soldiers had apparently convinced itself thoroughly enough to hold him.

"Back way," Sefa rasped. "Corvane's orders. Everyone out through the—"

"Where is Corvane?" I asked.

Sefa's face did something I had never seen it do in nine years of working beside her. It hesitated.

"He went for the master ledger," she said. "He said — Ada, he said to tell you to meet him at the Vael door."

The Vael door was four rooms away, through the burning archive.

"He said," I repeated.

"He said." She wouldn't look at me. Sefa, who once lied to an inquisitor's face for six hours straight about the provenance of a teapot that drank memories, could not lie to mine. That was answer enough.

* * *

Here is what I want written plainly, because there will be those who read this account later — Hale, perhaps, if the world is generous, or some other careful liar with a secret ledger of their own — and here is what I did, step by step, so that no one can call it impulse:

I went back toward the fire.

Not for Corvane. Let the record show I already knew, in the place where tasters keep the things their tongues know before their minds consent to, that Corvane would not be there. I went back for the ledgers that could still be saved, and for the people who couldn't save themselves, and — I will be honest, since honesty is the one luxury I've decided to finally afford — because some part of me wanted to see, with my own eyes, whether he would leave me.

Nine years. He bought me at eight years old off a kitchen cart in a city that has forgotten the transaction. He taught me letters on ledger paper. He taught me that invisibility was armor, that being unseen was the closest a person could come to being safe, and he held my hand the night of my first poisoning and said, *you survived, little ledger, you survived, that's all the guild asks.* I have drunk arsenic for him. I have worn the mask of a servant so long I sometimes forgot there was a face under it.

I went back to see if any of it had been real, knowing the answer, going anyway.

That is not courage. Courage would have been walking out and letting the question stay unanswered. What I did was simpler and stupider and more human: I wanted proof.

* * *

The archive was a cathedral of falling ash. The roof beams glowed like the ribs of some vast burnt animal, and the fire had reached the shelf of doors.

Understand: the guild doesn't keep doors themselves. Doors are kept elsewhere, in the spaces between, and the ledgers merely hold their addresses — the true name of a threshold, written so it can be found again. When the address burns, the door doesn't die. It forgets where it is. I watched it happen through the smoke: a page curling black, and somewhere far away — I felt it in my teeth, the way you feel a bell ringing two streets over — a door in a mountain pass quietly ceasing to be in a mountain pass.

And beneath that, deeper than the fire's noise, another sound. The Vael door, four rooms away, was singing.

It had never sung before. In all my years of logging trades past it, carrying ink and candle-wax, it had been silent as any door. Now it made a low continuous note, and the note was getting thinner, and I understood — the way my tongue understands poison before my head catches up — that it was singing because it was dying. The dragon-fire behind it was guttering. Whatever fed it, whoever fed it, the feeding had stopped.

Later I would learn why. Later I would learn what Corvane had been pouring into that flame for decades, and where he had gone that morning, and what he carried. That morning I only knew that the last door to a country that does not exist was fading like breath off glass, and the man who raised me had ordered everyone out through it, and had not gone himself.

* * *

I got Wren's sister out. I got the widow's clerk, old Marta, who had come in early to dispute an interest payment on her own borrowed youth. I got three others whose names I will enter in whatever book I keep next, because their guild entries burned with everything else and someone must remember they existed. Sefa went out the back with the rest. I told her I'd follow. She believed me, which is either a testament to my skill at lying or an indictment of her hope.

Then the corridor to the Vael door filled with grey coats, and the choice was taken out of my hands, which is how the Crown prefers to make its choices.

* * *

They took me in the map room. Six of them, and Inquisitor Hale at the front, holding a lantern he didn't need with the whole archive burning, holding it anyway the way careful men hold things — as if the holding itself were evidence of something.

Our eyes met over the shoulder of the soldier twisting my arms back, and I watched him do the arithmetic. The food-taster from the banquet. The one who'd answered every question with exactly as much truth as was required and not a drop more. He knew. Not everything — I'll grant him that much, he didn't know everything, nobody has ever known everything about me including me — but he knew I belonged to this smoke, and he had come at dawn specifically so that I would be in it.

"You're early," I said, because my mouth has never met a situation it wouldn't try to taste.

"You're late," he said. "Your master isn't."

And that was the moment, if you want the exact entry, the precise line in the account where the number changed. Your master isn't late. Meaning: they had seen him. Meaning: he had walked out past them, or been let past them, or — worse, infinitely worse — had arranged the timing himself, and the dawn raid had come down Ember Lane on a schedule he had approved.

The master ledger was gone from its case. I saw the empty space where it had slept for a century, outlined in dust, shaped like a country.

"Where is he?" Hale asked me.

"Gone," I said. It was the truest word available and I spent it gladly.

* * *

They marched me out through my own burning home. I want that sentence to sit on the page a while, because I sat inside it a while, on the long walk between the map room and Ember Lane, flanked by grey coats, watching nine years of my life come apart around me in embers.

The counting room went as I passed. My cold tea, my half-finished entry — *one hour, purchased fair, grief notarized* — curling orange at the edges. The widow would get her hour anyway; hours, unlike promises, keep. But the record of it was ash now, and somewhere in the arithmetic of the universe a debt existed with no witness, floating loose, waiting to be claimed by anyone cruel enough to say it had never been paid.

The hall of doors went. Each address burning was a small extinction I felt in my sternum, a series of tiny deaths spaced like heartbeats. By the time they pushed me over the threshold onto Ember Lane, I was cataloguing the losses out of habit, because cataloguing is what I am, and the habit held me together the way a ledger's spine holds pages that would otherwise scatter.

Outside, the morning was obscene. Ordinary. Bakers were arguing about flour prices two doors down. A dog was delighted about something. The lane smelled of woodsmoke and morning bread, and the bread won, because the world does not stop seasoning itself for your grief.

Hale walked beside me the whole way and said nothing, which I recognized as a courtesy between professionals, and hated, and was grateful for.

At the corner he paused. "The book you kept," he said quietly, not looking at me, addressing the burning doorway instead. "Was anyone harmed by it?"

It took me a moment to understand he meant the guild's ledgers, not the prophecy. That Hale, with the roof falling in behind us, was asking whether our crimes had victims.

"No," I said. "Only debts. Ours."

He nodded slowly, and wrote something in a small notebook he thought I couldn't see, and I let him think it, because a careful liar recognizes another's handwriting even upside down.

* * *

The cell they gave me was clean, which insulted me more than filth would have. They put me in the Tower's middle tier — not the traitors' floor above, not the common level below — the tier reserved for prisoners whose value is undetermined. Even in chains, the Crown insisted on filing me correctly. Somewhere in the smoke of that morning, I almost admired it.

But I'm getting ahead of the account, and this chapter belongs to Ember Lane, not to cells.

One more thing happened before the gates closed behind me, and I set it down last because I have saved it until I could write it steadily.

From the courtyard, through the gate bars, I looked back at the guild one final time. The roof of the archive had fallen in. Fire stood up out of it straight and yellow against the pale sky, and the smoke went east in a long dark banner, and beneath the roar of it — beneath the ordinary roar — I could still hear the Vael door, very faint now, thinner than a hair, singing its single lowering note.

And I understood, finally, fully, with the clarity that only arrives when everything else has been taken, why the door was dying.

It wasn't the raid. It wasn't the fire. The dragon-flame hadn't guttered because of anything the inquisition had done.

It had guttered because the man who fed it had chosen, that morning, to spend his coins elsewhere — on a pardon, on a passage, on a master ledger hugged to his chest like a child — and he had walked out through the front door past six inquisitors who stepped aside for him, and he had not looked back at the building, and he had not looked back at me.

Corvane used to say that every impossible thing must be recorded or it unravels.

He took the record. He left the unraveling to us.

* * *

They locked the gate. The lock was ordinary iron, and I tasted it on the air — rust, oil, someone's careless thumbprints — and I laughed, quietly, alone, in a manner that worried the guard.

Because here is what the ashes hadn't counted on, what no ledger, royal or impossible, had an entry for: the prophecy named my death, and the date on that page has not yet arrived. The book written in my own century-old hand says I die by poison never served, and I am, whatever else I am this morning — prisoner, orphan, heir to a burned map — still the finest living student of the guildmaster who abandoned me, and I have not yet taught myself everything I know.

Somewhere in that tower was a man named Hale who kept his own secret accounts, and somewhere in this city was a widow who owed the guild an hour, and somewhere beyond a fading door a country was dying with my name stitched into its last flag.

Corvane had the ledger.

But I had read every page of it, and he had forgotten the one lesson he himself taught me best:

A record means nothing without the person who knows how to read it.

They were taking me to a cell. Good.

I had work to do there.
