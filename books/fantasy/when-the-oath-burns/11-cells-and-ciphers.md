## 11. Cells and Ciphers

The Crown's cells beneath the Inquisition are better appointed than I expected, which tells you something about how often the Crown puts people in them.

There is a cot with an actual straw tick. A bucket that has been scoured recently enough to smell of lye rather than of its purpose. A candle stub on a ledge of dressed stone, and a window slit so narrow and so high that the light it admits is less illumination than apology. Someone has even left me a blanket, wool, grey, faintly moth-eaten at one corner.

I have tasted enough arsenic to know that comfort in a cage is usually a prelude. Either they want me calm for questioning, or they want me comfortable enough to grow careless about what I say. Or — and this is the possibility I like least — someone in this building believes I might be worth keeping alive, and whoever believes that is not the same person who ordered my cell made ready.

I have been here two days by the candles, three by my own stubborn count, because candles burn faster when you stare at them hoping they'll lie to you.

They have not questioned me yet. That is also a technique. Let the prisoner build castles out of silence, then knock the castles down one question at a time. Corvane taught me patience as a tool; the Inquisition is teaching me what it feels like from the wrong end of it, and I find I don't care for the symmetry.

What I do instead of panicking — panic being a luxury priced beyond a taster's wages — is inventory.

My body, first. Unhurt. Fed twice daily on bread and soup that I taste carefully before eating, out of habit so ingrained it's nearly devotional. The soup is clean. Bitter greens, old mutton, no metallic edge, no almond sweetness where sweetness doesn't belong. Whoever cooks for the Inquisition is honest if uninspired, and I respect that in a person I've never met.

The cell, second. Stone walls, but the mortar near the floor has been patched more than once, unevenly. The door hinge squeaks on its lower pin — I heard it every time the food came, a small bright complaint. Squeaky hinges mean the door is old and the mechanism simple. Simple mechanisms can be persuaded.

And third: the ledgers. Not physical ones — those burned, or were carried off by a man whose coat smelled of smoke and whose last look at me was the look a merchant gives stock he's decided not to salvage. No. The ledgers in my head. Every entry I ever made for the Guild of Impossible Things. Debts in memories. Doors in their seasons. Which hour was sold to whom, and what it bought, and who wept over the receipt.

Corvane escaped with the master ledger. But Corvane never memorized the accounts. He kept the book because he believed books were safer than heads.

He raised me to be the backup and never told me. That, too, goes in the inventory.

* * *

He comes on the fourth day, and he comes without fanfare, which in an inquisitor is either confidence or courtesy, and I suspect Hale is capable of both.

I hear him before I see him — boots on stone with a stride too even to be hurrying and too long to be casual. Then the lock turns — a good lock, oiled, unlike the hinge — and there he is in the doorway with a chair under one arm and a ledger of his own tucked beneath the other, and behind him a guard who looks disappointed to be sent away.

Hale sets the chair down facing my cot, sits, and does not speak for a long moment. He is a lean man built like a folded letter, all creases held deliberately in place. His eyes go around the cell once — the blanket, the bucket, the candle, me — cataloguing. I recognize the exercise. I've done it to every banquet table I ever sat at.

"Inquisitor," I say pleasantly.

"Thackeray." He sets his ledger on his knee but doesn't open it. "You're not afraid."

"I'm a royal taster. Fear and I share lodgings, but we keep separate accounts."

Something moves at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that a joke was made and filed. "Do you know why I haven't questioned you until now?"

"Because you wanted me to build castles?"

Now the smile arrives, briefly, and is escorted away again. "Because I wanted to know what you'd do with the time. Most people sleep. Some pray. One woman sang sea shanties for three days until I confessed to crimes just to make her stop." He opens the ledger at last, unhurried. "You inventoried the room. You checked your food for poison both days — yes, we watched — and yesterday you spent four hours reciting something under your breath with your fingers moving like you were turning pages."

"And what did you conclude?"

"That the Guild of Impossible Things keeps its true records somewhere no raid can reach." He looks up. "Your head."

I keep my face doing nothing, which is the hardest work I do. "Inquisitor, I'm a servant. I taste soup."

"You taste soup," Hale agrees mildly, "and survive things that kill kings' cousins. You were seen leaving Ember Lane the night of the banquet. You were found in a burning building surrounded by books written in cipher, and when my men brought you here, you asked them — politely — whether the kitchen used copper pots." He turns a page he hasn't read yet. "Copper reacts badly with certain compounds. You weren't asking about the pots, Thackeray. You were assessing whether we intended to poison you, and whether our cook would be competent enough to hide it."

"Paranoia is a professional qualification in my line of work."

"So is memory." He taps the ledger. "So let's trade, since we're both professionals. I ask questions. You decide how much truth each answer can afford. And I'll be honest with you about the exchange rate, because I find lying wastes everyone's time."

That surprises me into stillness. Inquisitors are not supposed to offer honest exchange rates. Inquisitors are supposed to offer the rack and a priest, in whichever order the budget allows.

"What kind of honesty?" I ask carefully.

"The kind that starts with this: I did not order your arrest. I requested you be *held*. There's a difference in the paperwork, and the paperwork matters." His jaw tightens, briefly, the first crack in the folded-letter composure. "The order to hold you came down signed by someone above me. The order to *question* you came down separately, from higher still, with instructions I did not write and do not like."

"Instructions?"

"To establish whether you can open a door."

The candle flame leans, though there's no draft. Or perhaps there is; perhaps the building itself is listening. I think of the Vael door in the guild's back rooms, oak older than the maps that forgot what it opened onto. I think of the city on the other side, lit by one guttering fire, full of people who cannot leave without forgetting themselves.

"A door," I repeat. "The Crown wants a door."

"The Crown," Hale says, with the flat precision of a man placing poison on his own tongue, "wants a weapon. And someone above me has decided a door that leads to a country that officially does not exist would make an excellent one."

* * *

Here is a thing about poisons that most people never learn, because most people have the good fortune never to need the knowledge: every poison is a language, and the antidote is rarely a counter-poison. Usually the antidote is simply knowing what you swallowed, quickly enough, and being willing to vomit up part of yourself to survive it.

Corvane taught me that. He taught me that the guild's whole economy ran on the principle — impossible things are safe only while unrecorded, and deadly the moment they're owned. He taught me that a debt in memories could ruin a life more thoroughly than any blade. And then, on Ember Lane, with the smoke rising, he took the master ledger and left me in the fire, because the ledger was the asset and I was only the apprentice.

So when Hale says *weapon*, I understand exactly what has been poured into my cup. And I understand that the man across from me has been made to drink from the same pot, and hates the taste, and is too disciplined to spit.

"Let's talk exchange rates," I say.

He inclines his head, granting me the field.

"You want to know if I can open the Vael door. I want to know who gave the order, what they intend to carry through it, and what happens to the people already on the other side." I hold up a finger before he can answer. "Partial truths, as offered. I'll give you real answers, but I choose the size of them. You'll know when I'm holding back, because I won't insult us both by pretending otherwise. In return, you tell me one true thing for every question you ask. Not a deflection. A truth."

"That's not how interrogations—"

"No," I agree. "It isn't. This is how *trades* work. And you said you hate wasting time."

He studies me for a long moment. I watch him weigh it — not whether I'm telling the truth about the method, but whether the method serves him. He is not a stupid man. Stupid men don't keep secret ledgers.

"First question," he says finally. "Who is the guildmaster?"

"Corvane. That's the name he uses; whether he was baptized with it, I couldn't swear. He raised me from the age of six. Bought me, actually, though I didn't learn the word 'bought' until much later — he preferred 'sponsored.'" My voice stays level. It cost me something to keep it level, and I let him see the cost, because that's part of the trade. "He fed the guild for forty years. Doors, hours, names. Everything recorded or it unraveled — that was his catechism. I wrote it down ten thousand times."

"Your turn. Truth for truth."

"The order came from the Lord Protector's office," Hale says. "Specifically from Undersecretary Vess, who signs orders the Protector never reads. Vess wants the door. What Vess wants it *for*, I wasn't told — but I was shown a requisition." He pauses, and I watch him decide to spend more than the minimum. "Two hundred pounds of quicklime. Forty barrels of lamp oil. Chains rated for beasts. And a writ authorizing 'containment of non-crown populations.'"

Non-crown populations. I turn the phrase over. It has the clean bureaucratic shine of language designed so that no one has to picture faces.

"There are people through that door," I say quietly.

"I suspected as much." He writes nothing down, which tells me he means this conversation to leave no record — or that everything he means to record, he records somewhere else entirely. "Next question. The prophecy. We found pages in the guild's library referencing dates and deaths. Dates that matched prior tasters."

"Matched how precisely?"

"Day and manner. Six tasters over ninety years. Each died on the date written for them, each of poison 'never served.'" He looks at me directly. "Yours is next month, Thackeray. That's why Vess wants you alive and cooperative. A dead heir is useless; a living hostage is leverage."

*Heir.*

The word lands like a dropped goblet — I hear it break before I feel the cut. I knew. Of course I knew, some animal part of me had known since the old woman in Vael called me by a name I'd never heard, since the dragon-fire leaned toward me like a dog toward its master. But knowing a thing in the marrow and hearing it said aloud in a Crown cell by a Crown officer are different economies altogether.

"I don't know what you mean," I say, and it comes out steady, which surprises us both.

"Truth for truth," Hale reminds me, not unkindly. "That one's yours to pay whenever you choose. I'll ask my actual question now: can you open the door?"

I could lie. He'd probably detect it; he's good. But lies are debts too, and I am done owing Corvane's kind of creditors.

"Yes," I say. "But not for them. And not the way they think. The door doesn't lead *to* Vael like a road leads to a town. Vael exists because the door holds it together — no. Wrong direction. The door holds because Vael holds. There's a fire there, an old one, and while it burns, the country exists and the door opens. When it dies—" I spread my hands. "No country. No door. Nothing to weaponize, nothing to contain. Just an oak frame full of ordinary air."

"And the people inside?"

"Cannot leave. Not won't — *cannot*. Step out and they forget themselves. Names, faces, griefs, gone. They stay because staying is the only way to remain anyone at all." I wrap my arms around my knees. "Vess's requisition makes sense now. Chains. Oil. Quicklime. You can't evacuate people who dissolve at the threshold. So the plan must be to bring the threshold to them, or drag them through anyway and call whatever shuffles out the far side 'contained.'"

Hale has gone very still. Stillness in an inquisitor, I'm learning, is the equivalent of another man shouting.

"How do you know the requisition details?" I ask. "Truth for truth."

"Because I copied them." He closes his ledger. "Twelve years I've served the Inquisition. Twelve years believing the ledgers I kept — arrests, sentences, confessions — were instruments of justice, however blunt. Last year I noticed the numbers stopped adding up. Executions ordered that never appeared in provincial records. Prisoners transferred to addresses that don't exist. So I started my own account, off the books, of everything that didn't balance." His mouth twists. "I thought I was hunting corruption. I find instead I'm hunting arithmetic so large it has its own weather."

"Welcome to the guild," I say. "We trade in impossible numbers."

* * *

He leaves after that, and I expect the silence to close back over me like water. Instead I sit with the candle and do what I always do: keep accounts.

Hale is a prisoner. That's the discovery of the evening, the entry I ink into the ledger behind my eyes. He's caged by rank, by procedure, by twelve years of believing in an institution that has been cooking its books since before he learned to read them. He came down here asking questions and left having answered mine, and he knows it, and he came back tomorrow-early anyway — I can tell by the way he paused at the door, half a heartbeat longer than necessary.

Two prisoners of different cages. Mine is stone. His is paper. But paper burns, and so do stones, given enough fire, and I happen to know a great deal about fire.

I think about Vael. About the old woman with the name on her tongue that fit my mouth better than my own. About the fire guttering low, and the people who cannot leave, and the fact that somewhere upstairs a man named Vess is signing requisitions for chains rated for beasts.

I think about the prophecy, and the death date written beside my name, and the growing suspicion — planted like a seed by Hale's mention of it, watered by everything since — that a book which predicts deaths accurately might have been written by someone who wanted those deaths *prevented*, and failed, century after century, because everyone who read it tried to change the wrong thing.

Six tasters died on their written dates of poison never served. Poison never served. As if the poison came from somewhere other than a cup. As if the ledger itself were the venom, and reading your own death aloud the dose.

Next month, then. Unless the ending changes.

I blow out the candle — no point letting them measure how late I lie awake — and settle onto the straw, and begin, silently, to compose a message I have no way to send:

*To whoever is writing this down a hundred years early: I've read your book. I know the dates were warnings. Tell me what you couldn't — what did you try?*

Somewhere above me, through four floors of stone and paperwork, a door that shouldn't exist hums against its hinges. Or perhaps that's the wind. Or perhaps it's the sound of an arithmetic so large it has its own weather, finally beginning to storm.

Footsteps in the corridor, past midnight. Not Hale's measured stride — quicker, lighter, wrong. They stop outside my cell.

The lock turns.
