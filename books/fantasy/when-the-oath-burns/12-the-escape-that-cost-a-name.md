## 12. The Escape That Cost a Name

There is a particular quality to the dark in an inquisition cell. It isn't the absence of light — they give you a candle, because cruelty in the Crown's service is always couched as courtesy — but the absence of anything for the light to land on. Grey stone, grey straw, grey bucket. I had counted the stones twice, which tells you how long I'd been there and also what kind of person I am.

Three days. Or possibly four. Time in a cell behaves like a guild debt: it accrues interest you didn't agree to.

I was lying on my back performing my finest impression of a woman who has given up — a performance I have polished over years of royal banquets, where surrender is often the safest posture — when the candle guttered sideways. Not from wind. There was no wind. The flame simply bent, politely, toward the door, the way a courtier bends toward a passing duke.

Then the lock opened without being touched.

Maren Voss stood in the doorway wearing a guard's uniform two sizes too large and an expression I recognized from the night she bought her stolen hour: grief with its sleeves rolled up.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been eating prison food," I said. "It's seasoned with despair. Also possibly turnip."

"Can you walk?"

"I can run, if the alternative is staying."

She tossed me a bundle — guard's coat, boots that almost fit, a cap. I dressed with the speed of a woman who had spent a decade changing out of banquet silks into work clothes in under a minute while pretending to be somewhere else entirely. Old skills. They keep.

"The corridor's clear until the shift change," Maren said. "Eleven minutes. Maybe nine, if Sergeant Ollery stopped at the cistern again, and he always stops at the cistern."

"How did you get a uniform?"

"I bought it."

"With what?"

"With what I have left." She said it lightly, but I heard the ledger underneath. Everyone who deals with the Guild of Impossible Things learns to hear ledgers under sentences. "Ada. You gave me an hour with him. One hour, and it was worth more than everything else I own put together. This is just the change coming back."

* * *

We went through the palace the way water goes through a wall — finding the cracks, taking none of the strength of it. Maren moved like someone who had walked these corridors before, and I realized after the third turn that she had: she'd been a scullery maid here, years ago, before her husband. Before the fever. Before she came to Ember Lane with all her money and asked us for something impossible.

Left at the laundry. Down the stair the guards used because it smelled less of the cells. Through a doorway whose hinges I oiled myself, once, in another life, on Corvane's orders, so that a taster could come and go unremarked. I had built my own escape route years ago without knowing it. There's a lesson in that, and I was too busy to learn it.

The courtyard gate was the problem, as gates always are.

Two sentries. Lantern light. The kind of boredom that makes men attentive to anything unusual, because unusual is the only entertainment on offer.

Maren pressed something into my hand. A small glass vial, stoppered, warm despite the cold air.

"What is this?"

"The hour," she said. "What's left of it. It's been keeping."

An hour, kept. In the guild's back rooms I had catalogued such things a hundred times: borrowed time, decanted, waiting. I knew the theory. I had written the entries. *One hour, sold to M. Voss, widow, for the purpose of sitting with the dead.* I had never held one. It felt like holding a heartbeat that wasn't mine.

"What do I do with it?"

"Unstopper it when you're past the gate. Not before. And Ada—" She caught my sleeve. Her hands were shaking. "It takes a price. Everything impossible does. You know that better than anyone. The hour will carry you out of here faster than any horse, but it'll take something from you on the way. It took nothing from me because I paid up front, gladly. You'll be paying on the other end, and you won't get to choose what."

I looked at the vial. Warm gold liquid, or what passed for liquid when time gets bottled — it moved slightly against the glass even when my hand was still, like something impatient.

"Maren," I said. "What did you choose? When you paid up front?"

"To forget how he sounded, at the end. So the last thing left in my head would be how he laughed." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, and one of the bravest. "Worth it. Go."

* * *

Here is what I remember about the gate:

I remember walking behind Maren, head down, carrying a lantern I had no business carrying. I remember the sentries' conversation, which concerned a dog and a woman named Petra and was riveting to them both. I remember stepping over the threshold, onto cobbles slick with night rain, and unstopping the vial.

And then the world folded.

Not fast. That's the thing no one tells you. Impossible things are never fast; they are only efficient. The city around me became a series of paintings hung in a dark gallery, and I moved between them — the fish market at dawn, the bridge at noon, Ember Lane in evening rain — each one glimpsed, each one gone before my eyes finished reporting it. An hour, spent in sixty strides. Somewhere in the middle of it I passed a version of myself still sitting in the cell, three days younger, and we did not wave.

And the hour took its price.

I felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave your mouth: not pain exactly, but a sudden wrongness, a hole where weight used to be. I stumbled against a wall on Ember Lane — real time now, real rain, the guild's burned shell rising black against the sky across the street — and reached instinctively for something. For a face.

My mother's face.

I want to be precise here, because precision is all I have left of her. I know she existed. I know she had hands, because everyone does, and I know — I *knew*, an hour ago — that she hummed when she worked, off-key, a song from a country I couldn't name. I knew her laugh started in her shoulders. I knew the exact color of — 

Nothing.

There is a room inside every memory, and in the room of *my mother* there was furniture, there was shape, there was the certainty of occupancy — but the window was bricked over. I stood in the rain outside the ruins of the only home I'd ever had and discovered that I could not see her face. Not blurred. Not faded. *Gone*, the way a page goes when the ink hasn't just dried wrong but never touched the paper.

She was Vael-born. I understand that now; I would understand it fully later, standing among people who called me by a name I'd never heard. But even then, in the rain, some part of me understood what the hour had taken and why. It takes what costs most. It had taken the one face that belonged to the erased country, because the erased country cannot keep even its reflections alive outside its borders.

I sat down on the wet cobbles and did something I hadn't done since I was small enough to be carried: I cried without managing it. No technique, no timing, no audience management. Just grief, leaking out of a hole shaped like a face I couldn't picture.

* * *

The knock, when it came, was three soft raps and a pause — the old guild signal for *friend, alone*. Which meant either luck or a miracle, and I'd used up my miracle on Maren.

I opened the hidden door on the alley side of what had been the Guild of Impossible Things, and found Sefa Tarrow standing in the dark with a satchel, a lamp, and the expression of a woman who has been rehearsing a speech and abandoning it repeatedly.

Sefa. Second-oldest of the tasters. The one who taught me that hemlock tastes faintly of mouse and that the best lie is a true thing told at the wrong volume.

"They said you were taken," she said.

"I was. I got better."

"Corvane—"

"Sold me. Yes. I've done the accounting." I stepped aside to let her in, and noticed my hand wasn't steady, and hated it. "How many made it out?"

"Nine. Of fourteen. Brannick didn't. Little Isbet didn't." Sefa's jaw worked. "Corvane took the master ledger and the deep keys and left the rest of us to burn with the evidence. Hale's men took six alive. Two of those haven't stopped screaming."

I lit the lamp properly and looked around the cellar that had been my sanctuary for fifteen years. Scorch marks climbed the walls where the shelves had been. The great ledger desk was split down the middle, axed by someone with strong feelings about bookkeeping. Ash lay over everything like the first honest snowfall of winter.

"He fed the fire with traded years," I said. "Decades of them. Did you know?"

"I suspected. You don't watch a man grow younger-looking through his fifties without suspecting something." Sefa set down her satchel and began unpacking it with the brisk economy of someone who copes by working: bread, cheese, a flask, papers. "But knowing and knowing are different animals. He raised us, Ada. He picked us out of gutters and taught us our own tongues."

"He picked me out of a gutter because a prophecy said I'd die."

"Yes." She didn't dress it up. That was Sefa's kindness; she never lied to soften a blow, only to buy time, and there was no more time to buy. "And he built your whole life around preventing it, like a man building a dam around a single drop of rain. It was love, Ada. It was also ownership. Most terrible things are both."

I ate the bread because grief is no excuse for wasting bread; Corvane taught me that too, and I resented how much of him was still stitched into me, useful and treacherous in equal measure.

"Sefa," I said, when I could trust my voice. "Do I have family? Before the gutter. Before Corvane."

She went very still, the way people do when they've been guarding an answer for years and have just realized the wall they hid it behind has burned down.

"Why?"

"Because I lost something tonight, getting out. Something I can't get back. And I need to know if what I lost was real, or if I invented her out of loneliness."

Sefa sat down across from me. She poured two cups from the flask and pushed one over, and drank hers first, which among tasters means *this is safe and also I need a moment*.

"There was a woman," she said slowly. "Years ago. Came to the guild when you were maybe four, five. Wanted to know if there was a door back to Vael. Corvane met with her himself — that alone should have told us something; he doesn't meet with anyone. She came three times. The third time she left crying, and Corvane watched her go from the top of the stairs, and he had the strangest look on his face. Like a man watching a fire he'd started on purpose."

"What did she look like?"

"I don't know. I saw her once, from a distance, in bad light." Sefa's eyes came up to mine, and I saw her realize, mid-sentence, what she was about to say, and say it anyway, because she was Sefa. "But I remember her voice. She had an accent I'd never heard anywhere else. And she hummed. While she waited. Off-key."

The room tilted. I gripped the table.

"She hummed when she worked," I said. "Off-key. A song I can't name anymore."

"Ada—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't be gentle yet. Give me a minute to not fall apart, and then you can be gentle."

* * *

We worked through what remained of the night, because work is a rope and I needed rope. Sefa had salvaged fragments — loose pages, a cipher key half-burned, the names of the scattered tasters and where they'd gone to ground. We laid it all out on the split desk like surgeons sorting instruments.

And in the middle of it, my hand found a scrap of paper I'd pulled absently from my coat pocket, and I stopped breathing.

It was a page from the prophecy book. I must have torn it out the day I found my death date in it — I barely remembered doing it, the way you don't remember swallowing. The old vellum, the century-old hand. My hand. My shorthand, aged a hundred years, written in the margins around a date that had already tried to kill me once.

And beneath the date, in that impossible familiar script, a line I had never let myself read closely:

*What the taster loses in fleeing, she carries instead. The fire does not live in books.*

"Sefa," I whispered. "I wrote this."

"A hundred years before you were born, yes, we've established the headache."

"No — I mean —" I pressed my palm flat against the page. "Look at the margin note. Look at what it says I lose. My mother's face. It knew. The hour taking it wasn't a price I paid. It was a step I took. Someone walked this road ahead of me and wrote down the directions."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the rain thinned toward that grey hour that isn't night or morning.

"Then whoever wrote it," she said finally, "wanted you to survive this part. To read this line *after* you'd paid, so you'd know it wasn't punishment. It was passage." She tapped the last phrase. "*The fire does not live in books.* What does that mean, Ada? What fire?"

And I thought of Vael. The door in the guild's back rooms, the dying city behind it, the one guttering dragon-flame that kept an erased nation from unraveling entirely. The fire Corvane had fed with decades of stolen years. The fire the Crown wanted weaponized, if Hale's questions meant anything.

"It lives in people," I said. "That's what it means. It has to live in people."

Sefa looked at me, and I watched her arrive at the same arithmetic I was arriving at, and I watched her hate it.

"The old woman in Vael," she said quietly. "The one who called you by a name you'd never heard. Ada. What was the name?"

I opened my mouth to say *I don't remember* —

And stopped. Because I did remember. The memory had been sitting there all along, untouched by the hour, too strange to lose: an old woman with river-grey eyes gripping my wrist in a dying city, saying a word that was not Ada Thackeray, saying it like a key turning in a lock.

"Vessarin," I said. "She called me Vessarin. Last of the line."

The lamp flame bent, though there was no wind, and somewhere far away — behind a door that no longer had a building around it — something ancient and patient took one breath closer to going out.

"We have to go back through," I said. "Before it's too late. Before Corvane, before the Crown, before the fire dies. Tonight, if we can."

Sefa began repacking the satchel.

"Then we'd better find a door," she said. "And Ada — whatever it costs this time — decide now what you're willing to pay. Because the doors have learned your prices."

I touched my own cheek, briefly, where a face I could no longer see had once kissed me goodbye.

"I already have," I said.
