## 13. Heir of a Burned Map

The Vael door had always felt like stepping into a held breath. Today it felt like stepping into a dying one.

I came through with my satchel pressed to my chest—ink, cipher notes, the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth like a wound I couldn't stop touching—and the smell hit me first. Not rot. Vael didn't rot; impossible things don't have the decency to decay on schedule. It was the smell of a feast kept warm too long, bread and woodsmoke and something floral I couldn't name, all of it thinning, as if the whole country were a candle burning down inside a sealed jar.

The city spread below the door's threshold: white towers gone grey at the seams, streets swept clean by people who refused to let a country die untidy. And above it all, in what I gathered had once been the royal sanctum, burned the dragon-fire.

I say *fire*, but that word promises more than it delivered. It was a column of light the color of banked coals, and while I watched, it stuttered. The whole city dimmed with it—lamps, windows, faces—and then rallied, the way a tired man rallies when he doesn't want the doctor called.

"Third time this hour," said a voice behind me. "You're counting too."

The old woman from before stood in the doorway of a house whose door was painted the exact blue of the guild's back-room doors. She'd called me a name last time. *Sarazen.* It had lodged under my ribs like a fishbone and I'd spent two days trying to cough it out.

"I count everything," I said. "Occupational hazard. I taste things for a living."

"You taste," she said, "and you keep accounts. And you read dead languages in tasters' shorthand." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile, but it was an honest one, which is rarer. "Come in, Sarazen. The fire won't gutter faster for our talking, and there's tea. Well. What we call tea. Don't ask what's in it. You'd only want to write it down."

* * *

Her house smelled of the same flowers as the city, and now I saw why: bundles of them hung from every beam, drying. Memory-herbs, the tasters called them—a poisoner's curiosity, because they were one of the few things that resisted the tongue. You couldn't taste what they did.

"You can't leave," I said, sitting at her table because my legs had opinions of their own. "Corvane told me. Cross the door without the fire's leave and you forget yourselves."

"He tells it like a lock," the woman said. "It's more like a debt. Vael kept us alive when the maps burned—the fire paid our existence out of its own account, you understand. Every year we've lived here is borrowed. Walk out carrying the loan and the ledger collects: your name first, then your mother's face, then everything down to the way you hold a spoon. What leaves this place is what the fire has already paid for." She set a cup in front of me. "Which is why it is very interesting that *you* walked in breathing."

"The fire knows me?"

"The fire has been calling your name for a hundred years. You just got here late."

I drank the tea. It tasted like plums and like an apology. "Tell me," I said. "All of it. I keep records. I'd rather have the true account than a comfortable one."

"Ah." She sat across from me, hands folded like a woman settling in for the long version of a story she'd told herself many times alone. "Then I'll give it to you the way it happened, not the way it hurts less.

"A century ago, Vael was not erased. Vael was *conquered*—there's a difference, though the Crown's cartographers worked hard to blur it. They couldn't take the dragon-fire, so they did the next best thing: they took us off the paper. Unwrote the borders, unwrote the treaties, unwrote the name from every registry their ink could reach. A country that exists nowhere on record cannot make claims. My grandmother watched her marriage certificate go blank in her hands."

"And the fire?"

"The fire is Vael's memory. As long as it burns, we are somewhere, even if the somewhere isn't on any map. But a fire needs feeding, and the dragons are gone, and so the last matriarch of the royal line made a bargain through a young Guild of Impossible Things—yes, Corvane's guild, though he wasn't yet born; he inherited the debt along with the ledgers. The bargain bought the country time. And it hid the heir."

My mouth had gone dry, which for a professional taster is practically a confession.

"There was a child," she said gently. "Last of the line. An infant when the unwriting came. The matriarch sent her through the doors—not to hide her *from* the Crown, child. To hide her from the prophecy. There was a book, even then. Written by a hand that hadn't been born yet. It named the deaths of everyone who would ever matter to Vael, and the matriarch believed—if no one knew who the heir was, the book couldn't find her."

"But it found the others."

"It found everyone." For the first time her voice cracked, just slightly, like ice over deep water. "Tasters. Doorkeepers. One by one, on their written dates, always of poison never served. The fire fed on grief as much as years, do you understand? Every death the prophecy collected made the flame burn a little lower, because those were lives Vael had promised to protect. We watched our own book eat us. And the heir—" she looked at me, and her eyes were doing something I didn't consent to, some old ceremony of recognition I wanted no part of— "the heir went into another country's kitchens and became invisible. Became safe. Became a nobody who tasted other people's food and survived poisons like a woman walking through rain without getting wet."

"The matriarch's daughter," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "What happened to her?"

"She took a servant's name in the kitchens of a foreign court. She bore one child. She died on her written date, of a poison never served—which is to say, she died in childbirth, which is the poison every mother swallows without tasting. And the child was sold north as an orphan, because a man named Corvane came looking for exactly such a child, having read in a certain book exactly what such a child was worth."

The room tilted. I gripped the table. Somewhere in the walls, or the world, the dragon-fire stuttered again and the lamplight dipped, and in the dip I saw my own face reflected in my tea, and under the reflection, faint as watermark, another face—an old one, mine in a century, mid-sentence, writing.

*Sarazen.* The name settled onto me like a coat cut for someone else. Heavy in the shoulders. Someone else's tailoring.

"No," I said, and heard how small it came out. "No, you've got the wrong ledger. I'm a taster. I'm nobody. That was the entire point of me—I made myself unrecorded, unclaimed, *uninteresting*. Nobody poisons a nobody. Nobody hunts a nobody. I built my whole life on being the person history walks past."

"Yes," she said, terribly kind. "And it worked beautifully, for exactly as long as no one read the book. But Sarazen—the prophecy never had to *find* you. It wrote you. You were born inside its pages. Invisible isn't the same as free. You weren't hidden from the account, child. You were hidden *in* it."

I want to tell you I raged. That I threw the cup, that I denied her, that I did anything dramatic enough to be worthy of the moment. What I actually did was sit very still and run the arithmetic, because that is what I am *for*. 

A taster raised by a guildmaster who bought orphans. A guildmaster who ordered one page of a prophecy burned rather than explained. A death date written a hundred years before my birth, in my own hand. A name in a dead language that an old woman recognized on sight. Add the columns. Sum them.

The sum did not change no matter how many times I checked it.

"So everything I am," I said finally, "the tasting, the surviving, the ledgers, the loneliness—it was all provision. Maintenance on an heir I didn't know I was. He wasn't raising a child. He was keeping a vessel dry until it was needed."

"Corvane loved you," the old woman said. "Don't mistake that for nothing. Love and ownership grow in the same soil more often than anyone wants to admit."

"That's worse," I said. "If he'd merely used me, I could hate him cleanly. Hate is simple accounting. This—" I pressed my palms against my eyes. "This is a debt with interest. Everything kind he ever did, I have to re-price. Every lesson. Every meal we shared after service. Was that affection or *inventory*?"

She let me sit with it. She seemed practiced at letting people sit with terrible things; a century of a dying country will do that.

When I could speak again, I asked the question underneath all the others. "The fire. How low is it?"

"Lower than I've ever seen." All pretense left her voice. "Three generations ago it would burn a full day between feedings. Now it gutters by the hour. When it goes out, Vael doesn't explode, child. Vael simply stops having ever been. Every borrowed year gets recalled at once. Everyone in these streets becomes a person who never lived, in a place that never existed. No grave. No mourning. Not even absence, because absence requires a prior presence." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever been offered. "We will not even be forgotten. Being forgotten is a kind of being remembered wrong. This is worse than that."

"And the people here—they know?"

"They've known for a hundred years. You learn to live toward an ending. It focuses the mind wonderfully. We plant gardens we won't harvest. We teach children songs for no reason except that the songs deserve to exist while we do." She looked at the window, where the light had gone coal-colored again, and her hands tightened on the table. "There is one way out, and you already suspect it, because you're a keeper of accounts and the answer is always in the ledger. The fire must be fed a life freely given. Given, Sarazen. Not taken, not traded, not bargained. Given."

"And if it isn't given?"

"Then it takes everything anyway, later, and gives nothing back."

* * *

She walked me up through the city toward the sanctum, because I asked to see it. I don't fully know why. Perhaps because I've never trusted an account I haven't inspected personally. Perhaps because some stubborn, stupid part of me thought that if I stood close enough to my inheritance, it would feel like mine instead of like a sentence.

The streets were quiet in the way of places where everyone is awake and pretending otherwise. Doors opened a crack as we passed. I felt eyes like fingertips. Word travels fast in a dying city when a stranger walks in wearing the old blood in her face—or maybe they could all simply tell. Maybe the fire announces its own.

In the sanctum, the dragon-fire rose from a basin of black stone that had been carved, I realized upon closer look, with writing. Ledger-lines. The oldest accounts I had ever seen, going down into the stone further than I could read, entries in a script older than the unwriting. The first book of Vael, carved where no conqueror could burn it.

Up close, the fire made a sound. I hadn't expected that. It sounded like breathing—shallow, quick, the breathing of someone saving their strength.

I put my hand near it—not in, just near—and it leaned toward me. Like a cat. Like something starving that has learned not to hope.

"Don't," the old woman said softly. "Not unless you mean it. It remembers touch. If you offer and withdraw, it will spend what it has left reaching after you."

I withdrew my hand. It cost me more than I can explain.

"It's not a gift," I said, staring into the coaling light. "Everyone keeps saying it like it would be. *Heir of Vael*, like a title on a document, like something you'd want. But it isn't a gift. It's a bill. A hundred years of borrowed lives, handed to the one person too late-born to have agreed to any of it. I didn't ask to be hidden. I didn't ask to be found. I didn't ask for any page of this."

"No," she agreed. "You didn't."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk out that door and live, and we end, and you carry it. That's what freedom means here, child: choosing which weight becomes yours. Nobody escapes the scale. You only choose what gets weighed."

I laughed. I couldn't help it—something in me broke loose and laughed, ugly and short. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me since Corvane taught me to read a balance sheet."

"He learned it somewhere," she said.

* * *

We came down from the sanctum as the light failed again—longer this time, the rally slower—and I stood in her blue doorway watching a city hold its breath, and I thought about invisibility. About the whole architecture of my life built on the premise that unseen equals safe. I had been so proud of it. The perfect taster. The blank page. And the whole time I was the central entry in the oldest book on the shelf, my death pre-written by my own hand, my hiding place itself a clause in somebody's contract.

Invisible isn't free. It's just a debt no one's come to collect yet.

"I need to get back through the door," I said. "There's a man I have to have words with. And a book I have to finish reading. And—" I touched my chest, where the fishbone-name sat, where something else sat beneath it, patient as embers. "And apparently a country."

"Sarazen." She stopped me with a hand on my arm, and her grip was stronger than a century should allow. "The door between here and your side—it narrows as the fire does. Soon it will only open from one direction. If Corvane has the master ledger, he doesn't need the door open wide. He needs it open *once*."

"Why once?"

"Because a door that opens once can bring anything through. An army. A chain. Or—" her eyes went to the sanctum's glow, "—a fire, carried out by force instead of given freely. Taken flame dies in a thief's hands. But that has never stopped a desperate man from stealing it."

I went through the door with the taste of plums and apology still in my mouth, and stepped out onto Ember Lane into air that smelled wrong.

Smoke. Not hearth-smoke. Ledgers burning—the specific acrid tang of vellum and binding-glue and a hundred years of careful handwriting going up.

And beneath the smoke, drifting down the lane from the guildhouse steps, a voice I would have known anywhere, raised in the tone he saved for closing a bad deal:

"—then we have terms, Inquisitor. The door for the pardon. And I'll need the girl delivered to me alive. She's the key."
