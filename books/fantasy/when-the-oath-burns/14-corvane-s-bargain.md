## 14. Corvane's Bargain

They tell you, when you take your first apprentice, that love is the one thing you must never put in a ledger. I was young when I learned otherwise. Love is the only entry that never balances. Everything else—hours, names, years, grief—you can debit against something. Love just accrues.

My name is Corvane. I have been guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things for forty-one years, and I would like it noted, before the inquisitors finish their arithmetic, that I never once lied to the Crown. I simply declined to volunteer. There is a difference. The difference is a door.

The Crown's man arrived on Ember Lane at dusk, which was either an insult or a compliment. Dusk is when the guild does its best work, and sending someone at that hour said: *we know when you're awake.* He came without soldiers. He came with a writ, a wax seal the size of a plum, and the particular smell of a man who has recently burned something and enjoyed it.

I let him wait in the counting room for exactly as long as it took me to finish logging a debt. A widow in the Salt Quarter owed us a memory of her wedding; she'd traded it for her husband's last hour back from a fever that had no business letting him go. I wrote the entry in my own hand, because the master ledger does not accept copies, and because I have never trusted anyone else's pen near that book. Not even hers.

Especially not hers.

"You keep him waiting," the man said when I entered. Not a question. Inquisitors of the Third Seal are trained never to ask questions they can't afford answered.

"He keeps himself waiting," I said. "I merely decline to interrupt. Wine?"

"I don't drink with traffickers."

"Then you'll have water, and we'll both pretend the distinction matters." I poured two glasses of the good red anyway, because I have found that a refused glass sits on the table between two men like a small accusation, and I wanted him to carry the accusation for once.

He did not touch it. He set the writ down instead, and I read it while he watched my face, which is a habit of theirs—watching for the flicker. I have been watched by better. I have been watched by kings' tasters who can detect hemlock at four parts in a thousand, and I raised the best of them myself, so believe me when I tell you: I know how to have no face at all.

The writ was simple. The Crown had become aware—through channels it would not name, though I could have named them; I keep accounts of everything, including other people's betrayals—of an organization trading in "temporal and geographical impossibilities." The Crown wished to acquire one such impossibility. A door. A specific door. The one that opens onto a country that does not exist.

"They want Vael," I said.

"The Crown wants *an asset*," he said. "Vael is the word you use."

"Vael is the word that's true. I find that helps, on the rare occasions I bother."

"Guildmaster Corvane." He leaned forward, and I will grant him this: he had prepared. "You are facing charges of fraud, trafficking in stolen time, unlicensed cartography, and—" he consulted the writ with theatrical care— "seventeen counts of murder by omission."

"Eighteen," I said. "You've missed one. It was very tidy; I'd hate for it to go unrecorded."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not fear. Appetite. Inquisitors are just auditors with a gallows, and every auditor loves a discrepancy.

"The Crown," he said, "is prepared to be generous. Full pardon. Sealed records. And in exchange—"

"You want the door, the key, and the ledger."

"The ledger is not on the table."

"The ledger is *always* on the table," I said. "That's what makes it a ledger. The only question is whose hand writes in it. You're offering me a pardon written in your ink. I'm telling you the book only takes mine."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him do the arithmetic every inquisitor eventually does: *is this man a monster, or is he a patriot with poor paperwork?* The answer, for the record, is neither. But I let him wonder.

"Forty years," he said. "That's what our sources suggest. Forty years of feeding something on Ember Lane. What have you been feeding, Corvane?"

And there it was. The question I had been waiting forty years for someone with the authority to hear the answer to.

I poured myself the second glass after all. Some stories you don't tell sober.

* * *

Let me tell you what the ledgers will not show, because the ledgers are hers and I kept mine separately, in the one place no auditor ever thinks to look: a man's memory.

Vael did not fall. That is the first lie in every history book, and I have read them all, the ones that survived the burning. Vael did not fall, was not conquered, was not drowned or starved or plague-taken. Vael was *erased*. There is a difference between dying and being unwritten, and the difference is that the dead at least get a date.

A century ago, the Crown of that era looked at a small mountain country with a dragon at its heart and a treaty it had signed in good faith and decided that both were inconvenient. You cannot conquer a nation whose fire can melt your fleets. So you do the other thing. You buy the maps. You buy the chroniclers. You buy the guilds that trade in doors, and you buy the one thing no one thinks to sell: the memory of the country itself, from every mind that holds it. And then you close the book.

They missed one guild. Mine. Or rather, my predecessor's, and before her his, all the way back to a woman named Sera Vael who understood that a country cannot be unwritten so long as one ledger still records it. The door on Ember Lane is not a door to Vael. The door to Vael is *gone*—there is no Vael to have a door to. The door on Ember Lane is a door to the *account*. To the country as it was written down, preserved, held in escrow. A nation in trust.

And a nation in trust requires a fire. That is the terrible arithmetic nobody warns you about. The dragon at Vael's heart did not survive the erasure—dragons are too large, too real, too *map-worthy* to fit inside a ledger. What survived was its fire, banked down to a single hearth in a single square in a single city that exists nowhere. And a fire, however holy, however patient, consumes.

The first guildmaster fed it her remaining years. She had forty and gave thirty, and lived long enough to train her successor and die at the age of what the arithmetic claimed was eleven. The second fed it memories. The third fed it names—his own, mostly, because a man's name is the cheapest thing he owns and the most expensive to lose. By the time the office reached me, we had learned to be more efficient. The guild trades in impossible things. Years are impossible things. Grieving people will pay years—*their own* years, freely given, signed and sealed—to buy back one hour with the dead. And a year freely given, freely traded, freely *surrendered*—that is the finest fuel a sacred fire ever burned.

I did not invent the system. I want that written somewhere. I inherited it, the way you inherit a debt, which is to say: completely.

But I did perfect it. And for forty-one years, every stolen hour that passed through my counting room, every borrowed year, every name sold before its owner had earned it—one tithe of each went through that door, into that square, into that fire. And Vael did not die. Vael *waited*. Its people walked their streets and married and quarreled and buried their dead inside a country the size of a memory, lit by a fire the size of a candle, and every one of them believed—because I made sure the guild believed, because I made sure *I* believed—that someday the door would open wide, the erasure would be undone, and they would walk out onto real grass under a real sky.

That was the love. There. I've said it. Write it down. I loved them. I never met one of them until recently, and I loved every soul in that impossible city, the way you love a debt you intend to honor. Forty years of tithes. Forty years of arithmetic in service of a promise.

And then, three years ago, I bought an orphan.

* * *

The inquisitor had stopped pretending not to drink. He held the second glass and hadn't touched it, which is its own kind of confession, and when I reached the part about the orphan his knuckles went pale around the stem.

"The taster," he said. "Ada Thackeray."

"You know the name."

"The Crown knows the name. The Crown has known it for some time. There is a file." He said the word *file* the way priests say *sacrament*. "It predates the taster's employment by decades. Would you like to know what's in it?"

"I wrote the original," I said, and watched him blink. "Whatever's in yours, mine is longer. Shall I save us both the theater?"

He gestured: *proceed.* Inquisitors love being lectured. It gives them time to think of the question that will end you.

The prophecy is a hundred years old. I know because I have handled it, catalogued it, and on one occasion—forgive me—slept with it under my pillow like a lovesick apprentice. It surfaced in a seized library, which is to say it *surfaced*, because books that want to be found have always known how. A hundred years ago, someone with a taster's training and a cipher only a taster could read wrote down a list of deaths. Dates. Names. Manners of dying. Every royal food-taster for a century, laid out in advance like courses at a banquet.

And at the end of the list, a name not yet in use, a date not yet arrived, and a manner of death that made my hands shake the first time I read it: *burning, from within.*

"Do you know what I did when I read that?" I asked the inquisitor.

"Burned it."

"I bought a child." I let that sit. "Every taster on that list died on their written date, of a poison never served. Do you understand what that means? It means the prophecy is not a prediction. It's a *schedule*. And a schedule can be kept—or missed. The guild's oldest law, the one Sera Vael carved into the doorframe before they erased her country: *every impossible thing must be recorded, or it unravels.* A recorded death is a death with a door left open. An unrecorded death—" I spread my hands. "Well. I have spent forty years learning what unravels and what holds. So I found the child the prophecy named, before the prophecy's name existed, and I bought him from a workhouse that had no idea what it was selling, and I raised him to be a taster. The finest taster the Crown has ever employed. A body so full of survived poisons that no written death could find a purchase. A shield made of immunity, walking around in a boy who thought I'd saved him out of kindness."

The inquisitor was very still. "You bought him to break a prophecy."

"I bought him to *spend* a prophecy. There's a difference, and the difference is the ledger." I stood, because some parts of this are better standing, and went to the shelf where the master ledger lives behind three locks and a silence. I did not open it. I only rested my hand on the cover, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you no longer entirely trust. "The prophecy says the taster dies burning from within. It does not say *when*. Dates in that book are suggestions, inquisitor—I've had a century to learn its grammar. A death can be postponed. A death can be *transferred*. And a fire that is guttering, a fire that has eaten forty years of traded time and is hungry still—a fire like that will accept a substitute, if the substitute is offered in the right ledger, with the right hand, in the right ink."

"You mean to feed him to it." His voice had gone flat. Inquisitors go flat when they've found the thing they came for.

"I mean to *keep a promise*," I said, and I will swear before any court, any god, any ledger, that it was the truth. "Ada Thackeray is the last heir of Vael. The old woman in the square has confirmed it; the blood confirms it; the fire *leans toward him* when he walks through the door, the way a hearth leans toward its own wood. The prophecy's author knew it too—knew it a century ago, wrote it down, sent the book forward through the doors so that someone would read it and act. I acted. Every year since, the fire has burned lower, and every year I have fed it what I could beg, borrow, or buy. But tithes are running out. The guild is scattered. And the Crown—" I turned to face him fully. "The Crown is at my door offering pardons, which means the Crown has finally worked out that a country-sized asset is sitting behind a door on Ember Lane, and the Crown wants to *weaponize* it. You want the dragon-fire. You want to bottle a nation's hearth and point it at your enemies."

He did not deny it. I'll say that for him. He had the decency to be silent, which in an inquisitor is a scream.

"So here is my bargain, and it is the only one on any table. I give the Crown the door. I give the Crown the key, the approach, the ritual, the whole liturgy—I will teach your artificers to hold it open. In exchange: my pardon, my records sealed, and the master ledger recognized in law as *mine*, with all its powers of entry, transfer, and erasure. The Crown gets its weapon. I get the one book in the world that can write a death out of existence—and I will use it to transfer a certain taster's written death into the fire, where it was always meant to burn, and close the account of Vael with its heir's name written in the margin as *paid in full*."

"And the people of Vael?" he asked. And I will grant him this: he asked it like a man who wanted the answer to be clean.

"The people of Vael," I said, "will finally be free of me."

He studied me for a long moment. "You love them," he said. Not kindly. The way you'd say *you're bleeding*.

"Of course I love them," I said. "I have paid for them for forty years. Love is just a debt you keep renewing." I took the writ from the table, read it once more, and signed the acceptance clause in my own hand, in my own ink, because the ledger only takes mine and I had grown tired of pretending otherwise. "The door opens at dawn. Bring your artificers. Bring your chains, if it makes the Crown feel official. And bring a strong box for the ashes, because there will be ashes, and the Crown will want proof."

"And the taster?"

"Ada Thackeray," I said, "is already on his way to the fire. He simply doesn't know it yet. He thinks he's going to save them." I smiled, and I am told that when I smile, men who have faced dragons have looked away. "That's the cruelest part, inquisitor. He'll walk in willingly. I raised him to."

He gathered his papers and left without finishing the wine, and I sat alone in the counting room with the master ledger under my hand, feeling its weight the way you feel a sleeping child's—precious, warm, and utterly unaware of what you've decided.

Because here is the entry I have never written, the one that lives only in me, the one that would unbalance every column if it were ever recorded: I do not want the pardon. I do not want the Crown's weapon, or its seal, or its gratitude. I have spent forty years feeding a fire other people's years because I could not bear to feed it mine, and the bargain I have just struck is not a betrayal of Ada Thackeray at all.

It is a rescue. Mine.

The fire is guttering. The tithes are spent. And a life freely given—*freely*, the old law is very strict—is the only coin left that will keep Vael's hearth alive through the winter of the world. I have arranged for the coin to arrive. I have arranged it so carefully, for so long, that the boy himself will carry it through the door in his own chest and thank me at the end.

I blew out the lamp. Behind the three locks, the master ledger waited, patient as a grave, and I thought I heard—though I am an old man and the hour was late—the faintest sound from beyond the Ember Lane door. A fire, settling. A city, breathing. A country, waiting for its heir.

And somewhere in the dark of the city above the guild, footsteps on wet cobbles: the taster, running toward the door I'd left unlocked, running toward the flame, running exactly as I had written him.

Every ledger balances in the end. I only ever asked to choose the date.
