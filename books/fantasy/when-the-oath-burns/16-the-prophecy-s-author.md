## 16. The Prophecy's Author

The guild's deepest door was not on any floor of the guild. That was the first thing I had to unlearn about it.

Corvane had told me once—years ago, in one of his teaching moods, when he'd been almost kind—that a guild like ours needed three kinds of doors. Doors for trade, doors for escape, and one door for apology. He'd never said what we were apologizing to. I'd assumed it was a joke. Corvane's jokes were always furniture for something heavier, but I'd been young enough then to laugh at the right moments and call it loyalty.

Now I stood in the ash of Ember Lane with soot still under my fingernails from the raid, Hale's warning ringing in my ears—*your superiors want the door weaponized, Ada, do you understand what that means?*—and I understood that the apology door had never been a joke at all. It was an admission. Someone in this guild's long, crooked history had done something so unforgivable that they'd built a door specifically for going back and saying so.

The widow had given me more than passage out of my cell. She'd given me the phrase, pressed into my palm on a scrap of paper that smelled of tallow and grief: *Third cellar down, where the stairs forget to end.* The tasters who'd scattered after the raid had found their ways back to me in ones and twos, the way embers find each other in a dead fire if you breathe on them right. Merrin came first, then the twins, then old Sefa with her burned hands wrapped in honey-soaked linen. They'd voted—not Corvane-style voting, where the outcome was decided before the question, but actual voting, which felt scandalously new—that I should go through.

"You're the heir," Sefa said, as if that settled anything.

"I'm a food taster with a prophecy problem," I said.

"Same thing," said Merrin, "apparently," in the tone of someone who'd spent two weeks recalibrating her entire understanding of me and had decided to be annoyed about it rather than awed. I appreciated that. Awe is very hard to work alongside.

So: third cellar down, beneath the guild's ruined bones, where the stairs forgot to end.

* *

I counted steps because counting is what I do when I'm frightened. It's why I survived the arsenic—the poisoner's hand shakes on the fourth measure, every time, and if you're counting you feel it. I got to four hundred and twelve before the stairs stopped pretending to be architecture and started being something else. The stone under my feet went warm. Then it went soft, the way ground goes soft near a hearth you've slept beside your whole life, and the darkness ahead wasn't darkness anymore. It was firelight, old and orange and patient, coming up through cracks in the air itself.

The door, when I found it, was not a door. It was a doorway with nothing around it, standing free in the cavern like a frame someone had pulled off a painting and kept the painting's absence instead. Through it: a room. A small room. A desk. A lamp burning something that wasn't oil—I could smell it from here, dragon-fire and beeswax and underneath both, faintly, the specific bitterness of ink made from burned names.

And at the desk, writing.

I want to tell you I strode through with the dignity of a lost heir confronting her destiny. What I actually did was stand there for the better part of an hour rehearsing opening lines. *Hello. I appear to be you. Is this a trap?* All of them sounded insane. Eventually my feet decided they were tired of waiting for my mouth and walked me through.

The room smelled like my childhood. Not the kitchens—something older, something from before the kitchens, from a time I'd thought memory had eaten entirely. Woodsmoke and a particular flower I couldn't name and milk warmed with honey. My chest did something complicated.

The woman at the desk did not look up.

She was old. Older than Sefa, older than anyone I'd ever seen, with white hair braided back in the Vaelish style I'd only learned existed three weeks ago, and her hands—her hands were mine. I knew them the way you know your own reflection: the scar across the left index finger from Corvane's first knife lesson, the burn mark on the thumb from the night I'd tested a wine laced with fire-salts and gotten greedy. A century hadn't changed them. Time, apparently, has no jurisdiction over hands that have already died on schedule.

She wrote. I watched. Her pen moved in the tight, economical shorthand of the tasting guild—my shorthand, the cipher I'd spent sleepless nights cracking—and the letters dried black as burnt sugar.

"You're writing it now," I finally said. "You've been writing it the whole time. Every copy. Every version."

"The whole time," she agreed, without looking up. Her voice was mine too, worn down to gravel. "Every version. Sit down, Ada. You'll want to sit for this. I remember wanting to stand, and I remember regretting it."

There was only one chair. Of course there was. I sat on the floor, cross-legged, like a kitchen girl waiting for scraps, which was arguably more honest than the chair.

"How long have you been writing?"

"Longer than the book is old." Now she looked up, and I met my own eyes a century deep, and neither of us flinched, which I suppose is the one mercy of being raised invisible—you learn early how to hold a gaze without giving anything away, even to yourself. "The book isn't a record of the future, child. It's a letter. I've just had a very long time to write it, and you've all had a very long history of misreading it."

"It killed four tasters," I said. "It named their dates and their poisons and it was right every time."

"It reported their deaths." She set down her pen, and the gesture had a finality to it, like a door closing somewhere far away. "It didn't cause them. Do you know why prior tasters died of poison never served? Think. You've thought about little else since chapter three of your own misfortunes."

I had, actually. I'd turned it over every night like a coin I couldn't stop biting. "Because once they read their date, they stopped trusting anything. They refused dishes. They refused wine. They hid. And the Crown—" I felt it land even as I said it, ugly and simple. "The Crown doesn't tolerate a taster who won't taste. If you won't drink what's poured, they find a way to pour it into you anyway. The prophecy didn't kill them. Fear did. Fear, wearing the prophecy's face."

"Fear wearing the Crown's hand," she corrected gently. "Let's assign blame accurately. It matters. Accuracy matters more in this room than anywhere else in the world, because this room is where accuracy goes to be written." She tapped the page in front of her. "I wrote their deaths because I'd watched them die. From here, the past is a corridor with doors on both sides. I can open any of them. I can write what I saw through each one. But I cannot change what I see, and I cannot step through. Do you understand what that means?"

"That the loop can't be broken."

"No." She smiled, and it was such a strange thing, seeing my own smile on a face that had earned it. "It means the loop was never closed. Everyone assumes a prophecy is a wall. It isn't. It's a window. Windows don't stop you from walking anywhere. They just show you what's on the other side of the glass. I sent the book back through the guild's doors because I wanted you—all of you, every Ada, every taster, every version—to *see*. To see and then to choose differently. That's all a warning is. A window held up by someone who's already walked into the wall."

"But the deaths came true anyway."

"Yes." The word cost her; I watched it cost her, watched my own face learn grief a century early. "Because everyone who read it treated it as a sentence instead of a map. They read *Ada Thackeray dies by poison on the ninth of Frostmonth* and they spent every day until the ninth of Frostmonth dying of it in advance, slowly, by inches, and then quickly, by decree. Nobody ever asked the next question."

"Which is?"

"Which is: *why does a prophecy written by a survivor describe a death?*" She leaned forward. Up close her eyes were clouded at the edges, cataract-pale, but the centers were sharp as needles. "I survived the poison, Ada. I'm sitting here, aren't I? I tasted everything the Crown ever poured and I lived, because I am the best taster the guild ever produced and I taught myself everything I know—which is to say, you taught yourself everything you know, and you are better than you think you are. The death in the book is real. I wrote it from memory. But the book ends mid-sentence for a reason. I ran out of time to finish it before the door took the manuscript back. The ending was supposed to say: *and she lives, and here is how.*"

The lamp flame bent, though there was no draft. Somewhere behind its light, I could feel the dragon-fire guttering, a heartbeat with too few beats left in it.

"The last line," I said slowly, "the one everyone treats as the doom-line. *When the oath burns, the heir will carry what remains.*"

"You've read it differently lately."

"Hale thinks it means the Crown will burn Vael's oaths of protection and take the flame by force. Corvane thinks it means he'll finally get what he's owed. I've been assuming it meant I die."

"And what do you think it means now?" she asked, and there was something almost playful under the gravity, the exact note I used myself when I wanted a student to arrive at the answer rather than be handed it. Corvane had stolen that trick from me a century before I invented it. The thought was dizzying. Everything I was had been borrowed from this room.

I thought about the ledgers. About debts paid in memories, hours sold to widows, years fed to a dying fire by a man who loved a country more than he loved anyone in it. About Corvane feeding the dragon-flame other people's lifetimes for decades because he could not bear to give his own. About an oath I'd sworn at nine years old with my hand on the master ledger: *to keep the accounts, to keep the secrets, to keep myself small.*

"When the oath burns," I said, and heard my voice come out steady, "it doesn't mean someone burns my oath *for* me. It means I burn it. Voluntarily. The ledger-keeping. The invisibility. The whole architecture of being safe by being nothing." My heart was doing something reckless and hopeful. "The prophecy isn't describing my death. It's describing my resignation."

The old woman laughed—a real laugh, rusty, delighted, the kind of sound I'd never once made in Corvane's presence. "A hundred years," she said, wiping her eye, "a hundred years and forty-one readings and you're the first one to get there in under a month. Yes. Yes, exactly that. The flame doesn't need a sacrifice, Ada. It needs a *keeper*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. Corvane feeds it stolen years because he thinks fire is a debt. The Crown wants to cage it because they think fire is a weapon. But fire is a *hearth*, if you have the courage to let it live inside you instead of inside your ledgers. It stops being a countdown and starts being warmth. I know because I've carried it. I carried it for ninety years before I built this room."

My throat was tight. "Then why does the book still name a death? Why leave that in?"

"Because taking it out would be a lie, and lies unravel faster than impossibilities—Corvane taught you that much, and for once he taught you true. I did die, Ada. On the ninth of Frostmonth, I drank the cup, and my heart stopped, and for eleven minutes I was dead as maps. And then the flame I'd carried for ninety years did what flames do when their keeper falls—it caught. It restarted me. The death is in the book because it happened. What couldn't fit before the door closed was the rest of the sentence." She picked up her pen again, dipped it in the name-black ink, and held it over the page, looking at me with my own eyes gone gentle with age. "*The ninth of Frostmonth is not your ending. It is the day the old Ada dies and the new one is born carrying fire. You will be afraid. Be afraid, and go anyway.*"

"Is that—is that the line? The missing ending?"

"It's a line," she said. "You'll write the rest yourself. That's the entire point, and the part everyone keeps failing to understand: I am not your fate. I'm your *first draft*. And you—" the pen touched paper, and the ink bloomed like a wound, like a flower, like dawn— "you were always meant to be the revision."

For a long moment neither of us wrote or spoke. The lamp breathed. Far away, the dragon-fire guttered, and I felt it answer something in my ribs, a second pulse, patient and enormous, waiting to be asked properly instead of seized.

"Can I ask you one more thing?" I said. "Something the book wouldn't dare record?"

"Those are the only questions worth asking."

"Was it worth it? Burning the ledgers. Losing Corvane. Ending up—" I gestured at the tiny room, the endless writing, the century of solitude with nothing but a lamp and a corridor of doors she could look through and never walk through. "Was any of it worth the price?"

She considered this with the seriousness I gave to a suspicious wine, and then she said, "I got to watch you figure it out. Through the doors. Every version of you, fumbling toward the same window. Do you know what I saw just now, when you sat down on the floor instead of taking the chair? I saw someone who finally knows the chair was never hers." She turned back to her manuscript. "Worth it. Ask me again on the ninth of Frostmonth, when you're the one answering. Now go. Your friends are about to be in a great deal of danger, and you have approximately one signature to forge."

The floor dropped out of my stomach. "What—how—"

"Corvane found them," she said, already writing, pen flying, the words of some future chapter drying black as ash. "He's taken them to the fire. He'll want the heirship signed over before dawn, and he'll put the ledger and the pen in front of you and make it look like mercy." She glanced up one last time, and her smile was sharp as my sharpest day. "Sign it, Ada. Sign it beautifully. And then teach him what happens to a transfer when the signature's a forgery—because a man who's spent sixty years forging other people's consent should recognize false handwriting when it ruins him."

* * *

I came up the stairs that forgot to end four steps at a time, the missing line of the prophecy beating in my chest alongside the ember-glow that had settled there without asking permission, warm as a hearth, patient as a promise. Above me, through the broken ceiling of Ember Lane, dawn was coming up the color of forged signatures, and somewhere between me and it, Corvane was putting pen and ledger in front of my friends and calling it kindness.

I had one signature to forge. I intended to make it my masterpiece.
