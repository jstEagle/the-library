## 17. What Loyalty Owes

There is a particular quality to being captured by someone who once taught you how to hold a teacup so the saucer wouldn't betray your nerves. It is not fear. It is not even betrayal, not anymore—betrayal was chapter seven, if my life had chapters, and gods know it seems determined to have them. It is something colder and more precise: the recognition that every kindness you were ever shown was a line item in an account you didn't know you were in.

Corvane had us trussed in the guild's own binding-ropes, which was almost funny. Almost. The ropes are made from the hair of women who outlived their own funerals, and they tighten when you lie. Mine had been squeezing my wrists for two hours now, which told me a great deal about what I'd been saying with my face.

We were in the counting room—the deep one, behind the door that only opens for debts—and he had arranged us around the table like a man setting a dinner party. Hale on one side, bleeding from a cut above his eye and radiating the furious dignity of a righteous man discovering his righteousness has been confiscated along with his weapons. Sera and the three tasters who'd come back for me, bound shoulder to shoulder like kindling waiting for a match. And me, in the chair at the head of the table, because Corvane had always liked me best, and because cruelty, done properly, is indistinguishable from affection right up until it isn't.

The master ledger sat between us.

I want you to understand what that book was, because I spent eleven years of my life pretending I didn't. Every impossible thing the Guild of Impossible Things had ever recorded, every debt paid in memories, every year sold twice, every name held in escrow against a future its owner hadn't earned yet. A century of Vael's slow strangulation, written down in Corvane's tidy hand, because he believed—I know he believed, this is the part that still makes me sick—that recording a thing was the same as keeping it safe.

He had fed the dragon-fire decades of traded years to keep Vael alive inside its erased borders. He had bought me as an orphan because a prophecy said I would die, and a dead heir cannot inherit. He had done all of it, I think, out of love. That is the worst sentence I have ever written, and I have written some truly awful ones.

"Ada." He opened the ledger to a page near the end, and turned it toward me, and there it was: the transfer contract, drafted in ink that moved slightly when you weren't looking at it directly. *The undersigned, sole surviving heir of the erased nation of Vael, does hereby convey all claims, titles, fires, and doors unto Guildmaster Emeric Corvane, freely and without coercion.*

*Without coercion.* The ink practically giggled when it wrote that.

"Sign," he said, "and your friends walk out of here."

* * *

Let me tell you about the arithmetic of a moment like that, because a food-taster's whole life is arithmetic. One gram too much and the ambassador dies; one gram too little and the king suspects the wine and the cellar-master loses his post. You learn to weigh things fast, and small, and without letting your hands shake.

Four friends' lives against one signature. Any reasonable person signs.

But here is what Corvane had forgotten, or perhaps never understood, about the girl he'd raised on arsenic micro-doses and ledger discipline: I had spent eleven years learning to read contracts the way other people read faces. Every trade the guild made passed through my bookkeeping. I knew what a valid transfer looked like. I knew the rules better than he did, because he'd written the rules and I'd written everything else, including—this is important—a set of margin notes in which a younger Ada had asked, in her careful invisible handwriting, *what happens if the signature is forged?*

And the answer, which Corvane himself had dictated to me over supper when I was fourteen, was this:

*A forged signature invalidates the transfer. Worse—it reverses it. The forger conveys their claim upon the thing to the true heir, doubled. Impossible things must be recorded honestly or they unravel, Ada. Honesty isn't sentiment. It's engineering.*

He had taught me the loophole himself. Over stew. When I was fourteen. I could have kissed him, and I mean that in the most violent possible sense.

So when he slid the pen across the table—an expensive pen, quill of a bird that had died famous—I took it, and I looked at my friends, and I did the thing that has gotten me through every banquet, every interrogation, every night of my strange double life:

I let him see exactly what he expected to see.

* * *

"You're hesitating," Corvane said. Not unkindly. That was always his genius—he could make ownership feel like patience. "Take your time. I'm not a monster, whatever the inquisition says about me."

"You're not," I agreed. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. The ropes at my wrists squeezed once, testing me, and I thought very carefully about nothing at all. "You're worse than a monster. Monsters don't keep receipts."

Hale made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"Ada," Sera said quietly. She was nineteen, the youngest of us, and she had been poisoned four times before her twentieth birthday, and she was looking at me with the specific terror of someone watching the last person who might save her do something stupid. "Don't."

"It's all right," I told her. And then, because the ropes punish lying and I needed my hands free eventually, I added the truth: "It really is. Watch me."

The pen scratched. I signed my name—Ada Thackeray, taster, orphan, heir of a burned map—in the flowing hand Corvane had drilled into me, and beneath it I pressed my thumb, because the old contracts wanted blood or thumbprint, and I have never been squeamish about either.

The ink drank the print. The page glowed faintly, the way guild paper does when it accepts a record. Corvane exhaled, and for one unguarded moment he looked like what he had always pretended to be: an old man relieved of a terrible weight.

"There," he said softly. "You see? No one had to be hurt. This is why I chose you, Ada. You understand that everything ends in the ledger. Everything ends in the writing down."

"Mm," I said.

He reached for the book.

"Now," I said, "before you take that—" and here is where I confess I enjoyed myself, just a little, just enough to feel guilty about later, "—you should check the seal."

His hand stopped.

"Guild law," I continued, conversationally, the way I used to recite poison symptoms while ladling soup, "section nine, the one you made me memorize because I kept asking questions at dinner. A transfer executed under duress requires verification of the signatory's free consent. Verification is performed by seal. Whose seal? Go on. Quiz me."

"Ada—"

"The signatory's own," I said. "Which means the seal must be applied by the signer, willingly, in the presence of no threat. Which mine wasn't. Because I signed it with your pen, under your ropes, with your knife at Sera's throat, and because—" I turned my wrist, showing him the rope-marks, red as a fresh wound, "—these haven't loosened. They tighten when the wearer lies, Emeric. They're still tight. What do you suppose that tells an honest piece of rope?"

He looked down at the ledger. The ink had begun to move again—not the gentle drift of settled record but the restless crawling of a document that knows it has been lied into. The letters of my signature were rearranging themselves, patiently, the way water rearranges itself around a stone, until they spelled something new.

Not *Ada Thackeray.*

*I, Emeric Corvane, keeper of the accounts, do hereby convey unto Ada Thackeray, true heir of Vael, all claims, titles, fires, and doors—doubled.*

* * *

I wish I could tell you he raged. I wish I could report a satisfying scene, furniture thrown, veins standing out in his neck. But Corvane had been a guildmaster for forty years, and guildmasters do not rage; they recalculate. I watched him do it—the flicker behind his eyes, the same look he got when a shipment of borrowed hours arrived short and he had to decide whom to bill for the difference.

"You learned that," he said at last, "from me."

"Everything dangerous I know is from you. It's practically your legacy."

"I could burn the ledger," he said. "Unmake the record. Start the transfer again, properly, with better conditions."

"You could try." I tilted my head toward the hearth, where a fire was burning that had nothing to do with coal. "But you wrote the rule yourself, old man. An unraveled contract doesn't reset. It reverses. Burn that page and you don't get a blank slate—you get a default judgment, and the judgment names you debtor." I smiled at him, and I am aware that smiling at Emeric Corvane while he held all the knives was possibly insane, but I had spent my whole life being invisible, and there is a particular intoxication in being seen saying exactly what you mean. "You built a machine that only runs on honesty. And then you tried to run it on me. You should have read your own margins."

For a long moment nobody breathed. Even the binding-ropes seemed to lean in.

Then Corvane laughed.

It was a horrible sound—genuine, rueful, almost fond—and somehow that was worse than raging. "Eleven years," he said. "I fed you, clothed you, taught you every cipher and counter-cipher in the trade. And you were building this the entire time."

"No," I said. "I was surviving the entire time. Building this took about four minutes."

He straightened. And I saw the calculation finish, saw him arrive at the answer I'd been dreading, the one I should have seen coming, because I had read his ledgers for a decade and I knew how the man thought when a door closed: he simply looked for another door.

Or, in this case, a fire.

"The contract stands reversed," he said quietly. "Fine. Then I don't need your signature anymore. I need the source. If the heirship flows to you doubled, Ada, then the fire will come to you when it dies—and I intend to be holding the cup when it pours." He snapped his fingers, and the ropes began hauling us upright, and the door to the counting room opened onto stairs going down, down, down, toward the deepest part of the guild, toward the place where the Vael door stood open and the dragon-fire beyond it guttered like a candle in a dying man's fist.

"Sera," I said, as we were dragged past each other. "Count to ninety after we leave. Then scream."

She blinked. "Scream?"

"You've heard me practice. You know I'm good. Make sure he hears yours."

Understanding moved across her face like weather. Nineteen years old, poisoned four times, and she grinned at me like a knife finding its sheath. "Ninety," she said. "Loud?"

"Louder than the widow."

* * *

They marched us down. Hale stumbled beside me, hands bound, and murmured, low enough that only I could catch it: "What happens in ninety seconds?"

"Something I paid for," I said. "In advance. With interest."

Because here is the part of the evening I haven't mentioned yet—the part I filed away weeks ago, in the quiet competent corner of my mind where I keep everything: the widow. The woman who bought the stolen hour back in chapter one of this disaster, the hour she spent sitting beside her son's empty chair. She owed the guild nothing after that. But she had come to us anyway, in the days after the raid, and pressed something into my palm, and said: *You gave me my hour honest. Debts go both ways or they aren't debts.*

What she gave me was a whistle carved from doorwood, and instructions, and a promise: *Blow it, and everyone who ever walked through one of our doors will know where you are. There are more of us than the ledgers say. There were always more of us than the ledgers said.*

I hadn't blown it. Not during the raid, not in the cell, not once. I'd been hoarding it the way I hoard everything—waiting for the moment of maximum return, because a taster learns early that you spend your antidotes when the poison is actually in the glass, not before.

Somewhere above us, Sera hit forty, and began, experimentally, to whimper.

Below us, the air grew warm and smelled of cinders and cinnamon, which is what Vael smells like when it's dying, and the light coming up the stairwell was the color of the inside of a coal. The last dragon-fire. The heart of a country that maps refused to remember. And somewhere in front of it, silhouetted against all that failing gold, Corvane was already reaching for the cup he'd carried down—lead-lined, rune-scored, big enough to hold a nation's soul if you poured carefully.

"Ninety," I whispered.

Above us, Sera screamed like the world ending.

And below us, in the dark of the doorway, something whistled back.
