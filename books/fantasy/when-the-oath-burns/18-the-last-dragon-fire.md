## 18. The Last Dragon-Fire

There is a particular quality to the air at the heart of a dying dragon-fire, and that quality is *expensive*. It tastes the way a rare vintage smells before you're allowed to drink it — every breath a withdrawal from an account you can never repay. I had tasted enough poisons to know the difference between something meant to kill you and something simply too vast for you, and Vael's fire was the second kind. It didn't want me dead. It wanted me *worthy*, which is infinitely worse.

The chamber beneath the ruined palace was round as a coin, walled in black stone shot through with veins of ember-light, like a heart seen through a ribcage. And there in the center, on its bed of ash and ancient bone-ivory braziers, burned the last flame of Vael.

It was smaller than I'd imagined. That's the thing nobody tells you about miracles: up close they are always disappointing in size. A fire no bigger than a hearth-flame, guttering in a draft only it could feel, throwing light that moved wrong — shadows leaning toward it instead of away, as if even darkness was homesick.

Old Sera stood at my left, her hand on my arm with a grip that said she'd been holding on to hope for a hundred years and had developed the muscles for it. Behind us came the tasters — Maren, quiet and steady; Osk, still favoring the ribs he'd cracked climbing out of the guild's collapse; little Wren, who was fifteen and should have been anywhere else in the world. Hale brought up the rear, inquisitor's grey gone to ash-grey, his ledger of carefully kept lies finally open in his hands where anyone could read it.

And ahead of us all, standing between me and the fire like a man who owned the deed to it, was Corvane.

He looked calm. That was the horror of him. Twenty years of raising me, teaching me letters and ledgers and the exact pressure of a thumb that stops a heartbeat, and I knew every weather of his face. This was his *satisfied* face. The one he wore when a trade closed exactly as written.

"You came," he said, and there was real warmth in it, which is the cruelest trick tenderness ever learned. "I told them you would. My Ada always follows the arithmetic to its end."

"I'm not your Ada."

"No," he agreed, pleasantly. "You're Vael's. That's rather the point." He gestured at the fire without looking at it, the way a man gestures at his own hearth. "You've worked it out by now, I assume. The flame can't be carried by force. It has to be *given*. A life freely offered — that was always the price of passage, the price of keeping it lit. Why do you think I spent forty years feeding it traded years? Borrowed decades from debtors who didn't read the fine print? Because I couldn't give my own. Not yet. Not until the work was done."

"The work," Sera said, and her voice was flint dragged over stone. "You call feeding our fire other people's lives *work*."

"I call it *bookkeeping*, madam." Corvane turned to her then, and for one unguarded moment I saw what was actually underneath him: not cruelty. Fear. Enormous, patient, century-deep fear. "Your country was erased. Do you know what that means, practically? It means every door out of Vael closes a little more each year. It means children born here who will never be remembered by anyone outside. I have kept this flame alive longer than any guildmaster before me. I have been *kind* about it. Ask anyone whose years I took whether I paid fair rates."

"They were not yours to buy!" Sera's hand tightened on my arm. "A country is not a shop."

"A country is precisely a shop," Corvane said, "and it has been in receivership for a hundred years. But you—" and here he turned back to me, and his voice went soft, went gentle, went *father*, and I hated how much of me leaned toward it like a plant toward a window. "You don't need to listen to any of this. You need only do what you were raised to do. Taste first. Choose wisely. Sign nothing you haven't verified." He held out the master ledger. "Or better — don't sign at all. Just stand aside. Let me feed the fire what it needs and take what it offers, and everyone walks out of here alive. Even the tasters. Even, if it comes to it, the inquisitor. I keep my accounts balanced, Ada. You know that better than anyone breathing."

"And what does it offer?" I asked. "When you feed it?"

"Immunity," he said simply. "Erasure works both ways. A man who owns the flame that keeps a nation alive can walk through any door the Crown builds, any cell the Crown locks. He becomes impossible to punish, because he becomes impossible to *record*." His eyes glittered. "No more inquisitors. No more dates written in ash. Just a man and his books, forever."

"That's not immortality," Maren said quietly behind me. "That's just loneliness with a longer budget."

But Corvane wasn't listening anymore. Corvane was watching the door.

*

They came down the stair in grey file, six inquisitors in their slate coats, boots striking the black stone in unison, and behind them — I felt my stomach drop through the floor and into whatever country sits beneath countries — behind them came the Crown's artificers carrying the containment engine. A cage of silver and glass, big enough for a fire the size of a hearth. Big enough for a miracle. Built, clearly, to hold one.

Hale moved before I did.

I will spend the rest of my life trying to understand what happened in the next four minutes, and I suspect the honest answer is that Hale didn't understand it either — that some part of him had been walking toward this moment since the day he'd shown me his own secret ledger and asked, in a voice stripped of all officialdom, *"Who taught you to lie this well?"* Two careful liars recognize each other. Two careful liars also, apparently, die for each other, though neither of us had put that clause in writing.

"Hale," Corvane said, without alarm. "Inquisitor. How nice. Your superiors send their regards, I'm sure."

"My superiors," Hale said, stepping forward until he stood alone in the space between us all, "want to put a country's soul in a box and study why it won't die." He drew himself up, and I watched him do the thing I'd only ever seen him do across interrogation tables: become entirely, weaponizedly *correct*. "By authority vested in me under the Edict of Recorded Things, I am seizing this asset on grounds of—"

"On grounds of what?" one of the inquisitors said. "You filed no warrant. You've been off the rolls since Ember Lane. You're nothing, Hale. You're a rumor with a badge."

"Yes," Hale said. "I know exactly what that's like." And he smiled — a thin, terrible smile I recognized from the banquet, from the wine, from every conversation where we'd each been counting the other's knives — and he threw his ledger into the fire.

Not the dragon-fire. There was a brazier near the wall, one of the old ivory ones, still holding a thread of heat, and he threw sixty pages of meticulously recorded impossibilities into it, and the paper caught, and the light came up gold across all our faces, and every inquisitor in the room understood at once what he had done. He had destroyed the evidence that could have convicted the Guild of Impossible Things. All of it. Every entry. Every name. Including, I realized with a lurch, the entries that could have convicted *me*.

"You're under arrest," Hale told the six inquisitors, "for trafficking in unrecorded assets. I'll be filing the paperwork presently. Assuming any of you survive the filing process."

They didn't laugh. Inquisitors never laugh at jokes made at their own expense. What they did was draw.

What followed was not a battle. Battles have shape, tactics, arcs. This was six people trying to get past one stubborn man in a narrow room, and one stubborn man discovering that a lifetime of rigid justice had built him into something load-bearing. He fought like an argument he intended to win on points. He used the braziers, the pillars, the fact that inquisitors are trained to fight in file and he had trained beside them and knew exactly which shoulder to strike to make the file break.

He also, I noticed with a coldness spreading through my chest, took hits he didn't have to take. He stepped into a blade aimed at the tasters' retreat. He put his body between Wren and a crossbow bolt and took it high in the shoulder, spinning, swearing with a fluency that suggested the inquisition's vocabulary training was more thorough than anyone credited. He fell, and got up, and fell again, and each time he got up slower.

"Hold the line," he told us, absurdly, formally, blood in his teeth. "That's — hng — that's an order from a man with no authority whatsoever."

"It's a terrible order," I said, moving toward him.

"It's the *only* order," he said, "because you—" and then a staff took him across the temple and he went down and stayed down, and the sound I made was not dignified and I have decided not to record what it was.

Sera's cry shook the chamber. Maren and Osk pulled Wren back against the wall, and the inquisitors regrouped, and Corvane — Corvane *watched*, with his satisfied face, because chaos served him. Chaos always serves the man holding the ledger.

"Enough," Corvane said, and the word landed like a stamp on wax. He walked past the fighting, past the bleeding, past Hale's prone body, as untouched as a man crossing his own front hall, and stopped before the fire. "Ada. Last chance. Stand aside, or watch me do this the expensive way."

"The expensive way being?"

"Being that the flame resists theft." He rolled up his sleeve, and I saw the marks there — dozens of them, fine white scars in a pattern I knew, because I'd catalogued them once in his private records without understanding. Taster's marks. He'd been tasting the fire for decades, small sips, building tolerance. Building *immunity*. "Every poison is a language, Ada. You taught me that. Or rather, I taught you, and you improved upon the lesson. This fire is a language too. And I have been studying my grammar for forty years."

"You fed it stolen years."

"I *invested* in it." He reached into his coat and drew out a phial — glass, stoppered, glowing faintly with a light that hurt to look at directly. Years. Decades of them, distilled from debtors' ledgers, from the widow's hour multiplied a thousandfold, from every unfair trade he'd ever called kindness. "One life freely given, the fire asks. Very well. I am giving it forty lives, freely *bought*, and the fire will simply have to learn to count the way I count."

"You can't forge a gift," Sera said.

"My dear woman," Corvane said, uncorking the phial, "you are standing in a country that was erased from existence by a signature. Everything is forged. The question is only who holds the pen."

He poured.

*

I have tasted everything. Arsenic dressed as honey. Hemlock in a bride's cup. Nightshade so refined it apologized on the way down. I thought I knew what it was to watch something terrible enter a body, but I had only ever known it from inside the body. Watching Corvane pour forty stolen years into a dying god's last heartbeat taught me the view from outside, and it is worse.

The fire *screamed*. Not in sound — in light. Every shadow in the chamber snapped upright, every vein of ember in the black walls flared white, and the flame itself leapt from hearth-small to the size of a doorway, roaring upward in colors that don't have names in any language that appears on maps. For one glorious, terrible moment it looked like it might work. Like the fire would take the payment, drunk and grateful, and Corvane would step into it and come out the other side unrecordable, unpunishable, free.

Then the fire counted.

I felt it happen the way you feel a floor tilt. Something in the chamber's deep arithmetic checked the ledger of the offering against the ledger of the giving, found the columns mismatched, and *rejected the entry*. The flame turned — there is no other word for it — turned on Corvane like a creditor.

He screamed then. Genuinely. Forty years of borrowed time began leaving him the way water leaves a broken dam, streaming off him in threads of pale light, and with every thread his face grew older, his posture bent, his beautiful satisfied expression collapsed into something naked and astonished. "No," he said. "No — I *paid* — I paid fair rates — I kept the accounts — Ada, I kept the *accounts*—"

"You forged the gifts," I said, and my voice sounded very far away. "You can't forge a gift. She told you. Everyone told you. You just never learned to read anything but numbers."

He looked at me across the burning space between us, and for a moment — one moment, I swear it — the fear underneath him showed all the way through, and it was the fear of a boy somewhere, long ago, who had been hungry, and invisible, and had sworn he would never be either again. "I did it for the work," he whispered. "Somebody had to keep it *lit*."

"I know," I said. And I meant it, and meaning it was the worst part. "Step back, Corvane. Please. Before it takes all of it."

He didn't step back. Of course he didn't. He lunged instead — both hands into the flame, grasping for it the way a drowning man grasps at light on water — and the fire took his purchase and gave back nothing, and he aged thirty years in three seconds, hair going white, going *gone*, skin mapping itself with a century of winters—

And the flame guttered.

All of it. The theft hadn't fed the fire; it had *spent* the fire, the way fraud spends a bank. The great roaring column shrank, and shrank, and settled, and when the light steadied again the last dragon-fire of Vael was no bigger than a candle.

Smaller than a candle. An ember, cupped in ash, breathing.

Dawn was coming. I could feel it in the stone, in the walls, in the way the ember-light veins along the ceiling dimmed toward grey. Above us, somewhere, the sun was rising over a city that existed on no map, and the sun did not care, and the sun would rise tomorrow over a city that existed nowhere at all unless—

"Ada." Sera's voice, very close, very quiet. Her hand found mine. Hers was cold. When had her hand gotten so cold? "The choice is now, child. There is no after this. There is only this."

I looked at the ember. I looked at Hale, unconscious, breathing shallowly, alive — barely, expensively alive, the way everything worth anything in this story had turned out to be. I looked at the tasters, my strange scattered family of professional survivors. I looked at the prophecy in my memory, my own handwriting aged a hundred years, the date, the manner of death — *fire, taken inward, freely* —

Oh, I thought. *Oh.* It was never a sentence. It was instructions.

The ember flickered. Outside, faintly, impossibly, through a hundred feet of black stone, I heard a cock crow in a city that had been waiting a hundred years for morning.

Everything, I thought. Everything staked on one choice.

I knelt before the last dragon-fire of Vael, and I opened my mouth to speak the words my older self had left me, and that was when Corvane — ruined, white-haired, half-unmade, dragging himself across the ash with the last of his borrowed strength — wrapped his fingers around my ankle.

"One trade," he rasped. "One last trade, Ada. Your life for theirs. That's the offer. Take it or—"

"*Or what?*" I said. And then, because I am my father-in-all-but-blood's finest student, and because the truth had finally arrived in my chest like a swallowed coal: "*Or nothing.* There is no 'or,' Corvane. There never was. That's the whole lesson. The impossible things were never for sale."

I reached down — not to pull his hand away, but to close my own fingers over his knuckles, gently, the way you'd close a ledger at the end of a very long night — and I said the words.

The fire heard me.
