## 20. A Country With No Door

The flame in my chest had opinions.

I hadn't expected that. I'd imagined carrying the dragon-fire the way you carry a lantern, or a debt—something held at arm's length, something with a handle. But it sat behind my sternum like a second heart, and like a second heart it beat slightly out of time with my own, so that I was always a half-step off from myself. When I was afraid, it flared. When I grieved—and there was a great deal of grieving to get through—it guttered low and sullen, sulking in its ember-bed of ribs, as though grief were an insult to fire specifically.

"It's judging me," I told Hale.

"Everything judges you," he said. "You've simply never been able to hear it before."

We stood at the mouth of the last door. Not a door, really—the guild's doors were oak and iron and brass fittings polished by centuries of hands—but this one had been a doorway once and was now mostly absence, a rectangle of older dark cut into the ash-stink air of Vael's dying streets, held open by nothing except the stubbornness of the people who refused to stop walking through it. Beyond it: Ember Lane, or what remained of it. Smoke. Rain. The city that appeared on maps, that had never heard of Vael, that did not know it was about to receive several hundred refugees from a country that officially did not exist.

Behind me, Vael waited to die.

* * *

You should understand what it means for a country to be erased, because no one ever explains it properly. It isn't that the maps are redrawn. Maps are redrawn all the time; ink is cheap and borders are fashion. Erasure is slower and more thorough than that. Erasure is when the rivers forget their names. When the songs lose their verses one by one, like teeth. When a grandmother tells her granddaughter a story and the granddaughter hears only weather.

Vael had been erased for a hundred years, and the people inside it had stayed because leaving meant forgetting themselves entirely—not death, which at least has the decency to be final, but dissolution. You walk out the door and by the third step your name is gone, your mother's face is gone, and you become a person-shaped absence wandering someone else's country with an ache you can't source. The dragon-fire had held them together. The dragon-fire had been their memory made visible, burning in the great brazier on the palace steps, and every soul in Vael drew themselves up out of it each morning like water from a well.

And now the fire lived in me, and the last door was closing, and I had perhaps an hour to move an entire nation through a hole in the world before it sealed itself and took everything left behind down into the dark with it.

"No pressure," I murmured to the flame.

It flared, which I chose to interpret as agreement.

* * *

The old woman—I had learned her name by then, Maren, though she would always be half the woman who called me by a name I'd never heard, *Vaelin*, the name I'd been born under before I was sold into kitchens—found me at the threshold with a ledger under her arm.

Of course she did. A hundred years of impossible trade, and here was the proof that accounting is the true universal language.

"You'll want this," she said, pressing it into my hands. "The roll of names. Every soul still living in Vael. Four hundred and eleven." She said the number the way other people say prayers. "When they go through, they'll need someone to call the names after them. Someone standing on the far side who remembers."

"Maren," I said, "the moment they step through, they start forgetting. That's the whole—"

"They won't," she said. "Not anymore." She touched my chest, lightly, where the flame sat. "That's what the fire was for. We thought it kept us alive. It kept us *remembered*. As long as it burns somewhere, anywhere, the door can't take what we are. It will try. Oh, it will try—it will pull at them like a tide going out. But they'll have you on the other side, calling them home by name." Her eyes were dry, which told me she had already done her crying, privately, sometime in the last hundred years, and had budgeted accordingly. "You're the ledger now, Vaelin Thackeray. Write us down."

I looked down at the book. Four hundred and eleven names in a careful old hand, and beside each name a small notation—trade, or craft, or kinship, the way guild ledgers mark what a thing is worth.

Except these weren't entries. They were people. And for the first time since I'd learned to hold a pen, I understood that a ledger could be an act of love.

* * *

They came in ones and twos at first, then in streams—a river of the almost-forgotten flowing toward the door. The baker whose bread had fed three generations of a street that no longer existed. Twin sisters who had spent a century keeping each other's memories by telling them back and forth every night, two halves of one archive. A boy of maybe ten who had been born inside the erasure and had never known any sky but Vael's grey one, and who stopped at the threshold and looked back with an expression I recognized from mirrors.

"I won't know anyone there," he said. "No one will know my name."

"The flame knows it," I said, and put my hand over my own chest, and felt the fire lean toward him like a dog hearing its name. "And I know it. Tam. Tam Orrel. You'll be the first Tam anyone in that city has ever met, and you'll be unforgettable, I promise you. It runs in your country."

He went through. I wrote his name in the new column as he crossed, my hand steady, because someone had to be steady and apparently it was me, the girl whose entire life had been built on the principle that being unnoticed was the same as being safe.

Hale worked the far side. I could see him through the doorway—or rather, I could hear him, his voice carrying back through the older dark, flat and clear and relentless, repeating the names as the people arrived, anchoring them: *"Maren Vosk. Aldous Ren. The twins, Sefa and Ilse Corren—"* Inquisitor Hale, who had spent his career proving impossible things did not exist, now employed full-time in making sure they continued to. He had not asked permission from his superiors. His superiors were, at present, dealing with the smoking ruin of their weaponized-door ambitions and had larger concerns. I intended to keep it that way.

* * *

Three hundred names. Three hundred and fifty.

The door began to close.

Not quickly—doors that remember do nothing quickly—but I felt it in the flame, a tightening, a drawing-in, like a fist slowly curling. The rectangle of dark narrowed at the edges. Behind me, the palace steps stood empty, the great brazier cold for the first time in a century, and Vael itself seemed to exhale, the whole ruined country settling into its long sleep with something that was almost relief.

Almost. Because at the edge of the crowd, apart from everyone, stood a man who was not moving.

He was old in the way furniture is old, worn smooth and functional, and he was looking at the door the way you look at a thing you've decided against. I knew before I reached him what he was going to say, because I had spent twenty years among people who traded pieces of themselves, and I knew the arithmetic of self-sacrifice when I saw it being done in someone's head.

"My wife went through an hour ago," he said. "Aldous Ren. Carpenter. She'll be looking for me on the far side and she won't—" His jaw worked. "If I stay, the country keeps one man. One memory, kept whole, here where it belongs. Someone should stay. Countries shouldn't end empty."

"Vael isn't ending," I said. "Vael is dispersing. There's a difference, and the difference is four hundred and eleven names in a book, and yours is written in it, Aldous, and I don't strike entries. Ever. Ask anyone who owes the guild money."

He almost smiled. "The flame. Will it hold?"

"It held me," I said. "And I'm mostly spite and arsenic. You'll be fine."

He stepped through, and I called his name after him, and on the far side Hale's voice caught it and set it down gently, like a man setting down something breakable that he had decided, against all evidence and inclination, to carry anyway.

* * *

Four hundred and eleven.

The door was a slit now, a line of older dark in the rain-washed air, and beyond it I could hear Hale's voice finishing the last names, and beyond *that*—nothing I needed anymore. No prophecy. No master ledger. No guildmaster. No death date written in ash beside my name. Corvane was gone, unmade by his own erased accounts, and I had burned every record that could make a person into a possession, and the ash of those pages was somewhere behind me in a country that was finally allowed to rest.

Maren stood beside me at the threshold. She was the last but one. The last was me.

"You could stay too," she said quietly. "The heir of Vael, in Vael. There's a poetry to it."

"There's a loneliness to it," I said. "I've done invisible. I've done trapped in a beautiful place nobody remembers. I want the version where I'm *known*. Where I walk into a room and someone says my name and it's true." I touched the flame through my shirt, warm as a sleeping bird. "Besides, this needs feeding, and I intend to feed it properly—on good bread and arguments and the smell of rain on streets that appear on maps. Vael doesn't need a queen in a dead country. It needs a hearth in a live one."

Maren looked at me for a long moment. Then she laughed—a real laugh, cracked and rusty, the first I'd heard from her—and pulled me into an embrace that smelled of woodsmoke and a century of endurance.

"Your mother argued exactly like that," she said into my hair.

And then she stepped through, and I called her name, and Hale's voice answered, and the door closed.

* * *

I stood alone in Vael for exactly as long as it takes to say goodbye to a country.

I walked the palace steps. I touched the cold brazier. I said, out loud, to the empty air, to the grey sky, to the rivers that had forgotten their names: "Rest now. You were real. I'm the proof, and I keep excellent accounts."

The flame in my chest flared once, high and bright, like a flag, like a farewell.

Then I turned around, and there was no door at all—only rain falling on ruins, only wind, only a country finally allowed to be a memory instead of a prison. I put my hand flat against the wet stone of the wall where the door had been, and I felt nothing but stone, and I found, to my enormous surprise, that I was glad. Doors are for people with something to hide. I was done hiding.

Getting home was its own kind of impossible, and I'll spare you the details, except to say that the widow's stolen hour still owed me interest, and that Hale met me on the Ember Lane side with dry clothes, a flask of tea, and an expression I had never seen on him before and took a moment to identify.

It was relief. On his face. For me.

"Don't," he said, before I could speak.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say something wry about how I've gone soft."

"I was going to say," I said, taking the tea, "that you look terrible, and that we both need to sleep for approximately a week, and that the city ahead of us has absolutely no idea what's about to arrive in it."

"Four hundred and eleven people," Hale said. He said it the way he said numbers—precisely, but with something underneath the precision now. "Scattered across a city that's never heard of them. They'll need papers. Work. Names that hold." He paused. "Someone should keep a record."

I stopped walking.

Because I had burned the ledgers. Every debt, every entry, every account that could own a person—and I had thought that was the end of it, that a life without ledgers was possible, that I could just be a woman walking down a rainy street with a fire in her chest and no columns to keep.

But a ledger, Maren's book had taught me, is only a cage if someone else holds the pen.

"Hale," I said slowly, "how do you feel about helping me start a different kind of book?"

He looked at me sideways, rain running off his inquisitor's coat, which he had not yet stopped wearing out of what I suspect was pure habit. "What kind?"

"The kind where nobody owes anybody. The kind where people write down what they survived, so it can't be taken from them. The kind with an open door." I smiled, and felt the flame answer it, warm and steady and mine. "Every good country starts with an accounting, Hale. This one's going to be honest."

He didn't smile back—he may not have known how—but he nodded once, and we walked together into the city that appears on maps, four hundred and eleven names between us, and the first light of a morning Vael never got to see coming up grey and gold over rooftops that had always existed.

Somewhere behind my ribs, the last dragon-fire settled in for the night, a hearth where a countdown used to be.

And in my pocket, Maren's roll of names pressed against my hip like a heartbeat that wasn't mine—except that it was, now. All of them were.

There was just one thing I hadn't yet told Hale. One small item, unentered, unwritten, sitting in the newest column of the newest ledger I was keeping—the one behind my sternum, where the flame filed things I wasn't ready to read.

Because as the last door closed, in the half-second before the older dark sealed itself forever, something had come through after me.

Not a person. Not quite.

A page. Old paper, taster's shorthand, my own hand aged a century—

and the ink was still wet.
