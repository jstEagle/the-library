## 22. Somewhere Real

The hill above Callowmere is called the Widow's Tooth, which I find either grimly funny or funny-grim depending on how much wine I've had. It's a bald green lump with one crooked hawthorn at the top and a view of a city that appears on maps — actual maps, printed ones, the kind merchants buy and sailors trust with their lives. I have been sitting up here for an hour watching the sun go down over rooftops that have never once tried to erase themselves, and I am pleased to report that it is the most boring thing I have ever done.

I mean that as the highest possible compliment.

For most of my life, boredom was a luxury reserved for people whose names didn't come with asterisks. Tasters don't get bored. Tasters get vigilant. There's a difference, and the difference is that vigilance eventually kills you while boredom just makes you fat and opinionated. I intend to become both.

The dragon-fire in my chest has settled into something like a banked hearth. That's not poetry; that's physiology, or whatever the word is when the thing living in your sternum used to be a nation's last hope and is now mostly a warm weight behind your ribs that flares when you're happy and crackles when you lie. It crackles right now, faintly, because I'm lying to myself about being calm, and it knows. It always knows. Living with Vael's flame is like living with a grandmother who can smell guilt through a closed door.

"Stop eavesdropping," I tell it.

It warms, smugly.

* * *

You should know what happened to everyone, because ledgers were my whole life and old habits die hard even when you've burned them. Especially then.

Hale got his justice, in the end, though not the kind he'd have recognized a year ago. He stood in front of the tribunal in his grey coat with his grey face and told them everything: the Crown's plan to weaponize the Vael door, the orders he'd been given, the ones he'd refused. They stripped him of rank, which was the point. He signed his confession in his own careful hand, the same hand that kept his secret ledger of every small cruelty he'd witnessed and filed away under *procedure*, and when they took his badge he looked lighter than I'd ever seen him. Two prisoners of different cages, we'd said once. Turns out the cages were the same size all along. He walks Callowmere now like a man who owns nothing and has finally noticed that this is a form of wealth.

He visits on Thursdays. He brings terrible bread from a bakery near the river because, he says, a man who spent thirty years eating at the Crown's table should learn what food tastes like when nobody's trying to kill anybody. The bread is genuinely awful. I eat all of it.

Maren — the widow, though she'd bristle at the title now; she's a bookkeeper, properly speaking, and a good one — her name went first in the new ledger, as promised. *Survived: a husband, a stolen hour, an inquisitor, and me.* She wrote it herself in a round, stubborn script. She runs the shop on Ember Lane where the guild used to be, except now there's no guild, no doors, no debts paid in memories. Just a counter, a kettle, and a book left open on the table where anyone may write down what they've lived through. Some entries are long. Some are a single line. One child wrote only *the dog died but I got a puppy* and Maren says it's the truest account the ledger holds.

Corvane left nothing. When I burned the master ledger and the accounts unmade themselves, he came apart the way ink comes apart in water — not violently, not screaming, just quietly ceasing to be a man who could be owed anything or owe it. I have thought about whether I grieve him. The honest answer is that grief requires a body to bury and I never got one, so instead I keep a different kind of grave: I remember the version of him that taught me cipher-work by candlelight and called me *little ledger* and meant it kindly, before kindness curdled into ownership. Both men existed. I bury the first and refuse the second. That's not forgiveness. It's accounting, and I was always better at accounting than forgiveness.

And Vael — Vael is scattered, and alive, and that was the whole trick. Four hundred souls walked out through the last door carrying their names in their mouths like embers cupped in bare hands. They live now in Callowmere and Thessaly and a fishing village whose name I still can't pronounce, working as smiths and cooks and one extremely successful maker of cheese that the old woman Ilsette insists is superior to anything produced in a country that exists. She may be biased. She may also be right. Nobody remembers them as refugees from anywhere, because there is no *anywhere* to be a refugee from; the door closed behind them and the erased nation finished dying, gently, the way a fire finishes when you've carried out what mattered.

What mattered was them. It was always them. It took me twenty-six years and one burning prophecy to understand that a country is not its maps or its borders or its dragon-fire. A country is whoever remembers it, and memory travels fine without doors.

* * *

Ilsette found me on the hill, the way she always does, moving at the pace of someone who has outlived three kings and considers hurry a personal insult.

"You're sitting in the cold," she said.

"I'm sitting in the evening," I corrected. "There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed, and sat down next to me anyway, arranging her skirts with the ceremony of a woman settling onto a throne. "Your fire's steady."

"It is."

"That's new."

"That's the point." I watched the last light go gold along the rooftops below. "It used to count down. Every morning I woke up and it was smaller and I could feel it, like sand running out. Now it just... sits. Burns low and warm. Like it's decided to stay."

Ilsette nodded slowly. "A hearth instead of a fuse."

"I was going to say a banked fire instead of a guttering candle, but yours is better. Yours sounds like something a person would actually say."

"A person," she said, "who raised four children and buried two husbands and one entire country. I've had practice with fires." She was quiet a moment. Then, in the tone she uses when she's about to say something she's rehearsed: "Do you know what I thought, the first time I saw you? In the ruined market, with the ash coming down?"

"You thought I looked like my mother."

"I thought you looked like your mother standing in a place she'd have hated." She smiled at whatever my face did. "She hated ruins. Said a people shouldn't live inside their own monument. Said we'd either leave or become ghosts, and she knew which she preferred." Ilsette turned to look at me fully, and her eyes were bright and hard as the last coals of anything. "You chose leaving. For all of us. Do you understand what that cost you? What it gave you?"

"Both," I said. "At the same time. That's the part nobody warns you about."

"No," she agreed. "Nobody does."

We sat until the stars came out, which in Callowmere takes a while because the city glows like a banked hearth itself, and I thought about how strange it is that I can look at light now without counting what it's costing me.

* * *

Here is the thing about identity that no prophecy ever mentions, and I would know, because I've read the only one that concerned me and then burned it: identity isn't something you find. Everyone says *find yourself* as if you're a set of keys misplaced in a drawer. You are not keys. You are not lost. You are a thing being made, continuously, by every choice, and the tragedy of my first twenty-five years is that other people held the pen — Corvane bought me, the prophecy named me, the Crown used me, even Vael claimed me by blood I never asked for. Heir of a burned map. As if a map could beget anything but more maps.

The flame chose differently. Or rather — and this is the part I've only recently been able to say out loud — when I took it into my chest, it stopped being Vael's heirloom and became mine. Not because I was royal. Because I was willing. Willingness turns out to be the only inheritance that matters. Everything else is paperwork, and I burned the paperwork.

So these days I am not the taster. I am not the heir. I am not the invisible girl in the corner of the banquet hall sipping poison with a straight face while lords discuss tariffs. I am Ada Thackeray, which is a name I chose to keep even after learning it wasn't quite mine, because names are like houses — you can inherit one, but you make it home by what you do in it. I keep a shop. I keep a ledger anyone can write in. I taste things for pleasure now, which remains the strangest sentence I have ever composed: last week Hale brought honey from a hillside apiary and I tasted it the way other people listen to music, eyes closed, one finger raised, and pronounced it *clean*. He looked absurdly proud, as if he'd made it himself.

I am visible. People know my name and use it in rooms I'm not in, and when I walk into those rooms the conversation doesn't change pitch. Do you know what that's worth? After a lifetime of being furniture that could die? I'll tell you: it's worth everything, and it cost a mother's face, and I would pay it again, and some mornings I wake up furious that I had to, and all three of those things are true at once, and the fire in my chest crackles softly at all three, because it understands that truth is rarely a single note.

* * *

The hawthorn above us rustled, and I smelled woodsmoke and wet wool and knew Sef before he dropped out of the branches like the oversized cat he essentially was.

"You're both sitting in the dark like a pair of crows," he announced.

"Crows sit in trees," I said. "You'd know."

"I know lots of things now. I'm learning letters." He plopped down on my other side, radiating satisfaction. "Maren's teaching me. I can read my own name and most of the alphabet and the word *survived*, which is honestly the important one."

"The only one," Ilsette said gravely.

Sef was one of ours — a taster boy, sixteen, quick hands, quicker mouth, who'd scattered during the raid on Ember Lane and spent three terrified weeks sleeping in haylofts before finding his way to the shop. He'd taken to the new ledger like a duck to arson. Half the entries in it are his, cataloguing everything from *fell off the roof, fine* to a three-page account of a fistfight outside the tannery that reads like epic poetry. He keeps saying he wants to be a writer. I keep saying the ledger will hold him until he's ready for something bigger. I keep meaning it.

"Message from the shop," he said, producing a folded paper with the flourish of a court herald. "From Maren. She says—" he squinted at it, lips moving— "*a traveler came asking after the woman who carries the fire. She wouldn't give a name. Left this.*"

He handed me a second paper, smaller, folded twice. I opened it by feel before my eyes adjusted, which is a taster's habit — hands first, always hands first — and felt the texture of it and knew before I read a single word.

Old paper. Century-old paper. The same stock as a certain prophecy book I had personally committed to ashes.

The handwriting, when I made myself look, was mine. Older. Steadier. The hand of the woman I met mid-writing in the deep door, the self who sent the warning back through time hoping to change the ending — the ending I did change, at cost, at ruin, at the price of my mother's face and Corvane's ghost and a country's final breath.

The note was four lines long.

*If you are reading this, the loop closed clean and you are somewhere real. Good. I wrote the date of my death and got to choose it instead — I hope you did too.*

*I have one more thing to send back. Not a warning this time. An invitation. Watch the shop on the first frost.*

Below it, a date. Next month. And beneath the date, a single word in tasters' shorthand, the old cipher, the language of poisons and survivals:

*Come.*

The fire in my chest didn't gutter. It didn't flare. It did something I'd never felt it do before: it leaned, the way a person leans toward a door they recognize.

"Well?" said Sef. "Bad news?"

I folded the note and put it in my pocket, over my heart, where the hearth warmed it like hands around a cup.

"No," I said, and found that I was smiling, and that the smile belonged entirely to me. "It's an invitation. From someone who survived."

Below us, Callowmere lit its ten thousand lamps, a city on a map, real as bread. Above us, the stars came out one by one, each one keeping its own small account.

First frost, then. I had a shop to run, a ledger to keep open, and — apparently — a future to meet, arriving the way futures do:

on time, unannounced, and written in my own hand.
