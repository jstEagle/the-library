## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The Hollow woke the way it always did: slowly, and without ceremony.

Lena Dravenmoor knew this because she had written it down. *Third of Harvestmonth. Fog in the low fields until midmorning. Old Maret's cow calved in the night—a heifer, brown, healthy. The bell in the temple tower rang six times instead of five; no one remarked on it.* She had written that last part three days ago, and already she could not remember whether it was true, or whether she had dreamed it, or whether she had simply wanted the valley to be slightly wrong so that her notebook would have something to say.

That was the trouble with the Hollow. It forgot things. Not the way people forget—fading, softening, letting go—but the way a river forgets a stone it has carried downstream. Things here did not fade. They were simply *taken*, gently, without malice, as though some patient hand reached into every mind each night and smoothed away whatever it deemed unnecessary. Names of travelers who passed through. The words of songs after the second verse. The reason anyone had ever built a wall along the eastern orchard, though the wall remained, moss-eaten and stubborn, and everyone agreed it must have been for *something*.

Magic could not follow you into the Hollow. That was what the old stories said, the ones the temple permitted, worn smooth as river pebbles from retelling. Whatever gift the world beyond the pass still carried—and there were those who claimed the world beyond the pass carried a great deal of it, and worse than a great deal—it stopped at the valley's rim like rain against glass. Inside, the air lay flat and quiet. Candles burned evenly. Bread rose the same every day. Nothing shimmered, nothing whispered, nothing *waited* just out of sight.

Most people called that safety. Lena had never been able to decide whether it wasn't simply another word for emptiness.

She blew out her candle, dressed in the dark, and went down the narrow stair of the rooms above the chandlery where she lodged. Mrs. Halloway, the chandler's widow, would not wake for another hour, but a pot of porridge left warming on the banked fire was part of their unspoken arrangement: room and breakfast in exchange for Lena carrying up the day's tallow and sweeping the workroom floor before she left for the temple. It was not generosity, exactly. Nothing in the Hollow was ever quite generosity. But it was steady, and steadiness was a currency Lena had learned to value above all others.

Outside, the fog was doing its slow work, erasing the far hills, then the near ones, then the orchard rows until the world consisted only of the lane beneath her feet and the sound of her own boots. Somewhere a rooster crowed and was answered by silence, as though even the other roosters had forgotten how.

*Fog thick enough to drink,* she thought, and then, out of habit, stopped at the gate to write it down. The notebook was small, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling, its pages crowded with seven years of such observations. Her handwriting changed across them—younger at the front, rounder and more careful; leaner now, slanted, hurried. She kept it wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, because paper was precious and because, though she had never told anyone this, she half-feared what would happen if the valley decided her book was one of the things it needed to forget.

The temple of Records stood at the center of the valley town of Emberlow, which was to say the temple stood at the center of everything, because in the Hollow everything was records. Births, deaths, debts, deeds, marriages, mill-stones, the price of wool going back two hundred years. The crown's writ ran thin this far from the capital, but ink ran deep, and the temple was where the kingdom's ink pooled. Its archive occupied three floors of stone vaults, cool and dry and lit by lamps that burned behind glass, because fire and paper had an understanding the registrars preferred not to encourage.

Lena had worked there since she was eleven years old, when her mother's debt passed to her like a coat too large to grow into.

Her mother. Even thinking the name required effort—the valley took names first, always names—and Lena made herself do it every morning as she walked, the way other people said their prayers. *Sera Dravenmoor. Sera Dravenmoor, who came over the pass in the spring of my ninth year. Sera Dravenmoor, who owed the temple four hundred crowns and died owing four hundred and sixty.* She repeated it because she refused to let the valley have it. It was the one theft she fought.

What her mother had done to incur such a sum, Lena had never fully learned. There had been talk once—she'd been young, and grief had blurred the edges of it—something about papers her mother carried, something about a debt incurred *before* she ever reached the Hollow, transferred, assumed, compounded. The ledger entry itself was maddeningly spare: *Sera Dravenmoor, principal 400 crowns, per agreement.* No agreement was attached. When Lena had asked, years ago, the under-registrar had given her the look reserved for scribes who asked about entries above their station, and she had learned to stop asking. Debt in the Hollow was not a question. It was weather. You lived under it.

Four hundred and sixty crowns. At her wage of two crowns the week, minus lodging, minus ink, minus the small fees the temple levied for the privilege of working off fees—that was thirty-one years. She had done the arithmetic so many times the numbers had worn grooves in her mind. Thirty-one years, and she was twenty-three already, and the interest turned over every season like a field.

She climbed the temple steps as the fog began to lift, nodding to Under-Registrar Pell, who was sweeping the portico and who nodded back with the particular blankness of a man who would forget her face by noon. Inside, the archive breathed its familiar breath: cold stone, lamp-oil, dust, and the faint vinegar smell of old iron-gall ink. It should have been oppressive. Instead it felt, as it always did, like the closest thing to home the valley offered her. Books did not forget. That was the whole point of them. Every page in this building was a small rebellion against the valley's appetite, ten thousand scribes across two centuries saying *no, this happened, I saw it, I set it down.*

"Dravenmoor." High Registrar Coram's voice carried down the vault stairs before he did, dry as the paper he loved. He appeared a moment later, a tall stooped man with ink stains to his elbows and spectacles pushed up into thin white hair. "You're early."

"I'm always early, sir."

"You're always early," he agreed, as though confirming a ledger entry. "Table nine needs copying. Estate inventories for the Vell estate, north orchards. The original is water-damaged, so work carefully—where the ink has run, mark it *illegible* rather than guessing. We do not record what we do not know." He paused, and his eyes, pale and quick behind the smudged lenses, rested on her a moment longer than the instruction required. "You do know that, don't you, Dravenmoor? We do not record what we do not know."

"Yes, sir."

"Mm." He moved off between the shelves, a grey figure dissolving into grey aisles, and Lena stood a moment with the strange feeling—familiar lately, growing more frequent—that the conversation had contained a second meaning folded inside the first, like a letter within a letter.

She put it aside. Table nine waited, and table nine did not care about feelings.

The morning passed in the rhythm she knew better than her own heartbeat: trim the quill, rule the lines, copy. The Vell inventory was tedious—barrels of apples by count, presses by number, a cider house valued at twelve crowns—and Lena's hands moved through it while her mind wandered the way it always did, out past the vault walls, over the orchards, up toward the pass.

The pass. She had never been through it. No one from the Hollow went through it, really; traders came in, sold, and left, and Lena had noticed long ago that they always seemed slightly relieved to go, though none of them could say why. The valley held you the way still water holds a leaf. And she had spent twenty-three years being that leaf, telling herself the stillness was peace, telling herself that longing was just an appetite like any other and could be starved polite.

But some mornings, copying lists of apples, she felt the wanting rise in her like water in a well, quiet and enormous. She wanted to see a thing she couldn't name. She wanted the world to be *larger* than a page. She wanted—

She wanted to matter somewhere other than in a ledger column.

*Stop,* she told herself, and copied: *Cider press, second, iron-bound, serviceable.*

At midday she ate bread and hard cheese in the scriptorium's antechamber, and took out the grey notebook, and reviewed. This was her true work, the work no one paid her for and no one knew about, though it used all the skills the temple had taught her. Because the valley forgot, and someone had to remember, and it might as well be the girl with the quill.

She read back through the autumn's pages. *The tinker with red hair who stayed two nights—no one remembers him now, though he mended every pot in Emberlow. The verse of the reaping song that goes "and the door stands open wide"—three verses existed once; the temple's own hymnal shows the scrape-marks where one was removed. The year the river ran backward for an afternoon, recorded nowhere but here, witnessed by me alone, age fourteen, standing on the mill bridge with my heart hammering like something waking up.*

That last one she had written and rewritten a dozen times, trying to make the ink hold what she had felt. The river running backward. For perhaps a hundred heartbeats the water had flowed upstream, silver and wrong and *glad*, somehow glad, and Lena had stood on the bridge and felt, for the first time in her life, that the world had a pulse. Then it settled. Then the valley smoothed it away from every mind but hers. She had run to the temple archives that same evening and searched for corroboration and found nothing—not in the mill logs, not in the weather records, not anywhere. As if the event had been unwritten everywhere except inside her chest.

She had started the notebook the next day.

It was not magic. She was careful never to call it that, even in the privacy of her own skull, because the stories all agreed on one point: the Hollow was where magic could not follow, and to speak of magic here was to invite either laughter or worse. It was only observation. Only diligence. Only a scribe's habit applied to the one text no registrar had thought to preserve: the valley itself, and everything it let slip.

Still. Some nights, writing by candlelight, she caught herself listening—to the silence, to the spaces between sounds—as though the silence were a language she'd known once, as a child, and might know again.

* * *

The afternoon brought the fog back, and with it a visitor to the archive: a wool-factor named Doss, come up from the lowlands to register a contract, loud and perfumed and impatient. Lena was assigned to witness his signature, which meant standing at his elbow while he read, watching his hands, certifying with her own countersignature that the man who signed was the man who arrived.

He signed. She countersigned. And then, gathering his papers, the factor paused, frowning at the window where the fog pressed gray against the glass.

"Strange country, this," he said, conversationally, to no one in particular. "I've been coming here eleven years. You'd think I'd remember the road better." He shook his head, chuckling at himself. "Every time I leave, it's like the place lets go of me. Can't recall my innkeeper's name to save my life. Can't recall—" He stopped. His brow furrowed. "Now isn't that odd. I had something I meant to ask your registrar. Something important." He looked at Lena, and for a moment his eyes were genuinely troubled, a man patting his pockets for a thing he could feel the shape of. "It'll come back to me on the road, I expect. It always does. Just past the pass, like as not."

"Just past the pass," Lena agreed quietly.

And there it was again, that feeling, cold and bright at once: the valley forgetting him even as he stood in it, reaching into a grown man's head and lifting a thought out like a splinter. He would remember by tonight, outside the rim. He would never know it had been taken. Nobody ever knew. That was the valley's genius—that its thefts were invisible precisely because they were total.

After he'd gone, Lena sat back down at table nine and looked at her neat columns of apples and presses, and found her hand had gone still.

*If it can take a thought from him,* she thought, *what else can it take? What has it taken from me?*

She wrote the question in the notebook that evening, by candlelight in her room above the chandlery, and stared at it a long while. Below it, almost without deciding to, her pen added:

*Things the valley has forgotten that I have found: the backward river. The third verse. The wall's purpose. The tinker's name (found it scratched on a pot he mended: JOREM). The bell that rang six.*

*Question: if I wrote down everything, every day, everything the valley drops—how long before I noticed a pattern? How long before I knew what it is the valley is trying not to be?*

She underlined the last word twice. Then, because the candle was guttering and Mrs. Halloway charged for wax, she closed the book, wrapped it in oilcloth, and tucked it beneath the loose floorboard where she kept it, along with the single keepsake her mother had left her: a scrap of parchment, older than she was, blank to every eye that had ever looked at it—including hers—yet too fine, too carefully edged, to be trash. Her mother had held it when she died. Had pressed it into Lena's hand with fingers that wouldn't close, and mouthed something no one caught, and the temple registrars had examined the parchment, declared it blank, and assessed it against the estate at nothing.

Lena had kept it anyway. Scribes keep things. That was the whole of her creed.

She blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the valley's perfect, terrible quiet, and told herself, as she did every night, that tomorrow would be the same as today: fog, porridge, ledgers, apples. Thirty-one years of apples stretching ahead of her like an orchard with no far edge.

She was nearly asleep when she heard it—a sound from the direction of the pass road, faint but unmistakable in the stillness. Hoofbeats. Many of them, moving fast, arriving after dark, which no trader ever did.

Lena sat up in the dark, heart tapping quick against her ribs, and reached for the oilcloth bundle.

Whatever was coming over the pass, she intended to write it down.
