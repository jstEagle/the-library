## 2. Ink and Interest

The hoofbeats, it turned out, belonged to no one worth writing down.

Lena watched from her window as a column of riders passed below on the pass road—crown couriers in grey livery, six of them, their lanterns swinging like low-hung fruit. They did not stop at the ford or the mill. They went straight up the temple hill, and the temple doors swallowed them, and by morning they were gone again, leaving nothing behind but churned mud and the particular silence that followed any visit from the capital.

She wrote it down anyway. *Six riders, grey livery, arrived after dark the 14th of Harvestmoon, departed before dawn. No word to anyone.* It was a small entry, the kind of thing the valley would forget by noon, and that was precisely why it belonged in her bundle.

The next morning brought fog, porridge, and ledgers—just as she had promised herself—and something else besides. She was halfway through copying the tithing rolls for Lower Fen when Tamsin appeared at the scriptorium door with a face like weather about to turn.

"He wants you," she said. "The High Registrar. Upstairs. Now."

Lena's pen stopped mid-stroke. In eleven years of service, the High Registrar had summoned her exactly twice: once to commend her hand, and once to inform her that her mother's funeral costs had been added to the family ledger. Both times, the summons had come with tea. That was how she knew this was serious. The temple's kindness always came poured into a cup.

"Did he say why?"

"He said—" Tamsin hesitated, glancing over her shoulder as though the corridor might be listening. "He said to tell you it's good news."

*It is never good news,* Lena thought, blotting her page with more force than necessary. But she set down her pen, squared her copied sheets, and climbed the narrow stair to the Registrar's office, where the air always smelled of sealing wax and old decisions.

***

High Registrar Ollen Vay sat behind a desk so wide it seemed designed to remind visitors of the distance between them. He was a soft-spoken man with soft hands and eyes like ledger entries—precise, unjudging, closed. A pot of tea waited between two cups, steam rising in a thin, patient line.

"Scribe Dravenmoor." He gestured to the chair opposite him. "Sit. Please."

She sat. He poured. The tea was the good kind, from the southern trade carts, dark and faintly spiced—the kind the temple served only when it wanted something.

"Do you know," he began, settling back with his own cup, "how long your mother held accounts with this house?"

"Twenty-two years," Lena said. "Nineteen before I was born, three after. I've copied the ledger myself."

"Indeed you have. Excellent work. Some of the finest script in the valley, if I may say." He smiled, and the smile was warm the way a hearth is warm—useful, contained, fed on purpose. "Which is why it pains me to be the bearer of difficult figures."

He turned the great ledger on his desk toward her, open to a page she knew intimately. Her mother's account. Column after column of debt in her own careful transcription—and then, beneath it, new columns in the Registrar's cramped violet ink.

"The original debt," Vay said, tapping the first figure, "stood at four hundred crowns, as you know. Interest has been applied faithfully each year. But the crown has recently required us to adopt standardized administrative fees. Record-keeping surcharges. Archival preservation assessments." His finger moved down the violet columns. "Storage of documents pertaining to the debt is itself chargeable, you see. Each year we keep your ledger, we must bill for its keeping. Each copy we make of the debt must be paid for by the debtor, naturally—it takes labor to record what one owes."

Lena stared at the numbers until they stopped looking like numbers and started looking like a wall.

"That's—" She made herself breathe. "That's tripled it. The fees are larger than the interest."

"They are calculated by formula," Vay said gently, as though this settled everything. "I did not devise the formulas, Scribe. I merely keep faithful records. As do you." He sipped his tea. "At the current rate, your remaining service—your working off of the debt at scribe's wages—comes to approximately forty-one more years."

Forty-one years. Lena did the arithmetic without wanting to; she could not help herself. She would be seventy-two years old before the last violet column was crossed out. Seventy-two, and the whole of her life spent copying other people's debts in a valley that forgot everything except what it owed.

"I see," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt.

"I hoped you would." Vay set down his cup and folded his soft hands. "Because it happens—I am pleased to say—that circumstances have arisen which may alter your situation considerably. Favorably."

Here it comes, she thought. The trap always opens slowly, like a hand offering bread.

"The crown," he continued, "has entered a period of heightened... vigilance. Regarding certain persons. Individuals born with, shall we say, irregular natures. The gifted. You will have heard the rumors from the traders—the burnings in Dunharrow, the tribunals in the east."

"I've heard rumors," Lena said carefully. "Rumors aren't records."

"No." His eyes gleamed, appreciative. "No, they aren't. Records are better. And here is the heart of the matter, Scribe Dravenmoor: the Hollow occupies a unique position. Magic cannot follow past our boundaries. Which means that those who possess it sometimes come here seeking refuge. They cross the pass believing the valley's nullness will hide them." He leaned forward slightly. "And it does hide them—from the hunters. But not, of course, from the temple. Nothing that enters the Hollow escapes the temple's records."

The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere below, Tamsin's pen scratched against parchment, the ordinary sound of an ordinary morning that was no longer ordinary.

"What is being asked of me?" Lena asked. Her mouth had gone dry despite the tea.

"Nothing so dramatic as you're imagining, I assure you." Vay opened a drawer and produced a single sheet of paper, laying it on the desk between them with the care of a man handling a relic. Upon it was printed a form—a simple form, half a page, with lines for dates and descriptions and a space at the bottom for a signature. "A standing report. Whenever a traveler of unusual character passes through—someone who casts no shadow quite right, someone whose purchases are paid in odd coin, someone who speaks words that make the candles lean—you write it down. Name if known. Description if not. Date and direction of travel. You submit it to me, and I submit it onward, and in exchange..."

He let the sentence hang there, delicate as a spider's thread.

"In exchange," Lena said flatly, "the fees vanish."

"The fees are forgiven. The interest is frozen. Your mother's original debt—four hundred crowns—is discharged entirely upon your first verified report." He smiled again, that hearth-warmth. "One report, Scribe. One truthful observation, properly recorded. You, of all people, know the value of accurate documentation. This is simply documentation in service of the crown rather than against it."

"And the gifted ones?" she asked. "What happens to them, after my 'documentation'?"

"What happens to anyone who defies lawful authority." Vay's tone did not change at all. "Justice. Order. The safety of the many." He slid the form across the desk until it rested under her fingers. The paper was fine stock, heavy, expensive—the temple spared no expense on the instruments of its kindness. "You are not condemning anyone. You are recording. There is a difference, and a scribe should appreciate it."

*There is a difference,* Lena thought, staring at the blank lines. *But it is a difference measured in someone else's blood.*

She thought of her mother then—not the tired woman bent over mending by candlelight, but the stories, the strange ones, the ones about the valley's founding and the price of its peace. Her mother had told them quietly, always quietly, as though the walls had ears. Perhaps they had. Perhaps they always had.

"If I refuse?" she asked.

Vay spread his hands, wounded and reasonable. "Refuse? Why, nothing happens. The formulas continue. The fees compound. Forty-one years become forty-two. I would never coerce a servant of this house." He rose, moved to the window, gazed out at the fog-bound valley with the satisfaction of a man surveying property. "I will say only this: the hunters are coming closer every season. They have learned that refugees favor hidden valleys. When they arrive—as they eventually must—they will ask the temple for its records. And the temple, Scribe, keeps *faithful* records." He turned back to her, and for just a moment the warmth fell away and she saw the thing underneath: cold, patient arithmetic, the same that lived in the violet columns. "Would you not rather your name appear in those records as a correspondent of the crown? Rather than... otherwise?"

Lena looked at the form for a long moment. Then she stood, smoothed her skirt, and gave him the curtsy of a temple scribe—precise, correct, empty.

"You'll have my answer in writing," she said. "As is proper."

"As is proper," Vay agreed pleasantly, and saw her to the door.

***

She did not take the direct path back to the scriptorium. She walked the long way instead, out along the cloister walk where the fog pressed gray against the arches, and she let herself feel what she felt, which was fury wearing fear's clothing.

The trap was beautiful, she had to admit. Beautiful the way all the temple's works were beautiful. No chains, no threats, only a cup of good tea and a form to sign and the gentle, crushing mathematics of institutional mercy. Refuse, and the debt ate her alive by formula. Accept, and she became the temple's eye, selling strangers to hunters one careful entry at a time—and the worst of it, the truly elegant part, was that they'd chosen *her*. The woman who wrote things down. The woman who could not bear to let anything go unrecorded. They were asking her to turn her one true gift into a leash.

She stopped at the cloister's end, where the fog thinned and the orchards showed below, black branches dripping.

Her oilcloth bundle was hidden beneath the third apple tree in the far row. Inside it were nine years of entries: the day the millpond froze in a perfect circle while the river ran free; the trader whose shadow pointed north no matter the sun; the night the stars over the pass rearranged themselves into a shape no almanac predicted. Small strangenesses. The valley forgot them all by morning, and she alone remembered, because she alone had written them down.

Some of those strangenesses, she realized now, might be *people*. Might be exactly the travelers the Registrar wanted named. Had some part of her nine-year archive already been anticipated—already priced?

She thought of the grey riders. Six of them, arriving after dark, going straight to the temple. What had they delivered, if not instructions? What had the violet ink been waiting for?

A bell rang somewhere below—third hour, the call back to work. Lena stood a moment longer, watching the fog eat the far edge of the valley, the place where the world beyond began.

Then she went inside, sat at her desk, took up her pen, and did the only thing she knew how to do with a truth too dangerous to speak aloud.

She wrote it down—but not in the temple's ledger, and not in the Registrar's violet. On a scrap of her own cheap paper, in her smallest hand, she recorded the day entire: the tea, the form, the forty-one years, the offer dressed as mercy. She folded it small and sewed it into the hem of her second-best cloak, where her mother had once sewed away coins and, Lena suspected now, other things besides.

Let the temple keep its records, she thought. She would keep hers. Two archives, running parallel, and only one of them honest.

She was grinding fresh ink for the afternoon's copying when Tamsin appeared again in the doorway, breathless, cheeks pink from the cold.

"There's a man at the ford," she said. "Collapsed right at the crossing, they're saying. Stranger—nobody knows him, nobody sent for him. Old Marta's covering him with blankets but he keeps talking in his sleep, and Lena—" Tamsin lowered her voice, eyes wide. "Marta says when he talks, the water in the ford glows."
