## 3. A Stranger at the Ford

The Hollow had a way of making even emergencies feel like errands.

Lena had been at the ford since midmorning, sent by Registrar Ollen to verify the water-stain damage on the eastern ledger crates—the third such verification this month, and the third time the answer would be *yes, they're ruined, and yes, someone will have to copy them again*. She knelt on the flat stone at the crossing with her sleeves pushed up, prying the lid off a crate that had swollen shut with damp, when the light changed.

It was a strange thing to notice in the Hollow, where light did little but lie there. Sunlight fell on the valley the way rain fell on oiled canvas—it touched everything and soaked into nothing. Lena had grown up inside that stillness the way a fish grows up inside glass. But she had spent eleven years copying records by candlelight, and candlelight taught you things about shadow that most people never learned. So when the shadows along the eastern bank shifted—leaned, all together, like wheat bending before wind that wasn't blowing—she looked up.

There was a man coming down the far slope.

He came wrong. That was her first thought, though she couldn't have said why. Travelers came down that slope sometimes—peddlers too stubborn or too poor to take the long road around the mountains, pilgrims who'd heard the Hollow was holy and left disappointed because nothing in it felt anything at all. They walked. They picked their footing among the scree. This man moved as if the slope were moving under him, stumbling from rock to rock with his arms half-raised, catching himself, losing himself again. His coat was dark with something wet across the ribs. Even at fifty paces, even in the valley's flat light, she could see the stain.

"Hello?" she called, and heard how thin her voice sounded against the water.

He didn't answer. He reached the last of the stones at the ford's edge, swayed there like a man deciding between two thoughts, and then his knees simply stopped being interested in holding him. He went down onto the gravel at the waterline hard enough that Lena flinched, and lay half in the shallows with the current pushing gently at his shoulder, patient as a debt collector, as if the river had all the time in the world to take him.

Lena stood very still for the space of three breaths.

She thought about the rules, which in the Hollow were mostly the temple's rules wearing other clothes. Strangers were to be directed to the registrar's office. The injured were to be brought to Sister Anwe, who kept the healing salves and the temper. Anything unusual was to be reported, recorded, filed. *Report, record, file.* Eleven years of copying ledgers had written those three words somewhere beneath her skin, in a hand steadier than her own.

But he was bleeding, and the water was cold, and whatever else the Hollow was, it was hers—or as close to hers as anything had ever been—and nobody bled out in her valley while she checked with a registrar.

She waded in. The cold climbed her shins and bit. She got her hands under his arms—lighter than she expected, the way driftwood is light—and hauled him backward up the gravel until his head was clear of the waterline. He groaned then, a low broken sound, and turned his face toward her.

His eyes were open. That was the second wrong thing. Men who collapsed like that usually had their eyes rolled back, their faces slack. This man's eyes were open and fixed on her with terrible focus, as if she were a door he'd been walking toward for days, and his lips were moving.

"No," Lena said, stupidly, to no question at all.

He spoke again, and the sound of it made the hair on her arms lift.

It wasn't any language she knew. She had copied documents in Old Court script and trade-tongue and the clipped merchant shorthand of the coastal houses; she had heard pilgrims pray in four different tongues on their way through the pass. This was none of them. It was less like speech than like—she groped for the word and found only *weather*—like weather given a mouth. And here was the thing that made her let go of him and sit back hard on the wet stones: as he spoke, the air above his lips shimmered. Not heat-haze. Shimmer. A faint tracery of light, gold-white and fine as spider silk, curling off each word and hanging in the still Hollow air where nothing, *nothing*, was supposed to be able to hang.

Magic. In the Hollow. Where magic could not follow.

The words died in the air almost immediately, unravelling like thread pulled back through cloth, and the man's eyes fluttered shut, and the ordinary gray silence of the valley closed over the place where the light had been as if it had never been at all.

Lena knelt there, dripping to the elbow, heart slamming.

*Report, record, file.*

She put two fingers under his jaw instead. There was a pulse, quick and thready, like a bird's. He was youngish—thirty perhaps, maybe younger, hollowed out by whatever had chased him over the pass. Dark hair matted with sweat. The wound, when she dared look, was a long slice below his ribs, ugly but not the worst she'd seen; Sister Anwe stitched worse after every harvest festival. His pack had come half loose in the fall, and from it, wedged between a waterskin and something wrapped in oilcloth, protruded a scroll case—and a folded square of heavy paper gone stiff and dark at one corner.

Dark with blood. His blood, presumably, though the stain had an odd quality to it, a faint iridescence at the edges, like oil on water, like—

Like the light his words had made.

Lena should not have touched it. Every rule she had ever copied said so in some form or another. But she had spent her whole life in a valley where nothing glowed, where nothing hummed, where wonder arrived already catalogued and stamped and slightly damp, and her hand went out before her caution caught up with it, the way a moth goes out before the flame catches up with *it*.

Her fingers brushed the stained fold of the map.

The map lit.

Not reflected light. Not the sun off wet paper. It *lit*—lines rising off its surface in threads of gold, a whole hidden geography blooming under her fingertips: rivers she didn't recognize drawn in fire, mountain ranges sketched in strokes like lightning frozen mid-fall, and everywhere, threaded through everything, writing. Writing that crawled slowly along its lines like ants of light, rearranging itself, correcting itself, alive.

And her hand—

Her hand was glowing too.

Light poured up from her fingertips the way water rises through a wick. It traced the backs of her fingers, the tendons of her wrist, pooled briefly in the cup of her palm, and Lena watched it with the detached, horrified clarity of a scribe watching her own name appear in a ledger she never opened. Under her skin, along the pale blue roads of her veins, something answered the map's golden script—faint silver answering gold, a calligraphy of the body—and for one suspended moment the ford, the crates, the flat dead sunlight of the Hollow all seemed to lean toward her the way the shadows had leaned toward the stranger, attentive, expectant, *hungry*.

Then the man gasped—a raw, tearing breath, like a swimmer surfacing—and the light snapped out. All of it. Map dark, veins dark, the world reasserting its dull gray order so completely that Lena might have believed she'd imagined it, except that her hand was shaking and the map was warm under her palm, warm as a living thing, and the man was looking straight at her.

At her hand, specifically.

"You're—" he started, and coughed, and tried again. "You're one of—"

"Don't." Her voice came out harder than she meant it to, edged with a panic she was furious at herself for feeling. She snatched her hand back and shoved the map deep into his pack. "Don't say whatever you were going to say. Don't say it here."

He blinked at her, slow, fever-bright. "Where is 'here'?"

"The Hollow." She was already moving, dragging his pack strap over her shoulder, hooking his arm across her neck. He was heavier now that he was conscious, all dead weight and good intentions. "Now stand up, because I am not strong enough to carry you, and if you die at this ford I will have to fill out the paperwork, and I promise you the paperwork is worse than dying."

Something that might have been a laugh rattled weakly in his chest. He got one leg under himself, then the other, and together they staggered up the bank like a pair of drunks leaving a wake.

"The Hollow," he repeated. His voice had dropped, gone urgent, the word landing in his mouth like a coin he hadn't expected. "The null valley. You live here? Born here?"

"Born, raised, indentured." She jerked her chin toward the temple roofs visible above the orchard line, gray slate against gray sky. "Keep talking and walk. Talking keeps you conscious, and consciousness is my whole plan for you right now."

"What's your name?"

"Lena." Then, because eleven years of institutional caution finally caught up with her mouth: "*Don't* use it. Names are currency here, and I'm poor enough already."

He looked at her sideways at that—at the sharpness in it, maybe, or the fear underneath the sharpness—and something in his fevered expression shifted, softened, settled into something horribly like recognition. Like he'd found exactly what his map said he'd find.

"My name's Corvin," he said anyway, deliberately, watching her face. "Remember it."

"I just told you not to—"

"Remember it," he said again, and his grip tightened on her shoulder, and his eyes—still glowing faintly at the edges, she realized, still trailing that silk-fine light with every blink—fixed on hers with an intensity that made the valley's stillness feel suddenly like held breath. "Because there are men behind me who pull names out of people's heads like teeth, and yours is about to become the most important name in either realm. I've crossed the pass with a knife in my side to tell someone what you are, and you're going to listen, and you're going to remember."

Lena's feet slowed on the orchard path. Around them the apple trees stood in their neat temple rows, heavy with fruit nobody tasted with any pleasure, and the air was cool and gray and utterly silent, and somewhere behind them the ford murmured over the place where the light had been.

"What I am," she said, "is a scribe. Third-class. Debt-registered."

"That's what they told you." Corvin stumbled; she braced him; he kept talking through gritted teeth, each word costing him. "That's what the ledger says. But ledgers don't hold everything, girl. I've seen what doesn't fit in them. And twenty minutes ago, in the middle of the one valley in the world where the gift cannot reach—" he lifted his hand, trembling, and turned it palm-up, and in the cup of it a thread of gold light kindled and hung, defiant, impossible, "—your blood answered covenant-script. Null-born. Glowing. Do you understand what that means?"

The apple trees leaned. Or she leaned. She couldn't tell anymore.

"It means," Corvin whispered, as the first bell of the temple began to ring noon across the valley, "that everything your ancestors broke is trying to mend itself. And it chose *you*."

Far away, down the eastern pass, so faint it might have been wind, something howled.

Corvin's face went gray. "They found the trail faster than I thought." His fingers dug into her arm. "Scribe. Lena. We need to get off this path. *Now.*"

* * *

Later—much later, years later, in lives neither of them could yet imagine—Lena would try to reconstruct those next minutes and find them full of holes, the way the valley's memory was always full of holes. What she kept was fragments: the weight of him against her shoulder as she hauled him off the main path and into the orchard rows; the smell of crushed grass and apples rotting sweet underfoot; the bell ringing and ringing overhead while she crouched him down in the shadow of a storage shed behind the drying barns, out of sight of the temple road.

What she kept, most of all, was the moment she knelt there in the dust with his blood on her sleeve, and looked at her own right hand—turned it over, once, twice, like a page she was checking for errors—and found nothing. No glow. No silver roads. Just her hand, ink-stained at the fingertips, chapped at the knuckles, an ordinary scribe's hand attached to an ordinary scribe's life in an ordinary valley that forgot things.

Except that the map, when she risked one glance at it where it protruded from his open pack, was still warm.

And except that when Corvin's breathing finally evened into fever-sleep, his lips kept moving. Faintly. Endlessly. Repeating something over and over in that language of unraveling light, and though no glow rose from the words now—too weak, too worn, the valley pressing its flat gray palm down over everything—Lena bent close and listened, because listening was the one skill eleven years of ledgers had perfected in her.

She couldn't understand the words.

But she understood, with a certainty that settled into her bones colder than the ford water ever had, that he was counting.

Counting down.

Somewhere beyond the pass, past the orchards, past the edge of everything safe and gray and known, something was answering him. And it was getting closer.

Above the drying barns, the noon bell finished ringing. In the silence after, Lena Dravenmoor sat in the dust beside a bleeding stranger, in the one valley in the world where magic could not follow, and waited—with her heart hammering a countdown of its own—to see what followed anyway.
