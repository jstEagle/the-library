## 5. The Crown's Long Shadow

The interrogation room at Fort Marrow had no windows, which Vess considered a professional courtesy to the prisoners and a practical necessity for herself. Memory, when it left a person, had a way of making light—thin silver threads that drifted upward like smoke from a snuffed candle—and she preferred to work in a darkness that let her see them clearly.

The man in the chair was called Aldous Fen. He'd been a weather-worker in the hill country east of the Salt Road, small talents mostly: coaxing rain off the mountains a day early, keeping frost from the orchard blossoms, the modest miracles that kept villages fed and never made it into songs. The crown did not hang men like Aldous Fen for what they did. It hung them for what they *were*, and then it took everything they remembered before the rope went taut.

Vess sat across from him with her hands folded on the table between them, waiting.

"You understand how this works," she said. It was not a question. She said it every time, in the same flat register, because the ritual mattered. The words were a kind of permission the prisoner gave themselves, and permission made the extraction cleaner. Struggling memories tore. Torn memories bled into the reader, and she would carry fragments of another person's terror home with her in the dark behind her eyes, where no amount of lamplight ever quite reached.

"I know how it works," Fen said. His voice was steady. His hands, strapped to the chair's arms, were not. "My daughter's name is Maret. I want to keep it as long as I can."

"The memory of her name goes last," Vess said. "I extract in order of recent first. You have an hour or more of yourself remaining after I'm done." This was true, and she offered it because it was one of the only mercies her office contained: the extracted memories were copied, not cut. What she took, he would keep, diminished—the crown wanted the original pressed into its archive vials, but a copy taken gently left the source faded rather than emptied, like a book whose pages had been read too many times by too many hands.

She had learned, over nine years of service, to think of it as reading. That was what the gift was, hers, the reason the Crown's Inquisitors had pulled her out of a customs clerk's office in Greyharbor at nineteen and put a black badge in her hand: she could touch a mind and read what was written there. Not crudely—not the screaming fumbling of the old torturer-art, hot irons and terror—but truly read, lifting whole passages whole. A gifted man's talent lived in his memory like ink lives in paper, and Vess could lift the page.

"Begin when you're ready," Fen said.

She laid two fingers against his temple, closed her eyes, and read.

* * *

Rain first. Always rain, with the weather-workers; their minds were full of the sound of it. She moved through his recent days the way one moves through rooms in a house visited often—his loft above the cooper's shop, the smell of wet wool and cedar shavings, a woman's laugh in the stairwell, the particular grey of a sky he had been asked to argue with. Then further back, because the crown's questions were specific this season, and specific questions required deep pages.

*Have you seen others of your kind moving south? Have you heard the word covenant?*

And there—it surfaced like a fish turning under ice—a night three weeks past, a stranger at his door with snow in her hair and a sigil drawn in something that glowed faintly gold on the back of her hand. Vess leaned into the memory the way she'd lean toward a fire.

The stranger had spoken fast and low. *The veil is thinning. You've felt it—in your workings, the effort doubling, the results coming strange. It's the covenant breaking down, the old pact our grandmothers' grandmothers shattered. There are those trying to reforge it. There are names being gathered.* She had pressed a scrap of paper into Fen's hand. *If you can't come, pass this south. To the valley. To the Hollow.*

The Hollow. Vess held the word up to the light of her attention and examined it from all sides.

Fen had refused—he had a daughter, a loft, a life measured out in careful small magics—and the stranger had gone back into the snow. But the paper stayed in the memory, and Vess read it as Fen had read it, letter by letter: a list of names, most struck through, and at the bottom, in handwriting that seemed almost to move, a phrase she did not recognize and could not look away from:

*The keystone sleeps where magic cannot follow. Null-born, null-kept.*

She withdrew her fingers. Fen slumped slightly in the chair, the particular exhaustion of the read settling over him like a coat too heavy for the season.

"There was a woman," Vess said. "Three weeks ago. Describe her."

"Dark hair going grey at the temples. A scar through her eyebrow. She carried herself like—" He stopped, swallowed. "Like someone who used to matter."

"And the paper?"

"Burned it. Same night." His eyes came up, defiant and frightened at once. "You'll take what you take. But I didn't help her, and I didn't send anything anywhere, and Maret doesn't know any of this. Whatever you write in your report—she doesn't know."

Vess stood, gathered her notes, and paused at the door.

"No one knows anything," she said, "except what the crown finds useful."

It was the closest thing to kindness she had to give, and she hated how little it sounded like kindness even to her own ears.

* * *

Captain Sera Vess kept her office in the eastern tower of Fort Marrow, where the wind off the pass sang through arrow-slits at night in a key that made sleep difficult. She told herself she liked it. Discipline began with discomfort, her first commander had said, and she had built nine years on sayings like that, mortared them tight, made a wall of them she could stand on and see far.

Her office was austere by design: a desk scarred by decades of use, a shelf of report ledgers bound in black calfskin, a rack of memory-vials—slender glass things stoppered with wax, each labeled in her own precise hand. Forty-one vials. One for each gifted soul she had read since her elevation to captain. She knew them the way a librarian knows a collection: the miller's daughter who could sweeten milk, the soldier who'd healed his own broken arm and been shot for it, the old woman who dreamed true and had paid for ninety years of dreams.

She did not open the vials. She never listened to them twice. But she kept them, because the crown required it, and because some part of her—the part she did not examine at lamp-lit hours—believed that a memory pressed in glass was at least *kept*, somewhere, by someone, even if the keeper wore a black badge.

Tonight she unrolled the map of the western provinces and weighted its corners with candlesticks.

The Hollow lay at the map's edge like a thumbprint smudge, a valley ringed by hills, marked only by the cartographer's dismissive note: *temple lands, unproductive*. Every hunter learned about the Hollow in training, though not for its geography. They learned it because it was the one place in the kingdom where the gift failed utterly. Magic could not follow a soul across the valley line. Gifted folk who fled there simply... stopped. Their talents went silent as a bell wrapped in wool. The Inquisitor Corps had long considered it a curiosity and a convenience—a place the hunted could go to be harmless, which was nearly as good as dead, and spared the crown the cost of rope.

But the stranger's note said otherwise. *The keystone sleeps where magic cannot follow.* As if the Hollow were not a refuge but a vault. As if something had been *put* there, deliberately, in the place where nothing magical could wake it—or guard it—or be guarded against it.

Vess lit a second candle and pulled the season's reports toward her.

There had been signs all autumn, scattered across a dozen provincial ledgers like drops of dye in water. Weather-workers reporting their craft coming hard and strange. Hedge-witches describing dreams of a second sky. Two independent accounts of lights over the northern passes—lights that moved with intention, witnesses insisted, lights that *watched*. And in the border towns nearest the Hollow, an uptick in refugees, gifted families moving south in ones and twos, all of them drifting, like iron filings finding a lodestone, toward a valley where their gifts would die.

They weren't fleeing the hunt, Vess realized, tapping her quill against her teeth. Or not only fleeing it. They were *gathering*. Someone had sent out a call, and the call had named the Hollow, and the note in Fen's memory named the prize: a keystone. Null-born. Sleeping.

She wrote the evening's report in her clean, upright hand, the one her instructors had praised and her subordinates privately mocked. *Prisoner 41 (Fen, A., weather-worker) yielded intelligence confirming courier activity between dissident elements and the Hollow. Recommend immediate deployment to the valley under writ of inquiry. The Crown's interest in the site designated 'the Hollow' should be understood as preemptive.*

She signed it, sealed it with the black wax, and set it aside for the morning rider.

Then she sat for a long while with her hands laced together, watching the candles burn, and admitted to the empty room the thing she had been circling all evening.

Forty-one vials on her shelf. Forty-one people she had read. And in nine years, she had never once found what the writs promised she would find: conspiracy, sedition, the great hidden network of malice that justified the hunt. She had found farmers. Grandmothers. A boy who talked to crows. She had found fear, mostly, and love, and small stubborn kindnesses, and the ordinary architecture of lives that happened to include one impossible room.

The crown said the gifted were a rot in the kingdom's timber. Vess had read the timber closely enough to know it was mostly sound. But she served the crown, and the crown's logic was simple and had carried her far: better to fell ten sound trees than leave standing one rotten one beside a schoolhouse. Certainty was the whole of her trade. You did not become Hunter-Captain by doubting the axe.

And yet.

She looked at the forty-first vial, Fen's, still warm from the pressing, its contents swirling with a faint weather-grey luminescence. Inside it: rain on orchards, a daughter named Maret, a stranger with a scarred eyebrow speaking urgently of a covenant Vess had never heard named in any briefing. Inside it: a whole human life, decanted, labeled, shelved.

*Null-born, null-kept,* the note had said. Somewhere in that silent valley was a person who had never carried the gift at all—and yet the gifted were risking rope and road to reach them. That was not flight. That was pilgrimage. People did not make pilgrimages to nothing.

Vess rose, snuffed the candles, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the wind worry the arrow-slits.

In the morning she would request the assignment herself. Not delegate it—there were three lieutenants who could ride west—but take it, because if there was truly a keystone sleeping in the Hollow, the crown would want it found and taken, and Vess had learned long ago that whatever the crown wanted found, it was better found by someone who would also look at everything around it.

She told herself that was diligence. She told herself it was thoroughness, prudence, the same cold competence that had built her career.

The dark of the tower office did not answer, and did not agree, and she slept badly, and in her dreams it rained on someone else's orchards, and a woman with a scarred eyebrow was counting names aloud, and every time she reached the end of the list there was one more than she'd counted before—one more, always one more—as though the list itself refused to be finished, as though somewhere in the world a name was still being added, patiently, in ink that glowed.

* * *

The writ came back in four days, faster than any request Vess had ever filed, bearing the Privy Seal and a single line of instruction in the Lord Inquisitor's own hand: *Find the scribe. Bring the keystone. Burn nothing you cannot replace.*

That last clause troubled her more than all the rest. Nine years of writs, and never once had the crown cared what burned. She read the line a third time by the window, watching the supply wagons load in the yard below—more wagons than a simple inquiry needed, and among them two long, low conveyances she did not recognize, iron-banded, windowless, their purpose unmarked on any manifest.

"Captain." Lieutenant Harrow at the door, young, eager, his black badge bright as a beetle's shell. "Riders ready when you are. The Hollow, yes? Gods, I've always wanted to see the place where the gift dies. They say it's quiet as a grave."

"They say correctly," Vess said. She took up her cloak and her dispatch case, and paused with her hand on the door.

"Harrow. If we find what we're sent to find—do you suppose it matters why the crown wants it?"

He blinked at her, honestly puzzled. "Does it matter? We're the crown's long shadow, Captain. Shadows don't ask what the hand is reaching for."

"No," Vess said. "They don't."

She went down into the yard, mounted, and gave the order to ride west—toward the hills, the pass, and the silent valley where, unknown to her, a scribe with ink-stained fingers was about to touch a wounded stranger's bloodstained map and watch it glow.

Behind her, the windowless wagons followed at a slower pace, and Vess did not look back at them, and did not ask again what the hand was reaching for, and carried the question with her anyway, like a splinter working its way toward the surface of a certainty nine years deep.
