## 6. Debt Collectors

The bell in the temple tower rang the hour of first light, and Lena was already at her desk, as she had been for eleven years.

She did not remember deciding to be there. That was the nature of debt—it did not announce itself each morning, did not require renegotiation or consent. It simply *was*, the way the valley's stillness was, the way the fog that pooled in the Hollow before dawn was. You woke inside it and moved through it and called it your life.

Her pen scratched across the ledger page. *Third of Harvestmonth. Two bolts woolcloth, Maren Ostler, paid in full.* The ink caught the lamplight and dried dark, and somewhere below her window a cart creaked past on its way to market, and the world went on being ordinary, and she copied it all down so it would not vanish.

That was the thing about the Hollow that no one said aloud: things *did* vanish here. Names blurred. Grudges softened into vagueness, then into nothing at all. A traveler passing through would leave and within a season even his face would grow uncertain in the minds of those who'd spoken with him. Lena had learned young to write everything down—births, debts, quarrels, kindnesses—in her own private books, because the valley forgot, and someone had to remember for it.

She blew gently on the page and reached for the next one.

That was when she heard footsteps in the archive corridor, too many of them, moving with a purpose the temple's usual shuffling traffic never carried.

* * *

High Registrar Ondrell came in without knocking, which he never did. Behind him stood two men in gray-and-crimson livery she did not know, and behind them, filling the doorway like a blade fills a sheath, stood a woman in a hunter's long coat.

Lena knew the cut of that coat from the temple's own records. She had catalogued the crown's requisitions for years: wax, parchment, iron collars stamped with the sunburst seal. Witch-hunters. Here. In the Hollow, where magic could not follow.

"Master Registrar," she said carefully, rising. Her chair scraped. "I wasn't told to expect—"

"No," Ondrell said. He would not look at her. His eyes went to the shelves, to the ledgers, to the ceiling—to anywhere her face wasn't. "No, you weren't."

The woman stepped forward. She had a face like a closed ledger herself: ruled lines, nothing wasted. "Lena Dravenmoor," she said. It wasn't a question. "Captain Vess. Crown Office of Inquiry. I have some questions regarding your family's records."

"My mother's debt is current," Lena said. "It's all here. Eleven years of payments, every one receipted—"

"I'm not interested in the debt." Vess's gaze moved over the archive slowly, the way a butcher appraises an animal. "I'm interested in you. A scribe born in the null-lands. No gift, no touch of the old blood—or so this valley believes." She drew out a folded paper, cracked the wax seal. "A traveler arrived at the ford three days ago. Wounded. Speaking in a tongue of light. And the temple ledger shows a hand copied certain pages that night—pages since removed from the record."

Lena's heart stopped, then started again, harder. Corvin. She had hidden him in the disused tithe-barn beyond the orchards, bound his wounds with linen and willowbark, kept him fed on bread stolen from her own ration. She had told no one. But the temple saw everything. The temple *weighed* everything.

"The pages were misfiled," she said. "Administrative error. I corrected the record."

"Did you." Vess unfolded the paper fully. "Then you won't mind coming with us to verify the correction. Both you and your guest."

"They're not my guest. I've never seen him."

"Lena." Ondrell finally looked at her, and what was in his face was worse than cruelty—it was apology, pre-packaged, already filed and stamped. "Child, the crown has offered the temple remission of its tax arrears. All of them. Three hundred years of arrears, forgiven. In exchange for cooperation in identifying persons touched by the gift who may have sought refuge here." He smoothed his robes. "You understand. The temple must survive. Records endure when institutions endure."

"You sold me," Lena said quietly. "For a tax exemption."

"I *recorded* you," Ondrell said. "Which is, I am afraid, the same thing."

The hunters moved. Not fast—there was no need. One took the door; the other drifted along the shelves toward her desk, toward her private books, and Vess watched Lena with those ledger-line eyes and said, almost kindly:

"Run, if you like. The valley has only two roads out, and we hold both. But running confirms what the glow-witnesses reported. So." She gestured, courteous as a clerk. "Shall we walk?"

And Lena—who had spent eleven years learning that every door in the Hollow opened onto the same room, that every path circled back to the debt, that the valley held her like a fist holds a coin—felt something crack cleanly through her, quiet and total.

Not fear. Something colder. *Accounting.*

"Yes," she said. "Let's walk."

* * *

They walked her through the cloister garth, past the scriptorium where junior registrars bent over their desks in the gray light, past the bell-tower whose shadow lay across the flagstones like a sundial counting down. Lena counted doors. Counted guards. Counted, always, because counting was the one thing they could not take from her.

Vess walked beside her, unhurried. "Your mother," the captain said. "Serra Dravenmoor. Died eight years ago, debt unpaid, transferred to next of kin. Curious thing—the original loan was never for coin at all. Temple holdings show it was recorded as 'services rendered, nature undisclosed.'" She glanced sideways. "What services, do you suppose?"

"She copied records," Lena said. "Like me."

"Mm." Vess smiled faintly, and it did not reach her eyes. "The women in your family are very good at copying things. Almost as though the words want to come through you." She let that sit in the cold air between them. "There are witnesses at the ford who say your hand glowed, girl. Glowed like script. Do you know what covenant-script looks like when it wakes?"

"I know what ink looks like," Lena said. "When it dries."

Ahead, the gatehouse loomed, and beyond it the orchards, bare-branched now, rows of apple trees standing in the frost like an audience waiting. Beyond the orchards, the tithe-barn. Beyond that, the pass, and the world.

Two guards flanked the gate. Vess raised a hand—

—and the bell tower began to ring.

Wrong. It was hours early, and the pattern was wrong—not the measured hour-stroke but a rapid, clanging alarm, and Lena heard, beneath it, a sound she knew from her childhood: the dry roar of flame finding paper.

Smoke rose above the eastern wing. Above the archive.

"*No*—" The word tore out of her before she knew she had a voice for it.

"Standard procedure," Vess said, and for the first time there was something uneasy under her flatness. "Records of the gifted are destroyed after extraction. It prevents—"

"That's my *life*." Lena turned on her, and the fury in her own chest frightened her, it was so vast, so sudden, eleven years of smallness igniting all at once. "Eleven years. Every birth, every death, every name this valley tried to forget—I wrote them down. My mother's receipts. My own receipts. Everything I *am* is in those ledgers, and you're burning them because a man bled on a map?"

For one heartbeat—one—something flickered across Vess's face. Doubt, or its cousin. Then it was gone, shuttered away, and she said, "Restrain her," and the guard reached for Lena's arm—

—and the guard screamed.

He staggered back clutching his wrist, where the skin had gone white as paper, blank as an unwritten page. The other hunter spun toward the smoke, toward the orchard wall, where a figure stood swaying among the bare trees—Corvin, gray-faced, fever-bright, one hand raised and trembling with effort.

"Run," he rasped. "*Now,* girl, while I can still—"

Lena ran.

She ran through the gatehouse before the guards recovered, through the frost-stiff grass of the garth, past the burning archive where windows burst outward in gouts of sparks and drifting ash—pages, whole pages of it, lifting on the heat like black-winged birds. Eleven years spiraling up into the gray sky. She did not stop. If she stopped she would go into that fire after them, and the fire would take her too, and the valley would forget her the way it forgot everything, and no one would even know there had been anything to forget.

She reached the orchard. Corvin caught her arm—his grip burning hot with fever—and hauled her down the rows, frost crunching, shouts rising behind them.

"The wall," he gasped. "Gap in the wall, third row, I came in—"

They ran. Behind them the temple bells rang on, and Ondrell's voice rose somewhere in the smoke, thin and bureaucratic even now, calling for order, for documentation, for the incident report, and Lena thought: *eleven years, and he'll file my escape between the woolcloth entries and the grain tallies.*

She thought: *it never held me anyway.*

That was the grief, when it came—not for the flames, not even for the ledgers, but for the understanding underneath them, arriving all at once as her boots tore through frozen mud: that the Hollow had never been home. It had been a holding cell with apple trees. It had kept her the way a ledger keeps a number: entered, totaled, owed. And now the number was fleeing the page.

The gap in the wall was barely shoulder-width. Corvin shoved her through first, and thorns raked her sleeve, and then they were through, and downhill, and the pass opened ahead—a gray wound in the mountains, breathing mist.

Behind them, smoke. Ahead, everything else.

"Can you walk?" Corvin panted.

"I've walked in circles for eleven years," Lena said. Her voice shook. "I can manage a straight line."

He laughed—a raw, broken sound—and put his shoulder under hers, and together they went up into the pass, while behind them the archive burned, and the valley that forgot everything went on forgetting, already smoothing over the place where she had been.

At the ridgeline Lena stopped, once, and looked back. The temple's towers stood against the smoke. Somewhere in that blaze were her mother's receipts, her childhood marginalia, the record of every small stubborn act of remembering she had ever committed against the valley's soft erasure.

Gone. All of it, gone.

Except.

Her hand slipped into her coat, to the inner pocket where—habit older than sense—she had tucked three loose pages torn from her private book that morning, as she did every morning, because a scribe who trusts an institution is a fool. Three pages. Her handwriting. Hers alone.

She pressed them once, hard, against her chest.

"Come on," Corvin said softly. "The road's long, and they ride faster than we bleed."

Lena Dravenmoor looked at the pass, at the world beyond it that she had never once seen, and stepped off the edge of everything she knew.
