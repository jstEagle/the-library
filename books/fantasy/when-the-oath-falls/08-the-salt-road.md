## 8. The Salt Road

The salt road ran white as a scar through the brown hills, and Corvin walked it the way a man walks toward his own trial—steadily, because stopping would be worse.

Lena walked ahead of him. She had done so for most of two days, keeping three paces between them as though the distance were a wall she'd built out of everything she'd seen in the orchards: the burning archive, the hunters' torchlight, the High Registrar's face going smooth and satisfied as he traded her name for a tax exemption. Three paces was not far enough to escape any of that. Corvin knew, because he had spent thirty years trying to put that kind of distance between himself and his own order, and had failed at every step.

Behind them, the Hollow sat in its valley like a stone at the bottom of a well. From this ridge he could see the whole of it: the grey temple roof, the orchards with their frost-burned branches, the fields where nothing grew quite as tall as it should have and no one remembered why. The stillness place. The null. His order's oldest sin, wearing the disguise of a peaceful valley.

"Is it true?" Lena asked without turning around. She hadn't spoken since they'd crossed the ford at dawn. "What you said at the river. That the Hollow wasn't always like this."

Corvin considered lying. It would have been easy. It was, in fact, the family trade—the Order of the Veiled Lamp had been lying for six generations, so fluently that by his grandfather's time they no longer recognized their own lies as lies. They called them *mercy*. They called them *stability*. They carved the words into the lintels of their hidden houses: *We broke it so the world would hold.*

"It's true," he said.

She stopped walking. The wind came up off the salt flats and moved her hair across her face, and when she pushed it back he saw that she had been crying, or nearly, the way people cry who have taught themselves not to waste the water.

"Then tell me," she said. "All of it. You've been giving me pieces, Corvin. Pieces are what the temple did. Pieces are how the debt grew." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it—he could see her hating it—and she lifted her chin anyway. "I burned my life down two days ago. I think I've earned the whole shape of why."

He looked east, where the road unspooled toward the shrine beneath the old mill, four days' walk if the weather held. Then he looked at the low stone marker beside the road, one of hundreds that dotted these hills, each one engraved with a symbol that meant *nothing* to any passing farmer but everything to him: a lamp with a closed eye.

"All right," he said. "But not standing. Guilt goes down easier sitting."

* * *

They sat beneath a rowan tree whose berries hung like drops of blood too bright for the grey season, and Corvin told her about the Order of the Veiled Lamp.

"We were oath-keepers once. Before we were anything else. When the covenant was forged—three names, freely given, binding the realm of flesh to the realm of light—we were the ones who kept the ledger of it. Not ink ledgers. Living ones. Each generation, seven keepers, each carrying one-seventh of the covenant in memory, passed mouth to mouth like a flame. We were the hinge, Lena. We were the reason the door between realms stayed a door instead of a wound."

"And then?"

"And then the plague came." He said it flatly, because there was no other honest way to say it. "The Grey Sweating, in the year your histories call 611. You won't find it in any temple record—the Hollow keeps records of debts, not disasters—but it killed one person in three across the northern kingdoms. In the cities, one in two. And the gifted died worst of all, because magic draws what passes through the veil, and the plague came *through* the veil. It was the first crack, you understand? The first hairline fracture in something that had held for nine hundred years. The covenant was straining. The other realm was bleeding sickness into ours like a wound gone septic, and the keepers were dying faster than they could pass their memories on."

Lena had gone very still. "So they broke it."

"So my ancestors broke it." He made himself meet her eyes. "Say it correctly. My ancestors. My blood. I carry the same hands in my hands."

The wind moved through the rowan. Somewhere down the slope, a crow complained about the weather.

"There were eleven keepers left," he went on. "Out of seven hundred years of succession, eleven. They met at the First Altar—you'll see it soon enough, if we live that long—and they voted. Nine to two. Unbinding. They reasoned it like clerks, because that's what six centuries of bookkeeping does to holy men: if the veil is cracking, close the door entirely. If the other realm is bleeding into ours, cut the connection. They took the covenant's three names and they did not release them—that would have required the names' consent, and consent was the whole architecture—they *buried* them. Sealed them under wards. Locked the door from our side and threw away the key."

"But the door didn't stay locked."

"No." Corvin picked up a fallen rowan berry and crushed it between his fingers; the juice stained his skin red for hours, always had. "A covenant isn't a lock, it's a living thing. You can't kill it, only maim it. So for three hundred years the realms have been pulling apart along a wound that never healed. Magic thinning in some places—" he gestured back the way they'd come, toward the Hollow— "and pooling wrong in others. The dead walking badly. Children born dreaming of a country that doesn't exist. Crops failing in valleys that were fertile for a thousand years before. Slow rot, everywhere, all at once. And on the other side—"

"On the other side it must be worse," Lena said quietly. "They're starving. Whatever lives over there. I saw it in the script on your map, when my hand lit up. It wasn't a warning. It was a *prayer*."

Corvin looked at her then, really looked, and felt the old guilt shift inside him like a stone settling deeper into mud. Six generations of his order had known what they'd done. Six generations had written justifications in cipher and hidden them in walled rooms, and every justification amounted to the same sentence: *we were afraid, and we chose ourselves.* But none of them had ever said it aloud to a descendant of the people their cowardice had hollowed out.

"The other realm is dying," he said. "Slowly, the way we're rotting, but from hunger instead of sickness. Everything there feeds on resonance—on contact, exchange, the crossing-over. We sealed the border and we sentenced an entire world to famine. And the crown knows. The crown has known for a century. That's what the witch-hunters actually are—not protectors, harvesters. They're cutting the gifted out of the population like gems from ore, and feeding what they take into the cracks, trying to pry the door open on *their* terms. A kingdom that owns the only key. That's the future Vess's masters are building."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a dry-stone wall, and Corvin took first watch because sleep had become a country he visited less and less often.

In the dark, the past came for him, as it always did. It came in his grandfather's voice.

*You are a keeper of nothing,* Aldous Vane had said, teaching ten-year-old Corvin the cipher that unlocked the order's false histories. *Remember that above all. We keep nothing. We guard a sealed room, boy, and our whole duty is that no one—including yourself—ever learns what's inside.*

Except Corvin had learned. At nineteen, in the archive-house at Greywater, he had broken the seventh seal out of nothing more than the ordinary human disease of curiosity, and read the confession of Maren Vane, his great-great-great-grandmother, one of the two who voted against the breaking.

*We are murderers,* she had written, in ink that had gone brown as old blood. *Not of persons but of a promise, which is worse, because a murdered person is mourned and buried and finished, while a murdered promise bleeds forever. My colleagues believe we have saved the world. I believe we have amputated it, and that the stump will fester until someone finds the courage we lacked. If you are reading this, then the festering has begun in earnest. Go to the shrines. Wake what sleeps beneath the mills and the wells. And forgive us nothing. Forgiveness is not yours to give—it belongs to the realms we wounded.*

He had run that same year. Thirty-one years ago now. He had told himself he was renouncing the order, but the truth was uglier: he had simply taken his inheritance and fled with it, carrying the guilt like a coin he couldn't spend and couldn't drop. He had spent three decades half-hiding among hedge-mages and relic-traders, telling himself that survival was a form of penance, knowing it was a lie.

And then the hunters had come for him too, and a null-born scribe with light in her hand had pulled him out of the ford, and suddenly the coin was spending itself whether he willed it or not.

Across the dead fire, Lena slept curled against her satchel like it might be stolen from her—which, he supposed, in every way that mattered, it already had been. Even asleep, her right hand stayed half-closed, as if still holding the memory of the map glowing under her fingers.

*An unwitting heir.* That's what he'd called her at the river, and it was true, and it was also the cruelest thing he could have said, though he hadn't meant it cruelly. She was heir to the Oathkeepers by blood-line and by the fragment of covenant-script that lived in her the way other people carried freckles. The old keepers had scattered their seed-wide after the breaking, deliberately, hiding fragments of the covenant in bloodlines across the north—a fail-safe, in case anyone ever grew brave enough to attempt the reforging. Lena's line had ended up in the one place in the world where the fragment could sleep undisturbed: the null valley, where magic couldn't reach to wake it.

Until she'd touched his map at the ford. Until the script had risen in her skin like a brand recognizing its owner.

She was the keystone. He'd known it within a heartbeat of seeing her hand shine. Which meant that everything that happened to her next was, in some ledger of the soul he couldn't stop keeping, his fault. He had brought the hunt to her. He had brought the knowledge. He had brought the road, and the road went through fire.

*Forgive us nothing,* Maren had written.

He intended to obey her.

* * *

On the third day the hills gave way to the salt flats proper, and the world turned to bone and mirror. Crusted white earth stretched to the horizon, cracked into polygons like the skin of something enormous sleeping just below, and the sky lay doubled in every pool of brine so that they seemed to walk between two heavens. The air tasted of minerals and old rain. Here and there, leaning posts marked the road where snow or flood could erase it, each post crowned with a bird skull—pilgrim-markers from an age when people had crossed these flats to worship at the shrines openly.

Lena had begun writing again. He'd found paper for her in the last village, traded his silver ring for it without mentioning that the ring was his order's signet, the veiled lamp stamped inside the band. Now she walked and wrote in equal measure, filling pages with everything the journey showed her: the color of the salt at noon, the names of birds, the exact words he used in his stories about the shrines. When he asked her why—though he suspected he knew—she said only:

"The valley forgot things. I learned young that if you don't write it down, it's like it never happened." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to herself: "I'm not losing anything else to forgetting."

He thought of what the reforging would cost. He thought of it constantly, the way a man tongues a broken tooth. The covenant demanded three names, freely given, and the third name—the keystone's name, the anchor-name—was always forgotten by everyone who loved its bearer. The original covenant's third giver was remembered nowhere on earth except in the sealed archives of his order, under a designation instead of a name: *the Third Voice.* Whole. Loved. Erased.

If Lena understood what she was walking toward, she gave no sign. Or perhaps—this thought arrived at night and kept him company against his will—perhaps she understood perfectly, and was writing everything down precisely because she had guessed that ink might outlast memory.

That evening, camped in the shadow of the first real hills, she asked him about the shrine.

"The mill shrine. What will we find there?"

"A keeper, if we're fortunate. The Millward line kept that shrine for two hundred years—millers, always, because a mill wheel turning water is a rhythm the other realm loves, a heartbeat it can lean against. If any of the old lines survived the hunt, it's theirs." He fed the small fire a stick of salt-bleached driftwood. "And if we're not fortunate, we'll find ash and a defiled altar, and we'll have learned that the crown is ahead of us."

"And if it's ash? What then?"

"Then we go to Dunharrow." He watched her face carefully as he said it. "The market city. There are... brokers there. People who trade in memories. If the shrines are dead, the covenant's fragments survive in other vessels—in extracted memories, sold and resold, scattered through the broker markets like seeds in chaff. It's an abomination, what they do. Slicing a life into sellable pieces." He hesitated. "It may also be the only map left."

Lena stared into the fire for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful evenness she'd probably learned copying ledgers, the voice of a person refusing to let cost show on the page.

"My mother," she said. "When she died, the temple seized everything. Letters, keepsakes, all of it, against her debt. I have never once heard her voice—not even in my own memory. I was seven. Memory wears out, Corvin. Mine of her is mostly gone. Just a smell, and a hand on my hair, and the feeling of a door closing." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and fierce and very tired. "You're telling me there's a city where I could buy a stranger's memory of her."

"I'm telling you memories are for sale there. What they contain—I can't promise. But yes. Possibly. Someone, somewhere, may have carried a piece of her."

The fire popped. Far off on the dark flats, wind moved the salt in long white breaths, and somewhere beyond the veil of the world, in a starving country made of light, something leaned against the thin places and waited.

"Then we go to Dunharrow," Lena said, "after the shrine. And Corvin—" she fixed him with a look that had ledger-lines in it, the look of someone auditing a soul, "—when we get there, you're going to tell me the rest. About your order. About what your family did to mine. All the pieces you're still holding back. Because I can see them behind your teeth."

He held her gaze, and the guilt sat where it always sat, patient as an heirloom.

"Yes," he said. "I will."

It was not entirely a lie. There were pieces he would give her willingly, when the road allowed. And there was one piece—the price of the third name, the forgetting, the erasure waiting for her at the end of this road—that he would carry alone as far as the mountain let him, because some inheritances, he told himself, were a guardian's to bear.

He was still telling himself that when the fire burned low, and still believing it not at all.

Three days later, they crested the hill above the mill village—and Corvin saw the smoke, and the crows, and the wheel turning slowly over a stream that ran red-tinged into the flats—and knew that the crown had gotten here first.
