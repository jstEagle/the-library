## 9. Memories for Sale

Dunharrow smelled of tallow and wet stone and, beneath it all, something faintly sweet that Lena could not name until she saw the first broker's stall.

The city clung to the side of a river gorge like a swallow's nest, tier upon tier of leaning houses connected by stairs that had been worn into shallow bowls by centuries of feet. Corvin had led them down out of the hills for two days, hood drawn low, speaking only when necessary, and now he stood at the mouth of the lower market with the wary stillness of a man re-entering a room where he had once been beaten.

"The Salt Road ends here," he said quietly. "Everything in the kingdom passes through Dunharrow eventually. Grain, iron, salt." He paused. "Memories."

Lena had thought she misheard him. But then the crowd shifted, and she saw it: a stall draped in indigo cloth, hung with dozens of small glass vials that caught the grey morning light and threw it back in colors that did not quite belong to this world. A woman behind the stall held a vial to a customer's eye the way an apothecary holds medicine, murmuring something Lena could not catch. The customer pressed a coin into her palm, lifted the vial, unstopped it—and drank.

The man swayed. His face went slack, then flooded—wonder, grief, a child's uncomplicated joy passing over his features in quick succession like weather. When he opened his eyes again they were wet, and he was laughing, and he walked away holding the empty vial against his chest as if it were made of gold.

"What did he buy?" Lena whispered.

"Hard to say." Corvin's voice was flat, but she had learned by now to hear the tension underneath it, tight as wire. "A first kiss, maybe. The birth of a child he never had. A summer that belonged to someone else. The brokers distill what people sell. Grief sells well—the bereaved want to be rid of the worst nights. Joy sells better. There are men in this city who have drunk a hundred other lives and can't remember which one is theirs."

Lena stared at the stall. At the vials. Each one held no more than a swallow of liquid, and each one glowed very slightly from within—not the clean silver light she had seen bloom from her own hand at the ford, but something dimmer, warmer, the color of lamplight through honey.

*Magic cannot follow into the Hollow.* She had lived twenty-two years in that stillness without knowing it was stillness, the way a fish does not know water. Now every step outside it was a lesson. In the Hollow her thoughts had been quiet things, contained. Here they *leaked*. She felt the city's thousand small magics brushing against her skin like moths—a charm woven into a fishwife's shawl, a luck-knot on a doorpost, the residual warmth of a healing done badly in some upstairs room. And beneath all of it, faint but constant, the pull of her own blood, humming like a struck bell that would not finish its note.

Corvin had taught her the anchor-words on the road. *Ink remembers when flesh forgets.* She wrote them now, mentally, tracing each letter on the inside of her wrist with two fingers, the way he'd shown her, and the hum settled to something bearable.

"Why here?" she asked. "You said the first shrine is north."

"It is. But shrines leave traces, and traces leave witnesses, and witnesses come to market." He nodded toward a crooked lane descending between two dye-houses, where the smell of mordant cut through everything. "Memory-brokers keep their own archives, unofficial ones. If any Oathkeeper passed through Dunharrow in the last thirty years—if anyone sold a memory that touched the covenant—it will be in a broker's ledger. And there's another reason." He hesitated, and she saw him decide, deliberately, to tell her. "You said you know nothing of your mother beyond the debt. Markets like this one... people sell memories of the dead, too. Sometimes the dead left things behind that only memory can carry."

Lena went cold all over. Her mother had been dead eleven years, and in eleven years Lena had learned exactly three facts about her: that her name was Maren, that she had owed the temple a debt large enough to bind her daughter to its copying desks, and that she had died with a look on her face that the temple physician had called *peace* and Lena had always privately called *recognition*—as if, at the end, her mother had finally remembered something important.

She had never once considered that the something might be for sale.

---

The broker Corvin sought worked out of a cellar beneath a chandler's shop, down a stair so narrow they had to go single file. At the bottom, a door of blackened oak swung open before Corvin knocked, revealing a round little man with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of weak tea.

"You," the man said to Corvin, and his voice dropped to a whisper that filled the whole cellar anyway. "They said the order was ash. They said the light-tongues were all dead or worse."

"They said a great many things, Master Quill." Corvin pushed back his hood just far enough. "And we were never called light-tongues to our faces unless the speaker wanted a duel."

The cellar was smaller than it had sounded, and fuller. Every wall was shelved floor to ceiling, and every shelf was ranked with vials—thousands of them, tens of thousands, each labeled in a cramped hand with dates and names and, sometimes, single words: *fire. sea. forgiveness. the last time.* Lamps burned behind colored glass so that the whole room seemed to swim underwater. Lena felt the memories before she understood what she was feeling: a pressure, a murmur, the sense of standing in a crowd of sleeping people all dreaming at once.

Quill caught her looking and smiled with genuine pride. "Forty years of collection, miss. Every vial consented and paid for, I'll have you know—I don't deal in theft. Stolen memory sours. Turns bitter in the bottle, goes grey. You can taste a lie in someone else's past." He bustled to a desk buried under ledgers. "Now. What does a dead order want in my cellar?"

"Traces," Corvin said. "Oathkeeper passage through Dunharrow. Any sale touching the covenant, the old script, the border-realm. Within the last forty years."

Quill's smile faded. He looked at Corvin for a long moment, then at Lena, and something in his gaze sharpened from merchant to craftsman. "That's crown-gallows talk now, you know. Since the hunters came through in autumn, half my stock is contraband. They burned Marrow-street's stalls. Took Old Nessa away in irons for selling a widow the memory of her son's face." He spat, delicately, into a cuspidor. "So whatever I show you, I showed you nothing."

"Nothing," Corvin agreed.

Quill pulled a ledger toward himself, licked his thumb, and began turning pages. Lena drifted along the shelves while he searched, unable to help herself, reading labels. *A wedding in the rain, 1189. The drowning and the rescue, both. My father teaching me letters.* Whole lives in glass. She touched a vial marked only *her laugh* and snatched her hand back when it warmed under her fingers, as if the memory inside had noticed being touched.

"Here." Quill's finger stopped on a page. His tea-colored eyes flicked up, cautious. "Covenant-sale, thirty-one years back. Seller unnamed—brokers honor that even after death, mostly. Buyer's mark is mine. It came in with a batch from a traveling peddler who worked the western valleys." He paused. "Hollow country, if you follow me."

Lena stopped breathing.

"There were three vials in the batch," Quill went on, softer now, watching her face with the attention of a man watching weather turn. "Two were dross—a fair-day, a quarrel. The third..." He rose, crossed to a shelf near the back, behind a curtain of beads, and returned cradling a single vial in both palms like a bird with a broken wing. "The third I've never sold. Not because it isn't fine. Because it's the finest thing I own, and because some things you don't put back into circulation. You just... keep the lamp lit over them."

He set it on the desk between them. The liquid inside was pale gold shot through with threads of silver, and it moved when no one breathed on it, swirling slowly around a center that was not a center, and Lena knew—she knew the way she had known the map at the ford would glow—that she was looking at her mother.

"How much?" Her voice came out strange, scraped thin.

"Lena." Corvin's hand closed gently on her arm. "We don't know whose memory this is. We don't know what's in it. Drinking a stranger's past unfiltered can crack a person. I've seen it. You wake up loving people you never met and grieving children you never had, and you spend the rest of your life sorting yours from theirs—"

"She was a scribe." The words fell out of Lena like stones. "The seller. Thirty-one years ago, western valleys, Hollow country. My mother copied ledgers for the temple her whole life. She died eleven years ago." She looked up at Corvin, and to her horror her eyes were burning. "You told me myself that the covenant needs scribes. That the Oathkeepers' whole art is *writing that remembers*. What else would my mother have been carrying? What else would she have sold?"

The cellar was silent except for the murmur of ten thousand dreams.

Quill slid the vial an inch toward her. "One copper," he said quietly. "Standard rate. But for you, miss—for this one—" he shook his head, "-no coin. Consider it a debt paid forward. Your mother's trade kept more than one of us honest."

Lena looked at the copper in her hand. She had carried temple coppers for years, each one weighed against her ledger, each one an hour of her life converted to currency. This one she set on the desk anyway, because some transactions need to be completed to be real.

Then she took the vial.

---

Corvin wanted her to wait. Wanted daylight, wanted a safehouse, wanted preparation, wards, an anchor written on paper rather than skin. But the vial was warm in her palm, warming further, and the murmur of it was not the crowd-murmur of the shelves; it was singular now, focused, patient. Waiting the way a letter waits to be opened.

They took a corner of the cellar, and Quill turned down the lamps, and Corvin knelt beside her with his hand hovering near her shoulder—not touching, ready to catch.

"Anchor-word first," he said. "Say it."

"*Ink remembers when flesh forgets.*"

"Good. Whatever you see, hold onto your own name. It's the rope across the river. Don't let go of the rope."

Lena unstopped the vial.

It smelled of nothing at all, and then of everything—woodsmoke, bread, lavender water, the particular cold-clean scent of temple parchment. She drank.

The world folded.

---

She was standing in a kitchen she had never seen and knew instantly: low beams, a window over the basin, frost ferning the glass. Her hands were older hands. Her mother's hands. She looked down at them and felt the ache of them, the copyist's ache, thumb joints swollen from decades of pens. Outside the frost-window a valley lay white and silent, and the silence of it was not emptiness but *held breath*, and Lena understood with a lurch that she was seeing the Hollow from the outside-in, seeing the stillness she had been born inside of.

*Maren,* said a voice—not heard but remembered, the way memory carries voices. *Maren, you can't keep writing it down.*

*I have to.* Her mother's voice, from inside her mother's chest, and Lena wept without weeping because it was the first time in eleven years she had heard it. It was lower than she had imagined. Warm, wry, tired. *They broke it, Aldric. Three names, freely given, and our grandsires spent theirs like tavern coins, and now the veil thins every winter and the valley has to forget harder every year to cover it. Someone has to keep the record. Someone has to remember what everyone else was made to forget.*

*The temple watches you.*

*The temple employs me.* A laugh, and the kitchen brightened with it. *Best place to hide a book is in the library, love. Best place to hide an oathkeeper is behind a debt ledger.*

The scene shifted, the way dreams shift, without transition. Now Maren was kneeling in a cellar much like this one, pressing a bundle of pages into a young man's hands—Quill, younger, dark-haired, terrified. *Keep these out of the archive. Out of any archive. Bury them if you must, but don't burn them, whatever they offer you.* And Quill saying, *Mistress, the hunters—they're asking questions in the western towns—* and Maren answering, calm as ledger-lines, *I know. I've known since my daughter was born. She's the keystone, Quill. The last thread of the third name runs through her blood, and the day the crown understands that is the day I make sure there's nothing left on paper to find. Only in heads. Only in memory, where they'll never think to hunt—*

The memory lurched. Years had passed—or minutes; memory keeps its own calendar. A sickroom. Candlelight. Hands that had once held pens now too weak to lift them, gripping instead the bedframe. And Maren thinking, in the clear deliberate cadence of a woman composing her last transaction:

*Eleven years I've watched her grow in that valley, copying other people's accounts, paying my debts with her life. She deserves better than a mother's grave and a number in a ledger. She deserves the truth, but the truth would paint a target on her, so—so I'll leave it where only she will ever find it. Where only someone with the covenant in their blood could even think to look. Let the market keep it. Let it wait. My girl always did love the archives.*

The last image was not an image at all but a feeling, vast and simple and unbearable: love, undiluted, aimed like an arrow through thirty-one years directly into the chest of the daughter drinking it. *I'm sorry I couldn't stay. I'm sorry the debt was the only shield I could give you. Read the margins, my love. Everything I couldn't say, I wrote in margins—*

The vial was empty.

---

Lena came back to herself on the cellar floor with Corvin's arms around her shoulders and Quill holding her head steady, and she was crying so hard she could not speak, and she did not try for a long time.

Secondhand love. That was what this was. Love poured into a stranger's keeping, sold and resold, stored under a bead curtain behind colored lamps for three decades, waiting. Her mother had never held her and said these things—but she had *bottled* them, had trusted the world's strangest marketplace to deliver what she could not, and it had. Eleven years late. One copper's worth.

"She was one of us," Corvin said quietly, when Lena could finally hear anything besides her own heartbeat. "An Oathkeeper in all but name. Hiding behind the temple's ledgers." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "All those years I searched the orders and the shrines and the mountain holds, and the last keeper of the western line was a debt-scribe in the most magic-dead valley in the kingdom. Of course she was. It's exactly what I would have done."

"The margins." Lena's voice was raw. "She said read the margins. Corvin—my ledgers. Every ledger I copied in that temple, she trained me to check the margins. She used to run her finger down the edge of every page and say, *the account is never the whole story.*" She sat up, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, and the grief in her chest was already transmuting into something harder and brighter. "Her records weren't destroyed with the archive, Corvin. Some of them were *copied out*. By me. Page by page, year after year. I've been carrying her covenant-notes in my saddlebag this entire time and didn't know what I was reading."

Corvin went very still. "The practice pages," he said slowly. "The ones you showed me at the ford crossing—the ones in the old script you thought were just penmanship drills."

"They weren't drills." Lena was already pulling the satchel toward her, already untying its strings with shaking fingers. "They were her hand disguised as mine. She hid the oath-line fragments in my exercises, and I memorized every one."

It was Quill who stiffened first. The little broker's eyes had gone past them both, up the cellar stair, to the chandler's shop above, where the floorboards had begun to creak in a rhythm too heavy and too numerous for customers.

"Hunters," he breathed. "They've been asking after strangers for two days. I thought I'd have warning." He was already moving, snuffing lamps, sweeping vials into padded cases with the speed of long practice. "Back door, through the tallow store. Go now, go fast, and miss—*" he caught Lena's sleeve, pressed something into her hand: a second, empty vial, stoppered with wax, "-write her name somewhere she can't be forgotten. Names are the only currency I've ever seen appreciate."

Above them, the shop door banged open, and a voice like a drawn blade called down the stairwell:

"This premises is seized under warrant of the Crown. Come up slowly, all of you—and bring whatever you're hiding."

Corvin blew out the last lamp.
