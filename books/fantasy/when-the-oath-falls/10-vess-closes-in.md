## 10. Vess Closes In

The memory tasted of copper and river water.

Hunter-Captain Vess sat alone in the interrogation room beneath the Dunharrow garrison, the extraction still humming in her mind like a struck bell. The prisoner—a hedge-touched fiddler they'd taken on the Salt Road three days past—had carried more than songs in his head. He'd carried a face. A girl with ink-stained fingers and a stranger bleeding at her side, slipping through the orchard gates of a valley that did not exist on any crown map.

Vess closed her eyes and let the memory play again.

Rain on leaves. The fiddler, younger in the remembering, standing at a crossroads shrine, watching two figures pass below him on the road. The girl moved like someone afraid of open sky—shoulders drawn up, eyes darting to every shadow as though the shadows might be watching back. The man leaned on her arm, his bandages dark with old blood, and when he spoke he gestured at things that weren't there: threads of light only he could see.

*Null-born,* the fiddler had thought, in the moment itself, in the flat way smallfolk thought of such things. *That's the scribe from the Hollow. The null-born. She's real.*

Vess opened her eyes. The candle on the table had burned down to a stub, wax pooling around a wick shaped like a tiny drowned thing. She had been sitting here for hours, walking through another person's stolen afternoon, and the strangeness of it never dulled—the way she could stand inside a stranger's skull and look out through his eyes, smell what he had smelled, feel the damp of his fear against his own skin.

Her gift was not a gift. That was what the Academy taught, and Vess had believed it once, in the white corridors where they trained girls like her to pull memories from the minds of the condemned. It was an instrument. A tool of order. The gifted could not be permitted to roam unchecked; therefore their thoughts must be read, catalogued, and where necessary, removed. That the reading hurt them—that the extraction left most prisoners hollow-eyed and weeping, reaching for pieces of themselves that no longer existed—was regrettable. Necessary. Regrettable.

She had repeated those words for eleven years.

Now she stood, straightened her grey coat, and climbed the stairs into the noise of Dunharrow.

* * *

Dunharrow in winter was a city holding its breath. Snow came down in fat, indifferent flakes, settling on the tiled roofs of the merchant quarter and the sagging shingles of the Tanners' Ward alike, as if the sky could not be bothered to distinguish between wealth and poverty. Vess walked the main thoroughfare with her collar up and her hunters flanking her—Brannoc on her left, broad and silent as a door; young Pell on her right, whose hands still shook after extractions, though he'd learned to hide it well enough.

"The fiddler's memory puts them on the Salt Road eight days ago," Brannoc said. "Headed east."

"Everyone in this kingdom is headed east," Vess said. "Half of them are fugitives, and the other half are chasing them."

"But the null-born—"

"Is one girl." Vess stopped beneath the eave of a chandler's shop and watched the crowd flow past: fishwives with baskets on their hips, a knot of students arguing outside the scriptorium, two soldiers from the city watch sharing a joke over mulled wine. Ordinary people, living ordinary lives, unaware that somewhere above them the veil between realms thinned by another hair's breadth with each passing week. Unaware that the crown's answer to that slow catastrophe was fire.

"She may be one girl who is also a keystone," Pell said quietly. "If the Archivist's theory holds."

Vess did not answer. The Archivist's theories were not hers to dispute—not anymore, not since the orders had changed. For years the hunt had been simple: find the gifted, extract, record, contain. Clean work, if you didn't look too closely at the contained. But six months ago the directives from the capital had shifted, subtle as a change in current. Fewer extractions. More burnings. And always, underneath the official language of *threat assessment*, a new word kept surfacing in the dispatches, written in the Archivist's cramped hand:

*Harvest.*

She pushed the thought away. There would be time to interrogate her orders later. First, the girl.

"Market square," Vess said. "The fiddler saw them near the shrine row. If they bought supplies, someone sold to them. Someone remembers."

*Someone always remembers.* That was the truth at the heart of her trade, and she hated how much comfort she took in it.

* * *

The market square of Dunharrow wore its winter market like a borrowed coat. Stalls huddled under awnings heavy with snow, selling chestnuts and salt-fish, charms against frostbite that were probably just painted wood, and—here, at the far end, in a lane the city watch pretended not to see—memories.

The memory-brokers worked out of booths hung with bells, each broker displaying their wares differently. Some kept vials, stoppered and labeled in faded ink. Some claimed to hold the memories in their own heads, renting them out by the hour. One old woman simply spread objects across her table: a child's shoe, a wedding ring, a lock of hair, each one soaked through with somebody's yesterdays.

Vess had raided such places before. It was standard procedure—unlicensed memory-trade was technically contraband, though the brokers paid the watch so regularly that raids had a ritual, theatrical quality, everyone playing their part until the coins changed hands. Today she wasn't here for a raid. Today she stood at the edge of the lane with her hunters and felt something she refused to name settle coldly under her ribs.

Because the brokers were mostly children.

Not all—but enough. Small figures wrapped in oversized coats, hawking their own pasts because a childhood memory of summer sold better than any trinket. A boy of perhaps nine stood at the corner of the nearest booth, calling out in a voice cracked with cold: "First snow! First snow, fresh, barely faded! Your name called by your mother, warm bread, the smell of the—"

"Move along," his keeper said, not unkindly, and the boy subsided into silence, rubbing his bare hands together.

Vess watched him a moment too long.

"Captain?" Pell asked.

"The fiddler's memory," she said. "There was a third figure. Behind the stalls. Watching them pass." She replayed it in her head even now: a flicker of movement at the memory's edge, a small shape ducking behind a rain barrel as the two travelers went by. The fiddler hadn't thought anything of it. Children were scenery, to most adults.

But children saw everything. Children remembered everything. And unlike the brokers' wares, a child's memory could not be bought.

It had to be given—or taken.

* * *

They found the witness in the Tanner's Ward, in a tenement that smelled of lye and wet wool, three flights up a staircase that groaned like an old ship. The mother met them at the door, took one look at the grey coats, and stepped aside without a word. Everyone in the ward knew what the grey coats wanted when they came asking after faces.

The boy was small for seven, sitting on a stool by the window with his legs swinging, watching the snow. His name was Tam. His mother said it twice, quickly, the way people offered up small true things hoping to satisfy the larger hunger.

"Tam," Vess said, crouching to bring herself level with him. This was technique—she knew it was technique, she had been taught it in the white corridors—but knowing did not make the boy's wide grey eyes any easier to meet. "I need your help."

He looked at his mother. His mother looked at the floor.

"You saw some people in the market. Eight days ago. Near the shrine row."

"I seen lots of people."

"These two." She drew from her pocket a sketch she'd had made from the fiddler's memory—the girl with the ink-stained fingers, the wounded man leaning on her shoulder. The artist's rendering had softened them, blurred them, but the girl's expression survived translation: wary, watchful, carrying someone else's weight.

Tam studied the sketch. Something flickered across his face—not recognition exactly, or not only recognition. Recognition plus something else. A decision being made, small and serious, behind a seven-year-old's eyes.

"I might of seen them," he said.

"If you have, I can pay you." She set a silver mark on the table between them. Real coin, crown-minted, worth more than his mother made in a month. She watched his eyes go to it and away, deliberately, the way a child looks away from a thing that frightens him.

"My ma says I shouldn't sell my rememberings to brokers," Tam said. "She says it makes holes."

"This isn't selling. This is telling. Telling doesn't make holes."

"It does if they take it out anyway." He said it simply, without accusation, and Vess felt the words land somewhere beneath her armor, in a place she had not armored. Because it was true. Because everyone in this ward knew it was true. Because extraction did not require consent—it required only proximity, and her will, and the prisoner's exhaustion. Telling was just the polite word for the same wound.

"I won't take anything from you," she said. "I give you my word."

"Your word." Tam considered her with the terrible frankness of children. "What's that worth?"

Behind her, Brannoc shifted. Pell made a small warning sound. No one spoke to Hunter-Captain Vess that way. No one had spoken to her that way in eleven years, and certainly no one seven years old with chapped hands and snow melting in his hair.

"More than theirs," she said finally.

Tam looked at her a long moment. Then he shrugged, the universal gesture of a boy deciding the strange adult had passed some test he couldn't name, and began to talk.

"They come past the pie stall. The man was hurt bad—he was leaning on her and she's little but she held him up, like my da used to hold the fence post while it set. They stopped at the shrine row and she touched the stones. All of them. Like she was saying hello." He frowned, remembering. "And the stones lit up. Just a little. Just for her."

"Lit up how?"

"Like when you hold a lamp behind your hand. Red, but soft. And then—" he hesitated, glancing again at his mother, who still hadn't raised her eyes from the floor—"and then the man got scared and pulled her away, quick, and they went down toward the river gate. But before that, the man looked right at me. Right at me, like he knew I was looking. And he said—" Tam's brow furrowed with the effort of exact recall, and Vess realized, with a strange vertigo, that this child had a better memory than half her extracted prisoners. Undamaged. Unharvested. Whole. "He said, *they're already burning what we left behind. Tell no one where we're bound.* And then he gave me this."

He dug into his shirt and produced a small wooden token on a cord: a circle bisected by a line, worn smooth, carved with characters Vess recognized instantly because she had spent the last six months staring at them in confiscated texts.

Covenant-script. Old covenant-script, the genuine article, not the garbled copies the Academies taught their hunters to fear.

"Where did the man learn covenant-script?" she asked, and heard her own voice come out wrong—too sharp, too interested—and watched Tam shrink back a fraction, and hated herself for it.

"I dunno," Tam mumbled. "It's just a pretty rock."

"It's not a rock. It's—" She stopped. Steadied. "Thank you, Tam. You've been very helpful."

She rose. The mother finally looked up, and her eyes met Vess's, and there was nothing in them Vess hadn't seen a thousand times—fear, resignation, the arithmetic of the powerless calculating what obedience would cost. But there was something else too, something that snagged like a burr:

Pity.

The woman pitied *her*. Vess carried it out of the tenement like a stone in her boot.

* * *

Outside, the snow was thickening. Down the street, the river gate stood open, traffic flowing through it in a thin winter trickle. Eight days. The travelers had eight days on her, but the fiddler's memory had shown her which road, and Tam had given her the gate, and tokens carved with covenant-script were not things that could be spent casually—they would be spent at shrines, at waystations, at places where the old network of the Oathkeepers still smoldered like roots under snow.

"We can reach the river gate by nightfall," Brannoc said. "If they sheltered anywhere between here and the eastern passes, the token will draw them like flies."

"Yes," Vess said absently. She turned the token over in her gloved hand. Three names, freely given. She'd read the fragments in the confiscated texts, the ones the Archivist circled obsessively in red ink. Three names, freely given, binding two realms—and the third name forgotten by all—

No. That fragment was damaged. Incomplete. The Academies said the covenant-breaking had been a mercy, a surgical severing that saved the kingdom from the other realm's predations. The textbooks were clear. The textbooks were always clear.

But Tam's voice kept echoing. *Telling doesn't make holes. It does if they take it out anyway.*

And the mother's eyes. Pitying her.

"Captain," Pell said softly. "Your orders?"

Vess looked at the token. At the snow falling on the ward where children sold their summers to buy bread. At the river gate, beyond which the road ran east toward the mountains, toward whatever remained of the Oathkeepers, toward a girl with ink-stained fingers who touched shrine stones and made them glow like lamplight through a hand.

The Archivist wanted the keystone harvested. The word sat in her mind now, unavoidable, ugly, true.

"The girl reaches the shrines first or we do," Vess said at last. "Double the pace. We ride tonight."

She pocketed the token, gave the boy's window one last glance—the small silhouette watching the snow, keeping his pretty rock safe against his heart—and led her hunters east into the darkening storm, carrying with her, unexamined and unwelcome, the first splinter of a doubt that would take root in the frozen ground of her certainty and grow toward the light of everything she was about to destroy.
