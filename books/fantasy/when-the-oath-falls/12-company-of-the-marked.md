## 12. Company of the Marked

Sefa smelled the trouble before she saw it.

That was the first thing the gift had ever given her—not spells, not wonders, just a nose for wrongness, sharp as a hound's and twice as cursed. For thirty years it had served her well in the kitchens of Dunharrow's wealthier houses, where she could taste rot in milk three days before it turned, smell fever on a guest before they'd finished their first course, and know—always, unfailingly—the exact moment her employer began to suspect that his cook was more than a cook.

Tonight the wrongness came in with the cold. It slid under the kitchen door of the Gilded Spoon like smoke, and Sefa set down her ladle, wiped her hands on her apron, and stood very still in the middle of her domain among the copper pots and the hanging bundles of thyme, listening to what her nose told her.

Wet wool. Horse sweat. And underneath it all, faint as a hymn sung from another room: *oath-ink.*

Someone had crossed her threshold carrying covenant-script.

She moved to the door and opened it before the knock could land. Three figures stood in the snow-dusted alley: a tall man swaying on his feet with exhaustion, a young woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes too old for her face, and between them, held upright by sheer stubbornness, a boy of perhaps twelve whose gaze kept drifting upward toward a sky that held nothing Sefa could see.

"We were told," the tall man said, "that a woman named Sefa keeps this door."

"Nobody was told that." She didn't move aside. "People guess. People get guessed wrong." But her nose was already confirming what her heart had begun to dread: the girl carried oath-ink in her blood, the man carried it in his wounds, and the boy—

The boy smelled like nothing. That was the strangest thing. The boy smelled like the space after a bell stops ringing.

"You're the hedge-witch," the girl said. Not a question. "Sefa of the Mill Road. You hid eleven gifted folk through the winter of the Red Census. Old Bram's letters said you'd take us in."

Sefa's chest tightened. "Bram wrote to you?"

"He writes to everyone," the tall man said, and there was something in his voice—a dry, worn tenderness—that made her step back from the door despite every instinct screaming at her to bar it. "He says it's how he prays."

She looked at them a long moment. Snow was settling on the boy's shoulders and he didn't brush it away; he was watching the eaves of the opposite house as though counting something there, or waiting for something to arrive. Thirty years of hiding had taught Sefa every way there was to say no.

She heard herself say yes instead.

"In. Before the street sees you. There's stew if you're not proud, and straw behind the flour sacks if you're not particular." She shut the door behind them and threw the bolt, and only then did she let herself feel the tremor in her hands. "And you'll tell me everything while you eat. I don't harbor strangers. I harbor stories, and the people attached to them."

* * *

Corvin slept like a man falling into deep water.

Lena watched him go under—watched the tension leave his shoulders all at once, as if someone had cut the strings that had held him upright since the pass—and then turned to find their hostess studying her over the rim of a steaming bowl.

"You're the scribe," Sefa said.

"I'm a debtor."

"Mm." The older woman set the bowl in front of her with the authority of a queen bestowing a duchy. "I've known debtors. Debtors count hours and hoard coppers and dream small. You walked out of a burning archive with one book clutched to your chest like an infant, and you haven't let go of it once, even to eat. Debtors don't do that." She nodded at Lena's satchel. "What is it?"

"The temple's oldest ledger." Lena touched the leather cover through the canvas. "Pre-covenant-breaking. Corvin thinks—" She stopped. Saying it aloud still felt like standing on a ledge. "He thinks my family helped write it. That we were Oathkeepers. That the reason magic can't follow anyone into the Hollow is because my ancestors took it out of the valley and locked it somewhere. Or broke it. He's not sure which."

"And you believe him?"

"I've seen it work." Lena flexed her right hand. Beneath the skin, faint as veins of ore, the glow came when she called it now—covenant-script rising along her fingers like light through water. "My blood reads the old writing. His can't. If we're going to reforge whatever was broken before the year turns, it has to be me who touches the shrines. Me who pays whatever price the altar asks." She met Sefa's eyes. "You knew Bram before. Is he coming?"

Something complicated moved across Sefa's face—grief and irritation wrestling for the same ground. "Old Bram comes where he likes. He sent you to me because he wanted to see what you'd do before he showed himself. That's Bram. He remembers everything, child. Everything he's ever seen, heard, smelled, said, or regretted, in perfect detail, forever, whether he wants it or not. Most folk would call that a blessing until they spent one hour inside his skull." She rose and gathered bowls. "He'll come. He always comes. He just needs to arrive dramatically, so he does."

The boy—Iyo—had fallen asleep sitting up, mid-bite, his spoon still in his hand. As Sefa draped a blanket over him he murmured something in his sleep. Not words. A sound like wind through a canyon that had never existed, like vast wings very far away, like a language made of light trying to become speech.

Sefa went still. "What realm does he dream?"

"The other one," Lena said quietly. "He says they're starving there. He says they've been starving since the covenant broke, and that the hunger is starting to leak."

* * *

Old Bram remembered everything, which meant he remembered exactly four hundred and six ways this plan could fail, and had spent the last two days walking to Dunharrow cataloguing each one in order of probability.

He arrived at the Gilded Spoon at dawn, not through the door but through the cellar, because dramatic entrances were wasted on the sleeping and because Sefa's kitchen window faced the watch-house. He came up the stairs into the warm smell of bread and stood in the doorway leaning on his stick, a bent old man wrapped in a shepherd's coat, with eyes that had seen too much to bother blinking anymore.

"Sefa," he said. "Your bread's burning."

"It is not."

"It will be in forty seconds. You always forget the second loaf when you're fretting." He shuffled past her and lowered himself onto the bench beside the sleeping boy with a sigh that seemed to come up from his boots. Then he looked at Lena, and Lena felt the look like a lamp being shone directly into her.

"So," Bram said. "Dravenmoor's granddaughter."

"You know my name."

"I know everything about you I've ever learned, which is currently seven facts, and I expect the number to grow tiresomely." He smiled without warmth. "I also remember your grandmother. Autumn of the Red Census. They dragged her through Dunharrow in a cage wagon, and she never once cried out, and I have been unable to stop seeing it for forty years. Every night, child. Every night, that cage." His voice didn't waver; that was the horror of it. It couldn't. "So understand me when I say I have thought long and hard about whether to help you, and every memory I own tells me helping you will get people killed."

"But you came anyway."

"Bram always comes," Sefa muttered from the hearth.

"He does," Bram agreed grimly. "Because the alternative arithmetic is worse. The veil tears at the turning of the year, and everything on both sides of it starves together. I've done the sums. My gift does sums whether I ask it to or not." He fixed Lena with those terrible, tireless eyes. "Now. Show me your hand."

She held it out. The script rose obediently, glowing along her knuckles, and Bram studied it for a long moment with an expression she couldn't read.

"Huh," he said finally. "It's changed since the letters described it."

"Changed how?"

"It's *answering* you now. In my grandmother's time the mark just sat there, like a scar. Yours listens." He leaned back against the wall. "Which means the covenant isn't just broken. It's *reaching*. Looking for its heir. Which means we have less time than I calculated, and I calculated badly enough." He rapped his stick on the floor twice, sharp as a gavel. "Right. Everyone awake. We have work."

Iyo woke instantly, the way children and soldiers wake—one breath, eyes open—and looked straight at Bram with an expression of such naked relief that the old man's sternness cracked visibly down the middle.

"You're real," the boy said. "In the dreams, there's an old man who remembers. I thought I made you up."

Bram went very quiet. When he spoke again his voice was rougher. "What else is in the dreams?"

"A bridge. A mountain stair. A door that opens both ways." Iyo hugged his knees. "And a name. Someone's name, being given away. I can't hold onto it. Every time I almost catch it, it slips." He frowned, puzzled by his own words. "It's like the dream doesn't want me to know it yet."

Lena felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs and stay there, cold and bright, like a swallowed coin.

* * *

They worked through the morning while Sefa baked, because Sefa coped with the end of the world the way she coped with everything: by feeding it.

Corvin unrolled his blood-stained map across the scrubbed table and weighted the corners with salt cellars. Three shrine-marks remained—two defiled, per his order's last reports, and one still burning, far north in the mountains: the First Altar, where the covenant had been forged and where, if any place in either realm still held the shape of the original rite, it must be reforged.

"Three names," Corvin said. "Freely given. That's the spine of the whole working. We know that much from the shrine echo. Two realms, bound by three willing voices—one from each side of the veil, and one..." He hesitated.

"One who stands between," Lena finished. The knowledge surfaced in her like something written in water, appearing as she watched. "That's what the echo showed. The third voice doesn't belong to either realm. They stand at the threshold and give their name to *both*—and the giving costs them everything anyone remembers of them."

Silence held the kitchen. Even the fire seemed to burn more quietly.

"That's why your valley forgets," Bram said at last, gently. "That's why the Hollow has no magic and no memory of having had any. Your ancestors weren't cowards, girl. They were the third voice, once. All of them, generation after generation, standing in the gap. And each time the covenant needed renewing, someone gave, and everyone forgot, and the family stayed anyway, tending a debt no one could name." He shook his head slowly. "Your mother's ledger-debt. The temple. It was never about money, was it? It was a keeping. A way for the forgotten to leave records for themselves."

Lena looked down at her hands, at the ink stains that had defined her entire life, and understood for the first time that she had been apprenticed to her own erasure since childhood—and that some part of her had always, always known.

"Well," said Sefa briskly, setting a fresh loaf on the table with a thump, "then we'd best make sure you're fed properly before you go giving your name to mountains. Sit. All of you. Nobody reforges anything hungry."

And it happened then, in that steam-warmed kitchen, in the space of one ordinary meal: the thing none of them planned and all of them needed. Corvin argued map-routes with Bram while Sefa corrected both their estimates using roads only cooks know. Iyo laughed—actually laughed, high and startled, at Bram's deadpan account of remembering his own christening from four angles—when he hadn't laughed once in all the miles since the ford. And Lena sat among them, eating bread in a stranger's kitchen, and realized with a shock that went through her like sunrise that for the first time since her mother died, she wasn't alone with the knowing.

They were marked, all of them. Hunted, burdened, branded by gifts that had cost each of them something irreplaceable. And here they sat passing butter.

"Company of the Marked," Iyo said suddenly, looking around the table. "That's what we are, isn't it? Like in the old stories."

"Stories make it sound grander than it is," Bram said. "Mostly it's blisters."

"It's grander than it is," Sefa agreed, "and less. Both at once. Eat your crusts."

* * *

The knock came at dusk.

Three knocks, spaced wrong—a hunter's rhythm, crisp and official, the kind of knock that expected doors to open rather than asked. Sefa was moving before the sound finished, snuffing candles with her fingers, herding Iyo toward the cellar hatch with one arm while she pulled open the shutter of the coal chute with the other.

Through the gap, Lena saw the street below fill with lantern-light. Six hunters. At their head stood a woman in the grey-and-silver of the crown's service, tall and composed, her breath pluming in the cold, her face lit from below in a way that made her beautiful and terrible at once.

Vess. Captain Vess, whom Corvin had spoken of the way sailors speak of storms: with respect, and the understanding that respect changes nothing.

"She tracked us from the ford," Corvin breathed. "How—"

"The child witness at the last village," Lena whispered back. "She read someone's memories. She's been one step behind us since Dunharrow's market." Her hand found Iyo's shoulder. The boy was trembling, but his eyes were fixed on the coal chute with a strange intensity, and his lips were moving silently, the way he moved them in sleep.

Down in the street, Vess raised one gloved hand, and the knocking resumed—harder now, rattling the door in its frame.

"Sefa of the Mill Road," Vess's voice carried through the wood, clear and unhurried. "By order of the crown, open your kitchen. We know you keep the marked. We know the null-born scribe is under your roof. Open, and the crown will be merciful to the cook."

Sefa looked back at them across the dark kitchen—this woman who had hidden eleven souls through the Red Census, whose nose for trouble had finally led trouble home—and Lena saw her mouth shape the word *cellar*, saw her turn back toward the door with her chin lifted and her hands, absurdly, wonderfully, reaching for her rolling pin—

Then Iyo gasped.

Every candle flame in the kitchen stood up straight, blue-white, silent. Through the coal chute, the lantern-light in the street flickered—stuttered—*hesitated*, and for one impossible moment Lena saw the alley outside not as it was but as Iyo dreamed it: washed in a light like the inside of a pearl, the hunters' shadows cast long and wavering across snow that glittered with something other than frost.

"I can hold it," the boy whispered, shaking, sweat beading on his forehead, his eyes wide and fixed on some middle distance where a bridge might be. "I can't hold it long. The door in the dream—it opens both ways. *Go.*"

Bram was already hauling open the cellar hatch. "Everyone down. Now. Sefa—"

"I'm right behind you," Sefa said, and Lena believed her, because Sefa of the Mill Road had never once lied about bread or anything else, and because the alternative was unthinkable. "Move, girl. *Move!*"

Lena went down into the dark with the ledger pressed to her chest, Corvin's hand steady at her back, and above her the door began to splinter, and in the pearl-light of a borrowed realm, Iyo stood in a kitchen full of standing flames and held the world's attention by a thread—

—while somewhere behind them, far off through the winter dark, the First Altar waited in the mountains, patient as stone, hungry as the turning year.
