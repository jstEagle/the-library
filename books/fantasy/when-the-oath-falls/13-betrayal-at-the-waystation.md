## 13. Betrayal at the Waystation

The dream came again that night, and this time Iyo did not fight it.

He had learned, over the long weeks of walking, that fighting the dreams only made them worse. When he struggled against the other realm, it pressed closer, like a hand at the back of his neck, patient and immense. So he let it in the way Sefa had taught him to let in cold water—slowly, breathing all the while—and stood once more on the shore of glass beneath a sky like spilled ink.

But tonight something was different. Tonight there was a door in the shore.

It stood alone on the glass, plain and wooden, the kind of door you'd find on a fisherman's cottage or a village well-house. It had no walls around it, no roof above it, and yet it cast a shadow that fell toward Iyo no matter where he moved. And carved into its grain, in letters that hurt to read because they kept rearranging themselves, was a single word:

*Soon.*

"Iyo." His mother's voice, from behind him—but his mother was three realms away and two years dead, and he knew the voice for what it was. The other realm wore the voices of the loved dead the way a crow wore stolen buttons: brightly, and without permission. "Iyo, come see."

He woke with salt on his lips—he wasn't crying, he'd checked, he always checked—and found himself gripping the strap of his satchel so hard his fingers had gone white. The waystation loft creaked above him. Somewhere below, Old Bram's breathing rattled like wind through a broken shutter, the sound it made every night since the shrine, since the defiled stone, since they had buried the keeper whose name even Bram couldn't hold onto. Lena slept in the corner by the window with her ledger open on her chest, one hand still curled around her stylus, as if she might take notes on her own dreams. Corvin was gone—on watch, or walking, or simply being the kind of man who could not bear to be still.

Iyo lay in the dark of the loft and thought about doors.

* * *

He told himself, in the morning, that the man at the crossroads was nobody.

They had stopped at the waystation because it sat where three roads met—the Salt Road running east toward the mountains, a drover's track going south, and the old pilgrim path north through the fens. It was a low stone building with a stable attached and a keeper named Marta who asked no questions, which was why Corvin had chosen it, and why Iyo should have known better than to trust it. Places where questions weren't asked were places where answers cost too much.

The man arrived while the others were loading the cart. He was ordinary in the way hunters' informants were always ordinary—muddy boots, a peddler's pack, a face like a closed book. He caught Iyo's eye across the yard and smiled, and there was nothing wrong with the smile except that it stayed a half-second too long, the way the other realm's borrowed voices lingered after they spoke.

"Boy," he said, ambling closer under the pretense of examining Marta's hens. "You're the dreamer, aren't you. The one who sees through."

Iyo's whole body went cold. "I don't know what you mean."

"No? Funny thing." The man picked up an egg from a nest, turned it over, set it down again. "There's a woman I work for. Captain Vess. She pays very well for information about gifted folk, but here's the thing—" he leaned in, conspiratorial, almost kind— "she doesn't want to hurt you. She wants to help you. There are places now, safe places, where folk like you are taken somewhere the dreams can't reach. Somewhere quiet. My sister went to one. Writes to me every month. Happiest she's ever been."

Every word was a hook dressed as bread. Iyo knew this. Some tired, stubborn part of him, the part that had watched Bram remember things no man should carry, the part that had seen what the hunters did at the shrine—that part screamed at him to walk away.

But another part, smaller and colder and fed by two years of nights spent on a shore of glass, whispered: *what if.*

"What kind of place," he heard himself say.

The man's smile widened by exactly one tooth. "That depends on what you can tell me. Where's the company headed? The mountain road? The fen crossing?" He named the routes lightly, idly, a man discussing weather. "Your friends—they're good people, I'm sure. But people like us, we have to look out for each other first, don't we? They'd understand. They'd *want* you to be safe."

"They're not my—" Iyo started, and stopped, because it wasn't true, and the falseness of it lodged in his throat like a fishbone. Sefa had taught him to cook. Bram had held his hand through the nightmares, murmuring old words until the shore receded. Lena—

Lena had written his name in her ledger. *Iyo, who dreams true.* Underlined twice. He had never been written down before. Not in anything that mattered.

"The waystation," he said, and hated himself with every syllable. "We leave tomorrow at dawn. Through the fens. To the mill shrine, then up the Stair."

The informant nodded, unhurried, writing nothing down because he didn't need to. "Good lad. You won't regret this. Come to the crossroads shrine at moonrise and I'll have word for you about the safe house."

Iyo walked away with his stomach full of stones, telling himself the lie he would keep telling himself until it killed someone: *it's for the best. They'll be safer without me drawing the hunt. It's for the best.*

At moonrise, the crossroads shrine was empty. At moonset, it still was.

By then, the riders were already in the fens.

* * *

Dawn came gray and slow, and with it the smell of smoke—not woodsmoke, not cooking-smoke, but the sharp chemical reek of hunter's tar, the stuff they used to burn wards out of stone.

Iyo was in the yard when the first rider crested the eastern rise, and he understood everything at once, the way you understand a fall only after you've hit the ground. Not one rider. Six. Eight. A dozen, spreading across the fen road in a line as deliberate as a closing hand.

"Marta," he said stupidly, to the keeper's door, and then: "*Corvin!*"

Corvin came out of the stable already moving, taking in the riders, the smoke, the boy's face—and Iyo watched him understand it too, watched it land on him like a blow. But Corvin did not waste time on blame. That came later; that would come for years.

"Back door. Get them moving. *Now.*"

Inside, chaos braided itself into purpose. Sefa threw her pack together with hands that didn't shake—she had hidden from hunters for eleven years, and hiding had made her quick if it hadn't made her safe. Lena snatched up the ledger and the loose pages and the little tin of styluses, and her eyes found Iyo's across the room, and for one terrible moment he thought she saw it, saw all of it, saw the betrayal sitting in him like a swallowed coal.

She only said, "Take my hand. Don't let go."

Then the front door exploded inward.

They came through in armor the color of wet slate, and behind them, filling the doorway, came the thing Iyo would see in his dreams for the rest of his life: a memory-wagon, its cage glowing faintly with the light of extracted recollections, and standing beside it a hunter in a captain's coat—Vess herself, pale-eyed, watching the room with the expression of a woman reading a page she'd read a thousand times before.

Except that today, Iyo would think afterward, something in her face was different. Today she looked less like a reader and more like someone who had just glimpsed what was written between the lines.

Bram was already at the inner door.

He had not hurried. He had put down his cup, folded his napkin—it was such a small, mad detail, folding the napkin—and planted himself in the narrow passage between the kitchen and the back door like a stone planted in a riverbed. In one hand he held the iron fire-poker. In the other hand he held nothing at all, because his gift had never needed a weapon.

"Go," Bram said. Not loudly. Old men who remembered everything had learned to speak quietly; shouting was for people who could afford to forget.

"Bram—" Lena began.

"Eleven hundred years," Bram said, and his voice was steady, and his eyes were already far away, already turning backward through the centuries like a man paging through the world's largest and most terrible book. "I've carried eleven hundred years, girl, and every day of them has been a debt. Let me pay some of it now. Go *through*."

The first hunter reached him and learned what a memory could do when it was given instead of taken.

Bram did not strike him. Bram *showed* him—one instant, compressed, total: the forging of the covenant, the light of two realms mingling, the sound the world made when it was whole. It poured out of the old man like a burst dam, and the hunter dropped his sword and sat down in the doorway of a burning building and wept like a child, because no mind was built to hold that much wholeness all at once.

The second hunter he showed the breaking. The third, the long decay—the slow gray rot that had crept into both realms for six generations, the failing crops in the other world, the hollowed-out years in this one. Each of them received a truth sized precisely to unmake them, and each of them fell or fled or stood frozen, weeping, transformed.

And Iyo, dragged backward through the kitchen with Lena's hand crushing his, felt the overflow wash past him—fragments of Bram's torrent, splashes of eleven hundred years—and for one heartbeat he stood not on the shore of glass but *inside* the other realm, whole and warm and singing, and understood for the first time that the dreams had never been hauntings.

They had been homesickness.

"HOLD THE DOOR, OLD MAN!" a hunter roared, and others surged forward, and Bram laughed—a real laugh, cracked and joyful—and braced himself in the doorway like a keystone deciding to remain an arch.

"I have held worse doors," he said. "I have held the door against forgetting itself."

What happened next, Iyo saw only in pieces, through the back window as they ran for the treeline. He saw the hunters finally overwhelm the old man—not with swords but with numbers, pinning him, dragging him toward the memory-wagon, toward the extraction rig that would crack his skull open like a geode and pour out everything inside.

And he saw Bram choose.

Later, Lena would try to describe it and fail. Later, Corvin would go silent whenever it was mentioned, and drink, and go silent again. But Iyo, who dreamed true, saw it most clearly of any of them, because Iyo alone could see both sides of the veil at the moment it happened.

Bram opened himself. All of it. Every year, every grief, every name, every buried shame and unbearable beauty, eleven hundred years given freely in a single direction—not to the hunters, not into their hungry machine, but sideways, into the one person in the world built to receive it. Into the null-born scribe. Into the girl whose valley forgot, whose blood held covenant-script, whose mind was the only vessel in the kingdom empty enough and holy enough to hold what he carried.

The light went through the wall like water through cloth.

Lena staggered in the treeline and did not fall, because Iyo and Corvin caught her, and then the three of them were kneeling in the frozen ferns holding a girl who was screaming without sound as centuries poured into her—Bram as a boy watching the last free shrine consecrated; Bram as a young man swearing an oath he didn't understand; Bram through plague and purge and the long lonely centuries of perfect recall; Bram seeing Lena for the first time in a temple archive and thinking, *ah, there she is, the one I've been remembering toward*—

And through it all, threaded like gold wire, the covenant itself: the three names, the two realms, the shape of the working, the exact and terrible architecture of what must be done.

Behind them, the waystation burned. In the doorway of it, small with distance, the hunters stood around an old man who was already gone—not dead, worse than dead, emptied, a shell with folded hands—and Vess stood apart from them, looking at the treeline, looking at nothing, her hand pressed flat against her mouth.

Iyo watched her lower that hand. Watched her look at the memory-wagon. Watched something in her face close, like a door.

*Soon,* said the carved word on a door in another realm, and Iyo finally understood that the door had never been a threat.

It had been an invitation. And it had meant this: the turning year was almost upon them, and the veil was thinning whether anyone willed it or not.

"We have to move," Corvin said hoarsely. His arm was around Lena's shoulders; her eyes were open but seeing someplace no one else could follow. "The Stair. Before they regroup. We have to—Sefa, help me—we have to *move*."

Sefa was not moving. Sefa was standing in the ferns with ash on her cheeks from the burning waystation, looking back at the doorway where Old Bram had stood, and Iyo watched her mouth form one word, over and over, like a prayer or a count or a name she refused to lose:

"Remember. Remember. Remember."

Lena stirred. Her voice, when it came, was layered—her own voice with centuries underneath it, an aquifer speaking through a well.

"He wanted us to know," she said. "The third name. He wanted us to know what it costs." She looked at Iyo then, and her eyes held eleven hundred years, and somehow, impossibly, they held no blame at all—which was the cruelest thing of all, the thing Iyo knew would break him slowly for the rest of his life. "Come. Both of you. We have work to finish, and I—" she pressed a hand to her temple, steadying herself against the flood— "I have so much to write down."

She rose. She took up her ledger. Behind them the waystation burned against the gray dawn, and ahead the mountains waited with their long white stair.

And Iyo, walking between them, carrying his betrayal like a stone he would never put down, heard—for the first time not in dream but in waking—the far-off, patient sound of the other realm singing.
