## 14. The Weight of Remembering

Lena did not sleep so much as fall through the floor of herself.

Bram's gift had poured into her in the waystation's burning doorway—not gently, not like water filling a cup, but like a river seizing a leaf. Centuries of it. She had thought a memory was a single thing: one moment, one image, held and released. She had been wrong. What Bram carried was an architecture, whole and humming, built by generations of Oathkeepers who had chosen him as their vessel because he was the strongest, and because—she understood now with a grief that sat in her chest like a stone—he had never once been asked if he wanted it.

She woke on a bedroll in the ruins of a shepherd's cot high above the tree line, her mouth dry, her hands aching as though she had been writing for a hundred years. Snow ticked against the broken roof. Somewhere near the dead hearth, Sefa murmured over Iyo, who slept at last, his face slack and tear-streaked. Corvin sat awake by the door, watching the dark, and when he saw her sit up he came at once, wordless, and pressed a warmed stone into her hands.

"You were calling out," he said. "Names. Dozens of names."

"I don't know whose they are." Her voice came out cracked. "That's the worst part. I know them now, and I don't *know* them."

"Tell me anyway."

So she did. She told him about Maren of the Low Fens, who could coax eels into nets with a hummed note and had died at ninety-one remembering every eel she'd ever caught. About Aldric Vael, who had signed the covenant's first witness-line and whose signature, Lena now realized, she had copied out a hundred times in the temple ledgers without ever knowing what she traced. About a girl called Wren who had dreamed the other realm the way Iyo did, and who had walked into the sea at seventeen rather than carry it anymore.

Corvin listened without moving. When she finished, he said quietly, "That's what Bram held. All of it. Every keeper, back to the beginning."

"He didn't just hold it." Lena flexed her fingers around the warm stone. "He *kept* it. There's a difference. He tended it like—" she searched for the word and found it in Bram's own voice, in Bram's own stubborn heart— "like a garden no one else would water."

* * *

The memories did not arrive in order. They came like weather.

That first day after the waystation, she learned to let them pass through her without gripping—but some of them gripped her instead. Walking the shepherd's path between the ruins, she would stop mid-stride and be elsewhere entirely: standing in a grove of silver-barked trees that did not grow anywhere in this kingdom; feeling snow that fell upward; hearing a language made of light that her mouth suddenly knew how to shape, syllables like struck glass. Each time she surfaced, Sefa or Corvin was there, hands on her shoulders, saying her name until it found her again.

*Lena. Lena. Lena.*

It occurred to her, on the second night, how much depended on that name being said.

But the deepest memory—the one that kept pulling her under like a current—was the forging itself. She had seen its echo at the shrine beneath the mill, a residue, a shadow of shadows cast on defiled stone. This was different. This was standing inside it. Three times now she had fallen into it, and each time she managed to stay a little longer, to turn her head, to look at faces.

The First Altar stood open to a sky with two moons in it—or one moon and something else, a slow pale vastness that might have been another sky looking back. The air had been different then. Thicker. The veil between realms not a wall but a membrane, and both realms breathing through it, and both realms starving for what the other had: the far realm for warmth, for earth, for the short fierce sweetness of mortal lives; this realm for wonder, for the deep green fire that made crops fail and children born strange and gifted and hunted before there was any law to hunt them.

Three people had come to the altar to make the pact.

She knew two of them now the way you know a story you've read a dozen times. Aldric Vael, young then, his hands still ink-stained from drafting the terms—a scribe, she thought, and something in her chest turned over at that, a recognition across four centuries. And beside him a woman of the far realm, tall and wrong and beautiful, her skin shifting like oil on water, her eyes full of patient hunger: Ysara, who had crossed the thin places first and loved this world's stubbornness enough to try to save it.

The third figure stood slightly apart, hooded, and every time Lena reached for that face, the memory closed over it like water.

She tried again on the third night, sitting up alone while the others slept, deliberately sinking into the oldest layer of what Bram had given her. *Go back. Go to the altar. Look at the third.*

And this time—perhaps because she was not fighting, perhaps because Bram's memories had begun to settle into her as though recognizing a new keeper—she stayed.

The third figure stepped forward. A mortal, like Aldric. Younger than she expected. Hands scarred from work, not ink. It was the hands she recognized first, and then the set of the shoulders, the habit of standing slightly outside a circle of light, watching, keeping accounts.

"No," Lena whispered, alone in the dark cot, snow ticking the roof.

It was a woman. It was a scribe. It was someone who wrote things down because the valley forgot, because the world forgot, because someone had to remember—and when Aldric spoke the covenant's terms aloud, Lena heard them in the old formal cadence she had copied ten thousand times in the temple ledgers, and she understood for the first time what the words had always meant:

*Three names, freely given, bind the pact. Two shall stand witness in memory everlasting. The third name is the hinge, and the hinge turns unseen: it shall pass from all recollection, even the bearer's own, even those who love her most. What is forgotten cannot be unmade. What cannot be unmade holds fast.*

The woman at the altar had known. Lena watched her knowledge in the memory—watched her weigh it, watch her look at Aldric and at Ysara with something that was not fear but a terrible practical tenderness, the expression of someone doing sums in which she herself was the coin. She had spoken her name then. Lena heard it—leaned toward it, strained after it the way you strain after a word on the tip of your tongue—

And the name slid away. Even in the memory. Even inside the memory of the moment it was spoken, it was gone, a hole in the record shaped exactly like a person.

Lena came back to herself gasping, hands pressed flat against the frozen ground, snow in her hair, and Sefa was crouched over her with a flame trembling in her palm and terror on her face.

"You went somewhere deep," Sefa said. "You were gone near an hour. You kept saying *I'll write it down, I'll write it down,* and then you'd say, *written where?*"

Lena couldn't answer. Her mind was circling the hole the way a tongue circles a missing tooth.

Because here was the thing she had not yet let herself think, the thing that sat at the edge of her vision like a figure in a doorway: the covenant was broken. The keepers were scattered, the shrines defiled, the veil failing at the turning of the year. To reforge it, three names must again be freely given. Corvin's order would provide one—had already, in a sense, doomed itself to provide one. Ysara's realm, or whatever remained of Ysara's people bleeding through the thinning veil, would provide the second.

And the third name, the hinge, the name that passes from all recollection, even the bearer's own, even those who love her most—

"I have to talk to Corvin," she heard herself say.

* * *

She found him at the eastern wall of the ruin, where the stones gave a view down the mountain's shoulder to the valley lands below, hazed blue with distance and woodsmoke. Hunters' fires, maybe. Or ordinary fires, ordinary lives, the kind she was supposedly trying to save. He had his map across his knees, the blood-stained one that had glowed at the ford a lifetime ago, marking the route to the First Altar. Three days' hard climbing remained.

"Corvin." Her voice sounded strange to her. Flat. Far away. "When your order broke the covenant—how did they do it? Not why. How."

He didn't pretend not to understand. That was one thing she'd learned about him: he never wasted time pretending.

"The same way it was forged," he said. "At the altar, at the turning of the year, with three names. My ancestors gave theirs willingly too. Renouncing, unbinding. But the terms are symmetrical, Lena. Whatever binds also releases. Whatever is given can be taken back."

"And the third name?"

His jaw tightened. "Was already gone. That's what my order discovered, too late, four generations after the forging. They went to unmake the pact and found they could not. The third name wasn't written anywhere. Wasn't remembered anywhere. Not in any ledger, not in any line of descent, not in any grave inscription. The family it belonged to simply—" he made a small, helpless gesture— "ended. Or seemed to. No heirs claimed the name. No neighbors recalled the people. There was a farmstead in the northern hills that everyone agreed had always been empty, except the agreement felt thin, like cloth worn through." He looked down at the map. "My order spent two hundred years searching the archives for that name. That's what the Oathkeepers became, in the end: archivists. Searchers. Men like me, haunting records, looking for a blank."

"A blank," Lena repeated softly.

"We never found it. We never will. Because it isn't hidden, Lena—it's *forgotten*, perfectly, by design, by the covenant's own terms. The strongest binding in creation, and its keystone is a name no one can hold." He laughed, short and bitter. "We broke what we could break and called ourselves damned for it ever since."

Snow fell between them for a while. Down in the blue distance, a plume of smoke rose that was too straight and too steady to be a hearth fire.

"Corvin," she said. "Read me the terms. All of them. The exact words."

He recited them from memory—of course he knew them by heart; his whole ruined order was built around those words—and Lena listened with Bram's centuries layered beneath her own understanding, hearing the old cadence do what old cadences do: hide the knife in the ceremony.

*The third name is the hinge... it shall pass from all recollection, even the bearer's own, even those who love her most.*

Even the bearer's own.

"That's why it has to be willing," Lena said. "Not as mercy. As mechanism. Whoever gives the third name can't be coerced, because coercion requires the coercer to remember what they're taking. The name has to be *given blind*. You have to hand over the one thing about yourself you'll never get back, and you can't even fully understand that, because the understanding goes with it." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "It's elegant. Whoever drafted it was very proud of themselves."

"Lena." Corvin had gone very still. "Why are you asking me this?"

"Because I've seen the forging now. All the way through. I've stood inside the third keeper's head while she spoke her name." Lena looked at him, and was surprised to find her eyes dry, her hands steady. Perhaps Bram's gift included this too: the long view, the archivist's calm in the face of loss. Things lost every day, and the record kept anyway. "She was a scribe, Corvin. She kept records because the valley forgot things. She stood at that altar and she did the sums, and she put herself on the ledger."

The wind moved through the ruin, lifting fine snow off the broken walls, letting it fall again.

"She's not the only one who's done it," Lena went on, quieter. "Every reforging needs a new hinge. There have been others since—I've got their memories too, fragments, gaps with shapes in them. Keepers who paid. And here's what none of them knew, what I know now that they didn't: the forgetting isn't instant. The name goes, but first—first there's the choosing. First you stand where I'm standing, knowing exactly what it costs, holding every memory of yourself that other people have, understanding that you're counting them down. Last winter in the Hollow. The ledger desk by the north window. Corvin collapsing at the ford. Iyo's dreams. Sefa's cooking, which is genuinely terrible, don't tell her I said so." Her voice caught despite everything. "You spend your remaining days memorizing the people who are going to forget you. That's the price under the price. Nobody writes that part down, because nobody remembers it afterward."

Corvin crossed to her slowly, the way you approach something wild, and knelt in the snow in front of her so their eyes were level.

"It doesn't have to be you," he said.

"Yes, it does." She said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "You know it does. I'm null-born. Magic can't follow me into the Hollow—that's why the keystone survived there, why the covenant left its seed in a valley that forgets. I'm the only one who can carry the third name to the altar without the veil tearing early. And I'm a scribe, Corvin. It's practically a job requirement."

"That's not funny."

"No," she agreed. "It really isn't."

Below them, the too-straight smoke multiplied—three plumes now, five, climbing the mountain's flank like slow dark vines. Hunters. Vess, if she still lived; or whoever the crown sent to replace her. Three days to the altar, and an army at their backs, and a boy asleep inside who dreamed the other realm bleeding through.

Corvin took her hands. His were cold and scarred and shaking slightly, and hers were steady, and she thought: *remember this. Remember his hands. Write it somewhere, even if no one ever reads it.*

"Then we go up the mountain," he said at last, "and we find another way."

"There is no other way. But yes." Lena squeezed his fingers and looked past him, up the white stair of the world toward the peak where the First Altar waited, where two moons hung in a sky that hadn't been hers in weeks and might soon belong to no one at all. "We go up anyway."

Behind them, from inside the ruin, Iyo cried out in his sleep—a sound like struck glass, a word in the language of light—and Sefa's voice rose, soothing, frightened, calling the boy back with his own dear name.

*Iyo. Iyo. Iyo.*

Lena sat in the falling snow and thought: *soon, no one will call mine.*
