## 15. Vess's Choice

The order arrived at dawn, sealed with black wax and the sigil of the Crown's new office—the Veilbreaker's mark, a sun split down its center—and Vess read it three times before the words stopped shifting in her vision.

*By command of His Majesty's Grace and the Office of the Sundered Sun: the gifted quarter of Dunharrow is to be purged by nightfall. All inhabitants bearing the mark or suspected of harboring it are to be gathered in the grain market and given to the flame. Let the example stand through winter.*

Given to the flame. Not tried. Not questioned. Not even catalogued, which had always been the grim courtesy of her profession—names recorded, fates witnessed, however terrible. This order did not ask for her ledgers. It asked only for ash.

Vess stood in the requisitioned house that served as her headquarters on Dunharrow's Salt Road, still dressed from a sleepless night, and listened to the city wake below her window. The gifted quarter began at the tanners' lane and climbed the hill toward the old wall: crooked houses leaning together like conspirators, laundry lines strung with gray cloth, the smell of woodsmoke and fish oil and, beneath it all, something faintly luminous that she had learned over fifteen years to recognize without naming—the scent of small magics, hedge-crafted, homely. A woman down there charmed her sourdough to rise in cold weather. A man mended nets with knots that never slipped. Children born with light under their fingernails.

Forty-one souls, by her own census. She had counted them herself, door to door, in the first weeks of the hunt, because that was what she did: she counted, she read, she *knew*, and knowing was supposed to be the whole of justice.

She read the order a fourth time. The words stayed where they were put.

"Captain?" Sergeant Hale stood in the doorway, helmet under his arm, his face carefully blank in the way of a man who has already heard and wants to be told he didn't. "Is it true? What they're saying about the market square?"

"It's true."

"The children too?"

"There are no exceptions written." She set the order down on the table, and found she could not make her fingers release it immediately, as if it might leap after her. "Hale. How many years have you ridden with me?"

"Eleven, Captain."

"In eleven years, how many pyres have we built?"

He didn't answer. He didn't need to; they both knew the number, though neither of them said it aloud, the way soldiers do not say aloud the things that would break the work in half if spoken. Nine. Nine pyres in eleven years, each one preceded by interrogation, extraction, documentation—each one, she had told herself, justified by what the memories revealed. Curses laid on neighbors. Pacts with things behind the veil. Once, a woman who had drowned her own child to feed a river-spirit, and Vess had walked away from that fire with no doubts at all, only a cold and useful anger.

But nine pyres in eleven years was also a rate. A discipline. A line drawn somewhere short of *all of them*, and she understood now, holding the dawn order in her hands, that the line had never been hers to draw. It had been tolerated while it served. Now the crown had found a better use for fire.

"Must we?" Hale asked quietly. It was insubordination, technically. She let it pass into the room like smoke.

"We must decide," she said, "what we are."

* * *

She spent the morning doing what she had always done: reading memories.

The extraction was not torture, whatever the pamphlets said. It was worse, sometimes, for being gentle. She sat across from the prisoner—a netmender named Ostrey, gray-bearded, marked along the collarbone with the faint silver tracery of the gift—and pressed her palms to his temples, and let the crown's technique open him like a book.

His memories came to her in fragments, bright and ordinary. A wife dead of fever. A daughter in the lowtown who didn't know her father's mark. The knots on the nets, glowing faint gold as his fingers worked them, and the quiet joy of that glow, which he had never once used against another living soul. And then—because she had learned to go deeper, because the hunt demanded depth—she found the thing the crown wanted: the old stories, passed down his family like a cracked heirloom. Stories of the veil. Of the covenant. Of the year the sky over the Hollow had gone still and the magic had begun, slowly, everywhere, to rot.

*We broke our word,* his grandfather's voice said inside the memory, inside the memory, an echo folded in echoes. *And everything since has been the interest coming due.*

She withdrew her hands. Ostrey slumped in the chair, breathing hard, eyes wet but steady. He had been read before. He knew the procedure: she would write her findings, weigh them, perhaps release him with a brand and a fine, perhaps not.

Instead Vess asked him a question that was not on any form. "What does the crown want with the gifted? Truly?"

Ostrey blinked. Then he laughed, a dry, broken sound. "You're asking me? You're the one who reads us, Captain. You tell me."

"I'm telling you what I see." She flexed her fingers. They ached after deep extractions; they had ached for months now, ever since the orders had changed character, ever since her reports had stopped being filed and started disappearing upward into the new office. "For two years I've extracted every gifted soul this side of the mountains, and my findings go nowhere. No trials. No verdicts. The prisoners don't go to the courts—they go to caravans that head north, toward the First Altar road, and the manifests list them as *materials*. I read minds for a living, Ostrey, and lately I feel like the only mind in the kingdom that isn't being read is mine."

The old man studied her. Somewhere outside, the garrison was drilling; the rhythm of boots came up through the floorboards.

"They're not hunting us," he said at last. "You know that already. I can see it in your face. You've known since the caravans." He rubbed his wrists where the manacles had marked them. "My grandmother said it plain, when the hunters first came to the coast. *They don't fear the gift, girl. They fear they can't hold it. And so they'll build a hand big enough.*"

"A hand big enough," Vess repeated.

"To take the veil down. All of it. Every thread." He said it simply, the way one says things that have been true in the family for generations. "The covenant isn't a chain around our necks, Captain. It's a stitch. Pull it out and see what the seam does."

* * *

By noon she had made her decision, and by noon she had also done the thing that made the decision irreversible: she had gone back into the archive caravan, past the guards who saluted her, and read the manifests.

The caravans' cargo lists were coded, but codes were her trade. *Material, class S* — sourced. Gifted prisoners, alive, transported north along the First Altar road to a camp that appeared on no map she'd ever surveyed. Quantities noted in barrels and crates, which meant the clerks were counting them the way one counts timber. And appended to the most recent manifest, in the high hand of the Office of the Sundered Sun itself, a directive she read twice and then could not stop reading:

*Yield projections acceptable. Veil-thinning at the Altar site exceeds expectation. Require increased flow prior to solstice. The null-born scribe remains priority; the keystone must not fall into covenant hands. Burn Dunharrow as scheduled—the resulting terror will drive the remaining gifted toward the collection points of their own accord. Fear is an efficient drover.*

Fear is an efficient drover.

She stood in the swaying dark of the archive wagon among her own stolen reports, and understood, with the same clarity she brought to other people's memories, that she had spent eleven years sharpening a knife on the belief that someone else was deciding where it would cut. That the deciding had never been happening anywhere at all. That there was no ledger in some chancellery balancing her nine pyres against the kingdom's safety. There was only the altar road, and the barrels, and a hunger on the far side of the veil wearing the crown's seal.

The null-born scribe. She had pulled that phrase from a hunter's memory weeks ago in the Hollow—a wounded courier, a glowing map, a scribe whose blood answered covenant-script. She had reported it. She had assumed the report would buy the girl a clean capture, a cell, a procedure. Now she read what the report had purchased instead: *the keystone must not fall into covenant hands.* They were not afraid the girl would break the world. They were afraid she would *fix* it—before the crown finished unmaking it at their leisure.

Vess took the manifest. She took three more, and the directive, and the map of the collection points, and she folded them into the oilcloth lining of her coat, and she walked out of the archive wagon past guards who saluted her, and no one stopped her, because for eleven years she had been the law's own hand and the law had never once imagined the hand might close.

* * *

She did not warn the quarter herself. That was the arithmetic she hated most and performed anyway: a captain seen running through the tanners' lane shouting warnings would be stopped, and then no one would be warned, and the intelligence would die in her coat. Instead she cut loose a prisoner—Ostrey, unbranded, unburned, sent out the gate on her authority and her signature, the last lawful act of Hunter-Captain Vess—and told him to run home by every roofline he knew.

Then she went to the garrison stables, saddled her horse, and rode out the west gate under a sky going the color of old pewter, and it was not until she reached the second milestone and heard, behind her, the first thin column of smoke rising from Dunharrow's hill that she let herself feel anything at all.

The quarter burned. Not all of it—she saw, when she turned in the saddle, that the fires had caught strangely, unevenly, as if half the quarter had emptied before the torches arrived, as if doors had been opened by charms and cellars swallowed families whole. Small magics, working frantically. Some had escaped. Some had not. The smoke stood over the city like a pillar of everything she had chosen not to become soon enough.

She thought of Hale, who had saluted her and stayed, because eleven years of loyalty does not dissolve in a morning and men cannot eat their doubts. She thought of the daughter in lowtown who would learn tonight what her father's mark meant to the crown. She thought: *I sought redemption once, riding out of the Hollow with my certainty intact. I called it duty. There is no version of this where I am forgiven. There is only the next right thing, and the next, done by a traitor, because the loyal are busy lighting fires.*

Redemption sought, not granted. Fine. She had never needed a verdict to keep moving; she had only ever needed a record.

* * *

She found their trail forty miles north, and followed it badly on purpose, letting herself be seen at the crossroads shrine near the salt cairns—hood back, sword hilt reversed, hands empty. If they had watchers, and Oathkeepers would have watchers, let them see a hunter coming openly rather than ambush a shadow.

They took her at dusk in a birch stand off the old drove road, and they took her competently, which told her much: Corvin's voice in the dark giving positions, a boy's dream-gift rippling the shadows like heat-haze, a hedge-witch's binding settling over her sword arm like cool water. And then a young woman stepping into the last light, dark-haired, travel-worn, with ink stains on her fingers and a weight behind her eyes that Vess recognized because she had seen it in every memory she had ever extracted from someone carrying more than one life's worth of grief.

Lena Dravenmoor. The null-born scribe. The keystone.

"I know what you are," the girl said. Her voice did not shake. "Captain Vess. Your name is in the memories I carry. I know what you did in the Hollow. I know what your kind has done for eleven years." Behind her, the old man Corvin's hand drifted toward a knife, and the boy watched her with eyes that saw, she suspected, more than any extraction ever had.

"You're right to bind me," Vess said. "I won't ask for trust. Trust has to be earned and I'm the one who built the thing you're running from." She opened her coat slowly, two fingers, and drew out the oilcloth packet. "So don't trust me. Read me. I know one of you can—I know what the dead keeper left you." She held the manifests out flat on her palm, the way one offers grain to a horse that has every reason to bite. "This is why Dunharrow burned. This is where the caravans go. This is what the crown is building at the First Altar, and how many days remain until the solstice. Take it. Take everything. And if afterward you still want to put me off this road, I'll walk it alone, but you'll walk it knowing what's ahead."

The birch leaves ticked in the wind. The boy's shadows breathed. Lena Dravenmoor looked at the packet for a long moment, and then—not at the papers, but at Vess herself, reading not ink but intent, the way a scribe reads a hand.

"You could have stayed," the girl said. "You could have burned us all and retired with honors. Why defect *now*?"

Because fear is an efficient drover, and I refuse to drive. Because I built the hand, and I saw what it was reaching for. Because nine pyres is either a discipline or a debt, and I have decided it is a debt. She said none of it. She said only:

"Because someone has to witness what the crown does, and I am very, very good at witnessing."

Lena took the packet. Her fingers brushed the manifests, and the covenant-script sleeping under Vess's awareness stirred—the faintest glow along the girl's knuckles, the light of old words recognizing new purpose—and Vess felt, absurdly, lighter than she had since the Hollow, as though a page long overwritten had finally been allowed to show its original line.

Behind Lena, Corvin spoke quietly to the hedge-witch: "Keep the binding on her. Kindness and caution both." But he was already unfolding the topmost manifest, and Vess watched his face as he read the yield projections, and watched the color leave it, and knew that whatever argument her presence faced in the hours ahead, the numbers had just argued her case for her.

"How long," Lena asked, "until the solstice?"

"Six days," Vess said. "And the crown's vanguard reaches the First Altar road tomorrow."
