## 16. The Turning Year

The mountain had been visible for three days before Corvin allowed himself to look at it directly.

He told himself it was caution—that a man who spent his life reading the sky learned not to stare at the thing he feared, the way a sailor does not watch the wave that will break over him. But it was a lie, and he was tired of lying, and so on the morning of the fourth day, when the company paused to water the mule at a stream the color of hammered tin, he stood at the trail's edge and let himself see it whole.

The First Altar sat in a saddle of stone between two peaks, too far to make out any detail, close enough now that its presence had weight. Snow smoked off the ridgelines in long white banners, torn away by winds that never stopped. Above the saddle itself hung a light that was not quite light—a pale shimmer, like heat-haze in winter, like the air above a candle seen through old glass.

The veil. Thinning. He could see it from here, which meant anyone with eyes could see it, which meant the crown's hunters could see it, which meant they were climbing toward it even now, from some other road, with their memory-irons and their orders signed in a dead man's hand.

"Corvin."

Lena's voice. He turned. She stood a few paces off with her hood back despite the cold, her breath pluming, and behind her the others made their small camp: Sefa stirring something into the pot with the brisk economy of a woman who had fed soldiers and would feed fugitives on the same terms; Iyo sitting cross-legged on a flat rock, eyes half-shut, watching the other realm bleed through the world the way other children watched clouds; and Vess apart from all of them, as she had been apart since Dunharrow, sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen.

"You've been looking at it for four days," Lena said. "You think I haven't noticed."

"I've been looking at the weather."

"You've been looking at the weather *around* the Altar." She came closer, boots crunching on frost-brittle grass. "I'm a scribe, Corvin. Noticing is most of what I do."

He almost smiled. It hurt to almost smile. "Then you'll have noticed we won't reach the saddle by solstice unless we climb through the night tomorrow."

"I noticed that too." She looked up at him, and her eyes in this light were the gray of ledger-ink, of things written down because someone had to write them. "And I noticed you've been carrying something since the shrine under the mill. Since Bram died, if I'm honest. You keep starting sentences you don't finish. You keep looking at me like I'm an account you're afraid to balance."

The wind came down off the ridge and flattened the grass and carried away whatever answer he might have found. She waited it out. She was good at waiting. He had learned that about her in the weeks since the ford—the patience of a woman who had copied ten thousand lines of other people's debts and knew that everything, eventually, came due.

"There's a fire tonight," he said. "Sefa's lighting one anyway, against the cold. When the others sleep—"

"They won't sleep," Lena said. "Not anymore. Not with three days left. Whatever you have to say, say it where they can hear it. They've bled for this. They get to hear it."

* * *

They built the fire small, the way fugitives do—low flames, high stones to throw the heat back down—and Sefa set the pot among them and then did not pretend to fuss over it, but sat with her arms around her knees and gave Corvin the flat, unblinking look of a hedge-witch who has smelled a confession coming and means to be present for all of it.

Iyo curled against Bram's old pack, which they still carried, though no one had said aloud why.

Vess did not sit. She stood at the edge of the firelight with her back to the dark, watching the trail below them, and said, without turning: "Whatever this is, say it fast. The night's short and the crown's longer."

Corvin knelt. It felt right, suddenly, to kneel—not before any of them exactly, but before the fire, before the truth of it, in the posture his order used for unburdening, hands open on his thighs, palms up, empty.

"My order," he began, and his voice cracked like ice underfoot, and he started again. "You know pieces of it. That the Oathkeepers broke the covenant. That they did it to save themselves. What you don't know—all of you, even you, Lena—is how deliberately it was done, and how much of what has rotted since is not accident but design."

He took out the map. The blood-stained one, the first thing she had ever touched of his, the thing that had glowed under her hand at the ford and ruined both their lives in the same motion. He spread it on the stones between them, and in the firelight the covenant-script along its borders moved like fish under river-ice.

"The covenant bound two realms together with three names, freely given. One name from each realm's people, freely offered. And a third—" he glanced at Lena, could not hold the glance, looked back at the fire, "—a third whose giving costs the giver everything. We knew that. Our founders wrote it plainly: *the third name is always forgotten by all who love its bearer.* It wasn't hidden knowledge. It was written on the first page of our first book, in letters a child could read. My order didn't stumble into breaking the covenant. We read the price, weighed it, and decided the price should be paid by no one—which is another way of deciding it would be paid by everyone."

Sefa's spoon had gone still. Iyo had opened his eyes all the way, and there was something in the boy's face that Corvin had to make himself look at: not anger. Recognition. The look of someone who dreams another realm nightly and has finally been told why his dreams are full of hunger.

"The breaking wasn't one act," Corvin went on. "It was a policy. Generations of it. Every keeper who found a fragment of the old script was ordered to destroy it or bury it. Every record of the third name's ritual was burned—except ours, kept locked, because my masters are cowards, and cowards keep receipts. And when the veil began to thin, when the decay we'd purchased started coming due—we didn't try to mend it. We went hunting for heirs. For null-born bloodlines, valley-born, magic-proof, descended from the families who first fled into the Hollow when the covenant was young." His jaw worked. "Hunting for *you*, though we didn't know your face. Only your shape. A scribe. A keeper of records. Someone who might carry the keystone without knowing what it was."

"That's why the hunters' memories led to me," Lena said quietly. "That's why Vess was sent."

"That's why the crown was sent," Corvin corrected, and now he turned to Vess, because this part belonged to her to confirm. "Tell them. You've seen the orders."

Vess did turn, then. Her knife went away. "The harvest writs," she said. "I thought they were storage protocols. They're not. The crown isn't gathering the gifted to cage them. It's gathering them to *drain* them—to pull every scrap of gift out of every captured soul and pour it against the veil at solstice, all at once, a battering ram of stolen magic. They don't want to mend the veil or break it. They want to tear it wide and take the other realm's power for the throne. And the tearing won't stop where they want it to stop. I've read enough extracted memories now to know what's on the other side. It isn't a treasury. It's a tide." She looked at Lena. "Your friend here's order taught the crown everything it knows about where the fragments hide. That part he hasn't said yet. Say it."

Corvin bowed his head. "Yes. The hunters have followed us shrine to shrine because my order sold the maps. Sold them decades ago, when the decay got bad enough that the masters decided a new covenant forged by force would serve as well as the old one forged freely. They couldn't give names themselves—they'd forfeited the right—but they could find those who could, and let the crown do the taking." He laughed once, and it was the worst sound he had ever made. "I was proud of my work, Lena. I was *good* at it. I catalogued shrines. I verified bloodlines. The entry that led the hunters to the mill—I wrote it. Twenty years ago, in a fair hand, by lamplight, and I sealed it with wax and felt the satisfaction of a job well done. The keeper who died there—Alder, his name was Alder—he taught me my first covenant-script. I put the knife in his hand myself, years later, though I never held it."

No one spoke. The pot ticked as it cooled. Somewhere above them, wind worried the rocks, and the pale shimmer over the saddle pulsed very slightly, like something breathing in its sleep.

Iyo's voice, when it came, was small and terrible. "My dreams. The hungry place. Is that because of you?"

"Yes," Corvin said. "Because of us. Because of me."

The boy nodded slowly, absorbing it, and Corvin understood that Iyo would carry this the way Bram had carried everything—as weight, honestly labeled—and somehow that was worse than hatred.

Lena had not moved. She sat with her hands in her lap, and he saw her doing what she always did: writing. He could nearly see the lines forming behind her eyes, the account being balanced, entries made in that flawless temple hand. When she finally spoke, her voice was steady, and that steadiness frightened him more than rage would have.

"You want punishment," she said. "That's what this kneeling is. That's what the confession is. You've carried the guilt across half a kingdom and now, with three days left, you want it settled—paid, entered, closed—so you can climb the rest of the mountain clean."

"Yes," he said. Because there was no dignity left in denying it.

"And I'm the one to settle it. Because I'm the heir. The keystone. The last voice of the covenant that matters." She unfolded her hands. "All right, Corvin. Here is your judgment, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide whether you can live under it."

He braced.

"I don't forgive you." She said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Not because I'm cruel, but because forgiveness isn't mine to give. The ones who could forgive you are dead—Alder at the mill, Bram on the stair, Sefa's sisters in the purges, every soul who starved a little when the veil thinned, in both realms, for six generations. Forgiveness would have to come from all of them, and they can't speak anymore. So I won't counterfeit their voices. I'm a scribe. I don't forge signatures."

Sefa made a sound that might have been approval.

"What I can give you," Lena went on, "is work. You will carry Bram's pack the rest of the way—you'll carry it up the Stair, and you'll set it down on the Altar itself, and you'll tell him you're sorry, out loud, knowing he can't hear you and that this is the point. You will stand in the circle at the turning of the year and hold it, whatever holding costs. And if the covenant reforges—if the realms knit—you will spend the rest of your life rebuilding what your order burned. Shrines. Records. Names. All of it, with your own hands, until your hands wear out. No absolution. No ceremony. Just the debt, entered in your name, paid in labor until the ledger closes."

She leaned forward, and the firelight caught the ink-stain on her fingers that never fully washed away, and her eyes were not hard but they were not soft either; they were true, which is rarer than either.

"That's the only mercy I deal in, Corvin. Not pardon. Purpose. Do you accept it?"

For a long moment he could not speak. He had imagined every verdict except that one—imagined exile, the knife, the curse, even forgiveness, which he had dreaded most of all because it would have let him stop. But this. This was worse than all of them, and better, and it was exactly what he deserved, which is to say it was exactly what a debt is.

"I accept," he said. His voice did not break this time, and he was grateful for that small remaining dignity.

"Then stand up," Lena said. "We've a mountain to climb, and you'll do it carrying his pack."

He rose. He lifted Bram's pack and settled its weight across his shoulders, and the weight was not only straps and buckles, and everyone there knew it, and no one said so, and that too was right.

* * *

They climbed through the last hours of darkness, and near dawn the world began to go strange.

Corvin felt it first in his teeth—a high sweet pressure, like biting foil—and then in the air itself, which thickened until each breath tasted faintly of green things and lightning. The shimmer above the saddle had grown from a haze to a presence. Where the trail crossed bare stone, frost formed in spirals instead of sheets, and once, in a hollow beside the path, Iyo stopped and pointed silently at a patch of snow where tiny lights moved beneath the surface like fish, like the script under the river-ice of the old map, and no one suggested it was nothing.

"It's the solstice pressure," Corvin said, when Sefa looked a question at him. "Two days early, the veil's already this thin. The realms aren't waiting for the appointed hour anymore. They're leaning on the door."

"Then we lean back," Vess said shortly, and took the lead, because she was Vess.

But it was Lena, near midmorning, who stopped the column with a raised hand and a sound none of them had heard her make before—a sharp intake of breath, half wonder, half wound.

She was staring at her own hand. The light was coming through it. Not reflecting—*through*, the veins of her wrist lit from within like pages held against a candle, and beneath her skin, faint and unmistakable, covenant-script was rising, line after line surfacing as though her body were a manuscript the tide was uncovering.

"It knows I'm coming," she whispered. "Corvin—it's *writing itself onto me.*"

He took her hand gently, and the glow passed into his fingertips where they touched hers, and far above them, beyond the smoking ridgeline, the First Altar caught the sunrise and flared—a single pulse of gold along the saddle, brief as a heartbeat, answered a moment later by a second pulse from somewhere deep beneath the earth, from the other side of everything.

Two realms. Two heartbeats. Out of rhythm, and closing.

"Three days," Corvin said, hoarse. "Maybe less."

Above them, the wind changed direction all at once, as if the mountain had inhaled—and down in the valley behind, distant and thin, they all heard it: horns. Many horns. The crown had seen the flare too.

Vess spat, shouldered her blade, and said what they were all already thinking:

"Then we run."
