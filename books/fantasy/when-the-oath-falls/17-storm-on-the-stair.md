## 17. Storm on the Stair

The mountain had been trying to kill them since dawn.

Sefa felt it first in her knees—the old cook's ache that came before weather, before trouble—and by midmorning the clouds had come down off the peaks like a gray tide swallowing the stair. The First Altar was a day's climb above them still, and the stair itself was ancient beyond reckoning: steps of black stone cut into the mountainside by hands that had not feared heights or gods, each one slick now with rime, each one narrowing as they rose until three abreast became two and two became one.

"Wind's turning," Sefa said, and spat to the leeward out of habit, though the wind took it anyway. "Turning wrong."

Lena stood ahead of her on the stair, one hand pressed to the cliff face, the other gripping Corvin's arm. Even through the storm Sefa could see it—the faint luminance under Lena's skin, the covenant-script that woke whenever she drew on Bram's gift too deep. The girl was carrying centuries in her chest now, and it showed at her edges like light leaking from a shut door.

"We can't stop," Iyo said behind them. The boy was white-lipped, his dream-gift restless even awake; his eyes kept sliding sideways toward things that were not there yet. "They're coming up behind us. I dreamed it last night—lanterns on the lower stair, dozens of them, like stars falling *upward*."

"How long?" Vess asked. She had taken point since the waystation, moving with the economy of a woman who knew exactly how hunters climbed mountains in winter, because she had trained half of them herself.

Iyo closed his eyes. His breath shuddered. "Sundown. Maybe sooner. They don't rest anymore. Whatever the crown gave them after Dunharrow—it burns them forward like pitch in a fire."

Vess said nothing, but Sefa saw her jaw tighten. The captain had been quiet since Dunharrow, quiet the way a blade is quiet in its sheath—not harmless, only held.

Corvin turned, snow gathering in the hollows of his face. "Then we choose ground. There was a landing below—a shelf where the stair widens. If we stand anywhere, we stand there."

"No," Sefa said.

Everyone looked at her. Snow caught in her gray hair, and she let it. Sixty years of hiding her gift in other people's kitchens had taught her patience; the last month had taught her something harder—that patience spent is not always patience wasted.

"You four go on," she said. "You have to reach the Altar, and you have to reach it whole. I'll hold the shelf."

"Sefa—" Lena began.

"Hedge-witch, girl." Sefa smiled, and it was the smile she'd worn over stew pots for thirty years, warm and immovable. "You think I learned nothing all those years pretending to be small? A kitchen witch knows fire better than any battle-mage alive. Fire doesn't care about rank or orders. And this mountain"—she laid her palm against the cliff, and beneath the stone something old and molten answered, a pulse like a sleeping heart—"this mountain is one enormous hearth, if you know how to knock."

"The storm will drown your fire," Vess said flatly.

"Aye. For a while." Sefa looked at each of them in turn—at Lena with the covenant burning under her skin, at Corvin carrying his order's guilt like a second spine, at Iyo who dreamed true, at Vess who had burned a life down in a single act of refusal. "Courage isn't measured in years, loves. It's measured in seconds. All I need is enough of them."

* * *

The argument lasted eleven seconds. Sefa counted.

Then Lena embraced her—hard, fierce, smelling of ink and cold and the strange ozone sweetness of waking magic—and whispered into her ear words that were not thanks but something older, a fragment of covenant-script that settled into Sefa's bones like a coal wrapped in wool.

*Hold,* the word meant. In the old tongue it meant more than holding: it meant *to be the thing that does not move while everything else moves.*

"I'll hold," Sefa told her, and meant it in both languages.

They went up. She watched them go—four small figures climbing into the white throat of the storm—and then she turned down the stair alone, and began, methodically, the work of a lifetime compressed into an hour.

She knocked on the mountain.

Not with fists—with names. Every hedge-witch carried a ledger in her head of small domestic devotions: the name fire answers to when it is hungry, the name stone answers to when it is tired, the name wind answers to when it wants to be flattered rather than fought. Sefa had spent decades using these names for bread ovens and bathwater. Now she walked the widening shelf, pressing her palms to the rock, and spoke them aloud into the storm.

Heat bloomed under the black stone. Steam rose where snow touched it. She built warmth the way she'd built stockpots—layered, patient, fed from below—and then, when the shelf glowed dull red through its skin of ice, she sat down in the middle of the stair, folded her hands in her lap like a woman waiting for tea, and waited.

The lanterns came at dusk, exactly as Iyo had dreamed: dozens of them, swaying up through the falling dark, borne by hunters in gray mail who no longer quite moved like men. She could see it from fifty yards—the wrongness, the burnished haste, the crown's new alchemy spending its soldiers like tallow candles. Some of their faces were familiar. She had cooked for a few of them once, in another life, in a garrison near Dunharrow. One of them had always asked for extra gravy.

"Turn back!" she called, and her voice carried strangely clear through the wind, the mountain lending her its throat. "This stair is warded. This stair is mine. Go home to your mothers."

They didn't turn back. Of course they didn't. Whatever the crown poured into them had burned the home out of their marching.

So Sefa did what kitchens do. She fed the fire.

The shelf erupted. Not flame exactly—or flame wearing the mountain's own body, stone gone suddenly molten along the first ten steps so that the lead hunters' boots smoked and their formation broke like a dropped plate. She heard shouting, the crack of ice, the shriek of a man learning that heat rises and he was standing in its throat. She held the names in her mouth like hot stones and did not stop speaking, and the mountain did not stop answering, and for a hundred and forty seconds—she counted, because courage is measured in seconds—nothing in the world existed except an old woman and a very old mountain agreeing on terms.

At a hundred and forty-one, the alchemy in the hunters found its answer. They stopped advancing and started throwing—vials of the crown's gray glass, which burst against the heated stone and drank the fire out of it like thirst given form. Cold spread where they broke. Her hearth died step by step, quenched by chemistry that had never heard of courtesy.

Sefa stood up. Her knees screamed. She ignored them.

"Well," she said conversationally, to the hunters, to the mountain, to whatever gods still bothered listening. "That's the oven done. Time for the stove."

She pulled every thread of heat left in the shelf into herself—all of it, the whole banked heart of the mountain's kindness—and let it go in one breath, outward, downward, a bloom of white-gold that lit the storm like a second dawn and threw the lead rank of hunters bodily off the stair into the dark below.

It also emptied her. She knew it the way you know a pot has boiled dry—by the smell, by the silence, by the sudden lightness of a thing that has given everything away.

The hunters closed over her position like water closing over a stone.

She thought of her kitchen at the inn. Of bread rising under cloth. Of Lena's face the day they'd met, guarded as a locked ledger. She thought: *a good life, actually, if you added it up right.* She thought: *seconds. That's all courage ever needed.*

The last thing she did was laugh—one short, warm bark of it, a cook's laugh, a hostess's laugh, the laugh that says *sit down, there's plenty*—and several of the hunters hesitated at the sound, because it was kinder than anything the crown had put inside them.

Then the storm took everything, and the stair was empty, and the mountain went on holding its heat in its deep bones, waiting for someone else who knew how to ask.

* * *

Higher up, Iyo fell to his knees on the ice and screamed.

He hadn't seen her death. He'd *felt* it—the way he felt dreams, the way he felt the other realm pressing close wherever the veil wore thin. A warmth going out below them, vast and domestic, like a hearth in a house where everyone had been loved.

"Iyo." Lena crouched beside him, snow streaming past them both sideways. Behind and below, faint through the white, came the glow of the hunters' lanterns again—more than before, spreading across the shelf where Sefa had stood, climbing. "Look at me. We have minutes. Maybe less."

"They killed her." His voice cracked apart. "She was laughing, Lena. Why was she *laughing?*"

"Because she chose it." Corvin knelt on her other side, and his voice was rough as bark. "There's no grief like chosen grief, boy, but there's no shame in it either. Get up. Grieve walking."

But the stair ahead was the worst stretch yet—a gap where some ancient quake had shrugged the mountain's shoulder and twenty feet of stair simply wasn't anymore, just air and cloud and a thousand feet of nothing below. Beyond it, the path continued, visible in lightning-flashes, climbing toward a saddle of black rock where the First Altar waited.

"There's no crossing it," Vess said. "Not in this wind. Not in hours."

"There's less than hours," Corvin said grimly. Below, the lantern-light was gaining. He could hear boots on stone now, and worse, the low synchronized chanting of the crown's alchemists working their quenching-cold upward through the storm.

Iyo got up. Wobbling. Something in his face had changed—grief transmuting, in the crucible of a fourteen-year-old's heart, into the thing that comes after grief when there is no time for it.

"I can hold it," he said.

Everyone looked at him.

"The bridge." He pointed at the gap, at the screaming void between the stairs. "In my dreams I walk on light all the time. Over oceans, over cities that aren't built yet. It holds me because I believe it—I'm asleep, I believe *everything*. Awake, I've never been able to..." He swallowed. "But I don't have to hold myself, do I? I just have to hold *you*. Four of you. For maybe ten seconds."

"The wind will tear at it," Vess said. "And when the light fails—"

"When the light fails, we'll be across." Iyo looked at Lena. "Bram showed me things, in the memories he gave you. The old Oathkeepers crossed gaps like this all the time. It's not magic, really. It's just... the other realm leaning close. And the realms are so thin here, Lena. So thin. I can feel it breathing through the crack in the world." His eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "Let me do this. Let it be for something. She bought us seconds. Don't waste them standing here being careful."

Lena took his face in her hands. Under her palms, the covenant-script flared, and she let it flow—not taking, this time, but giving: a thread of Bram's inherited memory, of every bridge ever walked in the old days, pouring into the boy like oil into a lamp.

"Ten seconds," she said. "We count together. And Iyo—we cross *fast*."

He planted himself at the gap's edge, feet wide, arms out, and closed his eyes, and reached.

What happened next, none of them would ever describe the same way. The air over the void *thickened*. Light gathered out of the storm itself—out of the lightning, out of the hunters' far lanterns, out of some sourceless radiance bleeding through the world's thin places—and braided itself into a span, narrow as a plank, trembling like a harp string. Through it they could see the other realm looking back: colors without names, geometry that hurt gently, a vast slow attention turning toward them like a whale turning in deep water.

"GO," Iyo gasped.

Corvin went first, quick and sure. Then Vess, who did not hesitate—she who had spent her whole life on solid ground stepped onto a bridge made of belief and ran it like a soldier crossing a siege-plank. Then Lena, the script blazing up her arms, the storm howling—

—and below, the hunters reached the edge of the gap and stopped, and one of them, the one who'd once asked for extra gravy, raised a gray-glass vial and hurled it at the bridge.

It burst against the light and *drank*.

The span guttered. Sagged. Iyo screamed with the effort of holding, blood threading from his nose, and the bridge thinned to a wire, and Lena was still six feet from the far side with the void opening beneath her like a mouth—

Vess's hand shot back across the gap. Caught her wrist. Held.

For one full second—counted, measured, paid for—the captain of the crown's witch-hunters hung out over a bottomless dark holding the hand of the woman she'd been sent to capture, and did not let go.

Then they were both across, rolling onto black stone, and Iyo released the bridge and collapsed into Corvin's arms, and the light snapped out behind them, and the storm rushed in to fill the empty space where a miracle had briefly stood.

On the far side of the gap, the hunters milled at the broken stair, chanting uselessly at a chasm no vial could quench. Above them all, the clouds tore open for a moment, and the First Altar's silhouette stood against the sky—close now, an hour's climb, crowned with the last pale light of the dying year.

Lena lay on the frozen rock, Vess's handprint bruised white around her wrist, and laughed—a short, broken, grateful sound that was entirely Sefa's laugh, borrowed for one moment and given back to the storm.

"An hour," she breathed. "We have an hour. Hold on, everyone. Hold on."

Behind them, far below, the hunters were already finding another way.
