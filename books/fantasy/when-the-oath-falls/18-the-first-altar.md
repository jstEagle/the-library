## 18. The First Altar

The mountain did not want them there.

Lena felt it in her boots before she understood it with her mind—the way the stone seemed to lean beneath her, the way the wind came down from the summit not as weather but as refusal, a long exhaled *no* that had been building since the world was young. The stair they had climbed these last two days was older than any road she had ever copied into a ledger, its steps worn hollow in the centers by feet that had made this ascent when her ancestors were still learning to fear magic instead of making bargains with it.

She paused on a ledge no wider than a hearth and looked back the way they had come. Far below, threaded through the pass like smoke, she could see the torches of the hunters who had followed them up the Stair. Too far yet to matter. Too close to forget.

"You should breathe," Corvin said behind her. He said it gently, but his hands were shaking, and he made no effort to hide them anymore. "Before we reach it. Whatever you're saving up—spend some of it now."

"I'm not saving anything." She turned back to the climb. "I'm just writing it down. All of it. In case."

*In case* hung between them, unspoken and enormous. In case she didn't come back down. In case whatever remained at the top of this mountain took her name the way the ritual demanded and left nothing behind but a body walking around with someone else's silence in it. In case the ledger pages sewn inside her coat—the last of her handwriting, the record of a valley that forgot and a debt that grew and a stranger who bled light onto a map—were all that would ever prove she had existed at all.

Sefa's death was four days old and still fresh enough to bleed. Iyo hadn't spoken since the bridge of light failed beneath him on the stair, since Sefa turned and walked back into the hunters' torchlight so the rest of them could run. The boy climbed now with his eyes fixed on Corvin's heels, small and silent, flinching whenever the wind carried a sound that might have been a shout.

And Vess climbed like a woman being dragged toward her own execution, which, Lena supposed, was close to the truth of it. The former Hunter-Captain had not slept more than an hour at a stretch since Dunharrow burned. She carried her branded face like a debt of her own now—the crown's mark seared into her cheek, the mark they gave traitors—and every mile north she seemed smaller, as though the mountain were collecting pieces of her as payment for passage.

"Stop," Vess said suddenly.

They stopped. Ahead, where the stair bent around a shoulder of black rock, something was glowing.

Not firelight. Not the cold silver of covenant-script. This was warmer, amber-gold, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart, and it spilled around the bend in the rock and lay across the snow like honey poured from a great height.

"The shrine-ward," Corvin whispered. He crossed himself in the manner of his order, an old habit, then seemed embarrassed by it. "It means we're close. It means it still—" His voice caught. "It means it still remembers us."

They rounded the bend together, and Lena forgot how to breathe.

* * *

The First Altar sat in a bowl of stone at the mountain's crown, a circle of standing monoliths each taller than the temple bell-tower of her childhood, their surfaces crawling with script so dense and ancient that half of it predated the letters Lena had spent her life copying. Snow could not settle here. She noticed that only after several heartbeats of confusion—the flakes simply slid away from the circle as if from heated glass, spiraling off into the dark air, leaving the altar bare and dry in the middle of a white storm-sky.

At the circle's center stood a slab of black stone, smooth as still water, and above it hung the veil itself—or rather, the place where the veil wore thinnest. Lena saw it now for the first time with unshielded eyes, and it was nothing like the stories. It was not a wall. It was a *wound*, a long seam of trembling light stitched across the sky directly over the altar, and through the stitches she could see—

*Them.*

The other realm pressed against the seam like faces against frosted glass. Shapes moved there. Beautiful shapes, wrong-shaped, luminous and hungry, and as the company stumbled into the circle one of them turned—slowly, with the terrible patience of something very old—and looked directly at Lena Dravenmoor.

She felt it like a hook behind her ribs.

"It knows me," she whispered.

"They all do," Corvin said grimly. "You carry the keystone. You've carried it since the Hollow, probably since you were born. That's why your blood woke the shrines. That's why Bram's memories settled into you instead of burning you out." He stepped past her toward the altar, and his voice dropped to something almost reverent. "This is where it happened. Where my order's founders stood with two others—a woman of the other realm, and a null-born scribe, just like you—and gave their names freely. Three names. Two realms. One covenant." He laid his palm flat on the black slab. "Three names, freely given, binding both sides. And when my order broke faith—" he swallowed "—we took ours back. We thought we could take back just ours. But a name given cannot be taken; it can only be *returned*. So we buried our names in vaults and iron, and the other realm's name-bearer faded without her counterpart, and the whole pact has been rotting ever since. Tonight, at the turning year, it fails entirely."

"And the ritual needs three names again," Vess said flatly. She stood at the circle's edge, arms crossed against the cold, and would not look at the seam in the sky. "Freely given. Yours, hers, and one from the other side."

"Yes."

"Then we have two."

The word fell into the circle like a stone into a well. Two names. Corvin had confessed everything on the climb—every betrayal, every generation of hoarded cowardice—and had asked Lena to decide his punishment, and she had told him the only punishment that mattered was the giving. His name, freely given, was the first coin in their purse. Hers would be the second, if she chose—and she had chosen, God help her, she had chosen weeks ago in a burning archive when she grabbed Corvin's arm instead of her ledgers, when she ran from the only home she'd ever known toward a debt larger than ink could hold.

But the third name belonged to the other realm. And the other realm was starving.

* * *

They prepared the altar as the light died.

Corvin taught them the words—he had known them since childhood, drilled into every child of his order alongside the shame they were never allowed to name—and Lena carved the circles while Vess kept watch on the stair with a hunter's crossbow and a hunter's patience. Iyo sat inside the innermost ring because he refused to be anywhere else, hugging his knees, watching the seam in the sky with the particular horror of a boy who dreamed this realm nightly and had never once dreamed anything gentle about it.

"They're not monsters," the boy said abruptly, as Lena finished the last line of script.

She looked up. "What?"

"Iyo." Corvin's voice was careful. "Son—"

"I dream them." The boy's chin came up, stubborn, tear-streaked, fierce. "Every night. They used to be beautiful. Before the covenant broke, they were *beautiful*, and they helped people, and then your order took back your names and the door started closing and they got hungry. That's what hunger does. It doesn't make you a monster. It makes you *hungry*." His voice cracked. "There's one who used to sing to the mill-race by my grandmother's village. Before. She has a name too. She's probably dead of the hunger by now, or worse, or—"

"Iyo." Lena knelt beside him. Behind her, the seam in the sky pulsed, and something on the far side pressed closer, and the light of it gilded the boy's wet cheeks gold. "Listen to me. If a third name comes tonight—if one of them chooses to give theirs—it will be because they choose it. Not because we take it. Do you understand? That's the whole law of the thing. Freely given, or it doesn't bind."

He nodded slowly, unconvinced, and Lena rose and faced the altar and understood that she was lying to herself as much as to him.

Because she had seen the faces at the glass. She had felt the hook behind her ribs. And she knew—with the certainty of every ledger she'd ever balanced, every debt she'd ever watched compound—that things which starve do not give. Things which starve *take*. And if the third name did not come freely before midnight, the veil would split anyway, unbound, unmediated, and what came through would come through as famine comes through a broken granary door: not evil, not malicious, just vast, and endless, and empty.

The sun went down. The turning of the year began.

* * *

Corvin went first.

He stripped off his gloves—the order's gloves, iron-threaded, worn thin at the fingertips from decades of touching nothing—and placed his palms on the black slab, and spoke his name aloud into the circle.

Lena felt it leave him. There was no other way to describe it. A warmth passed out of the man standing before her and sank into the stone, and the covenant-script along the monoliths flared amber, and somewhere far away a bell that did not exist rang once.

When Corvin turned around, he was smiling, and crying, and lighter—visibly, physically lighter, as though something he'd been carrying since birth had finally been set down.

"That's one," he said hoarsely. "One name returned. The account is open."

Vess lowered her crossbow. For a moment—one strange, suspended moment—she looked at Lena across the altar, hunter and hunted, and something passed between them that needed no memory-magic at all. Gratitude, maybe. Or apology. Or the recognition of two women who had both been sold by institutions that called themselves kind.

Then Lena stepped forward, and put her hands on the stone, and prepared to speak her name.

"Lena Dravenmoor."

The words rose in her throat.

And the sky tore.

It was not gradual. One heartbeat the seam was a seam, stitched and straining. The next, a hand of light punched through it—no, not a hand, though it had fingers, long ones, too many joints, trailing radiance like torn silk—and the whole circle screamed with released pressure, every monolith ringing like a struck bell, snow erupting outward from the bare stone in a perfect ring.

Through the wound came the other realm.

Not an army. Worse. An *outpouring*—color that had no names, music that bypassed the ear and played directly on the spine, air that tasted of every fruit Lena had ever imagined and none that existed. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and it was dying as it came, the radiance guttering even as it poured, because beauty needs a vessel and the vessels on this side were breaking.

Iyo was screaming. Not in fear—in *grief*, the sound of a boy watching his dreams bleed out onto real ground. Vess fired her crossbow at the seam and the bolt simply unraveled midair into moths of light. Corvin threw himself over the boy. And Lena stood with her name half-spoken, her hands on the stone, feeling the keystone fragment in her chest turn over like a key in a lock, feeling the covenant reach for her—

—reaching, reaching, three names, three names, *three names*—

—and finding only two.

The ritual stalled. The script on the monoliths flickered. And in the stall, in that horrible gap between two names and three, Lena heard it.

A voice. From the far side of the tearing sky. Thin, and cracked, and ancient with starvation—but underneath the ruin of it, underneath the hunger, unmistakably:

*singing.*

A song about a mill-race. A song about water going over a wheel. A song from a grandmother's village that no longer existed, sung by a voice that remembered being beautiful, sung now as a plea, as a bargain, as—Lena realized with a lurch that nearly brought her to her knees—as a *question*.

*Will you take it? Will you take my name, if I give it?*

The third name, offered. Freely. By a starving thing on the far side of a broken sky, asking permission from a scribe with her hands on the stone.

Lena opened her mouth—to answer yes, to answer anything—and found that the ritual was already moving, that her own name was rising out of her whether she willed it or not, that the covenant did not wait for courage, it waited only for consent, and consent was a door already opening—

*Iyo,* she thought wildly, *the singer, tell her yes, someone tell her—*

The boy was already on his feet. Already facing the torn sky, tears freezing on his cheeks, shouting in a language of light that he'd only ever spoken in dreams:

*"Yes! Yes—we accept! Give it freely and we'll keep it safe, I swear it, I swear it on the new covenant—"*

And across the wound in the world, the starving song changed key—from plea to promise—and something began, at last and terribly and wonderfully, to *give*.
