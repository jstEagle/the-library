## 21. The Unwritten Scribe

The temple had burned for two days before the rain came, and Vess had stood in the orchard through both of them, watching the ledgers curl and blacken like moths thrown to a flame. She had not ordered the burning. That order had come from the crown's steward, delivered by a courier who rode away before the first torch touched the archive doors. But she had not stopped it either. She had stood at the valley's edge with her hunters drawn up behind her and let a thousand years of record-keeping go up in smoke, because the alternative was to be branded a traitor weeks before she was ready to bear the name.

She thought about that often now, in the quiet hours before dawn, when the ash still sifted down from the scorched rafters and settled on her shoulders like gray snow. There were many kinds of cowardice, she had learned. Some of them wore the face of patience.

The war was over. The veil held. The turning of the year had come and gone without the realms tearing open like wet parchment, and the stories told in the taverns of Dunharrow grew stranger with each retelling—stories of light on a mountain, of a bridge held by a dreaming boy, of a woman who gave something no one could name because no one could remember what it was. Vess knew the truth of it better than most. She had stood on the mountain stair with her sword drawn against the very people she'd been sent to hunt, and she had watched the covenant reforge itself out of sacrifice, and she had come down from that mountain with her commission revoked, her company disbanded, and a brand on her forearm where the crown's magistrates had marked her traitor.

*Traitor*, the brand said. *Oathbreaker*. They had meant it as an ending.

Vess had decided to make it a beginning.

* * *

Rebuilding the Hollow was not glamorous work. The valley had been emptied of its gifted folk during the hunt—fled, or taken, or dead—and those who remained were the ones who had survived by being unremarkable: farmers, millers, a cooper with a bad hip, three sisters who kept bees and remembered nothing unusual about any year of their lives. They regarded Vess with the wary courtesy owed to a former witch-hunter, which was to say they spoke to her only when the road required it, and they never met her eyes.

She did not blame them. She had burned houses in Dunharrow on orders she had finally refused; she had extracted memories from frightened children strapped into chairs of iron and glass. The scar tissue of what she had done would never fully close. But there was a difference, she had come to believe, between atonement and penance. Penance was performed for an audience. Atonement was done whether anyone watched or not.

So she rebuilt the temple.

It took the better part of a year. She hired stonemasons out of her own purse—the purse she'd carried out of the crown's service, heavy with pay she no longer wanted—and when the money ran low she hauled stone herself, her traitor's brand bared to the sun because hiding it felt like a lie. The villagers watched her work with expressions she couldn't read. Slowly, one by one, some of them began to help. The cooper mended the great doors. The bee-sisters brought honey for the workers' bread. A widow whose son had been taken during the hunt came every seventh day to sweep the ash from the floor, saying nothing, leaving nothing, until one morning she left a sprig of rowan on the windowsill and Vess understood that she had been forgiven—or if not forgiven, then at least permitted.

The new archive opened at midwinter, one year after the night the world had almost ended. Vess stood at its doors and made a proclamation to the handful of villagers who came: that this place would keep records for everyone, freely, without fee or debt or interest accruing in the dark. That no ledger here would ever again be used as a chain. That anyone who wished to write down their life—their births and debts forgiven, their grievances, their recipes, their griefs—could do so, and the archive would hold it safe for as long as the walls stood.

"Memory belongs to the one who lived it," she told them, and heard how the words caught in her throat, because she had spent ten years prying memories out of people who belonged to them.

An old man in the back—a beekeeper's cousin, half-deaf—asked loudly what the point of writing things down was, when the valley forgot everything anyway. It was true. The Hollow's strange null-stillness persisted; names slipped here, small facts evaporated, the past went soft at the edges like fruit left too long. People had learned to live with it, the way one lives with a limp.

"That is exactly why," Vess said.

* * *

The scribe arrived in early spring, with the thaw.

Vess noticed her the way one notices weather—not all at once, but as a gradual change in the quality of the air. The woman had simply begun appearing among the reading desks, a thin figure in a gray dress with ink stains on her fingers, copying documents in a hand so precise it looked printed. When Vess asked her name, the woman looked up with an expression of mild, patient puzzlement, as though the question were slightly beside the point.

"I'm the scribe," she said.

"Yes, but your name."

A pause. The woman's brow furrowed faintly, the way a person's does when reaching for a word in a language they once knew. "I... don't seem to have one just now," she said at last, and smiled, and returned to her work, and somehow the answer did not feel alarming. It felt like a coat left on a hook—something that could be retrieved later, perhaps, or perhaps not, and either way the day would go on.

Vess should have pressed. Ten years of hunting had taught her to press, to notice the gap where a fact should be, to distrust smooth surfaces over hollow places. And there *was* a hollow where the woman's name should have been. Vess could feel it the way her tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth. But pressing required authority, and she had surrendered hers, and besides—there was something about the scribe's presence that made interrogation feel obscene, the way shouting feels obscene in a sickroom.

So instead she watched, and learned.

The scribe worked from dawn until the light failed, and her knowledge was uncanny. She knew the old cataloging systems of the burned temple—systems that had died with the archive, that existed nowhere in any surviving text. She restored them from memory, or from whatever lived in her in place of memory, murmuring shelf-marks under her breath as she sorted the flood of documents the valley's families began bringing in: deeds and dowry lists, letters from sons who'd gone to the wars, a recipe for plum cordial written on the back of a tax notice, a child's drawing of a horse with too many legs. She handled each one as though it were a relic.

"She's not right, that one," the cooper said, in the tone of men describing weather. "Can't tell you where she sleeps. Can't tell you when she came. Marta swears she saw her at the ford last autumn, but the archive wasn't even built." He shrugged. "Good with the records, though. Best I've seen."

That was the thing about the Hollow, Vess reflected. The valley forgot, and so the valley forgave. A woman with no name and no history fit into it the way water fits into a cup. The villagers simply poured their lives around her shape and stopped noticing the strangeness, because noticing required holding onto the observation overnight, and the Hollow did not permit that.

Only Vess held on. Only Vess lay awake cataloguing the wrongnesses: that the scribe never ate in company, though crumbs vanished from her desk; that she hummed songs no one recognized, in a language that sounded like light given grammar; that sometimes, when she copied certain passages—old covenant-script recovered from a shrine fragment, a line of the new pact transcribed by Corvin's order and donated anonymously—her pen would slow, and her eyes would glisten, and she would press her ink-stained fingers flat against the page as though steadying herself against a wind only she could feel.

Once, Vess found her asleep at her desk, cheek on an open ledger, and standing over her Vess saw that the page beneath her face bore a single word, written and rewritten in the margins dozens of times, in handwriting that trembled and firmed and trembled again:

*Lena. Lena. Lena.*

By morning the word was gone from the page. Vess checked. But it was not gone from Vess—for Vess had spent a year learning the discipline of keeping what the valley released, and some things, once gripped, she did not let go.

* * *

They fell into a rhythm across the seasons, the hunter and the scribe, though neither would have used those words anymore. Vess managed the archive's affairs; the scribe managed its soul. In the evenings they sometimes shared the last of the fire, the scribe copying by lamplight while Vess sharpened a sword she no longer had cause to draw, and they talked of small things—the roof leak over the western stacks, the widow's son whose letters had finally been found, the quality of the new ink. Once, only once, the scribe looked up from her work and said, apropos of nothing:

"You kept me. In your memory. All this time."

It was not a question. Vess set down her whetstone. "Yes."

"Why?"

"Because someone has to," Vess said. "Because forgetting you did to you what the crown did to others, and I won't be party to it. Because—" she paused, choosing honesty over comfort, "—because I owe a debt I can't repay to a person I can't fully mourn. Keeping you is the only payment I have."

The scribe considered this with grave attention, the way she considered everything. Then she reached across the table and briefly covered Vess's scarred wrist with her ink-dark fingers.

"It was worth it," she said softly. "Whatever you're carrying on my behalf—it was worth it. I need you to know that. I've written it down somewhere, I think. So it must be true."

And she smiled, and went back to her copying, and Vess sat in the firelight feeling something crack open in her chest that was not quite grief and not quite peace, but was, perhaps, the thing that comes after both.

* * *

Spring came again. The archive filled. Children learned their letters at the long tables under the repaired windows, and the widow who swept the floor began leaving two sprigs of rowan now—one on the sill, and one, shyly, on the scribe's desk. The valley went on forgetting, gently, the way it always had. But the pages endured. Ink endures. That was the whole theology of the place, scrawled above the door in letters Vess had painted herself: *WHAT IS WRITTEN CANNOT BE WHOLLY LOST.*

On an evening in late spring, with the orchards in blossom and the light going gold through the windows, Vess climbed the stairs to the gallery where the oldest documents were kept. She had a bundle under her arm: papers recovered from a shrine in the mountains, smoke-damaged, bearing fragments of the original covenant in three hands—one bold, one careful, one so faint it seemed afraid of the page.

She found the scribe at the central desk, bent over a fresh sheet, writing. Not copying—writing. Composing something new, in that precise and patient hand. As Vess approached, the woman finished the line, blotted it, and sat looking at what she had written with an expression Vess had never seen on her before: wonder, worn smooth by use, like a stone carried in a pocket for years.

"What is it?" Vess asked.

"A letter," the scribe said. "To someone I haven't met yet. Or perhaps I have. It's difficult to say." She turned the page toward the light, and Vess read the opening line, and felt the floor tilt gently beneath her:

*To the one who remembers me—I hope the valley has been kind. I hope the bees are thriving. I hope you know that I chose this, and that choosing it was the freest thing I ever did.*

Below it, the ink still wet, the scribe had written a single closing line, and as Vess read it, the woman reached out and added one more word in the margin—small, deliberate, permanent:

*Signed, the scribe who paid.*

Outside, the blossoms drifted down like snow, and somewhere beyond the mountains the other realm breathed quietly against the veil, held fast by a name no one could speak and a page no one could lose.
