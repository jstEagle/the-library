## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The trouble with living in a valley where magic cannot follow, Juniper Hartwell had decided by her nineteenth autumn, was that neither could anything else.

Not mail from beyond the ridgeline — the post came in on Thursdays or not at all, depending on the mule's mood. Not new songs; the last one to arrive was eleven years old and everyone still sang it like it was scandalous. Not even weather, really. The Hollow kept its own weather the way a miser keeps coins, doling out the same gray drizzle and the same honeyed October light year after year, as if variety were something that might attract attention.

Magic couldn't follow you into the Hollow. Neither could change.

She knelt in the herb garden behind Mistress Aldery's cottage, working her fingers into soil that smelled of thyme and cold stone, and tried very hard not to think about anywhere else. She failed, as she did most mornings.

"Stop sulking at the comfrey," said a voice above her. "It can feel it."

Juniper looked up. Mistress Aldery stood on the back step with a teacup balanced on a saucer and an expression that suggested she had been standing there long enough to enjoy several sips' worth of Juniper's sulk. She was small and round as a seed pod, with white hair pinned up by whatever pins happened to be nearest — today, one actual hairpin and what appeared to be a bent darning needle — and eyes the color of strong tea left to go bitter.

"I'm not sulking," Juniper said. "I'm weeding."

"You're weeding *sulkily*. There's a difference, and the comfrey knows it." Aldery took another sip. "You've pulled the same leaf nine times. It's beginning to take it personally."

Juniper looked down. Sure enough, there was a comfrey leaf lying beside her knee, intact and unbothered, while her fist was closed around air. She dropped the air, brushed off her hands, and stood.

"The beds need turning before frost," she said. "And the valerian's crowding the feverfew again. And—" she hesitated, then made herself say it, because saying things plainly was a habit she'd been trying to cultivate the way other people cultivated roses, "—and I was thinking I might walk up to the ridge pass tomorrow. Just to look. Everyone says you can see the lowlands from the top. Rivers. Roads."

Aldery's teacup paused halfway through its journey.

"Can you," she said.

"Not *go* anywhere," Juniper said quickly, which was a lie, or half of one, or possibly the honest confession of a lie she'd been carrying around since spring. "Just look. Looking isn't leaving."

"Mm." Aldery set down the cup and saucer on the step rail with a small ceramic click that somehow carried the weight of a gavel. "When I was your age, a girl in this valley told me looking wasn't leaving too. She went up to the ridge every Sunday for a summer. Then one Sunday she didn't come back down until she'd seen the sea."

"What happened to her?" Juniper asked, despite knowing better. Aldery's stories always ended somewhere Juniper didn't want them to end.

"She came back," Aldery said, "with salt in her boots and a great deal to say, none of it useful, and she spent the next forty years being restless in a place built for rest." She smiled then, sudden and crooked, taking the sting out of it the way she always did — too late, but thoroughly. "She also learned forty kinds of tea and how to deliver babies and how to talk bees out of swarming, so it wasn't all waste. But the restlessness stayed. Restlessness does that. It's the only thing that follows you into the Hollow."

Juniper's chest did the thing it did — a slow tightening, like a hand closing gently around something fragile. Because that was the cruelty of it, the quiet, patient cruelty of home: the Hollow didn't keep magic out. It kept everything out, and everything in, and the only thing it couldn't stop was wanting.

"Go turn the beds," Aldery said, gathering her cup. "And if you must sulk at the vegetables, sulk at the cabbages. They have no feelings whatsoever. I've checked."

* * *

The hum started at the tree line.

Everyone in the Hollow knew about the hum, though nobody talked about it much, the way nobody talks about the shape of their own nose. It wasn't a sound exactly. It was more like a pressure, a held breath pressed against the skin from the inside out, strongest at the valley's edges where the pines crowded close and the mist sat thick as wool. Old-timers said it was the valley breathing. Skeptics said it was wind through stone. Juniper privately thought both were wrong, and that the truth was simpler and stranger: the Hollow was holding its breath because it was waiting for something, and had been for a hundred years, and had forgotten what.

She felt it now as she walked the garden rows with the turning fork over her shoulder, that faint press against her sternum, familiar as her own pulse. Inside the hum, nothing strange could happen. Milk soured on schedule. Bread rose on schedule. A cut healed in exactly the time a cut takes. Once — this was the story every child got told, usually at bedtime, usually as a warning — a traveling tinker had come through with a charm that made his mule talk, and the moment he crossed the ridge the charm died mid-sentence and the mule never spoke again, and the tinker cried, and the mule looked embarrassed.

Nothing strange could happen inside the hum. That was the promise. That was the price.

Juniper turned the first bed, and the second, and thought about roads.

She thought about them the way other girls her age thought about suitors — constantly, guiltily, with a secret tenderness she'd have died before admitting. Roads going somewhere. Roads with wheel ruts deep enough to drown a coin in. She'd once found, wedged in the mud of the cart track below the mill, a horseshoe that had traveled farther than anyone in the Hollow, and she'd kept it under her bed for two years before shame made her bury it by the fence, where she could still find it if she needed to.

Because here was the thing she could never say aloud, the thing that sat in her like a swallowed stone: loving the Hollow felt like loving a room. Warm, safe, well-built, and with no door she was allowed to use.

"It's not that I'm ungrateful," she informed the valerian, who was indeed crowding the feverfew and showed no remorse. "I know what's out there. Witches. Wars. Plagues. Men who'll sell you a love potion that's mostly onion." She yanked a weed with unnecessary force. "I know. I'm not stupid."

The valerian said nothing, in the way of all plants everywhere, but Juniper felt understood anyway, which was one advantage of arguing with vegetation.

The trouble was the ledger of it. Every kindness the valley had ever done her was written down somewhere in her heart in careful ink. Mrs. Tallow the midwife catching her when she fell out of the pear tree at six. The whole village turning out with lanterns when she'd gotten lost in the mist at ten, calling her name into the fog until she'd followed the voices home. Aldery taking her in at thirteen, when her parents' fever ran through the east cottages like a lit fuse, and feeding her, and teaching her, and never once — not once in six years — making her feel like charity.

How did you tell people you loved that what you wanted most in the world was to be somewhere they weren't?

You didn't. You turned compost instead.

* * *

Midday brought the market bell, and with it Tam Broadhollow, who had appointed himself Juniper's conscience without asking either of them whether this was wanted.

"You're doing it again," Tam announced, leaning on the garden gate hard enough to test its structural integrity. He was broad the way his name implied, with a jaw like a plow blade and a smile that had gotten him forgiven for things Juniper would still be serving sentences for. "The face. The faraway face. My ma says you wear it like it's fashionable."

"Your ma says that about everyone who thinks."

"My ma says a lot of things." He let himself in the gate, because gates were suggestions to Tam, and flopped onto the chopping block with the grace of a felled oak. "Festival's in three days. Whole village'll be there. Honey cakes, the fiddlers, old Petch attempting the maypole again — it's going to be an event, medically speaking."

"Wonderful."

"You're coming?"

"I'm coming."

"You're coming *sulkily*."

"Does everyone in this valley have a commentary on my facial expressions, or is it just the two of you?"

"Just you," Tam said comfortably. "You're the only one whose face is going somewhere else." He picked up a fallen apple from the grass, polished it on his sleeve, and bit into it with a crack like green wood splitting. Then, quieter, around the apple: "It's the ledger book, isn't it. You've been odd since you started the autumn dusting."

Juniper's hands went still on the fork handle.

Mistress Aldery's shop fronted the lane the way old cats front windowsills: occupying it entirely, daring you to object. Herbs in jars, tinctures in brown bottles, charms that weren't charms (Aldery was firm on this point) but rather "concentrated teas of a nervous disposition." Juniper did the dusting, the labeling, the haggling, and the annual autumn ritual of clearing out the back room, where Aldery kept everything she'd ever owned on the principle that throwing things away was a decision best deferred indefinitely.

Among the back-room shelves stood the ledgers. Dozens of them. Recipe books, client books, garden journals, going back further than Juniper had ever bothered to count. This autumn, alphabetizing — a task she'd invented for herself out of pure avoidance — she'd worked her way back past her own arrival, past the fever year, past decades of neat brown ink, to a ledger bound in green leather gone soft as moss, its spine stamped with no title at all.

She hadn't opened it yet. It had been sealed, actually — wax along the edge, cracked with age but stubborn — and she'd set it on the corner shelf to deal with later, and then spent three weeks dealing with later.

"Odd how?" she said now, aiming for casual and landing somewhere nearer guilty.

"Odd like a person who's found something," Tam said. "You get a look. Like a dog that's heard the dinner bell from the wrong house." He finished the apple and tossed the core into the compost with a marksman's ease. "Whatever it is, either read it or put it back. Suspense is bad for the complexion. Ask Petch."

"There's nothing in it," Juniper said. "Probably recipes."

"Probably," Tam agreed, in the tone of a man agreeing with weather. He heaved himself up off the block, patted her shoulder with a hand like a ham, and ambled toward the gate. At the gate he paused, and some of the clowning drained out of his face, leaving underneath it the friend he actually was. "Juni. If you do go up to the ridge — just to look —"

"I'm not going."

"— take a coat," he finished. "The mist comes early this time of year."

He let himself out. Juniper stood alone among the herbs with the hum pressing softly against her skin and the afternoon light going long and gold across the beds, and admitted to the valerian, the comfrey, and the feeling-free cabbages that she had absolutely, definitely, one hundred percent decided to open that ledger tonight.

* * *

Evening came down the valley the way it always did, unhurried, folding itself into the hollows between hills like linen into a chest. Aldery retired early with a volume of bee-husbandry and instructions that if the kettle screamed, it was only performing. Juniper washed the supper dishes, banked the fire, walked past the back-room door twice, and on the third pass, went in.

The lamp threw soft circles on the shelves. Dust hung in the light like a held breath — like the valley itself, she thought, everything here holding its breath, all the time, forever.

The green ledger waited on the corner shelf where she'd exiled it.

Up close it looked older than the others, older than the shop, maybe. The leather had gone the color of deep water. There was no lock, only the seal along the page edges — dark wax, cracked into a spiderweb by a century of dry seasons. She touched it with one finger and the largest piece fell away on its own, as if it had only been waiting for permission.

"Don't," she said to herself, out loud, in the empty room.

She opened it.

The pages were dense with handwriting — the same hand throughout, Juniper saw, a fine slanted script that leaned forward like a runner at a starting line. Not recipes. Not clients. The headings alone made the hairs on her arms stand up: dates. Years and years of dates, each with a few lines beneath it, each entry written in the past tense about things that hadn't happened.

*On the third of May the miller's daughter will burn the bread and blame the cat.*

*On the eleventh of September, rain, and a stranger at the door with a lame horse.*

Every date. Every small, certain fact. Written down years — decades — before they came true. Juniper's mouth went dry. She flipped backward through the ledger, faster, the pages whispering, the lamplight shivering, until she reached the last written page near the front of the book, because whoever wrote it had begun at the end and worked toward the beginning, filling the future the way other people filled diaries.

The final entry sat alone at the top of the page, in ink darker than all the rest:

*On the night of the first frost, the hedge-born key will come. The valley will forget to hold its breath.*

Below it, a date.

Tonight's date.

Written, Juniper calculated with the distant, ringing calm of someone watching their own life tilt sideways, ninety-seven years ago.

"No," she said, to the ledger, to the room, to the sensible part of herself that was already reaching out to shut the cover. "No, that's — it's coincidence. It's a common name. Hartwell is practically half the valley—"

But it wasn't the name that stopped her breath. It was the little line beneath the date, added in the same slanted hand, smaller, almost apologetic:

*She will have dust on her sleeves and thyme under her nails. She will want to leave, and stay anyway.*

Juniper looked down at her sleeves. Dust. She looked at her fingernails. Thyme, green-black in the creases.

Outside, the hum — the constant, lifelong, unnoticed hum of the valley holding its breath — stuttered.

Cut out.

Like a snuffed candle. Like a heart skipping. The silence was so complete and so wrong that Juniper heard her own blood moving, heard the lamp flame lean, heard the ledger's pages settle. And the ink began to move — the dark letters of her date lifting off the page, swimming, rearranging themselves into words she hadn't read yet, words rushing up at her —

The world folded.

There was no better word for it, though she would spend a long time afterward searching for one. The back room folded like paper creased by a great careful hand, and the lamplight folded with it, and the smell of thyme and dust folded, and Juniper Hartwell — hedge-born key, keeper of dusty sleeves, girl who wanted to leave and stayed — fell through the crease into somewhere else entirely.
