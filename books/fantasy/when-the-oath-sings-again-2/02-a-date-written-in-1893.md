## 2. A Date Written in 1893

The trouble with dusting a prophecy shop, Juniper had learned by her seventeenth summer, was that dust in the Hollow had opinions.

It settled where it pleased — thick on the shelves of mundane cookbooks, nearly absent from the shelf of herbals, and absolutely coating everything that had ever belonged to magic. As if the valley's hum, that low held breath pressed against the world like a thumb over a flute's mouth, pushed all the residue of enchantment into one corner of one shop and left it there to sulk.

"You're sulking at the dust again," Juniper informed the shelf.

The shelf declined to answer. Everything in the Hollow declined to answer; that was rather the point of it.

She flicked her rag across the spines anyway — *Almanac of Unlikely Weather*, *Ledger of Small Prophecies, Vol. IV*, and beneath it, wedged back where even Mistress Thimble's long arms rarely reached, a book she'd never noticed before. It was bound in grey leather gone soft as moth wing, with no title stamped on it at all. Only a wax seal the size of a plum, cracked but stubborn, holding the covers shut like a mouth deciding whether to speak.

Mistress Thimble had rules about sealed things. Rule the first: *don't*. Rule the second: *if you must, tell me first so I can be elsewhere when it goes wrong.*

Juniper looked over her shoulder at the shop door. It was propped open with the good brick, letting in the smell of the herb garden — thyme going woody in the heat, the sharp green of rue, the particular sweetness of the honey-suckle hedge that gave the Hollow its name. Down the lane she could hear Old Bram arguing cheerfully with his mule, which was winning, as usual. The whole valley hummed its noteless note, the sound you stopped hearing after about three days alive and only ever heard again when it *stopped* —

Which is why she would later swear, hand on every herb pot she owned, that the hum went quiet for exactly one heartbeat before she touched the seal. Like the valley drawing breath to watch.

"Well," Juniper said to nobody, "I'm telling you first."

She told the shelf. She was fairly sure that counted.

* * *

The seal broke with no drama at all, which was somehow worse. Wax crumbled like old bread; the covers fell open; and a smell came up out of the pages that did not belong to dust or paper but to something living and far away — rain on hot stone, woodsmoke, a flower she had no name for. The Hollow smelled of thyme and turned earth and nothing else, always. This smell made her chest ache in a way she couldn't explain, the way a song can ache when you've only heard four notes of it.

The ledger was written in two hands. One was cramped and fussy, marching down the margins: dates, weather notes, little complaints about ink prices. The other filled the main columns in a slanting script that leaned like wind-blown wheat — confident, impatient, beautiful. Juniper knew that second hand. She'd know it anywhere. It was the same script that labeled the jam jars, the seed drawers, the tin marked POISONS (NO, REALLY).

Mistress Thimble's handwriting. Except the dates were impossible. Except the ink was brown with age, and Mistress Thimble, whatever she claimed about her age whenever anyone asked about the second cup at supper, had not been writing ledgers in 1893.

Juniper turned pages. Prophecies marched past — small ones, mostly, the kind the Hollow tolerated because they were useless. *A red hen shall confound the miller. Rain on the feast of Saint Alder, mark it well.* Each entry dated, each initialed **E.T.** in that leaning hand, each one followed decades later by the cramped marginal notes confirming it, gloating over it, occasionally disputing it (*"the hen was BROWN, E., do try to be accurate"*).

It was, she realized, an account book. A prophecy ledger kept the way a farmer keeps lambing records. Somebody had been very good at their work for a very long time.

And then the pages under her thumb went stiff and refused to turn, and she looked down, and the world got very simple and very loud.

There was a date. Tonight's date. Not approximately, not in some riddling prophecy-code — the actual day, month, year, written in brown century-old ink with the same sure lean as everything else. Beside it, a single line:

*The hedge-born key opens the shop at half past nine. Dusty. Late.*

"Dusty," Juniper repeated aloud, because her voice needed something to do while her mind went somewhere else entirely. "Rude."

And below that, in the cramped margin hand, added years afterward in fresher black ink:

*Confirmed. Do not let her see this page. — T.*

Juniper stood very still in the middle of the dusty shop, holding a hundred-year-old book that had known she was coming since before her grandmother's grandmother was a rumor, and felt the strangest sensation of being *read*. Of having been read for a long time. Of being a line in someone's careful records, filed between a red hen and the weather.

Her first instinct was to obey the margin note. Close the book, shelve it, forget it, go water the rue. That was the Hollow's whole teaching, wasn't it? Don't. Stay small. Stay inside the hum. Magic cannot follow you here, and neither, apparently, can your own life if you keep your head down enough.

Her second instinct — the one with the ache of unnamed flowers in it, the one that had spent seventeen years pressing its face against the valley walls like a child against a window — reached out and turned the page.

* * *

The ink moved.

Not blurred. Not smeared. *Moved* — the letters gathering themselves like startled birds, lifting off the page in a slow dark swarm, and Juniper had time to think, absurdly, *so that's why the rule is don't,* before the hum cut out.

She had never heard silence before. Nobody in the Hollow had, not truly; the hum was underneath everything the way bedrock is underneath everything, and you only know it's been holding you up when it stops. The silence hit her like cold water. In it she could hear her own heart, the tick of the shop clock, Old Bram's mule three lanes over, and underneath all of it a new sound rising out of the open ledger — a sound like singing, very far away, sung by something enormous and grieving.

"Oh," said Juniper Hartwell, who had wanted elsewhere her entire life without once believing it would come for her. "Oh, that's not fair."

The world folded.

There is no honest way to describe it, and Juniper spent years afterward refusing to try. The shop folded along a seam that hadn't existed a moment before, taking the shelves and the clock and the propped-open door with it; the light bent; the smell of rain-on-hot-stone swelled until it was the whole sky; and through the fold she glimpsed — just glimpsed, a frame of it, like looking through a keyhole at a cathedral —

Snow. Falling *upward.* Streets of grey stone under a bruised purple sky. Figures walking with the unhurried grace of people in a dream who don't know they're dreaming. And everywhere, everywhere, the grief-song, close now, vast, coming out of the ground itself, out of the stones, out of the air —

The ledger pulled. She was still holding it. She would always be glad, in a distant and horrified way, that she was still holding it, because a person falling between worlds ought to have something to hold.

The last thing she saw of the Hollow was the herb garden through the folded seam, impossibly small and bright, the thyme going woody in the heat, the honey-suckle hedge humming its one held note — and the garden was already fading when she thought, with terrible clarity:

*I never told her goodbye. I never told her anything. I was going to, someday, when I figured out how to want things out loud.*

Then the seam snapped shut behind her like a struck bell going quiet, and Juniper Hartwell fell out of the only home she'd ever known, clutching a prophecy with her own name in it, into a country that everyone else had forgotten on purpose.

* * *

She landed the way cats land, which is to say badly, with dignity intact and one knee bleeding.

Cobblestones. Real ones, cold through her skirt, slicked with frost that glittered wrong — glittering upward, she realized, the frost crystals standing on end and pointing at the bruised sky like tiny compass needles all agreeing on a direction. Snow drifted past her shoulder, unhurried, ascending. Somewhere above the rooftops it gathered into clouds that were themselves made of falling-up snow, and the whole sky breathed slowly around it, and the grief-song ran underneath it all like a river under ice.

Juniper knelt on the stones of a street in a city that should not exist, and did the only reasonable thing available to her.

She checked the ledger.

Still there. Still open. Her own name still sitting beside tonight's date in a dead woman's handwriting — except the margin note was different now. The fresh black ink, the *Do not let her see this page — T.*, had faded to brown like everything else. And beneath it, in that leaning, wind-blown, infuriatingly familiar script, one more line had appeared, the letters settling into place even as she watched, wet and shining:

*Too late. Be brave, girl. You were always going to be.*

"E. T.," Juniper whispered, and her breath hung in the frozen air a long time, longer than breath should hang, as if the country itself wanted to keep a piece of it. "You absolute — you *knew.* You wrote me down a hundred years ago and you didn't *warn* me?"

The grief-song shifted. Just slightly. Just enough that she could almost mistake it for an answer — a low minor chord resolving toward something that might, in a kinder century, have been comfort.

She got up. Her knee stung. The snow rose past her face, and a woman in a long grey coat walked by without seeing her, moving with that dreamlike patience, and then another figure, and another, the citizens of Vessia flowing past like memories of people rather than people themselves. None of them looked at the kneeling stranger. None of them looked at anything, quite.

That was when Juniper understood the worst of it, the thing that made her stomach drop through the cobblestones and keep dropping: they weren't ignoring her. They simply couldn't spare the attention. Every soul on this street was listening to the song. Living inside it. Held up by it the way the Hollow's hum had held up her whole world — except this hum was breaking, note by note, and everyone here was pretending not to hear the cracks.

Boots rang on stone. Not dreamlike boots. Deliberate ones.

Four figures came up the street in formation, and these moved like people — sharply, wearily, *purposely* — cloaked in grey-blue, armed, alert, their faces lit by lanterns that burned with a flame the color of the bruised sky. The one in front raised a hand and the others halted, and he looked at Juniper — actually looked, the first eyes in this whole impossible country to land on her and stay — and she saw him see three things in quick succession: a stranger. A stranger where strangers are impossible. And in her hands, glowing faintly in the frozen dusk, an open ledger that had no business existing anymore.

"Hold there," the oath-keeper said, and even his voice sounded like it was trying not to join the song.

Juniper Hartwell, hedge-witch's apprentice, late of the Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow, dusty and bleeding and one hundred years overdue, did the bravest thing she could think of.

She closed the book, hugged it to her chest, and said, "This isn't what it looks like."

It was exactly what it looked like. But she'd be hanged — possibly literally, given the look of them — before she admitted that to a man with a lantern-sword and a patrol at his back.

His eyes narrowed. Behind him, the snow fell upward, patiently, like the country rehearsing how to leave.

"That," he said, stepping closer, "is a Vessian ledger."

"It's my mistress's ledger."

"It is a *prophecy* ledger." He had stopped an arm's length away, and this near she could see frost in his eyelashes and exhaustion carved deep under his eyes, and she could feel warmth radiating off him — actual warmth, human warmth, the first she'd felt since the fold, and some traitorous part of her leaned toward it like the frost crystals leaned toward the sky. His gaze dropped to the book in her arms, and something crossed his face that looked almost like fear. Almost like hope. She couldn't tell which frightened him more.

"What's your name, stranger?" he asked quietly.

And Juniper, who had spent her whole life in a valley that taught her not to want things, found herself wanting — absurdly, terribly — to answer honestly.

"The ledger says tonight's date," she said instead. "Written in 1893. So I suppose you'd better decide fast whether you're arresting me or asking me to dinner."

Behind her, the grief-song changed key.

He heard it too. She watched him hear it — watched the whole street hear it, heads turning in unison like a field of grey flowers following a sun that wasn't there.

The oath-keeper's hand closed gently, firmly, around her arm, warm as a coal in all that cold.

"You're coming with me," he said. "Both of those options may yet be on the table."
