## 3. Arrival in a Country That Isn't

The first thing Juniper Hartwell noticed about Vessia was that the snow was falling up.

She was lying on her back on cobblestones that were far too cold for summer — she'd been dusting the shop in July, she was almost sure of it — and above her head, fat white flakes were drifting serenely toward a sky the color of a bruised plum. They rose past the crooked eaves of buildings that leaned into each other like old friends sharing secrets, and they kept going, up and up, until they vanished somewhere near the clouds.

Juniper watched this happen for a long moment.

"Well," she said, to no one in particular, "that's not right."

Her voice came back to her strangely, flattened at the edges, as if the air itself couldn't quite be bothered to carry it properly. She sat up slowly. Her hands were still holding the ledger — she had gripped it so hard on the way through *whatever that was* that the cover had bitten a line into her palm, and the ink of her own name had smudged against her skin. *Juniper Hartwell*, printed backwards on her hand like a very ominous stamp from a very ominous fairground ride.

Around her stretched a street. That was the simplest way to put it, and yet the word felt insufficient, the way *puddle* feels insufficient for the ocean. It was cobbled and narrow and lined with shuttered shops whose signs hung motionless in air that should have been moving, because snow was moving, snow was definitely moving, just not in any direction that made sense. The gutters were full of frost. The lampposts wore little caps of ice like tiny gentlemen. And the whole street was populated.

That was the part her brain kept snagging on.

There were people everywhere — dozens of them, walking the length of the street, stepping through doorways, pausing at windows — but they moved wrong. Not badly-wrong. Not zombie-wrong, thank goodness. They moved the way figures move in a memory: smooth and slightly too slow, their footsteps making no sound at all on the stones, their faces turned toward things as if remembering them rather than looking at them. A woman in a grey shawl stood before a bakery window for so long that Juniper got bored watching her, and the woman never once shifted her weight. A man crossed the street with a parcel under his arm, and when he passed beneath a lamppost his shadow didn't keep up with him. It arrived several seconds later, hurrying.

"Okay," Juniper said quietly. "Okay. Talking out loud helps. The book said nothing about this, but talking out loud helps."

The book, of course, was still in her hands. She looked down at it now — the prophecy ledger, the one with the cracked leather spine and the wax seal she'd broken without meaning to, because who seals a ledger shut with century-old wax and expects a person to leave it alone? Nobody reasonable, that's who. Her mistress had a lot to answer for, assuming her mistress existed anymore, assuming the Hollow existed anymore, assuming anything existed except this bruised sky and this silent street and these people who moved like memories.

*Juniper Hartwell,* the ledger had said, beside tonight's date, written in 1893. She flipped it open again, half-hoping the words would have rearranged themselves into something sensible, like a recipe or a laundry list.

They hadn't. The entry stared back at her in brown-black ink:

*On this night the hedge-born key shall cross, as the hum fails and the door remembers. Let the Bastion prepare. Let the Oath attend. She will come warm, and the cold will know her.*

"The cold will know her," Juniper repeated. "Wonderful. Very reassuring. Love that for me."

A flake of upward-falling snow drifted past her nose. On instinct — the same instinct that had her catching falling thimbles and dropped seed packets since she was six years old — she snatched at it.

It didn't melt in her hand. It wasn't even particularly cold. It sat in her palm like a small pale bead, weightless as a thought, and then, after a moment, it dissolved with a sound like a single distant bell note.

The sound traveled down the empty street and came back changed. And Juniper realized, with a slow cold slide in her stomach, what else was missing.

Sound. All of it. There was no wind. No voices. No clatter of cart wheels, no bark of vendors, no hiss of hearth fires behind shuttered windows. A whole street full of people, and it was quiet as a held breath.

It reminded her of something, and it took her a moment to place it, and when she did she almost laughed, because it was absurd: it felt like the Hollow. Like that particular quality of pressed-in silence her valley carried, the hum-that-was-not-quite-a-sound pressing against her skin her entire life, the feeling of standing inside a held note. Except here the note was broken. Here the silence had cracks in it, and through the cracks came —

Something. Faint. Far away.

Juniper went absolutely still.

Through those gaps in the silence, threading between the memory-people and the rising snow, there was singing. Not a voice she could make out — more like the shape of a voice, the ghost of a melody, low and patient and unbearably sad, the kind of song someone sings when they've been singing a long time and no longer expect anyone to listen.

It caught in her chest like a fishhook.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor thing."

She didn't know who she was talking to. She only knew that the song sounded the way the Hollow's herb garden looked in late autumn — everything good gone dormant, everything waiting, everything grieving something it couldn't name. Her eyes stung, which was ridiculous. She wiped them briskly with the back of her ledger-smudged hand and stood up fully, brushing frost off her skirt, and decided on a plan.

The plan was: walk until something made sense.

It was not a great plan. But it had served her well for twenty-two years of wanting-to-be-elsewhere, and she saw no reason to abandon it now that elsewhere had so rudely swallowed her.

* * *

She walked.

The street bent where streets shouldn't bend, curving gently uphill in both directions, which geometry had some opinions about and chose, mercifully, to keep to itself. The memory-people paid her no attention whatsoever. She tested this twice — once by saying "good evening" to a man polishing a shop window that hadn't had glass in years, and once by waving directly in front of a young woman carrying a paper-wrapped bunch of flowers that had long ago frozen into a bouquet of ice.

Neither so much as blinked. The flower woman walked through Juniper's wave as if Juniper were smoke.

"That's fine," Juniper told her retreating back. "Very polite of you. Lovely flowers, by the way. They're dead."

She was talking to strangers again, which meant her nerves were wearing thin. Back home, when Juniper started narrating to the chickens, Mistress Elowen always said it was time for supper and a good night's sleep, and Juniper felt a sudden hot spike of homesickness so sharp it stopped her mid-step.

Mistress Elowen. Who had owned the ledger. Whose handwriting — and Juniper had known the woman's spidery script since she was nine — had been all over that 1893 entry.

"No," Juniper said aloud, firmly. "Absolutely not. She's seventy. Eighty, maybe. She's not a hundred and twenty."

And yet. The old woman had always been strange about dates. Strange about Juniper's birthday, especially — *the longest night, child, mark it well* — and strange about the valley's hum, humming tunelessly back at it while she weeded, and strange in a hundred small ways that had seemed like ordinary hedge-witch eccentricity right up until an hour ago.

Right up until the hum cut out. Right up until the ink moved.

Because it had moved. Juniper had watched it move. Her name had slid across the page like a fish deciding on a different current, and the valley's familiar pressure had vanished all at once — and she had felt, for one terrible, glorious heartbeat, lighter than she had ever felt in her life. Unheld. Free.

Then the world folded like laundry and dumped her on these cobblestones, and freedom, it turned out, felt a lot like being lost.

The singing pulled at her again through the cracks in the silence, and Juniper found she'd stopped walking and was simply listening, head tilted, one hand pressed flat over her sternum as if to hold the melody in. Up close it resolved slightly. Underneath the grief there were words — or the bones of words — and though she couldn't translate them, she understood the grammar of them perfectly. It was a promise. A promise sung by someone who had been promised to, and left.

*We swore, and the land kept faith, and we did not.*

That was what the song said, or that was what it felt like it said, and Juniper stood in the middle of a frozen street in a country that officially did not exist and grieved for it, for all of it, the whole impossible nation, without ever having seen it alive.

Which is, of course, exactly when everything went wrong.

* * *

It began with the bells.

Not real bells — she would learn later that Vessia's bells had all gone mute decades ago — but something like bells, a resonance that came up through the cobblestones into the soles of her boots. Three tones, spaced evenly. And every memory-person on the street stopped at once.

All of them. Mid-stride, mid-gesture, the flower woman with her frozen bouquet halfway to her face. They stopped like music stopping, and then they all turned their heads — not toward each other, not toward the sky, but toward the same point down the street, as if listening to something Juniper couldn't hear.

Their faces, for the first time, showed expression. It was longing. Every single face wore the same soft, aching longing, and Juniper felt suddenly, urgently certain that she did not want to know what they were listening to.

Then they resumed walking, in unison, away from her — and around the corner ahead came six figures in grey.

They were the first things in Vessia that moved *well*. Crisp, deliberate, entirely present, boots striking the stones with real sound — the first footfalls Juniper had heard since arriving, and somehow that was the most frightening thing yet. They wore slate-colored cloaks, and high collars, and at each collar sat a small metal badge shaped like an open hand. Their breath fogged. Their eyes scanned.

People, then. Actual living people. Which meant actual living people could see her, and given how she'd gotten here — illegally, impossibly, according to a book — being seen might not be a blessing.

Juniper did what any sensible person would do. She ducked behind a lamppost.

The lamppost was perhaps eight inches wide. She was perhaps somewhat more than eight inches wide. This is what panic does to spatial reasoning.

"—contact," one of the grey figures said, and the voice was so startlingly normal, so bored-and-cold-at-the-same-time, that Juniper nearly answered it. "Third street, by the chandler's. Confirmed visual."

"There is no third street by the chandler's," said another voice, younger, sharper. "The chandler's burned forty years ago."

"It's *there*. Look."

Juniper peeked around her inadequate lamppost. Six faces were looking directly at her. Five of them wore expressions she recognized from every authority figure she'd ever met: suspicion, calculation, the particular hunger of people who had been waiting a long time for something to happen.

The sixth face belonged to a young man, tall and hollow-cheeked, with frost in his dark hair that never melted, and he was looking at her the way the flower woman had looked at her flowers — except warmer. Much warmer. He looked at her like she was the first fire in a very long winter, and he clearly hated himself for it, and that, oddly, made him the least frightening of the six.

The leader stepped forward. Up close, Juniper could see that the open-hand badges weren't decorations; they moved faintly, fingers flexing, like small metal things dreaming of gripping.

"You are on the Street of Remembered Chandles," the leader said, in the tone of someone reciting a formality, "in the city of Vel Sarras, in the nation of Vessia, which does not exist. State your name and your purpose."

"I—" said Juniper, with tremendous dignity for someone hiding behind a lamppost. "I don't suppose *lost tourist* covers it?"

Silence. Somewhere above them, snow rose past the rooftops. The singing in the stones swelled, very slightly, as if leaning in to listen.

"Your name," the leader repeated, and now all six of them had hands resting on something at their belts — batons, maybe, or worse. "Or I am authorized to take it from you."

And here was the thing about Juniper Hartwell, the thing her mistress had always said would either save her life or end it: when she was frightened, she got honest, and when she was honest, she was incapable of lying well. She opened her mouth to attempt a lie anyway, glanced down at the ledger in her hands — her own name smeared across her palm, *she will come warm, and the cold will know her* — and felt the truth climb up her throat like a cat determined to be let out.

"My name is Juniper Hartwell," she said. "I'm from the Hollow. I think your country wrote me a letter a hundred years ago, and I think I'm the — " she swallowed — "the key. The hedge-born key. And I would like very much to speak to whoever's in charge, because I have questions, starting with *how do I get home*."

The silence that followed had texture. The five senior keepers exchanged looks of grim vindication. The young one — the fire-looker — went very still, and Juniper watched something complicated cross his frost-rimmed face: recognition, dread, and underneath both, unmistakably, hope.

"So it's true," the leader breathed. "The Bastion was right." He raised one gloved hand. "In the name of the Broken Oath and the council that keeps it: Juniper Hartwell, you are remanded to the custody of the Order of Keepers. Do not run. Nothing that comes here runs anywhere worth going."

"I wasn't going to run," Juniper said truthfully, because her legs had gone to jelly and running was frankly off the table.

The young keeper stepped forward to take her arm, and the moment his glove touched her sleeve, he flinched — a small, sharp, involuntary flinch, quickly mastered, as if her warmth had come through the wool and burned him. His eyes met hers for a fraction of a second. He looked terrified. Not of her, precisely. Of the fact that she was real.

"This way," he said roughly, and did not let go, and did not quite look at her again.

They walked her down the Street of Remembered Chandles, past the memory-people who flowed around the patrol like water around stones, under the bruised sky where snow climbed patiently toward heaven. Behind her, the sad song in the stones rose once more — and this time, Juniper could have sworn, it bent. Just slightly. Just enough.

Toward her name.
