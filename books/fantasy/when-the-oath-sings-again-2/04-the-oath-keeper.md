## 4. The Oath-Keeper

The report came in at the fourth bell of the night watch, which was the hour when Vessia was at its most honest. By day, the city of Corvel performed its routines — patrols marching, shutters opening, the market stalls that sold bottled summers arranging their wares just so, as though commerce could hold a country together. But at the fourth bell, when even the snow falling upward seemed to tire, the truth showed through: Vessia was a country holding its breath, and had been for a hundred years.

Wren Ashvale stood on the Bastion's eastern rampart and read the patrol note by lantern light, because his eyes were not what they had been before the winter took his mother's good candle supply along with everything else.

*Stranger sighted, Lantern District. Female. Unregistered. Holding a document bearing the royal seal. No frost on her.*

He read that last line three times.

No frost on her.

In Vessia, everything grew frost. The stones, the bread, the sleeping faces of children, the words on gravestones. Frost was the country's way of touching things — the land's cold, patient hand laid gently on everything it loved and could no longer keep alive. An unregistered stranger with no frost on her was either a miracle or an error, and the Bastion did not believe in miracles. It believed in errors, and it punished them.

"Commander," said Pell, one of his younger oath-keepers, breath fogging in the air despite the fact that breath wasn't supposed to fog anymore, not since the deep freeze settled in proper. Some rules of the world had simply given up on Vessia, and Wren tried not to take it personally. "Shall I assemble a squad?"

Wren folded the note. "No."

"No, sir?"

"I'll go myself." He pulled his gloves on, finger by finger, the ritual of it steadying something in him. Protocol said a sighting like this required a full squad of six, two mages, and a writ signed by the Council of Ashes. Protocol also said the last commander who'd deviated from it had been demoted to counting the Bastion's candle stubs, so Wren added, carefully, "I'll assess first. If she's what the note suggests, we don't want six armed men startling her into running. A single officer is less alarming."

"Less alarming than *six*, sir?"

"Everything is less alarming than six, Pell. Six is how you say *surrender or else*. One is how you say *good evening, may I see your papers*."

"And if she runs?"

"Then I'll run after her," Wren said, "and we'll both look ridiculous, and the country will have one more absurdity to add to its collection."

Pell laughed — a small, startled sound, as if laughter were a coin he'd found in the street and wasn't sure he was allowed to keep. That was the thing about the young ones. They still laughed first and checked whether they were permitted to second. Wren envied it the way he envied summer: distantly, hopelessly, without resentment.

He went down through the Bastion's halls, past the singing wall.

Everyone pretended not to hear the singing wall. It was considered poor form, in Corvel, to acknowledge that the east wing of the Bastion hummed with a grief-song old enough to have worn grooves into itself. New recruits asked about it once, received a flat stare from their superiors, and learned quickly to walk faster past it. Wren had never managed the walking-faster part. He always slowed, just slightly, the way you slow passing a house where someone died — out of respect, out of guilt, out of some stubborn belief that grief deserved witnesses.

Tonight the song was thinner than usual. He noticed it the way you notice a clock stopping: not the silence itself but the shape of the space where the sound should be.

He filed the thought away, next to all the other thoughts he didn't have time for, and went out into the snow that fell upward.

* * *

The Lantern District got its name from a century ago, back when it had actually contained lanterns — real ones, lit ones, the whole street glowing amber on festival nights. Now the name was a kind of joke the city told against itself. The lanterns hung empty on their hooks like birds that had forgotten migration was an option, and the only light came from the moon, which over Vessia was the color of an old bruise going yellow at the edges.

Wren walked alone through the streets, boots crunching on snow that lay where it shouldn't and drifted where it couldn't, and let himself do the thing he only allowed on solo walks: he looked at the citizens.

They moved like memories. Everyone said it; nobody said it aloud. The people of Corvel went about their days with a strange smoothness, as though each one were being remembered rather than lived — the baker kneading dough he would never sell warm, the lamplighter lighting nothing, the schoolteacher leading children who had stopped asking questions years ago through lessons everyone knew by heart. The winter hadn't killed them. Killing would have been honest. Instead it had made them *fainter*, year by year, like ink left in the sun, until Wren sometimes wondered whether he was the only person left in Vessia who cast a proper shadow.

He found her in Tanner's Row, and every prepared speech he had rehearsed on the walk over went out of his head at once.

She was standing in the middle of the street looking up at the upward-falling snow with an expression he had not seen on a face in years — possibly ever. It was the expression of someone seeing something genuinely new. Not afraid yet. Not performing bravery yet. Just *wondering*, openly, the way children wondered, before the country taught everyone to wonder quietly or not at all.

And she was warm. He knew it before he knew anything else about her, the way you know a fire is lit in a house you're passing. There was color in her cheeks. Her breath came out in ordinary white puffs that fell instead of rising, because apparently even her breath refused to follow Vessian rules. Snowflakes landed on her dark hair and *stayed*, melting slowly, and Wren felt something crack in his chest that he suspected had been load-bearing.

"Halt," he said, and immediately hated himself for it, because she turned and looked at him — really looked, with those startled brown eyes — and *halt* was not the word this moment called for. Nothing in his training had covered this moment. His training had covered insurgents, smugglers, oath-thieves, and once, memorably, a man who claimed to be the reincarnation of King Aldous and had been talked down mostly with tea.

This was none of those. This was a woman in a coat too thin for any winter, holding a book against her chest like a shield, looking at him as though *he* were the impossible one.

Which, he supposed, was fair.

"You're not from here," he said, abandoning *halt* entirely.

"Is it the coat?" she said. "I've been getting the impression it's the coat."

It took him a moment to realize she was making a joke. An actual joke, dry and quick, offered to a stranger with a sword at his hip in a frozen country that officially did not exist. Something in Wren's mouth twitched toward a smile, and he wrestled it down with both hands, because he was an oath-keeper of the Bastion of Corvel, and oath-keepers did not smile at suspects in the middle of an arrest.

"It's everything," he said. "The coat, the color of you, the—" He gestured vaguely at her entire existence. "You're not frosted."

"Frosted," she repeated. "Like a pastry?"

"Like everything." He stepped closer, slowly, hands visible — habit, courtesy, and the fact that something about approaching her felt less like policing and more like approaching a bird you didn't want to startle. "Vessia frosts what it touches. Stone, wood, water, flesh. You're standing in falling snow and there isn't a flake on you that's lasted more than a breath. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That your country has opinions about temperature?"

"That you are impossible," Wren said, and heard how it came out — reverent rather than accusatory — and cleared his throat. "Ma'am. I'm going to need to ask you some questions."

"Are you arresting me?"

"Pending assessment."

"Of what?"

"Whether you're a miracle," he said, "or an error. The Bastion distinguishes between the two. Barely."

That earned him another look — warier now, but underneath the wariness, curiosity. She hugged the book tighter. He recognized it suddenly, even upside down, even across ten feet of impossible snow: the seal embossed on the cover, the broken-crown sigil, the spine gone soft with age. A ledger of the old royal archive. He'd seen one exactly like it exactly once, locked behind three doors in the Bastion's deepest vault, guarded by men who were forbidden to speak its contents.

"What's your name?" he asked.

A pause. Long enough to be interesting. "Juniper."

"Just Juniper?"

"Just Juniper," she agreed, in the tone of someone who intended that to be true for as long as possible.

"Officer Wren Ashvale," he said, and gave the little bow that protocol demanded and that he privately thought was ridiculous, since bowing to someone while planning to detain them was like apologizing to furniture before moving it. "Juniper. I'm going to need you to come with me to the Bastion."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I arrest you, which is the same thing with more paperwork."

"That's not very persuasive."

"I'm not trying to persuade you. I'm trying to be honest, which in my experience persuades better, just slower." He held out a gloved hand. "There are six more of me twenty minutes away, Juniper-Just-Juniper. I'd rather it was just me. Come quietly, answer questions, and I give you my word you'll be treated fairly."

"Do oath-keepers' words count for much around here?" she asked. And there it was again — not fear, not quite. Assessment. Sizing him up the way he'd been sizing up her, and finding him, perhaps, an interesting puzzle.

Wren thought about the question honestly, because lying to her felt suddenly, strangely expensive. "Ours are the only ones that do anymore," he said. "The king's broke. The council's ghosts. The priests forgot their prayers. All that's left keeping promises in this country is us, and there aren't many of us left either." He paused. "So yes. Mine counts."

Something shifted in her face — not trust, nothing so generous, but a recalculation. She looked past him, up the empty street, clearly weighing her odds of outrunning a trained soldier in a coat designed for a different climate entirely. Then she looked at his outstretched hand, and then — this was the part he would think about later, turning it over like a stone — she looked at *him*, at his face, for three full seconds, reading something there.

"All right," she said, and took his hand.

Warmth shot up his arm.

He had known she was warm. Knowing and feeling, it turned out, were entirely different countries. Her hand through his glove was like sunlight through a window you'd boarded up so long you'd forgotten windows let light through. He kept his grip correct and professional and released her half a beat later than regulation specified, and hated himself for the half beat, and could not for the life of him regret it.

"This way," he said, voice rougher than intended. "Stay close. Don't touch anything silver, don't answer anyone who asks your name twice, and whatever you hear coming from the east wing when we arrive—"

"The singing?" she said. "I already heard it. From the street. What *is* that?"

And there it was again, that cracking feeling, because nobody asked about the singing. Nobody heard it from the street. Citizens had stopped hearing it decades ago, the way you stop hearing a river you live beside, but this stranger — this warm, impossible, unregistered miracle-or-error — had heard the Broken Oath from Tanner's Row within an hour of arriving in a country that wasn't supposed to exist, and wanted to know what it was.

"You can hear it," Wren said. Not a question.

"Hard to miss. It sounds like..." She searched. "Like someone trying to finish a sentence and forgetting the words. Over and over. For a very long time."

He stared at her. Somewhere behind his ribs, protocol — which had served him faithfully for eleven years, through promotions and reprimands and the long gray centuries of the freeze — shifted its weight uneasily, like an old dog hearing a noise under the floorboards.

"That," Wren said finally, "is the Oath our last king broke. It's been singing since the night he broke it. Grief does that here, apparently. Outlives the grievers." He started walking, and she fell into step beside him — beside, not behind, which protocol also had opinions about, all of which he ignored. "The Bastion will want to know how you heard it."

"The Bastion wants a lot of things, I'm guessing."

"You have no idea."

They walked through the empty streets, the upward snow drifting around them like a slow-motion reversal, and Wren caught himself cataloguing things he had no business cataloguing: that she walked like someone used to uneven garden paths, that she smelled faintly of rosemary and crushed mint, that when a shutter banged somewhere she didn't flinch, just glanced, calm as a cat. That she was, unmistakably, undeniably, *warm*, and that standing next to her was like standing next to a lit hearth in a house that had been cold for a hundred years, and that he was in serious danger of wanting to stay there.

Duty, he reminded himself. She was a duty. An anomaly requiring assessment, containment, and reporting. Whatever the ledger in her arms was, the Council of Ashes would want it, and the Lord Inquisitor would want *her*, and Wren Ashvale's job — his whole self, really, the only self he had — was to deliver both and feel nothing about it.

"So," Juniper said, breaking into his thoughts with the ease of someone who had no idea she was trespassing. "This Bastion. Is it the kind of place where questions get answered, or the kind where they get asked at you?"

"Yes," Wren said.

She laughed. Actually laughed — short, surprised, real, a bright little sound in the dead street that made two memory-people in a doorway turn their heads, slow as weather vanes.

And Wren, oath-keeper, keeper of the last keeping promises in Vessia, walked on through the upward snow with a criminal at his side and a smile he couldn't fully suppress, and understood, with the clarity of a man watching the ice crack under his own feet, that whatever happened next, nothing was going to be simple ever again.

Behind them, faint and thin and growing thinner, the Broken Oath sang on — and beneath its grief, for the first time in a century, Wren could have sworn he heard it change key.
