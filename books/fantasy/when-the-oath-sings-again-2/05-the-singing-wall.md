## 5. The Singing Wall

The cell was not what Juniper had expected of a magical prison, though she supposed she hadn't actually known what to expect. She had imagined something with more glowing chains and less mildew.

It was round, which struck her as a strange choice for a room. Round rooms wasted corners, and corners were where you put things — chairs, mostly, in her experience, or the good teapot nobody was allowed to use. This cell had no corners at all. It was a smooth grey drum maybe ten paces across, with a floor that sloped gently toward a drain in the center, a bucket in the place where a drain would have been more civilized, and a narrow slot of a window set so high up the curved wall that she could see nothing through it but sky. Bruised, heavy, snow-colored sky. The same sky as outside, but somehow the seeing of it from inside made her chest tighten in a way the open street had not.

They had taken the ledger. That stung worst of all, worse than the manacles they'd replaced with a leather cuff that hummed faintly against her pulse like a trapped moth. The ledger — the book with her name in it, written by somebody's careful hand a hundred years before she'd been born — was somewhere else now, probably being read by people with titles like *Warden* and *Lord Inquisitor*, and she was here, alone, in a room shaped like the inside of a bell.

She had tried the door twice. It wasn't locked so much as *convinced*. When she pressed her palm against it, the wood gave off a sensation she could only describe as polite refusal, like a shopkeeper's smile when you asked to buy something clearly marked *not for sale*. There was no lock to pick. There wasn't even, as far as she could tell, a hinge. The door simply was a wall until someone on the other side decided otherwise, and then it was a door again, briefly, long enough for a bowl of something grey and steaming to be slid through, and then wall again.

The bowl had been lentils. She had eaten them because her body insisted on continuing regardless of circumstances, and because her mistress had raised her with the iron law that a witch who couldn't eat prison food deserved whatever happened next.

Juniper sat with her back against the curved stone and pulled her knees up and did the thing she always did when the world went wrong, which was inventory it.

One: she was in Vessia, a country that had officially stopped existing roughly a century ago, which meant either the histories were wrong or she was somewhere very difficult to find on maps. Two: the book had said tonight's date beside her name, in ink that moved. Three: the patrol of oath-keepers — grey-cloaked, frost-rimed, moving with that terrible unhurried grace of people who had nowhere warm to be — had found her within minutes of arrival, which suggested they had been waiting, which suggested the prophecy was not a secret here. Four: one of them had touched her arm to steady her over the cobbles, and he had flinched. Not from her. From the warmth. Like a man putting his hand into sunlight after years underground.

That one had eyes the color of winter beech leaves, and a jaw that looked carved by someone who'd run out of patience halfway through, and he had said, "You're coming with us," in a voice that apologized without meaning to.

Five, and this was the item her inventory kept circling back to no matter how she tried to file it elsewhere: the snow fell upward here. It rose off the streets in slow spirals, drifted past the windows, gathered on the undersides of eaves and balconies, and hung there like the country couldn't decide which direction grief should go.

"Right," she said aloud, because silence was worse. "Absolutely fine. Everything's fine."

Her voice came back at her wrong. In the Hollow, sound behaved — it bounced off hedge and hillside and came home slightly softened, the way a well-behaved echo should. Here, the cell took her voice and *kept* it a half-second too long, and returned it changed. Warmer. Almost harmonized.

She frowned. Said it again. "Fine."

There it was. Her flat, unremarkable voice going out and coming back with a faint second line underneath it, low and resonant, like someone humming along an inch behind her words.

"Stop that," she told the wall.

The wall did not stop. But as she listened — really listened, the way her mistress had trained her to listen to plants, which is to say with her whole body and no expectations — she realized the harmony wasn't responding to her at all. It had been there the whole time. Under everything. A note so constant and so deep that her ears had filed it away as silence, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat or the hum of the Hollow itself.

The Hollow's hum was pressure. This was different. This was—

*Singing.*

Juniper got up off the floor so fast her knees cracked in protest, and pressed both palms flat against the stone.

The stone was cold, obviously. Cold enough to ache. But under the cold, vibrating up through her wrists and into the bones of her forearms, ran the note, and the note had *texture*, and the texture had *words*.

She couldn't make them out at first. They were buried, the way a melody is buried under its own accompaniment, and she had to go very still and breathe very slowly and let her attention sink down through the surface of the sound the way you sink through the surface of cold water — one shock, then adjustment, then depth.

Then the words surfaced, and Juniper Hartwell forgot how to breathe.

*I swore by root and river,* sang the wall.
*I swore by salt and seed.*
*What I promised to the ground I keep,*
*what I promised I will bleed—*

"Oh," she whispered, and the wall took her whisper and folded it into the verse like it belonged there, and maybe it did, maybe everything spoken in this country became part of the song, and that thought was so enormous she had to sit down on the floor again with her hands still touching the stone because letting go felt like interrupting something sacred.

It was a song. An actual song, with verses, with structure, with the kind of repetition that meant it had been sung so long it had worn grooves in itself. And it was coming out of every wall in the Bastion, and if the walls were singing it, then the walls were only carrying it, the way a hedgerow carries wind, and the wind — the actual song, the source — was the country itself.

Vessia was singing. Had been singing. For a hundred years.

And the song was a broken promise.

She listened until her legs went numb and her fingertips ached with cold, and the verses came around again, and this time she caught more of them, because the song seemed to notice her listening and slow down, the way a shy singer might lean toward an attentive face.

*I swore by hearth and harvest,*
*by the naming of each child,*
*by the road that remembers walking*
*and the field that knows it's wild.*
*I swore the winters mercy,*
*I swore the springs return—*

and there the verse broke. Not ended — broke, mid-line, like a bone. The melody reached for a resolution that wasn't there anymore and fell into a lower register, darker, slower, the musical equivalent of a sentence that stops because the speaker has started crying:

*—and the spring did not return,*
*and the spring did not return,*
*I promised and I lied,*
*and the spring did not—*

It looped. It couldn't get past it. The verse snagged on the failure the way a sleeve snags on a nail, and tore, and rewove, and tore again, and each time it tore Juniper felt it in her teeth.

She had grown up inside a valley that refused magic, and she had always assumed — everyone in the Hollow assumed — that magic was loud. Fireballs. Prophecies. Books that ate people, which, fair, hers had. Nobody told you that real magic, old magic, the load-bearing kind, sounded like this: quiet, patient, and unbearably sad. A whole nation's worth of grief sung into bedrock, keeping the lights half-on, keeping the citizens walking their memory-rounds through the frozen market, keeping the snow confused about which way was down.

Keeping the country alive. Half-alive. The way you keep a fire alive by feeding it the furniture.

Juniper sat in the middle of the singing cell and cried, quietly and furiously, because she hadn't asked to feel this, she hadn't agreed to grieve for strangers, and yet the song poured sorrow straight through the stone into her sternum like the Bastion itself had picked her up by the collar and said, *look. Look what we did. Look what was done to us. Someone should know.*

Someone should know.

And then, because she was her mistress's student, and her mistress had taught her that noticing was a discipline and not a mood, Juniper dried her face on her sleeve and started counting.

Because here was the thing about songs. She didn't have magic — the Hollow had made sure of that, had raised her in a valley where magic bounced off like rain off waxed canvas — but she had spent nineteen years learning the rhythms of living things. When a plant was thriving, its growth came in waves with rest between them. When a plant was dying, the waves flattened, the rests shortened, the whole pattern stuttered toward a final flatline. You could stand in a garden and *hear* the difference, if you'd trained your ear, and she had, because her mistress believed ears were tools and most people let theirs rust.

So she counted.

She counted the length of the full cycle — all the verses, the breaking, the loop, the terrible pause before it began again. Then she waited through another cycle and counted again, lips moving, tapping the beat against her knee.

The second cycle was shorter.

She told herself she'd miscounted. Waited through a third. Counted twice, once forward and once backward from the pause, the way you check a sum.

Shorter again. By a little. By the length of a held breath.

By the fourth cycle she stopped needing to count, because she could feel it, the way you can feel a fever climbing in your own skin. The song was compressing. The pauses between verses were shrinking. Notes that had rung for three heartbeats now rang for two. The whole vast grieving anthem was speeding up — no, not speeding up. *Starving.* Running out of something. Running down like a clock whose weights had almost finished their fall.

And she understood, sitting in the dark with her spine against a singing wall, why the citizens moved like memories. Why the snow fell upward. Why the sky looked bruised. The song was the country's heartbeat, and the heartbeat was failing, and when it stopped —

When it stopped, Vessia wouldn't die dramatically. Countries that died dramatically left ruins, and ruins left stories. Vessia would simply *stop*, mid-verse, the way the king's oath had stopped mid-promise, and the snow would finally decide on a direction, and the smiling forgetful citizens would finish forgetting, and there would be nothing left to remember them by except a line in a ledger in a dusty shop in a valley that refused to believe in any of this.

"How long?" she asked the wall. Her voice cracked on it. "How long do you have?"

The song didn't answer in words. But the next cycle came, and it was shorter again, and somewhere in the gap between verses Juniper heard — or imagined she heard, though she'd have sworn on her mistress's best pruning knife that she didn't imagine it — a single note bend toward her. Curious. Listening back.

The wall knew she was there. The prophecy had named her; the song had heard her named. And now the dying thing behind the stones had turned its ruined face toward the girl in the cell and was waiting to see what she would do.

"I don't know what you want from me," Juniper said fiercely, to the wall, to the song, to the entire impossible frozen country. "I'm not — I'm nobody's key. I'm a gardener. I deadhead roses. I know eleven ways to get rid of aphids and none of them are mystical."

The wall sang on. *I swore by root and river. I swore by salt and seed.* Patient. Ancient. Running out of time.

And Juniper, who had spent her whole life wanting to be anywhere but the Hollow, discovered that what she wanted now, with her whole unmagical heart, was to understand the words of a dying song well enough to help — and hated herself a little for how badly she wanted it, and loved herself a little for wanting it anyway.

* * *

Time in the cell was slippery. No torches were lit or doused; the light through the high window stayed the same bruised twilight it had been since she arrived, as if the sky above the Bastion had forgotten evening existed along with everything else. She slept in fits, curled against the warmest stretch of wall, which was warm only because the song vibrated there strongest, and woke each time convinced she'd heard her name in the verses.

Once, she was nearly sure she had. *Hedge-born,* the low harmony seemed to murmur under the main line, *hedge-born, hedge-born,* the way a congregation murmurs an amen.

"Don't," she mumbled into her knees. "I'm asleep. This is a dream."

The song declined to cooperate with her theory.

She passed the hours the way she passed bad winters in the Hollow: working. Without herbs or soil she worked on the only material available, which was the song itself. She learned its verses properly, all four surviving ones, mapping their shapes the way she mapped garden beds — where the strength was, where the rot had set in, what could be saved by pruning. The first two verses rang clear and whole. The third wobbled, missing a rhyme it must once have had; she could feel the absence the way you feel a missing tooth. The fourth was the broken one, the one caught on *the spring did not return*, and around it the whole structure strained, because a song, like a wall, like an oath, is only as sound as its weakest line.

Somewhere in the middle of her fourth attempt to reconstruct the missing rhyme — trying *return/burn/yearn/learn*, discarding each, muttering — footsteps stopped outside her door.

She froze, mid-word, feeling absurdly like a child caught talking to the garden. Which, to be fair, she also had been, many times.

The door became a door. It swung inward without a sound.

Two oath-keepers stood in the frame, grey cloaks frosted at the shoulders, faces beneath their hoods younger than she'd expected and wearier than she'd feared. Between them, half a step forward, stood the one from the street. Winter-beech eyes. Impatient jaw. He was looking at her with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the look of a person doing arithmetic they don't like the answer to.

"You've been talking to the wall," he said.

It wasn't a question, but it demanded a response anyway, and Juniper, who had inherited her mistress's talent for dignity under ridiculous circumstances, lifted her chin from where she sat on the floor.

"The wall talked first."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile; the muscles tried and were overruled. Behind him, one of the other keepers shifted uncomfortably, glancing at the stone as if it might be taking notes.

"The Lord Inquisitor will question you at the ninth hour," the beech-eyed keeper said. "Until then you'll remain—"

"It's losing verses," Juniper said.

He stopped. The word *losing* landed among them like a dropped glass.

"The song." She pushed herself to her feet, legs stiff, and pointed at the wall behind her, hand trembling and not entirely from cold. "Your — the oath, the singing, whatever you call it. Each cycle is shorter than the last. The third verse has lost its rhyme, which means it lost the word, which means the word mattered. And the fourth verse can't get past the break. It's been stuck there so long the break has worn smooth. Songs don't wear smooth unless they've been failing for a very, very long time." Her voice was rising and she couldn't make herself care. "I don't know what your protocols say, and I don't care about your Lord Inquisitor, but whatever it is you think I am, whoever sent for me, whatever's written in that book — you should know your country is dying faster than your leaders are willing to count."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

The other two keepers had gone very still in the way of soldiers deciding whether they've just witnessed treason or truth. The beech-eyed one looked at her for a long moment, and then — she would replay this later, turning it over like a stone found on a path — he glanced at the wall. Just once. Quickly. The glance of a man checking on something he loves.

When he looked back at her, his jaw had set into its final carving.

"Ninth hour," he repeated. "Someone will bring you water."

The door became a wall again.

Juniper stood alone in the singing dark, heart hammering, and realized two things at once. First, that she had just bargained — clumsily, desperately, with nothing but observation and nerve — and that some part of her had already understood what the coming interrogation would really be about: information, traded both directions, whether anyone admitted it or not.

And second, that the wall behind her had gone quiet during her outburst — truly quiet, the first silence in a century — and that in the silence, very softly, it had begun to sing the third verse again. Searching for its missing rhyme.

Almost as if it had been listening to her try.
