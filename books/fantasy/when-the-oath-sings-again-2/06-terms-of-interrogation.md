## 6. Terms of Interrogation

*Wren*

The prisoner had been in the Bastion for six hours and had already reorganized his interrogation room.

Wren stood in the doorway with a tray of bread and broth and stared at the evidence. The chair he'd left facing the door now faced the window — the one narrow window, barred, that looked out on the inner courtyard where the snow fell upward and nothing else ever happened. She had dragged it there herself; the scrape-marks were still fresh on the flagstones. Her boots sat beside it. Her stockings were patched at both heels, which was somehow the most alarming thing he'd learned about her all day.

She was sitting in the chair with her knees drawn up, watching the upward snow with the expression of a woman auditing an account she hadn't opened.

"You moved my chair," Wren said.

"It was facing the wrong way." She didn't turn around. "If you're going to interrogate someone, you should be able to see the sky. Everyone tells more truth when they can see the sky. It's a known fact."

"Known by whom?"

"Hedge-witches. My mistress. Anyone who has ever actually talked to a person instead of at one." She turned then, and her eyes found him with the unnerving directness of someone who had not yet learned to be afraid of him properly. "Is that broth? Because if this is the interrogation, I want to renegotiate the terms."

He set the tray down on the table between them — deliberately, he noted, still facing the wrong way — and took his seat opposite. "There are no terms. You are a prisoner of the Bastion of Vessia under the authority of the Oath-Keepers' Charter, article—"

"Article nine, I heard you the first time. And the second time. And through the wall when you were telling the cook." She leaned forward. "Here's what I know, oath-keeper. I know I was dusting a shelf in a valley where magic doesn't work, and now I'm in a country that your own patrol captain called 'a country that isn't,' whatever that means. I know the snow here falls up. I know your walls sing. And I know that when your patrol found me, four grown men with swords looked at a ledger like it was going to bite them. So either I'm a prisoner, or I'm the only person in this building who knows anything worth knowing. Which is it?"

Wren had planned, for this first conversation, an approach of measured silence. Let her talk. Let her fill the quiet and betray herself. He had used it on smugglers, oath-breakers, and one memorable tax fraudster from the coast, and it had never failed.

It failed in under a minute.

"Broth's getting cold," Juniper Hartwell said, and reached over and took the bowl.

* * *

*Juniper*

The broth was terrible. Salty in the wrong places, thin everywhere else, and faintly flavored like the inside of a kettle that had seen things. Juniper drank all of it, because a girl who'd grown up in the Hollow knew better than to turn down hot food on principle, and because she suspected refusing it would look like fear.

"So," she said, setting down the bowl. "Your move."

"My move?"

"This is how negotiations go. One side makes a demand. That's me, just now, with the drinking of your soup. Now you counter, or you fold, or you sulk. Those are the three options. I've watched my mistress haggle with wool merchants for eleven years. I know all the plays."

Something crossed his face that was almost, almost a smile before it remembered itself and became a jaw-line again. He was younger than she'd first thought, she decided — mid-twenties maybe, with the kind of tiredness around the eyes that couldn't be fixed with sleep. His uniform was immaculate except for one glove, missing, and the bare hand beneath it was wrapped in a strip of linen gone brown at the knuckles.

"What happened to your hand?" she asked.

"Nothing."

"That's a very defensive 'nothing.'"

"It's none of your concern."

"It's absolutely my concern. I'm going to be bargaining with you. Bargaining works better when the other party isn't hiding an injury. My mistress says—" She stopped herself. "I say that," she corrected. "I say that."

"The walls are listening," he said quietly. "Everything said in this room goes into the stones. So if you're planning to trade secrets, understand that you're trading them with the whole Bastion, not just me."

Juniper considered this. Then she considered the singing she'd heard last night, pressed against her cell wall until dawn — the grief-song with its slow dying words, each verse coming back thinner than the last, like a fire being fed less and less wood.

"Then let them hear this too," she said. "Last night, the song skipped a note. Third verse, near the end. There was a hole in it, like a tooth knocked out. This morning there were two holes. Whatever your Oath is doing, it isn't holding. It's starving. And everyone in this building can hear it, and nobody will say it out loud, and that—" she pointed at him with the spoon, "—that is the worst kind of secret. The kind everybody keeps together."

The silence afterward had weight. Through the window, the upward snow drifted past the bars, patient as ever, like it had somewhere above to be and all century to get there.

"You heard the words," Wren said at last. Not a question.

"Some of them. Enough to know they're sad enough to crack stone."

"And you noticed the gaps." He rubbed the linen-wrapped hand absently, and she saw him catch himself doing it. "Most people hear the song the way you hear rain. It's always there. Nobody counts the drops."

"I grew up counting things," Juniper said. "Seeds, stitches, coins. When you live somewhere small, everything gets counted eventually."

"Where is that? The Hollow." He said the name carefully, tasting it. "My grandmother told stories about the Hollow. A valley where magic can't follow. She said her mother's cousin walked in once and came out three days later unable to remember any spells at all. Just — gone out of her head like water out of a sieve."

"That's roughly accurate," Juniper admitted. "It's why I'm useless to you, by the way. If you've read that ledger, you've read whatever nonsense it says about me. But magic doesn't work on me, around me, or apparently anywhere within shouting distance of my childhood. Whatever your prophecy thinks I am, it got the wrong Hartwell."

"Did it?" He leaned forward slightly, and for the first time since the arrest, he looked less like a wall with a sword and more like a man doing arithmetic. "Because the ledger named you. By name. A hundred years before you were born. In my experience, prophecies make many mistakes, but naming mistakes aren't usually among them. They're annoyingly literal creatures."

"Then your prophecy is broken, same as your Oath."

"Same as our country," he said, and there it was — the crack in the wall, brief as a flash of light under a door. Then he straightened, and the wall came back up. "You should rest. Tomorrow we begin formally."

"Formally?"

"Interrogation protocol. Article nine."

"You quoted article nine twice today. Is that the only article you have?"

"It's the only one that applies to you."

"See, that sounds lonely," Juniper said, settling back into her stolen chair. "Being a man with one article."

For a moment — one real, unguarded moment — Wren laughed. It was short and rough, like a cough he hadn't authorized, and it transformed his whole face into something younger and more exhausted and more human. Then he heard himself, and stopped, and stood, and gathered his composure off the floor along with the empty bowl.

"Tomorrow," he repeated.

"Tomorrow," she agreed pleasantly. "Bring two bowls. I bargain better fed."

* * *

*Wren*

He did not bring two bowls. He brought one bowl and a second spoon, which felt like a compromise his dignity could survive.

She noticed immediately, of course. Everything about her noticed immediately. She ate half the broth, pushed the bowl precisely to the midpoint of the table, and folded her arms.

"Terms," she said.

"There are no terms. Article nine—"

"—governs prisoners, yes, you've made that very clear. So let's establish what I actually am." She held up fingers, ticking them off. "One: I'm not Vessian. Your charter, unless it's even stranger than it sounds, governs citizens. Two: I committed no crime. Walking through a hole in the world while dusting is not, as far as I know, illegal anywhere. Three: I am, according to your own ledger, the single most important person in this entire frozen country. So. Either you're interrogating a criminal, or you're negotiating with a guest who holds the only key anyone cares about." She lowered her hand. "Guests get terms. Criminals get article nine. Choose."

Wren looked at her across the table and understood, with the slow dread of a man watching ice shift underfoot, that she was right. Not legally — legally she was nothing, a foreign body, an anomaly to be contained. But practically. Politically. Prophetically. The ledger had named her, and the Bastion needed her, and everyone in the chain of command above him knew it, and she had figured it out in two days without anyone telling her.

"How do you know the ledger names you?" he asked. "You claimed yesterday you barely read it."

"I read my own name next to tonight's date written in 1893. That's quite a lot of reading." She tilted her head. "Why? What does the full text say?"

And there it was. The trap he'd walked into with both eyes open: the information flowed both ways now, and she'd built the channel herself, brick by brick, out of broth and stubbornness.

"That's not something I can share."

"Then I can't share what the Hollow knows about doors."

His spine went cold. "What did you say?"

"Doors," Juniper said, sweetly, terribly. "The Hollow sits on one, you know. Has for generations. We don't call it that — we call it the hum, the pressure at the edge of the valley, the reason no spell ever takes. But my mistress taught me the old hedge-craft, and hedge-craft is mostly about edges. Boundaries. Where one thing stops and another starts. And she taught me that every boundary is also a crossing, if you know the words." She smiled, and it wasn't a soft smile. "So. The full text of the prophecy, oath-keeper. Or I keep my valley's secrets, and you keep guessing."

The stones hummed faintly around them, carrying every word up to whoever listened. Wren thought of Captain Aldery's face if he reported this conversation verbatim. Thought of the council. Thought of the Lord Inquisitor's people, who had begun asking after the prisoner within hours of her arrival — asking with the particular politeness that preceded knives.

He thought, unwelcome and unbidden, of the fact that she was the first warm-handed person he had shaken in four years. Every touch in the Bastion went through gloves. Protocol. But she had gripped his wrist when the world folded her here, panicking, and the heat of her hand had stayed on his skin like a coal for two days.

"One condition," he said.

Her eyebrows rose. "Negotiating already? I'm proud of you."

"Not here." He glanced, deliberately, at the walls. "The song drowns out listening in the east archive. Old stones. They're too busy grieving to eavesdrop. If you want the full text, you come with me tomorrow, under escort, in chains if protocol insists, and I show you the original. And in exchange you tell me about the Hollow's edge. All of it."

"In chains?"

"If protocol insists."

"Protocol," Juniper said slowly, "seems very flexible around you. You brought two spoons."

"One spoon and a spare."

"Mm." She extended her hand across the table — bare, warm-looking, offered without hesitation, which struck him as either tremendous bravery or tremendous foolishness, and he was beginning to suspect those were the same thing in her. "Terms accepted. Provisionally. Pending reading material."

Wren took her hand. Warmth again. He told himself it meant nothing, that hands were hands, that four years of gloves had simply thinned his resistance to ordinary human heat. He told himself this while shaking on a deal he had no authorization to make, with a prisoner who had no legal standing, in a country that officially did not exist.

"Tomorrow, then," he said.

"Tomorrow," she agreed. Then, as he reached the door: "Oath-keeper."

He paused. "Wren. My name is Wren."

"Wren." She tried it out, and something in her face suggested she was filing it away in the same careful place she filed everything. "When you showed me the ledger page — the part with my name — the ink was moving. Writing itself. Did you see what it added?"

"No," Wren said honestly. "The text changes constantly. That's half of what makes it dangerous."

"Well," said Juniper Hartwell, pulling her chair back toward the window and the upward-falling snow, "when you show me the original tomorrow, check today's date. Because I have a horrible feeling it's still writing. And I'd very much like to know what it thinks I'm going to do next."

That night, alone in the east archive with the original ledger open under lamplight, Wren checked.

Beside her name, beneath the date, in ink that had not been there that morning, a new line had appeared:

*She will ask to see the sky.*
