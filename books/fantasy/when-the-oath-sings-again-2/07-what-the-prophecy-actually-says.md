## 7. What the Prophecy Actually Says

Wren had decided, somewhere around the third sleepless night, that the prisoner in cell nine was a problem.

Not because she was dangerous. The hedge-born key had all the menace of a startled sparrow. Not because she was difficult — though she was, magnificently so, arguing with the guards about the quality of the bread until Corporal Dessa had come to him personally, red-faced, demanding to know if "the foreign girl" was permitted to file complaints.

No. She was a problem because she was warm.

He had arrested her six days ago, and his hand still remembered it. Everyone in Vessia was cool to the touch — not frozen, precisely, but banked, like coals covered against a long night. It was simply what a century of winter did to people. He'd forgotten there was any other way for skin to feel. Then he'd closed his hand around this stranger's wrist on a street where snow fell upward, and warmth had shot up his arm like a confession.

She'd called him a "glorified snowman with a badge." That was also memorable.

Now he stood outside the records room with the prophecy ledger under one arm and a lantern in his other hand, and he told himself — firmly, the way you tell yourself anything you don't quite believe — that this was protocol. The key required orientation. The Bastion's charter said the prophesied instrument must be shown its purpose before use. *Use.* He didn't love the word. He liked even less how naturally it came to him.

The sentry at the records door stepped aside without a word. Down here, beneath the Bastion, everything was quiet except for the Oath itself, singing faintly through the stone like wind through a bottle. Wren had grown up with that sound. Every Vessian had. You stopped hearing it the way you stopped hearing your own heartbeat — until someone from outside arrived and reminded you that most worlds were silent.

She wasn't silent, either. When he unlocked her cell — moved her here last night, to the records annex, over the council's mild objections — he found her sitting cross-legged on the cot with her ear pressed flat against the wall.

"It changes," she said, before he could speak. "The song. Yesterday it hit a note I haven't heard before. A low one. Like something giving up." She turned, and her eyes went immediately to the ledger under his arm. "Is that it?"

"You've seen a page of it."

"I've seen my own name written by a dead man a hundred years before I was born. I'd call that slightly more than *a page*." She stood, brushing off her borrowed dress — Bastion grey, three sizes wrong, sleeves rolled four times. "You didn't answer the question."

"No," Wren agreed. "I didn't." He set the ledger on the table between them and did not open it. "Before I do, you should understand something about me."

"That you're going to be insufferably formal about it?"

"That I'm an oath-keeper." He held up his left hand, palm out, so she could see the mark: a thin silver line circling his ring finger like a scar of light. "I swore when I was fourteen. My oath is to Vessia — its land, its people, its continuance. Everything I do, I do inside that promise. So when I show you this, understand that I am not showing you as a kindness. I am showing you because the country needs you to understand it. There's a difference."

Something flickered across her face — disappointment? He found himself disliking that intensely, and disliked *that*, too.

"Fine," Juniper said. "Show me what I'm for."

* * *

The full text ran eleven pages. Wren had read it perhaps two hundred times since his oath-day; the words lived behind his teeth now, worn smooth like river stones. But watching her read them for the first time — watching her face do the thing faces did when they met their own fate in someone else's handwriting — he felt as if he were reading it fresh himself.

She read standing, both hands flat on the table, leaning over the ledger as if physical pressure might change what it said. Her lips moved slightly. The lantern guttered once. Somewhere far above, snow fell upward past windows neither of them would see tonight.

"*When the hundredth winter whitens the ninth year,*" she read aloud, slowly, "*the hedge-born key shall come from beyond the hum, from the valley where magic cannot follow. She shall stand at the Door of Return, and the Door shall know her hand.*"

"Go on," Wren said quietly.

"*And she shall have two turnings, and no third.*" Juniper's finger traced the line. "*If she opens the Door and passes through it, then Vessia shall fold itself away like linen at season's end, and its people shall sleep unburdened into the long mercy, and the song shall end, and the cold shall be kind.*"

"The merciful reading," Wren said. "The Door of Return doesn't lead anywhere. It leads *out*. If you walk through it, the country ends — gently. No more slow freezing. Our people lie down and stop, the way an old dog lies down. And the winter stops being cruel, because there will be nothing left to be cruel *to*."

Juniper looked up. "That's mercy?"

"It's the kindest ending available." He kept his voice level. Practice. Years of practice. "Continue."

Her finger found the next passage, and he watched her go pale in the lantern light.

"*Or, if she chooses the second turning, she may reforge the broken Oath and bind herself to the land forever — her voice its voice, her breath its breath, her life given over to the holding. So Vessia lives, and she does not, being become the wall between the people and the dark.*"

Silence. The Oath sang through the stones, low and patient.

"Bind herself," Juniper said. "Forever." She laughed, one sharp note with nothing funny in it. "'Being become the wall.' That's not living. That's being *load-bearing*."

"Yes."

"And these are the only two? The book is certain? Two turnings, no third?"

"The book is certain," Wren said. "It has been certain for a hundred years. We have checked. Generations of archivists have checked, hoping to find a loophole, a third turning, a footnote." He turned a page for her, to the final lines. "There is no footnote."

She read the last passage silently first. Then aloud, and her voice cracked on the middle of it:

"*The key shall be chosen by no choosing of hers. Written before she was, needed before she knew, she belongs to the story as the river belongs to its bed. What she owes the country, the country owed her before her birth.*"

Wren watched her close the ledger with exaggerated care, the way people handled things they wanted to throw across the room.

"'What she owes the country,'" she repeated. "The country owed *her*. Before her birth. Do you hear how that sounds? It sounds like a debt collector. It sounds like — " she gestured vaguely at the walls, at the whole buried, singing, freezing country — "it sounds like I was born already mortgaged."

"Yes," Wren said again. He was saying *yes* too much. He couldn't seem to help it. Every denial she voiced was a denial he'd voiced himself, alone, at this same table, at fourteen and nineteen and twenty-five, and hearing it in another voice was like hearing his own ghost argue back.

"So explain it to me," she said. "Explain it to me like I'm a person and not a *key*. Why does it have to be me? Actually me, specifically. Not another outsider. Not some volunteer. Me."

"Because you're from the Hollow." He pulled out the chair across from her and sat, which surprised her — he saw it surprise her. "Magic can't follow you in there. Your valley hums it away at the border. Which means you grew up with nothing magical in your blood, your bones, your expectations. The Door of Return was built to admit exactly one kind of person: someone the magic cannot hold, claim, or compel. Everyone born inside Vessia belongs to the Oath already. We're woven into it. You're not. You're the only thread loose enough to pull."

"But the reforging — " She frowned. "If I bind myself, I'd be woven in too. Forever. Doesn't that defeat the point?"

"The point," Wren said, and here his voice did betray him, just slightly, fraying at the edge, "is that we get to keep existing. Forgive me if the philosophy of the arrangement matters less than the fact that my people are forgetting their own names. Have you been to the capital? People smile at nothing. They wander. The winter takes the memory first, then the wanting, then the rest. Two turnings, Juniper. Mercy or a martyr. Those are what a hundred years of archivists found."

"Don't say my name like that."

"Like what?"

"Like it's a *fact* you're reciting." She pushed back from the table and walked to the far wall, arms crossed, facing the stone. Facing, whether she meant to or not, the source of the singing. "You've been polite this whole conversation. Suspiciously polite. You want something, and it isn't information."

Wren stood too. Protocol said he should give her space. Protocol said many things; lately it mostly said things he couldn't stand to hear. "What I want," he said, "is for you to choose one of the two turnings. Willingly. Soon. Because the council is not made of people who will wait for willingness forever, and the inquisitors—" he stopped.

"Inquisitors," she repeated. "You said that like it tasted bad."

"There are mages who feed on oaths. Broken ones, mostly. They are... persuasive." He chose the word carefully and hated it. "If you do not choose, choices may be made for you. I'm telling you this because — "

"Because the country needs me to understand it," she finished dryly. "Yes. There's a difference. You mentioned."

They stood there, ten feet apart, the ledger between them like a border crossing. The Oath sang. Upstairs, somewhere, a council debated her like weather.

"Wren." She turned to face him fully, and her expression had changed — the anger settling into something steadier, worse. Pity, maybe. Or recognition. "Do you actually believe those are the only options? Genuinely? Because I've known that book for six days and I already hate it. You've known it your whole life. Either you believe it completely, or you've spent years pretending not to notice the cracks."

He should have said *I believe it completely.* It was the oath-keeper's answer. It was the answer he'd given at every review, every vigil, every cold morning of his adult life.

What he said was: "I believe the cracks are load-bearing too."

Her eyebrows rose. And then — unexpectedly, terribly — she smiled. Small and crooked and warm, warm like her hands, warm like a country he barely remembered from stories.

"That," she said, "is the first honest thing anyone's said to me since I got here."

"It won't help you escape."

"It might. Honest things usually travel better than lies." She tapped the ledger's cover once, lightly, almost affectionately. "Two turnings, no third. Fine. I'll need time. And I'll need you to stop looking at me like I'm already a monument."

"I don't—"

"You do. Right there. That look." She pointed at his face. "'Future Benefactress of a Frozen Nation.' Stop it."

For a moment — one unguarded, catastrophic moment — Wren nearly laughed. It rose in his chest like a bird that hadn't flown in years, and he strangled it professionally, but she saw it. Of course she saw it. Warm people saw everything.

"I'll petition the council for time," he said instead, gathering the ledger. "A week. Perhaps less. Use it well."

"And if they refuse?"

"Then I'll petition louder." He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and did not turn around, because turning around would mean she'd see whatever his face was doing. "Juniper. The passage about owing. About belonging to the story as the river belongs to its bed. I've read it two hundred times, and I want you to know that every single time, I thought it was a cruelty."

The singing filled the pause behind him.

"And now?" she asked.

"Now I think whoever wrote it never asked the river." He opened the door. "Sleep well. As well as you can, in a country that wrote you before it met you."

The door closed. Wren stood a moment in the corridor with the ledger under his arm, listening to the Oath sing through the stone, and admitted to the empty hallway the thing he could not say in the room:

He had shown her the text because it was protocol.

But he had told her about the inquisitors — unprompted, off-record, against every rule he'd sworn at fourteen — because when she'd pressed her ear to the wall and listened to his country dying, her face had done something no Vessian face had done in a hundred years.

It had grieved. Openly. As if the song were hers to mourn.

And some treacherous part of him, some part older than his oath, wanted very badly to know what else she'd hear, if he let her listen longer.

Above him, through fathoms of stone and snow falling upward, the council bells began to ring — not the hour, but the summons. The council was voting early.

On her.

Wren tightened his grip on the ledger and started up the stairs, taking them three at a time.
