## 8. Escape Through the Frozen Market

The ledger sat on Wren's desk between them like a third person who knew too much.

Juniper had been staring at it for the better part of an hour. Wren had been staring at her for most of that, in the way of a man trying to solve a problem he suspected was also a person.

"So," she said, because the silence had begun to develop a texture, and not a pleasant one. "Two options. I open a door and your whole country dies politely, or I bind myself to it forever and become some kind of... what did you call it? A living cornerstone."

"Anchor," Wren said. "And those are the readings the scholars agree on. There are others."

"Worse ones?"

"Different ones." He turned the ledger toward himself with one finger, careful, the way you'd handle something sleeping. "Prophecy is rarely worse than you fear. It's usually stranger."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be accurate." He looked up at her then, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before — not the flat oath-keeper mask from the street, not the interrogator's patience from the Bastion cells. Something more tired. More human. "You should sleep on it. The council will want your answer by morning bell."

"The council." She'd heard about the council. Seven old men who spoke to ghosts and had opinions about her the way farmers have opinions about weather. "And if my answer is no?"

Wren was quiet for a moment. "Then we're all very polite to each other until it stops mattering."

* * *

They gave her a room instead of a cell, which she supposed was progress. It had a bed with actual blankets, a window with actual glass, and a view of Vessia's frozen rooftops under a sky the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges. Someone had left a tray of food: dark bread, hard cheese, tea that smelled faintly of pine.

She ate the bread. She drank the tea. She did not sleep.

Every time she closed her eyes she saw the two futures laid out like garments on a bed — the door, the anchor — and every time, the same thought kept circling back around like a moth: *nobody asked me which one I wanted before they wrote me into the book.*

Her name had been sitting in that ledger for a hundred years. Her name, in ink, beside tonight's date, written by someone who had never met her, deciding what she was for. The hedge-born key. The one who opens. As if she were a thing with a keyhole where a heart should be.

"I'm not a key," she told the ceiling. The ceiling declined to argue.

But the ceiling wasn't the problem. The problem was that everyone here — Wren, the council, the prophecy itself, even the singing walls of the Bastion — had already decided the shape of her choice. Door or anchor. Mercy or martyrdom. Pick one, Juniper Hartwell, and be written down forever.

There was a third option. It had occurred to her somewhere around the second cup of pine tea, and it was childish and cowardly and absolutely the only thing she could bear: don't pick anything. Don't be here when morning bell comes. Find Maren Quill, the cartographer of dead places, and find out if there was a version of this story where nobody got sacrificed.

It wasn't brave. She knew it wasn't brave. But she was twenty-three years old and she had been ordinary for her entire life, and she refused — she *refused* — to let a century-old book decide how she stopped being so.

She waited until the corridor outside went quiet. Then she took the blanket, folded it twice, and jammed it into the window frame where the latch should have been — a trick her mistress used for keeping drafts out of the herb loft, working just as well, presumably, for keeping guards from noticing an empty room too quickly. Then she opened the window, winced at the shriek of old hinges, and climbed out onto the ledge.

The snow was falling upward here. She still hadn't gotten used to that. Flakes drifting past her face toward the sky like ash from a fire that burned in reverse, and each one cold as any snow she'd ever known. She pulled herself along the ledge to the drainpipe, tested it with her weight, and — remembering, absurdly, her mistress's voice saying *a witch who can't climb down her own chimney deserves the fall* — went down hand over hand until her boots hit the courtyard snow.

Then she ran.

* * *

Vessia at night was a country holding its breath.

The streets were cobbled and silvered with frost, and the lamps burned with a light that didn't flicker, because flickering, Juniper was learning, was a kind of change, and change cost this place something it could barely afford. The citizens she passed moved like memories of people — a woman shaking out a rug that shed frost instead of dust, a man rehanging a sign that read THE GILDED HARE in letters worn nearly smooth. They nodded to her. That was the worst part. They nodded like she belonged, because in their half-remembering way, everything belonged, nothing surprised anyone anymore.

She walked fast. Not running — running drew eyes — but walking with the particular urgency of a woman late for something important, which was true, though the thing she was late for was her own life.

She'd been in the Bastion long enough to learn the city's bones. The market district lay south, past the clock tower whose hands hadn't moved in sixty years, and Maren Quill's shop — if the gossip of bored guards was worth anything, and it usually was — stood somewhere near the old canal. She had a plan. It was a bad plan. It consisted mostly of *find the cartographer, ask questions, don't get arrested*, but she'd noticed something since arriving in Vessia: bad plans made by free people beat good plans made by prisoners, every single time.

The clock tower boomed midnight as she passed beneath it. Sixty years unmoving, and it still struck the hour, faithfully wrong.

And then the street opened up, and there it was: the Frozen Market.

She'd heard the guards mention it and assumed it was a figure of speech. It was not. It was a square the size of a village green, ringed with stalls under sagging awnings furred with ice, and it was — impossibly, gloriously — *open*. Lanterns glowed amber behind frosted glass. Figures moved among the stalls, bundled in coats of every era, haggling in voices gone soft with disuse.

And the stalls sold summers.

She stopped without meaning to. Right there in the middle of the square, breath steaming, staring at a vendor's table where rows of bottles caught the lanternlight — and inside each bottle was a season. Not a picture of one. A *season*. Golden wheat moving in wind that existed only behind glass. A jar of bee-loud July, honey-colored, humming faintly against its cork. A long blue bottle of a lake in August, waves lapping at the glass from inside, patient as a dog waiting to be let out.

"You'll want the small ones first," said the vendor, an old woman with cheeks like winter apples and eyes that had seen too much to be startled by anything. "First-timers always reach for the big summer. Big summer's greedy. Swallows you whole, and you come out the other side thinking you've only blinked."

"What are they?" Juniper breathed.

"Bottled days. From before." The woman corked a bottle of September with practiced hands. "When the Oath sang clean, the land gave us more than we needed. Good harvests, long light, warm rain. We saved the extra." She shrugged, a small sad motion. "Now we sell them back to ourselves. One hour of June for three hours' work. Keeps the people warm. Keeps them *remembering* warm."

Juniper picked up the smallest bottle — a thimble of a thing, holding what looked like a single afternoon: cut grass, a clothesline, a dog asleep in a patch of sun. When she uncorked it, just a crack, warmth poured over her knuckles like water. Real warmth. The smell of mown grass rose up out of nowhere, and for one dizzy second she was eight years old in the Hollow, barefoot, guilty of nothing, wanting nothing.

Her eyes stung. She corked it fast.

"How much?"

"For you?" The old woman looked at her — really looked, the way the valley hum used to look at strangers, weighing something invisible. "Nothing. Take it. You'll need warm pockets where you're going."

"I'm not going anywhere."

"Child." The vendor smiled with enormous gentleness. "You've been running since you walked in."

Juniper bought the little bottle anyway — paid with a coin Wren had left on her supper tray, which felt appropriately like theft — and pressed it deep into her pocket. Then she pulled her collar up and crossed the market, past a stall selling bottled thunderstorms, past a man offering tastes of October off a wooden spoon, past a child turning a bottle of first-snow upside down and watching it snow *downward*, the old way, giggling.

At the far edge of the square, a crooked sign hung over a narrow door: **MAREN QUILL — MAPS OF PLACES THAT ARE NO MORE.**

Of course. Of course the market sold everything except a way out, and the way out was hiding behind it.

She had her hand on the door when the voice came from behind her.

"You walk like a guilty conscience, you know that?"

She didn't turn around. Her heart was doing something acrobatic and unhelpful against her ribs. "Go away, Wren."

"You jammed a blanket in a window latch. On the *third floor*. My grandmother's trick, actually, but never mind." His boots crunched closer across the frost. "Do you know what happens to me when the council learns their key climbed out a window? Because I do. I've had time to think about it. It involves the word 'drawn,' and 'quartered,' and a great deal of committee discussion."

"Then lie to them."

"Lying to the council is a profession, not a hobby. It takes decades." He came around into her line of sight, and up close, in the lantern-spill from the market, he looked less like an arresting officer and more like what he probably was underneath: a tired young man in a coat built for someone broader. "Come back. Sleep. Answer in the morning. That's all I'm asking."

"And if my answer is 'neither'?"

"Then we find a third reading together. There are archives. There are—"

"There's *morning bell*," Juniper said. "That's what there is. Morning bell and seven ghosts and a book that wrote my name before my grandmother was born, and everyone standing around waiting for me to be sensible about it." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it. "I didn't ask to be sensible about this. I asked to exist."

For a long moment Wren said nothing. Snow drifted upward between them, past the crooked sign, past the door marked NO MORE.

"You could arrest me," Juniper said quietly. "Right now. Hand on my arm, back to the Bastion, done. Nobody would blame you. It's your job."

"Yes," he said. "It is."

He looked at her hand on the door. He looked at her face. And Juniper watched — actually watched, the way you watch a wall crack along a seam — something give way behind his eyes. Duty, maybe. Or the habit of duty, which looks identical right up until it doesn't.

"Maren Quill will charge you," he said at last. "Whatever she asks, it'll sound small and cost enormous. She deals in names. If she offers to help you for a price that seems reasonable, run."

"Is that professional advice? From an oath-keeper? To the prisoner he's supposed to be recapturing?"

"It's advice from someone whose grandmother jammed blankets in windows." He stepped back. One step. Two. Out of arm's reach, which was a specific distance, chosen deliberately, and they both knew it. "The canal district turns east after the tannery row. If someone were following you — hypothetically — they'd lose the trail there. The frost doesn't hold prints past the tanneries. Too much salt in the ground."

Juniper stared at him. "You're letting me go."

"I'm letting you go *one street further than I should*," Wren corrected. "There's a difference. Legally, morally, and in terms of what I'll tell the council at dawn, which is that I pursued you through the Frozen Market and lost you among the vendors, who are notoriously slippery." He paused. "Also I'm coming with you."

"You're *what*?"

"Not inside." He nodded at the crooked door. "Maren Quill knows my face. Everyone official knows my face, that's rather the occupational hazard. I'll wait at the corner of the tannery row. If you're not out in an hour, I'll assume she's eaten you, and I'll go home and file a report saying I never found you, and feel terrible about it forever." He said it lightly, but his jaw was set the way stone sets — something that had decided, and would now take a very long time to un-decide. "One hour, hedge-witch."

"I'm not a hedge-witch. I'm a shop girl."

"You're a shop girl the prophecy calls a key, running through a frozen market at midnight with bottled summer in her pocket." Something that was almost a smile touched his mouth and retreated. "In Vessia, that makes you practically nobility."

She pushed open the crooked door.

Inside, it was warm — the first genuinely warm room she'd entered since leaving the Hollow — and it smelled of ink and old paper and, strangely, of oranges. Maps covered every surface: countries she recognized, countries she didn't, and countries rendered in careful gray lines with a single word beneath each, in beautiful script: *gone.*

A woman sat at a desk in the middle of it all, small and sharp-eyed, spectacles pushed up into gray hair, drawing a coastline that seemed to shift slightly whenever Juniper wasn't looking directly at it.

"Well," said Maren Quill, not looking up. "The hedge-born key. Right on schedule. Sit down, sit down — and close the door. The cold gets in, and lately, so does everything else."

Juniper glanced back through the doorway. At the corner of the tannery row, barely visible in the lantern-dark, a tall figure stood with his hands clasped behind his back, facing ostentatiously away, radiating the studied innocence of a man committing treason by standing still.

An hour, he'd said.

Juniper closed the door, and began, "How much for a map to the Door of Return?"

Maren Quill finally looked up, and her smile was kind and terrible all at once.

"Oh, my dear," she said. "For that one, I don't want coin."

Behind the glass of the little bottle in Juniper's pocket, the dog in the sun went on sleeping, and somewhere beyond the Salt Meridian, the Broken Oath changed key.
