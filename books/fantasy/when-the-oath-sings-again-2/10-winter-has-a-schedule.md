## 10. Winter Has a Schedule

The Bastion had a way of making even courage feel like paperwork.

Wren stood at attention outside the Council Chamber of Ashes while the herald inside announced him — *Oath-Keeper Wren Aldous Vane, Fourth Circle, bearer of the Winter Watch* — and tried to remember when the titles had started sounding like someone reading his name off a ledger instead of out loud.

The doors opened. Cold light spilled out, the color of old ice, and Wren went in because that was what he did. He went where he was sent. It was the one thing about himself he had never once doubted, and lately it was the only thing.

The council sat in a crescent of high-backed chairs, seven figures draped in grey wool and ceremonial frost-sigils, their faces lit from below by the singing-stones set into the table. The stones hummed faintly — the Broken Oath, always the Oath, threading through everything in this country like blood through cloth. Wren had grown up hearing it the way other children grew up hearing wind. He had stopped truly listening years ago, which he suspected was its own kind of wound.

At the center of the crescent sat Chancellor Ilsa Marrowe, oldest of them, her hands folded atop a ledger bound in sealskin. Behind her, half in shadow, stood a figure Wren did not know: tall, silver-collared, still as furniture. One of the Inquisition's people, then. They rarely came up from the deep vaults unless something had gone wrong enough to interest them.

"Warden Vane." Chancellor Marrowe's voice was dry as chalk. "You lost your prisoner."

"I escorted her through the Frozen Market," Wren said. "She slipped me in the lantern-row. There were two hundred people between us and—"

"You lost your prisoner," Marrowe repeated, with the patience of a woman who had decided long ago which facts she preferred and saw no reason to revisit them. "The hedge-born key. The subject named in the Ledger of 1893. The single individual in a century upon whom the survival of this nation may hinge. You lost her among *turnip stalls*."

"There were also pickle vendors," Wren said. "It was a very well-defended pickle stall."

Someone down the crescent made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been a laugh strangled at birth. The silver-collared figure behind Marrowe did not move.

Marrowe let the silence do its work. She was good at that. Then she opened the sealskin ledger and turned it toward him, and Wren felt the temperature of the room change before he understood why.

"The key escaped with information," Marrowe said. "She has gone looking for the Door of Return. And last night, Warden, the Oath changed."

Wren went still. Everyone in Vessia knew the song was dying — note by note, verse by verse, like a fire going out in an untended hearth. You could chart it on the walls of the Bastion itself, where the grief-song sang through the singing-stones a little thinner every winter. It was the quietest apocalypse imaginable. Nobody screamed about it. They simply got colder, and forgot things, and smiled more, and one day there would be nothing left to forget.

"The Oath does not change," Wren said. "It only diminishes."

"It changed," Marrowe said. "Listen."

She pressed her palm flat against the largest singing-stone, and the chamber filled with sound.

Wren knew the Oath the way you know a lullaby from childhood — by shape, not by thought. What rose from the stone now was the same melody, worn and threadbare, but threaded through it was something new. A second voice. Thin, untrained, unmistakably human, and — this was the part that stopped his breath — unmistakably answering. Not echoing. Answering. Call and response, like two voices across a valley, like a conversation begun a hundred years too late.

He knew whose voice it was. Of course he did. He had spent three days interrogating her and one afternoon chasing her through a market, and he had heard her laugh exactly once, at a vendor selling bottled summers, when she'd held a jar of July up to the light and said, *oh, that's cheating.* The voice in the stone was not laughing. But it had the same warmth underneath it, the same stubbornness, the same un-Vessian aliveness.

"She is singing back to it," Marrowe said. "Without training. Without magic. Without, apparently, any understanding of what she is doing." She closed her hand over the stone and the sound died, and Wren realized his own hands had curled into fists at his sides. "The prophecy named her the hedge-born key. We now have confirmation that the naming was accurate. The question before this council is no longer *whether* the key can reforge the Oath. It is whether we can afford to wait for her consent."

The chamber went very quiet.

"We are speaking," Wren said carefully, "of forcing her."

"We are speaking," said Chancellor Corvess from the far end of the crescent, a narrow man with frost-burned knuckles, "of arithmetic. The Oath loses a verse every eleven days now. Eleven, Warden. Last year it was fourteen. At the current rate, Vessia has four hundred days of song remaining. Perhaps fewer. After that—" He gestured, vaguely, at everything: the walls, the city, the snow falling upward beyond the high windows, the citizens below moving through their streets like beautiful half-remembered dreams. "After that there is no country to save and no one left to grieve it."

"So we bind her," Wren said. "We take a woman from another world, a woman who has been here nineteen days, and we chain her to our oath forever, without her will, because our sums frighten us."

"Yes," said Corvess, simply, without cruelty or apology, and somehow that was worse than if he'd shouted it.

Wren looked around the crescent. Seven faces. He had sworn his oath to these people's predecessors at fourteen years old, kneeling on frozen ground with his family nine months dead, and he had believed — he still believed, most days — that the oath meant something clean. *Keep the watch. Hold the line. Save what can be saved.*

Nobody had told him, at fourteen, that *save what can be saved* would eventually mean *spend someone else.*

"The prophecy offers two readings," he said. "You know it does. The Door of Return ends Vessia mercifully. Reforging binds the key to the land forever. Both require her. Neither requires her *consent*, according to the text — but the text was written by hedge-witches, not by this council, and hedge-witches have a long tradition of writing choices into prophecies that men like us pretend aren't there."

"The text also says," Marrowe replied, "that a willing reforging holds stronger than a forced one. Which is why the key must first be found, and persuaded, and — if persuasion fails — compelled. Willingness can be manufactured, Warden. Isolation. Fear. Gratitude, even. A person with nowhere else to belong will agree to almost anything eventually." She said it the way a carpenter says *the wood must be seasoned*. "But none of it matters until she is found. Which brings us to the matter of the recapture."

Here Marrowe paused, and Wren watched her decide something — watched it move across her face like weather.

"The Inquisition has offered to handle it," she said.

The silver-collared figure stepped forward into the light, and Wren finally saw the face beneath the hood: smooth, ageless, courteous, the face of someone who has practiced looking kind. "Inquisitor Sable," the figure said, with a small bow. "The Order thanks the council for its confidence. Locating an oath-resonant individual is, after all, our particular craft. Give us three days."

Something cold walked down Wren's spine. He had heard stories about how the Inquisition located people — stories whispered by Fourth Circle veterans who drank too much afterward. They fed oaths to their instruments. They listened for the echo. And what they did to the people they found, once found, was not spoken of in front of the young.

"No," Wren said.

Every head turned.

He hadn't planned to say it. It had come out the way a splinter comes out — all at once, and bleeding a little. He stood in the center of the Council of Ashes with his own voice ringing in his ears and understood that he had just arrived at some kind of border, the kind you can't step back over.

"Explain yourself, Warden," Marrowe said quietly.

"The key trusts no one," Wren said. "She ran from me, and I arrested her, and she *still* argued with me for three days like we were old friends having a dispute. That is a woman who argues with the thing that frightens her instead of freezing. If your Inquisitor hunts her, she'll run harder, and she's already found help — the cartographer, Quill. Quill knows the memory-roads. Your people don't. I do. I've walked them since I was sixteen." He paused, and heard himself add, with terrible honesty: "And she'll come easier for me than for anyone else in this room. Possibly because I've already failed her once. People forgive failures. They don't forgive instruments."

Inquisitor Sable regarded him with mild, bottomless eyes. "The Warden speaks feelingly," Sable observed, to the council rather than to him. "One wonders whether his feelings are an asset or a liability."

"They're an asset," Wren said, "because they're honest, and she can smell a lie at forty paces. She told me so herself. She grew up in a valley where magic can't follow, Inquisitor. Everything there is exactly what it is. She's spent her whole life learning the difference."

Silence again. The singing-stones hummed their thinning song. Somewhere above, the upward snow ticked softly against the high windows, the way it had for a century, patient as guilt.

Chancellor Marrowe studied Wren for a long moment, and he had the uncomfortable sense of being read like a ledger — columns audited, discrepancies noted.

"You requested pursuit assignments twice before," she said at last. "The deserter at Grey Ford. The oath-thief in the Lantern Quarter. Both times you brought your quarry back alive, unbound, and cooperative. The council noted it. Some called it softness." Her mouth did something that was technically not a smile. "I am prepared to call it competence. Very well, Warden. The assignment is yours. Recapture the key. Deliver her to the Bastion within twenty days — sooner, if the verses continue to fail. And Warden—"

"Chancellor."

"If you cannot deliver her willing," Marrowe said, "deliver her. The schedule does not care about our consciences. Winter keeps accounts of its own."

* * *

He left through the Bastion's north postern, because the front gates meant salutes and questions and the particular loneliness of being congratulated, and he wasn't sure he'd survive being congratulated.

The stable-yard was empty except for old Tammas mucking stalls and the grey mare, Bracken, who stuck her nose over the door and breathed at him with the frank judgment of all horses.

"Don't look at me like that," Wren said, saddling her. "I know what I agreed to."

Bracken continued to look at him like that.

The truth was he didn't fully know. That was the trouble. He kept turning the council's arithmetic over in his head — four hundred days, eleven per verse, spend the girl — and every time he reached the part where they chained her to the land, something in his chest recoiled hard enough to hurt. And yet when he imagined the alternative — the Door opening, Vessia folding up like a letter, the song ending, everyone he had ever sworn to protect becoming a story told in some other country about somewhere that used to be — that recoiled too.

Both futures required her. That was the trap the prophecy had built. And he had just volunteered to be the hand that sprang it.

"Could've let the Inquisitor go," Tammas offered from the next stall, not looking up from his pitchfork. Old man missed nothing.

"And if he caught her?"

Tammas considered. "Then she'd be found," he said, which in the Bastion's dialect meant everything the word needed to mean and nothing comforting.

Wren cinched the girth strap. His hands were steady, which annoyed him. Hands should shake when a man signed on for this sort of work, even silently, even retroactively.

There was a knock of hooves on cobbles, and a runner came into the yard — a First Circle boy, breathless, holding out a folded paper. "For Warden Vane. From the wall-watch. The eastern singing-wall, sir. It — it happened again last night."

Wren took the paper. The watch-clerk's handwriting was neat and increasingly frantic:

*Third occurrence. The eastern wall answered the night-wind in a voice not the Oath's. Two wardens report hearing words. One reports the wall sang a lullaby "in the manner of a mother." Request guidance. Also request someone tell us what is happening, as the wall has never done this in living memory and Corporal Hess refuses to stand near it anymore.*

Wren read it twice. A lullaby. She had sung to the wall, and the wall — grieving, dying, a hundred years past comfort — had sung back the way walls apparently remembered mothers singing.

He folded the paper slowly.

Somewhere east of the city, past the Frozen Market and the shrine-road, Juniper Hartwell was walking with Maren Quill along roads made of memory, toward a door that wanted her dead-or-bound, pursued by an order of mages who ate promises. And she had paused, somewhere in all of that, to sing a lullaby to a wall.

*Come easier for me than for anyone else,* he had told the council.

He swung up onto Bracken's back and turned her east, toward the memory-roads, and told himself — in the firm, procedural voice he used for oaths — that he was doing his duty. That the assignment was the assignment. That loyalty was loyalty, and conscience was a luxury the schedule did not carry, and when he caught up with her he would say whatever needed saying to bring her home to the Bastion alive and unbound, and deal with the rest of the arithmetic after.

Bracken picked up a trot. The upward snow drifted past, glittering, going nowhere.

Ahead, far off across the white fields, the memory-roads shimmered faintly gold — roads remembering better days — and Wren spurred toward them, carrying orders he already dreaded keeping, hoping with a traitor's hope that the woman he was sent to catch would be fast enough to make him earn it.
