## 12. Caught Between Snowfalls

The shrine at Vell's Crossing had been built, according to the plaque above its door, to honor a saint who had once turned back a flood with nothing but a prayer and an unreasonable amount of stubbornness. The flood was gone. The prayer was gone. The stubbornness, in Wren's experience, was the only part that had survived into the present age, and it was currently walking around wearing Juniper Hartwell's face.

He tracked them for two days.

That was not difficult. Maren Quill left tracks like a confession — every road they took, she walked down the middle of it as if daring the landscape to remember her. What was difficult was deciding what he would do when he caught up. He had rehearsed the arrest eleven times on the second night, standing watch while they slept, his hand resting on his oath-cord the way it always did when his thoughts got loud.

Arrest the key. Return her to the Bastion. Save Vessia.

Eleven rehearsals, and every single one of them ended with him saying *you're under the protection of the Oath* in a voice that didn't believe itself, and then standing there in the snow like a man who had forgotten which door was his.

*You let her go one street further than you should have,* Captain Sera had told him, when he'd reported the escape with the careful omissions of a liar who respected the truth too much to tell a bad one. She'd looked at him a long moment — Sera missed nothing, she'd once identified a smuggler by the way he saluted — and then said only, *Bring her home, Wren.* He had nodded. He had meant it, in whatever way a man can mean something when he has already begun to suspect he means something else entirely.

Now, on the third morning, the snow fell upward past the shrine's crooked eaves, and Wren stood in the tree line with frost on his eyelashes and watched the two most wanted people in Vessia argue about breakfast.

"—all I'm saying is that a shrine dedicated to a saint ought to have *food* in it," Maren was saying. "That's basic theology."

"It's basic *burglary,*" Juniper said. She was crouched at the little offering alcove, turning over a clay bowl that contained, as far as Wren could see, nothing but a candle stub and three buttons. "These are offerings. You don't eat someone's offerings."

"You don't eat a living person's offerings. This saint is dead, dear, and so is everyone who left these. That's not theft, that's inheritance."

"That is *not* how inheritance works—"

Wren stepped out of the trees.

He hadn't decided to. His legs simply concluded that three days of rehearsal had produced no better plan than *walk forward and see what your face does*, and his face, as it turned out, did nothing at all, which was the problem with it.

Juniper saw him first. She went very still — the stillness of prey, or of someone doing rapid arithmetic — and then, absurdly, she straightened up and held the offering bowl slightly behind her back, as though the buttons were the crime.

"Oath-keeper," Maren said pleasantly, without turning around. She kept slicing something off a heel of black bread. "You walk loudly for a man trained in silence. I heard you before dawn. I let you get close because I wanted to see what you'd do." She glanced over her shoulder, eyes bright and merciless. "And here you are. Doing it. Whatever it is."

"Stand away from the hedge-born," Wren said, and his voice came out exactly as rehearsed, flat and official, and he hated it instantly.

Juniper's chin lifted. He'd learned that lift already, in the Bastion, across an interrogation table: it was the gesture she made when she was about to be stubborn about something, which was most things. "I'm not going back."

"I know."

The word escaped before he could catch it. It hung there in the cold between them, small and enormous.

Maren stopped slicing.

Juniper blinked. "You... know?"

"The Bastion voted." Wren made himself say it plainly, because if he was going to commit treason, he was at least going to do it without stammering. "Two nights ago. The council ruled that the key's consent is irrelevant to the reforging. They've sent for the Inquisitory."

Maren set down the knife. Very carefully. As though it might otherwise do something rash. "The inquisitors," she repeated. "They're sending oath-eaters after a girl from a magicless valley."

"They're sending oath-eaters after the only person who can end the winter," Wren said. "From their perspective—"

"Oh, I understand their perspective perfectly. Their perspective is a leech wearing a collar." Maren wiped her hands on her coat. "Girl, we're leaving. Now. There are old roads under this hill; I've mapped them."

"Wait." Juniper wasn't looking at Maren. She was looking at Wren, and her expression had shifted from wariness into something worse: attention. Full, undivided, cataloguing attention. "You came all this way. Alone. To tell us that."

"To retrieve you."

"You said *I know* when I said I wouldn't go back."

"A moment of imprecision."

"You stood in the trees for—" she squinted at the light, at the disturbed snow along the tree line where he'd paced his watch, "—hours. An arrest doesn't take hours. An arrest takes thirty seconds and bad manners."

"She's right," Maren said helpfully. "I've been arrested by better men. Well. Faster men."

"Maren." 

"Sorry. Continue confessing."

Wren looked at Juniper Hartwell, warm-faced and impossible, holding a bowl of buttons like a shield, and felt the last plank of the bridge go out from under him. There was a version of this where he drew the cord, spoke the words of custody, and dragged her back through the snow to be bound to a dying country whether she willed it or no. He had sworn that version at fourteen years old, kneeling in a chapel with his family four months buried, swearing on the singing stones that Vessia would not be allowed to die while he drew breath. The oath lived in him still. He could feel it under his ribs, patient and cold as the winter it served.

But he could also feel something else, and it had started, embarrassingly, with her laugh — the first laugh he'd heard at their small fire, startled out of her by one of Maren's terrible jokes, a real laugh with a hiccup in the middle of it, and he had thought: *that is what we're freezing to death to protect.* And then, more quietly, in the voice oaths hate: *is it? Is that what we're protecting? Or are we protecting the oath itself, the way a man guards a wound because touching it would mean admitting it hurts?*

"I requested this assignment," he said.

Juniper's eyebrows went up.

"When the council ordered your recapture, I asked to lead it. So that I would be the one who found you first." He exhaled, and the breath steamed white and hung between them like a small surrender. "So that whatever I decided, it would be my decision. Not theirs."

"And what did you decide?" Maren asked softly.

He reached to his belt, unhooked his oath-cord — the braided cord every keeper wore, the symbol of custody, of office, of the vow — and for one horrible moment his hand simply stopped working, because he had worn it since he was fourteen and his fingers did not know how to exist without it.

Then he laid it on the shrine's altar stone, next to the candle stub and the three buttons, and stepped back.

The silence rang.

"Treason," Maren said, with the satisfaction of a woman confirming a receipt. "Quiet treason, but treason. I've committed enough to know the flavor."

"It's not treason," Wren said. "Vessia isn't—"

"Don't finish that sentence. It's treason precisely because Vessia *is*, and you just chose her over the men claiming to speak for her. That's the good kind. The expensive kind." Maren slung her pack over her shoulder. "Well? We're burning daylight, and by daylight I mean the bruised purple approximation thereof. Inquisitors travel fast when they're fed."

"Fed?" Juniper asked.

"On oaths," Wren said quietly, and watched her face change. Right — she knew. She'd spent a night in the Bastion cells listening to the Broken Oath sing through the walls; she of all people understood what an oath-eater would make of a country's last living song. "They'll find the trail I left. I made it honest for two days. Then I made it dishonest. But the Inquisitory doesn't track the way keepers do. They listen for the shape of a choice, and I have just..." He gestured at the altar, at the cord lying there among the buttons. "...made a very loud one."

Juniper picked up the offering bowl and set the three buttons deliberately beside his oath-cord. "For the saint," she said. "Stubbornness. Seems appropriate."

Something in Wren's chest cracked — painlessly, the way ice cracks off a branch in thaw, revealing green underneath. He almost smiled. He suspected that if he started he might not stop, and a man committing treason ought to at least look serious about it.

"We should move," he managed.

"We should *eat* first," Maren said. "Treason on an empty stomach leads to rash decisions. Trust me. I once defected over a bad turnip."

* * *

They walked the rest of the day along roads that remembered being grander. Wren had traveled this route as a boy, when the milestones were whole and the hedgerows bloomed out of season because the land had been loved and love, in Vessia, leaked into everything. Now the milestones wore their numbers like faded tattoos and the hedges were bare white thorn, but the roads themselves still curved with old confidence, bending around farms that were no longer there, pausing at gates that led to empty air.

Juniper walked beside him. Not ahead, not behind. Beside. He noticed it the way you notice a fire in a cold room.

"You could still go back," she said, after a mile of comfortable silence. "Pick up your cord. Say you found nothing. Sera likes you — she'd cover for you. I'd want you to, honestly. You have a whole life back there. A purpose. I'm just the person the book pointed at."

"Do you regret it?" he asked. "Being pointed at?"

She considered this with the seriousness she gave everything, which was either exhausting or wonderful depending on the hour. "I regret that nobody asked me. Everyone keeps reading the prophecy *at* me. Door or anchor, martyr or key, as if I'm a hinge and not a person." A short, sharp breath that steamed the air. "But regret the pointing itself? No. My whole life I wanted elsewhere so badly I felt guilty about it. Turns out elsewhere needed me. There's something almost funny in that."

"There is," Wren agreed. "It's very Vessian humor. Tragedy with the timing of a joke."

"Your country sounds exhausting."

"It's why we invented so many festivals."

Ahead, Maren snorted. "Festivals. I attended the Festival of First Thaw in the capital, year of the Long Frost. It was canceled. For frost. They held a ceremony about the cancellation. Three hours."

"That was a difficult year," Wren said, with dignity.

"They gave a speech about resilience and then everyone went home and froze quietly."

Despite himself — despite treason and pursuit and the fact that his entire future now fit inside a shrine offering — Wren laughed. It came out rusty, half-frozen, but it came out, and he felt Juniper glance at him with an expression he pretended not to see, mostly because seeing it made his ears warm in a way the cold couldn't explain.

*Pragmatism,* he told himself. *You joined them because the Bastion's plan was wrong and you were best positioned to correct it from inside.* The justification sat comfortably in his mouth. It tasted, faintly, of a lie. He swallowed it anyway. Treason, he was learning, begins quietly — disguised as duty, dressed as caution, walking beside you on a road that remembers better days, until one morning you realize the quiet thing has grown teeth and a name and warm brown eyes that notice everything.

* * *

They sheltered that night in the shrine's root cellar, which Maren declared structurally superior to the building ("the roof leaks; the earth does not") and which smelled of old apples and older prayers. Wren took first watch at the cellar door out of pure habit, and was surprised when Juniper climbed the steps and sat beside him rather than sleeping.

"You don't have to guard me," she said. "Anymore. That's over, right? The guarding."

"It's not over. It's just changed employers."

She laughed — the hiccup-laugh, muffled in her sleeve against the cold — and then grew quiet. Above them, through the frozen ground, he could hear it faintly: the song. The Broken Oath, threading through the stones of the world, thinner here than in the Bastion, frayed at the edges like a hem coming undone. Her head tilted toward it the way it always did, helpless, attentive, a compass needle finding north.

"It's slower tonight," she murmured. "Or sadder. Can you tell the difference?"

"No," Wren admitted. "I never could. That was always the problem with us keepers. We memorized the words and forgot to ask what they meant."

"What do they mean? The oath. The original one."

He was silent a long time. Snow drifted upward past the crack of the cellar door, each flake rising like something returning home against its will.

"My mother used to sing a verse of it," he said finally. "When I was small. Before." The word *before* carried the winter that took his family; Juniper, who had listened to grief sung through prison walls, heard all of it. "*By root and roof, by bread and bell, I hold this land as it holds me.*" His voice roughened on it. "It wasn't a king's promise originally. Did you know that? The oldest versions say *we*. By root and roof, by bread and bell, *we* hold this land. Somewhere in a hundred generations of copying, the we became an I. One king instead of everyone. And when he failed, everything failed with him, because we'd handed him the whole song."

Juniper was looking at him with that cataloguing attention again, and this time he let himself be seen.

"Wren," she said. "That sounds like a verse that could be fixed."

"Careful," he said, and his mouth curved despite everything. "Talk like that gets a man exiled."

"You're already exiled."

"Yes. Conveniently."

Below them, Maren's voice floated up the stairs, drowsy and dry as paper: "If the two of you are going to draft constitutional reform over my sleeping body, the least you can do is keep your voices down. Some of us need our strength for outrunning leeches."

They lapsed into silence, but it was a different silence than before — shared, settled, the silence of people on the same side of a wall. Wren found his hand had come to rest near hers on the cold step, not touching, just near, the way a fire sits near a room and changes everything without apology.

It was nearly midnight when he heard it.

Far off — miles, perhaps more, distance meant nothing to it — a bell rang once. Not a village bell. Not a chapel bell. A deep, wrong note, like a struck tooth, and beneath it Wren felt the song flinch, all across the dark country, a shiver through the stones.

Juniper's head came up. "What was that?"

Wren was already on his feet, every rehearsed instinct returning, repurposed. "Inquisitor bell. They ring it when they've caught the scent of a broken vow." He looked down at his own wrist, at the pale band of skin where the cord had lain for twenty years. "They've caught mine."
