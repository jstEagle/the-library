## 13. Three at a Small Fire

The fire was Maren's doing, which surprised Juniper more than anything else that had happened to her since the world folded up and spat her out into Vessia.

She had expected the cartographer to be all business — and mostly she was, all sharp edges and ledger-voice, counting miles like coins and spending them reluctantly. But when the light began to fail and the memory-road dipped into a hollow ringed with frost-bitten pines, Maren simply said, "Here. We stop," dropped her pack, and within twenty minutes had coaxed a fire out of deadfall and what she claimed was "a trick involving patience and spite." The flames burned an odd color, blue at their hearts, gold at their edges, and gave off warmth that felt almost personal, like being wrapped in a blanket by someone who liked you.

"Vessian firewood remembers being warm," Maren said, catching Juniper's look. "Everything here remembers something. That's rather the whole problem."

Juniper sat as close to the flames as she dared and let the heat soak through her coat. She hadn't realized how cold she'd been until now — how long the cold had lasted, how it had settled into her bones somewhere back at the shrine and never quite left. Above them, through a ragged tear in the clouds, the stars were coming out.

They were wrong stars.

Not wrong exactly — they were stars, they glittered, they wheeled in slow constellations — but they weren't *hers*. There was no North Star where the North Star should be, or if there was, it was wearing someone else's clothes. The shapes overhead were strangers: great sprawled figures and long curved lines she couldn't name, arranged with the confident geometry of a sky that had never heard of the Hollow.

"You're staring," Wren said.

He sat across the fire from her, on the other side of Maren, his back against a pine trunk with his sword across his knees. He'd taken off his oath-keeper's gauntlets, and Juniper kept catching herself looking at his bare hands — pale, scarred at the knuckles, human in a way that seemed faintly scandalous after days of watching him be nothing but duty and wool and steel.

"I'm allowed to stare at things here," she said. "I'm a tourist."

"You're a fugitive."

"Then I'm a fugitive with excellent taste in skies." She tipped her head back. "What are those called? The ones that look like — I don't know. Like a fishhook?"

Wren followed her gaze upward. Something shifted in his face — not softening, exactly. Unbracing. Like a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying because he'd carried it so long it had grown into his shoulders.

"The Fishhook is the Hook of Sorrows," he said. "It hangs over the winter months. Fishermen swore by it, before." He paused. "Before means before the Oath broke. Everything has a 'before' here. It's the most-used word in the language."

"And the bright one next to it? The one that doesn't flicker even when the clouds go over?"

"The Anchor-Stone. Sailors believed it held the sky down so the wind couldn't steal it." He said it plainly, without irony, and Juniper found herself charmed despite everything — despite the arrest, the interrogation, the escape through the frozen market, the fact that he'd caught her and then, impossibly, chosen to run with her instead of dragging her back to the Bastion. Treason disguised as pragmatism, he'd called it. She suspected there was more under it than pragmatism, but she'd learned not to poke at Wren's under-things. They were load-bearing.

"Show me more," she said.

Maren snorted from beside the fire. She was sharpening a mapping stylus against a whetstone, a thin grating sound she'd been making for an hour purely, Juniper suspected, to keep everyone slightly on edge. "Oh good. Astronomy lessons by firelight. Shall I fetch my lute? Oh wait — I sold it for bread in the Year of the Grey Thaw, along with my dignity and two fingers' worth of circulation."

"You don't have to stay," Wren said mildly.

"I absolutely do. If you two are left alone unsupervised you'll either argue or do something worse, and both outcomes will slow us down tomorrow." The whetstone rasped. "Besides, I know these stars better than he does. He learned them from drill manuals. I learned them drunk, from a smuggler in Corrow Bay, which is the traditional method."

"Is that true?" Juniper asked Wren.

"Her version is more interesting," Wren admitted. "Mine's probably more accurate."

"They usually cancel out." Maren pointed her stylus at a cluster of dim stars low on the eastern horizon. "That one's the Loom. My smuggler said the goddess Vessa wove the world on it during the Long Night, thread by thread, and when she got tired she'd leave gaps, and the gaps are why nothing in this country works properly."

"That's not—" Wren began.

"It's a *myth*, Keeper. Myths aren't supposed to survive fact-checking." Maren went back to her stylus. "The official version is that the Loom marks where the first oath-keepers took their vows, and the three brightest threads are the virtues: Fidelity, Endurance, and—"

"Grief," Juniper said quietly.

Both of them looked at her.

"I heard it," she said, before they could ask. "In the Bastion walls. The Broken Oath. It sings, did you know that? Not just noise — actual words, underneath. And one of the words keeps coming back. Over and over, like a stitch someone can't stop pulling." She hugged her knees. "*Grief.* It's woven right into your founding promise. You built mourning into the constitution and then wondered why the country couldn't stop crying."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, ice cracked in a way that sounded almost like language.

"That's the third reading of it," Wren said at last. His voice had gone careful, the way it did when he was handling something fragile and pretending it wasn't. "Most scholars say the third virtue is *Gratitude*."

"Your scholars have never listened to a wall sing."

"No," he said. "No, I suppose they haven't."

Maren's stylus had gone still. She was watching Juniper with an expression Juniper couldn't read — something between appraisal and apology. Then she shrugged and resumed sharpening. "The Loom it is. Now, the big crooked one near the top, the one shaped like a question nobody wants answered — that's the Spindle, and the story there is genuinely obscene, so ask the Keeper about it if you want him flustered."

"I don't get flustered," Wren said.

"He gets flustered," Maren told Juniper confidentially. "Once, in the Bastion archives, he blushed so hard at a marginal illustration in the *Codex of Vows* that the junior clerks thought he was having a heat stroke. In a building made of ice."

"That illustration was anatomically impossible," Wren said with dignity.

"*Especially* the parts that were impossible," Maren agreed.

And Juniper laughed.

It came out of her before she could stop it — a real laugh, sudden and undignified, startling her almost as much as it startled the others. It echoed off the frost-bitten trees and came back sounding younger than she felt. She pressed her hand over her mouth, but it was too late; the sound was loose in the world.

Maren grinned like a knife coming out of a sheath. "There it is. Four days in Vessia and she finally laughs. I was beginning to think the prophecy book had summoned a corpse with opinions."

"I'm sorry," Juniper said, still half-giggling, mortified and light-headed. "I don't know why — that wasn't even funny—"

"It was funny," said Wren.

"You laughed too." She stared at him. "You did. I saw it. Your mouth moved."

"My mouth twitched. There's a difference."

"There is *not* a difference—"

"Twitching is involuntary. Laughing is a choice. An oath-keeper learns to distinguish—"

"Oh, *listen* to him." Maren threw up her hands. "A man who has codified his own joy into subcategories. Juniper, I want it on record that when I found you, you were running from the law with a stolen map and no plan whatsoever, and I joined you anyway, because the alternative was dying alone with my maps, and I want it further on record that this — this, right here, the two of you bickering about whether smiling counts — is the most entertainment I've had since the fall of the southern trade routes, and I attended the fall of the southern trade routes. There was a banquet."

Juniper was laughing again. She couldn't help it. It kept escaping in bursts, and every time it did, she felt something inside her unclench a little more — some knot she'd been carrying since the ink moved in the ledger, since the hum cut out, since she fell out of her ordinary life into a country that was singing itself to death.

Because that was the thing she hadn't let herself think: she hadn't laughed once. Not in the Bastion cell, not on the road, not even at Maren's driest jokes. She'd been saving everything for survival — attention, energy, feeling itself — rationing herself like the last jar of preserves in a long winter.

And here was a small fire, and two people who should have been her enemies, arguing about whether a mouth-twitch counted as laughter, and the knot was loosening anyway.

Warmth as rebellion, she thought. Nobody had written *that* in the prophecy.

* * *

Later, when Maren had rolled herself into her bedroll with the stylus tucked into her belt like a dagger ("habit," she'd said darkly, and refused to elaborate), and the fire had settled down to its blue-hearted embers, Wren moved around the flames to sit nearer Juniper.

Not *too* near. A careful arm's length away, angled toward her but facing the dark, the way men sit when they've spent their lives on watch. He'd put his gauntlets back on, she noticed, and tried not to feel disappointed about it.

"You asked about the stars," he said. "I didn't finish."

"There's more?"

"There are forty named figures in the Vessian sky. I know maybe twelve properly. My mother knew all of them." He said it evenly, but Juniper heard the seam in it. "She taught me the winter ones first, because we had so much winter. The Hook of Sorrows, the Anchor-Stone, the Lantern-Bearer — that one there, the red-tinged star that rises late. She said the Lantern-Bearer walks ahead of lost travelers and doesn't look back, because if he looks back he'll see how far behind they've fallen and lose heart."

"Did you believe that?"

"When I was small? Completely." A pause. The firelight moved on his face. "After the winter took them, I believed it differently. I decided that if I walked fast enough, and didn't look back, I could be the lantern for other people. For the country. It seemed like a reasonable trade — give up looking back, gain a purpose." His jaw worked. "I was fourteen. Fourteen-year-olds make terrible bargains and then spend thirty years honoring them."

Juniper turned that over. "You never told me about your family."

"You never asked."

"You never gave me the impression questions were permitted."

He huffed — almost a laugh, definitely more than a twitch. "Fair. The interrogation protocols may have set a certain tone."

"A certain tone," she echoed. "You handcuffed me to a chair."

"The chair was standard issue. The handcuffs were a courtesy. Some inquisitors use chains."

"Some inquisitors," Juniper said, "feed on oaths. I've met them. The bar for courtesy is on the floor."

He was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped, gone private. "My family died in the second hard freeze. The one they call the Widowing. Eleven years ago now. My father went out to check the boundary-stones and didn't come home. My mother went out to find him. My sister—" He stopped. Started again, flatter. "The village buried what it found in the spring. There wasn't much spring that year. I stood at fourteen years old in front of the recruiting officer of the Bastion and swore the Oath of the Vigil, and the officer said, *the country will remember your sacrifice,* and I believed him. I believed remembering was something a country could actually do."

"And now?"

"Now I know better." He looked at her across the ember-light, and his eyes were the color of deep water over stone. "Now I know a country forgets its own name while its citizens wander around smiling. Now I know the council votes to bind a stranger against her will and calls it salvation. Now I know the Oath sings grief because maybe grief is all it was ever given to hold." He shook his head slowly. "I used to think loyalty was a wall. You stand on it. It holds. Simple. But walls are built on foundations, and I'm starting to wonder what ours was poured over."

Juniper hugged her knees and watched the embers breathe. "For what it's worth," she said, "in the valley where I come from, magic can't follow. There's a hum in the air that presses everything flat. Nothing changes. Ever. I used to stand at the edge of the Hollow and imagine somewhere else, and hate myself for imagining it, because everyone said the valley was safe, and safe was supposed to be enough." She glanced at him. "I don't think I ever thanked anyone for letting me want more. My mistress came closest. But wanting was always a guilty secret. So when you talk about bargains made at fourteen — I understand. Mine were quieter, but I signed them just the same."

"What was yours?"

"*Be content. Be small. Don't wish.*" She smiled, rueful. "Three clauses. No exit date."

The fire ticked. Above them the Hook of Sorrows hung steady, and beside it the Anchor-Stone held the sky down, faithful and unmoving, and Juniper thought: *someone named those stars out of love, centuries ago, and the love is still up there working, even now, even here.*

"Wren," she said. "Why did you really warn me at the shrine? The truth. Not the pragmatism."

He didn't answer immediately. He picked up a twig and fed it to the embers, watching it catch.

"In the market," he said finally. "When I caught you. You were standing in front of a stall selling bottled summers, holding one up to the light, and you said — do you remember what you said?"

Juniper searched back. The frozen market swam up in her memory: the shuttered stalls, the jars glowing like trapped wasps, her own breath hanging silver in the air. She'd picked up a bottle — a small one, ordinary — full of some green-gold shimmer that might have been a July afternoon.

"I said, *it looks like it misses being opened.*"

"Yes." Wren nodded slowly. "And I thought — four years I've walked past that stall. Four years I've seen those bottles. I never once thought of them as missing anything. I thought of them as inventory. Evidence of what we lost, catalogued and priced." He met her eyes. "But you looked at a bottle of summer and felt sorry for *it*. That's when I knew the Bastion was wrong about you. Whatever the prophecy says you are — key, instrument, door — the person holding the bottle was kind. And I found I couldn't hand kindness over to the inquisitors. Not even for Vessia." His mouth twisted. "Especially not for Vessia. Vessia's done worse with less."

Juniper's chest felt strange. Too full, and too warm, and the warmth had nothing to do with the fire.

"That's the nicest accusation anyone's ever leveled at me," she managed.

"Don't get comfortable. I'm still technically under orders to return you to the Bastion."

"And I'm still technically planning to find a Door of Return and possibly end your entire country."

"See? Mutual treason. Very stable foundation for an alliance."

"For an alliance," she agreed.

But the word sat oddly in her mouth. Alliance was cold. Alliance was supply lines and shared objectives and not asking why the other person's bare hands had haunted you for three days. What was happening across this small fire, in the blue-hearted dark of a dying country, felt like something with a different name — a name neither of them had written yet, a page still blank, waiting.

She was aware of him the way you become aware of a star only when you stop trying to navigate by it: suddenly, wholly, as a burning thing rather than a tool. The line of his shoulder. The ember-light catching the scar on his knuckles. The fact that he had, at some point in the conversation, drifted six inches closer without either of them noticing, and that neither of them moved away.

From the depths of her bedroll, Maren said, without opening her eyes: "If you two kiss, I'm billing you both for the disruption of my rest. My rates are steep. I accept payment in bottled summers."

"We're not going to kiss," Wren said.

"We're *allies*," Juniper said, at the same moment, too fast, too loud.

"Mm," said Maren. "That's what the southern trade delegation said. Right before the banquet."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere above them the Lantern-Bearer rose, red-tinged and patient, walking ahead of lost travelers without looking back.

Juniper lay down in her bedroll and pulled the blanket to her chin and stared up at the wrong, wonderful stars, and found that she was smiling, and that the smile didn't feel like a betrayal of anyone — not the Hollow, not the prophecy, not the frightened girl who'd fallen through a folding world four days ago. It felt, absurdly, like the most honest thing she'd done since arriving.

Warmth as rebellion, she thought again, and this time added: *then let me rebel thoroughly.*

Across the ashes, Wren was still awake. She could tell by his breathing — the watchman's rhythm, too controlled for sleep — and by the fact that when she closed her eyes, she could feel his attention on her like a hand held just above a flame, warming itself, not yet daring to touch.

She was nearly asleep when he spoke again, so softly she might have dreamed it.

"The twelfth star-figure," he murmured. "The one I never learned from my mother, because it rose only after she was gone. It's called the Hearthkeeper. It doesn't mark seasons or guide sailors. Its only job in the old stories is to keep a light burning in an empty house so that whoever comes home last — however late, however changed — can find the way in."

Juniper's eyes stung. She didn't open them.

"Goodnight, Wren."

"Goodnight, Juniper."

And in the silence that followed, beneath the Hook of Sorrows and the Anchor-Stone and the patient red lantern walking ahead of them all, Juniper heard — or thought she heard, sleep bending the world — the faintest thread of song rise from the east, from the direction they were traveling.

Only this time, the Broken Oath wasn't grieving.

This time, it sounded almost like it was listening.
