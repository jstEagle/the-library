## 14. The Inquisitor Bell

The bell had not rung in a hundred years, and it had clearly spent the time developing opinions.

Juniper felt them the moment they climbed into the tower — a low, grudging resentment in the bronze, like a cat that had been left alone too long and intended to make its feelings known to whoever touched it next. She pressed her palm flat against the cold metal and flinched.

"It's *angry*," she whispered.

"Bells get that way." Maren Quill settled onto a beam with the ease of a woman who had slept in worse places and preferred them. "Everything in Vessia gets that way. Grief ferments. You leave a bell alone for a century, you don't get silence, you get *sulk*."

Wren stood at the narrow window with his back to them, watching the road below. He had been standing at windows for three days now, Juniper had noticed, and he did it the way other people prayed — completely, without embarrassment, as though the road might confess something if he watched it long enough.

"Nobody's following us," she said.

"That's what worries me." His breath fogged the glass. "The Bastion always follows. If we can't see them, it's because they don't want to be seen. Inquisitors aren't like oath-keepers. We walk where we're sent. They *listen* their way toward things."

"Listen to what?"

"Oaths." He turned, and in the grey light his face was hard to read, which was usual. "Every promise anyone in Vessia has ever kept is still... audible. A thread. The inquisitors were made to follow threads. And Maren—"

"I know," Maren said. "I swore an oath to find the Door of Return. Loud as a dinner bell, me." She said it cheerfully, but Juniper caught the flicker beneath it — the cartographer's habit of pricing everything before anyone else could. "That's why we're in a tower full of angry bronze instead of sleeping warm in the shrine house. Bronze drowns threads. Old trick. I mapped a country once that hid inside its own cathedral for eleven years."

"A whole country?"

"A small one. Very devout."

Juniper laughed despite herself, and the sound went up through the beams, and the great bell above them gave a single disapproving *toll* — soft, involuntary, like a sleeper muttering — and all three of them froze.

It hung there in the air after: that note. Not grief exactly. Not the Broken Oath's endless aching song, which Juniper had been hearing under everything since the Bastion, like a bruise she could press from the inside. Something older and simpler. The note a bell makes when it remembers being useful.

"We should rest," Wren said quietly. "In shifts. Dawn's four hours off, and if the inquisitors are listening, they'll listen hardest when the world is quietest."

He took first watch without discussion, because he always did, and Juniper wrapped herself in her coat on the far beam and tried to sleep, and failed, because her mind was doing the thing it did — turning over the map fragment, Maren's price, the true name she'd spoken aloud in a dusty shop that smelled of ink and old paper. *Juniper Hartwell.* It didn't feel stolen anymore. It felt spent. And she couldn't decide whether that meant Maren owned a piece of her or whether she'd simply mailed a letter to someone who hadn't read it yet.

Above her, the bell hummed its one remembered note into the dark.

She fell asleep somewhere inside the sound.

* * *

Wren counted the hours by the ache in his knees.

Fourteen years of standing watch taught you that ache was honest in a way people weren't. Knees never lied about how long the night had been. And this night was lying to him in every other way — too still, no wind, no owl, no fox crying down in the frozen fields. The shrine village below had gone to sleep the way villages did here, gently, all at once, like children pretending. But the roads—

The roads were empty the way a held breath is empty.

He heard it first as a wrongness in the bell. The old bronze had been muttering its note all night, faithful as a dripping tap, and then the note bent. Flattened. As if something enormous had leaned against the sound from outside the world and dented it.

Then, faint, from the west: singing.

Not the Oath. Wren knew the Oath the way you know your own heartbeat; he'd have known it deaf. This was thinner. Sweeter. A voice like frost forming on glass, three notes rising and falling, rising and falling, patient as tide. An inquisitor's lure-song — he'd heard it once, at fourteen, through the walls of the Bastion, and had spent years convincing himself he'd imagined it.

They fed on oaths. That was the old lesson every child learned and forgot, because it sounded like a fable. But it wasn't. They found the thread of a sworn thing and drank along it until they reached the swearer, and by the time you heard the singing, they already knew your name, your debt, and every promise you'd ever broken.

"Maren." He said it softly and she was already awake — she woke like a knife coming out of a drawer, no rustle, just suddenly sharp. "West road. Lure-song."

Maren tilted her head. Listened. Her face did something Wren had never seen it do: it went simple. No prices, no irony. Just an old woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Three voices," she murmured. "Maybe four. They've split around the village. Girl—" and her hand found Juniper's shoulder in the dark and shook once, precisely. "Up. Quietly. We're leaving by the north stair, and we're not stopping to be clever about it."

Juniper came awake the way she seemed to do everything — all at once, heart-first, asking questions before her eyes were open. "What's that singing? Is it the Oath? Is the Oath *wrong*?"

"Not the Oath." Wren was at the window again, and now he saw them: shapes at the edge of the lamplight below, moving wrong. Not walking so much as *arriving*, over and over, each step placing them somewhere new. Frost bloomed outward from their feet in fern-patterns, and where the frost touched, the snow stopped falling upward and simply died mid-air, hanging like a held note. "Inquisitors. Don't let them hear your voice. Don't answer them if they call you by name. And whatever happens—"

"Don't say 'whatever happens' and then stop," Juniper whispered fiercely. "Finish sentences, Wren, I swear—"

"*Don't*," he said, "make any promises while they're near. A promise given within earshot is a rope with your name on it."

She went very still. Below, the singing rose another half-step, sweet and terrible, and he saw her understand — really understand — that the song was searching for something, and that the something was a girl from a valley where magic couldn't follow, which meant she was the one thing in Vessia with no thread to follow, except—

Except the name she'd paid Maren.

Except the bargain she'd struck in a cell. Information for freedom, sworn aloud, witnessed by an oath-keeper.

His own oaths were a bonfire in the dark. He knew it. Every promise he'd made since fourteen, layered and loud, and he had led them straight across a country with it like a man carrying a torch through dry grass and calling himself careful.

"The north stair," he repeated. "Now."

* * *

The bell tower had one good secret and one bad one, and Maren knew both, because Maren knew both about everywhere.

The good secret: the north stair let out through the sacristy of the ruined shrine below, and the sacristy had a second door behind a cabinet that opened onto the graveyard, and the graveyard had a lych-gate, and beyond the lych-gate were fields, and fields were freedom, more or less, if freedom was cold and flat and far from anything.

The bad secret revealed itself halfway down: the stairs were iced. A century of snow falling upward had drifted back down through the belfry louvres and packed itself on every step, and the first descent was a slow, silent, agonizing negotiation between boot-leather and gravity, conducted by three people holding hands in the dark like children crossing a river.

Juniper, third in line, found the whole thing so absurd that terror briefly lost its grip on her. Here was Wren Ashmark, oath-keeper of Vessia, hero of a prophecy, moving one inch per breath down an icy staircase with his jaw set like a man defusing something. Here was Maren Quill, legendary cartographer of dead places, whispering curses in at least four dead languages. And the singing outside got sweeter and closer with every step, and none of them said a word about it, because the word would have been a rope.

At the bottom, through the cabinet, into the graveyard.

Snow stood upright in the air between the stones. The whole field of it was suspended — thousands of flakes hanging motionless at every height, catching the moonlight, and Juniper moved through them brushing them aside like curtains, and thought, absurdly, *this is what it looks like inside a held breath,* and then thought, less absurdly, *the singing stopped.*

It hadn't faded. It had *stopped*. Mid-phrase. Which meant—

"They're not searching anymore," Maren breathed beside her. "They've *found*. Run. RUN—"

They ran.

The lych-gate was iron and wouldn't open — of course it wouldn't, nothing in this country opened politely — and Wren hit it with his shoulder and it groaned and gave an inch and the singing started up again behind them, close, so close, three voices braided together now, and Juniper heard words in it for the first time:

*"Little key, little key, come and turn in us..."*

Her blood went to ice water. She put her shoulder next to Wren's and shoved, and the gate shrieked open, and they were through, into the fields —

— and the frost reached them.

It came across the snow like a hand closing, fern-patterns racing, and where it passed, the hanging flakes dropped dead and the ground itself seemed to flinch. Wren turned to face it, sword out, and Juniper saw his face in the moonlight and understood with awful clarity that he was going to stand there. That this was what his oaths had built him into: a door that closed so others could go through.

"No," she said. "No, we go *together*—"

"Fields end at a hedgerow, quarter mile, then the river path," he said, fast, in the voice of a man reciting orders because orders were easier than goodbyes. "Follow the water east until dawn. Don't wait. Don't come back. That's not a request, it's—"

"—a promise?" Juniper snarled. "You just told me not to make any!"

And the first inquisitor crested the graveyard wall.

It had been a person once. That was the horror of it — there was a face in there, smooth and smiling, and eyes like coins at the bottom of a well, and its mouth kept singing even as its body moved with that horrible arriving-not-walking gait. Frost poured off it. Behind it came two more, and the singing braided tighter, and Wren raised his blade, and Juniper grabbed his arm—

—and Maren stepped past them both.

"Children," the old cartographer said, "do shut up," and she walked *toward* the inquisitors, and she began, of all things, to speak her own name.

"Maren Quill. Sworn finder of doors. Sworn mapper of dead countries. Sworn — oh, a hundred small things, I was a chatty oath-taker in my day, weren't I just—"

The inquisitors stopped. All three. Their coin-eyes turned to her like compass needles, because she was offering them a feast, thread after thread after thread, a lifetime of sworn things laid out like a banquet, and Juniper understood one heartbeat too late what was happening, what the old woman had decided in the dark of the bell tower while everyone else slept.

"MAREN—"

"River path, east, don't wait," Maren said over her shoulder, cheerful and terrible, and then she raised her arms like a woman greeting an old friend, and said, "Come and drink, then. But you'll take it *slowly*, you rude creatures, or I'll sour the lot—"

The frost hit her like a wave.

Juniper screamed. Wren caught her around the waist and hauled her backward and she fought him, actually fought, and the last thing she saw clearly was Maren Quill standing in a storm of frost with her chin up, laughing — *laughing*, the mad, magnificent, unforgivable old woman — as the singing swallowed her whole.

Then the hedgerow, and the dark, and the river running black under ice, and Juniper running along it because her body obeyed Wren's orders even while the rest of her howled.

* * *

They went east until the sky went grey, and then they went east some more, because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant the scream that was still lodged in Juniper's throat like a fishbone.

When the sun finally dragged itself up, watery and ashamed, they collapsed in the lee of a fallen waystone, and Juniper discovered she was shaking, and Wren discovered she would not be held, and they sat a body's length apart with the disaster between them like a third traveler.

"She bought us twenty minutes," Wren said at last. "Maybe more. They have to drink an oath slowly or it sours — she wasn't lying about that. A life's worth of sworn threads, taken slowly..." His voice did something rough. "She could keep them busy till noon."

"And then what?" Juniper's voice came out cracked. "Then they finish drinking and she's — what? What's *left*? Wren, what do they leave?"

He didn't answer, and his not-answering was an answer, and Juniper put her face in her hands.

"She gave me back my name yesterday," she said into her palms. "Dying gift, she called it, joking, *joking*, she said she probably wouldn't need it anyway because she was too mean to die. She said—" Her breath hitched. "She said I reminded her why she maps places. Not to record them. So somebody remembers they were worth mapping."

The silence stretched. Somewhere downstream, ice cracked and resettled, patient as everything in this country.

Then Juniper looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear, and Wren, who had faced inquisitors and blizzards and the death of everything he loved, felt his spine go cold at the sight of a determined hedge-girl.

"She gave me my name back so I could spend it myself," Juniper said. "So that's what I'm going to do. The Door of Return is past the Salt Meridian. She marked the way. And we're getting there, and I'm opening it or reforging it or *whatever it actually takes* — but nobody else pays for me. Nobody. Not you, not her memory, no one. That's not a request."

"It's a promise," Wren said quietly.

"Yes," said Juniper Hartwell, and somewhere far behind them, faint as memory, she could have sworn she heard a bell toll — once, approvingly. "And I dare them to listen."
