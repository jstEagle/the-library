## 15. Maren's Price, Paid

The shrine they sheltered in had been a temple once, or a granary, or possibly someone's very ambitious garden shed — Vessian ruins declined to explain themselves. It had a broken dome through which the snow fell upward in slow silver threads, and a corner that still held some warmth out of sheer stubbornness, and it was there, wrapped in every cloak they owned, that Maren Quill lay dying.

She was doing it with characteristic annoyance.

"Don't hover," she said, without opening her eyes. "You hover like a debt collector."

"I'm not hovering." Juniper was, by any honest measure, hovering. She had been hovering for six hours, since the bell tower, since the inquisitor's oath-lash had caught Maren across the ribs as she shoved Juniper through the trapdoor, since Wren had carried the cartographer three streets and down two ladders while Juniper held the wound closed with shaking hands and the last of Maren's bottled summer, which smelled of cut grass and hot stone and did absolutely nothing for blood loss.

"You're hovering *audibly*," Maren said. "I can hear you worrying. It has a frequency. Like a mosquito with a conscience."

Juniper laughed, which was the wrong thing to do, and then her face did something complicated and embarrassing, and Maren opened one eye to look at her.

"Ah," said Maren. "It's like that, is it."

"It's not like anything."

"You're crying over a stranger who charged you your own name for a map. Child, your standards for kinship are *tragic*."

Across the shrine, Wren had built the smallest fire either of them had ever seen — a fire with an apology in it, barely taller than his fist. He'd been feeding it twigs from a bundle of old pews for an hour, methodically, the way he did everything: as if the world were a protocol that could be followed correctly if only he concentrated hard enough. He didn't look over at them. He looked over at them constantly, but he did it by way of not looking at them, which was its own kind of confession.

Maren's breathing had developed a rattle somewhere past midnight. By the grey hour before dawn — though dawn in Vessia was mostly a rumor, the bruised sky lightening from purple to merely bruised-adjacent — she called him over with two fingers, the gesture crisp as ever, a general summoning a corporal.

Wren came. He knelt. Up close, in the firelight, Juniper could see what the last day had done to him: the oath-marks along his forearms, usually a faint ceremonial blue, had gone dark and feverish, the oath itself pulling at him the way the winter pulled at everything.

"Oath-keeper," Maren said.

"Maren Quill."

"Still alive. Good. I hate repeating myself." She fumbled at her coat, and Juniper leaned in to help because Maren's hands had gone the color of the snow outside, and together they got the coat open, and there — sewn into the lining, where it had been since Juniper first met her, or so Juniper had assumed — was the map.

Except now, with the lining torn where the lash had caught her, Juniper could see the truth of it: the fragment wasn't one piece. It was three, stitched edge to edge with thread the color of nothing, and one of those pieces was newer than the others. Newer, and written in ink that hadn't been ink at all but something darker, and the lines of it were Maren's handwriting, and the lines of it were *wrong*.

"What is that?" Juniper whispered.

"That," Maren said, "is the part of the map I drew myself. Forty years ago. When I went looking for the Door of Return on my own account." Her mouth did something that was almost her usual crooked smile and didn't quite make it up the hill. "Everyone thinks I'm a cartographer of dead places because it's a fine romantic trade. Nobody asks why a woman starts mapping countries that don't exist."

Nobody asked. Juniper heard the whole weight of forty years in that nobody.

"My sister walked into the winter," Maren said. "When the song got too quiet. When forgetting started to seem kinder than remembering. Everyone else in our village followed her within five years, one by one, smiling about it. I stayed angry instead. Anger keeps." She tapped the false map-piece with one grey finger. "This says the Door is beyond the Salt Meridian. That much is true; the rest of my map agrees. But I added a lie, all those years ago. I wrote that the Door opens outward. Toward home. Toward whatever you lost." Her eyes found Juniper's, and they were terribly clear, clearer than they'd been all night. "It doesn't. The Door opens inward. Into silence. Into the mercy of being erased. I lied to you, hedge-child, because I wanted company at the end of the road, and because I am, at bottom, a petty and vindictive woman, and I apologize for nothing except that."

"I—" Juniper's voice cracked. "You don't have to confess anything. You need to save your—"

"Breath? For what? A longer eulogy?" But Maren's hand found Juniper's wrist, and gripped it, and the grip still had authority in it, still had the same brisk strength that had hauled Juniper over market stalls and memory-fords and half the geography of a dead country. "Listen to me. I'm going to do a thing now and you're going to let me, and you're not going to argue, because arguing costs breath and I've budgeted mine."

* * *

The thing Maren did took less than a minute, and Juniper would spend the rest of her life trying to find words big enough for it.

"Your name," Maren said. "True name, spoken aloud, freely given — that was my price. You paid it on the memory-road, do you remember? Under the willow that remembered being a coronation oak?"

Juniper remembered. Of course she remembered. She had felt the name leave her the way you feel a tooth leave your mouth — a strange lightness, an absence with edges. Since then, when she reached inside herself for the word that was most essentially hers, she'd found smooth blankness, like a page turned ahead of her.

"I held it in trust," Maren said. "Cartographers hold things in trust. Borders. Rivers. Names. It's the whole trade." She closed her eyes, and her lips moved, and though no sound came out — or perhaps a sound came out that was below sound, a frequency like the valley hum, like the Oath itself — Juniper felt it come back.

That was the only way she could describe it. *Felt* it. Something slid home inside her chest, quiet as a key turning, warm as a stone kept in a pocket against winter. Her name. Hers again. Not taken back — *given* back.

"There," Maren said, opening her eyes. "Released. Not sold, not traded, not owed. Given. Which means it's yours twice over now — once by birth and once by gift. Do you understand the difference, child?"

Juniper didn't trust herself to speak. She nodded.

"Good. Because names given freely can't be used as chains. Names taken can." Maren's gaze flicked, briefly, meaningfully, toward the dark feverish marks on Wren's forearms, and Juniper saw Wren go very still. "Remember that too, oath-keeper. Both of you remember everything I say next, because I'm only saying it once, and I refuse to haunt you for corrections."

* * *

She talked for another quarter of an hour, and it was the most alive she looked all night.

"The Door lies beyond the Salt Meridian, where the white goes silent — you'll know the place, the song stops, and you'll want to weep at the quiet, and you mustn't stop to weep, weeping is a luxury for people with functioning ribcages." She pressed the true map fragments into Juniper's hands, folding her fingers closed over them. "But hear me: the Door is a door. That's all it is. Doors don't know what's merciful. People decided erasure was mercy because it was easier than forgiveness. Don't you dare walk through it believing it's the kind ending. It's just the fast one."

"Then what's the other kind?" Juniper asked, though her throat was full of gravel.

"That," said Maren, "is above my pay grade. I draw maps. I don't write endings." She smiled then — really smiled, the crooked sharp one, the one that had needled Wren across a hundred miles and made Juniper laugh into her sleeve around a hundred campfires. "Though if I were writing yours, I'd point out that every prophecy in this country was authored by somebody, and authors can be argued with. I've spent my whole life correcting bad maps. Bad stories are the same trade with worse weather."

Wren made a low sound. It might have been a laugh. It might have been grief. With Wren, lately, it was getting hard to tell the difference, and Juniper suspected that was the beginning of something important happening to him.

"Oath-keeper," Maren said. "Come here. Closer. I'm dying, not contagious."

He moved closer. His jaw was set like a man walking into a siege.

"When you swore at fourteen," Maren said, "did they tell you what you were swearing *to*?"

"To Vessia," Wren said instantly. "To its preservation until the Oath is restored."

"Mm. And did they tell you who wrote that wording? Or which office profits from a restoration that never quite arrives?" Maren's voice was thinning now, the sentences coming with visible effort between the rattles, but her eyes never dimmed. "No. Another time. Or never. But keep the question in your pocket, boy. Questions weigh less than oaths and open more doors."

"You're delirious," Wren said quietly.

"I've been dying for six hours; I'm entitled." Her breath caught, held, released. When she spoke again it was softer, and it was to Juniper alone, and Wren tactfully turned his attention to a fire that needed no tending. "Hedge-child. One more thing, and this isn't in the budget, so listen."

"I'm listening."

"Your mistress. The witch in the Hollow. The one whose garden you tended and whose ledger you dusted." A pause, a rattle, a breath. "Nobody writes a prophecy naming a girl for a specific Tuesday unless they know the girl. And nobody keeps a girl in a valley magic can't enter unless they're keeping something safe in her. Or keeping her safe from something." Maren's hand tightened on Juniper's wrist one final time. "Ask better questions than I did. That's my last price. No — my last *gift*. Prices you pay. Gifts you carry."

And then, because she was Maren Quill to the end, precise to the last syllable: "There. Done. Everything settled, nothing owed." Her eyes drifted toward the broken dome, where the upward snow climbed past the gap in slow silver spirals. "Huh," she said. "It does look like the sky is grieving. I always thought that was poetic nonsense." A breath. "It's not. Note for the atlas."

Her hand went slack.

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, muffled by distance and stone and the general exhaustion of a country holding its breath, the Broken Oath sang on, note by dying note.

Juniper sat very still, holding a dead woman's maps and her own returned name, and discovered that grief for a stranger who became kin has a particular shape: not the vast crushing grief of losing someone you grew up inside of, but a smaller, sharper grief, like a splinter driven under the nail — specific, personal, absurdly deep. She had known Maren for eleven days. Eleven days of needle-sharp commentary, of bottled summers, of a woman who charged unfair prices and then, apparently, spent her dying breath quietly refunding them.

"She freed me," Juniper said. Her voice came out wrong. "She gave it back. She didn't have to—"

"No," Wren said. "She didn't."

"And she lied to me about the Door. And she told me she lied. And she—" Juniper pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Why would anyone do all of that? Confess and forgive and give and die, all in one night, for someone she met eleven days ago?"

Wren was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, he did it staring into their apologetic little fire, and his voice had lost its protocol edge entirely.

"Because kinship isn't time," he said. "It's choice. Repeated. Daily. Usually stubbornly." A pause. "She'd have hated hearing me say it that pretentiously."

A wet, hiccupping laugh escaped Juniper before she could stop it, which was somehow exactly right, exactly the note Maren would have wanted — laughter at her own funeral, priced accordingly.

* * *

They buried her at first light, or at what passed for it.

There was no ground to dig — the earth beneath the shrine was frozen to the consistency of policy — so Wren broke apart the oldest pew, the one whose wood had still held a ghost of warmth, and built a cairn instead, stone by stone, in the careful way he did everything. Juniper laid the false map-piece on her breast, the lie she'd carried forty years, because it seemed right that it should stay with its author. Then, after a moment, she took it back.

"No," she murmured. "You'd call me sentimental. Waste of good paper."

Instead she tucked it into her own coat, beside the true fragments, and stood there a moment with her palm flat over it, over the place where her name lived again, warm as a banked coal.

"What do we say?" she asked. "Is there a Vessian rite? Words for—"

"There's the Oath," Wren said. "But it's for the living. For the dead we have..." He hesitated, and then, looking embarrassed, offered: "Silence. Mostly. Vessia has a lot of silence available."

"Then let's not use theirs." Juniper cleared her throat. Her unmagical, ordinary, valley-raised voice, the only instrument she owned. "Maren Quill," she said. "Cartographer of dead places. Keeper of borders, rivers, and one extremely contraband name. She was petty, vindictive, overcharging, and the best person I have ever met, and if there's anywhere left unmapped, she's already drawing it." Her voice wobbled on the last word and she let it. "Given freely. Carried always."

Wren added, stiffly, formally, like a man translating himself into a language he was still learning: "She saved my life twice and insulted my competence daily. I believe that constitutes friendship in her system of measurement." Then, quieter, almost to himself: "I hope wherever she is, the maps are difficult and the prices are fair."

They stacked the last stones. The upward snow drifted past the cairn like a slow procession of mourners, and high above, impossibly far off, Juniper would have sworn the Oath's grief-song bent, briefly, around a new note — lower, graver, like a name being read into a record that never closes.

* * *

They left within the hour. Maren would have mocked them mercilessly for lingering, and lingering, she'd taught them, gets people killed by inquisitors.

Juniper walked with the map fragments against her chest and her name humming under her skin like a second heartbeat. Wren walked half a step ahead, the way he always did, but somewhere in the night he'd stopped checking over his shoulder for her and started simply listening for her footsteps, which was different, and she'd noticed, and he probably knew she'd noticed.

The road beyond the shrine ran northwest, toward a horizon gone white and featureless. Toward the Salt Meridian. Toward the place where the song began, and the silence waited, and a plain farmhouse door stood in all that whiteness offering the easy ending Maren had died warning her about.

"We should talk about what happens when we reach it," Wren said, after an hour. Carefully. The way he handled anything that mattered.

"Yes," Juniper said. "We should."

Neither of them said anything else for a long time. The white country spread out ahead of them, patient as a held breath, and behind them the Oath sang its grief into the frozen morning — and Juniper noticed, with a small cold shock, that the song sounded thinner today. Fainter. As if overnight, with Maren's passing, it had lost one more of the notes it couldn't afford to lose.

As if the country itself had attended a funeral, and was running out of time faster now.

"Wren," she said. "How many notes does it have left?"

He stopped walking. He stood very still, head tilted, listening the way only a keeper could listen — and Juniper watched the answer arrive in his face, watch it land like a blow he'd been bracing for and hoping never to confirm.

"Fewer than yesterday," he said.
