## 16. What Loyalty Costs

Wren had been fourteen the winter the river forgot how to move.

He remembered it the way you remember a scar — not the pain of it, exactly, but the shape of it, traced so often it had gone smooth. The winter of the Stillwater, the old people called it afterward, though afterward there were fewer old people to do the calling.

His family had lived in a crooked house at the edge of Lowmarsh, where the fields went soft toward the river and his mother kept bees that produced honey the color of late afternoon. He remembered the honey. He remembered thinking, even then, even as the cold came down like a lid on a pot, *if we can just get to the honey, we'll be all right.* Sweetness was a kind of argument against dying. His mother had believed that. She'd said it once with a spoon in her mouth and her boots off by the fire, laughing at something his father had said about the neighbor's fence, and Wren had thought: there. That's what Vessia is. Not the king, not the banners, not the Oath carved over every courthouse door. This. A woman laughing with honey on her tongue while snow piled up outside like an unpaid debt.

The snow collected the debt anyway.

It came early that year, and it came wrong — falling upward some days, which should have been impossible, which everyone agreed was impossible right up until they watched it happen. The Broken Oath had been singing for sixty years by then, a sound so constant most Vessians heard it the way you hear your own heartbeat: only when it changed. And that autumn, it changed. It slowed. Notes began dropping out of it the way teeth drop out of an old dog's mouth, one by one, until the song had gaps in it you could feel with your skin.

Wren's father said it was nothing. His father said everything was nothing; it was how he loved things, by refusing to be alarmed by them. He was a ferryman, broad and slow-smiling, and he spent that November patching the roof and telling Wren stories about the golden years his own father remembered — harvest festivals that lasted nine days, kings who walked the market unguarded, a country so warm with its own keeping that frost apologized before it settled.

"An oath is just a promise made out loud where the land can hear it," his father told him, mending net by firelight. "And our king promised us spring. Every spring, forever. That's not a small promise, boy. That's a marriage. And when a marriage breaks—" he shrugged his enormous shrug, "—somebody has to keep singing the vows, or everybody forgets why they bothered."

"And if nobody does?"

His father looked at him for a long moment, and Wren would spend thirty years trying to read what had been in that look. Pity, maybe. Or foresight. Or just the particular tiredness of a man who could see the winter getting into the walls.

"Then somebody has to start," his father said.

* * *

They buried the bees first. That was the detail that undid him later, when he let himself think of it at all: not his mother, not his sister, but the bees, frozen mid-cluster around their queen, a whole small nation loyal to a warmth that failed them. His mother cried over them harder than she'd cried over anything, and Wren understood even then that she wasn't crying about bees. She was crying because she recognized the shape of it. Loyalty, huddled around a thing that had stopped giving heat. Staying anyway. Dying of it.

By midwinter the river had stopped moving — truly stopped, not frozen but *paused*, water hanging in the air over the rapids like a held breath — and the Stillwater took its name. The cold that came off that paused river was a different order of cold. It didn't bite. It *considered*, the way a creditor considers a debtor, and then it took its time.

His sister Ilse went first. She was nine. She fell asleep in the warm spot by the stove and did not wake up, and his mother sat holding her for six hours because the house was too cold to bury anyone and grief, at least, gave off a little heat.

Then his father went out onto the paused river to break ice for the fishing holes, because a man who cannot fix the weather will fix something, and the river — the patient, considering river — took him the way winter takes everything in Vessia. Quietly. Without malice. As though it were owed.

His mother lasted until the thaw that never quite came. Wren found her in the garden in early March, kneeling in the dead bee-hive beds with her hands in soil hard as iron, planting seeds she had to have known would never come up. Planting them anyway. When he touched her shoulder she was already stiff, and her face — this was the part he never told anyone, the part he kept in the locked room at the back of himself — her face was peaceful. She had planted her hands into the ground like seeds herself, and whatever she'd been waiting for, she'd decided it was here.

Fourteen years old. One crooked house. No one left to be loyal to except a country that had stopped answering.

So he walked to the Bastion in the gray of a morning that couldn't decide to be dawn, and he swore.

* * *

They made him kneel on the Oathstone — a smaller one, a practice stone, nothing like the field-stones out past the Salt Meridian — and the oath-keeper administering it was an old woman named Sergeant Vale who looked at him the way farriers look at young horses: estimating how much work he'd be.

"You understand what you're swearing," she said. It wasn't a question, but he answered it anyway, because he'd rehearsed.

"I'm swearing to keep the vows the king broke. To hold the country's promise until someone can keep it properly."

"And if no one ever can?"

"Then I hold it forever."

Sergeant Vale's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "Forever's a long word for a boy whose voice hasn't finished changing."

"It's the only word I've got," Wren said, and she put her hand on his head, and the oath went into him like cold water into dry ground — everywhere at once, finding every crack.

Here is what they don't tell you about swearing an oath to a grieving country: you can feel it. Not metaphorically. The Oath-song ran through the stones of the Bastion and up through his knees and settled somewhere behind his sternum, and from that day forward Wren carried a second heartbeat, older than him and sadder than him, and every dropped note in the song was a small cold pin pushed into that space behind his breastbone. He learned to sleep through it. He never fully learned to stop counting the missing notes.

But he also felt what the others felt — the ones who'd sworn before him, the ones who lined the courtyard in their gray cloaks and nodded at him, one after another, like a row of doors closing gently. He felt the *together*. Sixty years of people holding a broken promise, and every one of them still holding, and the holding itself — that was the thing. That was the country, actually. Not the king's vow. The ten thousand small refusals to let go.

His father had been right, Wren thought that night, eating his first meal in the mess hall, sitting next to boys and women who'd lost their own crooked houses. Somebody had started. He had started. And if enough of them held long enough — if they sang the vows loud enough, patrolled the memory-roads, kept the festivals going on schedule even with frost on the drums — then someday, somehow, the land would hear them. The land would remember what it was owed. Spring would come back like a dog that had wandered off, sheepish, tail low, and everything would be mended, and his mother's garden would come up green, and Ilse would—

Ilse would be nine forever. He knew that. Even at fourteen, in the white heat of his certainty, he knew that some debts are not collectible. But the country could be saved even if his family couldn't be avenged, and that distinction mattered to him. He wasn't doing it *for* the dead. He was doing it *instead of* the dead — instead of lying down beside them, instead of planting his own hands in the iron soil. Loyalty, for Wren, had never been about the past. It was a rope thrown forward. Hold on, and pull, and maybe the future doesn't drown too.

He believed it the way you believe in a load-bearing wall. You don't examine it. You build your whole house against it.

* * *

Thirty years later, standing in Maren Quill's borrowed campsite with the cartographer's body wrapped in her own maps, Wren examined it.

Juniper was asleep by the fire — actually asleep, which amazed him; he'd seen her cry herself empty over a woman she'd known eleven days, and then wipe her face and say, "She'd hate it if we wasted the map being sad," and go through Maren's notes with red eyes and steady hands. Now she slept the way the young sleep, utterly, one hand curled near the flames. The firelight moved on her face. She was the first warm thing he'd touched in years and he had touched her now several times — steadying her elbow on the memory-roads, catching her when the shrine steps iced over — and each time it was like pressing his palm to a window in a burning house. *This side is cold. The other side is not.*

He should have been sleeping. Instead he sat turning his loyalty over like a coin he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it had two faces.

Because here was the thing the firelight was showing him, the thing he'd been refusing to look at since the shrine, since the moment he'd said *"the inquisitors ride at dawn, and I'd rather be a traitor with you than a good soldier without"* and meant it:

The Bastion council had voted to force a girl to bind herself to a dying country. Forever. They had used words like *necessity* and *the greater keeping*, and Wren had sat in that chamber for twenty years, nodding along to words like those, and it had taken a hedge-born stranger with sawdust on her sleeves to make him hear what the words actually said. *We will take someone's whole life and call it salvation.* His mother had died rather freely, planting seeds in frozen ground — a choice, even a mad one, was hers. And the council meant to leave Juniper Hartwell no choice at all, and they'd have done it gently, with forms in triplicate, and they'd have called it loyalty.

And underneath that — deeper, uglier — was the question he'd been avoiding for thirty years, the one Maren had needled loose without ever meaning to, back on the memory-roads, watching the golden replay of the harvest festival. *"You know what I map, oath-keeper?"* she'd said, squinting at the shining ghosts of vendors long dead. *"I map countries that died waiting to be saved. Know what they have in common? Everyone kept waiting for someone else to do the saving. Kings, councils, prophecy keys. Nobody ever asked what the country could do for itself."*

The king hadn't broken the oath. Wren had staked his life on that reading — the king was tricked, or coerced, or dead and blamed, and the Oath could be restored to its rightful shape, and everything would go back to the way it was in the golden replays. Back to nine-day festivals and unguarded kings and his mother laughing with honey on her tongue.

But the way it was — he was starting, horribly, to see it — the way it was included a king whose promise was a marriage the whole country was bound into whether they'd consented or not. Included an order of inquisitors who fed on oaths like ticks on a herd. Included a council willing to chain a girl to a glacier and call it mercy. Maybe the Oath wasn't broken. Maybe it was working exactly as designed, and the design was the problem, and every year Wren had spent holding the line had been — no. No, he couldn't finish the thought. Thirty years. His whole self was built against that wall.

Except houses built against walls fall when the wall moves. And the wall was moving. Juniper hummed three notes of the Broken Oath in a bell tower and the song *answered her* — answered a girl with no magic, from a valley with no magic, as though the Oath had been waiting thirty years for someone outside the story to pick it up. As though all his holding, all their holding, had been one long attempt to solve a lock from inside.

He looked at Juniper asleep by the fire, and at the map fragment Maren had paid for with her life, pointing east toward the Salt Meridian, toward the place where the song began. Toward answers. Toward, possibly, the end of everything he'd sworn to hold together — or the beginning of something he couldn't yet imagine, which amounted to the same terror wearing different clothes.

Loyalty, he thought, poking the fire with a stick more firmly than the fire required. Everyone tells you loyalty costs. They mean it costs comfort, or safety, or sometimes your life, clean and quick on a battlefield. Nobody tells you it can cost your *story*. Nobody warns you that the day might come when keeping faith with the country means breaking faith with everyone who taught you what the country was — and that both betrayals will feel exactly as heavy, and you will have to carry them both, and there is no sergeant's hand on your head to make it official.

The fire popped. Somewhere east, faint as a memory of a memory, the Oath-song shifted — and Wren, who had counted its missing notes for thirty years, heard something he had never heard before.

A new note. Small, uncertain, off-key.

Coming from the direction of the sleeping girl.

He sat very still, heart hammering behind the old cold pin, and understood that whatever came next — the Meridian, the inquisitors, the Door, the council's long arm reaching for them all — the country he had sworn to save was already changing shape in front of him.

And for the first time since he was fourteen, Wren was afraid not that Vessia would die —

but that it wouldn't. Not as it was. That it would live, changed beyond recognition, and his oath would have to learn a new verse or break entirely — and he honestly did not know which man he'd be on the other side of that choice.
