## 17. The City That Forgot Its Name

The road to the capital smelled wrong.

Juniper noticed it a mile out — or what passed for a mile in Vessia, where distances had a habit of renegotiating themselves when you weren't looking. Not wrong like rot, exactly. Wrong like a kitchen after someone has scrubbed away all trace of the meal: clean, and lying about it.

"There should be tallow," she said. "And smoke. And horses, if there's a city big enough to have walls."

"There's a city big enough to have cathedrals," Maren said from the cart seat, reins loose in her scarred hands. The cart horse — a patient grey thing that might have been carved from the same snow as everything else — plodded on without comment. "Vessia had eleven of them. I've mapped six."

"Had?"

Maren didn't answer. She'd developed a way of not answering that Juniper was beginning to understand was its own kind of answer, and Juniper settled back against the barrels of bottled weather they'd looted from the frozen market — two summers, one thunderstorm, and something Maren only called *the good fog* — and watched the capital rise out of the white.

It rose the way a memory rises when you're trying not to cry. Spires first, then walls, then the sprawl of roofs, all of it beautiful, all of it faintly transparent at the edges, as if the sky hadn't quite decided whether to keep it.

* * *

They left the cart outside the Gate of Nine Bells — nine bells, zero bells ringing; the bell tower stood empty and silent as a pulled tooth — and walked in on foot, because Maren said carts attracted attention and Wren said nothing at all.

Wren had been quiet since the shrine. Quiet in a new way, Juniper thought. Before, his silences had been the silence of a man holding a door shut against a gale. These were quieter than that. Warmer, maybe. Or maybe just more frightened, which in Wren seemed to look almost identical.

"Stay close," he said now, which was what he always said. But he said it while glancing at her first, quick and sideways, checking she was real, and Juniper had started to live for those glances in a way she was carefully not examining.

The streets were cobbled and swept and utterly deserted, which was strange enough. Then they turned the corner by a fountain — dry, its stone nymph smiling her stone smile — and found the people.

Juniper stopped walking.

There must have been hundreds of them in the great square beyond the fountain. They moved slowly, pleasantly, the way people move on a holiday afternoon with nowhere particular to be. A woman in a fine wool coat stood feeding invisible crumbs to invisible birds, her hands making small generous motions in the empty air. Two old men sat on a bench holding hands, gazing at the sky with soft contentment. Children played a game with no ball, no rope, no chalk marks — just running, and laughing, and stopping to run again.

Everyone was smiling.

That was the thing. Not one grimace, not one argument, not one tired face dragged toward some errand. Hundreds of people, and every single mouth curved gently upward, like a city of portraits painted by someone who had never met an unhappy person and was guessing.

"They're not ghosts," Juniper whispered. She'd seen Vessia's memory-people by now, the citizens who moved like recollections, but these were different. "I can hear them breathing."

"They're breathing," Wren said. His voice had gone flat, the way it did when feeling would be too expensive. "Watch."

He stepped directly into the path of a strolling man in an apron — a baker, by the flour ghosting his sleeves — and the man simply turned, without breaking his smile or his pace, and drifted around him like water around a stone. He never looked at Wren. He never looked at anything.

"Excuse me," Juniper called to the baker, stepping closer before Wren could catch her sleeve. "Sir? Can you tell me where we are?"

The man turned to her. For one heartbeat his smile held, warm and vacant, and Juniper felt the horrible vertigo of being looked at by eyes that saw straight through her into nothing.

Then he said, in a voice like a lullaby sung by someone who'd forgotten the words, "Isn't it a lovely day?"

"It — is it? What day is it?"

"A lovely day," he agreed warmly, and drifted on.

* * *

They found a tavern with its door open and its hearth lit, though nobody was tending it. A kettle whistled on the hook and nobody took it off. Behind the bar, a woman polished a glass that had been clean for what looked like years, smiling at it.

Maren went behind the bar, helped herself to three cups, and poured from a bottle of something amber that probably belonged to no one anymore. "Aurelian brandy," she said. "Or it was. Drink up. It's paid for."

Juniper wrapped her hands around the cup. The brandy burned going down, and the burning felt important — proof that she could still be affected by things, in a city that affected nothing.

"What happened here?" she asked quietly.

"The forgetting." Maren turned her own cup without drinking. "It starts at the edges of the country and works inward, like frost on a window. First you lose your street name. Then your neighbor's face. Then your own. It takes years, usually. This quarter went fast, though. Something accelerated it." Her eyes flicked to Wren, then away. "Inquisitors came through last spring. Feeding."

"Feeding on what?"

"On oaths. On memories of oaths. And what is a country, girl, but a very large oath everyone's forgotten they made?" Maren snorted. "You feed enough oaths out of a city, eventually the city forgets it was ever promised anything. Including itself."

Juniper looked at the hearth fire, burning for no one. At the kettle whistling for no one. At the woman polishing her glass, smiling at nothing, forever.

"Wren." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "The Door of Return. The merciful ending. The one where Vessia gets to die in peace —"

"Yes."

"That's this. Isn't it? This is what it looks like." Her stomach turned over, slow and cold. "The prophecy book said *end Vessia mercifully*. I pictured — I don't know. Sleep. A closing. Lights going out gently, like a play finishing." She stared through the window at the smiling crowds in the square. "But it's not lights going out. It's lights going on in an empty house. It's everyone staying exactly where they are and losing every reason they had for standing there. That's mercy?"

No one answered. Outside, the children laughed their ball-less laughter, and it sounded, suddenly, like the loneliest sound Juniper had ever heard.

"Forgetting is the mercy," she said, working it through, hating every step of it. "That's what the Door offers. No pain, because no memory. No grief, because nothing to grieve *for*. Everyone wanders happy forever in a country that isn't anywhere, that doesn't even know it's a country. That's the gentle ending the prophecy was so kind to offer." She set down her cup so hard the brandy jumped. "It's not dying peacefully. It's being erased so politely you thank them for it."

"Now you understand why I fight," Wren said.

But his voice wasn't triumphant. Juniper looked at him and found him watching the square with an expression she couldn't read — not horror exactly. Longing's cousin, maybe. The look of a man seeing an end to pain and despising himself for finding it beautiful.

"Part of me wants it," he admitted, barely above a whisper. "Fourteen years I've carried this. Some mornings the song gets so tired, and I think — they're not suffering. Look at them. They're *content*. Who am I to demand they remember their grief so I can feel my oath has meaning?" His jaw worked. "And then I remember my mother's name, and how it sounds, and I imagine a world where no one remembers it sounds like that, and I want to burn this whole smiling city down."

The kettle screamed on. Nobody moved to take it off the hook. Juniper got up and took it off herself, because somebody should, and the small ordinary act steadied her hands.

"Maren," she said. "Your maps. The countries that don't exist anymore. Are they all like this? In the end?"

Maren was quiet a long moment, turning her untouched cup.

"Some burn," she said finally. "Some drown. Most do this — fade into pleasant nowhere. Aurelia. Kessrin. The Twin Duchies of Varle." She ticked them off like names of the dead at a funeral she'd attended too many times. "I map them because someone should write down where they were, even if no one remembers. It's poor work. You can't interview anyone. But the stones remember longer than the smiles do." She reached into her coat and drew out a folded scrap of paper — the map fragment, Juniper realized, the one pointing past the Salt Meridian, toward the place where the song began. "Which is why we shouldn't linger. If what you carry in that unmagical head of yours is worth what certain people think it's worth, this city is the worst place in Vessia to stand still."

"Why here especially?"

Maren nodded toward the window, toward the square, and Juniper followed her gaze — and saw it.

At first she thought it was another statue. Then it moved, turning its hooded head with the slow deliberation of something that has all the time in the world, and Juniper's skin went cold in a way the Vessian winter had never managed.

An inquisitor stood at the edge of the square. Grey-robed, still, its face lost in shadow, and around it — Juniper saw it now, saw it and felt sick — the smiling crowds bent subtly toward it, like grass toward a draft, like iron filings toward a magnet. Wherever it stood, the forgetting pooled deepest. It was drinking the whole square, one gentle memory at a time, and the city smiled and let it.

And then the hooded head turned, unhurried, toward the tavern window.

Toward her.

*Don't run*, Juniper told herself, gripping the edge of the bar. *Running is what prey does. You are a hedge-born key. You have read one prophecy book and survived a country that wants to love you to death. You can walk calmly out the back of a tavern.*

"Back door," Maren said mildly, already moving. "Now. Walk. Don't run. Running is what prey does."

"I was literally just thinking that."

"Good girl. Wren — the alley on the left leads to the old cathedral close. Six bells still hang there. Even forgotten, bells remember how to ring, if you know the trick of them." She pressed something into Wren's hand — a small brass striker, worn smooth. "If we're separated, ring the ninth. I'll find you. It's an old cartographer's arrangement."

"What happens if I ring it and you *don't* find us?" Juniper asked as they slipped down a corridor smelling of stale ale and older smoke.

"Then you'll have learned something about the ninth bell," Maren said serenely, and held the back door open onto the cold.

* * *

The alleys behind the square were narrower and somehow sadder than the open streets — here the forgetting had eaten even the architecture's purpose. Signs hung over doors bearing words nobody could read; shop windows displayed goods arranged by hands that had forgotten why. A tailor's mannequin stood in one window wearing half a uniform — an oath-keeper's coat, Juniper realized, silver buttons gone green — and she felt Wren go rigid beside her as they passed.

They made it four streets.

Then the crowd thickened ahead of them — the whole drifting, smiling population of the quarter funneled into one plaza, drawn like moths, and Juniper understood with a lurch that they weren't in the way of the crowd. The crowd was in the way of the *inquisitor*. It had circled. Grey robe, patient as winter, standing at the plaza's center while the happy dead-of-heart orbited it, and between Juniper and any exit stretched a sea of people who could not be pushed aside because pushing them would be like pushing the weather.

"Don't touch them," Wren breathed. "Whatever happens. If you touch one while it's feeding, it feels you through them —"

"I wasn't planning to jostle anyone!"

"Through *them*," Maren said, appearing at Juniper's elbow with the calm of a woman who had escaped things professionally for decades. "It feels warmth. We're the only warm things in this city. Which means, children, that in here—" she gestured at the plaza, at the pale sky, at the entire concept of the situation— "we glow like lanterns."

The inquisitor's hood began to turn.

And Wren did something mad.

He stepped forward, alone, into the open edge of the plaza, and raised the brass striker, and struck the nearest hanging bell — a cracked old thing bolted under a crumbling arch — and the sound that came out was not a bell's sound at all.

It was a word.

Juniper felt it in her teeth, in her sternum, in the soles of her boots: a single tolling syllable of pure *remembering*, rolling across the plaza like a wave over sandcastles. The smiling crowd shivered as one body. Somewhere far away, a woman dropped her invisible crumbs. An old man on a bench sat up straight, blinking, and said, in a voice rusty as a gate, "*Ves—*" and got no further.

The inquisitor whipped around, robes snapping, and for one instant every hollow face in the square turned toward the sound with something raw and starving in their eyes — hunger, hope, the terrible ache of almost remembering —

And Juniper ran, with Maren's hand locked on her wrist, through a crowd gone suddenly turbulent, while behind them the bell spoke again, and again, Wren's arm swinging the striker, his voice rising over the tolls in words she couldn't make out — not Vessian, not quite, older — and she realized with a shock that he was singing. The Broken Oath. Loudly. In the middle of an inquisitor's feast. Calling the whole plaza to remember everything at once.

"You mad glorious fool," Maren panted, hauling Juniper down a stairwell between two leaning houses. "He's giving them back their grief. All of it. Every funeral, every betrayal, every cold night — you can't hand a city its memory in one bite, it'll —"

The scream that rose behind them wasn't one voice. It was hundreds, a chord of agony and joy and loss so enormous that Juniper's knees nearly buckled, and beneath it, thin and furious, a sound like frost forming — the inquisitor's voice, calling for its brothers.

They burst out into a lower street where the cart waited where they'd left it, impossibly, the grey horse standing patient as a saint. Maren vaulted to the seat. Juniper hauled herself up beside her, twisting to look back at the plaza's mouth, where figures were pouring across the square — grey robes, many of them now, converging on one lone figure by a cracked bell who had stopped singing and started running, coat flaring, fast and low and coming *toward* them.

"He rang the ninth bell," Juniper said. Her heart was doing something complicated and embarrassing in her chest. "That was the signal. He's — Maren, that was him signaling *us*, he stayed behind to cover —"

"I know what the ninth bell is, girl."

"Then drive!"

The whip cracked. The cart lurched forward through the gate just as Wren cleared it, taking the last thirty feet in a flat sprint, and Juniper leaned down and grabbed his forearm and hauled, and for one absurd moment he was airborne, hand-in-hand with her, both of them crashing into the barrel-bed among the bottles of stolen summer as the city of smiles fell away behind them and the first grey pursuit-shapes poured out of the Gate of Nine Bells.

For a moment neither of them moved. Flat on their backs among the barrels, shoulders touching, breath heaving white against the bruised sky.

"You sang," Juniper said finally. "In front of them. You gave yourself away."

"They were going to take you."

"You could have been taken instead."

"Yes," Wren said, staring at the sky, entirely calm. "That was the plan."

"That was a *terrible* plan."

"It had a bell in it," he said. "Plans with bells in them are traditionally considered excellent."

And Juniper laughed — really laughed, helpless and winded, the first true laugh since she'd fallen out of her own world — and beside her Wren huffed once, twice, and then he was laughing too, both of them lying among bottled summers while Maren drove and the capital shrank behind them into the haze.

Then Maren said, from the seat, in a voice with all the humor gone out of it:

"They'll send everything now. Every bell, every hound, every robe. There's one road left past the Meridian, and one price unpaid on it." She glanced back at them over her shoulder, and her eyes, for the first time since Juniper had known her, looked old. "So rest, children. Tonight we cross the Salt Meridian — where the song began."

Juniper's laugh died softly in her throat.

Because behind them, faint under the wind, the city was screaming — but ahead, further than hearing, further than sense, she could feel the Broken Oath waiting at the edge of the map, and for the first time, it was not singing grief.

It was singing, low and expectant, like an instrument tuning.

Like something about to learn her name.
