## 18. An Honest Lie

They made camp in the shell of a granary that had forgotten it was ever a granary.

That was the thing about Vessia, Juniper was learning: the country kept its shapes after it lost their purposes. The building still stood four-square against the wind, roof mostly whole, floor swept by decades of snowdrift that came in under the door and lay in pale ribbons like a hostess's attempt at tidying up before guests arrived. There were even iron hooks on the beams where grain-sacks had once hung, and a hearth that Wren eyed with the professional assessment of a man who had slept in a great many ruined buildings and trusted none of them.

Maren Quill had gone ahead at dusk — "I travel faster alone, and I snore artistically, which distracts from vigilance" — leaving the two of them with a small fire, a pot of barley tea, and approximately four hundred miles of things unsaid.

Juniper counted them while she warmed her hands. She'd gotten good at counting, these last weeks. Steps between herself and Wren when they walked. Seconds he looked at her before looking away. Heartbeats when he said her name, because he'd started saying *Juniper* instead of *the key* three days ago and she had not yet mentioned noticing.

The fire popped. Snow fell upward past the broken window, unhurried, like it had somewhere better to be.

"Wren," she said.

He looked up from feeding a stick into the flames. In the firelight his oath-keeper's gray uniform looked almost soft, which was a lie of lighting, because nothing about Wren was soft except possibly his hands, and she had exactly one piece of evidence for that — the moment at the shrine when he'd pulled her behind the wall, palm flat between her shoulder blades — and she had turned that single memory over so many times it had gone thin as coin.

"You should sleep," he said. "Tomorrow we cross the—"

"I know what we cross tomorrow. I want to ask you something first, and I want you to answer it the way you'd answer if you were dying." She kept her voice level. "You're good at honesty under pressure. I've seen it. So pretend."

Wren went very still. He was good at that too. She'd watched him hold motionless through an entire inquisitor patrol passing ten feet away, and she'd thought then, absurdly, *he could be a statue of himself and no one would know the difference.*

"Ask," he said.

"When you caught me in the frozen market. Chapter eight of my life, as it were. You let me go one street further than you should have. Maren said so, and I've been turning it over ever since, trying to decide what it meant." She drew her knees up. "Here's my question. If I hadn't run at all — if I'd just walked beside you, trusting, all this way — where were you taking me?"

The fire ate its stick. Somewhere out in the dark, the Oath sang one low line of its grief-song and trailed off, the way it had been doing more and more lately, phrases unraveling mid-note like knitting coming loose.

"The Bastion," Wren said.

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and Juniper listened to it fall.

"From the beginning," he went on, and his voice didn't waver, which she was grateful for and hated simultaneously. "From the night I arrested you. My orders were to deliver the hedge-born key to the Lord Inquisitor. I intended to follow them. When you ran in the market, I let you go one street because—" His jaw worked. "Because you looked happy. For half a second, looking at bottled summers, you looked like someone who'd never been written into anything. And I wanted thirty more seconds of it before I ended it."

Juniper breathed.

It wasn't shock, exactly. Some part of her — the part raised by Hedda Hartwell, hedge-witch, who taught that people will tell you everything if you watch what they don't say — had suspected. The Bastion's reach, Wren's precision, the way he steered every conversation back to *the prophecy says*. Of course he'd meant to deliver her. That was the shape of him: a man built around a duty the way a bell is built around its crack.

But suspecting a thing and hearing it are different animals, and the second one bites.

"Okay," she said.

His head came up. "Okay?"

"That's not the whole confession, is it? Keep going. You're dying, remember. Dying men don't ration truth."

Something moved across his face — pain, or admiration, or both sharing a coat. "I requested the assignment," he said quietly. "To recapture you, after Maren. Before the inquisitors were even dispatched. I sat in front of the council and asked for it, and told myself it was because I knew your habits, your route, your cartographer. Practical. Efficient." He looked at the fire. "And then at the shrine I warned you instead of arresting you, and I've been telling myself *that* was pragmatism too. That I joined you to keep you alive until I could hand you over properly. That I'm still going to do it."

"Are you?"

"I don't know." It came out raw. "That's the honest answer, Juniper. I have sworn an oath to Vessia since I was fourteen years old, and every hour with you I feel it stretch like old rope. I don't know which way I'll pull tomorrow. I would like to tell you I've chosen you. I can't yet. What I can tell you is that every plan I make now has you walking out the far side of it alive, and I no longer recognize the man who planned otherwise."

The silence afterward wasn't empty. It was full of snow moving upward, and the fire settling, and Juniper's own pulse being loud in her ears.

"Good," she said finally. "Thank you. Now it's my turn."

He frowned. "You don't have to—"

"Oh, I absolutely do. Fair trade. That's how this works, isn't it? That's what your oath is *for* — exchange, promise, both sides holding up their end." She shifted to face him fully, cross-legged, hands flat on her knees like Hedda did when she was about to say something the herbs wouldn't like. "The night Maren took us to see the memory-roads — the golden days, the king kneeling — you cried. You thought I was asleep. And I watched you cry for your country and I thought: *this man loves something so much he'd feed me to it.* And I decided, right then, that if it came to it, I'd use that."

Wren didn't move. Didn't blink.

"If loving Vessia made you capable of sacrificing me," she said, "then being sacrificed could make you capable of anything. A man who's given everything to a lost cause has nothing left to lose, and nothing-left-to-lose is the most useful creature alive. I catalogued you, Wren. Your grief, your guilt about me, your loneliness — you think I didn't notice you haven't touched anyone warm in years? I noticed. I thought: *good. Leverage.* I let you get close on purpose. Every question I asked you about Vessia, every time I hummed near you at the fire, every time I let you see me tired — some part of me was building a case for why you'd choose me over your oath. Not because I trusted you. Because I was *recruiting* you."

The words hung there. She made herself keep eye contact, because dropping her gaze would be another kind of lie, and tonight was for burning those off.

"So we're even," she finished. "You planned to sell me to the Bastion. I planned to buy you with your own heart. Neither of us did it. And here's the strange part, Wren — here's what I can't stop thinking." Her voice cracked, just slightly, and she let it. "Somewhere along the road, the plans stopped working. I'd go to deploy my leverage and find I'd actually just — missed you. During the day. When you walked ahead on the road and I could only see your back, I missed your face. That wasn't strategy. That was real, growing in the middle of all my lying like a weed in a garden bed, and I am *so angry* at myself for how long I mistook one for the other."

* *

Wren found that he could not speak for a while.

It wasn't the confession that silenced him — he'd braced for hers since his own began; fairness seemed to run in her blood like salt in seawater. It was the image. Juniper, sitting by memory-road light, watching him grieve and taking notes. Cool-eyed. Cataloguing. It should have frightened him.

Instead it undid him completely, because it was the most Vessian thing he'd ever heard.

"We do that here," he said at last, hoarse. "Love things strategically. My mother used to say Vessians hug with their elbows out. The oath-keepers are the worst of it — we're taught that devotion is a discipline, that if you feel it plainly it isn't strong enough, so you build machinery around it. Procedures. Plans. Ways to be loyal without ever having to say the word." He laughed, short and self-inflicted. "I had a plan for you. Written in my head like a report. *Subject cooperative. Subject responding to kindness. Continue.* I'd read it to myself at night like scripture."

"And now?"

"Now I keep trying to write the ending and the words won't stay where I put them." He set down his stick, crossed the small space between them, and sat beside her — close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, far enough that the choice to close that distance stayed open, offered, hers. "Do you know what the old vow-shape is? The one the king used? *I give you my keeping.* Not my love, not my life. My keeping. It means: I will hold what you are safe, without changing what you are." His throat worked. "I gave my keeping to Vessia at fourteen, at my family's graves, in the snow. And I find that oaths, unlike plans, apparently have room in them. Or maybe mine cracked and something grew in the crack. Either way."

"Wren."

"I'm not finished, I've rehearsed almost none of this." But he turned to look at her, and whatever was on his face made her breath catch audibly, which he chose — generously, permanently — never to mention. "I'm glad you lied to me."

She stared. "*Excuse* me?"

"Your lies required you to study me closely for weeks." A corner of his mouth betrayed him. "No one has ever paid me that much attention who wasn't trying to kill me, and even they were less thorough. You learned how I take my tea. You learned that I count steps when I'm nervous. You learned my face well enough to miss it." He shook his head slowly. "Most courtships begin with lies too, Juniper. Mine just happened to involve treason and prophecy."

"This is not a—" She sputtered. "We are not calling this a courtship, you enormous gray—"

"Would you prefer 'mutually confessed espionage'?"

"I would prefer you pass the tea before I confess to violence."

He passed the tea. Their fingers overlapped on the cup's handle, deliberately this time, neither pretending accident, and the warmth of him traveled up her arm and settled somewhere behind her ribs where she suspected it intended to live.

* *

They talked until the fire burned down to a red philosophy of embers.

Small things, first — the confessions had opened a door and now ordinary truths wandered through it, shy as cats. He'd never seen the sea; she'd never seen a mountain. He'd secretly named the Bastion's garrison cat *Chancellor* because the animal attended every council meeting with more gravity than the councillors; she admitted she'd talked to the herb rows in Hedda's garden as a child and had never entirely stopped, and if he laughed at that—

He laughed at that. Full, unguarded, head tipped back, and Juniper thought: *there it is. That's the sound I was recruiting toward, before I knew the difference between wanting to use a laugh and wanting to earn one.*

Then quieter things. He told her about the winter that took his family — briefly, in a flattened voice, and she understood he was giving her the map without walking her through the territory, and she took it the same way, because some gifts you honor by not weighing them aloud. She told him about the Hollow's hum, how it pressed on you like weather made of expectation, how she'd spent twenty years being told she belonged somewhere that felt borrowed.

"My mistress always said the valley keeps things *out* for their own protection," she said, staring into the embers. "I thought she meant magic. I'm starting to think she meant me."

"She wrote the ledger," Wren said softly. "If Maren's right about the hand."

"If Maren's right, then my whole life was a footnote to a plan. Same as yours." She leaned sideways until her shoulder found his — a deliberate weight, an anchor cast in miniature. "Two people written before we existed, sitting in a granary that's forgotten its name. We ought to start a society."

"We have. Membership three. The third member snores artistically."

Juniper giggled — actually giggled, an embarrassing sound she hadn't produced since she was eleven — and Wren looked at her like a man watching the thaw come early.

The Oath sang outside, one thread of melody, and then — strangely — it paused, as though listening, before continuing on a slightly different turn than she'd ever heard it take. Neither of them remarked on it. Later, both would remember.

* *

Deep in the night, wrapped in her cloak by the banked fire, Juniper woke to find Wren sitting upright on watch, and slid over to sit beside him in the dark.

"I need to say one more true thing," she murmured, "and then I'll never mention it again."

"Go on."

"When you finish confessing and I finish confessing, and all the lies burn off — there's ash everywhere, yes? Messy." She watched the embers rather than him, because some things could only be said to firelight. "Underneath the ash, there's something that was there before either of us started lying. I felt it the day you arrested me, if you want the humiliating truth. Your hand on my arm, and I thought, *oh no. Oh no, he's going to be important.* I've been running from that sentence ever since. Running toward it. Both. It's very inefficient."

Beside her, Wren exhaled slowly, and in the dark his hand found hers — not seizing, not claiming. Asking. She answered by not letting go.

"Then here's mine," he said, barely above the fire's whisper. "Every version of tomorrow I've imagined since the shrine has you in it. Even the ones where I do my duty. Especially those. I keep trying to imagine Vessia saved and it keeps coming out wrong unless you're standing in the picture, and I no longer think that's weakness." A pause. "I think it might be the only uncomplicated thing I've ever felt. Which for a Vessian is practically a scandal."

"Scandalous oath-keeper."

"Hedge-born criminal."

"Recruiter."

"Kidnapper of trust." His thumb moved over her knuckles, absent, wondering. "We should probably sleep. Tomorrow we cross the Salt Meridian, and beyond it—"

Beyond it. The Door. Both futures. Everything they weren't saying pressing at the granary walls like the snow pressed upward past the window.

But Juniper, drifting back down toward sleep in the circle of that warmth, heard something else — faint, threading under the wind, under the Oath's long grief.

The song had changed key.

And it sounded, impossibly, like it was following her breathing.
