## 19. The Song Learns Her Voice

They buried Maren Quill at the edge of the Salt Meridian road, under a cairn of stones that Wren insisted on stacking himself, each one placed with the solemnity of a man laying bricks for a cathedral.

"She was a cartographer," he said, when the last stone settled. "Shouldn't her marker at least point somewhere?"

Juniper looked at him. He was serious. Of course he was serious; Wren had never once in her company been anything less than entirely, exhaustingly serious.

"It's a pile of rocks," she said.

"A pile of rocks *with intent*." He adjusted the topmost stone a degree to the left, as though correcting a compass error. "There. Now it points north."

"Everything points north. We're in the far north."

"Then it points north *proudly*."

It was the first joke either of them had managed since Maren died, and it landed badly and then, unexpectedly, well — Juniper laughed, a short cracked sound that hurt on its way out, and Wren almost smiled, which for him constituted open celebration.

Maren had left them little else. A map fragment, oilcloth-wrapped and stained with what Juniper chose to believe was tea and not blood. A satchel of pens that no longer worked. And a hole in the shape of a sharp-tongued old woman who had called Juniper "girl" like an insult and "hedge-born" like a title and had died so that both could mean something.

Three days past the shrine now. The road climbed through country that had stopped pretending to be alive — grey grass, grey sky, grey villages where the windows were dark and the doors stood open onto empty rooms, because the people inside had forgotten why they'd ever wanted to close one.

And underneath everything, always, the song.

Juniper had been hearing it since the Bastion. The Broken Oath ran through Vessia like water through limestone, surfacing here and there — in walls, in wells, in the low drone of the frozen river ice. She had learned to ignore it the way you learn to ignore your own heartbeat: it was simply there, grief given melody, a century of mourning set to music by a land that didn't know how to stop.

Except now, walking, she caught herself humming along.

She didn't notice at first. It was just something her throat did, some small animal comfort, a habit from the Hollow where she'd hummed while weeding because Mistress Elowen said silence made the carrots bitter. She hummed the Oath's descending phrase — the one that fell and fell and never quite landed, like a stairway with its last step missing.

And then the land hummed back.

She stopped dead in the road.

Wren, ten paces ahead, turned. "What?"

"The—" She didn't have words yet. The sound had come up through her boots, through the frozen ground, a low harmonic answering the note she'd been holding, sliding into tune beneath it like a second voice joining a round. "Did you hear that?"

"Hear what?" But his hand had already gone to the oath-sigil at his collar, the way it did when something in the world felt wrong, and Juniper understood that he *had* heard it, or felt it, or whatever verb applied to a man whose whole life was tuned to this country's mourning.

"I hummed," she said. "The song. And it — answered."

Wren came back down the road to stand beside her. He looked at the ground. He looked at her. He looked mildly betrayed by both.

"That's not possible," he said.

"I'm aware."

"You don't have magic. I've been *near* you. There's nothing. You're the least magical person I've ever arrested."

"Thank you," Juniper said. "That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me in this country."

"I mean it as a fact, not a compliment. Magic has a texture. Like standing near a fire. You're — room temperature. You're the only thing in Vessia that's room temperature." He crouched, pressed his bare palm flat against the road, fingers spread. Juniper watched him listen with his hand the way Maren used to listen with her maps. After a long moment he straightened, frowning harder. "Nothing. The ground's quiet."

"Maybe it only talks to me."

"Why would it talk to *you*? You're not even from here. You're from a valley where magic *can't go*. That's the entire point of you. That's why the prophecy wants you — you're outside all of this. You're a key because you don't fit any lock."

"Flattering."

"You know what I mean."

She did know what he meant, and it was the same wall she'd been hitting since the ledger: she mattered here only because she belonged nowhere. Every explanation of her usefulness was also an explanation of her exile. But standing on this dead road with a dead woman's map in her pack and a dead nation's song under her feet, Juniper found she was done being embarrassed about it.

"Watch," she said.

She hummed again. Just the falling phrase, three notes, imperfect — she wasn't a singer, she was a weed-puller with a passable ear. The first note went into the air and vanished. The second wobbled. On the third, the ground answered: a deep, patient tone rising through the frost, finding her pitch, holding it, then — and this was the part that made the hair on her arms stand up — continuing. Carrying the phrase onward, past where she'd stopped, filling in the missing step of the staircase, resolving the fall into something that finally, after a hundred years, *landed*.

Wren's face did something complicated.

"What was that," he said. Not a question. An accusation aimed at the universe.

"It finished the line." Juniper's voice shook slightly. "The song's been falling off that edge for a century. I've heard it in every wall since the Bastion. It gets to that note and it — stops. Grieves. Starts over. And I hummed three bad notes and it went *past* it."

"That's impossible."

"You keep saying that word like it's going to start working."

He stared at her. And Juniper saw, with a clarity that frightened her more than the inquisitors had, what was happening behind his eyes: hope. Real, structural, load-bearing hope, arriving in a man who had spent his whole adult life rationing it out in thimbles. She knew what hope cost him. She'd read his file during the interrogation weeks ago — she'd read everything they'd let her read — and she knew about the winter that took his family, the fourteen-year-old boy who swore an oath to a dying country because someone had to, because the alternative was admitting that no one would.

Hope, for Wren, was not a feeling. It was a debt he intended to collect on.

"Don't," she said quietly.

"Don't what?"

"Don't look at me like I'm the answer. I hummed three notes. I'm not — Wren, I pull weeds. Professionally."

"You sang a hundred-year-old grief-song past the place where it breaks," he said. "That's not weeding."

"No. It isn't." She had to be honest here, even though honesty terrified her, even though every honest thing she said to Wren seemed to make him more determined to hand her his country's future like a parcel. "But I don't know what it is. And neither do you. So can we please not decide it means I'm supposed to reforge the Oath and bind myself to this land forever? Because that's where your face is going, and I'd like to get there by a different road."

His expression softened by degrees. "You always do this."

"Do what?"

"Stand exactly between me and the thing I want and ask me to want something else."

"I stand between you and the thing you want," Juniper said, "because the thing you want is *me*, chained to a rock, forever, and I have some objections."

They walked on. They had perhaps two days to the Meridian crossing, if Maren's fragment could be trusted, and Maren's fragments could usually be trusted, which was how she'd justified charging true names for them.

By late afternoon Juniper had stopped trying not to hum. There seemed no point. The song rose through the road whenever she gave it the falling phrase, and each time it went a little further before breaking — four notes, five, six — as though her voice were teaching it, patiently, the way you teach a bird a new whistle by whistling first yourself.

She tried to understand it while she walked, because understanding was the only defense she had against wonder, and wonder had already folded her through one wall this year.

The Hollow, she thought. The valley where magic cannot follow. She'd grown up inside that held breath, that pressure like a hand laid flat over the whole world. Mistress Elowen said the Hollow's hum kept magic out the way a good fence keeps out weather. Nothing enchanted could cross it. Nothing cursed. Nothing summoned. Juniper had spent her childhood pressing her palms against that invisible boundary, wanting out, wanting elsewhere, guilty about wanting, certain that the valley's gift — safety, ordinariness, a life without wonders — was also its sentence.

But a fence works both ways.

That was the thought that arrived somewhere around the sixth mile, uninvited, wearing work boots: a fence keeps things out, but it also shapes what grows inside. The Hollow hadn't just excluded magic. It had raised her. Twenty-odd years of anti-magic pressing on her skin like a held breath — what did that make her? Not empty. *Tuned.* A bell that had never been rung is still a bell. A valley that spent two decades refusing every enchantment might, without meaning to, have taught its hedge-girl the shape of refusal itself — the exact frequency of a door staying shut, a promise staying broken, a song stopping short.

Immunity, maybe, was a kind of instrument.

"I think I know what I am," she said aloud, to the road, to Wren, to whoever was listening underneath it.

Wren, ahead of her, didn't turn around. "Tell me."

"I'm the pause." She chewed on it as she said it, testing the weight. "The song breaks because the king broke his promise — there's a hole in it, a rest, a measure of silence where the vow should be. Everyone in Vessia has tried to fill that hole with magic. Mages, inquisitors, oath-keepers, kings. But you can't fill a silence with more sound. You fill it with — with someone who knows silence. Someone who grew up inside one." Her hands were cold; she tucked them into her sleeves. "I'm not a key because I'm outside magic. I'm a key because I'm *shaped* like the gap."

Wren had stopped walking. When she caught up to him he was looking at her with an expression she couldn't parse — awe, maybe, or fear, or the particular terror of a man watching his dearest theory become real and realizing reality will not behave.

"Say it again," he said.

"No."

"*Please* say it again."

"You'll write it down and show it to the council and they'll build a ritual around it before supper."

"...Yes," he admitted. "Probably."

"And then they'll chain me to an altar by spring."

"Yes," he admitted again, quieter, and this time the quiet cost him something visible. He was learning. Slowly, painfully, the way glaciers learn things, Wren was learning that loving a person and saving a country were not automatically the same project.

They stood in the grey afternoon light, two people on a road that remembered better days, and Juniper felt the strangest sensation: not the song this time, but her own self, arriving. All her life she had been the girl who didn't fit — too restless for the garden, too curious for the shop, too full of elsewhere for a valley built on staying. And here, at the end of the world, in a country that officially did not exist, the thing that had made her wrong everywhere else was the exact thing the road needed. Her restlessness had a use. Her outsideness had a shape. Her twenty years of silence had been, all along, an apprenticeship.

It should have felt like destiny. Prophecies are supposed to feel like destiny.

It felt instead like coming home to find all your furniture rearranged: everything familiar, nothing where you'd left it, and someone else clearly living in the house.

"I'm hungry," she announced, because the moment had gotten too large and largeness made her itchy. "Is there food, or does the immortal grief-nation expect me to sing on an empty stomach?"

"There's hardtack."

"Wonderful. Singing the soul of a nation back together fueled by dry biscuit. Very heroic."

"Vessian heroes historically fasted," Wren said, digging in the pack. "It was considered purifying."

"Vessian heroes historically froze to death, from what I've seen."

"...Also considered purifying," he said, and handed her the hardtack, and Juniper laughed again — easier this time, less cracked — and somewhere below their boots, faint and patient, the Broken Oath hummed the falling phrase back to her like a child practicing a lesson.

*

That night they sheltered in the shell of a waystation, one of the roadside shrines that dotted the memory-roads, its roof half-collapsed, its little alcove still holding the shape of a candle that had burned out decades ago. Wren built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter. Juniper sat closest to it, because she was still the warmest thing in Vessia and intended to stay that way.

She hummed while the fire ticked. Not the Oath, this time — she was too tired for grief, even borrowed grief. She hummed something from the Hollow instead, an old planting-song Mistress Elowen used to sing over the seed beds, nonsense words about rain and patience that Juniper had never examined closely because they'd always just been *there*, like the valley's hum, like her own heartbeat.

Halfway through the verse, the ground picked it up.

Not the falling phrase. Not the grief-song. The *planting song*. The land beneath the waystation took the little nonsense tune and harmonized with it, low and wondering, the way a stranger might lean toward an unfamiliar language spoken kindly.

Juniper sat very still.

"It likes that one too," she whispered.

Wren looked up from the fire. "What is that? It's not the Oath."

"No. It's a song about carrots."

The look he gave her deserved to be framed. "A song about *carrots*."

"Rain and carrots and patience. It's a hedge-witch planting song. It's extremely silly and I've known it since I was six." Her throat tightened unexpectedly. "And this country that's been dying for a hundred years just sang harmony with it."

"Maybe it's not picky," Wren offered. "Maybe anything sung freely sounds like spring to a place that's forgotten spring."

"Or maybe," Juniper said slowly, turning the thought over like a stone looking for what lived under it, "it's not about my voice at all. Maybe the Oath doesn't need a mage because mages *owe* it things. Every mage in this country is tangled up in the old vow — sworn, bound, descended from sworners. Their magic is made of the same promise that broke. But a song about carrots from a valley with no magic at all..." She poked the fire. Sparks went up and died young. "Maybe the Oath is starving for something that isn't part of the story. Something nobody wrote down in 1893. Something *unpromised*."

Wren was quiet for a long time. The firelight moved on his face.

"When you say things like that," he finally said, "I can hear the whole council having simultaneous heart attacks."

"Good. Their hearts could use the exercise."

"They'd call it heresy. Singing *carrot songs* at the national grief."

"They'd call it heresy right up until winter ended," Juniper said, "and then they'd claim they suggested it."

That startled a laugh out of him — a real one, brief and rusty, gone almost before it arrived, but real. Juniper filed it away next to the memory of Maren's laugh and the smell of the Hollow's herb beds, in the small treasury of warm things she was building against the cold. It was getting to be quite a collection. It was getting to be quite a reason.

She hummed the planting song again, and the land sang with her, and she fell asleep mid-verse with the extraordinary, terrifying sense of being listened to by an entire country.

*

Dawn came grey and thin. Wren was already awake — Wren was possibly always already awake; she suspected he slept standing up like a horse, one ear cocked for treason — and he was standing at the waystation's broken doorway, very still, in the posture he reserved for bad news.

"What," Juniper said, sitting up.

"Bells," he said.

She listened. Far off, behind them, back down the long road toward the shrine and the market and the city that forgot its name: bells. Not church bells. Inquisitor bells — she'd heard them once, in the Bastion, and never forgotten the sound, a bronze voice with something *hungry* in it, ringing and ringing and tasting the air between rings.

"They've found the trail," Wren said. "Maren's death bought us three days. It's been three days."

Juniper got up, rolled the blanket, shouldered the pack with Maren's map against her spine where she could feel it. The fear arrived on schedule and she let it sit down beside her without offering it tea.

"How far to the Meridian?"

"Hard march. One day, if the roads remember us kindly."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we run," Wren said, "and I hold the line, and you cross, and you don't look back—"

"We're not doing that speech," Juniper said. "I've heard that speech. That speech ends with somebody noble and dead and somebody else singing alone, and I am *so tired* of this country writing speeches where the ending is a funeral."

Wren opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "You've become very bossy for a weed-puller."

"I contain multitudes. Walk faster."

They walked. Behind them the bells rang closer, patient and terrible, and beneath their boots the Broken Oath rose through the frost to meet her humming — and this morning, for the first time, it wasn't the falling phrase it reached for.

It was the planting song. Rain and carrots and patience. Over and over, urgent now, like a land memorizing the sound of spring before the winter could take the teacher away.

Juniper understood the message perfectly, and quickened her step.
