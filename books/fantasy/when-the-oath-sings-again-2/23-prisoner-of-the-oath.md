## 23. Prisoner of the Oath

The cells beneath the Bastion were carved from the same singing stone as everything else in Vessia, which meant that even here, even in the dark, the Broken Oath never quite stopped humming. Wren had always found it a comfort. Tonight it sounded like an accusation.

They had taken his oath-keeper's badge first — standard procedure, though the sergeant who did it couldn't meet his eyes. Then his boots, for reasons no one could explain except cruelty dressed up as regulation. The stone floor drank the heat out of him through his socks, and Wren sat with his back against the wall and counted the ways he had been a fool.

He'd run out of fingers somewhere around *letting her go one street further than he should have*, and after that he'd simply stopped counting and let the list sprawl like Maren Quill's maps, all borders and no edges.

*You requested this assignment yourself,* he reminded himself. *You asked to hunt her down.* That had been before the shrine, before the fire, before she'd laughed at his attempt to pronounce her name properly — she had said it was "close enough," and then she'd laughed, and the sound had gone into him like a coal into snow, melting a hole straight down to some warm part he'd forgotten he owned.

Now he was the one in the cell, and somewhere above him the Lord Inquisitor was deciding what to do about a key that had slipped the lock.

The door opened without ceremony. It never did, down here. Ceremony was for people who still mattered.

Two inquisitors came in, and between them walked Brother Calloway, who was not technically a brother of anything anymore since the Order of the Oath had been absorbed into the Inquisition forty years ago, but who clung to the title the way a drowning man clings to a plank with nails in it. He was old and neat and smelled faintly of ink, and he smiled at Wren the way a man smiles at a ledger entry.

"Oath-Keeper Vess," he said. "Or should I say former? The paperwork is still settling."

"Wren," Wren said. "Just Wren, now. It's shorter."

"Modesty. Charming." Calloway settled onto the single stool — they'd brought one for him, which told Wren this would be a long session — and folded his hands. "I'll be honest with you, because I find honesty loosens people. You are going to tell us where she went."

"No."

"You haven't heard my method yet."

"I don't need to. I've seen your method." Everyone in the Bastion had seen the inquisitors' method, at least at a distance: the way oath-breakers came out of those sessions gray-faced and hollow, their memories of the oaths they'd sworn scraped thin as winter ice over a stream. The inquisitors fed on oaths. It was the founding principle of their order, the thing that made them both indispensable and unspeakable. An oath, once spoken, lived in the speaker like a second heartbeat, and the inquisitors had learned to put their hands around that heartbeat and squeeze until it gave up its secrets — where you'd been, whom you'd promised, what you'd die for.

And Wren's oath — the one he'd knelt and sworn at fourteen, in a chapel full of candles that the winter kept snuffing — his oath knew everything.

It knew the frozen market. It knew Maren Quill's shop with its maps of dead countries. It knew the shrine where he'd committed treason so quietly he'd barely noticed himself doing it. It knew the small fire, and the star-names, and —

*No,* he thought. *Not that.*

"That's the trouble with your order," Wren said aloud, because talking was better than thinking. "You assume everyone's oath is a leash. Mine isn't. Mine's a promise, and promises aren't leashes. They're spines."

"How poetic." Calloway produced a slate and chalk from his sleeve, the way another man might produce a knife. "Spines can be read too. Every vertebra has a story." He nodded to the two silent inquisitors flanking the door, and they moved forward, and Wren made himself very still.

Here is what no one tells you about being drained: it doesn't hurt, exactly. Pain would have been a mercy, because pain you can brace against. What the inquisitors did was colder than pain. They reached into the oath itself — that bright woven thing he'd carried since he was fourteen, all candle-smoke and grief and stubbornness — and they began, gently, terribly, to unweave it. Thread by thread. Each thread was a moment. Each moment, once pulled free, glowed briefly in their grip like a filament, and then it was theirs, and they could see through his eyes into the memory it held.

He felt the first threads go and fought the only way left to him.

*Think of something else,* he told himself. *Give them nothing but static.*

But an oath is not a room you can close. It is open country, and they were riding across it.

So he built a wall out of the only material he had.

He thought about the laugh.

Not strategically, not at first — that came later, when he understood what he was doing. At first it was just panic. His mind, fleeing the inquisitors' touch the way a hand flees a hot stove, seized on the warmest thing in reach, and the warmest thing in reach was Juniper Hartwell laughing at him under three strange stars while Maren Quill pretended to sleep and the fire popped.

She'd been trying to teach him "Hartwell," and he'd said it the Vessian way, hard on the H, and she'd made a face like a cat presented with a bath, and then she'd tried to say his surname back at him — *Vess* — except she'd said it *Vesst*, with a little extra consonant that didn't exist, and he'd corrected her, and she'd corrected herself wrong again on purpose, adding more letters each time — *Vessst, Vesssssst* — until she broke, and the laugh came out of her in a helpless cascade, and Maren had opened one eye and said, "Children, some of us are dying slowly of cold," and Juniper had laughed harder, and Wren had felt his own mouth do something it hadn't done in years—

That memory burned in him now, and the inquisitors' reaching threads slid past it like water off oiled wool. Not because it was hidden. Because it was *burning*. A laugh, freely given, in defiance of the dark — there was no oath in it, no vow for them to grip. It belonged to no contract. It was just his.

"Hm." Calloway tilted his head, listening to whatever his colleagues reported back through the slate. "There's a knot in you, Oath-Keeper. Something the threads won't pass through. Interesting."

"It's called a sense of humor. You wouldn't know it professionally."

Calloway ignored that with the ease of long practice. "Again. Deeper threads this time. We need the route. The Meridian crossing, the Door, any of it."

The deeper threads hurt more. Wren felt them go — the night roads, the memory-fields where the landscape replayed Vessia's golden days, the king kneeling at the Oathstone in the rain. Each one lit up in the inquisitors' hands, and he felt them *looking*, and he hated it with a hatred so pure it frightened him, because hatred was a kind of surrender too. Hatred meant they'd gotten under his skin far enough to matter.

*The laugh,* he thought, dragging himself back to it. *Go back to the laugh.*

But here was the trap, and he saw it clearly now, and it was beautiful in a horrible way: the more he retreated into Juniper, the more of Juniper he exposed. Because the threads were connected. Everything in an oath is connected; that's what makes an oath an oath and not a shopping list. The laugh connected to the fire, the fire to the escape, the escape to the shrine, the shrine to treason. If he hid behind her laughter, they would follow the warmth backward and find everything it warmed.

His love for her was the best armor he had and the worst thing he could possibly show them.

"She's important to you," Calloway observed mildly, watching his face. Wren realized his expression must have changed — some softness leaking out despite himself. Old man, ink-smelling, harmless-looking, and sharp as a paper edge. "That's useful. People guard what's useful badly. They think guarding means hiding, when really guarding means *advertising* — putting the treasure behind every door so the thief never knows which door matters."

"Has anyone ever told you that you talk like a burglar?"

"Frequently." Calloway smiled his ledger smile. "Continue resisting. It saves me chalk."

* * *

Time went strange in the cells. There was no window. The singing stone kept no hours. Meals arrived through a slot — gray bread, gray soup, gray water — and Wren ate because his body demanded it and resented every bite for tasting like cooperation.

Between sessions he sat against the wall and took inventory of himself, the way he'd been taught at fourteen, the week after the winter took his family and the oath-keepers came through the village offering purpose to anyone young enough and angry enough to kneel. *Inventory:* what do you still hold?

His oath: damaged, fraying, half-unwoven. He could feel the gaps now, places where a memory should be and wasn't — he knew he'd traveled the frozen market with her, but the market itself was a smear, colors without shapes. They hadn't erased anything, precisely. They'd *read* it, and reading an oath-thread leaves it stretched, worn, thinner than before. Like a book passed through too many hands.

His resolve: intact, mostly. Cracked in the middle where the softness was.

Her: safe, he hoped. Past the Meridian by now, if she'd kept moving. Alone, which gnawed at him worse than the cold. She'd crossed into white silence to save him from a choice, and he had gotten himself captured to buy her time for it, and between the two of them they had constructed a monument to mutual stupidity, and he missed her so much it was physically inconvenient, like a stone in his boot he couldn't take off.

*This is what they'll use,* he thought. *Not the oath. This.* And he resolved, with the particular fierceness of a man lying to himself, not to let them see it.

Then the door opened again, and Calloway came back with the stool and the slate, and the resolution lasted approximately ninety seconds.

* * *

The third session was different. Wren knew it before it started, the way animals know weather. The inquisitors moved differently — less routine, more hunger. One of them whispered to Calloway and Calloway's eyebrows climbed, and Wren's stomach dropped through the stone floor into whatever lay beneath the Bastion, which he suspected was nothing good.

"You've been holding out on us," Calloway said pleasantly. "Or rather, you've been holding *in*. That knot we found. The warm one." He set the slate aside, which was somehow worse than picking it up. "We've decided to stop pulling threads and start pulling the knot."

"There's no knot."

"There's always a knot." Calloway leaned forward. "Do you know why oaths feed us, Oath-Keeper? Truly? It isn't the words. Words are cheap; the Hollow is full of words and we can't drink a drop there. No — it's the *binding*. When a soul ties itself to something outside itself, it creates a tension. A pull. Like a plucked string. We eat the vibration." His eyes, mild and wet, fixed on Wren. "And you, my dear former colleague, are vibrating like a cathedral bell. Whatever you've tied yourself to — it isn't in your oath. It's newer. Fresher. Louder." A pause, delicately cruel. "Someone you care for more than your country. Imagine that. Imagine what the council will say."

Wren said nothing. Saying nothing was a strategy. It was also the only thing left in his inventory that hadn't been confiscated.

"They're going to do it anyway," Calloway continued, almost kindly. "So consider cooperating. Tell us the route, spare yourself the deep work. We only want the girl. Once we have her, the reforging proceeds, the winter ends, and everyone — including you — goes home to a spring. Isn't that what you swore your life for? Spring?"

And that was the hook under his ribs, wasn't it. That was fourteen years old and kneeling in candlelight, that was his mother's hands blue at the wrists, that was the whole architecture of his adult life: *endure anything, betray anyone, pay any price, so long as winter ends.* The Lord Inquisitor had known it. Calloway knew it. Everyone knew it, because Wren had never once made a secret of it. His love for Vessia had always been public property, a banner, a weapon others could aim.

But the laugh — the laugh was *his*. Nobody had aimed it. Nobody had ordered it. It had happened in defiance of duty, in defiance of the mission, in defiance of every plan either of them had confessed to in the honest dark beside a small fire, where they'd admitted they'd each meant to use the other, and found that the using had quietly failed, and what remained was just — them.

They wanted the knot. Fine.

He would make the knot unbreakable.

When the inquisitors reached in this time, Wren didn't flee the memory. He *inhabited* it. All of it, deliberately, completely, the way she had taught him — without meaning to teach him — that a person could belong to a moment instead of to an oath. The stars with their Vessian names. Maren grumbling. The fire. And Juniper, mid-laugh, looking at him with an expression that said she saw exactly what he was, the traitor, the doubter, the man whose loyalty was heavier than he'd expected — and stayed anyway. Stayed laughing.

The inquisitors hit the center of it and recoiled.

One of them actually staggered, catching himself on the cell wall, and made a sound Wren had never heard an inquisitor make: a short, involuntary gasp, like a man who has gripped a stove and found it alive.

"What—" Calloway began.

"It burns," the younger inquisitor said, staring at his own hand as if it belonged to someone else. "Sir, it — that's not an oath-tension. That's not binding at all. It's — *free*. How is it free? Nothing in a person is—"

"*Enough.*" But Calloway was looking at Wren now with an expression that had lost its ledger-smoothness, and Wren, exhausted and freezing and half-scraped, felt something bloom in his chest that he refused, absolutely refused, to call hope.

Because he understood it suddenly, sitting there with no boots on, and understanding arrived with the simple stupid clarity of a star-name learned beside a fire: she was from outside magic. From the Hollow, where nothing could bind. Her laugh carried no vow, no contract, no string for them to pluck. It was the one place in him the inquisitors could not drink, because it had never been promised to anyone.

Love as vulnerability: yes. They'd use it to find her eventually; he knew that; knots could be cut as well as pulled, and men with slates were patient. But love as armor too. The one shield they hadn't forged and therefore couldn't melt.

"You'll break eventually," Calloway said at the door, gathering his dignity. "Everyone does. The stone is patient, the soup is bad, and the council has voted, Oath-Keeper. Do you know what they voted?"

The cold went through Wren like a blade finding the gap in armor. "Tell me."

"The key escaped the Meridian. The Door stands unopened, the Oath hangs half-sung, and the winter is accelerating — you've felt it, even down here, don't pretend otherwise. So the council has authorized the Lord Inquisitor to proceed by *any* means. Including, if necessary..." Calloway paused at the threshold, savoring it, a man who had never learned that cruelty lands softer when you don't announce it. "...including the girl's own conscience. She'll come to us, Vess. They always come. Guilt is the strongest oath there is, and nobody swears it aloud."

The door closed. The lock turned. The singing stone hummed its century-old grief.

Wren sat in the dark with his heart slamming, doing arithmetic he despised: *she'll come to us.* Because she would. Of course she would. She'd crossed the edge of the map alone rather than choose, and she'd touched the handle of the easy exit and walked away from it, and a person like that — a person who had already traded herself once for him without asking — a person like that would hear "any means" and understand it perfectly, and come walking back into the Bastion with her chin up and her unmagical voice ready, and offer the one bargain the inquisitors couldn't refuse.

*Don't,* he thought at the ceiling, at the stones, at her, wherever she was. *Whatever they've taught you about duty — don't you dare—*

Far above, muffled by floors of singing stone, a bell began to ring.

Not the alarm bell. Not the council bell.

The visitor bell.

Wren closed his eyes and listened to the footsteps descending toward the cells — light, quick, unmistakably determined, wearing boots she'd almost certainly stolen — and hated himself for the softness, and held onto it anyway, because it was the last warm thing in the whole frozen country, and he was done letting go of things that mattered.
