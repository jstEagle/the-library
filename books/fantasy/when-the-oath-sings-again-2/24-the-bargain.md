## 24. The Bargain

The road back to the Bastion was the longest walk of my life, and I made it in borrowed boots.

They were Maren's boots, actually — or they had been, before we buried what was left of her under a cairn of river stones at the edge of the Salt Meridian. They were two sizes too big and smelled faintly of ink and pipe smoke, and I had stuffed the toes with moss because walking on grief is hard enough without blisters.

Wren walked beside me. He hadn't wanted me to do this.

That was the problem, really. Everything about this plan was the problem.

"You could still turn around," he said for the fourth time since dawn. "We could go north. There are passes through the Teeth that even the Bastion doesn't watch."

"And then winter eats Vessia by spring, and you and I live out our days as two very cold fugitives arguing about whose fault it was." I pulled the moss deeper into my left boot. "I've done the arithmetic, Wren. It's bad arithmetic all the way down."

"Then let me do it. Let me trade myself. The inquisitors want an oath-keeper's oath more than they—"

"They don't want your oath." I stopped walking, and he had to stop too, which I confess gave me a small and petty satisfaction. "They want *me*. The hedge-born key. You're just — packaging."

"I'm not—" His jaw did the thing it does when he's swallowing something true. "You're not going through those doors alone."

"Actually," I said gently, "that's rather the entire point."

We stood in the middle of a road that remembered being golden — the memory-roads still flickered here, ghosts of orchards blooming sideways through the snow — and for a moment neither of us said anything, because we'd already said everything, twice, badly, at volume, and once in whispers near the fire when he thought I was asleep.

I loved him. I had known it since the shrine, probably. Maybe since the frozen market, when he'd let me run one street further than he should have. Knowing it changed nothing about the plan, which was the cruelest part. Love doesn't get a vote when a whole country is dying. It just gets to stand there afterward, holding the coat.

"One condition," Wren said finally.

"You're not in a position to negotiate. You're being traded."

"Juniper."

"Wren." I used his name like a doorstop. "One condition of *mine*: you let them take the deal. No rioting. no heroic rescues. If I hear — and I will hear, the walls sing, remember — if I hear that you've done anything stupid and noble, I will reforge the Oath wrong on purpose. Out of spite. I'll bind the whole country to a promise that everyone has to eat soup with forks. Forever."

"That's not how oath-craft works."

"You don't know that. Nobody knows how it works anymore. That's my leverage."

He almost smiled. It was a terrible, crooked thing, that smile, like a house holding itself up out of stubbornness alone. "You're infuriating."

"I'm from the Hollow. We specialize in it. It's our only export besides turnips."

And then, because I am a coward in exactly one way, I hugged him first — quick and fierce, before either of us could make it mean more than I could afford right now — and stepped back, and walked the last mile to the Bastion gates with his eyes on my back the whole way, which felt less like being watched and more like being carried.

*

The Bastion had grown new teeth while I was away.

When Wren had first dragged me through these halls as a prisoner, the gates had been iron and the guards had been bored. Now the gates were iron wrapped in singing chains — actual chains, humming the Oath's grief-song in tight little loops, like knots tied in melody — and the guards looked at me the way people look at weather they've been warned about.

The Lord Inquisitor received me in a round chamber at the top of the tallest tower, where the snow fell upward past the windows in long silver threads. He was smaller than I expected. That was the trick of him, I think; everyone expects evil to arrive tall. Sereth Vane came up to my shoulder and had the mild face of a schoolmaster, soft hands folded over a ledger that I recognized with a lurch: a sister-volume to the prophecy book that had swallowed me out of the Hollow. Same binding. Same patient, hungry paper.

"Hedge-born," he said, and smiled like we were old friends. "You walked here. Through the Meridian. Past the ambush." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Where we took your oath-keeper."

My stomach dropped through the floor, through the tower, through the mountain. "He held the line so I could cross. That was the bargain between us. If you've harmed him—"

"He's alive. Inquisitors feed on oaths, girl, not flesh. Why would we spoil the meal?" He turned a page. The paper sound went through me like cold water. "But you know this already. You came back for him. It's written all over you. Grief always walks in ahead of the person carrying it."

"I'm here to make a deal."

"I assumed. They usually are, by the time they reach my tower." He gestured to a chair. I didn't sit. Sitting felt like agreeing to something. "Speak it, then. I'm told the hedge-born drive hard bargains. Though frankly I find that difficult to believe, coming from a valley where nothing magical can survive. What do they teach you there? Composting?"

"Mostly composting. Also stubbornness." I kept my voice level. Somewhere below us, Wren was in a cell, and the thought of it made my hands want to shake, so I put them behind my back where they couldn't embarrass me. "I'll reforge the Oath. Willingly. Freely. No chains, no compulsion, none of whatever you did to make your last key candidates scream. I'll sing the Broken Oath whole, and I'll do it well, because — and you may write this down — I'm apparently the only instrument this country has left."

Sereth Vane's eyebrows rose a fraction. Encouraged, I pressed on.

"In exchange, Wren Aldermarch goes free. Not released into a snowdrift with a head start. Free. His record cleared, his oath returned to him intact — un-drained — and passage wherever he wants. Out of Vessia if he likes. Anywhere the roads remember how to go."

"And why," the Lord Inquisitor said softly, "would you imagine I want him free?"

"Because he's worth ten thousand soldiers to whoever holds him, and worthless dead, and because you're a man who reads ledgers, and ledgers are arithmetic, and I've already told you how I feel about arithmetic today." I lifted my chin. "Also because I won't sing otherwise. That's the real line under the line. My willingness is the entire currency of this transaction, Inquisitor. Spend me gently or lose everything."

For a long moment the only sound was the snow ticking against the glass, falling up, up, toward a sky that had forgotten how to hold it down.

Then Sereth Vane laughed. It was a warm laugh. That was somehow the worst thing yet.

"Oh, I like you," he said. "The last three keys we located — and yes, child, there were others before you, other names in other centuries, the ledger is longer than anyone wants to read — the last three all arrived weeping, or raging, or catatonic. None of them negotiated. None of them understood that their fear was the least interesting thing in the room." He closed the ledger. "Your terms are accepted."

Just like that. No haggling. Which should have been my first warning, and would have been, if I hadn't been so busy being relieved.

"There's paperwork," he added, almost apologetically. "There is always paperwork. An oath reforged is a contract renewed, and contracts require... alignment. Consent on both sides. The land must agree to be bound again, and the binder must understand precisely what she is binding herself to."

"I understand I'll be anchored to Vessia. Bound to the country, its winters, its walls. Forever. Yes. I've read the prophecy. I've had a great deal of time to read the prophecy."

"Mm," said Sereth Vane. "Have you read the fine print?"

"There isn't any fine print. I've read it four times. It's forty-one lines and a marginal note about goats."

"The fine print," the Inquisitor said, rising, moving to the window, silhouetted against that terrible upward snow, "is not in the prophecy. The prophecy tells you what the reforging requires. Only the Oath itself knows what it takes." He turned. And his face — that mild schoolmaster's face — was suddenly, carefully kind, in the way people are kind when they're about to tell you someone has died.

"A reforging is not a patch, hedge-born. It is a re-weaving. The old Oath broke because one man's promise was asked to carry a nation, and one man cannot. So the new Oath will not be anchored to a king, or a stone, or a throne." He clasped his soft hands. "It will be anchored to *you*. Your voice, your breath, your name. Every note of the song will pass through you forever. And because the song must be woven into every living memory of Vessia — every hearth, every grave, every stone that remembers being young — the weave must include you entirely."

"Including me," I repeated. "Yes. That's what bound means. I did the reading."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "You did the *prophecy*. Prophecies are written by hopeful people. The Oath was written by the land, and the land does not hope. It only keeps accounts." He let the silence stretch until it creaked. "A thread cannot be woven into a tapestry and remain a separate thing. To anchor the song in you is to give you to the song. Your voice. Your face. Your name. Every moment you have ever spent inside Vessia will belong to the weave — and a weave cannot contain its own weaver as an outside presence. So the Oath will take you out of every story but its own."

The room tilted. Or I did.

"What are you saying?" My voice sounded far away, like someone else asking on my behalf. "Say it plainly. I'm from a valley where magic can't follow. Plain language is my whole religion."

Sereth Vane looked at me with something that might genuinely have been pity, and said:

"When you sing, they will forget you. All of them. Every soul in Vessia who ever knew your face or heard your name — the forgetting is the mortar, child. Memory is the material oaths are mortared with. To set you into the foundation of the new Oath, the land must pour every recollection of you into it like stones into wet cement. The oath-keeper you bargained for will walk out of my cells a free man, his oath whole, his country saved — and he will not remember why he fought for it. He will not remember you at all. No one will. You will spend eternity as the song beneath everything, sung by a country that cannot recall your name."

The window rattled. Snow ticked upward. Somewhere far below, through a hundred feet of stone, I thought I could hear the walls singing — the grief-song, thin and dying, and underneath it, so faint it might have been my own pulse, something listening. Waiting to learn my voice.

"You accepted my terms," I whispered.

"I did. All of them." The Lord Inquisitor spread his soft hands. "You asked for his freedom and his forgetting was never yours to withhold. You offered your willing song and your ignorance of the price was never mine to cure — though I confess, I find I regret that. I had hoped you'd ask the right question. Hopeful people always assume the ledger balances fairly. The land is not fair. The land is only *exact*."

I sat down after all. My legs decided without consulting me.

Somewhere in the tower below, Wren was alive, and whole, and — if the inquisitor kept his word — soon to be free. He would walk out into a thawing country, green returning in ribbons, citizens remembering themselves, and he would feel, vaguely, inexplicably, like a man leaving a house where someone had just finished singing. He would look for the source of the feeling and find nothing. He would go on. He was good at going on; the winter had taught him that.

And I would be everywhere. In the walls, in the wind, in the first birdsong of the first spring. Sung daily and known never.

"Well," I heard myself say, from a great distance, in a voice that had decided to be brave because the alternative was screaming. "I suppose I should have asked about the fine print."

"It's a common oversight," said Sereth Vane, not unkindly, and opened his ledger to a blank page. "Now. Shall we begin? The land is listening."

And far below us, beneath a hundred feet of stone, the Broken Oath went suddenly, terribly quiet —

the way a room goes quiet

when someone has just walked in.

* * *

They brought me down through the Bastion's oldest levels, where the stones predated the kingdom and the chains sang themselves hoarse, to a chamber shaped like the inside of a bell.

Wren was there.

They stood him at the far side of it, wrists ringed in humming iron, and when the door opened and he saw me, his whole face did several things at once — relief and fury and fear fighting for space like cats in a sack — and I watched him start forward and stop himself, because he'd made a bargain too, hadn't he, somewhere in the dark of his cell. Everyone makes bargains in the dark.

"Whatever they told you," he said, low and fast, "whatever terms they dressed up — Juniper, listen to me—"

"I know the price," I said.

His face went still. Completely still, the way the valley hum goes still, the held-breath quiet. And I watched him do the same arithmetic I'd done in the tower, the bad arithmetic all the way down, and I watched him arrive at the answer, and I watched him open his mouth to refuse—

"Don't," I said. "Please. Whatever you're about to do — don't. One of us gets to be sensible and it has never once been you."

The Lord Inquisitor cleared his throat, gentle as a landslide beginning.

"If the two of you are quite finished," he said, "the land is waiting. And it has waited," he added, turning the page, "a very long time."

The chains began to sing.
