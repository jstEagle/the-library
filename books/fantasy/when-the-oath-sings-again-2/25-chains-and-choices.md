## 25. Chains and Choices

Wren heard the news the way he heard most bad news: from a guard who couldn't keep his mouth shut.

They'd moved him to a deeper cell after the riot — down where the singing walls went quiet and the only sound was water dripping somewhere in the dark, patient as a debt collector. Three days, maybe four. He'd stopped counting when counting started to feel like surrender.

Then boots on the stair, and Tam's voice, low and guilty, drifting through the bars before the lantern even arrived.

"You're free."

Wren didn't move from the bench. "That's a strange way to open a conversation."

"The Lord Inquisitor signed the release this morning." Tam's face appeared at the grate, lantern-light carving him into slivers. "The hedge-witch bargained herself. She gives them the reforging, willing, and you walk out. It's done, Wren. Papers are drawn. You could be past the gate by dusk."

The words landed one at a time, like stones dropped down a well, and Wren listened for each splash.

"She offered," he said. "Or she was offered."

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously." He stood. His knees had opinions about it. "Tam. Where is she?"

"They've taken her up to the Refectory. Preparations begin tomorrow at dawn, they said. There's a ritual, and vestments, and — Wren, there's nothing to be done. She *agreed.* Willingly, they're saying. The whole Bastion's calling it a mercy. First mercy this country's had in a hundred years, they say."

Wren crossed the cell in two steps and gripped the bars. Iron so old it had almost forgotten it was iron. "And her name? Did anyone mention what happens to her *name*?"

Tam looked away. That was answer enough.

Every memory of her, gone. Every Vessian mind swept clean of Juniper Hartwell like frost off glass. She would sing the country whole and become a stranger in it — no, not even a stranger. Strangers could be met again. She would be an absence wearing a shape, walking through a world that had been scoured of her, and she would know it, every day, forever.

"And they gave me papers," Wren said quietly.

"Papers."

"Wonderful." He smiled, and Tam flinched, which was fair. It was probably not a kind smile. "Tell me, does the Lord Inquisitor's signature smudge if you spit on it?"

* * *

Juniper had expected a cell. What she got was a room.

It was, insultingly, a nice room. A bed with actual blankets. A basin and jug. A window slit through which the bruised sky leaked its permanent dusk. Someone had even left bread and butter on a tray, as though she were a guest and not a sacrifice, and the butter had been salted properly, which somehow made everything worse.

"They could at least starve me dramatically," she told the empty room. "I feel like I'm being fattened."

She'd made the bargain in the Lord Inquisitor's study, standing very straight while he'd unfolded the terms like a merchant laying out cloth. The reforging, performed willingly, would bind the Oath anew and end the winter. In exchange: Wren's freedom, full pardon, his record cleansed of treason. And she had said yes before he finished speaking, because it was the obvious arithmetic — one life against a country — and because some treacherous part of her had thought, *he'll understand. He swore an oath to save Vessia at any cost. This is any cost.*

What the Lord Inquisitor hadn't mentioned until her hand was nearly on the pen was the small print of forgetting. *The anchor must stand outside the story,* he'd said, in that dry voice of his, *and so the story must forget her.* He'd said it the way a tailor mentions that the coat comes without buttons.

She'd signed anyway. Her hand had shaken, but it had signed.

Now she sat on the too-comfortable bed and tried to feel noble about it and mostly felt like crying, which she refused to do on principle. Crying would wrinkle her resolve. Resolve was all she had left; she'd traded everything else.

Footsteps in the corridor. Not the shuffling of guards. Purposeful ones. Angry ones, if anger could be heard through stone.

The door didn't open so much as lose an argument with whoever was pushing it.

Wren filled the frame, still in his torn oath-keeper's coat, a fresh bruise blooming along his jaw, and two guards behind him looking like men who had been ordered to allow something they deeply disapproved of.

"You're supposed to be outside the walls by now," Juniper said.

"I'm supposed to be a great many things." He came in like weather. "A loyal officer. A freed man. A sensible person who takes his pardon and goes. The Bastion has high hopes for me."

"Well, don't disappoint them. Go."

"No."

"No?" She stood up. "Wren —"

"No," he said again, gentler, which was worse. "You signed yourself into a ritual that will erase you from every mind in Vessia, and you did it to buy my freedom, and I am telling you no. I am telling you I won't take it."

"You don't get a vote! This isn't a council meeting!"

"Neither is your sacrifice. Funny how that works."

The guards shifted. One cleared his throat. "Sir, we're meant to escort you out—"

"You're meant to escort me nowhere," Wren said pleasantly. "You're meant to stand there and pretend this is procedure. Do try."

Juniper planted herself between him and the door, arms folded, which put them close enough that she could see the exhaustion under his fury. "Think about what you're doing. If you refuse the pardon, they arrest you again. If they arrest you again, the bargain collapses, and then I'm erased *and* you're in a cell, and we've achieved absolutely nothing except symmetry."

"Then let's be unsymmetrical."

"That is the worst battle plan I have ever heard."

"I've heard worse. I grew up here." His voice caught on something. He mastered it. "Juniper. You knew what I was when you met me. An oath-keeper. Do you know what we're taught, first lesson, first day? *No one is saved alone, and no one is saved by being spent.* A country bought with a person isn't saved. It's just indebted. And I will not live in a country that owes its spring to you and can't even remember your name."

"Oh, that's rich." Her voice cracked upward. "*You* were going to drag me to the Bastion in chains when I first arrived. Don't think I've forgotten. Saving Vessia at any cost — those were practically your wedding vows to this place."

"Yes. And then I met you, and I learned what 'any cost' looks like from the inside, and it turns out I'm a coward about one specific thing."

"One thing?"

"You," he said simply, and the word landed between them like a dropped lantern, lighting everything whether they wanted it lit or not.

Juniper looked away first. "That's not fair. You can't just — say things."

"I've been in a cell for days. I've had time to compose."

* * *

The guards, wisely, had retreated into the corridor to discuss their pensions. Which meant that when Juniper finally spoke again, it was quieter, and the quiet was more dangerous than the shouting.

"If you take the pardon," she said, "you live. You plant things. You teach the new verse, or whatever it is you do when winters end. You get a whole life, Wren. A real one. And I get —" she gestured at the window slit, the bruised sky, the distant thread of the Oath still singing its grief-song through the stones — "I get to be the reason. That's not nothing. Most people don't even get to be a reason."

"And what do you get?" he asked. "Truly. Tomorrow morning, when they put whatever vestments they've chosen on you and start their ritual — what do you get? Not the remembering. Not the thanks. Not even the grief of people mourning you, because they won't be able to. You'll stand inside your own funeral and watch everyone smile through it."

"Don't."

"You'll hum the song you taught me by the fire — the one about the cartographer's dog — and no one will ever have heard it."

"*Don't.*"

"Because I will," he said. "That's the part you haven't thought through. I remember everything now, and after tomorrow I'll remember nothing, but *you'll* remember both sides. You'll carry every memory we made, alone, in a country full of people who look at you like weather. Is that your plan? Is that the mercy you bought with yourself?"

Her eyes were burning and she hated it. "Someone has to pay. That's how oaths work. That's how *everything* works — Maren paid, Wren, Maren died paying, and I didn't get to refuse that either —"

"Maren chose," he said. "Maren chose, and she gave you back your name first, because she understood what she was doing. Have these people given you anything? Or did they just hand you a pen and call your signature destiny?"

Silence. Somewhere above them, faint as breath, the Broken Oath sang on, note by dying note.

"We sound ridiculous," Juniper said at last, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Arguing over who gets to be destroyed. There should be a word for this."

"There is. It's called an oath-keeper's courtship."

Despite everything — despite the cells and the bells and the blood on Maren's map — a laugh escaped her, wet and unwilling. "That's appalling."

"I've been saving it."

She sat down on the bed. After a moment he sat beside her, not touching, shoulders almost touching, which in their private language was the same thing.

"I'm still going to do it," she said.

"I know."

"You're still going to stop me."

"I know," he said. "We're very predictable people. It's one of my favorite things about us."

She turned to look at him. "Us."

"Don't examine it. It's load-bearing."

* * *

He was gone by the time the night bell rang — escorted out, actually escorted, by guards who had apparently decided that discretion was the better part of treason. But Juniper noticed, when she finally lay down, that the room wasn't quite as empty as it had been.

On the tray, under the bread, someone had folded a scrap of paper. She opened it with hands that pretended not to hurry.

Not a message. A drawing — quick ink lines, badly proportioned, unmistakably the three of them at the small fire: herself mid-laugh, Maren's hat at a defiant angle, Wren's stars scattered overhead in entirely invented constellations. Underneath, in his cramped handwriting:

*So that if they take everything else, at least the paper remembers. Argue with me in the morning. I'll bring better points. — W*

Juniper held the little drawing a long time, the way you hold something warm in cold weather.

Then she looked up at the window slit, at the sky beyond it, and made her own decision — the one she'd been circling since the Lord Inquisitor's study, the one she hadn't dared say aloud even to herself.

If the ritual required her willing consent, then her consent was hers to give, withhold, or redefine. And if the Oath itself had begun learning her voice —

Well. Nobody had written *that* into the terms.

Outside, deep in the stones, the old song faltered — and, impossibly, seemed to listen.

* * *

Dawn found the Refectory prepared: candles, vestments, the Lord Inquisitor waiting with his dry smile and his dry voice and a circle of chalk that smelled of burnt promises.

It did not find Juniper Hartwell in her cell.

It found the window slit wide enough for a thin woman who had spent a childhood squeezing through hedges, a stolen guard's cloak, and a note pinned neatly to the blanket in handwriting the Lord Inquisitor would come to loathe:

*Gone to finish this properly. Both of us. Neither of us. You'll see. — J*

*P.S. The butter was excellent. That almost makes up for everything.*
