## 27. Riot of the Remembering

**Wren**

The singing wall had a crack in it, three fingers wide, just above the old cistern in the Bastion's undercroft. Wren knew because he'd put it there himself, four years ago, during a night of grief so complete he'd punched the stone without thinking. The wall had bled song through his knuckles for an hour afterward, and he'd stood there in the dark letting it, because it was the only thing in Vessia that ever answered him back.

Now he pressed his mouth close to the crack and told it everything.

"The king never broke the oath."

The words felt strange in his mouth, like speaking a dead man's name into a room where he might still be sleeping. Behind him, Juniper held the council ghosts' testimony written out in her careful, unmagical handwriting — she'd copied it three times, because "if we're committing treason," she'd said, "we're at least going to commit it legibly" — and Maren's map fragment was folded against his heart, and somewhere above them the inquisitors were chanting over an interrupted ritual that had left the whole Oath hanging like a bell half-rung.

He took a breath and sang.

Not well. That was the thing about oath-keepers: they were chosen for constancy, not voice. Wren sang the way a cart pulls — steady, grinding, not built for beauty. But he sang the truth the way the council ghosts had given it to him, verse by verse, the real founding of the Inquisitor order, the trick played on a kneeling king, the lie dressed up as law for a hundred years. And the wall did what singing walls do.

It carried it.

The crack hummed. The stones around it hummed. The hum went down into the foundation and out along whatever veins of song ran beneath Vessia's streets, and Wren could feel it leaving him the way warmth leaves a cup — drawn out, amplified, sent onward. The Broken Oath had been grieving through these walls for a century. Now, for the first time, someone was giving it new words.

Juniper's hand found his shoulder. "They can hear it?" she asked. "Out there?"

"The walls are the only thing every Vessian still touches," Wren said. "Doors. Floors. The walls remember even when we don't." He paused. "That used to be a saying. I just remembered it now."

* * *

**Juniper**

From the Bastion's high window, the city looked like a held breath finally let go.

It started small. A woman standing in her doorway with her head tilted, listening, the way you listen when a tune you've known forever comes back to you from a stranger's radio. Then a man dropped the bottles he was carrying — bottled summers, Juniper realized, summer-colored light spilling across the snow and not fading — and didn't bend to pick them up, because he was crying, and you don't bend to pick things up while you're crying like that.

"They're stopping," she whispered. "The forgetting is stopping."

Wren came up beside her. For a man who'd committed treason an hour ago, he looked remarkably calm, which Juniper had learned was either courage or exhaustion wearing courage's coat.

"What happens now?" she asked.

"Now?" He considered. "Vessia has spent one hundred years being politely empty. Politeness has a shelf life." Far below, a bell tower began ringing — not the inquisitor's bell, but the old hour-bell, which hadn't been rung by anyone official since before the winter. Someone was pulling the rope themselves. "Now we find out what a country does when it wakes up."

The answer, it turned out, was: quite a lot, all at once, mostly loudly.

* * *

**Wren**

The first thing the remembering city did was take down the gray banners.

Nobody ordered it. Wren watched from the window as citizens climbed the banner poles in ones and twos and then in dozens, hauling down the featureless gray cloth the Bastion had hung after the forgetting, folding it with the exaggerated care of people handling something shameful. From a rooftop on Tanner's Row, someone unfurled something else — actual color, red and gold stitched crookedly, the old Vessian sunburst that officially no one remembered how to draw.

Someone remembered. Apparently lots of someones. The stitching was terrible and Wren felt his throat close looking at it.

"They kept it," Juniper said softly. "All this time. Under the floors, behind the plaster. You don't unpick good embroidery just because they tell you to forget."

"My mother embroidered one," Wren heard himself say. "On my fourth birthday. A sunburst with seven rays, and she got six done before dinner and finished the seventh by candlelight and swore me to secrecy because candlelight embroidery was wasteful." He blinked. "I haven't thought of that in thirty years. It was taken from me. They reached into my head and took my mother's crooked seventh ray and I *thanked them for it.*"

His fists were shaking. Juniper didn't tell him it would be all right, which he appreciated enormously. She just said, "Then let's make sure nobody gets thanked for it again," and handed him his coat.

* * *

**Juniper**

The Bastion, being full of inquisitors, did not join the celebration. The Bastion instead did what institutions do when the world disagrees with them: it doubled down at speed.

The alarms went up in triplets. Boots in the corridors. And then the Lord Inquisitor's voice rolled through the halls on a tide of compelled air, smooth as poured cream:

*"Citizens. You are experiencing a memory disturbance. Remain indoors. The Oath protects you. The Oath forgives you. Return to your forgetfulness and be at peace."*

Juniper snorted. "Return to your forgetfulness. That's the most honest sentence they've said in a century."

"It's also a threat," Wren said grimly, because they both knew what the inquisitors fed on. Oaths. Memory was made of promises, and a city suddenly stuffed with remembered promises was, to an inquisitor, a banquet laid out backwards — or a battlefield. "They'll try to eat the remembering right out of them."

"Can they?"

"A hundred years ago? Yes." He was already moving toward the stairwell, counting doors in his head, doing the grim arithmetic of exits. "Today? Today there are ten thousand people remembering their grandmothers' names all at once. That's a lot of oaths to swallow." He glanced back at her, and there — under all the fear — was the corner of a smile. "Besides. We have chaos. I've read about it. Never had any use for it before."

"You're welcome," Juniper said. "I grow it naturally."

* * *

**Wren**

Getting out of the Bastion required passing through the warrant yard, which required the guards not to notice two fugitives, which should have been impossible.

It was not impossible, because the warrant yard's guard that evening was a round-faced young man named Pell whom Wren had once spent an entire rainy week teaching to hold a shield correctly, and because Pell was standing in the middle of the yard with his helmet off, tears freezing on his cheeks, whispering a name over and over.

"Sera," he was saying. "Sera. Sera." His sister. Taken by the winter when he was nine. He'd been made to forget her, and he had forgotten her, and now the wall-song had handed her back and he was holding the name like a bird with a broken wing.

Wren walked straight past him. It felt monstrous. At the gate he stopped, turned, and said quietly, "Pell. The east postern is unwatched tonight. If you happen to be walking that way—"

"I'm not deserting," Pell said immediately, wetly, with tremendous dignity. "I'm *patrolling*. Extensively. Toward my sister's old street." He jammed his helmet back on. "Which I remember now. Which I will report to no one, sir, because reporting requires paperwork and paperwork requires thinking and thinking is *not my duty tonight.*"

"That," Wren said, "is the finest interpretation of duty I've heard in this building in a century," and Pell saluted him with a shaking hand, and Wren walked out of his own prison into a riot, feeling lighter than treason had any right to make him feel.

* * *

**Juniper**

The streets were firelight and song and snow falling upward in confused spirals, as if the sky itself couldn't decide which way grief went anymore.

People were in the squares. Not fighting — not yet, though Juniper could hear the distant percussion of the Bastion's countermeasures, the dull booms of compelled silence being slammed down over neighborhoods and the brighter sounds of those silences being refused. Mostly they were *telling each other things.* An old man on a fountain's edge recited street names that hadn't appeared on any map in decades, and the crowd repeated them back, testing them like coins bitten for gold. Two sisters who'd passed each other blankly for years were clutching arms in front of a shuttered bakery, sobbing, "You kept the recipe, you KEPT THE RECIPE—" A boy of maybe eight stood on a barrel announcing to anyone who'd listen that his grandmother said the river used to have fish in it, actual fish, and half the square cheered as if he'd announced a military victory.

"They're not even angry yet," Juniper said. "They're just — glad."

"Glad comes first," Wren said. "Angry is coming. Angry always comes second, and it's bringing its friends." He steered her down a narrow lane away from the main square, one hand light between her shoulder blades, and Juniper noticed — not for the first time — that he navigated crowds the way other men apologized: constantly, gently, without making a fuss about it. "We need to be gone before angry arrives with the inquisitors riding on its shoulder."

"Where are we going?"

"Where the song began." He said it simply, but his eyes flicked to hers and held, and Juniper understood that he meant the Oathstone field, the place Maren's map fragment pointed to, the place where a king had once knelt in the grass — and that he was trusting her with the destination out loud, in open street, because hiding things from each other was a debt they'd sworn off.

"And if the inquisitors follow?"

"Then they'll arrive," Wren said, "at the exact spot where their entire order was founded on a lie, in front of a city that just remembered how to be angry." He smiled — really smiled, tired and fierce. "I'm rather looking forward to introducing them."

* * *

**Juniper**

They nearly made the south gate.

Nearly. Because the Lord Inquisitor, whatever else could be said of him, was not a man who wasted a crisis, and the last thing Juniper saw before Wren pulled her into an alcove was the square behind them filling with gray-robed figures moving in perfect, horrible unison — and the crowd, her rioting, remembering, laughing-and-crying crowd, turning to face them.

No one ran.

That was the thing Juniper would carry out of that night, the thing she'd press flat and keep like a flower in a book: *no one ran.* A washerwoman stepped forward first, planted herself in front of the gray robes, and began to sing — badly, off-key, the new verse, the one Juniper had sung into the broken wall not twelve hours ago, the promise among equals. Her voice cracked on the third line. Behind her a tanner picked it up. Then the sisters from the bakery. Then the boy with the fish story, shrill and fearless.

By the time the inquisitors' leading edge reached the fountain, forty voices were holding the line, and the line was made of nothing but memory and terrible pitch, and it did not break.

"They're using the song as a shield," Juniper breathed.

"They're using it as a *wall*," Wren corrected softly. "We build walls out of songs here. It's traditional." His hand found hers in the dark of the alcove. "Come on. While every eye in Vessia is busy being brave, ours is the escape that costs the least."

* * *

**Wren**

The south road out of the city ran white and quiet, the riot reduced behind them to a glow and a murmur, like a hearth seen through a shut door.

Wren walked fast and thought slow. Behind them: a city rising on the strength of words he'd sung into a cracked wall, defended by a washerwoman who couldn't carry a tune in a bucket, against mages who ate oaths for breakfast. Ahead of them: the Salt Meridian, the Oathstone field, and a ritual neither of them fully understood, attempted by a woman with no magic and a man whose faith had recently died and been replaced by something sturdier and less comfortable.

Somewhere in the dark behind them, a horn sounded — the Bastion's long horn, the pursuit signal — and then, absurdly, wonderfully, it was joined by another horn, and another, blown from the city walls by hands that had no orders and wanted none.

"They're signaling us forward," Juniper said, wonder in her voice. "Aren't they?"

Wren listened. The horns were ragged, unschooled, joyously out of coordination. But underneath them, faint and clear, carried up the road on the winter wind, the singing walls were doing what singing walls do.

Carrying it.

"Yes," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. "That's exactly what they're doing." He squeezed her hand once and lengthened his stride toward the meridian, the horns at their backs like a country learning to cheer. "Then we'd better not waste it. The song gave us a head start, Juniper. Let's see how far we can run before the winter notices what we're taking with us."

Behind them the city sang. Ahead of them the snow fell upward faster than it had ever fallen, as if the sky were gathering itself for one last argument —

— and far off across the white fields, at the edge of hearing, something ancient and buried began, very quietly, to listen.
