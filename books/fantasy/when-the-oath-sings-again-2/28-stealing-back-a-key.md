## 28. Stealing Back a Key

They had dressed me in white.

That was the first thing I understood when the fog in my head finally lifted enough to let me think again — not *where am I*, not even *what's happening to me*, but a small, offended observation: *they have put me in white, like a bride, or an offering, and I look terrible in both.*

The ceremonial robe was Vessian wool, heavy as a wet blanket, embroidered at the hem with the old oath-script that Wren had once traced for me in the dirt of our camp — *we hold, we hold, we hold* — repeated so many times it stopped being words and became a pattern, like wallpaper. Someone had braided my hair. Someone had washed the road off me. I resented every kindness retroactively, because kindness used as packaging is just another kind of chain.

I was standing in the center of the Bastion's Great Hall, which I had only ever seen from the wrong side of bars, and it was worse than I'd imagined. Not because it was grand — though it was, all ribbed stone and hanging banners gone stiff with frost — but because it was *loud*. The walls themselves were singing. The Broken Oath lived in this building the way water lives in a sponge, and now that they had scraped away whatever dampening charms kept it quiet during interrogations, I could hear it in full: a hundred voices woven into one long grief-note, rising and falling like breathing.

Except it wasn't breathing evenly anymore.

That was the second thing I understood. The song was *ragged*. It caught and stuttered, the way a singer catches when someone walks across their line of sight, the way a heart stutters when you hear bad news. Every few measures a whole phrase would drop out and leave a hole, and the holes were getting bigger. I had spent weeks learning to listen to this song — humming back at it over cookfires, letting it teach me its shape — and I could hear, with terrible clarity, that it was dying faster than it should have been.

Because of us. Because of what Maren gave us, and what we ran with, and what Wren had shouted through the singing walls until a city remembered itself. We had pulled a thread. The Oath, half-sung, interrupted in the middle of its century-long sentence, was unraveling at the speed of guilt.

And they thought reforging it would fix that. With me. Right now. Whether I agreed or not.

"Juniper Hartwell." The Lord Inquisitor stood before me on a dais of black ice — actual ice, grown into steps, each one singing faintly when his weight pressed it. He looked exactly as he had in my nightmares, which I suspect was deliberate; men like him curate their own haunting. "Do you understand what is about to happen?"

"You're going to erase me," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. Somewhere behind my sternum, where my true name had been given away and then given back, there was an ache like a bruised bone. "Every memory. Every mind in Vessia. Including—" I didn't finish. Saying his name felt like spending something I might need later.

"Including," he agreed, without cruelty, which was somehow crueller. "But you will live inside the Oath itself. You will be the note that holds the country together. Generations will feel your warmth without knowing your name." He smiled, thin as paper. "There are worse eternities."

"There are better ones too," I said. "I've seen them. They involve breakfast."

A ripple went through the circle of inquisitors ranged around the hall — twelve of them, robed in ash-grey, standing at points of some geometry I couldn't follow. One of them actually laughed, a single startled bark, and was glared into silence by six of her colleagues. I decided to remember her face. In a room full of people who fed on oaths, the one who laughed at my jokes seemed like the most likely person to eventually do the right thing.

"Begin the binding," said the Lord Inquisitor.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about having magic done to you from the outside: it feels like being read aloud.

The twelve began to chant, and the chant wasn't sound so much as attention — twelve pairs of hands reaching into me like I was a drawer full of papers, sorting, selecting, looking for the page they needed. The hedge-born key. The girl from outside magic, whose unmagical voice was somehow the one instrument this whole grieving country could be tuned by.

I fought the only way I could, which was badly and internally.

*I refuse,* I thought, throwing the thought at them like a plate. *I refuse, I refuse, I choose nothing you're offering, I—*

"It won't work," murmured the laughing inquisitor, who had drifted to stand near me, ostensibly adjusting my robes, actually close enough to speak under the chant. Her voice was dry as old paper. "Consent isn't required for the reforging. Only presence. That's the tragedy of oaths, girl — most of them get sworn by people who weren't asked." A pause. "I'd have voted for the Door of Return, personally. Mercy's cheaper."

"That's the bleakest comfort I've ever received," I managed, through teeth that wanted to chatter, "and Maren Quill once told me my name was worth more than a map of heaven."

Something flickered across her face at that name — recognition, maybe, or grief — but there was no time to dig into it, because the chant changed key, and the walls answered.

The Broken Oath, hearing itself invoked, surged up through the Bastion like floodwater finding a crack. The singing in the stones rose to a pitch that made my fillings ache — except I didn't have fillings, I had a whole mouthful of ordinary Hollow-raised teeth, and still they ached — and the frost on the banners bloomed outward, ferns of ice spreading across the fabric in seconds. The temperature dropped so fast my breath came out as a solid little ghost.

And the Oath did something I had never heard it do before.

It *answered back*.

Not with words. With refusal. A great discordant groan rolled out of the walls, the sound of a hundred voices trying to sing two songs at once — the old grief-song and something new underneath it, something the remembering city had planted there, the verse Wren had broadcast, *the king never broke it, we were lied to, we were lied to* — and the two songs ground against each other like continental plates.

The nearest inquisitor screamed and fell to her knees, blood threading from her nose. Feeding on oaths, it turned out, meant you also choked on them.

"Hold the sequence!" the Lord Inquisitor roared.

"The sequence is *gone*," shrieked another, "the song's split, it's splitting *us*—"

And that was when the doors blew open.

* * *

Later — much later, in the spring, when we could afford to laugh about things — Wren would insist that he had planned the timing perfectly, that the explosion of the east doors was precisely coordinated with the choir's dissonance peak, and that any appearance of chaos was tactical misdirection.

I was there. I know the truth. The truth is that the doors blew open because he'd packed them with three bottles of bottled summer from Maren's stall, and heat that concentrated detonates, and my oath-keeper — my careful, protocol-quoting, rules-are-load-bearing Walls-of-the-Bastion oath-keeper — had solved the problem of the impregnable east doors with *farmer's market contraband.*

He came through the smoke with snow in his hair and a stolen guard's cloak thrown over his shoulders like he'd lost a bet with it, and behind him — limping, bandaged, leaning on a spear she'd absolutely not been cleared to carry — came a grey-haired woman with a cartographer's burn scars on her hands.

"Maren," I said, and my voice cracked clean down the middle. "You're supposed to be dead."

"I'm supposed to be a *lot* of things," Maren said. "It's never once stopped me." She squinted at my white robe. "You look like a wedding cake that's been left out in weather."

"I've been saying."

"Enough." Wren crossed the hall in eight strides, and I want it noted, for the record of my own heart, that he did not slow down, did not check his flanks, did not do a single one of the tactically sensible things drilled into him since he was fourteen years old and swearing oaths over a frozen grave. He took my face in both cold hands and looked at me like checking whether I was real. "Are you whole? Did they start? Did they—"

"They started," I said. "Wren, they started, and something's wrong with the Oath, it's splitting, it can't—"

"I know." His jaw tightened. "I've been listening to it for two days. Half the city has. That's why the riot worked, and that's why we have about ten minutes before this entire building comes apart." He turned, putting himself between me and the dais, and drew his sword — his actual sword, the one he'd surrendered when they arrested him for treason, meaning he'd stolen it back, meaning somewhere between chapter twenty-seven and now my solemn lawman had become a full-time criminal. "Lord Inquisitor. You'll forgive the interruption."

The Lord Inquisitor stood very still on his ice stairs, watching the ferns of frost devour his banners, and for one strange moment he looked less like a villain than like a man doing arithmetic he hated. Around him his circle was in ruins — two inquisitors unconscious, three bleeding, the laughing one conspicuously unhurt and conspicuously standing nowhere near the fight.

"You were going to bind her without her consent," Wren said. "You wrote the protocols yourself. Article Four. An oath taken under compulsion binds no one but the one who forced it. Have you forgotten your own scripture?"

"The country dies otherwise," the Lord Inquisitor said quietly.

"The country dies *anyway*," I said, and everyone looked at me, and I found, to my enormous surprise, that I wasn't afraid anymore. Terrified, yes. But terror and fear turned out to be different animals, and only one of them owned my tongue. "Can't you hear it? Your ritual cracked it. There are two songs in those walls now and neither one can finish, and every minute you spend holding me here, the winter gets worse. Look outside. *Look.*"

The tall windows along the eastern wall had gone white — not frosted, not clouded, but filled edge to edge with snow falling upward so dense it was a wall, a living blizzard stacked against the glass like a mob. And beyond it, faintly, through all that white, the city was screaming. Not in pain. In *song*. Thousands of people who remembered their country now, singing the new verse into the storm, ragged and off-key and desperate, and the Oath above them tearing itself in half trying to be both things at once.

"Where?" I asked Wren. "Where do we go?"

"The Oathstone field." He said it like a man confessing. "Where he knelt. Where it began. If the song can be mended anywhere, it's there — sung by someone outside the story, freely given, or not at all." His eyes found mine, and in them was everything he wasn't saying in front of the enemy: *you don't owe this country anything. You never did. Say the word and we run for the Door instead and I will hold off every inquisitor in Vessia with this stolen sword and no regrets whatsoever.*

"We go there," I said. "Both halves of the sentence. Freely given." Then, because terror and humor apparently share a spine: "Also I want it recorded that I was kidnapped in a bathrobe."

"Noted," said the laughing inquisitor, and stepped aside, and opened, with one casual gesture, the service door behind the dais that none of us had known was there. "Cellar corridor runs to the river gate. Don't thank me. Thank Quill — she trained me, forty years ago, before I got respectable." She glanced at the wreckage of her colleagues. "Or before they did."

* * *

We ran.

I have been chased before in my life — mostly by Mistress Elsbeth's goose, which remains, in honest accounting, a more frightening opponent than any inquisitor — but I had never run *through* a country coming apart. The cellar corridors of the Bastion were singing so loudly that dust jumped off the ceiling beams in little dancing clouds. Ice grew along the walls as we passed, chasing us like a tide, and Maren, bless her scorched and stubborn soul, walked backwards part of the way just to watch it come, muttering measurements under her breath. "Three feet a minute. No — four. Yesterday it was one." She looked up at me, and her eyes were bright with the particular excitement of a woman witnessing data she'd kill to survive. "Girl, your interrupted ceremony didn't just fail. It *loosened* it. The Oath's half-unpicked, like a seam. Which means—"

"Which means it can be resewn," I finished. "By anyone. By me. Without their ritual."

"Without *anyone's* ritual," Maren said. "Ritual is just fear wearing formal clothes."

The river gate was a postern door crusted in ice a hand thick, and Wren hit it twice with his sword pommel and once with his shoulder, and it gave with a crack like the whole sky snapping a knuckle. Cold hit us like a wall of hands. The snow was falling upward everywhere now, streaming past us toward the bruised sky as if the country were exhaling itself, and the light was wrong — a dim green-grey twilight at what should have been midmorning.

And the streets were full.

Not soldiers. Citizens. Hundreds of them, thousands, wrapped in every scrap of wool they owned, standing in the falling-upward snow with their faces turned toward the Bastion, and as we stumbled out of the gate they turned toward *us*, and I braced for fury — we'd broken their ritual, we'd cracked their song, we'd—

An old woman reached out and touched my sleeve, just barely, the way you touch something holy or breakable. "It's looser," she whispered. "The song. I can hear the spaces between the notes for the first time in sixty years." Her eyes were streaming, freezing, streaming again. "Child, what did you do?"

"I refused," I said, which was the truest thing I had.

She nodded slowly, as if that explained everything, and maybe it did. Word moved through the crowd faster than we did — *she refused, she refused, the key refused* — and the crowd parted, and hands pressed things into ours as we passed: bread, mittens, a flask of something that burned going down and warmed going further, a child's knitted scarf in aggressive orange that Wren wound around my neck with the grave concentration of a man arming a knight.

"The Oathstone field," a man called from a rooftop. "It's clear! The snow won't fall there — it never has!"

"The inquisitors?" Wren shouted back.

"Gone to ground or gone to pieces!" A ragged cheer. "Go! We'll hold the city — we've had a hundred years of practice holding things!"

We ran again, out through the remembering streets, and the whole way the Oath sang overhead, torn and stuttering and *hopeful*, if a song can be hopeful — and I think, as my boots hammered the cobbles and Wren's hand found mine without either of us deciding it and Maren swore cheerfully at the ice trying to take her ankles, I think the whole city heard it too.

The song wasn't dying anymore.

It was waiting. Holding a half-finished measure like a held breath, like a valley's hum, like everything in this country had been holding its breath for a hundred years — and I knew, with the certainty of a woman who had been read aloud and refused to stay read, that the next note belonged to no prophecy at all.

Behind us, at the edge of hearing, the Lord Inquisitor's voice rose over the storm, calling his scattered order to hunt.

Ahead of us, past the last rooftops, the plain opened white and silent — the field where a king had once knelt, where a lie had been born, where a song had begun.

We ran for the middle of it.
