## 29. Where the Song Began

The winter had stopped pretending to be weather.

It came at them now like intent — snow driving sideways, the sky a low gray lid pressing down, and beneath it all the Oath's song fraying so badly that Juniper could hear whole verses simply *stop*, mid-note, like a singer struck dumb. The half-sung ritual hung over everything, an unfinished chord that made her teeth ache.

Wren ran beside her, his oath-keeper's coat streaming, one hand fisted in hers. He hadn't let go since they'd climbed out of the Bastion's burning undercroft, and Juniper had decided, somewhere around the third collapsed street, that she wasn't going to mention it.

"Left!" he shouted over the wind.

"Everything is left! The wind is left! The entire country is leaning left!"

"That's the Meridian pull. Don't fight it, lean into—"

He stumbled. She caught him. They caught each other, which was becoming a theme, and staggered down a street where the snow fell upward through a gap in the clouds and the cobbles remembered being warm — she felt it faintly through her boots, a ghost of summer sun stored in stone.

"The field," Wren gasped. "Where the king knelt. It's outside the walls. If we can reach it—"

"Why there?"

"Because—" He hesitated, and she watched him decide to stop lying to her. That was new. That was three days old, maybe four, and she was already greedy for it. "Because every plan I ever had ends somewhere else. The Bastion. The Door. Some clever arrangement where someone pays. I don't have a plan that ends at the place where it started. But if anywhere knows what the Oath wants — really wants, not what we've been told it wants — it's the ground it was spoken on."

Juniper looked back once. Behind them the Bastion rose black against the bruised sky, its singing walls cracked and howling, and on its highest balcony something moved — robed figures, inquisitors, and among them a shape she knew: the Lord Inquisitor, still as a held breath, watching them flee.

"He's letting us go," she said.

"No." Wren's jaw tightened. "He's letting the *Oath* hang. An unfinished reforging is worse than none — it makes everything desperate, everything negotiable. He wants us to come to him now. We're not going to."

They went instead through the Gate of Threshers, which stood open because its hinges had forgotten how to close, and out into the country that Vessia used to be.

* * *

The roads here didn't just remember; they *insisted*. Juniper had walked memory-roads with Maren, but Maren was gone now, her name-price paid back and her map folded into Juniper's coat, and walking without her felt like missing a step on stairs that weren't there.

The landscape replayed itself as they passed. A wheat field turned gold mid-winter, heavy heads bowing in a wind from a century ago. A village square filled briefly with market noise, laughter, the smell of bread — then emptied, the way a dream does when you look directly at it.

Wren walked faster past those.

"It hurts you," Juniper said. Not a question.

"It's worse when it's accurate." He nodded at the square, where a girl made of light had been chasing a hoop. "I trained in a town like that. Before my oath. The memories are getting stronger as the song gets weaker. The land is holding on to what it was because it's afraid of what comes next."

"What comes next," Juniper said, "is what everyone keeps deciding for everyone else."

"And you've got a better idea?"

"I've got a *worse* idea. Mine involves nobody sacrificing anybody." She pulled the map fragment from her coat. Maren's last gift: a scrap of vellum with the Salt Meridian crossed off in fading ink and, beyond it, a small careful drawing of a field, a kneeling figure, and words in Maren's cramped hand: *Where the song began. Ask it what it actually said.*

The storm thickened. The upward snow whirled around them, and through it, faintly, Juniper heard the Oath — or what was left of it. She'd been humming to it for days now, testing, and it answered her the way a drowning man answers a rope: desperately, gratefully, wrong. Half-sung, it reached for anything. Her unmagical voice was the only rope in the world it could find.

"It's calling me," she said quietly. "Not the prophecy. The actual song. Can you hear it?"

Wren went still. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he didn't brush it off. "I hear it every day of my life. It's the first sound I remember after my family—" He stopped. Started again, honest, which still cost him visibly. "I hear grief. What do you hear?"

Juniper listened properly this time, standing in the middle of a road that remembered wheat, with the wind trying to carry her away and the unfinished chord grinding against the sky.

She heard grief. But underneath the grief, so deep most people would never dig to it, she heard something else.

"A question," she said slowly. "Wren. The song isn't a lament. It's a *question*. It's been asking the same thing for a hundred years and nobody's answered it, so it just keeps asking louder and louder until the whole country freezes."

"What question?"

"I don't know yet. But I think we're about to stand where someone failed to answer it the first time."

* * *

They crested the last hill at dusk, though dusk had stopped meaning much in a sky like a closed fist, and there it was.

The Oathstone field.

It was smaller than she expected. History always was. A shallow bowl of land ringed by low hills, and in its center a single standing stone — not grand, not carved with kings' faces, just a gray boulder the size of a cart horse, worn smooth on one side as if by centuries of hands. Around it, in a wide circle, grew grass. Real grass. Unfrozen, stubborn, green-gray grass, the only living thing Juniper had seen in Vessia that wasn't a memory.

The snow did not fall there. It fell *around* the circle, curving away politely, like water around a stone in a stream.

"Oh," Juniper said.

"Yeah," Wren said.

They descended into the bowl together. At the edge of the circle, Wren stopped, and Juniper saw him do something she'd never seen: he removed his oath-keeper's badge — the iron sigil he'd worn since he was fourteen — and set it on the frozen ground. Gently. Like laying down something heavy rather than discarding something precious.

"You don't have to—" she started.

"My oath was to save Vessia at any cost," he said. "Any cost. I've been reciting that since before I understood what costs were." He stepped into the circle. "In here, I want to be just a man who'd like his home back. Is that allowed?"

"Ask the stone."

The stone, naturally, said nothing. But the song changed. As they crossed the grass line, the fraying chord steadied — barely, like a candle flame cupped from wind — and Juniper felt it lean toward her, the way a cold person leans toward a fire, and she realized with a lurch that she'd felt this exact sensation before.

In the Hollow. Pressing against her skin like a held breath. Magic wanting in, and unable to follow.

Except now she was on the other side of the hedge. Now she was the warmth the cold thing wanted.

"Wren." Her voice shook. "When the king knelt here. What exactly did he swear?"

Wren frowned. "Loyalty. Service. His blood to the land and the land's keeping to him. Every schoolchild learns it."

"Those are the *words*. What did he *say*?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. And Juniper watched the truth arrive in his face the way weather arrives over hills — gradually, then all at once.

"He said..." Wren's voice dropped, wondering. "He said *we*. I've read the transcript a hundred times. The formal record says loyalty, service, blood. But the eyewitness accounts — the ones the council dismissed as rustic embellishment — they all agree. He didn't speak to the land. He spoke *as* the land. 'We will keep each other.' Not 'I swear and thou art bound.' He said *we*."

"We will keep each other," Juniper repeated.

And the Oath — the broken, grieving, century-old Oath — went quiet.

Not silent the way silence falls after a bell. Quiet the way a room goes when someone finally says the thing everyone has been thinking. The storm above them stilled. The upward snow hung suspended, thousands of flakes paused mid-ascent, glittering like a ceiling of stars.

Then the song resumed, and it was not loud, and it was not desperate, and it was not a lament.

It asked its question.

Juniper heard it clearly now, heard it with the part of her that the Hollow had raised immune to magic and therefore, somehow, perfectly tuned to hear it plain. The song said, in the wordless way songs say things:

*A hundred years I have kept my half. Who will keep theirs?*

She sank down onto the frozen ground inside the circle, grass prickling through, and laughed — a wet, disbelieving laugh that steamed in the cold.

"It's not broken," she said. "Wren, it's not even angry. It's been *waiting*. A hundred years of winter and it's just — it's a promise that hasn't been answered. Everyone thought the king broke it. Even the ghosts think he broke it. But he didn't break it. He was tricked, yes, but more than that — he spoke a *different oath* than the one they wrote down. He promised partnership and they recorded ownership. The song has been singing his real words this whole time, waiting for someone to answer *those* words, and instead everyone who came here tried to answer the fake ones. Sacrifice. Binding. Martyrdom. No wonder it froze. You can't answer 'we will keep each other' with 'I will give myself up.'"

Wren had gone very pale. "The Door of Return."

"—is the mercy option for answering the wrong question," Juniper finished. "And the reforging — binding me forever — is the other wrong answer. The inquisitors have spent a century enforcing a transcription error." She pressed her palm flat against the frozen ground. Beneath the frost, faintly, she felt it: the hum. Not the Hollow's hum. Vessia's own. The land, alive, patient, asking.

"But why does it need anyone?" Wren asked. He crouched beside her, and his hand found hers without either of them deciding it. "If the true oath was spoken, why did the answer fail? Why does the song need *you*?"

Juniper stared at the stone. The smooth side faced them, worn by hands, and she thought of her mistress's herb garden, of the valley that magic couldn't enter, of a sealed ledger with her name in it written a hundred years ago by a hedge-witch's hand.

"Because everyone who could have answered was inside the story," she said slowly, feeling the shape of it as she said it, the way you feel the shape of a key by touch in the dark. "Every mage, every keeper, every inquisitor, every king — they were all born into the Oath, shaped by it, bound before they could walk. When they sing to it, they're not answering. They're echoing. The song asks *who will keep their half freely*, and a bound person saying 'freely' is still a contradiction. The song can taste the difference." She looked up at him. "It needs someone the story never got its hooks into. Someone who owes it nothing. Someone who can walk away."

"That's why the prophecy named a hedge-born key. From outside magic."

"That's why it named *me*." She laughed again, shakier. "Not because I'm special. Because I'm *optional*. Wren, do you understand? Every version of this that anyone offered me — door, anchor, martyr — required me to be trapped. But a free answer can't be forced. It can't even be *asked for*, properly. It can only be given."

The suspended snow began, very slowly, to fall again — upward, downward, both, directionless, as if the sky no longer knew what rules applied. The song swelled, gently, and Juniper felt it turn toward her like a vast blind face turning toward a small warm light.

It waited.

Her heart hammered. "It's listening," she whispered. "Right now. It's asking me."

"Then answer it," Wren said softly. "Or don't. That's rather the point."

She almost laughed at that — him, the boy who'd sworn any-cost oaths, handing her the choice like it was nothing. Like it was everything.

"Not yet." She stood, legs unsteady, and faced the way they'd come. Far off, across the darkening fields, the Bastion's towers were visible, and on the highest balcony lights were moving. Lanterns. Many lanterns, descending.

"They know where we are," Wren said grimly, following her gaze. "Of course they do. The unfinished ritual points to her like a compass needle. They'll come to finish it here — force the answer at the very spot where the Oath was spoken. Poetic, for inquisitors."

"How long?"

"Until dawn. Perhaps less."

Juniper looked at the stone, at the stubborn green grass, at the sky full of confused snow, and felt something settle in her chest that had been restless her entire life — the ache of belonging somewhere that was never meant for you, transmuting at last into the possibility of belonging *exactly* where you chose.

"Dawn, then," she said. "We hold the field until dawn. And when they come with their chains and their rituals and their transcription errors—"

The song surged through her, sudden and bright, and to her astonishment she found she knew exactly what she was going to say. What she was going to *sing*. A verse that had never been written, in a voice that had never been asked, carrying a promise no king had ever made:

Not obedience. Partnership.

"They're going to try to make me answer the wrong question one more time," Juniper said, and smiled — the first real smile since the Hollow, wild and calm at once, "and I'm going to sing them a different verse entirely."

Far across the fields, the lanterns began to move faster.
