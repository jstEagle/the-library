## 30. The Inquisitor's Last Argument

The storm had followed them from the Oathstone field, and now it followed them into the Bastion's great hall, rattling the high windows like a beggar demanding entry.

Juniper stood in the center of it — the hall, not the storm, though at this point she wasn't sure there was much difference. Frost climbed the pillars in patterns that almost looked like writing. Oath-keepers lined the walls with their spears grounded and their faces carefully blank, and above them all, on a dais of black ice that no one had bothered to explain, sat the Lord Inquisitor.

He was smaller than she'd expected. That was the first unfair thing about him. She had built him, over these long frozen weeks, into something enormous — a tower of a man, a glacier with a voice. Instead he was spare and gray and neat as an accountant, with folded hands and eyes the color of a winter sky just before it decides to snow. He looked like someone's patient grandfather. He looked like he would remember your birthday.

He looked like he had already counted every way this conversation could go, and had liked none of them, and had proceeded anyway.

"Juniper Hartwell," he said. His voice was quiet in a way that made everyone lean in to hear it, which was, she suspected, exactly the point. "Of the Hollow. Hedge-born. Unwritten." He unfolded his hands. "You have caused my order a great deal of trouble."

"I've been told I'm a fast learner."

Someone along the wall made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been a laugh strangled at birth. The Lord Inquisitor did not smile, but something around his mouth acknowledged the attempt, the way one acknowledges weather.

"Where is Wren?" she asked, before he could arrange whatever he'd arranged to say next. Her heart was going like a fist on a door. They had taken him at the Meridian. They had dragged her here in chains of ordinary iron because, the guards had muttered, nothing else would hold her — which she chose to find flattering. "If you've hurt him—"

"He is unharmed." The Inquisitor gestured, and a door at the far end of the hall opened, and Wren walked through it.

Her whole body forgot what it was doing for a moment. He looked tired. He looked bruised along one jaw. But he looked *whole*, and he looked at her the way people look at fires in winter, and then — deliberately, she saw him do it — he looked away. He walked to the side of the hall, stopped beneath the frost-written pillar, and folded his hands behind his back like a soldier at review.

Not beside her. Not across from her. *Aside.*

She understood, and it cost her something sharp to understand: he was refusing to stand anywhere that could be read as pressure. This was hers. Whatever came next, it was hers alone, and he would not even let his silence point in a direction.

"Sit," said the Lord Inquisitor, and a chair appeared — carried forward by two silent acolytes, plain wooden, set down in the exact center of the hall facing the dais. She didn't sit. He nodded as if she hadn't, as if the refusal were also a data point he'd predicted and filed.

"You are wondering why you are alive," he said. "You crossed the Salt Meridian. You touched the Door of Return — yes, we know; the Door tells us things, it is one of the few honest parties left in this country. And then you turned your back on it and walked back into our hands to bargain for an oath-keeper's life." He tilted his head. "Sentiment. Inefficient. And yet." He rose, at last, and descended the steps of the black-ice dais with the unhurried care of a man whose knees had opinions. "And yet here we all are. So before we proceed to the unpleasant portion of the evening, I am required by custom — and, frankly, by curiosity — to offer you a third path."

"There were only supposed to be two," Juniper said. "The Door, or the reforging."

"Yes. Two paths written by a hedge-witch a hundred years dead who thought two was generous." Something moved behind his eyes then, brief and cold and old. "I have spent forty years studying that ledger, Miss Hartwell. Do you know what I found in its margins? Nothing. No third option. No mercy clause. A prophecy is a cage with the bars drawn in ink, and I have hated it my entire life — which is why it gives me no pleasure whatsoever to inform you that I intend to offer you a cage of my own. A better one. Gilded. Roomy." He stopped three paces from her chair. "Rule Vessia."

The word went through the hall like a crack through ice.

"Excuse me?" Juniper said.

"The Oath requires an anchor. It always has. A king knelt once and swore, and when the oath broke, the land began to die — because a promise needs somewhere to *live*, Miss Hartwell. In a throat. In a chest. The inquisitors feed on oaths; we know their anatomy better than anyone breathing. And we have learned — expensively, tragically — that the Oath cannot simply be handed a new king. Kings are made of politics, and politics rots. What the song wants—" he spread his neat hands, "—is a *keystone*. Someone outside the story. Someone magic cannot touch, cannot twist, cannot corrupt. You are the only person born in a century who fits the specification."

"So your plan is to chain me to your country forever and call it a job offer."

"To *ask* you. Which is more than the prophecy ever did." He began to walk, slowly, a small gray figure pacing between the frost pillars, and the hall leaned in again. "Consider what I am offering. Not the Door — you have seen what waits past the Meridian. White silence. Erasure dressed up as rest. Your country would forget you ever drew breath, and you would forget it back. Not the reforging, either — the ritual the council planned. That binds the singer to the song until the song ends or she does, whichever comes first, and the song is very old and very stubborn. But anchoring—" he turned, and his voice softened into something almost kind, "—anchoring is different. The land would live *in* you rather than you dying into it. You would be Vessia's living heart. Every hearth lit would warm you. Every spring, when it comes — and it will come, under you — every green thing breaking soil would be yours. Children would learn your name before they learned their own. There would be statues, eventually. I give you fair warning: the statues will be terrible."

A ripple along the walls. Almost, almost laughter, killed quickly.

"And the alternative," Juniper said quietly, "is what you'll do if I say no."

"The alternative," the Inquisitor agreed, without flinching, "is that we complete the interrupted reforging by force, tonight, while the Oath hangs half-sung and unstable, and you become the anchor anyway — screaming, unwilling, and broken, and the country gets a mad ghost for a heartbeat instead of a queen. Or the Oath collapses entirely, the winter finishes its work, and Vessia dies with four hundred thousand souls inside it who never asked to be part of anyone's story." He clasped his hands again. "I do not enjoy presenting terms this way. I want you to understand that. But I have buried too many of my own people to pretend that your comfort is the largest thing at stake in this room."

It was a good speech. That was the horrible thing. It was a *good* speech, and most of it was probably even true, and Juniper stood in the middle of the hall with the storm hammering the windows and felt the trap close around her with a click so gentle it sounded like affection.

Because here was the shape of it: say yes, and she lived, glorified, worshipped, kept — a bird in a cathedral, adored and never again alone, never again free, never again *Juniper Hartwell of nowhere in particular*. Say no, and they took the choice out of her hands entirely and she got the worst version of everything. He had built the offer so that refusing it felt like vanity. Like pride. Like choosing her own small freedom over four hundred thousand lives.

And Wren stood by the pillar with his hands behind his back and said nothing.

She looked at him. Just once. He was already looking at the floor, jaw tight, doing the hardest thing she had ever watched anyone do — harder than holding a line against inquisitors, harder than getting captured. He was letting her walk toward the edge without reaching for her, because reaching for her was how they'd all been controlled from the beginning. The prophecy had reached. The Bastion had reached. Maren had paid a price to hand her back her own name, and even that had been a gift given freely, without strings, the only gift in this entire frozen country that hadn't come with a hook in it.

*Freely,* Maren had said, pressing the map fragment into her hand with bloodied fingers. *That's the whole trick of it, girl. Everything freely given works. Everything taken doesn't. Write that down.*

Juniper breathed in. The air tasted of iron and candle smoke and cold stone, and underneath it, faint as a pulse through a wall, the Broken Oath was still singing — half its notes missing since the interrupted ritual, limping along like a three-legged dog, but singing. Waiting to hear what its keystone would say.

"May I ask a question?" she said.

"You may ask anything," said the Lord Inquisitor. "I will answer truly. It is the one luxury of being old: you can afford honesty."

"When your order was founded — on the lie you haven't admitted to yet, though Wren dug it out of your council ghosts and sang it through your own walls, so don't bother pretending — when your founders wrote themselves into power over a grieving country, did any of them ever stop to wonder whether the land wanted a keeper at all?"

The silence had texture. Several oath-keepers along the wall shifted, and one — she saw it clearly — looked at the Lord Inquisitor and did not look away again.

"The land," the Inquisitor said at last, evenly, "does not want. That is the first lesson of inquisitory training. Land *endures*. People want. People grieve and hope and make promises, and the land simply holds still underneath them like a floor. My order exists to keep that floor from giving way."

"That's your last argument? The floor?"

"It is a strong argument."

"It's a rug," Juniper said, and heard her own voice come out steady, and was grateful to the Hollow for that — for a hundred years of a valley that hummed and held its breath and taught her that the ground itself has opinions. "You've been standing on it so long you've forgotten it's alive."

She stepped away from the empty chair. Toward the dais, actually, toward him, because some stubborn part of her refused to deliver even her refusal from the spot he'd assigned.

"Here's what I notice about your third path," she said. "The Door offers forgetting. The reforging offers sacrifice. And you — you're offering worship. Three cages, and in every single one, the door locks from the outside. Did it never occur to you — any of you — to build a door that locks from the *inside*? A promise somebody can keep because they choose it every morning, not because they're chained to it? Because that's the only kind of promise worth anything. Ask any married couple. Ask any friend. A vow you can't leave isn't a vow. It's a hostage situation with nice stationery."

"Poetry," said the Inquisitor. "The country cannot run on poetry."

"It ran on a broken oath for a hundred years," Juniper said. "Poetry is the *upgrade*."

And there — there, at last — she saw it: the flicker in his winter-sky eyes, quick as a fish under ice. Not anger. Something worse for him. Recognition. He had studied that ledger for forty years, and somewhere in those forty years a small gray boy had read the margins and found nothing, and had sworn *then*, quietly, privately, that no one would ever be caged by ink again — and had grown up into the man who built cages anyway, because the winter gave him no other materials.

"You will refuse," he said. It was not quite a question.

"I'm going to refuse so hard," Juniper said, "that the word 'refuse' is going to need a holiday afterward."

This time the laugh escaped — one young oath-keeper, third from the door, a snort he immediately disguised as a sneeze, badly. The Lord Inquisitor closed his eyes briefly, like a man praying for patience or remembering spring.

"For the record," he said, opening them again, "the offer stands until midnight. After that, the council votes, and the ritual proceeds with or without your cooperation. Sentiment is lovely, Miss Hartwell. Midnight does not share it."

"Then I guess we both know what happens at midnight."

"We do." He inclined his head — formally, precisely, the bow of one chess player to another. Then, quieter, almost beneath hearing, meant for her alone: "For what it is worth, I hoped you would say no. I have hoped it since the Meridian. I simply could not say so where the council could hear." He straightened. "Guards. Return the prisoners to the south cells. Separately."

"No," Juniper said.

Every spear in the hall lifted six inches.

"She stays with me," Wren said, from the pillar, and his voice cracked the room open. He hadn't moved. His hands were still behind his back. But he was looking at the Lord Inquisitor now, and the look had a hundred years of oath in it. "You offered her the choice. All of it, hers. Then honor the smallest piece of it: she chooses who stands with her tonight. That's the whole test, isn't it? Agency. Either it's real or it isn't."

The Lord Inquisitor regarded him for a long, cold moment. Then, to Juniper's astonishment, the corner of his mouth moved — not quite a smile, but the fossil of one.

"Oath-keeper Wren," he said, "you are either the worst traitor this order has produced or the finest argument for treason I have ever encountered. I genuinely cannot decide, and I have decided many difficult things." He turned to the guards. "They stay together. South cells. If either of them sings anything at the walls, I want to know instantly — and I mean *anything*."

As they were led away, past the frost pillars, past the terrible dais, Wren fell into step beside her, close enough that their shoulders brushed, and said nothing, because there was nothing yet that words wouldn't spoil. But at the door Juniper glanced back once, and found the Lord Inquisitor watching them go with his folded hands and his accountant's face, and thought — absurdly, wildly — that he looked less like a jailer than like a man standing on a shore, watching a boat he'd helped build sail toward water he was afraid of.

Midnight was four hours away.

Somewhere under the Bastion, the Oath was waiting to hear its new verse.

And Juniper Hartwell, walking down into the dark with Wren's shoulder warm against hers, began — very quietly, so the guards wouldn't hear, so maybe even he wouldn't hear — to hum.
