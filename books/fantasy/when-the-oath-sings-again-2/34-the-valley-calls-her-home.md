## 34. The Valley Calls Her Home

The Door had been open for three days.

That was the strange part — not that it stood there in the middle of the meadow where the Oathstone field used to be, but that it *stayed*. Juniper had expected it to vanish once the land answered the new verse, the way a fever breaks and leaves nothing behind but sweat and shaking. Instead it simply remained: a plain farmhouse door with iron hinges and a latch worn smooth by generations of hands, standing upright in the grass with no wall around it and no reason at all.

Snow no longer fell upward here. That was the other strange thing. Winter was retreating in ribbons, as if someone had pulled threads out of a tapestry, and where the white went, green followed — not all at once, not everywhere, but in veins and patches, like ink soaking through paper. The meadow itself was still half-snowed, half-grassed, so that walking across it felt like walking between two seasons who hadn't yet agreed on anything.

Juniper came every morning. She told herself she was studying it. Documenting it. Maren would have wanted documentation — Maren, who was buried under a cairn of river stones on the hill above the Salt Meridian because Juniper couldn't bear to leave her anywhere the inquisitors' ashes might drift. Maren would have mapped this Door within an hour of its appearing, labeled it in her sharp little script, and charged someone an outrageous fee just to look at her map.

So Juniper documented. She sat cross-legged in the grass ten feet from the Door — never closer, not yet — with a borrowed notebook and Wren's spare pencil, and she wrote things like *Day three. Door remains. Hinges rusted on left side. Latch cold to touch.* And then, in smaller letters, squeezed against the margin where she hoped her own eyes wouldn't linger: *It's still open.*

It was. The door wasn't shut. It stood open perhaps four inches, a dark seam in the bright morning, and through that seam came a smell that made her chest ache every single time.

Thyme. Crushed thyme and woodsmoke and the particular green smell of the Hollow after rain, which was not like any other green smell in any world, because the Hollow's anti-magic hum pressed everything down into itself until even the herbs grew dense and stubborn and utterly, defiantly ordinary.

Through the seam she could see a slice of the garden. Just a slice — a vertical band maybe six inches wide — but it was unmistakably Mistress Ellery's herb garden, the one Juniper had weeded every morning of her life until a ledger in a dusty shop had folded the world around her like a letter being sealed. She could see the low stone wall. A corner of the beehive. The crooked beanpole that Ellery kept meaning to replace and never did, because, she claimed, the beans liked it better than they'd ever like anything straight.

And beneath it all, faint as a pulse felt through a wrist, came the hum.

*Hmmmmmm,* said the valley, in the voice of held breath. The sound of nowhere magic could follow. The sound of home, if home could be something you were kept in rather than something you chose.

Juniper sat in the grass with her notebook and listened to two countries breathe at once — Vessia waking green behind her, the Hollow humming patiently ahead — and understood that she had walked three thousand miles, sung a broken oath whole, refused a godhood, and unmade a century of winter, and now the hardest choice of her entire life was standing four inches open in front of her, smelling of thyme.

She hated how small it looked.

"You're talking to it again."

She didn't jump. She'd stopped jumping at Wren about a week after he stopped arresting her, which was, she suspected, roughly the schedule such things ran on. He came up the meadow path carrying two cups of something hot — Vessians drank a barley infusion they insisted on calling tea, and Juniper had given up correcting them around the time she'd given up correcting Wren's habit of saying "we" when he meant "I" — and he settled into the grass beside her with the careful ease of a man who had learned, over one long impossible year, exactly how close to sit.

"I'm documenting it," she said.

"Of course."

"It's important work. Countries don't just grow doors."

"No," Wren agreed solemnly. "They grow them very rarely. Usually only after being sung back together by a hedge-born key with a voice like a kitchen argument."

"My voice does not sound like a kitchen argument."

"It sounds like two kitchens arguing." He handed her a cup. "About whose bread is better."

Juniper took the cup mostly so she'd have something to do with her hands. The tea was terrible and warm and she'd have fought anyone who tried to take it from her. For a while they sat in the kind of silence that had gotten easier and easier to keep — the silence of people who had argued through cell bars about who got to be sacrificed for whom, and had therefore used up most of their capacity for pretending.

Through the seam in the Door, the beanpole swayed in a wind she couldn't feel.

"She's probably furious," Juniper said finally. "Mistress Ellery. I've been gone—" she counted, and lost count, and gave up. "A long time. I left mid-chore. I left the comfrey unmulched. She'll have opinions about the comfrey."

"She sounds like a woman with strong feelings about mulch."

"She once made a delivery boy wait forty minutes because he set down a crate of valerian root too near the rue and she said they 'disagreed.' They're plants, Wren. Plants can't disagree."

"Vessia was nearly destroyed by an oath disagreeing with a king," Wren said. "I've learned not to underestimate what things can feel."

That shut her up in the way his quiet observations always did — not wounded, just rearranged. She drank her terrible tea and watched the garden through the gap, and the hum pressed gently against her skin like a held breath, the way it always had, the way she'd spent her whole childhood believing was simply what the world sounded like.

"Do you know what I thought, the first night I heard the Broken Oath?" she said. "In the Bastion cell. I thought — oh. *Oh.* So this is what it's like when a place wants you. Because the valley never wanted anything. It just held its breath. Forever. I used to think the hum was the valley holding me, keeping me safe." She turned the cup in her hands. "Now I think it was holding its breath because it was afraid I'd leave."

"And did you?"

"Obviously. Look at me."

"No," Wren said. "I mean — in here." He tapped his own chest, once, awkwardly, the gesture of a man raised on oaths and protocols trying to say something without a single formal word available. "Did you ever actually leave? You've been here a year and you still mulch things. You still talk to the greenhouse cuttings like they're apprentices. You alphabetized the Bastion's seed archive *without being asked.*"

"The seed archive was a disaster—"

"It was a *war crime*, Juniper, the archivist cried—"

"—and somebody had to fix it," she finished, and they were both smiling, and the smile hurt a little, the way good things sometimes do when you're looking straight at the thing you might lose.

Because that was the shape of the morning, under everything else. Through the Door: thyme and hum and the crooked beanpole and a mistress who had known, apparently, all along — whose hand had written the prophecy a hundred years before Juniper Hartwell was born, *hedge-born key, such-and-such date*, and had then dusted her shop and watered her herbs and let a girl grow up ordinary right up until the exact minute the ink moved. Through the Door: a life where nobody sang to her, nobody needed her voice, nobody had ever once asked her to hold a country together. A life that was small and safe and smelled like crushed thyme, and had never once been enough, and had also, she was beginning to understand, never once been the problem.

Behind her: a country learning its own name again. Children in the rebuilt quarter who called her "the singer" and demanded verses. Wren, teaching the new oath in a schoolroom with a broken window he refused to have fixed because, he said, the draft reminded him to stay honest. Green coming up in ribbons. Terrible barley tea. Home, possibly, though she hadn't dared use that word yet, even inside her own head, because words like that were doors too, and hers had already been opened by prophecy once and she'd sworn no book, no ghost, no god, no *anyone* would choose for her again.

The Door stood open four inches. Waiting. Not demanding — the land had learned that lesson along with everyone else; that had rather been the point of the whole new verse. But waiting. Patient as a valley.

"Wren," she said.

"Mm."

"If I went through. If I went back to the Hollow." She kept her eyes on the seam, on the beanpole, because she could not have said the next part to his face. "What would you do?"

He was quiet long enough that she finally made herself look at him. He wasn't watching the Door at all. He was watching her, with that expression she'd catalogued over a year of interrogations and escapes and campfires and arguments through bars — the one that meant he was refusing to give her an easy answer out of respect, which was either the most annoying or the most loving thing about him depending on the hour.

"That's not my question to answer," he said.

"It's a hypothetical."

"It's your life." He set down his cup. "Juniper. When the Lord Inquisitor offered you the third path — rule us, be our anchor, be worshipped — I stayed silent. You were angry about that for a month. You said I abandoned you to the choosing."

"I remember."

"I stayed silent because I meant it. Whatever you chose, I would have lived beside it. If you'd taken the crown, I'd have knelt. If you'd gone through that Door, I'd have—" his jaw worked, "—I'd have taught your verse and swept the schoolroom and missed you in a way that would have made the old winter look sociable. But I would not have asked you to stay. Asking is a chain with soft links. I swore an oath about chains once. I've become opinionated on the subject."

"So you won't ask."

"I won't ask."

"Not even a little?"

"Not even a little." And then — because he was Wren, and honesty between them had been earned the hard way, burned off like fog in chapter after chapter of their lives — he added, quietly: "But I'll tell you something freely, since telling is allowed. The Door is offering you your old life. That's real, and I won't cheapen it by pretending otherwise. But the old life is not the only life that smells like thyme." He nodded toward the town below, where the greenhouse they'd built together last spring caught the light. "We planted comfrey in the east bed. I'm told it disagrees with nothing, if you keep it away from the rue. I checked. I wrote to a herbalist in Tarrowmere. I wrote *three letters*."

Juniper stared at him. "You researched comfrey."

"I researched comfrey *thoroughly*."

"For me."

"For the east bed," said Wren, with tremendous dignity, and drank his tea, and went slightly pink at the ears, and Juniper laughed — really laughed, the laugh he'd told her once he'd remembered in a cell to keep himself alive — and for a moment the Door, the hum, the ache, all of it receded behind the simple ridiculous warmth of a man who had written three letters about herbs because she might stay.

* * *

She went closer on the fourth day.

Not through — closer. Close enough to touch the doorframe, which was oak gone silver with age, and to see that the hinges were not rusted after all but polished, recently, deliberately, by a hand that cared for things. Ellery's hand, almost certainly. Somewhere beyond that seam a witch was oiling a door she'd built a century ago, keeping it ready, keeping it *kind* — because that was the thing Juniper finally saw, standing there with her palm flat on the wood, and it undid her a little:

The Door wasn't a trap. It wasn't even a test. Prophecies tested; oaths tested; the Lord Inquisitor had tested. This was just a door, propped open by someone who loved her, saying *whenever you're ready, or never, both are fine, mind the step.*

Through the gap: morning in the Hollow. Mist on the garden. And — she leaned closer, heart hammering — movement. A figure in the bean rows, gray-haired, sleeves rolled, doing something to the beans that was probably illegal in three agricultural traditions. As Juniper watched, Mistress Ellery straightened up, pressed her lower back with both hands in the universal gesture of the aging gardener, and turned her head directly toward the Door. Toward the four-inch gap. Toward her.

For one wild moment their eyes met across the seam of two worlds.

Ellery didn't wave. She didn't call out. She did something far worse, far kinder: she smiled — the dry, knowing, insufferable smile of a woman who had written *her name in a ledger a hundred years ago* — and then she pointed, slowly and deliberately, at the crooked beanpole.

Then at the ground beside it.

Then she mimed, with exaggerated patience, the act of staking a bean.

*Come fix the beans whenever you like. Or send instructions. Either way, the beans are your problem forever.* It was the least pressuring message ever sent across the boundary of existence, and it hit Juniper harder than any prophecy ever had, because it was the first summons in her whole life that came with the door left open in both directions.

She stepped back from the Door. Her legs were shaking.

* * *

She found Wren at dusk on the schoolhouse steps, marking exercises by lantern light, because the man had resigned a sacred post to teach children and still approached lesson plans like military campaigns. She sat down beside him without asking, which was its own small vow, and he shifted the lantern so it lit her page too, which was his.

"I touched it today," she said.

"The Door."

"The frame. And I saw Ellery. She — Wren, she *staked beans at me.*"

"An invitation to garden," he said gravely. "From a woman who writes prophecies. That's practically a coronation."

"It's practically *home*," Juniper said, and heard the word come out of her mouth and land softly in the space between them, and realized it hadn't referred to the Hollow at all. She looked at the lantern, at his ink-stained fingers, at the broken window letting in the evening air he refused to fix. "Both of them are. That's the horrible wonderful thing. There isn't one door. There never was. There's just... me, deciding, and both places leaving the light on."

Wren said nothing. He was very good at that — at silence as a form of respect. But he turned his hand palm-up on the step between them, an offer, not a request, and she put hers in it, and they sat like that while the first honest stars of spring came out over a country that belonged to no prophecy at all.

Tomorrow she would walk to the meadow. Tomorrow she would stand at the Door with her true name in her mouth like a coin she could spend either way. Tonight she only knew this: whatever she chose, she would choose it standing up, with her eyes open, singing her own verse — and somewhere behind her, patient as spring, a man who never asked would be keeping the tea warm.

Above the schoolhouse, high past the rooftops, the evening sky did something none of them had seen before.

The stars flickered — all of them, once, together — and in the sudden hush Juniper heard, faint and clear and impossibly far away, a second hum answering the first. Not the Hollow's held breath. Something older, farther, stranger — a valley she'd never known, singing back.
