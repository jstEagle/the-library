## 36. After the Long Winter

The first thing Wren learned about peace was that it was loud.

For a hundred years Vessia had been quiet in the way that graves are quiet — the hush of snow falling upward, the Oath singing its grief through the stones, citizens drifting through their days like half-remembered melodies. He had grown up inside that silence. He had sworn his oath into it at fourteen, kneeling on frozen stone with his breath clouding and his family's names still fresh enough to hurt. He had believed, then, that silence was the natural condition of things, and that his whole life's work was to keep the quiet from becoming total.

Now the country would not stop talking.

It started at dawn, which no longer arrived grey and apologetic but gold and insistent. Somewhere below his window in the rebuilt quarter of the Bastion — they had argued for months about what to do with the building, and eventually decided that a fortress made a fine school if you removed the bars and let light in — a rooster crowed. An actual rooster. Wren lay in bed and listened to it with the expression of a man being personally insulted.

Under the rooster: cart wheels on cobbles, real cobbles now, the frost-heaved ones pried up and relaid by crews who sang while they worked because apparently nobody had told them singing was supposed to be reserved for grief. Under the carts: children, dozens of them, running toward the schoolyard with the particular shrieking joy of children who had spent their formative years as memories and were making up for lost time. And under everything, so faint you had to be still to hear it, the Oath — the new verse of it, Juniper's verse — humming through the foundations of every building in Vessia like a hearth-fire that never quite went out.

*We are the land and the land is ours,* it went. *Not owned but kept. Not ruled but held.*

Wren had heard the old verse every day of his life. It had been beautiful the way winter is beautiful, which is to say from a distance. The new one was beautiful the way bread is beautiful — ordinary, warm, necessary. He was not sure he would ever get used to it. He was fairly sure he didn't want to.

He got up. His knee ached where an inquisitor's chain had caught him during the escape, and his shoulder ached where a wall had nearly fallen on him during the riot, and he catalogued both aches with something close to fondness. Painful mornings meant he had survived to have them. For thirty years he had woken without pain and without much else either.

Downstairs, the corridor smelled of paint and sawdust and, incongruously, baking bread. The Bastion kitchens — built to feed garrisons — now turned out loaves for the neighborhood every morning, because the council had voted that a building which once held people against their will ought to start its penance by feeding them freely. Wren took two rolls off the cooling rack when the baker wasn't looking. She saw him. She always saw him. She let it go, which was her way of saying she remembered what he'd been, and forgave what he'd been, and would absolutely dock his wages if he did it again.

Outside, spring had committed fully. The Salt Meridian, that white silence past the edge of every map, had gone green in ribbons — Maren Quill would have loved to map it, and someone probably would, someday, with better instruments and less terrible prices. The upward snow had long since righted itself. Trees that had stood frozen mid-gesture for a century were finishing their gestures now, buds unclenching like fists opening after too long held shut.

Wren walked to the old oath-keeper's post at the edge of the square, where he had reported every morning for fifteen years, and stood before it for the last time.

The desk was the same scarred oak. The ledger of assignments sat open on it, waiting for his signature. Behind it hung the badge of office — a small iron circle, the symbol of the vow that had once been a cage and was now, thanks to a hedge-born girl from a valley where magic couldn't follow, simply a piece of history.

Commander Ilsa Vey was already there, arms crossed, watching him with the patience of a woman who had buried more friends than she could count and had decided, somewhere along the way, to be done with ceremony.

"You're certain," she said. Not a question, really. She'd known since the riot, probably. Since the cells. Maybe since the shrine, all those months ago, when an oath-keeper chose treason disguised as pragmatism and found, to his surprise, that it fit him better than duty ever had.

"I'm certain."

"The council could refuse your resignation. You're a hero of the Remembering. They'll want you in uniform for the anniversary."

"They can want," Wren said. "I've spent my whole life doing what Vessia wanted." He touched the ledger but didn't sign it yet. "There's something else I'm meant to do."

"Teaching." Her mouth twitched. "Juniper Hartwell's doing?"

"Juniper Hartwell suggested it. Loudly. Over dinner. Three nights running." He allowed himself the smile that had become easier this year than in all the decades before combined. "She said I explain things well when I stop pretending I don't care about them."

"She's not wrong." Commander Vey picked up the iron circle from its hook and weighed it in her palm. "You know what I keep thinking? When we were young, they taught us the Oath was a wall. Something you defended or died behind. And this girl walks in from nowhere, hums at it, and turns out it was a door the whole time."

"A song, actually."

"Don't correct me, Wren, I'm grieving my worldview." She pressed the iron circle into his hand and closed his fingers over it. "Keep it. Not as a badge. As a lesson. Every teacher should carry proof that the thing they were most sure of was wrong."

He tucked it into his pocket, where it sat heavy and cold and precious.

"And Wren?" Vey said, as he turned to go. "The council voted yesterday. The old verse — the king's verse, the obedience verse — it's struck from the curriculum. Your new one goes in its place. First lessons begin next week."

"Whose name is on it? As author?"

"Hers, obviously. With a note underneath." Vey's voice went dry. "*'Add your own line.'*"

* * *

The classroom had been a cell block once. You could still see it if you knew where to look — the window frames too high and too narrow, the walls thick enough to muffle screaming. But someone had knocked three cells into one room, put in real windows facing east, and lined the walls with shelves that Juniper had insisted on filling with books from every discipline, "because the point," she'd said, "is that nobody gets to tell a child what they're allowed to wonder about."

Wren stood at the front of it on the first morning with twenty-three students aged eight to fourteen, and discovered that teaching was harder than holding a line against inquisitors.

Inquisitors, at least, wanted something specific from you, and you could deny it. Children wanted *everything*, simultaneously, and immediately, and they could smell a fraud the way cats smell rain.

"So you're famous," said a girl named Tamsin, who was nine and missing a front tooth and clearly going to run the country someday whether anyone authorized it or not.

"I wouldn't say—"

"You're in the song books. My gran sings the fourth verse at you when she passes your house. *'And warmth was promised back to us,'*" Tamsin recited, "and then she curtsies, and you look like you swallowed a bee."

"That's... roughly accurate."

"My da says you arrested the author first," said a boy at the back, Pip, who collected facts the way other children collected marbles. "Is it true you let her go one street further than you should have?"

"It's true that I was slow," Wren said carefully.

"Slow on purpose," Pip insisted.

"History is complicated, Pip."

"Da says it isn't. Da says either you arrest people or you don't."

"Your da," Wren said, "sounds like a man who has never been twenty-two years old and in love with a country that was dying." He heard himself say it and stopped. Twenty heads swiveled toward him with the synchronized interest of a shoal of fish spotting bait.

Tamsin raised her hand. "Are you in love with the author?"

"I believe," Wren said, with tremendous dignity, "that today's lesson is about the difference between a promise and a cage."

"We'll come back to it," Tamsin informed her classmates, in the tone of a general postponing a battle.

They came back to it. Of course they came back to it — every day, from seventeen directions, with the tireless appetite of children who had grown up in a country where love had been a word carved into stone rather than something you watched happen across a breakfast table. Wren learned to deflect with stories instead of denials, because denials only fed them. He told them about the shrine at Coldharrow, where an oath-keeper broke his own rules. About the market of bottled summers, and the cartographer who traded in true names. About the Door of Return, plain as a farmhouse door, standing in a field that was now just a meadow.

And every story ended the same way, because every story he had was the same story underneath: that the bravest thing anyone did in the whole century of winter was done by a girl with no magic at all, who refused every path written for her and wrote a new verse instead.

"What does the valley feel like?" asked a small boy called Aldis, near midwinter's end, apropos of nothing, while the others copied lines from the board. "The Hollow. Where she's from. Where magic can't follow."

Wren considered the question honestly, because the children deserved honesty; that was rather the point of everything.

"Loud," he said finally. "But a different kind of loud. Like a held breath. Like somewhere waiting for something." He thought of the letters that arrived through the residual seam of the Door, carried on no postal route any map acknowledged — letters in a spidery hand from a hedge-witch who had written a prophecy a century ago and waited patiently for it to come true. "I've never been. She describes it as ordinary. She means it as a compliment."

Aldis nodded solemnly, as though this confirmed some private theory, and went back to his copying.

That evening Wren walked home through streets that were learning their own names again. Shopkeepers called greetings. Someone had planted crocuses in the cracks of the Bastion steps out of what he could only assume was pure spite toward the concept of fortresses. A woman was teaching her daughter the fourth verse on a doorstep, getting the words wrong, getting them wrong *warmly*, and Wren slowed to listen because the wrongness was somehow the best part — the verse belonging to them now, imperfect and alive, no longer a monument but a habit.

The Oath hummed beneath it all. *Not owned but kept.* He had spent fifteen years defending a wall and had needed a stranger to show him it was a garden.

At home — a small house with a crooked chimney that Juniper had declared "perfect" in the tone of voice she used for things she intended to renovate anyway — there was light in the kitchen window, and the smell of her attempt at Vessian stew, and the sound of her arguing with the recipe in two languages.

"Tamsin cornered me again," he announced, hanging up his coat.

"Did you tell her anything good?"

"I told her history is complicated."

"Ha!" Juniper emerged, flour-dusted, triumphant. "That'll hold her for a week, tops. Sit down. The stew is either going to be remarkable or a war crime and I need witnesses either way."

He sat. Outside, the last light went gold over a country planting itself green, and under the table his knee ached, and in his pocket the old iron badge sat next to chalk dust and a child's confiscated marble, and Wren thought: *this*. Not the climax, not the singing, not the moment the winter cracked open like ice on a river. This. Bread stolen off cooling racks. Wrong verses sung warmly on doorsteps. A stew of uncertain allegiance.

This was what they had bought, with all of it. Ordinary evenings, earned.

The rooster crowed again, absurdly, at nothing.

"Tomorrow," Juniper said, ladling stew with the confidence of a woman who had not tasted it, "you're teaching the solstice lesson. And I've had a letter from Mistress Elowen — she wants to know when we're coming through the seam for Midsummer. Apparently my old herb garden has opinions about neglect."

Wren reached across the table and took her floury hand.

"Tomorrow," he agreed — and outside, faint beneath the crickets and the cart-wheels and the shouting joy of a reborn country, the Oath hummed on, adding, somewhere in its endless patient music, another line.
