## 37. Letters Across the Hum

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Juniper would later consider very like Agatha Pinch — her old mistress had never once managed to do anything remarkable on a day that wasn't thoroughly ordinary.

She was in the garden behind the house she shared with Wren, kneeling in dirt that was still learning how to be dirt again. A year after the winter broke, Vessia's soil remained an earnest overachiever: you could plant a radish seed and it seemed to take the whole enterprise personally, straining upward as though it had something to prove. Juniper had come to love that about it. Nothing in this country did anything halfway anymore. Not the growing things, not the singing walls, not Wren when he decided the kitchen needed reorganizing at midnight.

The letter simply *was* there when she looked up — resting on the flat stone beside the bean rows, sealed with green wax pressed in the shape of a sprig of thyme.

Her hands went cold even though the morning didn't.

She knew that wax. She had licked that seal shut a hundred times as a girl, carrying messages to the far end of the Hollow for a woman who considered walking anywhere under half a mile to be a scandalous waste of knees.

*Juniper Hartwell,* said the front, in handwriting she would have known blindfolded, upside down, in the dark, or mid-earthquake. *Hollow-side post. Mind the smudge.*

There was, indeed, a smudge.

Juniper sat back on her heels and stared at it for a long moment while somewhere above her the Oath hummed its low, contented note through the stones of the wall — the sound it made now instead of grieving, like a house settling comfortably around its people.

Then she opened it.

---

*Dear Girl,*

*I have been informed by certain parties — do not ask which, the answer involves a badger and I refuse to elaborate — that letters can cross the seam where the Door used to stand, provided one is patient and the moon is being cooperative and one does not get greedy about frequency. So. Once a season, no more. I know you. If I let myself write weekly you'd drown in instructions and guilt, and I've spent a century parceling those out carefully.*

*You'll want to know how I knew. Sit down. You're sitting? Good.*

*Who do you think wrote the ledger, child?*

*Someone had to put your name in that book a hundred years before you were born, and I promise you it wasn't prophecy. Prophecy is just gardening with worse tools. I planted you the way I plant anything — late, in poor soil, against all advice — because I knew what the valley was. The Hollow doesn't hold magic out, Juniper. It holds magic *in reserve*. It's been saving up for a long time. And it raised you the way you raise a seedling in a cold frame: protected, unspoiled, so that when the time came you'd carry nothing into Vessia but yourself.*

*It turns out yourself was enough. I never doubted it. Well. I doubted it twice, but only on Tuesdays, and I didn't tell anyone.*

*Your radishes are planted too close together. This is true across worlds, apparently. Some things are simply in you.*

*Eat properly. Write back if the seam allows it. Tell me his name is Wren, because I saw it in the margins of the ledger years ago and I've been insufferably smug ever since and require confirmation.*

*— A. Pinch, who is fine, stop worrying*

Juniper read it four times. On the fourth read she laughed — a startled, wet-edged laugh that turned into something else partway through and required her to sit down properly on the stone and press her wrist against her eyes for a minute.

*A. Pinch.* In the margin of the ledger, in faded brown ink beneath the words *hedge-born key*, there had always been a small mark she'd assumed was an ink blot. She'd seen it again just last month, helping copy the text for the schoolhouse.

It was a thyme sprig.

A hundred years ago, a hedge-witch in a valley where magic couldn't follow had sat down with a blank book and written a girl into existence — or rather, had written a *door* into existence, and then gone outside to water the beans and wait a century for the girl to walk through it.

"That absolute menace," Juniper said aloud, to nobody, and meant it with her whole heart.

---

Wren took the news the way he took most news: quietly, completely, and then sideways, three days later, when she found him in the kitchen at dawn holding the letter like it might go off.

"You've memorized it," she said.

"I've *read* it."

"Twice."

"Three times." He set it down with great care, the way he handled everything he didn't understand, which included oaths, feelings, and most baked goods. "She wrote you into a prophecy. Before you were born. She watched you pull weeds for twenty years knowing—" He stopped, and Juniper watched him arrive at it, watched the thought land on him the way snow used to land on Vessia: gently, totally, everywhere at once. "Knowing what it would cost you. Knowing you might not come back."

"Yes."

"And she let you go anyway."

"She *sent* me," Juniper said. "I found the ledger dusting a shelf, but Agatha Pinch has never once lost a book in her life. That book was waiting for me the way the Door waited. Everything in the Hollow waits politely. It's the valley's whole personality."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then, in the tone of a man approaching a difficult tactical position: "Does this mean my mother-in-law is a hundred and some years old?"

"She's not your mother-in-law."

"I'm establishing precedent."

"She's seventy-nine," Juniper said, "and if you call her that in a letter she will hex the seam shut out of spite, and I believe you now know she is entirely capable of it."

He considered this. "Seventy-nine," he repeated, with the reverence other men reserved for ancient kings. "She planned a country's resurrection from a vegetable garden."

"She planned it between arguments with the goats."

"Even more impressive." He handed the letter back, then hesitated. "What does she mean, *badger*?"

"No one knows," Juniper said. "No one has ever known. There's a badger involved in roughly a third of everything Agatha does and we have collectively agreed not to investigate."

---

The second letter came at the autumn solstice, and with it came the discovery that the seam worked both directions.

Juniper had prepared for weeks. She'd bought proper paper in the market — real paper, from the mill at Coldharbour that had reopened in spring, staffed by two brothers who argued constantly and produced the smoothest stock in the new Vessia. She'd drafted eleven versions of her reply and burned ten of them in the stove, because every draft either sounded like a report to a commanding officer or dissolved into crying by the second paragraph, and neither felt right for a woman who signed off *who is fine, stop worrying*.

In the end she wrote:

*Dear Agatha,*

*His name is Wren. You were smug for nothing; everyone here is named Wren, or might as well be. (This is a lie. There is one Wren. He says hello. He asked whether you actually know a badger and I told him asking was how people got cursed.)*

*The radish thing was deliberate. Thin them yourself if it bothers you so much from a hundred miles and a world away.*

*I'm teaching at the schoolhouse now. Solstices, the whole town adds verses to the Oath — you'd hate the committee format and love the results. Last week a six-year-old proposed a line about "no taking other people's bread without asking," and the council adopted it unanimously, so Vessia's founding legal document now contains a clause about bread. I think the king who broke the original oath would find this either mortifying or wonderful. Possibly both.*

*Here is the thing I should say plainly, because you always said plain things were wasted on me until I was forty: I'm glad you sent me. I was angry about it for most of a year — not out loud, I did it privately, like a civilized person — but I'm done now. You gave me a door and pretended it was an accident, and I walked through it and found a country that needed someone exactly as stubborn and unmagical and hedge-taught as me. Belonging isn't a thing a place grants, you said once. I finally believe you.*

*Also I am eating properly. Mostly. There's a baker.*

*— J.H., who is fine, stop worrying (I see what you did there)*

She carried it to the meadow where the Door had stood — just a meadow now, soft grass, a ring of white flowers that bloomed there year-round and refused to grow anywhere else — and laid the letter on the stone that remained, and waited through most of an afternoon feeling extremely foolish.

At dusk, the air above the stone shivered like heat-haze. The letter sank, slowly, the way a leaf settles on still water — except upward-then-downward, through some fold in the world that had never quite finished healing. When it was gone, something lay in its place.

A jar of honey. Hollow clover honey, dark amber, with a scrap of paper tied to it: *For the baker. If he's worthy. Test him on scones.*

And beneath the jar, a second sheet, covered edge to edge in cramped handwriting:

*P.S. Since you're being plain, I'll match you. The valley is waking up. Whatever I saved in it all those years — it's stirring. The hum changed key last month. I don't yet know if that means nothing, or something enormous, and I dislike not knowing enormously. Your letters may become useful for purposes beyond filial affection. Keep writing. And Juniper — if the hum starts singing words, you write to me THAT DAY. Not the next. That day.*

*I remember what happened the last time a song learned to talk.*

Juniper stood in the meadow a long while, the honey heavy in her hands, the evening light going gold and then blue over grass that had never known winter.

Somewhere behind her, faint as a held breath, the Oath hummed through the stones of the distant city walls — steady, contented, ordinary.

Mostly ordinary.

She could have sworn — and would spend the walk home deciding she hadn't — that underneath its low warm note, just for a heartbeat, it had hummed something almost like a word.

* * *

The third letter came in deep winter, and it was not in Agatha's hand.

Juniper found it on the garden stone crusted with frost that hadn't melted, though the sun was high — frost that had crossed a seam between worlds carrying cold from a valley that had never once in her life allowed weather to be dramatic. Inside, in careful, unfamiliar script, slightly crooked, the way writing looks when the hand that makes it hasn't held a pen in decades:

*Miss Juniper,*

*Agatha is resting. She is FINE, she will be furious I wrote that first, but she made me promise that if she ever slept past noon two days running I was to send word, and also that I was to say it plainly because plain things are wasted on you till forty, which I confess I do not fully follow but she was very firm.*

*She is tired. A hundred years of planning tires a body, she says, though she also says it mostly to make me stop hovering, and then she gets up and argues with the goats, so draw what conclusions you like. But she sleeps more. And yesterday — this is the part she'll deny — the hum started singing words.*

*Just one. Over and over, soft as anything, all through the valley. The animals all stopped and listened. So did I.*

*It sang "soon."*

*I don't know what it means. Neither does she, and that frightens her more than the sleeping does, though she'd sooner eat the badger than admit it. She wanted me to tell you that whatever happens next, she chose it — the ledger, the door, all of it — and she is not sorry, and you are not to come home on her account, because home is apparently wherever you decided to stand, which she says she taught you, though between us I think you taught yourself.*

*Write back quickly. The seam likes quick things.*

*Tobbin (the badger is real. His name is Chancellor. I have questions I will never get answered)*

Juniper read it standing in the snow with her breath coming short and her heart doing something complicated between fear and fierce, ridiculous love.

"Soon," she whispered.

Behind her, through the open kitchen window, Wren had gone very still over the dishes. She didn't need to look at him to know he'd heard her. A year of marriage teaches you a person's silences the way a century teaches a valley its songs.

"Juniper," he said carefully. "Say that word again."

She almost didn't. But the Oath was humming through the walls of the house — their house, built on the old Bastion foundations, where every stone remembered singing — and as she breathed the word out into the winter air, *soon*, the hum beneath the floorboards shifted, gentle and unmistakable.

And hummed it back.
