## 38. The New Verse

The solstice morning began, as all the best mornings in Vessia now did, with someone arguing.

Juniper heard it from the kitchen window of the house she and Wren had taken on Larkspur Row — a narrow, crooked thing with a roof that slumped like an old man dozing by a fire. The argument was coming from the square, where the scaffolding crews had been assembling the Oathstone platform since dawn, and it had the particular rhythm she'd come to recognize as *Dagny Holt versus everyone*.

"—all I'm saying," Dagny's voice carried clear across the frost-bright rooftops, "is that last winter we sang the verse about the miller's daughter first, and the wheat came in *fine*, and I don't see why this year the fishermen get to go before the farmers—"

"Because the herring don't wait for your wheat, Dagny!"

"The herring can learn patience!"

Juniper smiled into her tea. A year ago — two years ago, if you counted the years the long winter had stolen — no one in Vessia had argued about anything. That was the part people forgot when they talked about the forgetting, about the smiling citizens wandering the capital like sleepwalkers. Forgetting wasn't only losing what you loved. It was losing what you could *quarrel* over. A country that couldn't argue couldn't care.

She set down her cup and pulled on her coat, the one with the deep pockets, because today she'd need room for chalk, and song-cards, and whatever small treasures children pressed into her hands along the way.

The walk to the square took her past the baker's, where Tomas was hauling trays of solstice buns shaped like coiled songs — the old Vessian symbol, a spiral that meant *a promise going forward*. He waved her over and pressed a warm bun into her hands without being asked.

"For the teacher," he said gravely. "You've got the hardest work today. I only have to feed them. You have to make them sit still."

"They're not so bad."

Tomas gave her the look of a man who had once been a child himself and remembered exactly what he had been like. "May the Oath protect you, then."

*The Oath doesn't protect anyone,* Juniper thought cheerfully. *That's rather the point.* But she bit into the bun and said nothing, because some things were nicer left as blessings.

* * *

The square was filling by midmorning. The Oathstone field outside the city was still the true heart of things — pilgrims went there, and the grass grew green in ribbons around each stone like veins of spring running through the land — but the solstice gathering happened here, in every town square across the country at once, because Wren had insisted. *The Oath shouldn't live only where the king knelt,* he'd said. *It should live where the people stand.*

A low wooden platform stood at the center, built around a shard of Oathstone that the masons had cut with great ceremony and considerable complaint (Oathstone, it turned out, did not enjoy being cut; it hummed reproachfully for weeks afterward). The shard glowed faintly even now, in daylight, the way a banked fire holds its light.

And there, at the edge of the platform, sitting on the steps with his boots unlaced and his collar open despite the cold because he claimed formal dress made him itch, was Wren.

He wasn't teaching yet. He was listening to a girl of perhaps eight who was talking at tremendous speed, her mittens gesturing wildly, while he nodded with the complete seriousness he gave to everything children said. Juniper caught fragments — something about her grandmother, something about a promise involving a goat — and saw Wren nod again, solemn as a judge.

"You're letting her talk you into something," Juniper said, arriving beside them.

"I am *listening* to a citizen of Vessia," Wren said with dignity.

"She wants to add a verse about a goat."

"It's a very well-argued case." He looked up at her then, and did the small private thing he always did — the half-second pause, the look that said *you're here, good, the day can start now*. Two years married, and he still did it every time, as though her arrival were a small mercy he hadn't learned to expect.

"What's the goat's name?" Juniper asked the girl.

"Clementine," said the girl, with the gravity the name deserved.

"And what did you promise Clementine?"

"That I wouldn't sell her. Even when she ate Papa's hat. Even when she ate the *second* hat." The girl leaned close. "Papa says goats are a test of character."

"Papa sounds wise," Wren said.

"Papa says that about everything since he started going to the remembering circles." She shrugged with the weariness of the young confronted by their elders' self-improvement. Then she spotted her friends and thundered off, shouting something about holding her place in line.

Wren watched her go. "Clementine's verse might actually be good," he said. "Promises kept under provocation. That's the whole oath, really."

"That's the whole *marriage*," Juniper said, and was rewarded with the laugh she'd crossed a frozen country to earn.

* * *

The teaching circle gathered at noon, in the shadow of the guildhall, on benches arranged in a ring — never rows; Juniper had been firm about that. Rings meant everyone could see everyone. Rows meant someone was in front and someone was behind, and children absorbed hierarchy through their skin faster than through their ears.

There were eleven of them today, ranging from the goat-girl down to a boy of four named Pell who spent most gatherings trying to eat the chalk. Their parents sat further back on the low wall, pretending not to listen and failing completely, which was fine. Half of what Juniper taught was really for them.

"All right," she said, clapping chalk dust from her hands. "Question first. What's a promise?"

"A thing you say you'll do!" called the goat-girl, whose name was Bets and who answered every question as though it had personally challenged her.

"Good start. Anyone else?"

"A thing you have to do," said a serious boy named Aldric, whose father worked the Bastion records now, cataloguing what the inquisitors' order had buried.

"Mm." Juniper crouched to the slate propped against her bench and wrote two words: PROMISE. CAGE. "Here's the hard part, then. Listen carefully, because grown-ups get this wrong too, and when they get it wrong they build whole countries wrong."

She drew a door under the first word.

"A promise is a door you choose to walk through. Nobody shoves you. And — here's the important bit — you can walk back out. Maybe walking out costs you something. Maybe someone's hurt or angry. Maybe you have to say sorry until your voice goes hoarse. But you *can*. A promise you can't ever leave isn't a promise anymore. It's a cage with nice words painted on it."

Silence, except for Pell, who was chewing chalk with the focus of a scholar.

Bets frowned. "But my mama says a vow is forever."

"Sometimes forever is what you choose, freely, every single day. My husband vowed—" Juniper paused, savoring it, "—an oath to this country when he was fourteen. Do you know how he keeps it?"

"By being an oath-keeper," Aldric said.

"He *was* an oath-keeper. Now he teaches. He keeps the same promise by doing something different, because the promise underneath — the true promise — was *I will take care of this place*. The words are just the coat the promise wears. If the coat tears, you mend it. You don't nail the coat to the man."

One of the mothers on the wall laughed quietly, and Juniper pretended not to hear.

"Now." She turned the slate around. "Who can tell me what a cage looks like when it's disguised as a promise? Anyone? Think of stories."

A small girl in the back — Mira, who almost never spoke — raised her hand halfway, then all the way, surprising everyone including herself.

"When..." Her voice came out thin. She tried again. "When somebody says *if you loved me you would*."

The parents on the wall went very quiet.

"Yes," Juniper said gently. "Exactly that. Well seen, Mira." She held the girl's eyes and let the moment be what it was — a small, brave thing, witnessed. Then she let it go, lightly, the way you release a bird so it doesn't notice its own wings. "A real promise leaves you whole. A cage always starts by taking a piece of you and calling it love, or duty, or destiny. And if anyone ever tells you the whole country depends on you doing exactly one thing, one way, forever—"

"You run?" Bets asked hopefully.

"Well." Juniper grinned. "First you ask a lot of rude questions. *Then* you run if the answers are bad."

* * *

The adding of verses began at sundown, which was the tradition now: sing the old verse as the light went, add the new ones in the dark, so that every year the country's promises were born under stars instead of suns. Someone had decided this was symbolic. Someone else had pointed out it was mostly because farmers couldn't come until after chores. Both were true, and nobody minded.

The crowd filled the square — three hundred souls, maybe more, bundled in wool, breath rising in clouds around the Oathstone shard. Dagny Holt had won her appeal; the farmers would sing second, after the fishermen, but before the millers, and she stood near the front radiating the satisfaction of a woman whose grievance had been honored by committee.

The old verse went up into the winter dark, hundreds of voices together:

*We are the ground that holds,*
*the hand that holds, the hold*
*a traveler keeps of home —*

and Juniper, as always, felt the shard answer, a low golden hum beneath the words, the Broken Oath long since mended but still singing, because that was what living oaths did. They didn't stop. They just stopped grieving.

Then Wren stepped up to call the new verses, because he had the voice for it — a keeper's trained voice, deep enough to carry to the back row — and Juniper took her place beside him with the ledger.

Not the prophecy ledger. That one lived in the archive now, behind glass, her own name faded to brown ink on a page that scholars visited and argued over. This was a new ledger, plain paper, bought from a stationer in the capital for six coppers. Its first page bore lines added the first solstice after the winter broke. It was nearly full now.

"From the fishing villages of the Salt Coast," Wren announced, "a verse offered by Ilse Marrow, net-mender, aged sixty-one."

Ilse climbed the steps, terrified and thrilled in equal measure, and sang her four lines in a wavering voice about promising the sea to take only what was needed and give back the rest — and the crowd repeated it, and the shard hummed, and somewhere in the ledger Juniper's pen followed along.

Verse after verse. A widow promising her street that no neighbor would eat alone. Two brothers who'd quarreled for a decade offering a verse about the cost of silence. The goat verse, delivered by Bets on behalf of Clementine ("who cannot attend, as she is a goat"), promising patience with creatures who ruin hats, met with roaring laughter and sung back with such enthusiasm that Pell woke up and cried, then stopped crying to clap.

And then, near the end, Wren glanced sideways at Juniper, and she knew what was coming, because it came every year and every year it ambushed her anyway.

"Last," he said, "a verse from our teacher. From the hedge-born key." He said the old title with deliberate irony now; it had become a joke between them, and half the country didn't know it had ever been literal. "Juniper?"

She mounted the steps. The crowd hushed — they always hushed, and she always wished they wouldn't, and Wren's hand found hers at the edge of the light and stayed there, and that helped.

She had written her verse three nights ago, crossing out lines until the candle drowned. It was short. The best ones were turning out short.

*I was taught that belonging*
*is a valley's gift to grant.*
*I unlearn it here, aloud:*
*belonging is the song we plant.*

She sang it alone, her unmagical voice, plain as a farmhouse door — and the crowd took it up, and the shard flared gold, and high above the square the first snow of the season began to fall, downward, obediently, beautifully, the way snow was supposed to.

* * *

Later, when the square had emptied and the lanterns burned low, they walked home the long way, past the guildhall, past the bakery dark and fragrant with tomorrow's dough. Snow gathered on Wren's shoulders and he didn't brush it off, because he was mid-argument.

"—all I'm saying is that next year the children should offer the *first* verse. Before the adults. Set the tone."

"Bets will propose Clementine for the council."

"Bets would win."

"Bets *would* win," Juniper admitted. "That's rather terrifying."

They passed the corner where the Door's residual seam still flickered on certain evenings — a shimmer in the air like heat off summer stone, the scar of the passage between worlds. Letters came through it, now and again. One was waiting tonight, wedged in the seam's cold glow: thick envelope, wax seal, handwriting she knew better than her own.

Mistress Ivy's hand.

Juniper broke the seal right there in the snow. Inside were two sheets. The first was a letter, full of garden news and complaints about the Hollow's weather and a postscript asking whether Wren had learned to cook yet or whether Juniper was still doing all of it (*tell him thyme goes in early, men never believe this*).

The second sheet was older. Much older. Yellowed, creased, written in the same hand but younger — and Juniper's breath caught, because she recognized the verse copied out upon it in ink gone soft with a century.

Her solstice verse. The one she'd written three nights ago. Word for word, line for line, in her mistress's hand, dated ninety-eight years past.

At the bottom, in fresher ink, a single line added recently:

*Some pages write themselves, dear. Come visit soon — we need to talk about the other valleys.*

Juniper looked up. Down the street, the seam shimmered, and beyond it — just for a heartbeat, just long enough — she glimpsed not the Hollow's familiar hum, but somewhere stranger: a coastline under a green sky, and a figure standing at the water's edge, waiting, as if they had known she would look.

Wren read the page over her shoulder. He was quiet a moment.

"Well," he said finally. "I suppose we'd better start packing."
