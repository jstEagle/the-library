## 40. When the Oath Sings Again

The evening the Oath changed its song, Juniper was arguing with a goose.

"You can't eat the seedlings," she told it, for the third time. "They're not for you. They're for everyone, which is sort of the whole point of this country now."

The goose regarded her with the flat contempt of its kind and took another step toward the bean rows. Behind it, three more geese waited in formation, because Vessian geese had apparently decided that the woman who sang winter out of the sky was simply another obstacle to be outflanked.

"I defeated an order of oath-eating inquisitors," Juniper said. "I have bested prophecy itself. I will not be beaten by poultry."

She would be beaten by poultry. Everyone knew she would be beaten by poultry. Wren had taken to calling the lead goose *the Lord Inquisitor* — "it has the same eyes," he said, "judgmental and hungry" — and the name had stuck so thoroughly that even the children in the village used it. *The Lord Inquisitor got into the cabbage again.* Somewhere, presumably, the actual Lord Inquisitor's memory was spinning in whatever oblivion had swallowed him when his founding lie dissolved, unaware he'd been demoted below waterfowl.

Juniper scooped up a handful of spilled barley from her apron pocket — she kept it there specifically for diplomatic purposes — and scattered it well away from the beans. The geese wheeled after it, honking their victory, and she stood in the warm gold light of evening feeling entirely, ridiculously happy.

That was when the Oath began to sing.

* * *

It sang every night now. That hadn't been true at first. In those raw early weeks after the reforging — after the Salt Meridian, after Maren, after the Door — the land had been mostly quiet, like a person who has wept for a century and needs to simply sit still for a while. The green came back in ribbons first, then in floods: moss over the Bastion's grey stones, wildflowers cracking through the cobbles of the frozen market where vendors had once sold bottled summers. The upward snow had righted itself on the day the new verse finished spreading, all at once, like a held breath released. Wren swore he could pinpoint the moment. He'd been mid-argument with the council about grain stores, and the snow outside the window had simply turned around and fallen down, and he'd lost his place in the argument and never found it again.

But the singing — the singing had come back slowly, note by note, the way Maren had once told her the old song died. Juniper liked to think of that symmetry. Things in Vessia seemed to die and return at the same pace: patiently, stubbornly, one voice joining another.

Tonight, though, the song was different.

She felt it before she heard it — a change in the air like the moment before a key turns. She went still in the garden, dirt under her nails, and listened.

For a hundred years, the Broken Oath had sung grief. Even after the reforging, even after the winter cracked and the inquisitors unraveled into ordinary, forgettable men, the song had carried something of mourning in it — the echo of what had been lost, the memory of the cold. Beautiful, but bruised.

This was not that.

This was the sound a door makes when it opens onto a summer morning. This was the first four notes of a song someone is about to teach you. It moved through the soil and up through the soles of her feet and settled somewhere behind her sternum, warm as a hand laid flat between the shoulder blades, and Juniper Hartwell — hedge-born, valley-raised, immune to magic and somehow its instrument anyway — felt her eyes fill with tears for no reason she could defend in any court.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, hello."

Because it wasn't grief. It wasn't even celebration, exactly, though celebration lived in it like light lives in water. It was *invitation*. It was the Oath leaning toward the horizon the way a person leans toward a stranger who might become a friend.

And far away — so far she might have imagined it, except she had learned long ago never to dismiss the things she almost imagined — something answered.

A hum. A single low note, steady as a held breath, coming from beyond the eastern hills, beyond the Salt Meridian, beyond the edge of every map Maren Quill had ever drawn or dreamed of drawing.

The hum faltered.

Just for a heartbeat. Just a stutter, a skipped stitch, the way the Hollow's anti-magic press had once stuttered around her the night the ledger pulled her through. Then it resumed, steady as ever, but Juniper had heard it. And she knew — the way she'd known her own name was written in a book a hundred years before she was born — that somewhere out there was another valley. Another place where magic couldn't follow. Another door.

Another key.

"Well," she said to the empty garden, and her voice shook only a little. "Well, then."

* * *

Wren found her twenty minutes later sitting on the doorstep of their house — their house, a phrase that still fit her the way new boots fit, snugly and with occasional pinching — with the prophecy ledger open on her knees and the goose Lord Inquisitor standing beside her like a co-conspirator.

He'd come up the lane carrying two cups of chamomile and the particular walk he used when he wanted to seem casual and failed completely. Four years of loving him had taught her all his walks. There was the walk for bad news (brisk, jaw set), the walk for good news (he whistled, badly), and the walk for *I have noticed something and I want to see if you've noticed it too*, which was shoulders loose, eyes careful, hands full of tea.

"You heard it," they said to each other, simultaneously, and then laughed, because after everything they still did that.

"The whole valley heard it." He handed her a cup and lowered himself onto the step beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Half of them think it means rain. Old Sable swears it means the king's ghost finally left. The children at my lesson today insisted it meant the geese are planning something."

"The geese are always planning something."

"That's fair." He looked out over the garden — the beans, the foxglove, the little stone wall they'd rebuilt together one absurdly optimistic afternoon last spring — and then, quieter: "You felt the other thing. I saw your face just now. You had your listening face on."

"I don't have a listening face."

"Juniper. You have eleven faces and I have catalogued all of them. There's the *I'm fine* face, which means you're not. There's the *the ledger is doing something alarming* face—"

"That face retired years ago."

"—and there's the listening face, which is this one." He tapped her gently between the eyebrows, and she batted his hand away, and for a moment they were just two people on a step being foolish, and she loved him so much it made the evening brighter, literally; the foxgloves along the path had begun, in these years, to lean slightly toward them both when they laughed. Maren would have mapped that. Maren would have charged somebody's true name for the privilege.

She opened the ledger instead of answering.

It lay on her knees the way it had lain on the dusty shelf of Mistress Ivy's shop a lifetime ago — except that now most of its pages were blank. She and Wren had spent the first winter after the thaw going through it line by line, reading the hedge-witch's hundred-year-old handwriting, learning at last what the woman in the Hollow had known and never said. *The key must be unmagical,* the old entry read, *because magic cannot argue with itself. Send someone the song cannot own, and let her choose.* And beneath that, smaller, in ink gone brown with age:

*I am sorry, little sprout. I hope the garden was kind to you.*

Mistress Ivy had known. Had raised her, fed her, taught her to tell yarrow from Queen Anne's lace, and all the while carried a prophecy with Juniper's name folded inside it like a letter never posted. The letters across the seam had healed most of that wound — *you were never a pawn, sprout, you were a granddaughter; the difference is that pawns don't get to be angry at me* — but Juniper still touched the page sometimes, the way you touch a scar to check it's yours.

Tonight she turned past her own name. Past tonight's date, written in 1893, now crossed out gently with a single stroke — done, finished, fulfilled, hers. Past the verses about doors and anchors and martyrs, all refused. She turned to the last written page and then to the vast white country of pages beyond it.

Blank.

The ledger, which had once moved its own ink and dragged a girl across the seam of the world, sat quiet now. Prophecies, it turned out, only wrote themselves while there was an oath broken enough to need one. The new verse belonged to people.

"So," Wren said, watching her fingers rest on the blank paper. "Another valley. Another door."

"Maybe. Probably. The hum stuttered — I'm sure of it. And the Oath was singing *toward* it, Wren. Like it knows them. Like countries recognize each other." She laughed, a little helplessly. "Which is ridiculous. Countries don't have feelings."

"Countries absolutely have feelings. I married a woman who is technically a national instrument; I've had time to adjust my worldview."

"I am not a national instrument."

"There are songs about you. Plural. One of them involves a lot of accordions."

"It involves *one* accordion and I was promised it would stay in the northern villages."

"It has migrated." He sipped his tea, insufferably calm. "The point stands. If anyone gets to decide what you are, it's you. That was rather the entire thesis of the war we didn't have."

Juniper looked down at the blank page. The evening light had gone the color of honey, and somewhere downhill the village was lighting its lamps one by one, and the Oath was still singing its open-door song, soft beneath everything, patient as soil.

Here was the thing she had learned, the thing she suspected she'd spend the rest of her life relearning in different fonts: belonging was not a place's gift to grant. The Hollow had taught her that by withholding it; Vessia had taught it by offering too much. Belonging traveled with you. It lived in the space between two cups of tea on a doorstep, in a goose named after your enemy, in a dead cartographer's laugh that still surfaced in your head at odd moments (*charge them double, sprout, kindness is a seller's market*). You could carry home across any border, through any seam, into any story. Which meant —

"Someone should go and see," she said.

Wren didn't tense. That was the miracle of him, still, after everything — that when she said frightening things he leaned in instead of bracing. "Go and see."

"Find where the hum comes from. Find the other valley, if there is one. Find whoever's name is written in *their* dusty old book, if they've got one, and — I don't know. Warn them? Help them? Tell them what I wish someone had told me?" She turned the thought over, testing its weight, and found it lighter than she expected. "That nobody gets to write your ending for you. Not even in nice handwriting."

"They could put that on a plaque."

"I will put that on a plaque. I'll carve it into the schoolhouse myself."

"You'd misspell 'prophecy' again."

"*Once*, Wren. Once, and the paint was running."

He was smiling. She could hear it without looking. But underneath the smile, when she finally turned to him, there was the other thing — the careful thing, the question he was letting her answer first. Because he was an oath-keeper still, in the ways that mattered, even though he'd resigned his post and taken up teaching children the difference between a promise and a cage. His promise now was simple and enormous: *wherever you belong, I belong; whichever road, I walk it beside you or I wait at the end of it, and either way you go free.*

She had married a man who offered to wait at the end of roads. Sometimes she wanted to shake him. Mostly she wanted to kiss him, which was inconvenient during arguments and had reportedly scandalized the council twice.

"I'm not leaving tomorrow," she said. "Or next month. Maybe not for a year. There's the solstice gathering, and the east field won't plant itself, and somebody has to keep the Lord Inquisitor out of the beans."

"An unwinnable posting. You'd be abandoning your troops."

"And—" she hesitated, then said it plainly, because plainness was the whole religion they'd built out of the ashes of all their lies, "and I might come back having found nothing. Empty hills. Weather. Sheep. It might just be wind stuttering against mountains, and I'll have crossed the Meridian chasing a sound."

"Maren crossed worse for less," Wren said. "She mapped countries that didn't exist anymore because she couldn't stand for anything true to go unmapped. I think you caught that off her, like a fever." He set down his cup. "Besides. When the Oath sings invitation, somebody ought to RSVP."

Juniper laughed — really laughed, the kind that startled birds out of the hedgerow — and then, because the light was right and the song was right and four years had taught her not to waste rightness, she picked up the pen that lived in the ledger's spine.

She wrote on the blank first page, in her own crooked hand, no prophecy in it at all:

*Tonight the Oath sang toward the east, and something answered. Tomorrow I will pack badly and argue about provisions and probably cry at least once, because departures. But someday soon, past the Salt Meridian, past the edge of every map — there may be another door, and another key, and another person standing in a garden somewhere, sure they don't belong, wrong about that in the best possible way.*

*We're coming to find you. Take your time. We did.*

She signed it — not her true name, which slept safe in the Vessian soil, freely given, but the name she'd been called in a valley that couldn't hold magic and somehow, impossibly, had held her.

Then she closed the ledger on her own crossed-out date and all the white pages waiting after it, and leaned her head on Wren's shoulder, and watched the first stars learn their places over a country that belonged to no prophecy at all.

Far off in the dark, the hum stuttered again — once, twice, like a knock.

Like someone, very far away, opening a dusty old book.

And the Oath, joyful and unbroken, began — very softly, the way all new things begin — to sing the next verse.
