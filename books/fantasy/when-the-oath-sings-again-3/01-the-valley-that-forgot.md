## 1. The Valley That Forgot

The hinges of the Hollow Gate had not turned in two hundred years, and every year on the eve of the Oath-Renewal, Sebastian Oyelaran oiled them anyway.

She knelt in the gatehouse shadow with the oil-flask cradled against her ribs, working the spout into the seam where iron met iron. The metal drank the oil with a slow, greedy sound, like something sleeping and dreaming of thirst. Around her the gate rose out of the mountainside itself — not built against the stone but grown from it, as if the mountain had once opened its mouth and the ancestors had persuaded it to close. Black basalt veined with something paler, something that might have been bone. No lock. No handle. Only the oath-words cut into the lintel in letters no one in the valley could read anymore.

Sebastian could pronounce them. That was her whole inheritance: the sounds without the meanings, passed mother to daughter like a song whose words had worn smooth with use until only the melody remained.

*Hollow Gate*, the children called it, and then, because children are honest, *the Door That Isn't*. It stood at the valley's northern throat where the mountains pinched together, forty feet of seamless dark, and beyond it — everyone agreed — there was nothing. Not wilderness, not another country. Nothing. The world ended politely at the Hollow Gate, the way a sentence ends at a period, and everything past it was silence.

Two centuries ago the maps had been folded up and burned, or so the story went, and the valley had forgotten what lay on the other side so thoroughly that forgetting had become a kind of geography itself.

Sebastian screwed the cap back onto the flask and pressed her palm flat against the basalt.

It was warm. It was always warm, faintly, the way a hearthstone stays warm hours after the fire dies, and she had long ago stopped finding this strange. A body stops noticing its own heartbeat. She let her hand rest there a moment anyway, the ritual before the ritual, and felt — as she always felt, and never said aloud — the faintest tremor underneath, rhythmic and patient, like a pulse heard through a wall.

"Good evening," she told the gate, because her mother had told the gate good evening, and her mother's mother before her, all the way back through the names carved into the warden's ledger in the gatehouse, twenty-two lines of Oyelaran women going down into the ink like roots into soil.

The gate, as always, said nothing.

* * *

By the time she climbed to the top of the watch-stair, the sun had gone down behind the western peaks and the valley below had filled up with blue dusk like a cup filling with water. Lamplight bloomed in Underhill village, scattered and golden — the baker's window, the meeting-hall, the little shrine of Saint Aldhelm-that-wasn't, which had been rededicated four times in living memory and still smelled, stubbornly, of older incense. Smoke rose straight up in the windless air. Somewhere a dog barked twice and gave up.

Tomorrow was the Renewal. Tomorrow the whole valley would walk up the pilgrim road in their festival clothes, and Sebastian would stand before the gate in the warden's grey mantle with the iron key-shaped pendant that opened nothing, and recite the oath-words at noon exactly, while the elders watched and the children fidgeted and the bell in the gatehouse tower rang nine times for reasons also lost. Then there would be honey-cakes and dancing on the green, and the year would be bound up tight again, safe, sealed, the way it had been sealed for six generations of her family.

The thought should have been comfortable. It was, mostly. It sat in her chest like a well-worn coat.

But tonight, climbing the last steps to the belfry where she kept the warden's lamp lit from sundown to dawn — a duty no one had ever explained and she had never questioned — Sebastian paused on the landing and looked north, past the gate, at the place where the world was supposed to end.

There were stars above the mountains. Of course there were stars. But she found herself counting them without meaning to, the way you count the breaths of someone sleeping beside you, checking they're still breathing. And between one star and the next, low over the ridge line behind the gate, something flickered. Not a star's steady burn. A redder light, deeper, guttering, like the last coal in a banked fire taking one more breath.

She blinked. It was gone.

"Old eyes," she said to herself, though she was thirty-four and her eyes were excellent. She said it the way her mother used to say it, standing on this same landing, and the echo of her mother's voice in her own mouth unsettled her more than the light had.

She lit the warden's lamp. Its flame stood up straight and gold in the glass, and threw her shadow long across the belfry floor — a tall thin shadow with a key pendant swinging at its breast, and if you didn't look too closely it might have been any of the twenty-two women in the ledger, standing any of the thousands of nights they had stood, keeping a light burning toward a door that never opened.

Below, faint on the wind, she heard the first festival-singers rehearsing in the village square, their voices rising and tangling and resolving. They were singing the harvest rounds. Tomorrow they would sing the oath-melody, and she would give them the words.

She knew all the words. She knew none of the meanings. In nineteen years as warden she had never once asked herself why, and tonight, for the first time, the not-asking felt like a door left unbarred.

* * *

The child came at moonrise.

Sebastian heard small feet on the pilgrim road before she saw anything — the particular scuff-and-hop of someone walking who is trying very hard to sneak and has never once succeeded. She stepped out of the gatehouse onto the threshold stone, and there came Tamsin Reeve, eight years old, the miller's youngest, wrapped in a blanket against the autumn cold, clutching something white to her chest.

"You shouldn't be here," Sebastian said, not unkindly. "Festival's tomorrow."

"I know." Tamsin stopped three paces off and looked up. In the lamplight her face was blotchy, her lashes wet. "I'm not s'posed to be anywhere. That's why I came here."

Sebastian crouched to be less enormous. This was in the warden's unwritten duties too: mothers sent their grieving children up the hill to her sometimes, the way they sent them to the shrine, except the shrine god was a polite distance away and Sebastian was right here, made of the same dust as everyone. "Who died?"

"Bram." The name came out cracked. "Our ram. Papa says he was old, he says rams just die, but—" The child's jaw worked. "He got sick and I prayed at Saint Aldhelm's, I prayed *every night* like Nan taught me, and he died anyway. And Nan said—" she lowered her voice, glancing back down the dark road as though the village might hear her commit heresy— "Nan said the old prayers work better. The ones from before. She knows one. Her gran taught her. She said say it at the Gate, 'cause the Gate remembers."

"Tamsin." Sebastian kept her voice level, gentle, the voice she used on frightened animals and frightened people, which were often the same. "Your grandmother means well, but the Gate doesn't answer prayers. Nothing answers prayers here. That's rather the point of us."

"But it's the eve," Tamsin whispered. "Nan said on the eve, the door gets thin. Just for one night. Just for a breath."

*The door gets thin.* Sebastian had heard the phrase her whole life, sung in the festival round like a joke, like a superstition, *the door gets thin, the door gets thin, hold your breath when the bell rings in.* She had never once considered it as a claim about reality. Claims required somewhere to stand outside them, and the valley offered nowhere else to stand.

"Say your prayer, then," she heard herself say. "One prayer. Then I'm walking you home, and tomorrow you eat honey-cakes and stop frightening your grandmother with theology."

Tamsin unfolded the white thing she'd been clutching. A lamb's wool tuft, curling and clean. She held it up toward the gate in both hands, the way you'd offer bread to a stranger, and closed her eyes, and said:

"*Vael-en doru, vael-en simma. Kethra min, kethra min, anath kethra.*"

Sebastian went cold all over.

Those were the oath-words. Not the whole sequence — a fragment, three lines of it, the opening cadence — but unmistakably, bone-deep unmistakably, the same tongue her own mouth moved in every noon of every Renewal. A language preserved by one family for two hundred years, guarded, hoarded, *hers* — and an eight-year-old had just spoken it fluently enough to break her heart doing it, learned from a grandmother, learned from a grandmother's grandmother, passed hand to hand in kitchens like a recipe.

How many households held pieces? How many fragments of the oath slept in the valley's memory, unassembled, like shards of a mirror nobody knew was a mirror?

Before she could speak — before she could decide whether to be angry or afraid or simply curious, which was already its own small betrayal of nineteen years of dutiful not-asking — the bell in the tower above her began to ring.

Not the dawn bell. Not any hour she kept. It rang once, a single deep bronze note that she felt in her teeth, and Sebastian's head snapped up toward the belfry, because the warden's bell had no mechanism. It hung on a hook. It had hung on a hook since before her grandmother. It rang only when pulled, and she was the only one on this mountain.

The note faded. The night held very still, the kind of stillness that listens.

And Bram the ram walked out of the dark.

He came up the pilgrim road at a slow amble, gray and gaunt and unmistakable, the notch in his left ear that Tamsin had described with her hands shaped like crescents. He stopped at the edge of the lamplight and looked at the child with mild yellow eyes, and chewed, once, with terrible patience.

Tamsin made a sound Sebastian had no word for — joy and terror fused into one syllable — and did not move. Neither did Sebastian. Every instinct of her office, nineteen years deep, screamed at her to explain this, to file it, to find the ordinary shape of it: a ram lost not dead, a shepherd's error, grief playing tricks. But the explanation wouldn't come, because she had seen the miller's barn two days ago. She had stood in the yard while they dug the grave. She had put her hand on Tamsin's shoulder and said the comfort-words, and the ground under the apple tree had been freshly turned, and the ground was still freshly turned now, and yet.

Yet.

Bram took one step closer. Where his hoof struck the threshold stone, the stone *glowed* — briefly, faintly, a ring of pale gold spreading out from the impact like ripples in reverse, sinking inward instead of outward, into the rock, into the mountain, gone. And beneath Sebastian's feet she felt the gate's warmth deepen, the sleeper-pulse quicken, and from the black basalt came a sound she had never heard in her life and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize a parent's footsteps:

The Hollow Gate sighed.

A long, low exhalation of stone and iron and something under both, vast and relieved, like a held breath finally released after two hundred years. The oil she'd rubbed into the hinges caught the lamplight and gleamed wet along seams that had, for one impossible moment, seemed to shift — a hair's width, a shadow's width, the mountain's jaw loosening —

Then nothing. Stillness. A dead ram standing in moonlight, a weeping child, a warden on her feet with her heart slamming against her ribs like a fist on a locked door.

"Nan was right," Tamsin whispered. "The door *did* get thin."

Sebastian looked at the gate. At the unread letters on the lintel, which in the lamplight seemed almost — not quite, but almost — to have rearranged themselves into an arrangement she'd never noticed, the way clouds make faces only after someone points. At the warm stone under her palm, beating its slow patient beat.

*What are you,* she almost asked aloud, and stopped herself, because the question had teeth. Questions like that, in this valley, were how you ended up like old Marta Ellery, who had asked too loudly what lay beyond and been gently, permanently moved to the far farms where no one had to hear her.

"Come on," she said instead, and her voice only shook a little. "I'll carry the ram's wool. You walk in front of me where I can see you."

They went down the pilgrim road together, the child and the ghost-gray sheep trailing behind like something that hadn't decided yet whether it was finished with existing, and Sebastian did not look back at the gate. She was proud of that. She looked back only once, at the bend where the road dropped toward the village lights, and by the light of the warden's lamp burning high above it, she saw that the Hollow Gate stood exactly as it had always stood — sealed, silent, certain.

Except that now, in the space of a single day, she had oiled hinges that never turned, heard a bell with no rope, watched the dead walk uphill, and heard her own most secret words come out of a stranger's small mouth.

Tomorrow at noon she would sing the oath, and the elders would nod, and the year would be bound up tight.

Tonight, walking down through the blue dark with a dead man's ram breathing on her heels, Warden Sebastian Oyelaran admitted to the night what she had spent nineteen years refusing to admit to the morning:

She no longer knew what the oath was for.

Behind her, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the gate was warm, and warmer, and warmer still — and high on the northern ridge, where the world was supposed to end, the red light kindled again above the mountains, guttering, patient, like a fire deciding whether to go out or begin.
