## 2. Ash on the Wind

The ash came on a morning that smelled of nothing at all.

Sebastian Oyelaran noticed it first as a wrongness in the light — the dawn over the Hollow Gate had gone the color of skimmed milk, diffuse and sourceless, as though the sun had been wrapped in linen. She stood on the gatehouse steps with her oil-can still warm from the brazier, her fingers stiff from another night of tending hinges that would never swing, and watched the sky snow downward in slow grey spirals.

Not snow. Snow was cold and clean and melted into the gutters by midmorning. This fell without hurry, without weight, settling on the lintel stones and the bramble hedge and the backs of her hands like the dust of something enormous that had once been alive.

She caught a flake on her palm and held it there until her skin warmed it. It did not melt. It did not do anything at all except sit there, grey and faintly iridescent, catching light the way an opal catches it — from somewhere inside itself.

"Dragon-ash," she said aloud, and heard how absurd it sounded in her own voice, the way her grandmother's voice had sounded saying it, telling stories to children by the winter fire. *When the world was younger and wider,* her grandmother used to say, *the dragons burned so hot their ashes drifted for years, silvering the fields of far countries.* A story. A thing said to make children gasp. There had been no dragons in living memory. There were no dragons anywhere; the valley's oldest records, the ones written before writing got careful, mentioned them the way they mentioned floods and plagues — as weather, as history, as things that happened elsewhere and long ago.

But the ash kept falling.

By the time she finished the morning rite — oil in the hinge-grooves, salt across the threshold, the words recited in their dead language while the gate stood patient and shut beneath her hands like a sleeping animal — the fields below the ridge had begun to change.

* * *

She saw it best from the top of the mill path, where the barley ran in long rows down toward the village of Underhollow. Overnight — between one dusk and one dawn, between her evening rounds and her morning ones — the green of the crop had gone strange. Not withered. Not frosted. The stalks had turned the pale grey-white of moonlight made solid, each head of barley furred with a fine crystalline shimmer that broke the low sun into tiny prismatic stings when she brushed her hand through it.

Silvered. That was the word the old stories used, though they'd always applied it to orchards in lands so distant they might as well have been invented. *The ash silvered the orchards of Ambrun, and the fruit that year was sweeter than honey.* Another bedtime marvel. But the barley under her hand wasn't sweet. When she rubbed a grain loose and pressed it between her fingers, it crumbled into glittering grit that smelled faintly, horribly, of burnt hair.

She walked the rows for an hour, and everywhere the silver followed the drift-lines of the wind, thick where the ash had settled thickest, thin at the hedgerows' shelter. The farmers would see it soon. The farmers would come up the hill soon after that.

They did.

Maren Ostwick arrived first, because Maren Ostwick arrived first everywhere, his bad hip and his good lungs carrying him up the mill path faster than men half his age. Behind him came the Sallow twins and old Mother Ives with her stick, and behind them a straggle of others, faces tilted upward toward the milk-white sky, hands full of silvered grain, voices rising in the particular register of people who have decided in advance whom to blame.

"Warden." Maren planted himself at the field's edge, close enough that she could smell the sourness of fear under his sweat. He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. The word itself was the accusation, delivered flat, like a coin slapped onto a counter.

"The ash came in the night," Sebastian said. "From above the ridge. From beyond—"

"Beyond what?" Mother Ives's voice cracked out like a whip-crack of dry wood. "There is no beyond. There's the valley, and there's the Gate, and there's you, girl, and your father before you, and his mother before him, keeping what we were told to keep. And now look." She thrust out her stick-hand, palm up, where a single flake of ash sat unmelted against her liver-spotted skin. "Look what falls out of a sky that has been empty since my grandmother's grandmother."

"The crops," one of the Sallows said. He was the younger one, Tam, who'd once brought her wild strawberries because she'd taught him to read. Now he wouldn't meet her eyes. "It's not just the barley. The beans are silvered. the squash. Whatever this is, it's in everything that grew overnight, and everything that grows tomorrow, if it keeps falling. What happens to seed that's born already turned to glass?"

Sebastian looked at the field. She looked at their faces. She felt, with terrible clarity, the shape of the thing they wanted from her: not answers — she had none — but a posture. Firmness. The warden's calm, the Oyelaran steadiness, two hundred years of shoulders squared before a door that never opened. They wanted to be told the valley was safe by someone whose voice didn't shake.

What came out instead was defensiveness, and she heard it even as she spoke.

"I have kept every rite," she said. "Every oiling, every salting, every word, dawn and dusk, for eleven years. You've all seen me do it. You've stood at the fence and watched me do it. If something has changed, it has not changed through any laxness of mine—"

"There it is," Mother Ives said softly, and somehow the softness was worse than shouting. "Laxness. Her own word. Mark it."

"That is not what I—"

"Eleven years," Maren said, "and the Gate has never opened, fine, good, that's its nature. But tell me, Warden, honestly, as one keeper of accounts to another: have you been listening? At the rites? Have you heard anything you shouldn't? Because my wife says the well-water tastes of iron this week. Because the Sallows say their dog won't go within fifty paces of the Gate road anymore, stands at the fence and cries at it like a thing bereaved." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped to the register men use when they think they're being reasonable. "We're not accusing. We're asking. Something is different. You're the one whose family has touched that door longest. So: is it the door, Warden? Or is it you?"

The question went through her like a splinter finding the quick. *Is it the door, or is it you.* She thought of the child — only three days ago, little Wren Callow, kneeling at the fence with her grubby hands laced together, praying in the artless way children pray, *please let my brother's fever break* — and of the fever breaking by morning, sudden and total, so complete that the healer had frowned at his own medicines as though they'd betrayed him. Sebastian had told herself it was coincidence, then, standing in the doorway of the Callow house with the smell of recovery all around her and a feeling like a struck bell humming somewhere behind her ribs.

She hadn't oiled the hinges any differently that day. She hadn't done anything differently at all.

"I'll walk the boundary," she said finally. "I'll check the seal-stones, every one. If the ash keeps falling, keep your animals from drinking the low streams until we know what it does. And I will send word down if—" She stopped, because the sentence had nowhere honest to end. *If I learn anything*, she'd meant to say. But she had no way to learn anything. No records spoke of ash. No elder remembered a rite for it. Two centuries of careful, closed, complete tradition, and not one page for the sky coming apart.

The villagers dispersed slowly, unhappily, leaving her alone with the silvered field and the taste of guilt that had no clear object. That was the worst of it, she thought, walking back up toward the Gate through the drifting grey. Guilt usually came with a memory attached — the thing done, the word unsaid, the watch slept through. This guilt was free-floating, unmoored, a debt collected without any loan she could recall taking out. As though the fault predated her. As though it had simply been waiting, down the generations, for the sky to remember it.

* * *

That evening the ash fell thicker.

She performed the dusk rite with the flakes settling on her shoulders like a shroud being fitted. The words came out of her in their familiar cadence — *vel atha seren, vel atha morren, the door remembers, the door remains* — syllables she had spoken ten thousand times without ever once understanding, and tonight, for the first time, she heard them as sounds rather than ritual, and thought: *what am I actually saying? Who decided these words? Why these words?*

Her grandmother had refused to teach translation. *You don't need to know what the oath says, child. You need to know that it says.* Her father had been the same. The whole lineage of Oyelaran wardens stretched back behind her like a rope of knots, each generation holding fast and asking nothing, and Sebastian felt suddenly, violently, the weight of being the latest knot in a rope whose other end was tied to something nobody living had ever seen.

She finished the rite. She set down the oil-can. And because the ash was still falling, because the sky was still wrong, because Tam Sallow's dog had cried at the road and Wren Callow's brother had woken whole — because of all of it — she did something no warden in the record had ever done.

She looked at the Gate.

Really looked. Not the dutiful glance of maintenance, checking hinges and threshold-salt, but a long, open-eyed examination, as a daughter might finally study a parent's face. The Hollow Gate filled the cleft in the ridge-wall like a stopper fills a bottle: a slab of dark stone, forty feet high, seamless, uncarved, older than every building in the valley put together. No keyhole. No handle. No seam where doors are meant to part. Just stone, and the faint tracery of lines across its surface that everyone called natural veining and that Sebastian, staring now with the ash sifting down around her, saw were too regular for veins and too curved for cracks.

And fused into the stone, at the height of her own heart, something new.

She crossed the threshold-salt without thinking — her own feet breaking her own rule — and pressed her palm to the Gate beside it. Cold. Always cold, colder than the mountain around it, cold as deep water. But near the object, an inch away, the cold changed quality: not dead-cold but spent-cold, the cold of a hearth an hour after the fire.

The thing was a scale.

She knew it the way you know a face from portraits — from carvings on old lintels, from the dragon-shapes stitched into festival banners that no one remembered the meaning of. Broad as her spread hand, teardrop-shaped, edged in fine serrations like a feather's barb, and scorched nearly black at its root as though it had been torn from its owner in fire. Its surviving surface held that same interior iridescence as the falling ash, but a thousandfold richer: colors moved in it when she moved her head, greens and violets and a red like banked coals breathing.

Fused. That was the word her mind insisted on. Not embedded, not affixed — fused, the stone flowed around the scale's edges like candlewax around a dropped bead, as though the Gate had reached out and taken hold of it. As though the Gate had *wanted* it.

Sebastian stood with her hand hovering a hair's breadth from the thing, and the hum behind her ribs — the one she'd felt in the Callow house, the one she'd been ignoring for eleven years, the one she'd been ignoring her whole life if she was honest — rose to a pitch just short of audible. The ash settled on her hair and shoulders and did not melt. Somewhere below, a nightjar called its wooden call across silvered fields.

A scale from a dragon that did not exist, welded to a door that never opened, under a sky that was empty and was no longer empty.

*Something is burning*, she thought, with a certainty that bypassed reason entirely and arrived fully formed, the way knowledge arrives in dreams. *Something is burning, very far away, and it is dying, and the ash is what's left of it, and the Gate knows.*

She snatched her hand back. The hum cut off like a string severed.

For a long moment she stood in the drifting grey, heart slamming, and made herself breathe. Then, because she was an Oyelaran and Oyelaran women squared their shoulders, she fetched the record-book from the gatehouse, sat beneath the lintel with a storm-lantern, and wrote in her own careful hand:

*Ninth day of the Reed Moon. Ash falls from a clear sky. Crops silvered overnight, cause unknown. Scale of unknown origin found fused to the Gate's face. No precedent in the records. Rites performed in full. I have kept the oath.*

She stopped. The lantern hissed. The last line lay there looking exactly like what it was: a defense, entered into evidence, addressed to whoever came after her — and, less forgivably, to herself.

Below the line, small, almost hidden, she added: *I have kept the letter of it. I no longer know what the oath says. Tonight, for the first time, I want to know.*

She closed the book. Above the ridge, through the milk-white dark, the ash came down without end, soft as forgiveness, patient as ash — and far off, at the very edge of hearing, at the very edge of believing, the Gate gave a sound she had never heard it give in eleven years of dawns and dusks.

A low note. Held. Like the first syllable of a song deciding whether to continue.

Like something, on the other side, beginning to sing back.
