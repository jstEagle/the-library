## 3. A Priest Without a God

The rain came to the valley the way everything came to the valley: slowly, and without permission asked.

Ivo Marren stood beneath the eave of his crooked house at the edge of Hollow's Reach and let it fall on his outstretched hands. He had been in the valley eleven years now — long enough that his exile had worn smooth as a river stone, long enough that he had almost stopped flinching when children called him *the godless man* in passing. The rain was ordinary. The valley's rain always was. That was precisely what had kept him awake for three nights running, because nothing here was supposed to be ordinary anymore. Something here had *answered*.

He had heard about the child. Everyone had. Tam Weller's daughter — six years old, a fever that would not break, her mother carrying her up the switchback road to the Hollow Gate at dusk because there was nowhere else to carry a dying thing in a valley with no healers left and no roads out. And near the gate, in the shadow of a door that had not opened in two hundred years, the girl had whispered a prayer into the seam of the world — not to any god, because no one in the valley prayed to gods, they only recited the oath-words like a charm against forgetting — and something on the other side of the stone had *leaned in*.

The fever broke before midnight. The girl woke asking who had been singing to her.

Ivo knew who had been singing. He had known the moment the story reached him, standing in the market square with a basket of turnips going heavy in his hands. He had known the way an old soldier knows the sound of a trumpet from a war he swore never to speak of again.

The gods of Vael were awake. And they were starving.

* * *

He waited four days before he went to her. Long enough to seem less desperate than he was; not so long that desperation stopped leaking through him anyway. He rehearsed what he would say as he climbed the keeper's path in the grey hour before dawn, mist pooling in the hollows of the hillside like spilled milk, and every version of it sounded either mad or blasphemous, and both, in this valley, amounted to the same crime.

Sebastian Oyelaran lived in the small stone house attached to the gate itself, as her mother had, and her grandmother, and a line of keepers stretching back so far that even the valley's oldest records — such as they were — simply said *always*. She was already outside when he reached the last bend, which did not surprise him. Keepers rose with the dark. He had watched her from a distance for eleven years, the way one watches a lighthouse: regularly, impersonally, with a gratitude one never expects to have to explain.

Today she was kneeling at the base of the gate with a cloth and a clay jar of oil, tending hinges that had never turned in living memory. It was a ritual he found quietly devastating — all that care lavished on a door that existed, as far as anyone could say aloud, only to be cared for.

"Warden Oyelaran." His voice cracked on the honorific; he hadn't used it in years.

She didn't look around. "Ivo." Just his name, flat as a dropped tool. "If you've come about the festival seating again—"

"I haven't."

"Then you've come about the ash." Now she looked, and he wished she hadn't. There were shadows under her eyes like bruises, and her hands — strong hands, oil-slicked, careful — had gone still in a way that told him she had been waiting for someone to come about the ash. Waiting and dreading it.

"The ash," he agreed. "And the feather-scale fused to your gate. And the girl who sang to a locked door and was answered." He came closer, boots silent on the wet flagstones. "I know what is happening here, Sebastian. Better than anyone alive in this valley. Possibly better than anyone alive anywhere."

She rose slowly, wiping her hands, and studied him with the particular patience of a woman who has spent her life listening to people say things at doors. "You're a drunk priest without a parish," she said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse. "That's the whole of what this valley knows about you. What exactly do you think you know?"

"That prayer was answered," Ivo said. "Not coincidentally. Not by medicine or luck or the fever breaking on its own schedule. It was answered by name, in the old tongue, with a song your grandmother's grandmother would have recognized if she'd ever been permitted to hear it." He watched the words land. Watched her jaw tighten. "I know because I served them. The gods of Vael. For nineteen years. Before the synod stripped my orders and put me on a cart and sent me somewhere prayers go to die."

The mist moved between them. Somewhere below, the valley was waking — smoke from chimneys, a dog complaining, the ordinary music of a place that believed itself the whole of the world.

"You're mad," Sebastian said at last. But she said it the way one tests ice: weight first, carefully, listening for the crack.

"Yes," Ivo said. "Almost certainly. Madness runs in men who watched their god die slowly and were ordered to call it peace." He held out his hand, palm up, an offering and a surrender both. "But hear me out before you send me down the hill. Because the fire behind that gate is guttering, warden, and when it goes out, it will not be only Vael that ends. It will be your valley too. Everything. Both sides of the door. And you are the only person alive who can do anything about it — though I promise you, it is not what you were trained to do."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, instead of taking his hand, she turned back to the gate and pressed her palm flat against the stone beside the scorched scale, and Ivo felt the air change — felt something vast and half-asleep shift its attention toward them through six feet of mountain granite, the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice.

Sebastian snatched her hand back as if burned.

"You felt that," Ivo said softly. "Didn't you. You've been feeling it for weeks."

"Don't," she said. Her voice had gone thin. "Don't tell me what I've been feeling. My mother kept this gate for forty years and never once—"

"Your mother was never answered," Ivo said. "Because your mother was never doubted." He stepped closer, close enough to see the pulse jumping in her throat. "The oath doesn't sing for the faithful, Sebastian. It sings for the doubter. It has been trying to talk to you since the day you were born, and it has finally gotten loud enough that you can't pretend otherwise."

* * *

She made him tea. It was terrible tea, over-steeped, brewed in a kitchen where every surface spoke of single occupancy and long habit — one cup, one bowl, one chair pulled slightly out from the table as if its owner could never quite commit to sitting. She set the second cup down in front of him without comment, and Ivo understood that he had just been granted something rarer than belief: an audience. In this valley, where ritual was comfort and questions were rust, she was giving him the thing no one had given her.

They sat across from each other while the rain returned, soft against the shutters, and he told her.

He told her about the seminary at Carrowmere, in the country whose name he still could not say without tasting salt — the country on the far side of the mountains, the one the maps showed as blank parchment and the histories showed as nothing at all. He told her how he had been a gifted novice, how he had heard the gods of Vael in the liturgy the way other boys heard music: not as doctrine but as presence. Hearth-mothers whispering in chimney smoke. Road-wights humming under cobbles. The great slow voices underneath everything, older than the synod's tidy hierarchy, patient as geology.

"They weren't distant," he said. His own hands were shaking now; he let them. "That's what no one understands who hasn't served them. They were *near*. You could trip over a small god in a doorway. You learned to say excuse-me to the wind. And then—" He stopped. Drank tea he didn't taste. "Then the synod decided nearness was the problem. A god in every doorway is a god you can't tax, can't rank, can't control. So they built the great quiet. They sealed the loudest country in the world behind a door and taught everyone on this side to forget there had ever been a door at all. And the priests who wouldn't forget—" He smiled, and knew it was a bad smile. "Well. You know where they send us."

"And you stayed," Sebastian said. "Eleven years. Right at the edge of it. Why? If you hated them for exiling you, why live within sight of their wall?"

Ivo set down the cup. This was the part he had not rehearsed, because he had never said it aloud, not to anyone, not in eleven years of drinking alone in a house with one chair.

"Because exile is a strange mercy," he said. "They meant it as punishment. Send the heretic to the most forgotten corner of the world, where he'll never hear a hymn again. But they sent me to the *door*, Sebastian. To the one place in creation where my gods are closest — separated from me by six feet of stone and two hundred years of silence. Some nights I put my hand against the rock of this hill and I swear I can feel them breathing. Starving. Dreaming. Whatever starving things do." He laughed, short and joyless. "They punished me with proximity. It's the cruelest thing I know. And I have never once wanted to be anywhere else."

The rain filled the silence. Sebastian was looking at him with an expression he couldn't read — recognition, perhaps, or its opposite, the face of someone seeing their own future in a stranger's ruin.

"The girl," she said finally. "Tell me exactly what happened to the girl. From your side of knowing."

"A prayer went through," Ivo said. "Through the seam. It happens — rarely, accidentally, when grief makes a hole in a person wide enough for a voice to slip out. Most of the time nothing hears it. The seal is good work; I hate the men who made it, but it's good work. Except the seal runs on dragon-fire, and dragon-fire is not infinite, and it is failing. Has been failing for years. Decades. Every year the mesh thins. And this autumn—" He gestured vaguely at the window, at the silvered crops, at the whole wrongness settling over the valley like early frost. "This autumn it thinned enough that something on the other side heard a dying child ask for help. And a starving god, warden, does not weigh its answers. It *pours*. It gave that girl every scrap of healing it could push through a keyhole, and it sang while it did it, because it was so hungry to be needed that being needed was the miracle."

"And the ash?"

"The dragon is burning itself faster to hold the door," Ivo said. "The last dragon. When it gutters out, the seal fails entirely, and everything the synod buried comes home at once — every god, every hunger, every unanswered century of prayer, all of it pouring through an open wound into a valley that has forgotten gods exist." He leaned forward. "Or we go through first. Deliberately. While the door can still be managed. We cross into Vael, we reach the mountain, we find the dragon, and we learn what it needs. Because I promise you it is not more oil on hinges that have never turned."

Sebastian was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had the careful flatness of someone holding a door closed from the inside.

"Do you understand what you're asking?" she said. "I am the warden of the Hollow Gate. That isn't my job. It's my *name*. Oyelaran. It means keeper in the old tongue — the real old tongue, the one nobody speaks. Every Oyelaran for two hundred years has stood at that door and said the words and oiled the hinges and kept the valley safe by keeping the door shut. And you want me to open it. You want me to become, in a single morning, the woman who ended the world."

"No," Ivo said. "I want you to become the woman who saved it. Those are different sentences, Sebastian, however much they rhyme."

She stood abruptly, chair legs scraping, and walked to the window. Rain traced lines down the glass, and beyond it the gate stood black against the grey morning, immense and patient, the scorched scale glinting faintly like a closed eye.

"My whole life," she said, to the glass rather than to him, "I have known exactly what I was. It's a kind of wealth, knowing that. Other people lie awake wondering what they're for. I never wondered once." A pause. "Lately I lie awake hearing singing."

Ivo rose too, slowly, an old man's knees announcing themselves. He crossed the kitchen and stood a respectful distance behind her, looking at the same rain, the same door.

"I'm going to ask you for the impossible now," he said. "And then I'm going to leave, and let you hate me at your own pace, and come back tomorrow."

"Ask anyway."

"Open the gate."

"Ivo—"

"Not tonight. Not rashly. But begin learning how. Because I have read the records that survived the purging — fragments, heresies, things smuggled out in the lining of coats — and they all agree on one point that the synod never managed to burn." He watched her shoulders go rigid in the grey light. "The gate does not open from this side. Not for me, not for any army, not for any prayer. It opens for one bloodline only. The line of the lock. And warden—" he made himself say it plainly, gently, the way one sets down something fragile, "—if the stories are true, that means the door was never built to keep Vael's gods out of your valley. It was built to keep *you in*."

The silence that followed was the longest of his life.

Sebastian turned. Her face had gone very pale and very calm, the calm of deep water over a drowning.

"Get out," she said.

"Yes," Ivo agreed. He collected his coat from the peg, and at the door he paused, because some things must be said at thresholds or not at all.

"For what it's worth," he said, "they exiled me here believing a forgotten man at a forgotten door could do no harm. They were wrong about the door. I think they may have been wrong about you too." He bowed, the old bow, the one that had cost him his orders. "Tomorrow. Same hour. Bring your doubt."

The rain took him down the hill. Behind him, high in the black stone of the Hollow Gate, in a seam no wider than a knife-blade, something flickered — a thread of light, gold-white, gone before any waking eye could swear to it.

But Ivo saw. And walking down through the mist toward the valley that had forgotten its gods, the defrocked priest of Vael began, very quietly, very dangerously, to sing.
