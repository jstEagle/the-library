## 4. What the Oath Remembers

The morning of the Renewal dawned grey and windless, the kind of morning that seemed to hold its breath. Sebastian woke before the bell, as she always did on this day, and lay listening to the silence of her own room in the warden's house — the creak of old timber settling, the drip of last night's condensation from the eaves, the far-off complaint of a goat that had opinions about being milked late. Ordinary sounds. Valley sounds. She had heard them every morning of her thirty-one years, and until this season she had never once thought to wonder whether they were the only sounds there were.

She dressed in the ceremonial grey. The wool was heavy, dyed with ash-madder from the eastern slopes, and it smelled faintly of the cedar chest where it slept all year between festivals. Her mother had worn the same robe; her grandmother before her. There was a stain near the left hem that no one had ever tried to wash out, because no one remembered what had made it. Sebastian had decided, sometime in childhood, that it was wine. It was easier than deciding it was something else.

Downstairs, the house was cold. She lit the stove, put water on for tea she would not have time to drink, and stood at the window looking east toward the Hollow Gate. From here it was only a dark seam in the cliffside, a vertical line of shadow no wider than a handspan at this distance, cutting the rock face like a scar through skin. Two hundred years of ritual had grown up around that seam like lichen around stone. Every year, on this day, the Oyelaran went down to it and renewed the oath. Every year the valley held its breath. Every year nothing happened.

*Nothing happened.* She turned the phrase over now and found it strange, the way a word goes strange when you repeat it too long. For two centuries, *nothing happening* had been the whole point — the proof of the oath's strength, the seal holding, the door sleeping. But lately nothing had begun to feel less like safety and more like a held note that someone, somewhere, had stopped sustaining.

The ash was still coming, though thinner now, sifting down like grey snow when the wind came off the heights. The elders had ordered the fields covered with woven mats, and the crops beneath them silvered anyway, slowly, as if frost had moved in under the straw and refused to leave. Tam Vessick's boy had taken to collecting the flakes in a jar, saying they glittered if you held them to firelight. They did. She had checked. That was the worst part — that even the ash was beautiful.

She drank half the tea standing up, out of duty more than thirst, and went out into the cold.

* * *

The procession formed at the well-square an hour after sunrise: six elders in their brown robes, the choir of children in white, the drummer with his taut-skinned frame drum, and Sebastian at the rear, carrying the oath-book in its wrapping of undyed linen. The book was older than any building in the village. Its cover was boards overlaid with hammered tin that had gone black as slate, and its pages were not paper but something thinner and tougher, like membrane, like the skin inside an eggshell. No ink on those pages had ever faded. No one knew why. No one asked. Asking questions about the oath was like asking questions about weather — you endured it, you marked it, you did not interrogate it.

But Sebastian had been asking questions for three weeks now, silently, in the privacy of her own skull, and the questions had teeth.

They walked the gate-road together, the whole village turning out along the verges the way they always did, heads bowed as the procession passed. Old Marta Brannigan pressed a sprig of dried rowan into Sebastian's hand as she went by — *for protection, child* — and Sebastian closed her fingers around it and felt the small sharp leaves bite her palm and was grateful for the pain, because pain was simple.

The Hollow Gate rose before them as the road bent north into the cliff's shadow. Seen close, it was never quite what memory promised. Memory said *door*. Eyes said otherwise: a slab of some material that was neither wood nor metal nor stone, tall as three men, set flush into the living rock, its surface worked all over with lines so fine they looked drawn rather than carved. In certain lights the lines seemed to move, the way water moves under ice. Around its edges ran a border of script — dense, looping, utterly unlike any alphabet used in the valley or named in any of the warden's books. The script was the oath. Everyone knew that. The words Sebastian recited each year were said to be the script read aloud, passed down mouth to mouth across twenty generations, worn smooth as river stones by the friction of repetition.

Sebastian had recited them since she was seven years old. She could say them in her sleep. She had said them in her sleep once, her mother reported, and the hinges had hummed.

The elders took their places. The children arranged themselves in their crescent. The drummer began the slow heartbeat rhythm — *dum, dum-dum, dum* — that was said to mimic the pulse of the mountain itself. And Sebastian stepped forward alone, as every Oyelaran had stepped forward alone, and laid her bare palm against the gate.

It was always cold. That was the first thing they taught you: the gate is cold, colder than stone has any right to be in summer, and your hand will ache, and the ache is good, the ache means the seal knows you. She braced for the cold.

It was warm.

Not hot. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, the way a cheek is warm an inch above a fevered brow. Warmth moved up through her palm and into her wrist while the elders chanted the opening responses behind her, and Sebastian stood very still with her hand flat against the door and felt, for the first time in her life, that she was touching something alive.

*You are early,* said a voice that was not a voice.

It did not come through her ears. It arrived fully formed in the center of her mind, the way you sometimes know who is knocking before the knock finishes. It was not Ivo Marren's careful diction, not the elders' nasal chant, not any human timbre she could name. It was layered, many-voiced, a chord pretending to be a single note. And it spoke not in the valley tongue but in the dead language — the language of the border-script, the language of the oath itself — except that for the length of a heartbeat she understood it perfectly, the way you understand your own name.

Then the understanding folded shut like a fan, and what remained was the recitation, the one she had performed every year of her life, rising in her throat on schedule because her body knew the rite even when her mind reeled.

She sang.

That was the thing no one told you about the renewal: it was not spoken, it was sung, though the melody was so flattened by generations of cautious mouths that it barely qualified. Twenty notes, maybe, rising and falling within the range of a bored congregation. Her mother had sung it exactly so. Her grandmother. The line went back and back, and somewhere at the root of it, Sebastian understood suddenly and terribly, someone had sung it *properly* — had known what the words meant, had meant them.

Her voice found the old flattened tune. The elders answered in their parts. The children's crescent lifted their thin high harmony. And underneath it all, under her hand, the gate began — very quietly — to sing back.

At first she thought it was resonance, the way a wineglass answers a sung note. But the gate was not answering her melody. It was singing *underneath* it, a second song in counterpoint, deep and vast and patient, the way a mountain might hum if mountains hummed. And threaded through the drone came words.

She knew the shape of the words. That was the horror of it. Every phrase the gate sang matched, syllable for syllable, a phrase of the recitation she was performing — but where her version was smooth, worn, emptied of meaning, the gate's version was *specific*. It named things. It named the cliff. It named the seam of light behind the door — she could feel that seam now, a thread of pressure against her palm, like a vein of molten glass running floor to lintel on the other side of the slab. It named the ash. It named the fire.

And then it named her.

Not *warden*. Not *keeper*. Not *Oyelaran*, which she had always assumed was simply their family name, old and honorable, meaning something dignified in some older tongue. The gate sang a word that her recitation rendered as *guardian* — she had always understood that much, the first great word of the third verse, *I am the guardian of the door* — but the gate's version of the word was longer, compound, built of roots she could suddenly feel the edges of the way you can feel the edges of a word in a language you almost speak. One root meant *to bind*. Another meant *the binding itself*. A third, small and terrible, meant *the knot*.

*I am the knot,* the gate sang, using her voice, in the moment her own mouth shaped the flattened word *guardian.*

The world tilted. She kept singing. Some deep animal discipline held her upright and on-key while her mind tore open like a seedpod. *Guardian* was a person who watched a thing. *Knot* was a thing that *was* the fastening. You could untie a knot. You could cut one. A knot did not watch the rope — a knot *was* the rope, gathered and held and made into the very place where holding happened.

Twenty generations of Oyelaran wardens, and none of them had been keeping the gate.

The gate had been keeping *them*.

*Blood of the first singer,* the chord-voice said, gentle as snowfall, and the dead language poured through her understanding again, translating itself as it went: *blood of the hinge, blood of the lock, flesh given to the fastening, breath spent upon the seam. Year by year the knot is tightened. Year by year the singer sings herself into the door.*

*Renewal.* She had never thought about the word. Renewal of what? Of an agreement? Of a promise? She had renewed the oath the way you renew a roof, maintenance, upkeep, the dutiful preservation of inherited things. But the gate's song made the word naked. Each year the rite drew something out of the singer and set it into the seal, the way you'd feed a fire, the way you'd pay a debt. Each year the knot tightened. Each year —

Each year her mother had come down here, and sung, and walked home a little grayer. Her grandmother had done the same. She remembered now, with the sick clarity of hindsight rearranging everything, how her mother had aged in jumps — not gradually, but in annual increments, each spring adding a year's worth of wear to her face, her hands, the light in her eyes. The village had called it the burden of office. They'd said it with respect.

They had been describing a tithe.

The recitation reached its final verse. Sebastian's voice carried it. Somewhere very far away, the elders were bowing, the drummer softening his rhythm toward silence, the children's harmony thinning out into the long closing note that meant the valley was safe for another year. Her hand was still on the gate. The warmth under her palm had become heat, and the heat had become a pulse, and the pulse matched her own heart so exactly that she could no longer tell which of them was beating and which was being beaten.

*Doubt,* said the chord-voice, and it did not sound angry. It sounded *interested*. *The knot doubts. The knot has never doubted.*

"I didn't mean—" she began aloud, and caught herself, because the elders were watching, because the rite was ending, because you did not speak to the gate, you sang to it and you went home. But the words had already left her, and the gate had already heard, and doubt, once spoken to a door, does not go back through it.

The crack of light came without sound.

Later she would try to describe it and fail. It was not a glow around the edges, not a leak through the keyhole of the world. It was a line — a hairline fracture of white-gold radiance that split the gate's face from top to bottom in a single instant, bright enough to print itself on the inside of her eyelids, brief as a struck match. The elders cried out. A child began to cry in earnest. The drummer dropped his stick, and the stick rolled with absurd slowness across the flagstones, ticking, ticking, ticking into the silence.

Then the light was gone, and the gate was only the gate again — cold now, abruptly, bitterly cold, her palm aching with it as if she'd touched iron in January — and the border-script along its edge was *moving*, the loops of dead letters crawling like startled insects, rearranging themselves, and then stillness, and then nothing.

Sebastian withdrew her hand. There was a mark on it — she saw it clearly, in the flat grey light, before she curled her fingers closed: a fine white line across the pad of her thumb and forefinger, precise as a surgeon's incision, in the shape of a knot.

"Daughter." Elder Sorrel's voice, cracked with age and alarm. "Daughter, what did you do?"

The question of her life, asked at last, and she had no answer that was not also a confession. Behind Sorrel, the other elders clustered, brown robes rustling, faces pulled tight. Beyond them the villagers pressed at the edge of the courtyard, and beyond the villagers the road climbed away toward the fields, the silvered crops, the ash-sifted roofs of the only home she had ever known — a home that was, she now understood, the inside of an answer she had never been allowed to hear.

"The rite," Sebastian said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; steadiness felt like something happening to her rather than something she chose. "The rite was completed."

"The gate *sang*." Old Marta Brannigan, clutching her rowan sprig like a weapon. "In my mother's day, in my grandmother's day, the gate never sang. The gate sleeps. The gate has always slept."

"It woke," Sebastian said. "Or it was already awake, and we never listened."

Sorrel seized her wrist — the unmarked one — and turned her hand over, searching the palm, the fingers, finding nothing, because Sebastian had already closed the marked hand. "You will come to the council house. Now. You will tell us everything."

Everything. The word opened in her like a door of its own. Everything was a country larger than the valley; everything lay on the far side of the gate; everything was written in a language her blood could speak and her upbringing forbade. She thought of Ivo Marren, defrocked and exiled, who had looked at her three weeks ago with the expression of a man recognizing a fellow prisoner, and who had begged her — *begged* her — to consider that the door might need opening rather than guarding.

She thought of the crack of light, and how, in the fraction of a second it blazed, she had seen — or dreamed she saw, or would spend the rest of her life deciding whether she saw — a shape on the other side. Tall. Winged. Patient. With one vast ancient eye pressed to the seam, the way a child presses an eye to a knothole, watching her.

Watching her the way you watch a knot you are deciding whether to untie.

"I will come to the council house," Sebastian said, and freed her wrist gently from the old woman's grip. "And I will tell you what I can. But the rest—" She looked back once at the gate, seamless, silent, cold, its script settled into new arrangements that she was certain, with a certainty that frightened her more than fear itself, spelled a sentence she could almost read.

Almost.

"The rest," she said, "is not mine to give. It's the gate's. And I think—" her marked hand curled tighter around the knot-shaped line, hiding it, keeping it, the first secret the door had ever given her instead of the reverse— "I think it has only just begun telling me."

Behind her, as the elders turned her toward the road, the Hollow Gate gave one low sound, below hearing, below feeling, a note in the bones of the mountain rather than the air.

Anyone else would have called it settling stone.

Sebastian knew a held breath when she heard one.
