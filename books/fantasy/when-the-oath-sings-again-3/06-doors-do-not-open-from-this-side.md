## 6. Doors Do Not Open From This Side

They came to the gate at dawn, when the light came down the valley in long grey ribbons and everything looked like a thing remembered rather than a thing seen.

Sebastian had not slept. She had sat by her cold hearth until the third hour, the oath-words turning over in her mind like stones in a riverbed, and then she had dressed in her warden's coat — the heavy one, oiled against weather that never came — and walked up through the sleeping village with a lantern she did not need to light. The flame inside it guttered anyway. It had been doing that for three days, leaning toward the gate as if pulled on an invisible thread, and she had stopped pretending it was the wind.

Ivo Marren was already there. Of course he was. He stood at the foot of the Hollow Gate like a man at the grave of someone he had wronged, his defrocked cassock buttoned to the throat despite the warmth rising off the stones. The feather-shaped scale — scorched black at its edges, fused into the iron of the door as though it had grown there — caught the first light and threw it back in colors Sebastian had no names for.

Kestrel Vane arrived last, quiet as frost. He had been in the valley only four days and already moved through it like water finding its level: unremarkable, unhurried, everywhere at once. He carried his sword wrapped in sacking, which fooled no one, least of all Sebastian.

"You didn't tell them," Ivo said, without turning. "The elders."

"I told them I was performing a rite of maintenance." Sebastian set her lantern on the worn step where generations of wardens had knelt to oil hinges that had never once turned. "Which is true. It just happens to be a rite they've never seen."

"And if it works?" Kestrel asked. His voice was low, even, the voice of a man who had learned to ask questions he already feared the answers to. "If it opens?"

She looked at him. He was watching the gate, not her, and there was something in his stillness that made her want to lie to him less than she wanted to lie to anyone.

"Then everything I am is a lie," she said. "And everything my mother was, and hers, back two hundred years. So let's find out."

* * *

The rite began as all her rites began: with the oil.

It was absurd, and she knew it was absurd, and she did it anyway, because ritual is what holds a shape together when the shape has forgotten its own reason. She knelt at the base of the left-hand leaf — the gate was a double door taller than three men, banded in iron gone green-black with age, carved over every inch with script that no one in the valley could read — and worked the oil into the hinge with slow circles. The metal drank it the way dry earth drinks rain. It had always done that. She had told herself, all her life, that this meant the hinge was alive and waiting. Now, kneeling there with a disgraced priest behind her and a deserter knight at her shoulder, she wondered if it meant something else entirely: that the door was not sealed shut at all, but *fed* — kept soft, kept supple, kept ready — so that it would never have cause to break.

"Doors rot when they're abandoned," she said aloud, mostly to herself. "This one's been tended for six generations. Why tend a lock?"

"Because locks are meant to open," Ivo said. "For the right hand."

"Then let's see if mine is the right hand."

She rose. She pressed her palm flat against the iron.

It was warm. It was always warm, blood-warm, and she had spent her childhood telling other children it was the sun stored in the metal, and she had believed it right up until the night she'd heard the oath singing through her own bones and understood that the warmth was not the sun's at all. It was hers. It had been hers all along, drawn out of her year by year at every renewal, fed into this door the way a mother feeds a child from her own body, and the thought made her want to snatch her hand back —

But she held it there, and she spoke the words.

Not the valley's words. Not the mumbled festival syllables, the half-remembered blessing the elders called the Old Tongue because they had nothing else to call it. She spoke the words she had heard under the gate's skin three nights ago, the ones that had risen in her throat unbidden, the dead language that had named her bloodline not as guardian but as binding. Her accent was surely terrible. Her grammar was guesswork stitched from sound alone. But the gate knew its own name in any mouth.

The iron shivered.

Not a knock-from-the-other-side shiver, not the tremor of something striking. A deeper thing. A resonance, like a bell struck underwater. The scale fused above her head flared — once, briefly, a bloom of ember-light that threw their shadows huge and wavering across the empty square behind them — and along the carved script, letter by letter, a faint gold light kindled and traveled, following the lines of the words the way fire follows a fuse.

Sebastian's breath went out of her.

"It's reading," Ivo breathed. "Saints' mercy, it's *reading* you—"

The light reached the seam between the doors. Gathered there. Waited.

And then, gently — with something that felt horribly like apology — it died.

The gate did not move. Not a groan, not a shift, not the width of a hair. The iron was warm under her palm and utterly, perfectly immovable, and after a long moment Sebastian took her hand away and found her print lingering in the faint condensation of her own heat, five fingers and a palm, fading even as she watched.

"Again," Kestrel said.

She tried again. And again. The third time she spoke louder, shaping each syllable like a blow; the fourth time she whispered, coaxing, the way you speak to a spooked animal. The light rose and traveled and gathered at the seam and died, rose and died, rose and died, patient and sorrowful as tide against rock.

On the fifth attempt she put both hands flat and leaned her weight against the door, forehead touching iron, and felt — she would swear this afterward, though neither man contradicted her — felt the gate lean *back*. Not opening. Holding her. The way you hold a child who wants to run out into a storm.

"No," she said to it, her voice cracking on the word. "No. Don't you do that. Don't you comfort me. Open."

The gold light flickered along the script one last time and went out, and the warmth beneath her hands dimmed to something ordinary, and the Hollow Gate was only a door again — a beautiful, impossible, silent door, keeping its counsel as it had kept it for two hundred years.

* * *

They sat on the warden's step afterward, the three of them, sharing water from Kestrel's flask because no one suggested going home. The sun had cleared the eastern ridge now and lay full on the gate, and in honest daylight the thing looked almost benign: old iron, old carvings, a door at the end of the world.

"It opened inward," Sebastian said finally.

Both men looked at her.

"The light," she said. "When it gathered at the seam. It pooled toward us. Toward the valley side." She turned her flask-hand over, staring at her palm as if the answer were written there. "A door that opens outward gathers its force at the far edge. You push the energy through, past the threshold. This one drew it *in*. Toward me. Toward the valley." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, hollow. "My mother used to say the gate opens onto the world. Every warden says it. *One day the door will open and we'll walk out.* We say it like a promise. Like a reward waiting for us if we keep faith."

"And?" Kestrel said quietly.

"And it isn't a door onto the world." She heard the words leave her and could not call them back, and did not want to. "It's a door into it. It opens from the other side. It was built so that it *can only* be opened from the other side."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full — full of the village waking below them, smoke rising from forty chimneys, a dog barking, someone's loom clacking out its small patient rhythm. Full of everything Sebastian had ever loved, arranged suddenly at a new angle, like a room seen reflected in dark water.

"That's not possible," she said, and heard how young she sounded. "We're in the valley. The valley is — the valley is the world. There's the pass, there's the high meadows, there's the ridge road that goes down to the low country—"

"There is no low country." Ivo's voice was gentle, and that gentleness was worse than cruelty. "There is no pass, Sebastian. Have you ever walked it? Has anyone? Your maps show roads beyond the ridges, yes? Rivers, towns, a coast? Who drew those maps? When? Where are the surveyors' journals, the travelers' accounts, the merchants who came and went?"

She opened her mouth. Closed it.

Six generations of wardens, and not one journal of arrival. The valley's histories began with the gate and ended with the gate. The ridge roads were beautiful and empty and always had been, and she had walked them her whole life and never once, never once, met anyone coming up.

"Oh," she said. The word was very small. "Oh, saints. We're not at the edge of anything. We're in the middle. We're in the *middle of it*, aren't we. The valley is—" Her stomach dropped, actual vertigo, the ground tilting under the step though the stone had not moved. "Vael. Vael wasn't destroyed. Vael was sealed. And we're *inside* it. The whole valley, the ridges, the sky—" she gestured upward, at the blue, at the innocent drifting clouds, and her hand shook. "Is any of it real? Is the sun real?"

"The sun is real," Ivo said. "The wind is real. The crops you grow are real. This isn't an illusion, Sebastian. It's a prison, not a lie. Someone built a wall around a piece of the world and called the wall a horizon."

"How large? How far does it go?"

"I don't know. Farther than anyone's cared to walk, or farther than anyone who walked far enough was permitted to remember." He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked every year of his exile. "In the seminaries — before, when I still — we had fragments. Heretical fragments, burned more than once. They said the priests of Vael did it themselves. That the gods were growing too strong, too hungry, answering too many prayers, and the priesthood sealed the whole country rather than let divinity run unbounded. And they needed a lock that couldn't be picked or broken. A living lock. A blood that belonged to the seal itself."

"Oyelaran," Kestrel said.

"Oyelaran." Ivo nodded slowly. "I didn't understand the name when I first heard yours, warden. In the fragments it appears only once, in a list — the names of the founding families of Vael. Yours was one of them. One of the oldest. The seal-makers didn't appoint a stranger to guard their door. They used *their own*. A daughter of the blood, generation after generation, standing at the gate, feeding it, renewing the oath — believing herself its protector." He looked at her with an expression she could not bear: pity, plain and undecorated. "You were never the keeper of the lock, Sebastian. You are the lock."

The vertigo came again, worse. She gripped the edge of the step. Below them the village went on waking, the smith's hammer starting up its first strokes of the day, and she thought: *they don't know. Two hundred years and none of them know. My mother didn't know. She oiled these hinges until her hands failed and she never knew she was feeding a chain around her own ankle.*

"The dragon-fire," Kestrel said. He had gone very still, in the way she was learning meant his mind had gone somewhere hard and fast. "The ash on the wind. The scale in your door. If the valley is sealed Vael, then the fire that sustains the seal—"

"—is failing," Ivo finished. "Yes. There was a dragon. The oldest stories agree on that much, whatever else they argue about. Something vast, something burning at the heart of Vael, whose fire is the mortar of the seal. The fragments call it the Last Flame. And if ash from it is reaching the valley for the first time in living memory, if prayers are being answered through the gate, if the gods' voices are bleeding through—" He spread his hands. "The mortar is crumbling. The lock is starving. And the only way to learn how much time any of us have is to go to the fire itself."

"Across Vael," Sebastian said.

"Across Vael. Through the ghost-country. Whatever it has become in two hundred years of silence." Ivo hesitated, and she watched him choose honesty over mercy, and respected him for it. "I won't pretend it will be gentle. The villages outside the valley weren't evacuated. They were *sealed in*, same as you. What walks those roads now, I can't say. Echoes, perhaps. Prayers with nowhere to go. And the crown—" he glanced at Kestrel — "the crown has its own interest in miracles lately, or so our knight's rumors suggest."

"My rumors cost me my rank," Kestrel said dryly. "They're reliable."

Sebastian barely heard them. She was looking at the gate — at the great beautiful door she had served since she was seven years old, whose hinges she had oiled with love, whose warmth she had trusted the way children trust the heartbeat of a parent — and she was seeing it truly for the first time. Not a shield facing outward. A wall facing inward. Every carving was on this side; the far face, she realized, might be blank as bone. A door decorated for the prisoner, so the prisoner would look at beauty instead of bars.

"They sang to us," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; somewhere beneath the shaking, some deep cold part of her had already known and was simply waiting for the rest of her to arrive. "Every renewal, every festival. We sing the oath and we call it devotion and it's — it's a chain being checked for rust. And I'm the link."

"Yes," Ivo said softly.

She stood up. Her legs held. That seemed important to notice, that her legs held.

"Then I'm done checking chains," she said. "Pack what you need. Both of you. We go tomorrow night — no, tonight. Before the elders hear what I did at the gate this morning and decide their warden needs rest, or prayer, or replacing." She picked up her lantern. The little flame leaned hard toward the iron door, toward the ghost-country beyond it, trembling like a living thing that smelled home on the wind. "If the only exit from my whole life lies across a dead country at the foot of a dying fire, then I intend to walk out of here while I still have legs to walk on."

Kestrel rose beside her. Ivo crossed himself with a god's name he no longer served, out of habit, and then laughed at himself, low and bleak.

"One question, warden," the knight said. "When we cross — if the gate truly opens only from the far side — how do we get *back in*? To warn your valley. To bring them out."

Sebastian looked at the Hollow Gate a long moment. The morning light lay on it warm and golden, and it looked, as it had always looked, like the most faithful thing in the world.

"We'll burn that bridge when we reach it," she said. "Or build it. I've recently learned the difference between a door and a wall is mostly who's singing."

She turned down toward the village, toward the chimneys and the loom-clack and the smith's bright hammering, and behind her the lantern-flame bent toward the gate like a bowing pilgrim — and did not straighten again.
