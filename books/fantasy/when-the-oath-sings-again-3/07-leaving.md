## 7. Leaving

The oath-words came easier in the dark.

Sebastian had written them out three times that evening, by the light of a tallow candle she was careful not to let gutter, on paper so thin her handwriting showed through like veins beneath skin. The old script — the one the gate spoke and no one in the valley remembered learning — flowed from her hand without her willing it, each character a small closed door of its own. She had recited these words at every renewal since she was seven years old, standing before the Hollow Gate in her mother's shadow, and never once had anyone asked her what they meant. That had always seemed like piety. Now it looked like anesthesia.

She folded the pages into oilcloth, wrapped them twice, and tied them with the red cord that only the warden's line wore. Then she sat a long while with her hands flat on the table, listening to her house settle around her the way it had settled around every Oyelaran before her — beams ticking as they cooled, wind worrying the eaves, the far-off hush of the valley breathing in its sleep.

*You are leaving,* said the silence.

*You are leaving, and you have not told anyone the truth.*

She lit no lamp to dress by. She had spent twenty-two years learning this house blind — where the third stair complained, where the doorframe leaned a finger's width out of true, where the iron key to the gate's inner cabinet hung behind her mother's old cloak, cold as a held breath. She dressed as she did for winter rounds: wool, oiled leather, boots she had resoled herself because asking the cobbler questions was how rumors were born. She took food for six days though Ivo had promised her the journey would take four. She took the whetstone Kestrel had given her without explanation, its stone worn concave by someone else's years of edge-keeping. She stood a long moment before the shelf where her mother's journal lay, and did not take it, because taking it would mean admitting her mother had known, and she could not yet afford to know that either.

* * *

Tamsin Hale lived above the granary, which meant Sebastian smelled the place before she reached it — warm grain, mouse, the ghost of last summer's chaff-fire. She climbed the outside stairs quietly and knocked the pattern they had used since they were girls: two, pause, three, the knock that meant *I need you awake but no one else*.

A lamp flared. Tamsin opened the door already pulling her robe straight, gray-streaked hair unbound, eyes going from Sebastian's face to the bundle under her arm and back. She was forty-one and had been deputy warden for nine years, and she had never once asked why the gate needed warding, only how. It was the kindest thing about her, and tonight it made everything harder.

"You're doing the rounds," Tamsin said. Not a question. "At this hour."

"I'm leaving."

Something moved through Tamsin's face like weather — confusion first, then the swift arithmetic she did better than anyone in the valley, then fear arriving last, as it always did, wearing duty's coat.

"The elders won't—"

"The elders will be told I've gone on pilgrimage." Sebastian held out the oilcloth bundle. "North loop. Ash-shrine at Cold Ford, then the high pass shrine if the weather holds. Three weeks, four if the snows argue." Every word of it was a lie built on real bones; those shrines existed, that route was walked every generation, and no one questioned a warden who went to pray. That was the cruelest part of the lie: it was believable precisely because everyone believed she would sooner die than neglect the gate. "You hold the words until I return."

Tamsin didn't take the bundle. "Seb."

"Tamsin."

"Look at me and tell me it's a pilgrimage."

The lamp between them hissed. Somewhere below, a cat crossed the granary floor, setting loose a soft avalanche of dust. Sebastian thought about all the truths stacked inside her like stones in a wall — the gate that named her bloodline its binding, the crack of light that had answered her doubt, the ash falling silver over the barley, the scale fused to the door like a wound cauterized shut — and she found that she could not pour even one of them out here, in a doorway, with the whole sleeping village below. Some truths needed distance or they crushed the people you loved most.

"It's a pilgrimage," she said, and hated how steady her voice came out. "And when I come back I'll tell you everything. That's the trade. Hold the words, keep the rites, and don't let the elders change anything while I'm gone. Especially the feeding-oil. They'll want to stretch it."

"Why especially the—"

"Because I'm asking." She pressed the bundle into Tamsin's hands and closed the other woman's fingers over it. Through the cloth, Tamsin must have felt the weight of the red cord, the weight of everything it meant, because her grip tightened suddenly, convulsively, as if Sebastian had handed her a sleeping animal that might wake.

"If you're not back by the new moon," Tamsin said, low, "I'm telling them everything I suspect. And Seb — I suspect more than you think."

For one naked moment Sebastian almost laughed, almost wept. "I know," she said. "That's why it has to be you."

She went down the outside stairs without looking back, because looking back was a luxury she'd priced that afternoon and couldn't afford.

* * *

The valley at midnight was a held note.

Frost had come early, the first hard frost of the season, and it had turned every surface to quiet silver — fences, gateposts, the black glassy furrows of the turnip fields. Her breath plumed. The moon was a paring of itself, three days from dark, which was the only reason she'd chosen tonight; the gate's threshold read best by little light, Ivo said, the way a scar reads best in shadow. He and Kestrel would be waiting at the millrace below the terraces, where the water ran loud enough to cover voices.

She walked the long way round anyway. Past the well where she'd learned to draw water standing on a stool. Past the schoolhouse whose bell she had rung every morning of her childhood, past the smithy where the coals banked themselves down to a dull red memory. Each building wore frost like a shroud, and she found herself memorizing them against her will, cataloguing angles and smells — woodsmoke and wet stone and the particular sweetness of the cider press — the way a traveler memorizes an inn she does not expect to see again.

*Don't,* she told herself. *You're coming back.*

But her body didn't believe her, and bodies are honest creatures. Her stride slowed at the low wall by the meeting-house, where generations of children had scuffed their initials, including hers, cut crooked at eight years old beside her mother's neat ones. She laid her palm on the letters. The frost bit her skin and she welcomed the bite, because pain was simple, and nothing else about this night was.

Behind her, somewhere up the slope, a dog began to bark — one sharp note, then another, then stopped, undecided. She stood very still until it lost interest. Her heart went on barking long after.

* * *

They were shadows at the millrace, two shapes resolved out of darker dark. Ivo Marren stood with his hood back despite the cold, gray hair plastered to his skull, holding a lantern shuttered to a coin of light. Beside him Kestrel Vane had his sword slung across his back rather than his hip — deserters, he'd explained once, wear their weapons apologetically — and his eyes found her before any lantern could, the way they always did, and she looked away first, the way she always did, and both of them pretended the ritual meant nothing.

"You wrote them out," Ivo said, nodding at the bundle against her chest. Not a question either. He'd heard her copying them through the wall of his room for three nights, he admitted, the way he seemed to hear everything, a man tuned like an instrument to frequencies no one else claimed existed.

"All of them. Every rite, every seasonal variation, the feeding-schedule, the hinge-oil recipe, the words for closing if—" She stopped. There was no *if*. "Everything Tamsin needs."

"And the lie?"

"Delivered. Pilgrimage, north loop, back by the new moon."

Kestrel shifted. In the shuttered light his face was all planes and restraint. "New moon's nineteen days. If the crossing takes longer—"

"It won't." She said it too fast and knew it. "It can't."

No one answered that, which was its own kind of answer. Nineteen days. She had counted them against the dragon-fire's faltering, against the ash on the wind, against the image that had haunted her since the gate sang to her — a flame in a black country, burning down like a candle in a draft, and no one left alive who remembered it was there.

"We cross at the ford," Ivo said softly. "Not the gate."

Sebastian turned on him. "What?"

"You heard me." His voice stayed gentle, maddeningly gentle, the voice of a man who had delivered bad news in shrines for thirty years. "The Hollow Gate opens inward or not at all — you proved that yourself, all three of us did, and I watched you bleed for the trying. But Vael is a whole country, girl, not a room. A sealed room still has walls, and walls have places where the mortar failed. The boundary is thinnest at the old ford, where the river crosses the line. Your grandmother's records call it the Weeping Stone. Prayers offered there used to leave the valley. That's how you know the seal is weak — prayers leak."

"So we walk out through a crack in the world instead of the door I've kept my whole life." The bitterness surprised her with its heat. "All those years. All that oil. All those words I didn't understand."

"They weren't wasted." Ivo's hand hovered near her shoulder and did not land; he had that courtesy, at least, the courtesy of priests who have learned that comfort can bruise. "Whatever the gate is, whoever built it — it chose your blood to trust. Tonight it may be glad of it."

She wanted to ask what he meant. She was afraid he'd answer.

They went up along the millrace single file, Ivo leading with his dead-god sense of direction, Kestrel behind her close enough that she could hear his breathing, measured and deliberate, a man rationing himself. The valley fell away behind them terrace by terrace, and with every step something tore in her a little, quietly, like cloth giving way stitch by stitch. She did not look back. Looking back was for people whose homes would still be there when they returned.

The river announced the boundary before anything else did. Its sound changed — deepened, thickened, as though it flowed over something submerged and vast. Mist gathered on the water in a way mist shouldn't, pooling in a standing wall across the current, and through it she saw it: a great stone leaning over the far bank like a mourner, slick and pale, weeping actual water down its face in slow beads that caught the shuttered lantern light. The Weeping Stone. Around its base the reeds grew bent outward, all of them, every stalk straining away from the valley as if pointing home to a country none of them remembered.

Ivo raised his hand. Stopped. Turned to her.

"When you cross," he said, "it will hurt. I won't pretend otherwise. You were born inside the seal, Sebastian. Everything you are has grown around it, the way a tree grows around a fence wire. On the other side, the fence comes out."

"What does that mean?" Kestrel asked sharply. "Concretely."

"It means she'll feel the world." Ivo's eyes were kind and terrible. "All of it. Every prayer still rising off the land, every god still smoldering in his ruin, every thread of the old magic your valley was built to keep out. For a few minutes, perhaps longer, she will feel more than any living person was meant to feel. And then—" he hesitated, "—then either it becomes bearable, or she learns why they built the cage."

Silence, except the river, except the stone weeping its slow beads.

Sebastian found she wasn't afraid. That frightened her more than anything — that some part of her, the part the gate had sung to, the part that had heard her own name in a dead language, was *eager*.

"Well," she said. "I didn't walk all night to stand at the crack in the world admiring it."

She stepped forward, and Kestrel's hand caught her wrist — quick, instinctive, gone almost before she registered it, but the warmth of it stayed like a brand.

"For what it's worth," he said, and his voice was rougher than usual, "whatever's on the other side — you won't meet it alone."

She nodded, because speaking would have undone her, and stepped into the mist.

* * *

The world broke open.

There was no threshold, no line drawn in the water, no moment she could later point to. There was simply the valley's hush — that lifelong, blessed, suffocating quiet she had never once noticed because she had never known anything else — and then its absence, total, instantaneous, like a man deaf from birth hearing his first sound and it is every sound ever made at once.

She screamed. She was fairly sure she screamed. Sound was one of the things flooding her, after all — the river singing in a register below hearing, the stones humming their slow geological psalm, the frost on the reeds chiming as it melted molecule by molecule in the warmth of her passing breath. Light poured into her that had nothing to do with the moon: the land itself glowed, layered, *haunted*, every field a palimpsest of everything that had ever happened on it, wheat-ghosts and wedding-dances and funeral fires all burning simultaneously in her sight. And underneath it all, threading through it, prayer. Thousands of threads, tens of thousands, rising off the earth like heat-haze — old prayers, dead prayers, prayers two centuries unanswered still rising faithfully from ground that remembered the people who'd spoken them — and every single one of them pulled at her, plucked at her, called her by a name that was and was not Sebastian Oyelaran.

Her knees hit the far bank. Gravel. Real gravel, ordinary gravel, and she clung to its ordinariness like a drowning woman. Her blood felt molten. Her skin felt transparent. Something was pouring *out* of her as fast as the world poured in — she could feel it now, unmistakable, the truth Ivo had tried to soften: a golden thread running out of her chest and back toward the valley, back toward the gate, feeding it, feeding whatever slept behind it, and the thread was thinner than it should have been. Thinner than it had been last year. Thinner every year.

*Oh,* she thought, with terrible clarity, through the roaring blaze of the world. *That's what the renewals cost. That's what I've been paying.*

Someone was holding her. Broad hands, one at her shoulder, one bracing her head off the stones, and a voice saying her name — her plain valley name, just Sebastian, no dead-language harmonics — over and over. Kestrel. When she could finally focus, his face was above hers, white-lipped, terrified, and beyond him Ivo knelt with his head bowed, lips moving, a defrocked priest praying to gods he'd sworn he no longer served, and neither of them mattered as much as the fact that she was lying in a country erased from every map and she could *feel it*, feel the whole vast grieving body of Vael beneath her, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever touched.

"Grief and wonder," Ivo murmured, when she could sit up. He said it like a diagnosis. "In equal measure. That's how it felt for us too, the old ones said. The faithful called it being seen by God. The honest called it being flayed."

Sebastian laughed. It came out cracked and wet and real. She wiped her face with a frozen hand and looked back the way they'd come.

The mist wall hung over the river, seamless. Beyond it, faint as memory, the valley's lights — two or three stubborn windows, one of them surely Tamsin's, burning for her. Home, reduced to three candles and a lie.

She stood. Her legs held. The world roared in her ears and her blood ran gold and thin, and the Weeping Stone wept beside her, and somewhere north, past ruined roads and drowned villages and a king's hungry soldiers, a dying dragon waited to ask her the only question that had ever mattered.

"Walk," she said, before her courage could cool. "While I still remember what I'm walking away from."

And the three of them went up out of the river-mist together, into the country with no name.
