## 8. The Country With No Name

The road remembered them before they reached it.

Kestrel felt it first as a warmth under his boots, faint as sun-struck stone at dusk, though the morning was gray and cold enough that his breath hung in ribbons. He stopped, crouched, and pressed his palm flat against the surface. The cobbles were old — older than anything in the valley, older than the king's grandfather, older than the language the king's grandfather had spoken — and beneath the frost-slick surface they carried a heat that had no source he could name. Not fire. Not sun. Something more like *use*, the accumulated warmth of ten thousand feet that had walked here once and would never walk again.

"It knows we're on it," Sebastian said behind him. She had her hand on the strap of her pack and her chin lifted, the way she did when she was trying not to look afraid. Three days out of the gate and she still moved like a woman walking on the deck of a ship — her body expecting walls that weren't there, expecting the valley's close green horizon, and finding instead a sky that went on and on like an accusation.

"Roads don't know things," Kestrel said, and stood, and wiped his hand on his coat, and did not say *but this one does.*

Ivo Marren laughed softly from the rear of their little column. It was not a happy sound. "In Vael," the priest said, "everything remembers. That is the whole of the country's tragedy, knight. It was never destroyed. It was only *interrupted*."

They walked on. The road ran straight as a hymn through country that had once been wheat.

That was the thing Kestrel could not stop seeing, the thing that made his chest ache in a way he had no honest name for: the ghost of the harvest. The fields on either side were fallow, gone to gray scrub and pale grass, but the furrows were still there, curving with the land in long patient lines, and every few hundred paces a stone fence post stood where a farmer had set it, wearing two centuries of weather like a coat. Once — he counted, because counting was how he kept his mind from running — once there had been a boundary oak at each field's corner. The oaks were gone, cut or burned or simply aged into nothing, but their stumps remained, wide as wagon wheels, soft with moss. He mapped them anyway. He marked each one on the paper he carried, each stump, each fallen wall, each place where the road forked around a village that no longer existed, because mapping was the only prayer he had left that anyone might answer.

*Here stood a thing,* his map said, in his small careful hand. *Here. Here. Here.*

He did not write down the other thing he knew. The thing he had known since the first night, when they'd made cold camp in the lee of a ruined tollhouse and he had lain awake listening to Sebastian breathe and understood, with the sick certainty of a man reading his own death warrant, that he had walked this road before.

* * *

They came to the first village on the fourth day, and it was singing.

The sound reached them before the sight of it — a thin, layered murmur, like wind through a field of dry wheat, but with voices in it. Kestrel held up a fist and the party stopped, and for a long moment the three of them stood in the road listening to a sound that had no right to be.

"It's not a trap," Ivo said at last. His voice had gone gentle, the way it did when he spoke of the gods, which was the only time it went gentle. "It's a memory. They replay. The villages — the ones that were full, when the sealing came — they replay their last days. Over and over. I told you the country was interrupted. This is what interruption looks like when it's been running for two hundred years."

"Like a wheel with a broken spoke," Sebastian said. "Turning and turning and catching on the same place."

They crested the low rise, and there it was.

The village sat in a shallow bowl of land, perhaps forty houses around a well and a market green, and every one of the houses was whole. That was the horror of it, Kestrel thought. Not ruin. Ruin he could have borne; ruin was honest. But these houses had roofs of thatch gone silver-gray, doors hanging open on warm interiors, washing lines with shirts still pinned that had not moved in two hundred years because there was no wind here — he noticed that only now, the terrible stillness of the air, as if the whole valley-bowl were holding its breath. And in the lanes between the houses walked the people.

They were not people. He knew that. They were light, mostly, or the memory of light — figures with the texture of heat-shimmer, going about their final afternoon. A woman carried a basket toward a door. A man repaired a fence rail with motions so practiced they had worn smooth as river stones. Children chased a dog that was only a suggestion of a dog, a brown blur of devotion, around and around the well. And everywhere, under everything, the murmur: fragments of ordinary speech, the last conversations of the last day, worn down by two centuries of repetition until they were no longer words so much as the *shape* of words, the melody of a market afternoon with the meaning boiled away.

"—and if he doesn't pay by Friday—"

"—told her, I told her, marigolds, not—"

"—Mama, look, look what I—"

Kestrel stood at the edge of the green with his hand on his sword hilt out of pure reflex, and hated himself for it, and took the hand away. Sebastian walked forward as if pulled on a line. She stopped at the well, where a girl of maybe eight was drawing water with a rope that cast no shadow, and Kestrel saw my lady warden reach out — slowly, the way you'd reach toward a bird — and her fingers passed through the girl's shoulder like fingers through smoke.

The girl did not notice. The girl had been drawing that water, in that light, for two hundred years.

"She can't see us," Sebastian said. Her voice was very quiet. "Or she can't *anymore*. Ivo — is she suffering?"

Ivo had not come onto the green. He stood at the lane's mouth with his head bowed, and when he answered he did not lift it. "I don't know. I have asked myself that since I was a boy and found the first of these. I believe they don't suffer. I believe it is like — like the moment in a dream when you know you are dreaming and cannot wake, and cannot mind. But I don't *know*, warden. No one knows. The priests who might have known were the ones who did this."

Kestrel crouched at the edge of the green and took out his map, and his hands were not quite steady, and he drew the village. Forty-one houses. One well. One church — small, whitewashed once, its bell still in the tower, though when the memory-people passed beneath it none of them ever looked up, and he understood with a lurch that the bell had been rung already, that the last ringing was part of the day they were all still living, and had perhaps already happened, or perhaps was always about to happen, and there was no difference here anymore.

He wrote the village's name on the map because the name was carved on the well: *Harrowmere*. And then, because he was a coward in exactly one direction, he did not write the other thing. He did not write: *I have been here. I was here in the spring of the king's war, with a column of forty men, and we camped on this green, and the village was already empty then, and Sergeant Hollis had said it was like walking through a held breath, and I had laughed at him.*

He did not write: *I know the way to the ford because I forded it at midnight with my boots around my neck.*

* * *

That night they camped in the church at Harrowmere, because the walls were sound and the roof did not leak and because, Kestrel suspected, all three of them wanted to be near the one building that had been built for holding fear.

He took the first watch. The memory-people did not come into the church — he had watched all afternoon, and their loops of light never crossed the threshold, as if the last day of Harrowmere had contained no one going to prayers, which was its own small grief. So the church was theirs, and quiet, and in the dark the murmur from the green was only just audible, like surf heard from a hill.

Sebastian could not sleep. He heard her moving near the altar, and then her voice, low: "Kestrel. Are you awake?"

"No," he said, and heard her huff, and sat up.

She came and sat against the pillar near him, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and the valley's green soap she still used, hoarded from home. In the dark her face was a pale oval and her eyes were the eyes of a woman who had seen a child draw water from a well for two hundred years and could not put it down.

"Your map," she said. "You've been marking everything. Stumps. Walls. The places where the road forks. Why?"

"Because no one else has," he said. "Because the crown erased this country from every map it owned, and someone should know where things were. Because—" He stopped. The true answer was on the tip of his tongue and he swallowed it like a stone. *Because I was here. Because I deserted on this soil, three miles south of where you're sitting, and I have spent six years telling myself I ran toward mercy when the truth is I ran toward the valley because it was the one door out of this country and I could not bear to stay in it another night.*

"Because?" she prompted.

"Because if we're going to cross a country that remembers," he said, "someone should remember it back."

She was quiet a while. Then she said, "You knew the ford at the Graywater before Ivo described it. This morning. You said, *the crossing is wide, we'll want the old bridge stones two miles down.* Ivo never said anything about bridge stones. I've been thinking about that all day."

The surf-sound of Harrowmere filled the silence. Kestrel looked at the dark window where the memory of a market afternoon glimmered, and he thought: *tell her.* He thought: *she broke rules for her valley; I broke the worst rule of my life for a village like this one; we are the same animal, she will understand.* He thought all of that, and then he thought of the word *deserter*, which was stitched into him now like a second name, and of how it would sound in her mouth, *deserter*, after she had begun to look at him the way she had begun to look at him, and he was ashamed of the thought even as he had it, and he had it anyway.

"I was in the king's army," he said. "The southern campaigns. I saw — a great deal of this country, before it was closed to us. Some of it stayed with me." It was true. Every word was true. It was also a lie the way a wall with one stone missing is a wall.

She watched him in the dark, and he felt her deciding whether to press, and he braced himself, and she did not press. "All right," she said. "Keep your map honest, Kestrel Vane. That's all I ask of anyone."

After she went back to her bedroll he sat his watch and did not sleep, and out on the green the children of Harrowmere chased their ghost of a dog around the well, and around, and around.

* * *

The second village was drowned.

They smelled the water before they saw it — a green, standing smell, pond-weed and silt and something under it, something sweeter that Kestrel chose not to name. The road led them down through alders that grew in a place alders should not grow, and then the trees ended and there was the valley floor ahead of them, and it was a lake.

It had not been a lake. His map told him it had not been a lake: the road ran straight into the water and did not come out the other side, and the tops of chimneys broke the surface in a ragged line a half mile out, and a church weathervane — a fish, ironically, or perhaps not ironically at all — tilted just above the ripple-line, turning slowly, though there was no wind.

"A prayer," Ivo said.

Kestrel turned. The priest stood at the water's edge with his hands hanging open at his sides, and his face was the face of a man watching a doctrine die in front of him.

"What?" Sebastian said.

"A prayer did this." Ivo's voice was flat with a horror that had no heat in it, which was somehow worse than screaming. "Someone in that village — someone prayed for rain. Or for water. And something *answered*. Look at the water line on the alders — it's dropping. It has been dropping for years. Whatever came here came, and gave, and gave past all sense, and then moved on, or burned out, and now the village is just — a wound that is slowly closing." He crouched and touched the water and drew his hand back as if scalded, though it could not have been more than cold. "This is what the gods of Vael are now, warden. This is what two hundred years of starving does to a divine nature. They answer. That is all they remember how to do. They answer without judgment, without measure, without *love* — because love is a discipline, and they have been locked in the dark too long to keep any discipline at all. A starving dog does not take one bite, Kestrel. A starving dog takes the whole hand."

Kestrel looked at the chimneys. He thought about the farmer who must have knelt somewhere under that water — in a field gone to dust, maybe, in a summer like the summers they'd been having — and prayed with his whole chest, *please, let it rain, let it rain, let it rain.* And something vast and starving and kind had leaned down out of the broken sky of a sealed country and said *yes*, and *yes*, and *yes*.

He took out his map. His hand was steady now, steadier than it had been at Harrowmere, because horror past a certain point did that to him, froze him into precision the way cold froze water. He marked the lake. He marked the drowned road's bearing, where it entered the water, and estimated where it would emerge on the far shore, and drew the route around the lake's northern rim, and wrote, in his small careful hand: *Village of —* and stopped.

There was a signpost at the water's edge. It had been tall once; the water had taken its lower half. What remained above the surface bore a carved name, softened by moss, and Kestrel waded in to his knees to read it, because his map had to be honest, because that was the one thing he had left that was honest.

*Wetherlow.*

He stood in the cold water with the name in his eyes and the sweet rot-smell in his throat, and he did not write it down. Not because he didn't know it. Because he knew it too well. Wetherlow. He had eaten a meal in Wetherlow. He had slept one night in its inn, on the king's coin, the spring before the sealing, and the innkeeper's wife had given him extra bread because he was young and she had a son his age, and he had thought nothing of it, nothing at all, and now her house was forty feet down in the dark under his boots.

He waded back. He wrote: *Wetherlow, drowned.* And beneath it, where no one would look unless they already knew to look, in a shorthand only he would ever read, he added the small mark that meant, in his private grammar of guilt: *I was here.*

* * *

They rounded the lake through the night and the next day, and the country changed around them the way a face changes while you watch it, so gradually you doubt your own eyes.

The echoes thinned. Harrowmere's warm murmur and Wetherlow's drowned silence gave way to villages that were only foundations, only names on stones, only roads that forked around absences. And in their place came other things. Kestrel began to find them on the high ground: cairns that were not cairns, stacked in spirals rather than heaps, warm to the touch. Trees with ribbons tied to their branches, the ribbons not rotted though they must be two centuries old, turning in a wind that touched nothing else. Once, at dusk, he saw lights moving on a far hillside in patterns no shepherd would use — deliberate, slow, like a procession — and when he called Sebastian's attention to it the lights were gone, but the hillside grass was bent in a long arc, as if something many-footed had passed that way recently and carefully.

"They're coming out of hiding," Ivo said, watching the bent grass with an expression Kestrel could not read. "The small ones. The hearth-spirits, the road-wights, the gods of wells and doorsteps. The dragon-fire is guttering — we've known that since the ash came — and when the fire fails, the seal fails, and everything sealed is going to be loose. They can feel it. The whole country can feel it." He turned to Sebastian, and in the last light his face looked very old. "It's going to get crowded on these roads, warden. Everything in Vael is going to be moving toward the mountain. Everything is going to want to be near the fire before it goes out."

That night they made camp in the shell of a waystation — a walled yard, a roof half-fallen, a hearth that Kestrel built a fire in because a fire was a small defiance of all this silence. Sebastian slept first, exhausted, her hand near her pack the way she still slept, the gatekeeper's habit, guarding the thing she carried. Ivo prayed silently, or something like praying; his lips moved and his eyes were open.

Kestrel sat by the fire and worked on his map by its light, and it was while he was drawing the day's route that he found it.

A road. On the western edge of his survey, where he'd sketched the country from a ridge at noon, there was a road he had not marked, because he had not seen it at noon. It ran south from the main way, toward the hills — toward the border country, the marches, the place where the king's army had mustered in the last spring before the sealing. He knew that road. He had marched down it in the rain with forty men and a captain who read orders aloud like sentences of death, because they were. He had marched down it, and three days later he had stood in a temple square in a town whose name he still would not say, and he had held a torch, and he had not lit it, and he had not put it down either, and the men behind him had lit theirs, and that was the whole of his desertion: a torch he did not light and did not drop, held burning in his fist while a temple burned around the torch's smaller light, and then the long walk south, and the gate, and the valley, and six years of telling himself a gentler story.

The road on his map ran through all of it. He had drawn it without meaning to. His hand remembered what his mouth would not say.

"Kestrel." Sebastian's voice, soft with sleep. She had turned toward the fire. "You've stopped drawing. You've been staring at the same place for a long time."

He looked up. The firelight moved on her face. Behind her, through the broken roof, the stars of the sealed country were coming out — stars that were almost the stars of home, but not quite, arranged by some older and stranger reckoning.

"There's a shrine ahead," he said, because it was true, because he had seen it sketched on the oldest of the waystation walls that afternoon, a chalk drawing centuries old: a spring, a stone, an offering bowl. "Ivo will want to see it. And the country's changing — you felt it today. Whatever's waking in Vael, we're walking straight into the middle of it."

She watched him a moment longer, with those eyes that noticed bridge stones and unmarked roads and the things men did not say, and then she nodded and lay back down. "Keep your map honest," she murmured, already half asleep.

Kestrel looked at the road he had drawn. Then, slowly, he turned the map over, and on the blank back, in the same small careful hand, he wrote the name of the town he had never written anywhere, the name he had carried sealed for six years like a coal in his fist:

*Corven's Cross.*

And outside, far off in the dark, something answered — not in words, but in a long, low sound like a bell rung underwater, rolling across the hills from the direction of the shrine, and the fire shivered, and Sebastian's eyes opened, and none of them slept again that night.
