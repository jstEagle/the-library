## 9. Prayers Answered Badly

They smelled the water before they saw it.

Ivo Marren had spent eleven years in a valley that had forgotten what weather was — where rain arrived on schedule, polite as a tax collector, and never fell harder than the crops required — and so his body had unlearned the language of water. But standing on the ridge above the shrine road at first light, he felt something older than learning stir in him: the mineral breath of standing flood, the green rot-smell of things drowned and swelling, the particular silence that hangs over deep water the way a sheet hangs over furniture in a house whose owner will not return.

"There," Sebastian said beside him, and her voice did something he had not heard it do before. It went small.

Below them, where Kestrel's map showed a village called Merrow's Ford — three hundred souls, a mill, a shrine to a nameless god of wells — there was only water. A lake that should not exist. It lay across the valley floor like spilled mercury in the grey dawn, perfectly still, perfectly flat, without a single ripple, as though the whole drowned world beneath it were holding its breath. The mill's roof broke the surface at one corner, black and tilted, hung with weed. The shrine's bell-tower stood further out, submerged to its second-story windows, and the bell itself — Ivo could just make it out through the clear, terrible water — hung silent, furred with silt, its rope trailing upward like something reaching for help.

The fields around it were flooded too, but not ruined. That was the thing that made his stomach turn over slowly, like a key in an old lock. The barley stood in six inches of water and it was *flourishing* — silver-green, heavy-headed, growing even now as they watched, stalks creaking audibly in the stillness as they lengthened. The gods of Vael had answered a farmer's prayer for rain with perfect fidelity and no judgment whatsoever, and the answer had not killed the crops. It had killed everything else.

"Three hundred people," Kestrel said quietly. He had gone the color of ash himself, and his hand rested on the pommel of a sword he had sworn never to draw again. "Where are—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. The water told them.

Ivo made himself look. He was a priest; looking was his trade; he had spent years refusing to look at anything and this, he thought, was the penance for it. Beneath the glass-clear surface, shapes moved in the slow currents — or didn't move, which was worse. A figure sat upright in the doorway of the sunken millhouse, hands folded in its lap, hair streaming like ink. Another drifted near the bell-tower, arms out, face turned up toward a sky it could no longer reach. They did not decay the way bodies ought to decay. The water kept them. The water *tended* them, the way a devoted servant tends a room after the master has died: everything preserved, everything arranged, everything horribly, meticulously cared for.

"The prayer," Sebastian said. Her knuckles were white around her staff. "Tell me the prayer."

"I don't know it." Ivo heard his own voice come out flat and strange. "But I know the shape of it. Every farmer in every country prays the same prayer when the rains fail. *Send water. Send enough.*" He swallowed. "No one ever says *but not too much*. Why would they? You don't guard against your own salvation."

* * *

They went down to the water because they had to. That was becoming the shape of their journey — each of them compelled forward by things they could not bear and could not refuse. Kestrel led, picking a path along the flooded road's high crown, where the water came only to their shins. Sebastian walked with her lantern lit in full daylight, which Ivo understood she had done since the night the far-off bell-sound rolled over them; she would not say why, and he did not ask, because he had seen the small warm glow inside the glass move once, faintly, against its wick, like something turning over in sleep.

Ivo walked last, and Ivo prayed, and for the first time in eleven years his prayers went somewhere.

It was not like the valley, where words fell from his mouth like stones down a dry well. Here each word left him and *landed*, and he felt it land, felt the land take note of it, felt something vast and famished attend to the sound of a human voice addressing the divine. He stopped praying mid-sentence. He was afraid of being understood too well.

At the edge of the village proper, where the water deepened past wading, they found the boats.

There were seven of them, drawn up on the drowned road in a neat row, tied off to a fencepost with tidy knots. Fishing skiffs, a coracle, a flat-bottomed punt — all of them empty, all of them freshly bailed, all of them pointing out toward the bell-tower as if waiting. And kneeling in the shallows beside the third boat was a man.

He was alive. That detail reached Ivo a half-second before the rest of him did — the mud-caked clothes, the hands worn through to rawness, the eyes so red-rimmed and wide that the whites showed all around the irises like a frightened horse. Alive, and digging. His hands scooped at the flooded roadbed, lifted a double handful of mud and gravel, and cast it into the water. Then again. Then again. The motion had the terrible smoothness of long repetition, hours of it, days perhaps.

"Hallo," Kestrel called softly, the way you speak to something wild. "Friend. Hallo."

The man looked up. Mud streaked his face in stripes, and Ivo realized with a lurch that the stripes were old — dried and re-wetted and dried again, layers of them, the geology of grief.

"You're not from the water," the man said. His voice was rusted from disuse. "You're not from the water. You're not—" He clambered up, slipping, staring at them as if they might dissolve. "Are you real? Put your hand in the water. Please. Please put your hand in the water."

Kestrel glanced at Sebastian, then crouched and slid his bare hand into the flood. Nothing happened. The water lay quiet around his wrist.

"It doesn't take you," the man whispered. He began to laugh, or weep, or both; the sound had no floor to it. "It took everyone. Everyone who lived here. It took them the first night, gentle as anything, like the water loved them. But it won't take me. I've walked in nine times. Nine times. It sets me back on the bank like I'm a — like I'm a *cat* someone's carrying by the scruff." He held up his hands, wrecked and bleeding at the cuticles. "So I'm building the road back up. If I can raise the road, maybe the water will go down. Maybe they can come up. They're all still down there. Have you seen? They're all still *down there*, and they're not dead, they're not dead, they're just — waiting for me to finish the road."

Ivo stepped forward, and the man's eyes fixed on him, and narrowed.

"Clergy," the man said. The word came out with a lifetime of small-town contempt in it, and then, horribly, it collapsed into pleading. "You're clergy. Real clergy. From outside. Tell me — tell me which god did this. Tell me its name, so I know who to hate. Because I prayed, see. I prayed at the shrine like my father and his father, proper prayers, proper offerings, first pour of every barrel. And the rains failed two seasons running, and the wells dropped, and I got desperate, and I prayed *hard*. I prayed the way you're supposed to. With everything. And something *answered*." He laughed that floorless laugh again. "It rained for nine days. Nine days of the sweetest, softest, most blessed rain you ever saw, warm as milk, falling straight down, no wind, no thunder, like the sky had been waiting its whole life for the chance. And the river came up kind. And the wells filled sweet. And on the ninth night the water walked up out of the channels — *walked*, Father, I watched it lift itself up over the levee like a man stepping over a fence — and it went house to house, door to door, patient as a shepherd, and it gathered them in."

His voice cracked apart.

"And the last thing my daughter said — she was seven — the last thing she said was, *Papa, the rain loves us, look how much it loves us.*"

Nobody spoke. The barley creaked and grew. Somewhere out in the lake, beneath the surface, in the sunken streets of Merrow's Ford, Ivo knew that three hundred people were going about the motions of their lives in slow motion, tended by the water, kept, preserved — answered. Their prayers for safety, their prayers for good harvests, their small nightly graces — all of them answered too, all of them granted, forever, here at the bottom of a lake that loved them with a love that had never once asked what love should *do*.

"I'm sorry," Sebastian said, and her voice shook. "We — there may be a way. We're trying to find a way to stop it. All of it."

The farmer looked at her for a long moment. Then he went back to his knees and picked up another double handful of road.

"Stop it," he said, not unkindly, already elsewhere. "Yes. Stop it. I'll have the road finished soon. Go on, then. Don't stand in my light."

* * *

They took one of the boats. It seemed right, somehow, and also seemed like theft, and Ivo offered a silent apology to no one in particular for both. Kestrel sculled them out across the drowned village toward the bell-tower, because Sebastian said she needed to see the shrine, and because none of them had the heart to say aloud what they were really going to see.

The water was clearer than any natural water Ivo had ever known. It was clear the way glass is clear, the way a lens is clear — made for seeing, made for showing. Below the keel, the village of Merrow's Ford lay complete and lit by some sourceless gentleness: streets, gardens, laundry lines with sheets drifting like jellyfish, a dog asleep on a doorstep that was now twelve feet under. People moved along those streets. Slowly. So slowly. A woman raised a hand to wave at someone across a lane, and the wave took the length of a spoken sentence to complete, graceful as kelp. Children played a game with a hoop, rolling it down a street in a dreamer's time, and Ivo watched one child's laughing mouth open around a word that would not arrive for another minute, and he had to close his eyes.

"They're alive," Sebastian whispered. It was not relief in her voice. It was horror wearing relief's clothing. "Ivo — they're *alive*. Down there. Living."

"Yes."

"That's worse." She pressed her fist to her mouth. "God forgive me, that is so much worse than if they were dead. Dead ends. This doesn't end. This is their answered prayer going on and on and *on*—"

"Look at the shrine," Kestrel said.

The bell-tower rose out of the water ahead of them, and as they drew alongside, Ivo saw what had happened to it — what the answering power had done to the place where the prayer had been spoken. The stone of the tower was not eroded, nor cracked, nor burned. It had been *fed upon*. The granite had a gnawed quality, a honeycombed delicacy, like bread after locusts, like bone picked clean by something that had eaten with enormous care and infinite hunger. And through the gaps in the devoured stone, filling the tower's interior from belfry to flooded nave, grew light — a pale gold radiance like the memory of harvest, dense and pulsing very slightly, very slowly, with a rhythm.

A rhythm. Ivo counted without wanting to. One pulse every four seconds.

The rhythm of a human heartbeat. Of three hundred human heartbeats, slowed and blended into one.

"It's still here," he breathed. "The god. Whatever answered him — it's still *here*. It ate the shrine — the old offerings, the consecrated stone, centuries of stored devotion — and it's still here, wrapped around them, keeping them, because keeping them is what was asked. *Keep my family safe. Keep them through the hard winter. Keep them.*" His voice failed and he forced it on. "It's not malicious. That's what I keep — that's what none of you understand yet. Look at it. It's *starving*. It found one lit candle in a dark country and it has thrown itself onto that flame like a moth, like a beggar at a feast, and it cannot stop eating and it cannot stop giving, because giving is all it has left, and it does not know how to do either one *moderately*."

Sebastian stared down into the golden pulse below them, and Ivo saw her hand drift, unconsciously, to the lantern hanging at her belt — the lantern with the small warm glow inside that moved sometimes against its wick.

"And the others?" she asked quietly. "The ones that answered the child in my valley. Are they like this?"

"Worse, probably. Or better. There's no scale for it anymore." Ivo gripped the gunwale. "Understand what I am telling you. I served these gods. I was ordained into a faith with ten thousand names and I thought — I was taught — that divinity meant discernment. Judgment. The wisdom to say *no*, or *not yet*, or *not thus*. That was the whole covenant, Sebastian. We gave them worship and they gave us measure. Boundaries. A god who could withhold was a god who could be trusted to give." He looked down at the drowned, tended, endlessly-preserved village, at the woman waving her eternal wave, at the child's laughter arriving a minute late, and he heard his own voice go ragged. "These gods have been sealed in the dark for two hundred years with nothing. No worship. No offering. No voice. And now the seal is thin and prayers are leaking through like light under a door, and they are answering *everything*, *all at once*, *without limit*, because a starving creature cannot taste what it swallows. Rain without judgment. Mercy without measure. Love without sense. Every prayer granted exactly as spoken and not one grain further, and not one grain less."

"Then we stop them," Kestrel said. "Whatever's feeding them. Whatever door this is coming through. We shut it."

"Do we?" Ivo turned on him, and the grief of eleven years came up in his throat like floodwater. "Shut it on *that*?" He pointed down through the crystal water at the sleeping village. "Those people are alive. Wrong, damned, unbearable — but alive. Every miracle since we crossed the border is a starving god spending its last hoard on strangers' wishes, and if we slam the door, every one of them gutters out in the dark, and everything they've half-granted — everyone they've *kept* — goes with them. You want to shut the door on a child's laughter that takes a minute to finish?"

"Better a finished grave than an endless—"

"*Don't.*" It was Sebastian. She said it quietly, but the boat went still around the word, and even the water seemed to listen. She was staring down into the golden heartbeat-light, and her face had changed; behind the horror there was something else, something Ivo recognized from his own mirror on bad nights. Recognition. "Don't talk about graves yet," she said. "Not until we've seen the fire that's failing. Not until we know what happens to a kept thing when the keeper starves." She lifted her eyes to Ivo, and they were the eyes of someone doing arithmetic she hated. "Because that's the question, isn't it? These gods are starving. The dragon-fire is guttering. Something is feeding one with the other, and something is running out. What happens to Merrow's Ford — to all of them, all the answered prayers in this whole drowned country — when the last of it goes?"

And Ivo opened his mouth to say he didn't know —

— and the bell rang.

Once. Deep and sweet and appalling, rolling up out of the flooded belfry through the crystal water, through the boat's keel, through their bones. Three hundred voices far below them, in the sunken streets, all sighed at once — a sound like wind through a wheatfield, like a congregation settling into pews. The golden heartbeat-light pulsed, quickened, brightened.

And out across the water, from the direction of the rising road, the farmer's voice floated thin and clear over the still surface, singing — tunelessly, endlessly, happily — the way men sing when they have finally stopped needing to be heard:

*"Road'll be done by winter. Road'll be done by winter. Then they can all come home."*

Ivo looked north, toward Mount Cindral's distant smudge of smoke, and understood that whatever waited for them there had already begun to wait more urgently.
