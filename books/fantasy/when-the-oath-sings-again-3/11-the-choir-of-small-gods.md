## 11. The Choir of Small Gods

The cathedral announced itself long before they saw it.

They had been walking since dawn through a country that could not decide whether it was dead or dreaming — past orchards whose trees bore fruit made only of scent, past a mill whose wheel turned on water that had not flowed for two hundred years. Then the wind changed, and Sebastian heard it: a sound like a hymn sung underwater, rising and falling with a patience that no living throat possessed.

"There," Ivo said, and his voice had gone strange, the way it always did when the old country reached for him. He pointed through a stand of birches gone silver as candle-smoke.

The cathedral rose out of a shallow valley like a whale's ribcage picked clean. Its spire had fallen inward centuries ago, collapsing into its own nave so that the whole structure seemed to be bowing to itself. The stained glass had mostly gone, but here and there a pane survived, and the afternoon light came through them in colors Sebastian had no names for — a violet that tasted of plums she'd never eaten, a gold that hummed against her teeth. In the Hollow Gate valley, glass was for keeping weather out. Here it had clearly been for letting something *in*.

Kestrel drew up at the treeline and did not go further. She had learned to read his silences in the days since the ford; this one meant *I know this place*, and beneath it, quieter, *I am not sure I should be seen in it*.

"We'll make camp inside," Ivo said. "The roof over the apse still holds. And—" He stopped. Tilted his head like a bird. "Do you hear that?"

Sebastian heard it. Under the underwater hymn there were other voices now, thin and many, and they were not singing anymore.

They were arguing.

* * *

Inside, the cathedral was a forest of stone trees. Ferns grew from the cracks in the flagstones, softening every footfall, and the air smelled of cold incense and rain that had soaked into the walls so long ago it had become part of them. Doves — or something shaped like doves — stirred in the broken clerestory, and their wings let down little avalanches of pale dust.

Sebastian stopped three steps past the ruined doors.

The voices were coming from everywhere. From the hearth-shaped hollow where the great altar fire had once burned. From the worn dip in the threshold where ten thousand pilgrims' feet had polished the stone. From a wayside shrine tucked into an alcove, its saint's face rubbed featureless by touching hands. Small places. Humble places. Places that had once been loved.

And in each of those places, something was looking at her.

She did not see them at first so much as feel them, the way you feel a draft. Then the light shifted — the surviving panes dimming all at once, as if a cloud had crossed a sun that wasn't there — and she saw them.

They were small. That was the first thing, and somehow the most heartbreaking thing. She had spent her life beside a gate built to hold back gods, and she had imagined vastness, terror, forms too large for the eye. But these were hearth-spirits no bigger than cats, crouched in the shape of the fires they had tended, their bodies flickering orange at the seams. Road-wights hung in the air like heat-shimmer above the pilgrim path, wearing the suggestion of cloaks and staffs. A well-spirit pooled in the font like dark water with a patient face. There must have been dozens of them. Perhaps hundreds. Filling the cathedral the way dust fills a sunbeam, all of them turned toward the three travelers with an attention that had weight, like a hand laid gently on the back of the neck.

"They're starving," Ivo whispered. He had gone to his knees without seeming to decide to. His defrocked hands, which he usually kept hidden in his sleeves, were spread open on the ferns. "Gods below. Look at them. They're *starving*."

He was right. Once she saw it, she could not unsee it. The hearth-spirits guttered between shapes, unable to hold even the form of flame. The road-wights were translucent, their edges fraying like old cloth. The well-spirit's pool had shrunk to a puddle ringed by cracked stone. Everything about them was diminished, thinned, drawn down to the last ember of what they had been — and yet they had gathered here, in their god-house, and they had waited, and now they were looking at the first living souls to cross their threshold in two hundred years.

One of the hearth-spirits detached itself from the altar-hollow and came toward her.

It moved badly, stuttering, like a candle flame in a draft, and where it passed the ferns curled slightly away from its cold. Up close Sebastian could see that its fire-heart was nearly out — a red point no bigger than a spark, wrapped in layers of grey ash that used to be radiance. It stopped an arm's length away. It looked up at her.

And it spoke. Not in words. Not in the oath-language either, though she felt the shape of language underneath, the way you feel the shape of a bone through skin. What it gave her instead was meaning, arriving whole in her chest:

*You are warm.*

"I'm sorry," Sebastian said aloud, because she did not know what else to say to a dying god. Her voice sounded enormous in the nave. "I don't— we can't stay."

*You are going to the Fire,* the spirit said. All of them said it, suddenly, the whole congregation at once, a chord of need that made the surviving glass sing in sympathy. *We have felt it failing. We have felt it for years, the way a man feels winter coming in an old wound. You are walking toward Mount Cindral. Take us with you.*

"Take you—" Kestrel had come inside after all; he stood near the doors with his hand on his sword hilt, not drawing it, just holding it, the way a man holds a railing in rough seas. "How would we even—"

*Carry us,* said the road-wights, shimmering closer, and Sebastian understood that they meant it literally. Meant themselves as things that could be carried. *A coal from our hearth. A knot of our road-dust. A cup of our water. We are small. We have always been small. We ask only to be small in your direction.*

The well-spirit spoke last, and its voice was the deepest, a voice like the bottom of a cold well: *When the Fire dies, everything it holds up dies with it. We will not be killed. We will be* — and here the meaning bent, strained, reached for a word that did not exist in any tongue she knew — *unremembered. Even by the stones we lived in. Even by ourselves.*

Sebastian looked at Ivo. Ivo was weeping, openly, without shame, tears cutting tracks through the road-grime on his face.

"You see," he said hoarsely. "You see what they are. They're not the great ones. They're not the storm-gods and the death-lords your gate was built against. They're the god of the kettle. The god who kept travelers from twisting ankles. The god of the first warm room after snow." He laughed, wet and broken. "This is what we starved when we starved the old country. Not monsters. *These.*"

"And if we take them," Sebastian said slowly, "and the dragon-fire fails anyway—"

"Then they die warm," Ivo said. "Near the Fire that made them. Instead of here, alone, in the dark, waiting for a rescue that was never coming."

It was a good answer. It was the answer her heart wanted. And that was exactly why she made herself stand very still and look at the truth underneath it.

Because she had touched the gate. She had heard the oath name her bloodline as its binding, and she had felt the crack of light answer her doubt, and she knew now — knew in the marrow the way the spirits knew winter in an old wound — that she was not a traveler in this story. She was a mechanism. Her warmth, her life, poured year by year through the oath into the seal, and the seal fed the boundary, and the boundary was failing because *she* was being spent. Every mile she walked toward Cindral was a mile walked on credit she did not understand how to repay.

And these small gods, begging to be carried — they were asking her to carry them *on that credit*. To pour her dwindling self through whatever thread connected her to the Fire, warming a hundred dying embers on the way. Compassion said yes. Compassion said yes so loudly she could barely hear anything else.

But she had watched her mother age twenty years in the five years before she died. She knew what spending looked like from the outside.

"No," Sebastian said.

The word fell into the nave like a stone into the well-spirit's pool.

"No?" Ivo turned on her, and she had never seen him angry, not at the elders who mocked him, not at the rain that drowned the drowned village. "Sebastian—"

"No." She made herself meet the hearth-spirit's spark. It was the hardest thing she had done since leaving the valley; harder than the ford, harder than the echo-villages. "I can't. I'm sorry. I don't know what I am yet — what I cost, what I carry, what happens if I add you to it. If I say yes because it's kind, and it kills me on the ash road, then you die anyway, and so does everyone counting on me behind that gate. Kindness that spends what isn't yours to spend is just another kind of theft." Her voice cracked on the last word, because the hearth-spirit was still looking at her, patiently, the way a fire looks at someone who has come in from the cold. "I won't steal from myself. Not until I understand the price."

Silence. The doves resettled overhead. Somewhere far off, thunder that was not thunder rolled along the spine of the mountains — the dragon, perhaps, turning in its sleep of embers.

Then the hearth-spirit did something she did not expect.

It bowed. A small, stuttering inclination, flame bending toward her like grass toward wind. And the meaning that came with it carried no anger at all — only an old, old courtesy, the courtesy of a thing that had spent centuries learning to wait:

*You are careful. That is why we asked you. The careless ones would have said yes.*

* * *

They camped in the apse, under the one stretch of vaulted roof that still held, and the small gods withdrew — not away, she noticed, but to a polite distance, gathering in the transepts like parishioners giving the strangers room. Through the night she woke again and again to find them watching, dozens of faint lights in the dark of the crossing, and each time the watching felt less like hunger than like hope, which was worse.

Near dawn, Kestrel sat down beside her at the edge of the fire. He had taken first watch and given her the last hours of dark, and he handed her a strip of dried apple without comment, which was how he said good morning.

"They'll follow us anyway," he said quietly. "Some of them."

"I know."

"You could order them off. Ivo says you have the authority. Oath-blood."

"Ordering a starving creature away from food isn't authority." She chewed the apple, which tasted of a summer the valley had never grown. "It's just cruelty with better posture."

Kestrel was quiet a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "In the king's records — the ones I wasn't supposed to read — there was a term for gods like these. *Vestigia.* Footprint-gods. The crown's doctrine says they're residue. Leftovers. That when the great powers return, the small ones burn away like mist at sunrise." His jaw worked. "I helped enforce that doctrine for six years. Confiscated shrines. Smashed a road-wight's stone once because my sergeant told me it was idolatry." He looked at his hands. "It screamed. Did you know they scream? Nobody tells you they scream."

Sebastian looked at him — at the deserter knight who had walked out of an army rather than light one fire, and had never forgiven himself for the fires other men lit while he stood in the square doing nothing — and she put her hand over his scarred knuckles, and left it there.

"When we get to Cindral," she said, "if there's a way to save them that doesn't spend me blind, I'll find it. That's a promise I can keep. It's the only kind I'm offering anyone anymore."

From the darkness of the transept, faintly, the choir of small gods sighed — a sound like wind through a keyhole, like a congregation saying amen.

* * *

They broke camp at first light, and the small gods lined the ruined nave to watch them go. None of them begged again. That restraint undid her more than pleading would have. As she passed the altar-hollow, the hearth-spirit raised one guttering hand — a gesture she somehow understood as blessing, the kind given freely to those who refuse you, which is the only kind worth having.

They walked out through the collapsed western wall into grey morning, and the underwater hymn resumed behind them, softer now. Almost content.

It was Ivo who noticed, four miles on, at the place where the pilgrim road climbed toward the high moors.

"Sebastian." He had stopped. He was staring at her pack, at the iron lantern swinging from its strap — the valley lantern she'd carried since the gate, its candle long burnt out, its glass smoked black. "Your lantern."

"What of it?"

"It's lit."

She took it down with fingers gone suddenly clumsy. Behind the blackened glass, where no candle was, a small red spark sat in the bottom of the lantern like a seed in a furrow — pulsing gently, stubbornly, in a rhythm she recognized. The rhythm of the hearth-spirit's dying fire.

Along the lantern's inner surface, written in soot-faint letters that faded even as she read them, were words in the dead language of Vael. She could not translate them. But the meaning arrived in her chest the way all their meanings did, warm and small and unbearably polite:

*You said you could not carry us. You never said we could not carry ourselves. Burn slow, keeper. We will be quiet.*

Kestrel looked at the spark, then at her, and something that was half laugh and half dread crossed his face. "Congratulations," he said. "You've got a stowaway."

And far behind them, back along the road they had come, a horn sounded — a long, disciplined note, nothing like thunder, nothing like any god. It was the sound of men riding in formation, and it was coming from the east, from the direction of the king's country, and it did not stop.

Ivo went pale. "That's not for us," he said. "That's a hunt horn. Someone is driving the roads ahead of us — flushing everything that answers prayers out of the cover of the ruins."

The spark in the lantern flared once, sharply, like a small animal smelling smoke.

Sebastian closed her hand around the glass, hiding the light, and started to run.
