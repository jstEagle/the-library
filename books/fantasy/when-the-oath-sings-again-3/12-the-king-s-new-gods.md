## 12. The King's New Gods

Kestrel Vane had learned, in nine years of soldiering, to read a road the way other men read faces. This road was lying.

It lay in the way its ruts ran too straight for any cart that had ever carried grain. It lay in the hedgerows, hawthorn and blackthorn, that had been cut and re-cut for generations by hands that no longer existed. It lay in the milestone they passed at midmorning, a squat stone worn smooth by centuries of weather, on which the king's road-surveyors had chiseled fresh numbers over older, stranger letters — as if a name could be painted over the way a wall is whitewashed, and no one would ever smell the old paint beneath.

*You know this road,* it said to him, in the voice of every lie he had ever told himself. *You have marched it before.*

He said nothing. He rode at the rear of their little procession, where a rear-guard belonged, and watched the country of Vael unspool ahead of them like a banner someone had tried to burn and failed.

Sebastian rode first. She had taken to the saddle the way water takes to a crack in stone — quietly, inevitably, finding the shape of the thing that held her. Nine days out from the valley and she had stopped flinching at the horizon. That frightened him more than anything else. A woman who flinched was still measuring the world against what she knew. A woman who had stopped flinching was already becoming someone new.

Behind her rode Ivo Marren, gray and stooped in his saddle, muttering over the scrap of shrine-linen he'd taken from the drowned village. And behind Ivo, in a wicker cage lashed to the pack-mule, the thing they had not yet told Sebastian about.

The lantern. The hearth-god. The stowaway.

Kestrel had seen it the second night, when Sebastian slept and Ivo kept the fire. A light in the old man's lantern that was not flame — a warmth without color, a small gold breathing, like a coal that had decided to be alive. He had watched Ivo cup his hands around it and whisper, and he had watched the light lean into the old man's palms the way a cat leans into a known hand.

He had not told Sebastian. He told himself it was because she had enough to carry. He knew, in the honest hour of the night watch, that it was because he was afraid of what she would do — afraid she would turn around, afraid she would carry it back to the valley, afraid she would carry it *forward* and it would burn her the way everything in this country burned everything that loved it.

* * *

They came down out of the hills at noon and saw the village of Saint-Alder's-Reach, or what the maps would have called it if there had been maps. Kestrel knew its true name. He kept the true names the way a man keeps a wound — privately, and always touching it to make sure it still hurt.

The village was occupied.

Not garrisoned. Occupied. There was a difference, and every man who had ever served a crown knew it. A garrison protected. An occupation *harvested*. Wagons stood in the square, heavy-wheeled, canopied in oiled canvas, and soldiers in the king's slate-and-silver moved between the houses with the unhurried efficiency of men collecting taxes — except that what they carried out of the houses was not grain.

It was light. Kestrel saw it from the ridge, and his stomach turned over and kept turning. Cages, small ones, the kind falconers use, the kind made for songbirds — and in the cages, small burning shapes. A wisp of gold. A curl of green. Something blue and many-legged that pressed itself against the bars and was pressed back by a shimmer he recognized from chapel doctrine, from the diagrams in the banned books: *warding lattice, standard pattern, by royal warrant.*

"Down," he said, and it came out of him before thought, the old command voice, and both of them obeyed, and he hated that they obeyed, hated that the voice still worked, hated that some part of him was pleased it worked.

They lay in the bracken on the ridge and looked down at the king's new harvest.

"What are they doing," Sebastian said. Not a question. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the way it went when she was keeping herself from breaking something.

Ivo's answer came out of him like blood from a reopened cut. "They're catching them."

"Gods." She said the word the way she said *gate* — with weight, with the weight of a woman who had spent her whole life on her knees before a door. "They're catching *gods*."

"Small ones." Ivo's hands were shaking. Kestrel watched the old man watch the cages and understood that for Ivo this was not horror at a distance. This was his own congregation, netted. "Hearth-spirits. Well-wights. The god of a particular bridge, the saint of a particular birthing. The ones that answer prayers because they *cannot help it* — because answering is what they are. The big gods, the storm-fathers and the death-mothers, they're still too strong, still sleeping too deep. But the small ones woke first when the fire began to fail, and the small ones are—" his voice caught, "—the small ones are *grateful*. Someone prays, and they pour themselves out to answer, and then they're empty, and empty things are *easy to catch*."

Below them, a soldier carried a cage to the wagons. Inside it, something small and silver flared — once, twice, like a moth against a window — and went dim.

Sebastian made a sound. Kestrel had heard her make it before, at the drowned village, at the shrine where the farmer's prayer for rain had killed everyone he loved. It was not a gasp. It was the sound of a woman swallowing her own scream whole, and he thought: *she has been doing this her whole life. Swallowing. Keeping the valley safe by keeping herself quiet.*

"They're weaponizing them," he said. Because someone had to say it, and he was the one who knew how crowns worked. "A caged god answers the prayers of whoever holds the cage. You want rain on your enemy's harvest — drown it. You want a plague village to recover — it recovers. You want a battle won —" he watched the wagons, four of them, six, the wheels cutting the honest ruts of the ghost-road into mud, "— you put a god of victory in a lantern and carry it at the front of the line, and every man who fights under that light believes, *truly believes*, that heaven is on his side. And belief wins battles. Belief has always won battles. The crown has simply found a way to *manufacture* it."

"And the men carrying the cages," Sebastian said. "Do they know?"

"Some of them." He heard his own voice go cold and distant, the voice he used for reports. "The rest are told they're transporting relics. Sacred salvage. The crown has been very careful. There's a doctrine now — I heard it preached before I —" before I deserted, before I ran, before I became the thing they hunt. "The Doctrine of the Empty Throne. It says the old gods abandoned Vael. It says the miracles are the king's by right of faith. It says that gathering the holy fire into the king's keeping is not theft but *stewardship*."

"Stewardship." Sebastian turned her head and looked at him, and her eyes were the gray of the gate's iron, and he could not hold the look and could not look away. "You said that word like it tasted of ash."

"Because I've heard it before." He sat up in the bracken, slowly, and made himself say the rest. "I heard it the day I left. They burned the temple at Carrowmere — the one on the border, the last one still openly serving the old rites. My commander gave the order. He quoted the Doctrine. *We do not destroy the holy, we gather it home.* And then he gave the temple to the flames and stood there watching with his hands folded, like a man at a funeral he had personally arranged, and he was *at peace*, Sebastian. That's what I couldn't —" his voice cracked and he hated it, hated the crack, hated the boy he had been, "— that's what I could not stand. Not the burning. The peace. He burned a house of God and slept well that night, and they gave him a commendation."

Ivo had gone very still. "Your commander," the old priest said carefully. "He has a name."

"You know his name." Kestrel kept his eyes on the wagons. "Everyone who served under him knows his name. Ser Aldous Veyra. Knight-Commander of the Order of the Gathered Flame." He said the order's name and felt it in his teeth like a bitten coin. "They gave it that name two years ago. Before that it was just the Crown Wardens. Before that it was the men who burned Carrowmere."

Below them, the last cage went into the last wagon. The canvas was drawn. The soldiers mounted. And the column — Kestrel counted them with the part of his mind that had never stopped being a soldier, the part he could not cut out of himself any more than he could cut out his own heart — the column formed up in marching order, sixty lances at least, and turned south.

South. Toward the ash road. Toward Mount Cindral.

Toward the dragon.

"They're not harvesting," Sebastian said slowly. "Not only harvesting. Look at the direction. They're going where we're going."

"Of course they are." Kestrel felt the dread settle over him like a wet cloak, cold and familiar. "Think like the crown, Sebastian. The miracles are getting stronger. The prayers are being answered all across Vael, and every answer burns a little of the last dragon-fire, and the crown can feel the world *changing* under its feet. They don't know about the dragon. They don't know about the gate, or the oath, or you. But they know the source is in the mountains, because the miracles thicken as you go south, like weather. So they gather gods as they march — fuel for the road — and they march toward the fire." He closed his eyes. "And Ser Aldous Veyra leads them. And Ser Aldous Veyra knows me. And he knows that when a hound deserts, it runs to ground in the wildest country it can find, and there is no wilder country in the world than Vael."

"He'll be looking for you," Ivo said.

"He *is* looking for me. He has been looking for me for three years. Not because I matter." Kestrel laughed, and it came out wrong. "Because I'm an insult. A knight of the Gathered Flame who walked away from the Gathered Flame — you don't let that stand, not because it's dangerous, but because it's *contagious*. Every soldier who hears my name thinks: he chose. And crowns cannot afford men who think they can choose."

Sebastian was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed — gone low and level, the voice of the warden, the voice that had oiled hinges and recited dead words for twenty years without once asking why.

"Then we don't go south."

"We have to go south."

"Then we go south *differently*. Not the ash road. Not the road he'll be watching." She was already moving, already scanning the ridgeline with those gatekeeper's eyes, eyes that had spent a lifetime reading a single door and now had a whole country of doors to read. "The old road. The one under this one. The one the milestone is trying to cover up." She pointed, and her finger was steady. "The ghost-roads still remember being walked. Ivo, you said the villages replay. The roads must too. We walk the road that only the dead remember, and the living army rides right past us."

Ivo made a sound that was half prayer and half despair. "The old roads go through the echo-villages. You'll see them, girl. You'll see the last days of Vael played out in front of you, and you won't be able to look away, and neither will he—" a nod at Kestrel, and Kestrel understood that Ivo knew, had perhaps known for days, that the deserter knight carried this country in his chest like shrapnel.

"Good," Kestrel said. "Maybe it's time I looked."

* * *

They skirted Saint-Alder's-Reach in the blue hour before dusk, single file along a sheep-track the color of old bone. Kestrel took the rear again. It was where he belonged. It was where cowards and rearguards lived, and he had long since stopped pretending to know the difference.

They were half a mile past the village when he heard it.

Singing. Thin and small, from the pack-mule, from the wicker cage that was not a wicker cage at all but Ivo's lantern, wrapped in sacking, lashed down like contraband — which was, Kestrel supposed, exactly what it was.

He reined in. The others did not notice; the track was steep and the mule was stubborn. He let them draw ahead. Then he leaned down and pulled the sacking back.

The hearth-god looked up at him.

It was smaller than he remembered — smaller than the night he'd first seen it breathing gold in Ivo's lantern. It had spent itself getting them past the flood-shrine, past the collapsed ford, and it had not refilled, because there was nothing in this starved country to refill it with. It was a coal now, a bead of ember-light no bigger than a thumbnail, and it trembled when it saw him, and it sang.

The song had no words. It didn't need words. Kestrel had stood in enough chapels, heard enough choirs, to know what a small voice in a large dark is asking. It was asking not to be put out. It was asking to be carried toward the fire, because the fire was its mother and its home and its god, and it had hidden itself in a stranger's luggage and crossed a dead country to get there, and it was so *tired*.

"I know," Kestrel said quietly. His voice came out rough. "I know. I haven't told her. I'm a coward about the wrong things — it's a specialty of mine."

The little light flared, gently, reproachfully. Even a hearth-spirit the size of a spark, he thought, could make you feel judged.

"She'll forgive you," he told it. "That's the worst thing about her. She forgives everyone. It's the rest of us who can't forgive ourselves." He tucked the sacking back over the cage, careful, the way you cover a sleeping child. "When the time comes, I'll tell her myself. Before he finds us. Before any of it. I swear it by —"

He stopped. He had almost sworn by the crown. Old habits. Old oaths, still living in the body like shrapnel, like the true names of villages.

"By the road," he finished. "By every road I ever marched and every one I burned. I'll tell her myself."

The little god dimmed to a contented ember. Somewhere south, beyond the ridgeline, beyond the dark coming up out of the east, sixty lances were riding toward the last fire in the world, and at the head of them, Kestrel knew, rode a man at perfect peace, carrying cages full of stolen heaven, and singing the Doctrine to himself like a lullaby.

Kestrel turned his horse and followed the others up the track, into the country that remembered him, toward the fire that everyone was hunting, and the last light of the day went out behind him like a small god being put away in a cage.
