## 13. What Was Done to Vael

The archive had been breathing long before they found it.

Ivo felt it first as a warmth in the soles of his feet, rising through boot leather and the cold stone of the stair that spiraled down beneath the ruined cathedral — a warmth with a pulse to it, slow and patient, like a sleeper's heart. He stopped on the fourth landing and put his palm against the wall, and the wall was faintly, impossibly alive: not flesh-warm but book-warm, the temperature of a page held too long in a reading hand.

"Ivo?" Sebastian's voice came down the stair after him, hushed the way people go hushed in tombs and temples and other places where the living are outnumbered by the dead. "What is this place?"

"The Library of Vael." His own voice sounded strange to him, scraped thin. "I read about it when I was a novitiate. They said it burned."

"It didn't burn," Kestrel said from above, in the flat tone of a man stating a fact he wished were otherwise. "It's down here. Breathing."

They came down together into the dark, Sebastian's lantern lifting its small stolen light before them — the hearth-god inside it dimming modestly whenever anyone looked directly at it, the way cats pretend they weren't on the table. The stair let out into a hall that went back farther than the lantern could reach, and the hall was lined with books.

Not shelves of books. Growing books.

They stood rooted in beds of black loam, each one sprung from the floor like a sapling, spine upward, pages fanned open to the air like leaves catching light that wasn't there. Some were tall as men. Some were no bigger than hymnals. And all of them, all of them, were turning their pages slowly, softly, in the stillness — a sound like rain beginning, like a thousand small hands turning over in sleep.

"They're alive," Sebastian whispered.

"Living books." Ivo walked forward as though walking into water. "The priests of Vael didn't write their history. They grew it. You planted a memory the way you'd plant a cutting — took a living witness, a person who had seen a thing with their own eyes, and grafted them onto paperstock, and what grew kept remembering. Kept growing new pages as long as the memory stayed green." He reached the nearest one and stood before it without touching it, the way you stand before an altar. "When Vael was erased, everyone assumed the library died with it."

"And instead it starved," Sebastian said quietly. "Like everything else here."

"Some of them did." He could see them now, further back in the dark: gray husks, leafless spines, books gone to ash-colored wood. Dead witnesses. But the one before him was green along its edges, faintly green, the way moss is green in winter, and its topmost pages trembled at their approach like something waking.

Kestrel hung back by the stair. "You're sure we should be doing this? Reading other countries' dead feels like grave-robbing with extra steps."

"They're not dead," Ivo said. "That's rather the whole horror of it."

He touched the cover.

The book opened under his hand the way a face turns toward a familiar voice, and the smell of it rose up — not dust, never dust, but ink and rain and woodsmoke and underneath it all something older, the mineral smell of deep stone, of the world before doors. The pages turned themselves past his fingers until they found the place they wanted, the way a dog finds its own bed, and Ivo Marren, defrocked priest of three gods who had never once answered him honestly, began to read the true history of Vael.

* * *

He had expected war.

Everyone expected war. It was the story told in the seminaries beyond the mountains, the story even the Doctrine told in its careful, footnoted way: Vael had grown proud, Vael had grown rich in miracles, Vael had made war on its neighbors and been destroyed for it, or had destroyed itself, or had simply — this was the version the king's scholars preferred — *ended*, gone out like a candle, leaving only empty roads and villages of echoes for travelers to be haunted by. Catastrophe was comfortable. Catastrophe meant no one was to blame.

The book did not tell that story.

The book told him about the year the rains came wrong, and then the year the prayers came loud — the gods of Vael grown fat on two thousand years of devotion, answering everything, healing every wound, raising every harvest, until the people of Vael had forgotten how to do anything but ask. There were pages here that made his stomach turn, written in the hand of some archivist whose name had worn away: grain fields that needed no sowing because the earth-god loved them, and so the art of sowing died in one generation; children born without the will to walk because a hearth-spirit carried every tired toddler; a plague-year in which no one died, and the following year in which no one could bear to live, because the gods had saved them all and saved them all and saved them all, indiscriminately, endlessly, without judgment, without limit, without ever once asking whether being saved was what a soul required.

"We thought we were feeding them," the book said, in the dry rustling voice of a page. "We were drowning them. Or they were drowning us. By the end there was no difference."

And then the book turned to the part no one had ever written down anywhere outside these walls, and Ivo felt the floor tilt under him though his feet never moved.

Because it wasn't the gods' fault. That was the first thing. The gods of Vael had not grown monstrous; they had grown *hungry*, which is a different disease, and one with a known cure. When the dragon-fire that fed them began to fail — when Mount Cindral's great heart began, century by century, to cool — the gods did what any starving creature does. They took more. They answered more prayers, drank more devotion, reached wider and grasped harder, and the people of Vael, loving them, gave and gave and gave until there was almost nothing left of themselves to give.

And the priests of Vael looked at their dying country, and their starving gods, and their own hollowed faces reflected in the shrine-water, and they did not try to save the world.

They tried to amputate it.

"There is a door," the book said. Ivo read it twice, three times, his lips moving. "There has always been a door between what is Vael and what is Not-Vael, and the dragon-fire keeps it shut, and the gods keep it fed, and if the fire dies the door opens and the starving gods pour out into a young world that cannot survive them. So we will make the door permanent. We will seal it with something better than fire. We will seal it with an oath, and the oath will burn slower than any fire, and it will feed the boundary for as long as it is fed, and behind it our country can rest, and our gods can starve gently, honestly, at the pace of centuries instead of days, and the world outside will never know what we spared it."

Ivo's mouth had gone dry. "Fed how?" he asked the book, uselessly, aloud. "An oath is just words. What feeds an oath?"

The pages turned. The archive breathed around him, hundreds of living memories listening, and somewhere far off in the stacks a dead book shed a leaf of itself with a sound like a bone breaking.

"The oath must have a keeper," the book said. "And the keeper must be bound into the keeping. Words alone are wind. An oath needs a body to live in, a life to burn, a bloodline to carry it down the years so the lock is never left unattended. We searched long for a family willing to give what was asked. In the end we did not need to search."

"No," Ivo said. He said it very quietly, to himself, to the book, to whatever was listening in the dark. "No, you didn't."

"The first Oyelaran volunteered."

The name landed in the silent hall like a dropped chalice.

He knew it — of course he knew it; everyone who had served in Vael's old orders knew the name Oyelaran, though the records beyond the mountains called it a gate-warden's family name, humble, hereditary, a job like miller or smith. But the book said otherwise. The book said the first Oyelaran had been a priest herself, the finest of them, the one whose voice could hold a congregation of ten thousand in the palm of its hand; the book said she had argued longest against the sealing and lost the argument; and the book said that when the others flinched from the cost, she had not.

"She sang the first oath herself," the pages whispered, turning. "She sang the door shut around her own body. She became the hinge. Her heartbeat became the measure of the burning. Every year on the eve of the founding, her heirs would come to the gate and renew her words, and the oath would take from them what it needed, and give the valley another year, and another, and another."

"How much," Ivo said. His hands had closed into fists without his permission. "How much did it take?"

The book considered him. Books should not be able to consider anyone; this one managed it anyway, its open pages angled toward him like a face deciding how much truth a man can stand.

"A little," it said at last. "A little, every year. A season of strength. A year of life. The color from the cheeks, the warmth from the fingertips. Slowly. Kindly, if a thing like this can be kind. The founders were careful to make it gentle — they were cowards about many things, but they were cowards even about their cruelty. No Oyelaran was meant to die of it. No Oyelaran was meant even to feel it, much. Only to spend, and spend, and spend, generation upon generation, a family turned into a wick, so that the flame of the door would outlast the memory of why it was lit."

Ivo stood very still in the breathing dark.

Behind him, he heard Sebastian's breath catch — she was reading the neighboring book now, or it was reading itself to her, its pages turning in the lamplight, and he did not turn around, because he already knew which book had chosen her, and he already knew what it was saying.

Cowards, the book had said. And the fury came up in him then, slow at first, like groundwater rising in a cellar, filling the low places first — fury at the archivists who had grafted living people into paper and left them here to remember forever; fury at the founders who had sealed their own gods away to starve rather than teach their people to pray less; fury at the Doctrine across the mountains that had wrapped the whole atrocity in the word *war* because catastrophe was easier to bury than cowardice. Two hundred years. Two hundred years of a family standing at a door, oiling hinges that never opened, reciting words they didn't understand, spending themselves coin by coin into a lock built by frightened men who had gone home afterward to warm beds and full granaries and gods they had decided, in their infinite wisdom, to murder slowly.

And the valley. Gods, the valley. All those years he had lived there in exile, watching the seasons refuse to turn properly, watching prayers fall flat as stones, watching children grow up believing that heaven had simply forgotten their address — and it had never been forgetting. It had been rationing. Every unanswered prayer in the Hollow Valley was a coin withheld from a starving god by a lock made of a woman's life.

"They didn't destroy Vael," he said aloud, and his voice cracked on it, and he let it crack. "They entombed it. Alive. With the lights left burning so no one would notice."

"Ivo." Sebastian's voice. Very quiet.

He turned.

She was standing before a book that had grown tall as a doorway, its pages spread wide, and the lamplight lay on it, and on the page nearest her hand was a drawing — ink faded to the color of old tea, but unmistakable — of a woman standing before a great door, arms raised, singing. And below the figure, in the archivist's careful hand, ran the notation Ivo had read a hundred times in fragments and never once seen whole:

*Oath-bearer: the Oyelaran line. Function: the Lock. Lifespan of the binding: as long as the bloodline sings. Cost per renewal:* — and here the ink had blurred, or been blurred, deliberately, by a thumb two hundred years dead — *see verso.*

Sebastian turned the page.

Ivo watched her read it. Watched her lips move through the archivist's careful accounting, the ledgers of a crime committed in installments: years spent, strength spent, warmth spent, generation listed after generation after generation, right down the column, right down through two hundred years of names, and he saw her finger stop moving before her eyes did, and he knew which name it had stopped at, because he had done the same arithmetic on the walk down the stairs.

"You've done twelve renewals," he heard himself say. "You told me. Twelve festivals, since you were sixteen."

She didn't answer. The lantern-light flickered, and the hearth-god inside it made a small distressed sound like a kettle taken off the heat, and Kestrel had come down the stairs without any of them noticing and stood now at the edge of the lamplight with his hand on his sword-hilt for no enemy either of them could name.

"The dragon-fire isn't failing because it's old," Sebastian said. Her voice was level, terribly level, the voice of someone holding a door shut against a flood. "It's failing because I am. It was never failing at all. It was always me. Every year I sang the oath, I wasn't guarding the fire, I was *substituting* for it, and now—"

"Now the wick's burned short," Ivo finished, because someone had to say it and it might as well be the man who had begged her to open the gate. "And your elders blamed you for the laxness. And the crown hunts caged gods while the real mechanism stands in a valley reciting words she doesn't understand." Something hot was running down his face and he realized distantly that he was crying, furious tears, priest's tears, the useless kind. "Two hundred years of cowardice, and they dressed it up as duty and handed it to a child."

The archive stirred around them. All down the hall, in the dark, the living books were turning their pages faster, hundreds of witnesses remembering at once, and the sound they made was like rain, like applause, like a crowd rising to its feet.

And then, above them, muffled by forty feet of stone, came a sound that did not belong to any century-old grief.

Hoofbeats. Many horses. Coming fast along the ridge road, south by southeast, toward Mount Cindral — and threaded through the drumming of them, faint but unmistakable, drifting down the stairwell like smoke, a human voice singing the Doctrine in a clear and contented baritone.

Kestrel went white. "That's Halvard's marching song," he said. "They're not behind us anymore. They're ahead of us."
