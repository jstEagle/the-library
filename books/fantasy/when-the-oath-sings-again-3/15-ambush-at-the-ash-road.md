## 15. Ambush at the Ash Road

The ash road announced itself before they saw it.

Kestrel felt it through his boots first — a fine tremor, like the ground remembering a heartbeat it had buried long ago. Then the smell: hot stone and old grief, the scent of a country that had burned once and never stopped smoldering somewhere beneath its own skin. The road ran straight as a spear-throw toward Mount Cindral's distant cone, and it was paved in ash that had been pressed into glass by some ancient heat, so that their footsteps rang faintly, musically, with every stride.

"Someone walked this road recently," Ivo said, crouching to touch the glassy surface. His fingers came away black. "Days. Maybe less."

"People are heading for the mountain?" Sebastian asked.

"People are always heading for the mountain now," Kestrel said. "They just don't know why yet." He had seen it on the road behind them, in the shrine-towns: a drift, a migration of the desperate. Farmers whose prayers had been answered wrong. Mothers carrying children who spoke in voices not their own after sleeping near standing water. The whole ghost-country tilting, slow as a continent, toward the last fire.

Sebastian said nothing. She hadn't said much since the reliquary scouts' campfire smoke they'd spotted two days back — since she'd learned what the crown did to answered prayers, what her commander built cages for. But Kestrel watched her hands when she wasn't paying attention. They moved constantly now, small unconscious gestures, tracing patterns in the air. Writing something. Or being written.

*She's hearing the oath more,* he thought. *Every mile closer to Cindral, louder.*

The hearth-god rode in her lantern.

Kestrel had noticed it first because he'd been raised to notice such things — a knight's training included the old liturgies, even if he'd spat on most of them. The lantern's flame burned the wrong color. Too gold, too warm, with a flicker-rhythm like breathing. And sometimes, when the wind died completely, he could swear he heard a sound from inside the glass: a tiny crackle, arrhythmic, almost like humming. A lullaby sung by someone who'd forgotten all the words except one.

He'd said nothing. Sebastian carried it everywhere, refilled its oil every night without comment, shielded it from rain with her cloak. Whatever bargain she'd made with the small stowaway god, it was hers to keep. But he found himself walking beside the lantern more than he needed to, the way you stand near a candle in a cold room without deciding to.

* * *

The ambush came at dusk, on a stretch of road where the ash-glass gave way to cinder-gravel, where the land dipped into a shallow bowl between two ridges of slag.

Kestrel smelled them before he saw them: steel oil, leather, horse sweat. The specific sourness of men who'd ridden hard for days and hated it. He'd grown up around that smell; his whole boyhood had been the interior of a barracks. He knew it the way you know a childhood illness returning.

"Down," he said quietly. "Riders. Six — no. Eight. In the bowl ahead, holding position." He pulled Sebastian down behind a ridge of slag as he spoke, his hand finding her shoulder, feeling her pulse jump once under his palm and steady itself. That steadiness frightened him more than panic would have. She was becoming someone who expected violence.

"They've been following us since the reliquary," Ivo whispered, easing down beside them. His priest's face had gone gray in the failing light. "Since the fortress. Word travels fast when a defrocked priest, a deserter, and the Oyelaran woman rob a king."

"We didn't rob a king," Sebastian said. "We robbed a cage."

"The crown won't appreciate the distinction."

Kestrel eased forward along the slag ridge until he could see into the bowl. They were arranged well — he gave them that much. Eight riders, dismounted, horses picketed in the lee of the far slope. Two sentries walking a lazy perimeter. The rest gathered around a brazier whose light they'd screened with oiled cloth, so the glow wouldn't carry. Professional. Patient.

And at the center, talking to a man who held a map weighted with stones, stood a figure Kestrel would have known anywhere in the world, in any light, dead or alive.

*Commander Aldric Voss.*

Twenty years older than the man who had trained him, but the same stillness. Voss had never fidgeted in his life. He stood over maps the way other men knelt at altars, absolutely certain that what he looked upon was his to order. Even at this distance, even in the bad light, Kestrel felt the old reflexes wake in his body: stand straighter, wait for orders, be worthy.

He killed the reflexes the way he'd taught himself to, one by one. *You left. You chose the temple over the torch. Everything after that is weather.*

"He'll have outriders behind us too," he breathed. "Voss never leads with everything. There'll be four more on the road we came by, closing slow, herding us toward the main group." He turned back to Sebastian. Her face was half-lit by the lantern's strange gold, and there was ash in her hair, and she was looking at him with an expression he couldn't name — waiting, trusting, terrible.

"How many can you fight?" she asked.

"At full strength, against eight, with surprise? Maybe." He touched the sword at his hip — the one he'd taken from the reliquary, good Vaelish steel, older than the kingdom that claimed it. "But we don't fight eight. We run the gap on the north side while they're watching the south approach. It's narrow but it goes through. We make the caldera road by morning if nothing goes wrong."

"And if something goes wrong?"

Kestrel looked at her. At Ivo, already fumbling his prayer-beads loose — not to pray, Kestrel realized, but to count, the way he did when calculating. At the lantern sitting between them, its little flame leaning toward the ambush like a plant toward sun.

"Then one of us makes sure the other two get through," he said. "That's the arithmetic. It always was."

"No," Sebastian said.

"Sebastian—"

"*No.*" Not loud. Just absolute, the voice of a woman who had spent twenty years keeping a door shut through sheer stubborn love of people who didn't know she existed. "Nobody is arithmetic tonight. We go through the gap together or we find another way entirely. Swear it or I'll walk into that bowl right now and negotiate with your commander myself."

Ivo made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "She means it," he told Kestrel. "Gatekeepers are worse than priests about vows. It's the oath in the blood. They can't help it."

Something cracked open in Kestrel's chest then — something that had been sealed since the night he'd stood in front of a burning temple and done nothing, since the night he'd walked away from everything and called it cowardice. Because she wasn't asking him to save her. She was refusing, flatly and forever, to let him spend himself as currency. Nobody had ever refused him that before. He'd offered himself up his whole life — to the order, to the crown, to the shame afterward — and everyone had taken the offer.

"All right," he said, and heard how rough his voice came out. "Together. I swear it."

The lantern-flame flared.

Not flickered — *flared*, standing up straight inside the glass like a soldier called to attention, and for the first time Kestrel heard the humming clearly, no longer a lullaby fragment but a wordless rising tone, urgent, insistent. Leaning toward the gap on the north side.

"It knows something," Ivo whispered.

"It knows everything," Sebastian said softly. "It's been listening to us for weeks. It's a hearth-god, Ivo. Hearth-gods know houses. And roads are just houses that move." She lifted the lantern, and the flame leaned harder, pointing like a compass needle at the northern gap. "It's telling us the gap is watched."

* * *

They went anyway, because there was nowhere else to go, and because the alternative was the bowl and Voss.

The gap was exactly as wrong as the god had promised. Kestrel felt it fifty yards out — that prickle at the neck, the animal certainty of eyes. He raised a fist, halted them in the shadow of a slag heap, and listened past the wind.

There. The soft double-click of a crossbow being readied. More than one. Men breathing deliberately slowly, the way soldiers do when they're holding position and trying to be quiet about it.

"Crossbows in the narrows," he breathed. "Four, maybe five. They let the gap look open." Of course they had. Voss knew him. Voss had taught him terrain; Voss had drilled into every cadet the truth that a clean escape route is the oldest lie in war. *If the door is open, it is bait.*

They backed out of the narrows as silently as they'd come, and regrouped in the shadow of the slag heaps, and for a long moment none of them spoke. Behind them, the bowl glowed with its screened fire. Ahead, the narrows waited with their quiet crossbows. Somewhere on the southern road, four more riders closed like a hand closing.

"We're pinched," Ivo said. He said it calmly, the way he reported all disasters, and began counting beads again. "Pinched and out of clever options. So. Do we surrender and talk, or do we die loudly?"

"Neither," Sebastian said, and set the lantern down on a flat stone between them.

The flame stood up very straight.

"You've been hiding in my lantern since the cathedral," she said to it, conversationally, the way she must have spoken to the gate for twenty years — to a thing that could not answer, or would not, or answered only in ways no one else heard. "You followed us when I told you not to. You've eaten my oil and my bread-crumbs and half my patience. And you know these men are here for us, and you know what the crown does to gods it catches."

The flame bowed. Actually bowed — bent low, twice, like a servant.

"So I'm asking you plainly. Will you help us? Knowing what it costs? Knowing that whatever you burn of yourself tonight, you will not get back?" Her voice caught, just slightly. "Knowing that I would rather lose this fight than lose you?"

The lantern was silent for three heartbeats. Four.

Then the glass began to sing.

* * *

What came out of the lantern was not large. Kestrel wanted to make that clear to himself, even in the moment, because awe lies about size. What came out of the lantern was the size of a child, made of the light you see through a doorway on a winter evening — the warm gold spilling across a floor that says *someone is home, someone is cooking, you are expected*. It had a shape like a grandmother, broad and stooped, with an apron of woven sparks, and its face was every face you've ever missed: the cook who fed you, the mother at the window, the neighbor who sat with you through the worst night of your life.

A hearth-god. One of ten thousand that had once kept every kitchen fire in Vael company, and one of perhaps a handful left, and it had hidden in a lantern and eaten bread-crumbs and ridden toward its own probable death because a woman had been kind to it in a ruined cathedral.

It looked at the three of them, and its face crinkled into something infinitely fond and infinitely sad, and it raised one spark-woven hand toward the narrows.

The night changed.

Kestrel felt it as heat first — a wave of it rolling outward, gentle as an oven door opening, smelling absurdly of fresh bread and woodsmoke and cedar chests and every safe place he'd ever slept. The crossbowmen in the narrows cried out. Through the gap he glimpsed them stumbling backward, swatting at themselves — because every man jack of them had, in the same instant, remembered home. Not remembered abstractly. *Relived.* Kestrel could see it happening to them: one dropped his weapon and sat down in the ash and wept openly; another started calling a name — "Mara, Mara, the stew's burning—" — clawing at his own collar as though trying to reach a kitchen thirty years and two hundred miles gone.

Homesickness as a weapon. Grief for the hearth, poured out without judgment or limit, the way the gods of Vael answered everything now — starving, indiscriminate, magnificent.

"That won't hold them long," Ivo said, awed and horrified at once. "It's not hurting them. It's just... reminding them. They'll shake it off and—"

The hearth-god staggered.

Kestrel saw it clearly: the apron of sparks guttering, the outline going thin, translucent, like a coal collapsing into its own gray. It was spending itself. All of it. Every scrap of essence it had hoarded in a lantern for weeks, poured out in one great act of hospitality — because that was the whole of a hearth-god's nature, the entire theology of the thing, the only prayer it had ever known how to answer: *come in, sit down, be warm, be safe.*

And it turned its fading face toward the bowl where Voss waited, and raised both hands, and pushed.

The screen over the enemy brazier burst into true flame — roaring, joyful, enormous — and the light that spilled across the bowl showed everything: eight soldiers, horses screaming, Voss's face upturned, momentarily blinded by his own concealed fire. And under that cover, the hearth-god seized the three of them — Kestrel felt its grip as warmth in his bones, as the unmistakable sensation of being *sheltered* — and hauled them sideways, off the road, into a fold of the slag hills that no map would show because the god itself remembered it from before the sealing, from when shepherds used it.

They ran. Stumbling, sliding on ash-glass, the sounds of chaos fading behind them — and then a new sound that stopped Kestrel's heart: the ring of a crossbow loosed wild, and then another, aimed not at the fleeing shadows but upward, at the small bright figure standing on the ridge above them, visible now to every soldier in the bowl, blazing like a bonfire in the shape of a beloved dead woman.

"Don't," Sebastian screamed, wrenching free of the god's warmth, spinning back. "Don't look at them, *run*—"

A bolt took the hearth-god through the chest.

It didn't bleed. It *thinned* — the bolt passing through light and taking a fistful of it away, and the small god swayed on the ridge like a candle in a draft, and still, even then, even pierced, it did the only thing it knew. It spread its arms wide over the three of them, over the whole ash road, and poured out one final blessing: warmth, safety, *be well, be fed, go home*, spent down to almost nothing, down to a spark, down to—

A second bolt, loosed by a steadier hand.

Kestrel didn't think. Later he would try to reconstruct the thinking and find there was none — just his body moving, the old training and the newer love arriving at the same instant from opposite directions. He threw himself between the bolt and Sebastian, who had started up the slope toward the dying god, and the Vaelish steel point went into his side below the ribs, punching through muscle, angling cruelly, and the world went white and then red and then very simple.

He hit the ash on his back. Above him, the sky. Above the sky, the small god, guttering, kneeling — actually kneeling beside him, pressing both spark-hands to his wound, and he felt its last essence flow into him like broth, like a poultice, like every remedy a grandmother ever applied without asking permission. *Foolish boy,* said a voice directly inside his skull, tasting of woodsmoke and cinnamon. *Taking bolts for girls. Your mother taught you better.*

"I never knew my mother," Kestrel tried to say, and got maybe half of it out loud.

*I know,* said the hearth-god. *I was at your barracks fire the night you were born, boy. Somebody has to keep the kettle on.*

And it folded itself down smaller, and smaller, and smaller, until it was a single golden spark no bigger than a grain of wheat — and it drifted down, past his blurring vision, and settled into the wound in his side, and the pain went quiet, and the spark went dark.

Gone. Spent. Burned out past any rekindling, down to the last ember of itself given to a stranger it had known for six weeks.

* * *

They got him upright between them — Sebastian on his wounded side, white-faced, her hand pressed hard to the hole in him, Ivo taking his arm across her shoulders. They moved through the fold in the hills while the bowl behind them still seethed with confused firelight, while Voss's voice rang out ordering a sweep, sharp and furious and too late.

They made perhaps two miles before Kestrel's legs quit, and they laid him against a slag ridge, and Sebastian knelt in the ash and put her forehead against his, and for a moment neither of them managed any words at all.

"You swore," she finally said, and her voice broke clean in half. "Together. You swore."

"I kept it." Blood on his teeth, copper-bright. "All three of us went through the hills. Together. Check the arithmetic."

"This isn't funny."

"It's a little funny." He coughed, and it hurt magnificently, and he laughed anyway, because the alternative in that moment was telling her the thing pressing up his throat like a tide, and he wasn't ready, or he was, or it didn't matter, because the spark in his side pulsed warm as he looked at her ash-streaked face and the words came out on their own, quiet, wrecked, certain:

"I deserted a crown for strangers. I let a temple burn because I was afraid. I have been running from that night for nine years, and then some small god hid in your lantern and I let myself hope, and now it's spent itself buying us two miles of darkness, and I am lying in the ash of a dead country bleeding for a woman who refuses to let anyone be arithmetic." He lifted his hand — shaking, ash-gray — and laid it against her cheek. "I'm not running anymore, Sebastian. From Voss, from the order, from any of it. Wherever you're going, that's where I stand. Even if where you're going is a mountain that eats oath-keepers."

Sebastian covered his hand with hers and held it there, and her tears cut clean tracks through the ash on her face, and she said, "Even if I have to stay behind the door?"

"Especially then," Kestrel said. "Doors need keeping. I'm told I'm good at standing places."

Behind them, Ivo had gone still. When they turned, the priest was standing with his beads hanging forgotten from his fist, staring east toward Cindral — where, impossibly, faintly, the horizon had begun to glow. Not dawn-glow. Ember-glow. A deep red pulsing, rhythmic, like breath, like a bellows drawing, like something vast turning over in its sleep and noticing.

"It felt the god die," Ivo whispered. "Or — no." His face went gray. "It felt the god *arrive*. Whatever was left of her. It went into the wound, into Kestrel — the dragon-fire is answering it. Answering *him*." He looked at Kestrel with something like reverence and something like terror. "The mountain knows we're coming now. And I think, Kestrel Vane, that it thinks you're carrying mail."

The ember-light pulsed again, stronger, washing all their faces red.

Kestrel looked down at his wound. Beneath Sebastian's bloodied fingers, deep in the torn flesh, something glowed — faint, gold, patient, beating in time with a fire two days' ride away.
