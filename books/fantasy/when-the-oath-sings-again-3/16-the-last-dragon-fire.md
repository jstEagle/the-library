## 16. The Last Dragon-Fire

The mountain had been visible for two days before they reached it, a smudge of grey on the horizon that might have been cloud, might have been nothing at all. By the third morning it resolved into something that could not be mistaken for anything but what it was: Mount Cindral, rising out of the ash plain like a black tooth, its slopes bare of even the stubborn lichen that clung everywhere else in Vael.

Sebastian felt it before she saw it properly. That was the strangest part — not the sight of it, but the pull. Since crossing the threshold she had been learning her own body again, cataloguing the ways magic moved through her like blood through veins she'd never known she had. And now, with every step toward the mountain, something in her chest leaned forward. Not hunger exactly. Recognition.

*Home,* said some voice too deep to be hers. *Almost home.*

She didn't tell the others. She wasn't sure yet whether the voice was memory or warning.

The ash plain itself was a lesson in patience. It gave nothing away. No wind broke its surface; their footprints filled in behind them within moments, as though the ground resented being marked. Kestrel rode point with his sword drawn and his hood back despite the cold, watching the sky for riders, while Ivo walked beside Sebastian's mule reciting scripture under his breath — not prayers, she had learned, but lists. The names of every small god they had passed and refused, spoken aloud each morning so they would know they hadn't been forgotten.

"You're doing it wrong," Sebastian said on the second day.

Ivo glanced up at her, startled. "Doing what?"

"Praying. You pray like you're filing paperwork." She nodded at the mountain ahead. "Whatever you're saying to them — say it like you mean it."

"I do mean it."

"No," Sebastian said, "you mean to *mean* it. There's a difference. I spent twenty years oiling hinges that never opened, so I know the difference better than anyone alive."

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. Then he began the list again, slower, and this time he did not recite so much as speak, and the ash plain seemed to lean in and listen.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a ridge of fused glass — sand that some ancient heat had melted and never let cool properly. It hummed faintly when Kestrel touched it, and he snatched his hand back as if bitten.

"Everything in this country remembers being burned," he said.

"Then we should feel at home," Sebastian said. "So does my gate."

But she didn't sleep well. In the dark she could hear it now, unmistakable and growing stronger with every mile: a sound beneath sound, a slow rhythmic breathing that came not from the air but from the ground itself, from the bones of the world. In-out. In-out. So vast and so slow that an hour passed between one breath and the next, and if you weren't listening for it you would mistake it for silence.

She had been hearing it all her life, she realized. Through the gate. Through her oath. Every night of her childhood, lying awake in the warden's house above the Hollow Gate, listening to what she'd thought was wind in the pass — that was this. That was him.

*Him.* She didn't know why she was certain it was a he. She just was.

Near dawn, Ivo found her sitting upright with her lantern lit, though there was no need of light for anything but her own face.

"You hear it too," he said. Not a question.

"Everyone in the valley hears it," Sebastian said. "We just call it quiet."

* * *

On the fourth day the ash began to fall upward.

Sebastian noticed it first as a tickling against her cheek, warm flecks drifting past her face toward the sky instead of down from it. Then she looked properly and saw the whole plain exhaling — fine grey flakes rising off the ground in slow spirals, drawn up the mountainside like breath drawn through a throat. The dragon's fire was failing, and the failing drew everything toward it. Even warmth. Even memory, maybe. She thought of the echo-villages they had passed, replaying their final days over and over, growing fainter each time, and wondered how long before the whole country rose off the ground like this and simply went.

Kestrel reined in and pointed. "There."

Where the mountain met the plain, a road emerged — or rather, the ash withdrew from it, pulled back by whatever force governed the mountain's breathing, revealing a causeway of black stone that wound up the slope in long switchbacks. Along its edges stood pillars, hundreds of them, each carved with the same spiral script Sebastian had seen once before: around the rim of the Hollow Gate, in the oath-language no living tongue remembered.

Except one.

She dismounted and left the mule with a wordless gesture. Her legs carried her onto the causeway before her mind caught up with them, and this time neither Ivo nor Kestrel tried to stop her. They fell in behind, close enough to reach her if she stumbled, far enough to give her room to be what she apparently was.

The pillars sang as she passed.

Not aloud — nothing so kind as aloud. Each one vibrated in her teeth, in the marrow of her arms, in the scar tissue over her heart where the oath lived, and each spoke a fragment of the same sentence, distributed across the mountainside like a psalm split among a congregation:

*When-the-fire-fails-*
*-the-lock-must-choose-*
*-between-the-wall-and-the-door—*

Sebastian stopped walking. The words kept going without her, rising from pillars further up the slope, and she stood in the middle of them shaking.

"That's the oath," she said. "That's my oath. But it's not finished."

"The oath was never finished," Ivo said quietly behind her. "It's been waiting two hundred years for someone who could hear the rest of it."

* * *

The caldera's rim was a knife-edge of obsidian, and beyond it the world dropped away into a bowl three miles wide. Sebastian crested the rim at sunset and forgot, entirely, how to breathe.

She had expected fire. What she found was a cathedral of embers.

The caldera floor was carpeted in coals — not scattered, not wild, but laid in patterns, spirals within spirals, the same script as the pillars rendered in burning. At the center, coiled around a spire of fused stone, lay the dragon.

He was smaller than the stories. Or perhaps the stories had never seen him like this: wings folded to sticks wrapped in parchment skin, neck stretched along the ground because holding it up had become too expensive, eyes gone pale as milk except where two dying points of gold still guttered deep in each iris. His scales were the grey of the ash plain, and in places they were simply missing, and the exposed flesh beneath glowed dull red like a banked stove. With every breath — that vast, hour-long tide Sebastian had heard all her life — the coal-spirals on the floor brightened and dimmed, brightened and dimmed, and the whole bowl of the world pulsed with him.

And above him, filling the caldera's throat like smoke poured in reverse, hung the boundary.

Sebastian couldn't see it, precisely. Seeing it was like seeing the edge of your own vision. But she could see what it did: the light of the sunset came down into the bowl and stopped, bent, folded inward, and where it folded there was a shimmer, and the shimmer was the seam between Vael and everything else. Between existence and erasure. The dragon lay beneath it like a man holding up a ceiling with his spine.

"There he is," Ivo whispered. He had fallen to his knees on the rim without seeming to notice. "There is the last one. Oh, gods — oh, all of you — look what we let him become."

Kestrel said nothing. His hand had found Sebastian's shoulder, and she let it stay.

She descended alone.

The switchback path took an hour. The dragon did not move, but as she came down the coals beneath her feet shifted, dimming politely wherever she stepped, clearing a road for her the way snow never cleared for anyone. By the time she reached the caldera floor the heat had soaked through her boots and her shirt and was pressing against her skin from inside and outside at once, and the boundary-shimmer hung so close overhead that she could have touched it by raising the lantern.

She did raise the lantern. The stowaway hearth-god was long gone — burned out on the ash road saving Kestrel's life — but the lantern still held a ghost of its warmth, and when the light touched the shimmer, the shimmer rippled like pond water.

"He liked you," Sebastian said to the empty lantern. "I'm sorry we only had the one of you to spend."

Then the dragon opened his eyes.

Both gold points fixed on her, blind and seeing at once, and the pressure of his attention was physical — a hand laid flat against her sternum, reading her heartbeat like a page.

*Oyelaran,* he said.

He did not speak aloud. The word arrived the way the oath-words arrived, bypassing ears entirely, resonating in the scar of her binding until her ribs rang with it. His voice was enormous and exhausted and, beneath the exhaustion, unfathomably gentle — the gentleness of something that has held a weight for two hundred years and learned tenderness toward everything smaller than its burden.

"You know me," Sebastian managed.

*I have known your name since before your valley had a name. I knew the first Oyelaran. I helped her write the lock. I helped her hide the key inside it.* A plume of smoke escaped his nostrils, grey and thin. *You are the last page of a book I have been reading for two centuries.*

"Then you know why we've come."

*I know what you've been told. Tell me what you believe.*

Sebastian looked up at the shimmering seam, at the ceiling of the world he was holding up with his dying body. She looked at the coal-script spiraling away from her feet in every direction, the unfinished sentence written across the floor of creation. And she understood suddenly, with terrible clarity, what the pillars had begun to say on the causeway — what the oath had been trying to finish for two hundred years.

"When the fire fails," she said slowly, "the lock must choose. Between the wall and the door." Her mouth had gone dry. "That's me. That's what I am. Not the gatekeeper. The gate."

*Yes.*

"And if I choose the wall — if I renew the oath, feed you the stolen gods' fire, keep the seal whole —"

*Vael remains sealed forever. Your valley sleeps on inside it, safe and dreaming, until the last ember dies and takes both worlds down together. Slowly. Gently. Like falling asleep in snow.* His eyelids drooped, gold nearly swallowed by milk-white. *I would not call it a lie. I would call it a longer way of dying.*

"And the door?"

The dragon was silent for so long that Sebastian counted four full breaths of the coals, four tides of light swelling and fading, before he answered.

*The door is not mine to describe,* he said at last. *Every lock dreams of becoming a key. But the dream has no words. When the time comes, the oath will sing it through you, and you will understand it all at once or not at all.* The great head lifted — an effort that cost him visibly, scales grinding, flesh glowing brighter with strain — until his ruined face hung level with hers, close enough that his breath stirred her hair and smelled of woodsmoke and rain and, impossibly, of the warden's house above the Hollow Gate, of bread and lamp-oil and every ordinary evening of her life. *But hear me, daughter of the lock. There are things on either side of that choice that no one has told you. There is a king gathering caged gods to force the first answer. There is a priesthood's cowardice buried in living books that will make you ashamed of your blood. And there is a third option — unwritten, unrecorded, untried — which I can see the shape of but not the substance of, because it requires a freedom I have not possessed in two hundred years.*

"A third option," Sebastian whispered. "What third option?"

*Finish what the priests started,* the dragon said, *or undo it honestly. Those are the choices history left you. But history was written by people who were afraid.* The gold in his eyes flared — brief, fierce, almost young. *You are not afraid, Oyelaran. I have watched you since you were a child oiling hinges that never opened. Do you know what I saw?*

"What?"

*I saw a girl who tended a locked door with love. Not fear. Love.* His head sank back to the stone with a groan of settling mountains. *No priest who built this place loved it. No king who fears it loves it. If any answer exists beyond wall and door, it will come from that. From you.*

Behind her, boots on cooling stone — Kestrel and Ivo picking their way down the coal-road, faces lit orange from below and grey from above, standing in the space between the last dragon and the end of everything. Kestrel's hand found hers again. Ivo knelt and pressed his forehead to the ground before the dragon, murmuring the names of the small gods one final time, and the coals around him flickered in patterns that might have been listening.

The sun was gone now. Only the dragon's body lit the world, pulsing with that vast tidal breathing, and overhead the seam between existence and erasure shivered with every beat of his failing heart.

*Choose carefully,* the dragon said, and his voice was already fraying at the edges, thread by golden thread. *And choose soon. I have held this ceiling since your ancestors' grandmothers were girls. I can hold it perhaps another season. Perhaps less.*

Sebastian stood in the heart of the dying fire, her oath singing low and expectant in her chest like a held breath, and understood that whatever she said next, the whole shape of two worlds would arrange itself around the words.

"We need to talk about the king's reliquary," she said. "And then —" her hand tightened around Kestrel's — "we need to talk about a third option."

Deep in the caldera, beneath the coals, beneath the ash, something that had not moved in two hundred years shifted its weight — and listened.
