## 17. The Bargain of Embers

The dragon spoke, and Ivo Marren felt his faith break a second time — not with the shattering of the first, but with the slow, terrible tenderness of ice giving way beneath a pilgrim's foot.

He had spent thirty years learning to live without gods. He had built that life like a man builds a house on scree: carefully, humbly, knowing it would never hold against weather. He had learned to pray the way he'd learned to walk after his defrocking — one hand pressed flat against nothing, testing every step. And now, kneeling on the obsidian shelf at the heart of Mount Cindral while an ancient creature the color of banked coals regarded him with eyes like the last two windows of a burning house, Ivo found that the old reflexes had not died after all. His lips were moving. Words were coming out of him. The liturgy of Vael, the dawn-office of the Choir of Ashes, the prayer he had recited ten thousand times before the altar and once, unforgivably, before the king.

*We who are small, attend the fire. We who are hungry, wait for morning.*

"Stop that," said the dragon. Its voice was not sound so much as heat given grammar; Ivo felt it in the fillings of his teeth, in the marrow behind his sternum. "Your words tickle. And they are not yours anymore, little priest. Nothing in this country is anyone's anymore. That is rather the problem."

Kestrel stood behind Sebastian with his hand on her shoulder, the bandage on his wound seeping through in a dark crescent, and he was looking at the dragon the way soldiers look at a battlefield they have just realized they are standing in. But Ivo could not look away from its eyes. Half-blind, they were — filmed over with centuries of ash — yet what remained of their sight was turned entirely inward, fixed on something behind the world, and Ivo understood with a lurch that the dragon was watching the boundary itself: the thin bright seam where existence was stitched against erasure, and that seam was guttering like a lamp out of oil.

"How long," Sebastian asked. Her voice was steady. It always was; it was one of the things Ivo both admired and grieved about her, this steadiness that she wore like armor over a wound no one had ever been permitted to name. "How long until you go out?"

The dragon considered. Steam rose off its flanks where the caldera's cold air met its dying warmth. Up close, Ivo could see that it was not the vast winged terror of the valley's oldest stories. It was gaunt. Its scales had gone grey as hearthstone, and along its spine ran a ridge of hollows — sockets, Ivo realized, sockets where fire had once lived, each one dark now except for a handful near the shoulders that pulsed with a light like memory.

"I was old when your ancestors' ancestors learned to write," the dragon said. "I burned when this country had a name and gave names to everything else. When the priests of Vael came to me with their great cowardice — forgive me, priest, I see it in your face, you carry their guilt like a stone in the mouth — I agreed to become what they needed. A wall that burns. A border made of appetite. Everything that crossed me would be consumed; therefore nothing crosses; therefore the gods inside starve slowly and the world outside forgets us politely." A plume of smoke, almost a sigh. "Two hundred years I have eaten my own body to keep the wall alight. Two hundred years, and your oath has fed me the rest. Do you understand what you are looking at? You are looking at the last meal."

Ivo's mouth was dry. "And when the meal ends?"

"Then I go out. And when I go out—" the dragon's head swung toward them, ponderous, patient, "—the seal dies with me. Not gently. Not gradually. A seal is not a door, children. A door opens. A seal *breaks*. Every god this country has starved for two centuries will wake at once, ravenous, unmeasured, with no consent asked and none required. They will pour into a world that has forgotten how to say no to them. Your farmers will drown in answered rain. Your lovers will be granted love until there is nothing left of them to grant. The small gods will be the merciful ones. The great ones—" it did not finish. It did not need to. Ivo had read the living books; he had seen what Vael's own villages looked like when one starving hearth-spirit got loose among echoes.

* * *

They argued, afterward, in a hollow of the caldera wall where the dragon's heat reached them only as a pressure, like standing too close to a held breath.

Sebastian sat with her back against the stone, her lantern between her knees — and Ivo watched her watch it, because all three of them knew by now that something lived in the lantern. Something small and warm and stubbornly present, a hearth-god that had followed them out of the ruined cathedral despite her refusal, and which had burned most of itself away on the ash road to save them, and which flickered even now with a light that seemed, impossibly, attentive.

"The terms are clear," Kestrel said. He spoke like a man laying out a map, because maps were how he made fear manageable. "Option one. We take the reliquary, we free the caged gods, we feed their stolen fire to the dragon. It reignites. Vael stays sealed forever — but sealed *alive*, not dead. The valley keeps its seasons-that-aren't-seasons, the prayers keep getting swallowed instead of answered, and you—" he touched Sebastian's arm, lightly, "—keep being the lock. Yearly. Until it kills you. That was the part it didn't say out loud, but we all heard it anyway."

"And option two," Sebastian said quietly.

"Option two, we do nothing. The dragon dies. The seal breaks. Everything comes back at once, all teeth and hunger, into a world that has had two hundred years to forget why the door existed." Kestrel's jaw worked. "I've stood in a country of echoes, Sebastian. I know what one loose god does to a village of memories. Now multiply it by everything."

"Not everything," Ivo said.

Both of them turned. He hadn't meant to speak. The words had risen out of him the way groundwater rises, without permission, and once begun he found he could not stop — thirty years of silence cracking open along its old fault line.

"You're framing it as the dragon framed it," Ivo said, and his voice shook, and he let it shake, because he was done performing steadiness. "Seal or break. Wall or flood. But those are a jailer's options, and the jailers wrote them, and the jailers were cowards. I've read the archive. I've read what your grandmother-grandmothers did, Sebastian — they didn't ask whether the gods could learn restraint. They didn't ask whether *people* could grow strong enough to bear divinity. They panicked, and they locked the nursery, and they put a girl in the lock and told her she was honored." He knelt, deliberately, so that his eyes were level with Sebastian's. "Freedom isn't the absence of danger. Freedom is danger *consented to*. That's what makes it holy. A prayer freely answered and freely refused — that's a relationship. A prayer stolen or compelled — that's just another cage with incense in it."

"And if the world isn't ready?" Kestrel's voice was rough. "You want to bet everyone outside on the goodness of starving gods?"

"I want to bet on the *asking*," Ivo said. "That's all priesthood ever was, before we made it empire. Asking. And being refused, and asking again, and building something together out of the refusals. The gods went mad in isolation, Kestrel. So do people. So did I." He laughed, short and bitter. "I served the old gods of Vael and then I served a crown that caged them, and both times the sin was the same: someone decided they alone knew what the divine should be permitted to do. I will not make myself the third jailer. Even a kind jailer is still a jailer."

Silence. The lantern-flicker leaned, very slightly, toward Ivo — and he felt it like a hand laid on his knee, weightless and warm, and had to close his eyes.

"It's easy to argue freedom," Sebastian said at last, "when you won't be here when the floods come."

It was not cruel. That was what made it devastating. She said it the way she reported the weather, and Ivo bowed his head to the truth of it.

"No," he admitted. "I'm an old man with a defrocked soul and no country. Whatever comes, I'll meet it at the front of the wave, singing. It's not my valley that drowns." He looked up. "But it's also not only yours. That's the thing none of us can escape, Sebastian. The choice you make at this mountain belongs to everyone who prays anywhere. There is no version of this where you decide only for Hollow Valley. There never was. That's what the oath stole from you, and it's what breaking it gives back — terrible as any gift ever is."

* * *

They went back down to the dragon at dusk — though dusk in Cindral's caldera was a strange thing, a bruised violet ring of sky above and ember-light below, and the shadows lay in two directions at once.

The dragon had not moved. Perhaps it could not; perhaps its body had become less a creature than a landscape, ridges and ravines of cooling scale, and the thought made Ivo's chest ache with a grief he couldn't locate — grief for it, or for himself, or for every living thing that had ever been asked to become architecture.

"You have deliberated," the dragon said.

"We have," Sebastian said. She stepped forward, out ahead of them both, and Ivo saw her hands trembling and her spine straight as a lit candle. "Before I answer anything, I want to know what I'm choosing between. Show me. Show me what feeding you looks like. Show me what letting you die looks like. Don't give me theology — show me."

Something moved across the dragon's ancient face that Ivo, after a moment, understood to be amusement — dry as tinder.

"Fair," it said. "Priests rarely ask to see. Very well, keeper. Look into the fire."

And it breathed.

Not flame. Flame would have been simple. What came out of it was memory given heat — the caldera filled with images that burned without consuming, and Ivo stood inside the first vision with tears already freezing on his cheeks.

He saw the reliquary-fortress, rendered vast and immediate: the cages of glass and iron, the small gods pressed flat against their walls like moths against a window, each one shining with fire that had been *taken* — taken from shrines, from hearths, from the mouths of praying children. He saw that fire funneled through pipes of consecrated silver into the dragon's hollows, and he saw the hollows fill, one by one, like windows lit at evening, and the dragon rising whole and terrible and eternal, and beyond it — the seam holding, bright and unbroken, forever. Forever. The valley sealed. The oath renewed, year upon year upon year, and Sebastian at the center of it, growing translucent as a woman made of held breath, smiling her steady smile, until the day the smile finally failed and some deputy oiled hinges that would never open and the whole arrangement began again with someone else's blood.

"There," the dragon said softly, from everywhere. "That is mercy, of a kind. That is safety. That is the price."

The vision shifted.

Now Ivo stood in the valley — the real valley, the green hidden valley, under a sky that had never known true weather. He saw the seal break. He saw it happen the way the books described the fall of Vael's cities: not as explosion but as *opening*, a vast involuntary inhale. The gods came through like water finding every crack at once. He saw the fields silver over and drown. He saw a child's careless wish for snow bury a village in July. He saw the great ones rise at the horizon, shapes too large for grief, and he saw — and this was the thing that broke him, this was the detail the dragon chose last and best — he saw people *praying to them anyway*. Running toward them with their arms up. Because two hundred years of silence had not cured the hunger; it had concentrated it, distilled it, until the valley folk wanted the gods the way the drowning want air, wisdom and consequence alike abandoned, and the gods — starving, mad, magnificent — answered *everything*.

"That is freedom," the dragon said. "Of a kind. That is honesty. That is the price."

The visions guttered. Ash settled. In the ringing dark, Ivo heard Kestrel breathing hard and fast, and heard Sebastian saying nothing at all.

*It's a lie,* Ivo wanted to shout. *Both of them, or half of each — visions are arguments, not truths, and dragons are creatures with interests.* But even as he marshaled the objection he knew the deeper truth underneath it: the dragon had shown them honestly. Both futures were real possibilities. Both prices were real prices. The horror was not that the dragon lied. The horror was that the dragon had told the truth twice.

"Ivo." Sebastian's voice, very quiet. "You said freedom means consent. Ask and refuse and ask again."

"Yes."

"Then look." She was staring past him, past the dragon, at the lantern at her feet — and Ivo followed her gaze and felt the ground tilt.

The lantern was blazing. Not with the guttering remnant that had limped beside them since the ash road — with something gathered, something *arrived*. And around it, faint as breath on winter glass, the air of the caldera had filled with lights: motes, embers, whispers of fire drifting down from the broken cathedral's direction, from the freed cages' direction, small gods come home the way birds come home, drawn to the one creature in the world still burning for them.

And within that light — within the lantern's blaze and the drifting embers and the dragon's dying glow — Ivo heard it. Faintly. Unmistakably.

Voices. Hundreds of voices. The voices of Hollow Valley — the miller's wife, the child at the gate, the elders with their blame and their fear, the deputy he had never met, the whole small stubborn congregation of the place Sebastian called home — praying. Praying not to be sealed and not to be flooded, but praying the way people pray when no one has told them what they're allowed to want: *keep us, keep us safe, but don't keep us blind.*

Sebastian's face in that light was the face of a woman hearing her own name sung correctly for the first time.

"They're asking," she whispered. "Ivo — they're *asking*."

And Ivo Marren, priest without a god, felt the old faith stir in him one more time — not as certainty, but as possibility — and turned to the dragon, whose hollows had begun, very faintly, to pulse in time with the lantern's light.

"Then perhaps," Ivo said slowly, "there is a third term to your bargain that no one has dared speak aloud."

The dragon's ancient eyes narrowed — and somewhere far below, in the deeps of the mountain, something that had not stirred in two hundred years rolled over and listened.
