## 19. When the Oath Sings

The caldera breathed.

Sebastian felt it before she understood it — a slow tidal pull in the stone beneath her boots, the mountain inhaling and exhaling like something asleep but not dead. The air at the rim tasted of iron and old smoke, of centuries compressed into cinders. Below them, far down in the throat of Mount Cindral, the last dragon-fire guttered in its bed of black glass, a red heart barely larger than a bonfire now, dimming by the hour.

And around that dying ember, the gods were falling.

They came down the mountainside like weather — not the small hearth-spirit that had ridden hidden in her lantern since the ruined cathedral, nor only the handful they had freed from the reliquary's cages two days past, but all of them, every minor divinity the crown's hunters had missed, every road-wight and well-mother and threshold-warden that had survived two hundred years of starvation in a country with no name. They poured down the ash slopes in streams of pale light, thin as breath on winter glass, and where they passed the scorched ground greened for a heartbeat and then grayed again, as if even hope could not afford to linger here.

"They're coming to die," Ivo said beside her, his voice cracked raw from days of argument and dust. He stood at the caldera's edge with his hands open at his sides, palms up, an old habit of supplication he probably didn't know he still carried. "Or to be born again. I don't know which. Maybe there's no difference anymore."

Kestrel said nothing. His wound was bound tight beneath his mail, and he stood slightly behind Sebastian's left shoulder the way he had taken to standing since the Ash Road — close enough to shield, far enough to let her lead. The hearth-god in her lantern had gone very bright and very quiet, its small flame standing perfectly still, like a child holding its breath at a doorway.

"Come on," Sebastian said, though she did not know to whom she spoke. Perhaps to herself. Perhaps to the oath coiled in her blood like a second pulse. "It's time someone asked it what it wants."

* * *

The descent into the caldera took the better part of an hour, down switchbacks of fused glass that rang faintly under their boots like the bells of a drowned church. The heat rose as they went, but it was a strange heat — dry, patient, without malice. It reminded Sebastian of the gate chamber back home: the same hush, the same sense of standing inside a held breath. Of course, she thought. Of course it feels the same. The gate was built from this. The valley was sealed with this fire. She had spent her whole life tending one end of a thread whose other end was here, burning low in the dark.

The dragon lay at the bottom like a landscape.

She had imagined — when she had allowed herself to imagine at all — something vast and coiled and terrible, wings folded like storm-fronts, eyes like furnaces. What waited for them instead was ruin wearing the shape of grandeur. The creature sprawled across half the caldera floor, and even diminished it dwarfed everything Sebastian had ever seen, but its scales had gone the color of old charcoal, cracked and dull, and along its flanks whole swathes had simply crumbled away, leaving weeping channels of cooled slag. Its head rested flat against the glass. Its eyes were filmed white, half-blind, tracking them by sound alone — the great ears twitching, swiveling, drinking in their footsteps.

When it exhaled, the wind of it bent the small trees growing from the caldera wall, and the smell was of every fire that had ever been lit in Vael, all of them at once.

"You are the Oyelaran," the dragon said.

Its voice was not sound so much as pressure, a resonance that moved through the floor and up through her bones, and beneath it, unmistakable, ran the language of the oath — the dead tongue she had recited every festival of her life without understanding, the syllables that had sung through her fingers when she touched the gate. Here, at the source, she found she could almost follow it. Almost. Like hearing a song through a wall.

"I am," she said. "Sebastian Oyelaran. Warden of the Hollow Gate." She stopped, and the words rearranged themselves in her mouth before she could stop them. "No. That isn't true anymore, is it. I'm not the warden. I never was. I'm the lock."

The dragon's nostrils flared. A thread of genuine flame, the first real fire she had seen from it, curled up from its jaws and died.

"The first of your line asked me the same question," it said, "two hundred years ago, kneeling where you stand. She asked whether she was the keeper or the kept. I told her the truth then too. She chose not to hear it. Her priests wrote their version in their living books, and their version became the world." A long pause, filled only with the tick of cooling glass. "You have read their books, little lock. You know what they were afraid of."

"The gods," Sebastian said. "They were afraid of the gods."

"They were afraid of what the gods ask." The dragon's head shifted, grinding against the floor, and the sound was an avalanche slowed to the speed of grief. "A god answered is a debt incurred. A god fed is a god grown. Your ancestors looked at a country full of listening things and saw only appetite. So they starved the listeners. They sealed the country, they bound the seal to a woman's blood, and they called it salvation." One vast eye rolled toward her, white-filmed, terrible. "And every year, at your festival, you renewed the binding. And every year, the renewal drew a little more of my fire to hold the door. And every year, I burned a little less brightly, because I was burning for two worlds."

Ivo made a small broken sound. Kestrel's hand found the back of Sebastian's shoulder — not gripping, just resting there, an anchor offered rather than imposed.

"Why tell us this now?" she asked. "Why not two hundred years ago? Why let it come to this?"

"Because no one asked until now." The dragon settled lower, its breathing slowing. "Your ancestor came with soldiers and scripture. The wardens who followed came with oil and ritual and fear. They came to maintain. You—" the ear swiveled toward her, precise as a compass needle— "you crossed a dead country to reach me. You freed what gods you could free. You walked through the graves of villages and did not look away. You are the first Oyelaran who came asking instead of commanding. So I will give you what none of them received. The choice. All three doors of it."

* * *

The dragon spoke for a long time after that, and Sebastian listened the way one listens to a landslide — unable to interrupt, able only to witness.

The first door was the old bargain, renewed. Feed the dragon the stolen fire of the caged gods — the reliquary's hoard, which they had shattered and scattered across the ash roads, its embers now drifting down the mountain with the rest of the freed divine — and it would reignite enough to sustain the seal for another age. Vael would remain erased. The valley would remain safe, small, godless, warm. And Sebastian would return to it, and kneel at the gate each festival year, and pour her life into the lock until the lock consumed her, as it had consumed every Oyelaran before her — slowly, invisibly, disguised as duty. She thought of her mother, who had died at forty-one with silvered crops outside her window and everyone calling it a blessing. She thought of her grandmother's early grave. The lineage of locks, each one worn smooth and buried young.

The second door was Ivo's door. Let the dragon die. Let the seal fail. Let the old gods return unfettered to a world that had forgotten how to pray with caution — hungry, indiscriminate, answering every wish with a flood, every prayer with a miracle that did not check whether the wisher deserved it. Freedom, yes. Honest freedom, the kind Ivo had argued for through three days of cold camps and hard miles. But also the drowning of the farmer's village, multiplied ten thousand times, until the world learned reverence the way children learn fire: by being burned.

"And the third?" Sebastian asked, when the silence finally opened space for the question.

The dragon was quiet for so long that she thought it had fallen asleep mid-breath.

"There has always been a third," it said at last. "But a third option cannot be recorded, little lock. Do you understand why?"

She did not, and then, suddenly, she did — the way one understands a thing one has known all along and never turned to face. Because anything written down becomes law. Anything spoken aloud becomes ritual. And ritual performed without understanding becomes exactly what the Hollow Gate had been for two centuries: a cage maintained by people who mistook maintenance for devotion. A third option written in a book would become, in time, merely a fourth wall.

"It can only be given," she said slowly. "Freely. Each time. By whoever stands where I'm standing."

"Now you begin to see." The dragon's voice softened — if a voice like tectonic pressure could be said to soften, this was softness, the difference between a quake and a tide. "The first oath was a chain because it was taken once, under fear, and inherited forever. The answer was never to break the chain or polish it. The answer was to replace chain with song. A song must be sung anew or it dies. A song requires a singer who consents. A song—" and here the great head lifted, agonizingly, inches off the glass, and both white eyes fixed on her— "can be changed."

* * *

It began, as all the truest things in her life had begun, at the gate.

Not the Hollow Gate itself — she was five hundred miles from it, across a country that maps refused to name — but the gate within her, the thing the priests had installed in her bloodline, the hinge of light she had felt crack open the day she doubted during the renewal rites. She felt it now the way she felt her own heartbeat: always there, mostly unnoticed, essential. And into that inner gate, the freed gods were pouring.

They came down into the caldera in rivers now, hundreds of them, thousands, drawn by the dying fire the way moths are drawn by the honest promise of flame. The hearth-god in her lantern pressed against the glass, and Sebastian opened the little door without deciding to, and it streamed out over her wrist — weightless, warm, smelling of bread and woodsmoke and the particular comfort of a room where someone is waiting up for you — and joined the descent.

And as they fell, the oath in her blood began to sing.

She had heard it before, fragments of it: in the gate chamber, in her dreams, in the strange harmonics of the ash storms. But this was different. This was the oath unbound from its function, the chain dissolving into music, every syllable she had ever recited without understanding suddenly revealed as a note in a chord so vast it had taken two centuries and a dying dragon to give it room. The song rose through her sternum, out of her mouth before she knew she was singing, and her voice — her ordinary, unremarkable, warden's voice — braided itself into the chorus of the falling gods, and the caldera became a bowl of resonance, and the dragon-fire below began, impossibly, to flicker brighter.

"What's happening?" Kestrel's voice, hoarse with awe, at her shoulder. His hand was on her arm now, gripping, steadying himself or her, he couldn't have said.

"She's hearing it," Ivo whispered. He had dropped to his knees on the glass, tears cutting tracks through the ash on his face. "Gods forgive us. She's hearing what we were supposed to be all along. Not keepers. Singers."

The song showed her things.

It showed her the first Oyelaran, kneeling on this exact spot, and it showed her the moment of her choice — not the choice the histories recorded, but the true one, the private one, the instant when the priests had pressed the oath upon her and she had accepted it not from faith but from terror, and how that terror had calcified, generation by generation, into law. It showed her the valley as it might have been: seasons turning, gates opening, prayers answered gently because the gods doing the answering were fed and known and loved rather than starved and feared. It showed her the third option — not as a plan, not as words on a page, but as a living thing, a covenant that breathed, that required renewal not because it was weak but because consent, to mean anything, must be given continuously or not at all.

Neither seal nor cage. Neither starvation nor flood. A door with a singer at it — a door that stayed shut because someone freely sang it shut each dawn, and opened because someone freely sang it open, and the singing was the choosing, and the choosing was the worship, and the worship asked nothing of her blood except her voice.

"Oh," Sebastian said, and the syllable came out in the dead language, perfect, and the caldera rang with it.

The dragon-fire surged. Freed gods poured into it like wind into embers — she watched it happen, watched the pale streams funnel down into the red heart and vanish, and with each one the fire leapt higher, burned whiter, spread outward along the dragon's cracked flanks like dawn moving across a frozen field. Color returned to the scales nearest the blaze, deep bronze and living gold, and the great lungs drew a breath that shook loose a rain of two centuries of ash, and somewhere in the depths of the creature a sound began that was neither roar nor groan but something older than either: the sound of a fire remembering what it was for.

The hearth-god reached the fire last of all the small ones, and just before it plunged in, it turned — a flicker, a hesitation, a glance thrown back up the slope at the woman with the lantern. Sebastian felt it like a hand briefly squeezed. *Thank you,* said something that had no mouth. *For opening the door.* And then it fell, and the fire took it, and the warmth that bloomed afterward smelled, for one impossible moment, of her own kitchen in the warden's house, of morning tea and the specific silence of a valley at peace.

She was crying. She discovered this only when Kestrel's thumb brushed her cheek and came away glittering with ash and salt.

"Sebastian." His voice was very quiet. Around them the caldera blazed with returning light, and the dragon's breathing had become a great bellows rhythm, steady, strong, alive, and still he spoke as if they were the only two people in the world. "What do you need us to do?"

She looked at him. She looked at Ivo, still kneeling, his face upturned like a man watching rain after drought. She looked down at the fire that was becoming a dragon again, scale by scale, and she felt the new oath rising in her — not the old words, not the chain-song, but something that had no words yet, something that was waiting, patiently, for her to choose it into being.

"Not do," she said. "Sing."

* * *

The dragon rose.

It took a long time — a mountain learning to stand — and they retreated up the switchbacks to watch it unfold, joint by joint, wing by wing, until the creature that had lain like a landscape stood like a horizon. New fire ran beneath its scales in visible rivers. Its eyes had cleared: not the furnace-blind glare of legend but something gentler and far more frightening, eyes that had watched two hundred years of diminishment and had chosen, at the very bottom of its strength, to keep burning anyway.

When it spoke again, the pressure was gone from its voice. What remained was almost tender.

"One thing remains, little lock. The fire is rekindled, but fire without purpose is only destruction. The gods have given me their essence; now the essence must be given shape. Someone must sing the new oath into the flame — freely, in her own words, in her own name — so that the boundary between the worlds becomes a door that opens and closes by consent, and not a wall that crushes whatever leans against it." The great head lowered until its eyes were level with hers, near enough that she felt the heat of its breath ruffle her hair. "I cannot write it for you. No one can. That is the entire point. Whatever you sing at the caldera's heart will bind you — not your bloodline, not your descendants, you — to the door, for as long as you choose to sing, and no longer. And understand what it will cost. Once sung, the oath cannot be unsung. The door will know your voice forever. And voices that the door knows..."

It paused, and for the first time since she had met it, the ancient creature sounded almost sorry.

"...do not get to go home the same way other people do."

Below them, the caldera's heart burned white and waiting. Above them, the freed gods wheeled in slow constellations, settling into orbits around the peak like a sky learning new stars. Ivo was already climbing down toward the fire, arms wide, laughing or weeping or both. Kestrel stood at her side, silent, his hand finding hers without ceremony, as if it had always belonged there.

Sebastian Oyelaran, warden of nothing, lock of nothing, daughter of a valley that had forgotten the world, closed her eyes and began, quietly, to compose the song that would cost her everything she had ever called home.

She already knew its first line. It had been waiting in her all along, in the dead language that was not dead at all, in the voice her mother had used to sing her to sleep:

*I am the door, and I am not afraid.*
