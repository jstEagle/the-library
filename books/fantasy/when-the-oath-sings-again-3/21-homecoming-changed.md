## 21. Homecoming, Changed

The first snow Kestrel Vane ever saw fell on the valley in the middle of what should have been harvest.

He stood at the mouth of the Hollow Gate with Sebastian's hand cold in his and watched it come down — fat, slow flakes, turning like dancers, settling on the barley that had silvered over weeks ago and never been cut. Behind them the door stood open for the first time in two hundred years, a tall rectangle of ordinary daylight where a wall of silence used to be. Ahead of them the road ran down through terraced fields toward rooftops he had known only from Sebastian's descriptions: the bell tower, the mill, the long slate roof of the meeting hall where the elders kept their careful ledgers of an unchanging year.

Snow was not in any ledger.

"It doesn't do this here," Sebastian said. Her voice was rough; she had been singing for hours, and the oath still echoed faintly in her throat, in his chest, in the stone under their boots. "It's never done this. We have planting rains and harvest suns and nothing between."

"Then you have seasons now." Kestrel caught a flake on his sleeve and watched it hold its shape there, impossibly perfect, before it melted into the wool. He thought of Vael's roads, the ones that remembered being walked, and wondered if weather could remember too — if somewhere in the erased country's deep memory, winter had simply been waiting for permission. "You'll learn to hate it by February."

"I don't know what February is."

"That," he said, "is my favorite thing I've heard all day."

They came down the road slowly, because Sebastian walked slowly now. The oath had taken something out of her even as it gave everything else back; her hair had gone white at the temples overnight, a streak like frost that hadn't been there when she sang, and she tired easily, and she stopped twice to lean against him and look back up the mountain at the caldera's faint ember-glow, visible even by day now, a red star fixed above the pass. She was both stranger and daughter to this valley, and she knew it, and she walked anyway.

The villagers came out to meet them at the third terrace, and they did not come the way a village comes out to greet its warden.

Kestrel saw it happen and understood it from the outside, the way he understood most things: first old Marta Hale, who had once accused Sebastian of laxness over the ash on the crops, standing in the road with her hands pressed together like a woman at prayer; then the children, running ahead of everyone, because children always run; then the whole slow tide of the valley behind them, spilling down the paths, stopping at a distance no one seemed to have agreed on but everyone obeyed.

They looked at Sebastian the way people look at a story they were told as children and never believed until now.

"Warden," someone said, and then someone else said, "Sebastian," and then an old man Kestrel didn't know dropped to his knees in the muddy road and began to weep without any sound at all, and after that the distance collapsed.

They did not touch her so much as gather around her, hands hovering, voices overlapping — *the bells rang themselves, the gate is open, there are birds, Tam's daughter prayed for her sick goat and it lived, is it true you're the lock, are the gods coming, is it over* — and Sebastian answered what she could and let the rest go, and Kestrel watched her become something new in real time: not the gatekeeper, not the mechanism, but the woman who walked through. He saw the villagers reach for the word *warden* and find it didn't fit anymore, and reach for *hero* and distrust that too, and finally settle, uneasily, on the only word that covered everything: *hers*. Their own. Returned from a country that didn't exist, changed.

Marta Hale pushed through last of all. She was carrying bread wrapped in cloth, which Kestrel would learn later was the valley's oldest gesture of welcome, older than any office, and she thrust it at Sebastian with the fierce embarrassment of a woman apologizing without words.

"You went out," Marta said. "You went out and you brought us winter."

"I'm sorry about the winter."

"Don't you dare be sorry." Marta's voice cracked. She looked up at the falling snow, at the silvered fields, at the open door far above them all, and her face did something complicated — grief for the sealed certainty of her whole life, and underneath it, unmistakable, joy. "My grandmother told me the world outside had weather like this. Weather you couldn't plan. Weather that was a gift or a punishment and you never got to choose which." She pressed the bread into Sebastian's hands. "Welcome home, warden. Welcome home, stranger. Both. I don't care which one you are. Eat."

* * *

Ivo Marren took a room above the meeting hall that same evening and hung a sign on the door before the mortar on his window was dry.

Kestrel read it by lantern light, amused despite himself. It was a strip of board, lettered in the defrocked priest's careful hand:

**PRAYERS HEARD HERE. ANSWERS NOT PROMISED. NO FEE.**

"You realize," Kestrel said, leaning in the doorway while Ivo arranged his few possessions — three books, a cracked chalice, a vestment folded around something soft that Kestrel pretended not to see, "that half the valley will think you're mocking the gods."

"The other half will think I'm mocking the crown. Both halves are correct." Ivo set the chalice on the shelf with the tenderness of a man handling an old wound. "Do you know what I spent thirty years doing, Vane? Answering prayers. Standing between desperate people and a divinity that had no judgment and no limit, deciding which wishes deserved to come true." He shook his head. "I was the judgment. Me. A man who couldn't keep his own smallest vows. And every time I granted something, I felt the gods getting thinner behind me, like a fire fed on its own smoke."

"And now?"

"Now they answer by consent or not at all. So I will sit with people while they pray, and sometimes the gods will listen, and mostly they won't, and it will hurt, and that is the honest shape of faith." Ivo smiled — the first unguarded smile Kestrel had seen on him since the reliquary. "A priesthood of unanswered prayer. It's either the end of my vocation or the beginning of it. I've decided not to find out which until morning."

Below them, through the floorboards, the sound of the village: doors, voices, someone laughing, someone crying, a fiddle being tuned badly and then well. A valley learning to make noise.

"There will be pilgrims," Kestrel said. "You know that. Through the gate. People coming to see the door out of a dead country."

"Yes. And some of them will pray at my window, and I will hear them honestly, and I will tell them the truth: that the gods are young again and clumsy and learning limits, and that a wish asked aloud is a seed, not a command." Ivo looked out at the snow. "That's more religion than I've managed in fifty years."

He turned back, and for a moment the exile fell away from him entirely, and Kestrel saw the priest he might have been if the world had been kinder.

"They'll ask about you too," Ivo said quietly. "The knight who broke his order to save a temple he failed to save. They'll want your story, Kestrel. What will you tell them?"

Kestrel thought about it honestly, the way he tried to think about everything now.

"That I burned nothing," he said. "And that it wasn't enough. And that a woman taught me the difference between keeping a rule and keeping faith." He shrugged. "It's a short sermon. Good sermons usually are."

* * *

Three days later, when the snow had settled into a rhythm the valley was already grumbling about and the children had invented six games involving it, Sebastian found Kestrel in the stable, mucking out stalls for a farmer whose name he'd learned that morning.

She leaned against the stall door and watched him work for a while without saying anything. He was aware of her the way he was always aware of her now — a warmth along his left side, a weight in the air — but he kept shoveling. Some things you did better with your hands.

"You don't have to do that," she said finally. "There's coin. There's the house the wardens kept. It's empty. It has a view of the gate."

"I know."

"You could go back. Through the door. Your king's soldiers won't follow past the threshold — we've watched them turn around at the pass, confused, like men walking into a wall of forgetting. You'd be safe. You could petition for reinstatement. A deserter who freed caged gods — even the crown might call that service, eventually, when it becomes politically useful to do so."

"I know that too."

"Then why are you mucking out Doss Merrow's stalls?"

Kestrel set the shovel against the wall and turned to face her, and found that the speech he'd been composing for three days — the honest accounting, the confession-shaped offer — dissolved entirely when he looked at her. So he said the plain thing instead.

"Because I deserted once already," he said, "and I learned what it costs to leave a place because staying was hard. I'm not doing it twice." He crossed to her. "You asked me once, on the ash road, why I followed you into a dead country when I'd never even seen your face before that week. Do you remember what I said?"

"You said it was because I looked like someone who needed following."

"I lied." He took her hands. They were scarred from the oath, the fingers marked with fine pale lines like frost-fern. He traced one without thinking. "I followed you because I recognized you. That night at the ford — you chose mercy over the rule, and I'd done the same thing, and I'd paid for it alone for eleven years, and I wanted to know what it cost a person who didn't pay alone." His voice went rough. "I know now. It costs everything. And it's worth everything. And I'm not going anywhere, Sebastian Oyelaran, warden, lock, key, woman who walks through. If you'll have me."

The stable was quiet except for the horses shifting and the snow ticking softly against the roof.

"You understand what you're choosing," she said. "This valley is going to change faster than anyone can bear. There will be winters that kill the orchards before we learn them. Pilgrims. Soldiers eventually, whatever the forgetting does at the pass. Gods in the hedgerows answering children by accident. I can't give you a quiet life, Kestrel. I don't even know how to promise you a long one. The oath marked me."

"You sang yourself to a door instead of a wall," he said. "Doors get opened and closed and walked through. Walls just stand there until someone tears them down." He lifted her scarred hand and kissed the frost-line across her knuckles. "I'll take the door. Every version of it."

Outside, the light was failing early — another novelty, dusk in late summer — and the first stars came out over the terraces. Somewhere up the mountain, faint as breath, the caldera glowed.

"Well," Sebastian said at last, and her voice was wet and laughing at once. "I suppose the warden's house does need a second pair of hands. There's a great deal of oiling to undo. Hinges that were never meant to open — someone ought to teach them how."

"I've been told I'm good with doors."

"You're good with locks," she corrected. "There's a difference. Locks you break. Doors you convince."

* * *

They moved into the warden's house on the evening the first thaw came — absurdly early, the valley's first-ever autumn thaw, snow melting off the eaves in bright ropes of water while the elders argued furiously about whether a season that lasted four days counted as a season at all.

Kestrel stood in the doorway of the house that had been built to guard a prison, listening to water drip, watching lamplight move in the windows of the village below. In the room behind him, Sebastian had hung her father's old oath-book open on the wall, and beside it, on a fresh page in her own hand, the new oath — the one she'd sung at the caldera — written out as song, with the notes marked, because she intended to teach it to children that way. Not as chain. As music.

Ivo had come up for supper and stayed for the light, and the three of them sat in the kitchen while the thaw roared gently off the roof, eating bread Marta Hale's recipe, and for a long while nobody spoke, because none of them needed to.

It was Ivo who finally broke it, tilting his head toward the window.

"Do you hear that?"

Kestrel listened. At first: nothing but water and wind. Then, underneath, from the direction of the gate — a mile up the switchback road, at the top of the valley — singing. Thin, high, many voices. Children's voices.

"They're at the gate," Sebastian said softly. "Deputy Pellin was supposed to—"

"He's with them," Ivo said. "I saw them go up. Half the school followed him." He paused. "He's teaching them the oath, Kestrel. As a round. Four parts. They've been practicing for two days without telling us."

Sebastian stood so quickly her chair scraped. She was at the window, one hand pressed to the glass, and Kestrel joined her, and below them and above them the valley lay in its strange new light — snowmelt flashing on the terraces, woodsmoke rising straight in the windless air, the door itself a pale seam at the head of the valley with a small crowd gathered before it, small figures moving in patterns that were unmistakably a dance, and the song drifting down clear and sweet and utterly transformed, the chain reforged as a garland.

And then the song changed.

Not wrong — changed. A fifth part slid in under the children's four, low and vast, a voice like the mountain itself humming, and the lantern hanging by the warden's door flared bright gold though no one had lit it, and every hair on Kestrel's arms rose.

"The dragon?" he breathed.

"No," Sebastian whispered. Her eyes had gone wide and shining, reflecting the flare of the lantern. "Dragons sleep. This is—" She laughed, startled, hand flying to her mouth. "That's the ember. The little god. The hearth-spirit from my lantern — it survived, it's been living in the gate-hinges, I *knew* the hinges weren't squeaking anymore—"

The fifth voice swelled, harmonizing with the children, with the mountain, with the thaw-water drumming on the roof, and the lantern blazed like a captive sunrise, and Ivo got to his feet slowly, hat in his hands, looking like a man witnessing the exact miracle he had spent fifty years failing to promise anyone.

Down in the village, one by one, other lights were answering — candles kindling in windows no hand had touched, hearth-fires standing up taller in their grates, every flame in the Hollow Gate valley leaning toward the mountain like grass toward sun.

"They're singing back," Ivo said wonderingly. "All of them. The small gods are singing *back*."

Sebastian turned from the window, and the light of the lantern gilded her white-streaked hair and her scarred hands and her face, which held more than Kestrel could read — joy and grief and vigilance and home, all of it at once, all of it hers.

"Get your coat," she said to Kestrel, and to Ivo, "bring your book — we're going up to the gate. Tonight. While the singing lasts."

She was already reaching for the door handle when she stopped, head cocked, listening.

From beyond the open gate — from outside, from the world — came hoofbeats on the switchback. Many riders, climbing fast, and riding ahead of them a horn call that Kestrel Vane knew in his bones.

The king's hunt-horn. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, carried on the thaw-wind from the pass:

a voice shouting a name he had buried eleven years ago.

His own.
