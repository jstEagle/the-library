## 1. The Valley That Forgets

There were exactly four hundred and eleven days left on Yara Crane's debt, and she knew it the way other people knew their own heartbeat — constantly, irritably, and without any hope of forgetting.

She counted them each morning before the ink was even mixed. The ledger of her obligation lived in the temple's counting room, in a hand not her own: *Yara Crane, daughter of Tomas Crane, owes the Silent Temple of the Vale two thousand days of service, to be rendered in fair and honest labor.* Her father had signed for a loan of grain during the blight winter. He had repaid the grain. He had died owing the interest.

The temple did not call it interest. The temple called it *continuation*, which was the sort of word that sounded gentle until you spent ten years inside it.

"Day nine hundred and eighty-nine," Yara said aloud, because saying it made it real, and making it real was the closest thing to power she had. She dipped her pen. "May it be short."

The scriptorium did not answer. It never did. The temple of the valley was silent by design and silent by nature, which were two different silences stacked one atop the other like stones in a wall. The first silence was the vow — every soul within these walls had sworn not to speak except in transcription, so that the words they copied would remain cleaner than their own thoughts. The second silence was older, stranger, and hung over the whole valley like weather: magic could not live here. It simply couldn't. Sparks died at the boundary stones. Charms curdled into bad smells. Wishes, if you were foolish enough to make them, came back to you sounding like your mother's voice telling you to stop being silly.

Nobody remembered who had built the temple here, though everyone agreed someone must have been very clever or very frightened to do it.

Yara had her own theory, which she kept folded up small in the back of her mind like a letter never sent: someone had wanted a place where nothing could listen.

She copied.

The morning's work was the tithe-rolls for the villages down-valley — bushels of barley, lengths of wool, one suspicious entry reading *two goats (dispute)* in Brother Aldous's cramped hand. Aldous always wrote disputes as though the goats themselves might be held responsible. Yara corrected nothing; correcting was not a scribe's place. She transcribed. She made the crooked straight only in appearance, never in fact.

Around her, the scriptorium breathed its particular breath — the scratch of forty pens, the shuffle of pages, the occasional creak of Brother Fen's chair, which had squeaked for thirty years and was, as far as anyone knew, the loudest thing in the valley.

Fen caught her looking and raised one white eyebrow.

"Nothing," Yara said. "Counting."

He nodded approvingly. Counting was respectable. Counting was what the temple understood best: days owed, pages owed, prayers copied out for patrons who believed — wrongly, but comfortably — that the Silent Temple's copies carried some special grace. They didn't. That was the joke Yara never told. The temple sold holiness it did not possess, and paid its debts with the proceeds, and called the arrangement duty.

Her pen scratched. Outside, the first true cold of the season pressed its forehead against the windows, and the light came in thin and silver, the color of the tin cups in the refectory.

*Four hundred and eleven days.*

At this rate, she would be free at thirty-one. Free to do what? She turned the question over the way you turn a smooth stone in a pocket. She had been copying since she was twelve. She knew the weight of every ledger in the archive and the temperament of every brother and sister in the order — Sister Maren, who hummed through her nose while she worked and denied it; Brother Aldous, who loved the goats dispute more than he loved most people; old Fen, who had come to the temple sixty years ago to forget something and had succeeded so thoroughly that now he forgot where he'd put his spectacles too.

And herself. She knew herself the way you know a room you've cleaned a thousand times: everything in its place, nothing surprising, dust in the corners if you looked hard enough.

What she wanted — and she only admitted this in the privacy of the counting, where wanting couldn't be charged against her account — was to write something of her own. One page. One sentence, even. Something that had never passed through another person's hand first.

Instead she wrote: *Item, barley, forty bushels, from the village of Lowmere, delivered sound.*

The morning wore on. At midday the bell rang once — the temple's bells rang only once, always, a single note that rolled down the valley and came back flattened, as though even sound grew tired here — and they went to the refectory for bread and lentils and the comfortable noiselessness of forty people eating.

It was in the afternoon that the silence trembled.

Yara noticed it the way you notice a draft: not with the ears but with the skin. She was deep in the third tithe-roll, her hand moving in its well-worn rhythm, when the pen seemed to hesitate — not her hesitation, the *pen's* — and for half a heartbeat the ink in her well shivered, all across the scriptorium, forty little black mirrors trembling at once.

Then it stopped. Then it was still.

She looked up. Forty faces bent over forty desks. Fen's chair did not creak. Even the fire in the hearth stood very straight, the way fire does not normally stand.

Yara looked down at her page. In the margin, beside *barley, forty bushels*, there was a mark she had not made. A small curl of ink, like a bird's foot, like the beginning of a letter in no alphabet she knew.

She stared at it for a long time. Then, being a scribe, and being bound by rules older than her resentment, she did the proper thing: she scraped it away with her knife, smoothed the page, and wrote over the spot, neatly, *delivered sound.*

But her heart was doing something unprofessional.

"You felt it too." Sister Maren had drifted close under cover of fetching a fresh quire, her voice pitched below hearing, her nose-hum conspicuously absent. "The ink."

"I spilled," Yara said. "Clumsy of me."

Maren looked at her the way Maren looked at disputed goats. "Sixty years I've worked this room," she murmured. "The ink has never moved. Not once. Not in a storm, not in an earthquake, not when old Brother Cassius knocked the whole well onto the floor and wept about it." She glanced toward the abbot's door at the end of the hall, shut and shuttered as always. "He'll want to know."

"He won't want to know," Yara said. "He'll want it not to have happened. Those are different things, and he's very good at the second one."

Maren's mouth twitched — the closest thing to laughter the vow permitted — and she withdrew to her desk. But Yara saw that she did not resume humming, and that twice in the next hour she stopped entirely, pen lifted, listening to a silence that no longer sat quite right in its bones.

The thing about a silence, Yara thought, is that you only notice how much it was holding when it shifts. The valley's silence wasn't empty. It was load-bearing. And somewhere beneath the tithe-rolls and the bread and the four hundred and eleven days, something had leaned against it.

* * *

Evening came early, as it does in the turning season, and with it the small ceremonies that made up the temple's idea of a life: vespers conducted in total silence, each worshipper signing their prayers in the old hand-tongue, forty pairs of hands moving like moths in the candlelight. Yara signed hers without attention — gratitude, contrition, the standard inventory — and added, privately, in the dialect of the fingers that no one taught and everyone knew: *and let tomorrow be ordinary.*

She should have known better than to ask the valley for anything. The valley granted nothing. That was rather the point of it.

Supper was soup. After supper, the brothers and sisters took their hour of recreation, which consisted mostly of sitting near one another in companionable muteness, mending robes and carving small useless objects. Fen carved birds. He had carved four hundred of them, none alike, all of them destined for the same box under his bed, and when Yara had once asked him — in signs — why birds, he had shrugged in a way that contained an entire unwritten memoir.

Yara took her hour instead in the archive, as she often did, ostensibly shelving returns, actually reading. The archive was the temple's heart and its secret shame: three rooms of ledgers going back four hundred years, records of every debt, every tithe, every contract ever brought within the valley's borders for safekeeping. People trusted the Silent Temple with their paper the way other folk trusted shrines with their sick. Nothing rotted here. Nothing changed here. Ink stayed put.

Ink stayed put.

She found herself pulling the oldest shelf, the one marked with the faded sign reading *ANTECEDENTS — DO NOT FILE HERE*, which was the sort of instruction that practically filed itself. These were the contracts predating the current order, written in hands so ornate they looked less like writing than like fences around meaning.

And there — she told herself afterward that she had gone looking for nothing, that her hand had simply wandered, though she suspected both statements were lies — she found a ledger bound in grey leather, soft as ear skin, with no title at all.

Inside, the entries were not debts of grain or wool.

She read three lines before her training asserted itself and she closed the book, set it back precisely where it had stood, squared its spine with the others, and walked out of the archive with her face arranged into its usual expression of mild, dutiful boredom.

Then she stood in the corridor in the dark and let her hands shake, because no one was watching, and shaking was free.

The entries had names in them. Names, and beside each name, in a column headed — she made herself remember it exactly, scribe's discipline, record what you see — a column headed *consideration received*, figures. Not sums of money. Years. Health. Children unborn. And one name, one single name out of hundreds, written in a hand she knew better than any living hand, because she had grown up copying letters from it, because it was the hand that had taught her own hand everything it knew:

*Crane.*

Her grandmother's name, in her grandmother's writing, entered in a ledger of souls.

No. She stood in the dark and argued with herself, briskly, the way you argue with a child about whether there's anything under the bed. Old family papers traveled. Grandmother Crane had done clerical work for half the valley; her hand appeared on a hundred documents; it meant nothing; it was a coincidence of handwriting; the word in the consideration column had probably been *grain* and the light was bad.

The light had not been bad. Yara had read by worse light for fifteen years.

She went to her cell. She lay down on her narrow bed and listened to the temple settle around her, stone cooling, timbers ticking, the great held breath of the valley pressing gently down on everything, holding everything still.

Magic cannot exist here, they said. Nothing listens here. Nothing changes here.

Then why, she asked the ceiling, does the ink move? Why tonight, after four hundred years?

The ceiling declined to answer. Ceilings in the Silent Temple were excellent at their vows.

She slept badly, in the shallow way of people carrying a secret too large for the room, and woke before the bell with the certain, unreasonable feeling — the feeling she usually disciplined out of herself before breakfast — that the ordinary days were finished.

That morning she mixed her ink, sat at her desk, and spoke the count aloud, softly, under her breath, a ritual worn smooth as a stair-stone.

"Four hundred and ten days."

And then she paused, pen above the day's first blank line, and added the thing she had never once added in fifteen years:

"May whatever's coming get here quickly."

Across the scriptorium, forty wells of ink lay perfectly still, black and obedient and waiting — and Yara Crane bent to her work, and pretended not to notice that she was no longer pretending.
