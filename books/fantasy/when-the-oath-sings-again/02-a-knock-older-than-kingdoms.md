## 2. A Knock Older Than Kingdoms

The knock came at dusk, which was the first strange thing about it.

The temple of the valley received no visitors. This was not a boast but a structural feature, like the roof or the lack of windows in the east wall. The valley itself discouraged approach — the road wound down through hills where compasses sulked and travelers reported a sensation of being *politely declined* — and so the heavy door at the foot of the bell tower had not been knocked upon in Yara Crane's eleven years of service. She had dusted it. She had oiled its hinges twice a year as part of the autumn rites. She had once, in her fourth year, pressed her ear against it to listen for the world beyond, and heard nothing but her own pulse, which she had decided at the time was very poetic and had since decided was probably a warning.

Eleven years, three months, and — she checked the tally column in her mind with the ease of long habit — forty-one days remaining on her family's debt. Not that the days moved quickly. The days here moved like honey poured uphill.

So when the knock came — three blows, spaced with terrible patience, like someone knocking who had all the time that had ever existed — Yara's quill skidded a full inch across the ledger page, leaving a ink-worm where a number should have been.

She stared at it. The worm stared back, or seemed to.

"Well," she said to the empty scriptorium, because talking to herself was one of the few indulgences the temple permitted and she took full advantage. "That's going to need blotting."

The knock came again. Three blows. Same spacing. Same patience.

Every instinct Yara had cultivated over eleven years said: *fetch the abbot.* Every instinct Yara had been born with said: *open the door, obviously, are you made of ledgers?*

She compromised, as she always did, by doing the interesting thing while narrating it to herself as duty.

***

The door opened onto the last violet light of evening, and onto a child.

Yara had prepared, in the half-minute between the second knock and the first turn of the latch, for several possibilities: a lost pilgrim, a tax collector (the temple paid no taxes, but collectors were optimists by trade), a dying traveler seeking the famous silence of the valley. She had not prepared for a child of perhaps seven or eight years, standing alone on the threshold with snow-dusted hair — snow, in early autumn, a detail her mind filed under *later, deal with later* — and eyes the color of deep well water.

The child looked up at her. Looked past her, into the dim hall behind. Looked back at her with an expression Yara could only describe as *reading.*

"Hello," Yara said carefully.

The child spoke.

Yara would spend much of that night trying to describe what came out of the child's mouth, and would fail every time. It was not that the sounds were harsh or alien. They were — this was the only word that ever came, and it embarrassed her every time — they were *older.* Each syllable landed like something that had been waiting a very long time to be said, the way a stone dropped into a still pool lands heavier than its size suggests. The language had no edges. It flowed like water finding old channels, and every channel it found seemed to run through some part of Yara's chest she had not previously known was open for traffic.

"I'm sorry," Yara said, when the child paused for breath. "I don't — I don't understand you."

The child frowned, tried again, slower. Same river-sounds. And something in the cadence tugged at Yara strangely, the way a half-remembered melody does — not the words, never the words, but the *shape* beneath them, as if the language were a garment cut for someone her size.

"You're alone," Yara guessed. "You're cold. You want to come in."

The child nodded at *come in*, which might have meant anything, and stepped over the threshold before Yara formally extended the invitation, which felt significant in a way Yara couldn't name and chose not to examine.

Up close, the child smelled faintly of paper. Not musty paper, not library paper. New paper. The smell of a page before anything is written on it.

"My name's Yara," she offered, crouching. "Yara Crane. What's yours?"

The child answered. The answer was four syllables of that impossible tongue, and Yara felt each syllable settle somewhere under her ribs like coins dropped into a well she hadn't known she was carrying.

"Right," she said faintly. "Lovely. We'll... work on it."

***

Brother Aldous found them twenty minutes later in the refectory, where Yara was attempting to feed the child bread and honey and the child was watching the honey with the wary interest of someone encountering a new species of weather.

"You opened the *door,*" Brother Aldous said. He was the temple's keeper of stores, a round, anxious man whose default expression was that of a man doing arithmetic he didn't trust.

"There was knocking."

"There is never knocking!"

"And yet." Yara pushed the bread closer to the child. "They walked out of the winter, Brother. Alone. Speaking—" she groped for a description and settled on honesty, "—speaking something I've never heard and couldn't begin to write."

Aldous peered at the child. The child peered back. Something passed between them, and Aldous took an involuntary step backward and knocked his hip on the table.

"That," he said, "is not a child from any village I know."

"It's clearly a child from somewhere."

"Children don't walk out of the *snow*—"

"Early snow," Yara conceded. "Which is its own question, yes. But questions don't get answered by leaving small people on doorsteps. Fetch the abbot if you like. Feed them first. Look at them — when did they last eat?"

Aldous looked at the child. The child, sensing leverage, arranged their face into an expression of tragic abandonment that Yara privately rated as masterful. Aldous crumbled immediately and went for the stew.

It was while the child ate — with careful, oddly formal manners, holding the spoon like a ritual object — that Yara noticed the second oddity of the evening, the one that would keep her awake long after the first had faded.

The child hummed between bites. A little descending phrase, three notes, repeated. And every time the phrase ended, Yara's left hand twitched.

She looked down. Her fingers, resting on the tabletop, were moving. Tracing. Writing, against nothing, the shapes of characters she did not know.

She pulled her hand into her lap and held it there like a misbehaving animal.

*Later,* she told herself firmly. *Deal with later.*

***

The abbot came at full dark, carrying a lantern and the particular gravity he reserved for problems involving the temple's purpose, which was silence, and the maintenance thereof.

Father Ossen was not a tall man, but he had spent thirty years practicing the art of seeming taller than whatever stood in front of him, and he deployed it now on a seven-year-old with limited effect, because the child simply looked up higher.

"You speak no tongue of the lowlands," the abbot said. "No tongue of the highlands either, I'd wager. Or the coast. Or the river kingdoms."

The child regarded him and replied in that flowing, ancient cadence.

"Curious," said the abbot, in the tone of a man finding a scorpion in his boot. "Yara. You are our best transcriber. Write down what they say. Exactly. Word for word, sound for sound. If the tongue cannot be rendered in our letters, render it however you can. I want it *on record.*"

"On record, Father?" Yara blinked. "Forgive me, but the temple doesn't record. That's rather the point of us."

"The temple records what must be contained," the abbot said, "so that it may be recognized if it returns. There is a difference between memory and record, child. Memory can be forgiven. Record must be managed." He turned back to the child. "Again, if you please."

So it was that Yara Crane sat down at her own desk, in her own corner of the scriptorium, with the most extraordinary dictation client of her career, and dipped her quill, and discovered that her hands already knew things her mind did not.

The child spoke. Yara listened. And then — this was the part she would return to again and again, turning it over like a stone with something underneath — she stopped *transcribing* and started *taking dictation.*

Her hand moved without consultation. The letters that came were not the temple's alphabet, nor any of the six scripts she'd studied for her certification in copying. They were angular and flowing at once, dense with marks above and below the line like birds perched on wires, and they came out of her hand fast, faster than she could read them back, faster than thought, while her conscious mind stood to one side watching her own wrist with the distinct sensation of being a passenger.

"Father," she heard herself say, from a great distance, "I think something is wrong with me."

"Keep writing," said the abbot.

"But I'm not writing it. It's writing me."

"Keep. Writing."

She kept writing. The page filled. The child's voice rose and fell like weather, and the ink kept pace, and somewhere around the middle of the third line Yara realized with a lurch that the shapes were becoming familiar — not the language, never the meaning, but the *rhythm* of it, the grammar of the gestures, as though the tongue were a house she had lived in as an infant and forgotten, and her hand remembered the floor plan even if she did not.

The final phrase the child spoke that night was short. Four syllables — Yara counted them, clinging to counting the way a drowning scribe clings to arithmetic. Her hand wrote them in a single unbroken stroke, and then, unbidden, added a mark beneath the line that she did not recognize and very much did not like. It looked like a signature.

She put down the quill. Her hand shook. The shaking stopped the moment she released the pen, which was somehow worse.

The abbot examined the page by lantern light for a long time. His face did something complicated and settled on blankness, which in Father Ossen was the equivalent of a scream.

"Where did you learn these characters?" he asked quietly.

"Nowhere, Father. They came out like—"

"Burn nothing. Copy nothing further. Show this to no one." He folded the page with exaggerated care, as though it might bite through the paper. "And Yara — how did it feel?"

She considered lying, decided the abbot would know, and told the truth: "Like remembering. Like it was mine before it was theirs."

Something crossed the abbot's face then, brief and cold, a shadow of a shadow. Later — much later, too late — Yara would understand that what she had seen was recognition.

"Go to your cell," he said gently. "Sleep. In the morning we will speak more about this child."

"What happens to the child, Father?"

"The child will be kept safe," said the abbot, which was one of those sentences that means precisely what it says and nothing whatsoever that it implies. "Safe from the world, and the world safe from them. Go."

***

Yara went, but sleep declined to accompany her.

She lay in her narrow cell listening to the temple's famous silence, which tonight sounded less like peace and more like held breath. Above her, faint through the stone, came the muffled sounds of the child being escorted to the east tower room — footsteps, a door, a bolt sliding home. The bolt slid with unusual smoothness. Someone had oiled it recently.

Someone had oiled a bolt on a room that had supposedly never held a prisoner.

Yara lay staring at the dark and assembled the evening's evidence the way she assembled ledger columns: the snow in autumn. The door that hadn't been knocked on in eleven years being knocked on at dusk, by a child, alone, unafraid. A room prepared. An abbot who wanted the impossible language *on record* in a temple whose entire sacred purpose was to forget. And her own hand, moving without her, writing characters that felt like home.

There was a version of events in which all of this was coincidence. Yara had copied enough contracts, in her years of debt-work, to develop a professional allergy to coincidences. Coincidences were what powerful people called the seams in their arrangements.

And beneath all of it, humming like the child's three-note phrase, ran the feeling she could not shake and did not fully want to: fascination. Terrifying, gravitational fascination. Her whole life had been rows and rules and the slow arithmetic of a debt she hadn't incurred, and tonight a door had opened — literally, physically, a door with hinges she herself had oiled — and something older than every kingdom she'd ever copied the histories of had walked through it and spoken, and her own hand had answered.

She should have been afraid. She was. But fear, she was discovering, was not the opposite of fascination. Fear was fascination wearing sensible shoes.

Somewhere past midnight she gave up on sleep, lit her candle, and took out the private notebook she kept beneath her mattress — the one place in the temple where Yara Crane wrote as Yara Crane and not as Debtor Scribe Third Class. She recorded the day properly, as training demanded: times, observations, the child's appearance, the abbot's words.

At the bottom of the entry, her pen hesitated.

Then — because she was tired, because she was rattled, because the hand that had written the impossible script belonged to her and apparently knew things — she drew, freehand, from memory, the four syllables of the child's name. The angular letters. The marks above and below the line. The strange final stroke beneath, like a signature.

She looked at it. The candle guttered, though there was no draft.

And the ink moved.

Not smeared. Not bled. *Moved* — settling, rearranging, the strokes crawling a hair's width into new positions until the four syllables sat differently on the page, and beneath them, in small, precise, unmistakable temple script — in a handwriting Yara knew better than her own, because she had spent eleven years copying documents annotated in exactly that cramped, slanted, impatient hand —

Beneath the child's name, in her grandmother's handwriting, appeared a single word:

**Crane.**

The candle went out.

Yara sat in the dark with her heart hammering like the knock on the door, and understood, with the total clarity available only to people at the exact center of catastrophe, two things at once: that the child downstairs had come looking for something.

And that whatever it was, it had her family's name written all over it.
