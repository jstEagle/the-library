## 4. What the Abbot Knows

The abbot's study smelled of lamp oil, old vellum, and — faintly, beneath everything — of nothing at all. That last smell was the one that had always unsettled Yara most. The temple of the valley was a place where magic came to die, and the abbot's study was where it went to be forgotten on purpose.

He sat behind his desk with his hands folded, a man shaped like a question mark that had given up asking questions. Brother Aldous, the door-warden, stood by the wall with the particular stiffness of someone who had recently been shouted at and did not wish to be again.

"Sit down, Scribe Crane."

Yara sat. Her ledger lay on the desk between them, closed, weighted with the abbot's inkstone as though it might get up and walk. She rather feared it would.

"You were transcribing," the abbot said.

"Yes, Reverend Father."

"And the child arrived."

"At dusk, yes. It knocked. It spoke. I wrote down what it said, or tried to—"

"What you *wrote*," the abbot said, "was not what the child said."

The lamplight made his face look older than usual, which was saying something; the abbot had been old when Yara arrived at the temple at nine years old, owing forty-one years of service against her family's debts, and he had been exactly that old ever since, in the way that stones are old.

"I don't know what you mean, Father."

"Don't you." He opened the ledger.

Yara looked, because not looking would have been worse. The page she had copied at dusk — the page where the child's strange syllables had appeared unbidden, curling out of her own pen like roots through a floorboard — was still there. But it had changed since she'd last seen it. The letters had settled into rows. They looked almost like script now. Almost like a language someone had once spoken in a kitchen, over bread, to a grandmother who hummed while she worked.

And beside the foreign lines, in her own handwriting, in ink she had no memory of spilling, someone had written:

*Balance carried forward.*

"It does that," Yara said, before she could stop herself. "It moves. The ink moves when the child speaks nearby."

The abbot closed the book gently, the way one closes a coffin lid.

"How long," he said, "have you been hearing things?"

* * *

The child heard everything.

Not with ears. Ears were a courtesy the world extended to people, and the child was only mostly a person. What the child heard was the sound underneath sounds: the abbot's voice arriving like a stone dropped into a well, and below it, far below, the well-water answering.

*The well says: keep it quiet. The well says: we have done this before. The well says: oh, not again.*

The child sat in the small room they called the guest cell, though no guest had slept in it for sixty years, and listened to the building think. Buildings thought slowly here. That was why this valley had been chosen, long ago, by people who wanted a place where thinking could be stopped. The mountain far to the north thought quickly — thought in contracts, in clauses, in the click of a seal pressing wax — but here, thought moved like honey in winter, and magic starved before it could grow teeth.

The child knew about starving. The child was, in a manner of speaking, made entirely of hunger — someone else's hunger, someone else's wanting, walking around in borrowed shape until somebody claimed it or destroyed it.

Mostly the child thought about the scribe woman.

The scribe woman had a name. Names were delicious. The child could taste hers from across the courtyard: *Yara*, warm as bread, with a bitter rind of debt around it, like cheese kept too long because there was never quite enough. And under the name, older, wrapped around it like a second skin, another taste — a taste that made the child sit up straight on the narrow cot, because the child knew that flavor.

That flavor belonged to the ledger beneath the mountain.

The child had been in that ledger. Was still in that ledger. And so had —

But thinking about that hurt, the way biting ice hurts, so the child did what children do with hurts, which is set them down carefully somewhere out of sight and hum instead.

The humming was dangerous. The child knew it was dangerous. Here in the dead valley the song came out flat and silent even to the child's own deep hearing, muffled like a bird in a sack. But the ink in the scribe woman's book had heard it anyway. Ink remembered. Ink was loyal in a way people rarely managed to be.

*Come back,* the ink had said, in whatever way ink says things. *You know me. You were written in me once.*

*I know,* the child had answered, silently. *I'm sorry about your margins.*

Footsteps in the corridor now. Two sets. One heavy, one light and quick and familiar, carrying its name like a lantern.

*Yara. Yara Crane. Crane, Crane, the bird that stands in cold water and waits.*

The door opened.

* * *

"—hearing *things*, Father?" Yara repeated. "I hear the bells. I hear Brother Fenwick snoring through the chapel wall, which frankly the whole valley hears. I hear—"

"Scribe." The abbot let the word fall between them like a coin on a counter. "Forty-one years your family owes this temple. Thirty-two you have served. Nine remain. In nine years you will walk out of this valley a free woman, and the world beyond will receive you with open arms, because you will be useful and quiet and utterly without any gift the courts can tax. That was the arrangement. Your grandmother signed it. I have her signature in front of me, as it happens."

He drew a folder from his desk drawer, and Yara's stomach dropped through the floor, through the cellar, into whatever cold bedrock lay beneath the temple.

She knew that handwriting. Of course she knew it. She had spent thirty-two years copying ledgers, and half of them were in that hand — the strong forward slant, the crossbar on the T like a sword drawn halfway from its scabbard. Her grandmother's hand. Her grandmother, who had taught her letters with barley sugar and patience, who had died three winters ago with Yara's hand held in both of hers, whispering *pay what you owe, little bird, and owe nothing else, promise me*.

"That's not—" Yara began.

"The indenture is ordinary," the abbot said. "Grain debt, roof debt, a year of physician's fees when your mother took the fever. Ordinary paper for an ordinary family. What is *not* ordinary, Scribe Crane, is this."

From the same folder, a single loose leaf. Old. Yellowed to the color of weak tea. And on it, in her grandmother's handwriting, a line of contract text — formal, sealed at the bottom with a mark Yara did not recognize, a spiral like a shell cut in half.

Yara read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two readings refused to be true.

*...in consideration of grain sufficient for seven souls through two winters, the undersigned conveys one (1) soul, being the third-born of her line, unto the Court Beneath, hold and term perpetual, redeemable only by oath of equal weight...*

"The third-born of her line," Yara said. Her voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing very far away. "Father. My mother was the third-born."

"No," said the abbot, and there was something terrible in how gently he said it. "Your mother was the fourth-born. The third-born died at birth. Or rather — the third-born was *recorded* as dying at birth. A soul conveyed is a soul absent, Scribe. The court requires a body's absence to match the ledger's entry. Paper must agree with flesh, or the whole system unravels."

Yara stared at him.

"There was never a third-born," she said. "There was my mother, and my uncle Tomas, and Aunt Wren—"

"There was a third-born," the abbot said, "and then there was not. And the family survived two famines that killed every other household in the eastern villages. Grain does not fall from the sky, child. It falls from contracts."

The room tilted. Yara gripped the arms of her chair. Somewhere in the walls, or in her blood, or in the ledger lying shut on the desk like a sleeping animal, something whispered *balance carried forward*.

"And the child?" she managed. "The child at our gate. The child speaking that tongue. What does the child have to do with—"

"With your family's purchase? Nothing. Everything." The abbot folded his hands again. "Do you know why this temple has no bells that ring true, Scribe? Why we copy ledgers by hand in a valley where no spell will light, no charm will hold, no prophecy will come? Why the Order of the Quiet Page was founded at all?"

"To record debts honestly," Yara said. It was the catechism. She'd recited it every morning for thirty-two years. "To keep the count clean."

"We keep the count clean," the abbot agreed, "because a dirty count is loud. Every soul sold beneath the mountain leaves an echo. A residue. A remainder, if you like — the part of a person that doesn't fit in a ledger, walking the world with no owner. Usually these remnants scatter within a season. Wind takes them. But sometimes — rarely, perhaps once in a generation — one coheres. Takes a shape. Learns to knock on doors."

His eyes went to the window, toward the guest cell across the courtyard, where a lamp burned behind frost-furred glass.

"The Order exists to find them first," he said. "Before anyone else does. Before a court claims them, before a hedge-witch binds them into a charm, before a *storyteller* makes them memorable. We bring them here, into the dead valley, where their nature cannot take root. And then we erase them."

"Erase," Yara repeated.

"The mind. Not the body — the body is innocent, and bodies make fine novices. But the memory, the language, the *song* — that goes. There is a rite. It is painless. It is, if anything, a mercy. What walks out of that room afterward will be a child like any other child, who will grow up sweeping floors and copying psalms and never once hear the mountain calling its name." He paused. "It is the kindest thing anyone will ever do for it."

* * *

The child felt the words arrive before they arrived — felt them coming up through the floor like frost through soil, the way it always felt decisions that concerned it. Decisions were heavy. Decisions fell downward.

*Erase. Erase. Erase.* The word rang in the child's chest like a struck bell with the clapper removed — all resonance, no release.

The child got off the cot.

The lock on the guest cell door was iron, and iron in this valley was only iron, dumb and honest, and honest things could be talked to. The child put one palm flat against it and asked it, politely, in the old tongue, whether it had ever really wanted to be a lock.

The lock considered. The lock decided it had, at some point in its manufacture, dreamed of being a hinge instead — hinges saw more of the world. The lock became a hinge. The door swung open with a small apologetic creak.

Everything was negotiable, in the end. That was the first law, the oldest one, the one the mountain ran on. Everything was negotiable except the terms already signed.

The child padded down the corridor, barefoot, following the lantern-warmth of *Yara, Yara, Crane* — and stopped outside the abbot's study, where the voices had gone quiet, and pressed an ear (an ear! such an odd, lovely invention) against the wood.

Inside, the scribe woman was saying, in a voice like a rope pulled taut: "No."

* * *

"No," Yara said.

The abbot sighed, and for a moment — just a moment — he looked tired in a human way, the way men look when they have done a hard thing many times and hoped, each time, to be talked out of it. "Scribe Crane—"

"She's a *child*. She knocked on our door at dusk. She was hungry. I gave her soup. That's the entire sum of what's happened, and you want to hollow her out like a gourd because her language frightens you."

"Her language is a *contract*, Yara." It was the first time in nine years he had used her given name, and he spent it like a man spending his last coin. "Every word of it is a clause. When she speaks, she is reading aloud from the ledger beneath the mountain — she cannot help it, she *is* a fragment of that ledger wearing shoes. Do you understand what that means? If she reaches a city. If she speaks where a broker hears her, where a magistrate's agent hears her, where *anyone* with a claim outstanding hears her — do you know what happens?"

"No," Yara said again, quieter.

"Nothing happens to her," the abbot said. "Something happens to everyone who *listens*. Because a living clause can be enforced, Scribe. A living clause can bind. There are people in this world who would give a great deal to stand next to that child while she says certain sentences aloud. Debts forgiven. Kingdoms refinanced. Wars ended, or begun. And the price is always paid by someone who isn't in the room."

He leaned forward.

"You of all people should understand the arithmetic. Your family ate for two winters because a soul went south. Now the remainder of some other poor transaction has walked into your care, and you want to keep it? Keep it *how*? On what? With what claim? You have nine years of debt and a soup ladle, child. Mercy without means is just grief with ambitions."

The words landed hard because they were true, and Yara hated him a little for making them true, and hated herself more for the small, traitorous flicker inside her — the scribe's flicker, the flicker that said *the arithmetic does work, the ledger balances, hand it over, go back to your desk, nine years and you're free*—

And that was when the door opened.

Neither of them had locked it. Neither of them had thought to. The child stood in the doorway with frost-melt dripping off one bare foot, looking from the abbot to Yara with an expression of perfect, ancient calm — the calm of a thing that has read the ending of every story it appears in.

Then it spoke.

In the dead valley, the sound should have fallen flat, should have died an inch from its lips. But the ledger lay open on the desk between them, and the ink rose to meet its native tongue like a plant turning toward the sun — and for one impossible heartbeat, Yara *heard* it. Truly heard it. Not syllables but meaning, pouring into her the way water pours into dry ground:

*"You cannot erase what is owed. I am not her memory. I am her receipt."*

The ink on the desk swam, rearranged, and settled into a single line of plain, legible Common — her grandmother's slant, her grandmother's sword-drawn T:

*Third-born of the line of Crane. Account open. Balance due.*

Silence. Long enough for the lamp to gutter and recover.

The abbot rose from his chair very slowly, and Yara watched thirty years of institutional composure leave his face like color leaving a sunset.

"That," he said finally, "is unfortunate."

"Father," Yara said, and her heart was hammering but her voice, her scribe's voice, the voice that read figures aloud to men with power over them, came out steady as a ruled line, "you told me the Order keeps the count clean. So tell me truly. If the child is a receipt for a debt in my family's name — whose property is she? Under the law you serve. Whose?"

The abbot looked at the line of ink. At the child. At Yara.

And Yara saw it happen — saw the answer arrive behind his eyes, watched him weigh it, watched him choose the institution, the way institutions are always chosen, quietly and without drama, the way a man chooses to bolt a door.

"Brother Aldous," the abbot said, without raising his voice, "fetch the rite-oils. And send word down-valley to the ferryman. No one crosses until I say."

The child slipped backward into the corridor, quick as smoke.

Yara stayed exactly where she was, hands folded, face blank, a scribe to her bones — while inside her, page by page, she was already tearing the chapter out.
