## 6. The First Winter Road

The valley's edge was a line drawn in frost.

Yara felt it before she saw it — a thinning, like the last note of a bell held too long. Behind her lay the temple, its silence already closing over the space where she had slept for eleven years. Ahead lay winter country: brown fields gone to stubble, hedgerows like stitched wounds, and a road that wound northward through land she knew only from other people's ledgers.

The child walked beside her, holding her sleeve with one hand and a stolen honey-bun with the other. The honey-bun had been Yara's breakfast. She was trying not to resent this.

"Do you have a name?" Yara asked for perhaps the sixth time since dawn.

The child looked up at her with eyes the color of old ink and said something in that language — the one older than kingdoms, the one that had crawled unbidden onto Yara's own page two nights ago. It sounded, as it always did now they were past the valley's border, less like speech and more like singing. Like someone humming a contract.

"No," Yara said wearily. "I don't speak *ledger*. That's rather the whole problem."

They walked on. Somewhere behind them, faint but persistent, Yara could still feel the temple — the way one feels a hand resting between one's shoulder blades. The abbot would have discovered the empty cell by now. He would have discovered the missing pages by midday. She tried to imagine his face and found, to her mild alarm, that what she felt was not fear but a bright, fizzing sort of relief, like a fever breaking.

*Eleven years of copying other people's debts,* she thought. *And mine were on the shelf the entire time.*

She touched the satchel at her hip, where six pages of vellum lay wrapped in her spare shift. Her grandmother's handwriting marched across them in columns — names, prices, terms. And there, third from the top: **Crane**. One soul, held as collateral beneath the mountain, payment for a famine survived.

The child tugged her sleeve and pointed ahead with the honey-bun.

A cart stood at the roadside, half in the ditch of a frozen stream, drawn by a mule whose expression suggested it had been having this argument for some time. Beside the cart, stamping warmth into his boots and swearing with the fluency of a man who had once been paid by the word, stood a stranger.

He was perhaps fifty, wrapped in a coat that had been fine ten years ago and was now merely ambitious. He had a broker's ink-stained fingers and the particular look of a man doing arithmetic about everyone he met.

"Good morning," he called, before they were even in speaking distance. "Good morning! You look like honest travelers, which is either a compliment or an opportunity, I haven't decided."

"We're not stopping," Yara said.

"Excellent. Neither am I. My cart disagrees, but carts are famously bad listeners." He gestured at the mule. "You wouldn't help an old man push? There's bread in it. Actual bread, not a metaphor."

Yara kept walking. But the child stopped — stopped dead, tilted their head, and looked at the stranger with that unnerving attention they gave to things that interested them. Then the child spoke.

One sentence. Two syllables of song.

The man went very still. His breath came out white and hung in the cold air a moment too long, as if even his exhale had paused to listen.

"Well," he said quietly. "Well, well."

"We're leaving," Yara said, taking the child's hand.

"You'll want to hear me out first." He didn't move toward them; that, more than anything, made her pause. A dangerous man would have stepped closer. This man stood exactly where he was, hands visible, and said, "Because I've just heard a language I haven't heard since I was twenty-two years old and standing in a hall under a mountain, and I would very much like to know why a child is carrying it down a winter road. And because—" he sighed, deeply, the sigh of a man who resented his own honesty, "—you're going the wrong way. The north road forks at Miller's Rest, and if you take the left fork you'll end up in the fens by dark, and the fens in winter are not survivable unless you're a fen. Are you a fen?"

"...No."

"Wonderful. Push my cart out of the stream and I'll show you the right fork. Bread included. I'm Merric, formerly of the Guild of Oath-Brokers, currently of nowhere in particular."

Yara looked at the child. The child looked back, then at the cart, then took another bite of the honey-bun with the serene entitlement of royalty.

"I don't trust you," Yara told him.

"Oh, absolutely not. Nobody should. That's practically my trade description." He beamed. "But you're carrying stolen temple documents, that child is a walking clause from a court I spent four years trying to forget, and neither of you knows where the forks are. So let's not pretend trust is on the table. Let's call it what it is: mutual inconvenience."

Against every instinct her eleven years in a silent temple had sharpened to a point, Yara set her shoulder against the cart wheel and pushed.

* * *

The mule was named Debt, which Yara found either very funny or very grim depending on how cold her feet were.

Merric talked the entire way. This, she learned quickly, was simply what he was — a man who filled silence the way other people filled ledgers, compulsively and in neat rows. He talked about the weather (bad), the road (worse), the Guild (a den of vipers with excellent stationery), and the mountain (not at all, pointedly, though he glanced north whenever he thought she wasn't watching).

"So," she said finally, as the light went long and gold across the stubble fields, "oath-broker. What does that actually mean? Besides swindling people."

"Swindling is the amateurs' department." He said it without offense. "A proper oath-broker drafts promises. Real ones. Binding ones. When a lord wants to swear fealty without meaning it, when a bride and groom want certainty instead of hope, when a merchant wants tomorrow guaranteed today — they come to us. We write it so it *holds*. Words with weight. Ink with teeth." He wiggled his stained fingers. "I was good. That was the problem. Good brokers get hired for the interesting jobs, and the interesting jobs all lead downhill eventually. Toward the mountain."

"And yours did."

"Mm." For the first time since the stream, he was quiet for nearly a full minute. "Let's say I drafted something for a client I shouldn't have, the client didn't pay, and the collateral clause was — creative. The Guild stripped my seal. Now I do honest work, which is to say any work, which is to say pushing carts out of streams and hoping travelers are gullible."

"You admitted to being untrustworthy earlier."

"I contain multitudes. Mostly debts."

The child, riding in the cart among sacks of turnips, hummed softly to itself. Yara noticed Merric flinch each time — small, controlled flinches, like a man hearing a tune he'd danced to once and regretted.

"That language," she said carefully. "You recognized it. At the stream. Before I said a word about mountains or courts."

"I did."

"How?"

Merric chewed on the question along with the last of the bread he'd shared. "When you broker an oath under the mountain — and the big ones are always under the mountain, that's where the original registry lives — you learn the sound of it. The court doesn't speak like us. They speak in terms. Clauses. Every word carries its obligations with it, like a mule carries freight." He nodded at the child. "That's registry-tongue. Old registry-tongue. First-draft tongue. I heard it exactly once, from the mouth of the Herald of Terms himself, and I have been paying for the privilege ever since." He looked at her sideways. "So. Scribe. Whose name is in those pages you're smuggling, and how closely are you related to them?"

Yara's hand went to the satchel before she could stop it. She cursed herself silently.

"Ah," said Merric, with terrible gentleness. "Family, then."

"It's nothing of yours."

"Nothing of mine, no. But here's my situation, told plainly because lying to you would waste both our evenings: I owe money in three towns and my life in one city, my seal is broken, my prospects are turnips, and you are a woman alone on a winter road with stolen property worth more than everything I've ever owned, guarded by a child who speaks the language of the court that ruined me." He spread his hands. "I can get us to the mountain. I know the roads, the tolls, which tollkeepers can be paid and which must be believed. In exchange, when whatever you're planning up there comes to pass — I want a cut. Ten percent. Of whatever a freed soul is worth."

"A soul isn't *worth* anything. That's the entire point."

"Then you can pay me ten percent of nothing and we'll both feel generous." He shrugged. "Or five percent, if your conscience is expensive. I'm flexible. Desperation makes excellent negotiators of us all."

They reached the fork at Miller's Rest as the sun went down — a leaning signpost, a shrine to some forgotten road-saint, and a single inn with smoke rising like a flag of surrender. The left fork sank away toward gray flats. The right climbed north, toward hills already wearing snow like a borrowed coat.

Merric reined Debt to a halt and pointed with his whip-hand, then visibly decided against whipping anything.

"There. Three days to Coldharbor Ford, a week after that to the mountain's shadow, if the weather holds. Which it won't. Weather never holds for anyone with somewhere to be."

Yara looked at the right-hand road. Then at the child, asleep against the turnips, breathing in that slow, singing rhythm. Then at the satchel. Six pages. One grandmother's sin. One soul in a vault under stone.

If she turned back, the temple would take the child's mind, smooth as a wet thumb across wet ink. If she went forward alone, she'd be lost in the fens by tomorrow night, and the mountain would seal its gates for a generation with her grandmother still filed away inside.

"I don't like you," she said to Merric.

"Noted and reciprocated."

"But I need you."

"Also noted." He clicked his tongue at Debt. "Welcome to the party. It's a terrible party. There will be turnips."

* * *

The inn at Miller's Rest was called the Kindly Hearth, which told Yara immediately that it was neither. It was warm, though, and warm counted for more the further north they went. Merric negotiated them a room over a shared supper of barley stew, and Yara learned that his negotiating style consisted mostly of telling the truth in a tone of voice that made it sound like a favor.

"One room," he said to the innkeeper. "Two beds, one floor pallet. The lady keeps watch over the little one. I keep watch over the door. Nobody trusts anybody, everybody sleeps better. Standard arrangement."

The innkeeper, a woman built like a doorframe, looked at Yara. Yara, exhausted to the bone and unwilling to perform innocence she didn't have, simply nodded. The innkeeper shrugged and took Merric's coin.

Later, with the child asleep between the beds and the fire burned to a red murmur, Yara sat by the window and unwrapped the pages — just to look, just to check they were real, just to read her grandmother's name one more time in the dark where no one could see her do it.

She should have known better than to do it in front of Merric.

He appeared at her shoulder without sound, which for a large man was a professional accomplishment. His eyes moved down the columns and stopped.

"Those aren't copies," he breathed.

"They're originals."

"These are *original originals*. These came off the registry desk itself. Do you know what the court does to people who carry these outside the valley?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Neither do I. That's the worst part. Even I don't know. There's no punishment recorded because nobody's ever been stupid enough to—" He caught himself, ran a hand through thinning hair, and laughed, a small cracked sound. "And here you are. With a child made of the same ink."

"She's not made of ink."

"Mm." He crouched beside her, and for a moment the broker's patter fell away and something older and wearier looked out of his face. "Scribe. Yara, was it? Listen to me, because I'll only say this honestly once and it goes against my nature. Whatever you think you're going to do under that mountain — argue with them, bargain, charm them — understand what they are. They're not cruel. Cruelty requires wanting something. They only *hold*. Every soul in that vault is a line in a book, and lines don't get released because someone feels sorry for them. Lines get released when the contract breaks." He tapped the page, right beside the name **Crane**, with one ink-stained finger. "So before we're three days further north, you'd best tell me what's written here. All of it. Because if there's a way to break this contract, I need to know it, and if there isn't—"

"If there isn't?"

He was quiet a moment. Downstairs, the innkeeper laughed at something; ordinary sounds, an ordinary world, muffled and far away.

"Then we'd best find out what a soul costs to buy back," Merric said, "and start counting what we have."

He rose, wished her goodnight with genuine-seeming courtesy, and rolled himself into his blanket by the door. Within minutes he was snoring, lightly, professionally, the snore of a man who could stop at a moment's notice.

Yara sat by the dying fire and did not sleep.

She read the contract until the words wore grooves in her. And it was near midnight, firelight guttering, that she noticed what she should have noticed in the temple — what grief had blurred and terror had smudged. There, at the foot of the page, below her grandmother's signature, was a second line of text. Smaller. Older. Written in a hand that was not her grandmother's at all.

Not the court's formal script either. Something hurried. Something scratched in desperation, in a shorthand Yara almost recognized — a clerk's hand. A scribe's hand.

And the fragment she could make out, by tilting the page to the firelight, read:

*"…the debtor named herein died three winters prior to the sealing of this bond…"*

Yara sat very still.

Her grandmother had died three years ago. Peacefully, in her bed, in the valley, mourned by everyone.

But the line did not refer to *her*. The line referred to the debtor — the soul — the one whose name stood at the head of the contract, the name Yara had skipped past a hundred times because grief had taught her not to look at it directly.

She looked at it now.

And beside it, in the margin, in that same desperate clerk's hand, someone — long ago, under the mountain, holding the pen — had written two words:

*Already dead.*

In the bed across the room, the child stirred in sleep, and sang one soft syllable into the dark — and every hair on Yara's arms rose, because for the first time she understood it. Not translated. Understood.

It meant: *ask me.*
