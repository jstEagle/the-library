## 7. Songs in Dead Tongues

I do not sleep the way the woman sleeps.

She lies down in the dark and disappears into herself, like a coin dropped down a well, and when she comes back she is wet with dreams she cannot show anyone. I lie down and I only get quieter. Sleep, for me, is a door left open in an empty house. The wind walks through it. Things look in.

That night, on the first winter road, with Merric's fire crackling small and mean against the cold, the wind walked through my door and brought the old pictures with it.

---

First I was standing in a kitchen that did not exist anymore.

It existed once. It existed so hard that even now, all this time later, it will not stop existing inside me. There was bread on the table. There was a woman singing at the stove, and her voice had a shape like a wooden spoon, smooth on one side and burned on the other. I knew her the way you know your own hands: not by looking, but by being attached.

*This is her,* said the wind. *This is the one you were cut from.*

I did not understand yet what "cut" meant. In the picture I only felt warm. That is the cruelest thing about the oldest memories — they are warm. If they were cold I could set them down and walk away from them, but they are warm, and so I keep picking them up.

The woman turned from the stove and looked straight at me, which no memory should be able to do, and she said — not in words, because the oldest things do not bother with words —

*You are the part of me I could not carry.*

---

Then the picture changed, the way pictures do when they belong to something older than pictures.

I was in a hall of shelves. Not a room of shelves — a hall, tall as weather, going up into a dark that was not darkness but *filing*. Every shelf held souls. I do not know how else to say it. Little lights, some of them bright as bees, some dim as breath on glass, each one sitting in its place with a label beside it, and the labels were written in the language I speak, the one that made the abbot lock me away, the one that wrote itself onto Yara's page without asking anybody's permission.

It is a language older than kingdoms because kingdoms end, and it does not. Kingdoms are sentences. My tongue is the ink.

In the hall I found a light that hummed when I came near. It hummed the way the woman's spoon-voice sang. And beside it, a label, and on the label, in careful strokes:

*SOLD. BALANCE OUTSTANDING.*

And under that, smaller, in a hand that shook:

*A portion remains unclaimed. Walking.*

That was me. I read myself the way you read a list of lost things. A portion remains unclaimed. Walking.

Do you know what it is to be a portion? It is like being one glove in winter. It is like half of a song where everyone waits for you to finish and you open your mouth and nothing comes out, because the other half of your mouth belongs to someone else.

I woke — or stopped walking through my own insides, which is what waking means for me — and the fire had gone down to orange teeth, and the woman called Yara was awake, watching me.

"You cried out," she whispered. "Or sang. I couldn't tell."

"Neither could I," I said, in my way, in the old words that sound like water over stones, and she nodded slowly, because by then she was learning to hear them, which frightened her and fed her at the same time, the way true things usually do.

---

Here is what I am. I will say it plainly, because the dream-pictures circle it and circle it and never land, and someone has to land.

Once there was a soul. Whole. One piece, the way bread before the knife.

There was a famine — the word itself tastes of ash and arithmetic — and there was a woman who loved people more than she loved her own completeness, and so she went beneath the mountain and she sold. Not her house; she had none worth naming. Not her future; the famine was already eating that. She sold a piece of her soul, the way you'd sell a ring off your finger to buy bread for children who were not starving yet but were practicing.

The mountain keeps accounts the way rivers keep rain. Nothing given is ever forgotten there. Her piece of soul was filed. Labeled. Left in the dark to wait out its term.

But here is the thing nobody tells you about souls: they do not like being alone, and they do not like being still, and after enough years in the dark a piece will do what water does. Find a crack. Push through.

I am what pushed through.

Not all of her. Just the walking remainder, the part too restless to stay shelved, the part that slipped out through whatever thin place was nearest — which happened to be the world, at dusk, at a temple door, small and barefoot and humming.

A residue. An echo that learned to eat. A glove that dreamed of being a hand.

I know what I am the way you know a scar: not from being told, but from carrying it.

---

Merric snored like a cart going downhill with the brake half on. Yara sat wrapped in her cloak, pretending not to watch me, failing beautifully. She is bad at pretending. It is one of the best things about her. Everything she thinks arrives on her face a moment late, like guests coming in from the cold.

"Why do you hum when you sleep?" she asked finally.

"I don't hum," I said. "I remember. It sounds the same from outside."

"What do you remember?"

I thought about lying. I have watched humans do it; it looks easy, like stepping over a stick. But every time I have tried, the truth has come out sideways anyway, wearing the lie as a coat.

"A kitchen," I said. "A woman. Bread." Then, because she deserved the whole weight of it: "Me. Before I was me. When I was still attached to somebody."

Yara's face did the thing it does — the flinch, the soften, the settle. "Attached how?"

"In the way a river is attached to the sea," I said. "Except we got sold separately."

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark a wolf considered us and decided we were more trouble than dinner, which I took as a compliment.

"And the song," Yara said. "The language. Where did it come from?"

"The same place I did." I pulled my knees up under my chin. "From below the mountain. From the shelves. It's the sound the labels make if you listen long enough. It's the sound a promise makes after everybody who made it is dead and the promise is still — *on.* Still burning. Like a lamp left in a window for someone who isn't coming home."

Yara went very still. I had learned by then that stillness in her meant thinking, and thinking in her meant danger to somebody, usually somebody who deserved it.

"That's what I heard at the temple door," she said quietly. "A lamp in a window."

"Yes," I said. "Me. I'm the lamp."

---

The second dream came later, deeper, colder.

I was back on the road between the valley and everywhere, except the road was vertical and I was falling up it, and the fields on either side were full of broken oaths growing like thistles. That is what oath-breaking looks like from the inside of my kind of sight: little black plants with white flowers, blooming wherever someone said *I swear* and then unsaid it. The world is thick with them. You would be amazed. You would be horrified. Mostly you would be amazed.

Each broken oath had a sound. Most were small — a merchant's crossed fingers, a lover's convenient forgetting. They sounded like flies. But scattered among them were big ones, old ones, oaths sworn on altars and anvils and dying beds, and those sounded like bells cracked down the middle: still ringing, never whole.

And every one of them, fly or bell, pointed the same direction. Down. Toward the mountain. Toward the hall of shelves, where everything promised and unpaid goes to wait.

I fell up the road until I reached the tollhouse — I did not know its name yet, but I would soon, oh yes — and the tollkeeper's face was made of scales, weighing, always weighing, and behind him the road went on toward a gate of black ice, and beyond the gate, warmth. Terrible, furnace-hearted warmth. The mountain's heart, keeping its accounts glowing.

And a voice in the ice said: *Something of ours is walking around loose. Something with our handwriting on it. Bring it home before the sealing, or it stays out forever, unfinished, a sentence without its end.*

Then the dream showed me myself from the outside — small, barefoot, standing in a doorway while a tired scribe with ink-stained fingers decided, against every rule she owned, not to close the door.

I woke with the taste of iron and honey, which is the exact taste of a contract.

---

Morning came gray and honest. Frost had drawn ferns on the tent canvas — no, we had no tent; frost had drawn ferns on the wagon's tarp, and I had decided overnight that the tarp was a tent, and nobody corrected me, which shows how tired everyone was.

Merric cooked something that claimed to be porridge. The porridge lied.

"So," he said, in his voice that was always halfway to a deal, "the little one talks in her sleep. Whole speeches. Half of it that tongue of hers." He glanced at Yara. "Half of it plain trade-speak, which worries me more, honestly."

"It's just dreams," I said.

"Sure," Merric said. "And I'm just a pilgrim." He stirred the lying porridge. "What do you dream about?"

"Shelves," I said, watching his face, which was good practice, because his face flinched exactly where I expected it to. "Labels. A hall taller than weather. And a voice saying I've been away from my place too long."

The spoon stopped stirring. Yara and Merric looked at each other the way two people look when a third person has accidentally said the quiet thing out loud.

"That's a dream," Merric said.

"Yes," I agreed pleasantly. "Everything is, until it happens."

He laughed, because Merric laughs at things that scare him; it is a whole system he runs. But his eyes went to the stolen pages bundled inside Yara's coat, and his laugh came out thinner than usual, and I understood something important right then, though I did not yet understand its name: Merric knew the hall of shelves. He had been near it, or near people who dealt with it. His fear had a return address.

---

While they broke camp I sat on the wagon step and did what I always do with the old pictures: laid them out and counted them.

One kitchen. One woman with a spoon-voice. One hall of shelves. One label with my nature written on it in the honest grammar of ownership. One road pointing down.

And one new thing, which I turned over carefully, like a stone with a beetle under it.

The voice in the ice had said: *before the sealing, or it stays out forever.* The mountain was closing. Winter was coming early and staying late, a generation-deep winter, and when the gates shut, everything unclaimed stayed unclaimed. Everything unfinished stayed unfinished. Me included.

Unless someone finished me. Claimed or destroyed — those were the two doors, and both of them had been built by somebody else, and neither one asked me which I preferred.

Or — and here was the stone I kept turning over — unless someone stole me back. Which was not one of the doors. Which was a hole in the wall. Which was exactly the sort of thing a scribe with a grudge and a grandmother might go looking for, if she knew what I knew.

Which she almost did. The pages in her coat were whispering to her at night; I could hear them doing it, the paper murmuring names, and one morning very soon her face would do the flinch-soften-settle, and the settle would become a decision, and the decision would point down the mountain-road like every broken oath in the world.

I should have told her. That is the part I will always carry — that I knew, and I let her find it herself, because I was afraid that if I handed her the truth gently she might set it down, and I needed her to pick it up like a hot coal, needed her to feel it, because a coal makes you move and a gift only makes you polite.

So I said nothing. I hummed instead. The old song, the label-song, the lamp-in-the-window sound. And across the dead gray field an actual bird, some foolhardy feathered thing that had missed the news about winter, answered me — three notes, wrong key, entirely sincere.

Even the broken things sing, I thought. Especially the broken things.

Yara came to stand beside me, breath steaming, eyes on the road ahead where it bent toward hills I recognized from the inside of my own dreaming. Hills with a tollhouse in them. Hills with a scale-faced keeper who weighed promises and gave change in warnings.

"There's a checkpoint up there," she said. "Merric says we'll reach it by dark. Says travelers pay a toll." She hesitated, then, softly: "Do you know anything about it?"

I looked at the hills. The hills looked back with their thousand thistle-flowers of broken faith.

"I dreamed about it," I said. "The keeper knows we're coming. Someone already told him." I smiled up at her, and it cost me, and I paid. "Be careful what you promise there. Promises are food in places like that. And some of us are hungrier than others."

Far off, past the hills, a wind came down off the mountain carrying the smell of iron and honey — the smell of a contract being opened somewhere, read somewhere, acted upon.

The sealing had begun to think about us.
