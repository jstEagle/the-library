## 9. Grandmother's Hand

The fire in the waystation had burned down to a red heart by the time Yara spread the stolen pages on the floorboards.

She had been avoiding this all day. She'd walked eleven miles with the contract folded against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and every mile she'd told herself she would read it *later*, when there was light, when there was privacy, when her hands stopped shaking — and now there was nothing left between her and it except firelight and a sleeping child curled against Merric's coat, breathing in that slow way children breathe when they have finally decided a place is safe.

Merric sat across from her, sharpening a knife he didn't need to sharpen. He'd been doing it for an hour. It was, Yara suspected, his version of prayer.

"You don't have to sit here," she said.

"Probably not." He turned the blade. "But if you faint, I want to be the one who gets to say I told you so."

"You haven't told me anything yet."

"I've implied a great deal. You simply weren't listening."

Despite everything, despite the road behind them and the mountain ahead, Yara felt the corner of her mouth twitch. Then she looked back at the pages, and the twitch died.

The paper was old. Not temple-old, not the bleached and pressed vellum she'd spent six years copying until her fingers knew its grain better than her mother's face. This was older, rougher, the kind of skin-parchment made before anyone thought to make things *nice*. The ink had gone brown at the edges, like a bruise healing in reverse.

At the top of the first page, in letters that seemed to sit slightly *above* the surface rather than on it, was written:

**CONTRACT OF SOUL AND SUBSISTENCE**
*One soul, freely given, against the lives of four.*

Yara read it three times. The words did not change. She almost wished they would; changing words she could blame on the child's strange language, on magic, on the road. But these were plain trade-tongue, plain as a butcher's sign, and they did not move.

*Four lives.* Her family had been five, in those years. Grandmother, father, two uncles, and the baby aunt who hadn't survived the winter anyway.

Which meant the fifth life had never been part of the bargain.

Yara didn't realize she'd said it aloud until Merric looked up. "Fifth what?"

"Nothing." She wet her lips. "Keep watching the child."

"She's asleep."

"Keep watching her anyway."

He raised his eyebrows but went back to his knife, and Yara bent over the page, and the firelight tilted, and the waystation fell away, and she was seven years old again with flour on her nose.

* * *

Grandmother Crane had hands like tree roots and a laugh you could hear through two walls, and she had taught Yara her letters before the temple ever saw her, tracing them in spilled flour on the kitchen table because paper cost too much.

"*A* is a roof," she'd say, drawing two strokes and a crossbar. "A roof keeps the rain off. Every letter is a little house for a sound. You build enough houses, and one day you can live anywhere."

"Even in books?"

"*Especially* in books. Books are where the poor keep their palaces."

She'd smelled of woodsmoke and dried sage and, faintly, of the tallow candles she made and sold three streets over. She sang while she worked — old songs, valley songs, songs with words nobody used anymore, and when young Yara asked what the words meant, Grandmother would tap her nose and say, "Older than me, love. Older than the temple. Some words you carry without carrying their meaning, the way you carry a scar."

There had been a famine year — Yara knew this the way everyone in the valley knew it, as weather, as background — the year the river forgot how to fill the irrigation cuts and the granary lords tripled their prices overnight. The Cranes had come out of it alive. Everyone said so. *Lucky*, the neighbors called them. *Blessed*, said the ones who were less lucky, with a flatness that wasn't quite kindness.

Grandmother never said lucky. When the famine came up at all — and it rarely did — she'd go quiet in a particular way, her hands stilling in whatever work they held, and she'd look toward the mountain the way you look toward a door when you're waiting for someone who isn't coming.

Yara had thought, all these years, that it was grief for the baby aunt.

Now, kneeling over a parchment older than her grief, she understood it might have been something closer to *waiting for a bill*.

* * *

The second page was the terms.

She read slowly, the way the abbot had taught her to read contracts — which was, she realized with a sour twist, the same way you read a trap: assume every generous word conceals a tooth. The court beneath the mountain, it appeared, had been in the business of generosity for a very long time.

*The undersigned offers one soul entire, undivided, of sound condition, in exchange for: sufficient grain to sustain four persons through the winter and seed-corn for the spring planting; remission of debts owed to the House of Aldous Granary; and passage of the said debt to the heirs of the undersigned, that the bargain be honored across bloodlines until such time as the soul is reclaimed or redeemed.*

*Redemption price: an oath of equal weight, tendered by an heir of equal standing.*

*Equal standing,* Yara murmured. "What does that even mean?"

"It means the court decides," Merric said, without looking up from his knife. "It always means the court decides. That's the whole architecture of the place. Every word is a door and every door opens only inward."

"You know a lot about doors."

"I was a broker, scribe. Doors are the trade." He set the knife down at last, which she chose to interpret as a good sign. "Read the signature line. Read it properly. Don't skim."

She hadn't been going to skim. But she was glad of the warning, because when she reached the bottom of the page, her eyes tried to slide off it the way a boot slides off ice, and she had to force them back, letter by letter.

The name was written in a hand she knew.

Not the careful schoolroom hand Grandmother had taught her in the flour. The other hand — the hurried, slantwise scrawl from the backs of recipes, from the margins of the one book the family owned, from the note pinned to the door the last morning Yara had ever seen her alive:

*Gone up-valley. Feed the hens. Back by dark.*

She had not come back by dark. They had found her three days later on the mountain path, peaceful, as though she'd lain down for a nap in the snow, and the temple priest had said her heart gave out, and everyone had nodded because hearts do give out, especially old ones, especially after hard winters.

The signature on the contract was dated nine days before she died.

Yara's vision blurred. She wiped her eyes impatiently with the heel of her hand, angry at them, angry at herself, and read the rest of the line.

Beside the name, in the same slantwise scrawl, was a small notation — not part of the formal text, something added after, squeezed into the margin the way Grandmother used to squeeze corrections into the edges of her accounts:

*Forgive me. I chose the living.*

And below that, in different ink, dry and precise and horribly official, in a script Yara recognized from the temple's own ledgers — the script of clerks who served powers older than temples:

*Received in full. Condition: intact. Term: indefinite.*

"They let her write that," Yara whispered. "They let her add her own words to it."

"The court likes signatures," Merric said quietly. "A coerced soul doesn't hold its value. Everything under that mountain has to be *freely given*, or the whole edifice comes down. So they wait. They wait until someone is desperate enough that free will and no choice at all look identical." He paused. "It's very elegant. I mean that as an obscenity."

Yara laughed. It came out wrong — a cracked, ugly sound — and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth until it passed.

* * *

She worked backward through the arithmetic of it, because working was better than feeling, and numbers had never once lied to her.

Nine days between signing and death. Nine days of grain in the house. Four lives sustained — hers, her father's, her uncles'. And then Grandmother walking up the mountain path alone, in winter, nine days later, as if delivering something.

As if she'd carried it up there herself. As if she'd wanted the walk to be the last thing she did on her own legs.

Or — Yara's stomach turned — as if the contract required delivery in person, and the mountain had called, and she had answered the only way a woman of her word could.

"Why did the debt pass down?" Yara asked. Her voice sounded far away. "It says *heirs*. Why would the court honor a bargain across generations? What do they get?"

Merric was quiet for a moment. Outside, wind worried at the shutters, and the child stirred, sighed, and settled deeper into sleep.

"What does any lender want?" he said finally. "Perpetuity. A soul redeemed after one generation is a transaction. A soul that sits in a vault for three, five, seven generations, accruing interest in the only currency the mountain cares about—" he tapped the page, right beside the word *indefinite*, "—that's an annuity. That's *leverage*. Somewhere under there is a ledger of families like yours, scribe. Families who think they got lucky. Families who tell stories about blessed grandmothers." His voice softened, which somehow made it worse. "You weren't lucky. You were invested in."

Yara looked at him. "You enjoy saying terrible things clearly."

"It's a professional habit. The alternative is saying them unclearly, and unclear bargains are how I ended up here." He gestured vaguely at the waystation, the road, the whole ruined shape of his life.

"And me?" she asked. "Why am I *equal standing*? I'm nobody. I copy other people's words in a building where magic can't exist."

"Is that what you think that temple is for?" Merric shook his head slowly. "A valley where magic cannot follow, staffed entirely by scribes, sitting directly in the shadow of the mountain? Scribe, your temple isn't a refuge from the ledger. It's a *branch office*. Why do you think they taught you to read contracts the way you just described — assuming every generous word hides a tooth?" He leaned forward. "They were training you to read *their* handwriting."

The fire popped. Yara sat very still.

Because he was right, and she had known it since the night the ink moved, and knowing it changed nothing about the fact that she had loved that temple once, or thought she had, the way you can love a room you've never left.

* * *

The anger came later, the way storms come over the valley — visible for hours before it arrives, and then suddenly all around you.

She watched the sleeping child — small, hollow-eyed, humming fragments of a language older than kingdoms in her sleep, a language that had shown up unbidden on Yara's own page — and the arithmetic assembled itself with terrible neatness.

One soul, given. Four lives saved. One life lost anyway, the baby aunt, outside the terms. And then decades of quiet, of luck, of survival — and underneath all of it, a soul in a vault growing old in the dark, and a debt accruing against a family that didn't know it existed.

And the child.

The child who was, Yara understood now — had perhaps understood from the first knock at the temple door — not a stranger at all. The residue. The echo. Whatever the mountain had taken from Grandmother Crane, some part of it had walked back out, wearing a child's shape, speaking a child's version of an ancient tongue, and knocked on the one door in the valley where a Crane still worked.

*I chose the living,* Grandmother had written.

"Well," Yara said to the empty room, to the dead woman whose handwriting trembled slightly even now, after all these years, "so did I."

Her voice cracked on the last word. Grief and fury braided together so tightly she couldn't tell which was which — fury at the court, yes, but also at Grandmother, at the beloved root-handed singing woman who had traded a soul and told no one, who had left her grandchildren holding a bill disguised as a blessing, who had *died* instead of explaining, and left Yara to learn the truth from a thief she'd known four days.

"You could have told us," she whispered. "You could have told *me*. I was the one who could read."

And then, horribly, gently, the memory arrived — unbidden, the way the child's words had arrived on her page — of a kitchen evening, flour on the table, Grandmother teaching her letters, pausing mid-stroke to say, apropos of nothing:

"One day, love, someone will put a piece of paper in front of you and ask you to sign it. Promise me you'll read every word. Promise me twice."

Seven-year-old Yara had promised twice, laughing, thinking it was about market receipts.

Grandmother hadn't laughed. Grandmother had looked at her for a long moment with an expression Yara had spent twenty years failing to identify, and had kissed the top of her head, flour and all, and gone back to singing in a language older than the temple.

Yara put her face in her hands.

*She knew.* She had known what she was leaving in the world, and she had armed the only person she could reach, the best way she knew how — with letters, with caution, with a promise extracted twice. She hadn't told her because telling her would have made Yara liable. Knowledge was collateral. Grandmother had kept the debt alone so long as she could bear it.

It didn't stop the anger. Anger, Yara was learning, doesn't care about understanding; it just sits beside it, arms crossed, refusing to leave. But the anger had acquired a companion, and the two of them together weighed more than either alone.

Across the room, the child opened her eyes.

Not all the way. Just a sliver, firelit, watching Yara with an expression far too old for the face it lived on. And then the child hummed — three notes, soft, half-asleep — and Yara's spine went cold, because she knew the tune.

It was the song Grandmother used to sing over the bread dough. The one with the words nobody used anymore. The one she'd said was older than the temple.

The child closed her eyes again and slept on, and Yara sat alone with the contract and her grandmother's trembling hand and the growing, certain knowledge of what the song had been.

It was not a lullaby.

It was a receipt.

* * *

Merric found her an hour later, still awake, the pages repacked and strapped tight against her ribs.

"We reach the city of Counted Days by tomorrow's dusk," he said, crouching to feed the fire. "Assuming the roads behave, which they won't. There are people there looking for both of us, and now, thanks to your honesty at the tollhouse, they'll know exactly which road we're on." He glanced at her. "You should sleep."

"I will." She didn't move. "Merric. The redemption price. An oath of equal weight, tendered by an heir of equal standing. If the court decides what counts as equal—"

"Then start deciding what you'd pay," he said, "before they decide for you. That's the only advantage anyone's ever had in that courtroom: arriving already knowing your own price." He fed another stick to the flames. "Most people never manage it. Most people find out what they'd pay *while they're paying it*, which is a terrible way to learn."

Yara looked at the sleeping child. At the strap across her own ribs where the contract lay. At the memory of flour letters spelling little houses for sounds.

"I'm starting to have an idea," she said quietly.

"Don't tell me," Merric said. "For both our sakes. Ideas like yours have a way of becoming everyone's problem."

Outside, far to the north, past the black line of the hills, the sky above the mountain flickered — once, briefly — with a light that was not lightning and not dawn. The long winter, gathering itself. Days, not months.

Yara pulled her coat tighter and watched the road ahead of them appear, faintly, in the fire's dying light.

Tomorrow, the city. Tomorrow, the counting.
