## 10. The City of Counted Days

The city announced itself three days before we saw it, which I found suspicious. A bell in the mountains rang at odd hours, and Merric said it was the Counting Bell — the City of Vell and Marrow measured its days aloud so that no one could claim ignorance of a deadline. Every debt in the city was rung into the air, he said, like a man confessing his sins to the whole valley.

"And what happens when someone misses a count?" the child asked.

Merric smiled the way a knife smiles. "The city remembers for them."

I had learned by then that Merric answered most questions about consequences with a certain relish, as though consequences were old friends he'd fallen out with but still respected. He walked ahead of us on the frozen road with his coat collar up and his hands jammed in his sleeves, and he whistled when he thought we weren't listening — small, tuneless fragments of songs from places he clearly hadn't been honest about visiting.

The child walked between us, wrapped in my spare cloak, humming back at him.

That had become the shape of our days: Merric ahead, whistling lies; the child behind him, humming truths; me at the rear with the stolen contract pages sewn into the lining of my scribe's coat, checking the stitches every hour like a woman counting her own heartbeat.

The pages were the whole reason any of this was happening. Grandmother's handwriting on a contract older than the famine it had bought our survival with. A soul sold beneath the mountain, walking now beside me in the body of a child who liked to name crows.

I checked the stitches again.

* * *

The City of Counted Days rose out of the winter plain like a stack of ledgers someone had abandoned mid-argument. That was my first impression and I stand by it. Walls of pale stone banded in darker strata, roofs pitched steeply against snow, chimneys everywhere — hundreds of them, all smoking, as if the entire city were one enormous document slowly being burned to keep itself warm.

The gates were open. The gatekeeper didn't even look up from his tally-slate.

"Business?" he said.

"Passage," said Merric smoothly. "We're archivists."

I nearly choked. The child, who had developed a gift for mimicry over the past week, looked up at the gatekeeper and repeated, in a voice of perfect bureaucratic boredom: "Archivists."

The gatekeeper waved us through without interest. Later I would understand why: in Vell and Marrow, *archivist* was the least remarkable thing a person could be. The whole city was archives. Streets named after filing systems. Doors marked with catalog numbers instead of house signs. I saw a bakery called *Miscellaneous Receipts, Subsection D* and a tavern called *Pending Disputes*, and I felt, absurdly, homesick.

"They file everything," Merric said, watching my face with amusement. "Births, deaths, debts, grudges. Especially grudges. There's a hall where they keep every insult ever spoken inside the walls, cross-referenced by speaker and victim." He tapped his nose. "Which is why I haven't been here in eleven years."

"Eleven years," I repeated. "What did you do eleven years ago?"

"Nothing worth filing," he said, and turned down the main street before I could press him, which was how Merric answered most questions about his past: by developing an urgent interest in architecture.

* * *

He found us lodging above a chandler's shop on a street called *Unresolved Correspondence*. The room had two beds and smelled of tallow and old paper, and the window overlooked a courtyard where clerks sat even in the cold, copying under awnings, their breath fogging over their work.

"Why here?" I asked, once the door was shut and bolted.

"Because it's cheap, central, and full of people who mind their own business because minding other people's is a licensed profession here." He dropped onto a bed with a groan that suggested his bones were also keeping accounts against him. "Three days' rest. New boots for the little one. Then north, before the mountain seals."

The child was already at the window, watching the clerks. "They write the same word over and over," they reported. "But it changes. It gets tired."

I went to look. The nearest clerk was indeed writing the same phrase — *day four hundred and six of the matter of Hessel v. Ondrey* — and by the twentieth repetition his letters did seem to sag, like a person asked to carry something heavy across a very long room.

"The city counts its days out loud," I murmured. "And the days get heavier the longer they go uncounted—unresolved." Something cold moved through me that had nothing to do with the season. Our stolen pages were eleven years old and unresolved. What weight would they have accumulated?

Merric, behind me, had gone quiet in a way I'd learned to distrust more than his talking.

"Merric."

"We'll rest," he said. "Then we'll move. Nobody's looking for you. Nobody knows you exist."

"And nobody's looking for you?"

He smiled his knife-smile. "Yara Crane. If anyone were looking for me in this city, do you think I'd have brought you here?"

I considered the many possible answers to that and chose, generously, not to voice them. The child laughed — a small bright sound, entirely at Merric's expense, though he pretended not to know it.

* * *

We rested one day. On the second day, Merric went out alone to buy boots and came back white around the mouth.

He tried to hide it. He was actually good at hiding things; I'll grant him that. But he set the boots down too carefully, the way you set down a thing you're afraid will ring, and when the child asked if the cobbler had been nice, he said, "Very," in the tone of a man describing his own funeral arrangements.

"You saw someone," I said.

"I saw a hat."

"A hat."

"A particular hat. Belonging to a particular person who may have been wearing it." He began repacking his bag with great efficiency, which was itself alarming, because Merric packed the way other men prayed — slowly, ritually, with much sighing. "Change of plan. We leave tonight. North road, straight through, no stops."

"Who wears the hat?"

"A colleague." He cinched a strap hard enough to make it complain. "Former colleague. We parted ways over a disagreement regarding the definition of *colleague*."

The child looked at me. I looked at the child. Somewhere in those weeks on the road we had developed an entire silent language of shared judgment regarding Merric Vane, oath-broker, disgraced, and it required no words at all.

"Names would help," I said.

"Would they?" said Merric. "Because I find names make everything worse. You say a name in this city and within the hour it's been filed, cross-referenced, and sold to whoever hates them most. Names are currency here, Yara. Don't spend yours."

It was good advice. It was, in fact, exactly the kind of advice a man gives when he's withholding a name.

We agreed to leave at dusk. And then the bells began.

* * *

Not the Counting Bell. This was faster — a quick, urgent hammering from somewhere near the river district, and beneath it, faintly, voices. Shouting. Not the ordinary shouting of a market day; this had structure to it, purpose. Men calling positions to each other the way fowlers call to each other across a marsh.

Merric stood very still in the middle of the room, listening, and I watched eleven years catch up with his face all at once.

"That's not a hat," I said.

"No," he said. "It isn't."

Downstairs, the chandler's wife was leaning out her door, talking to a neighbor. I caught fragments: *the Quorum men*, *the archive-streets*, *they've sealed the bridge*. Merric heard it too. His hand found the wall and stayed there.

"What are your options?" I asked quietly.

"Options." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "My options are a walled city with a filing system for grudges, a north road I can't reach because they've sealed the bridge, and two witnesses who've seen my face for two hundred miles." He looked at me, and for once there was no performance in it at all. Just a tired man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "You should take the pages and go. Now, while they're hunting me. I'm loud, Yara. I'm a bonfire. You can be smoke."

And here is the thing I have thought about since, in the long years after, when I try to understand why I did what I did next: he was right. Every part of it was correct. Take the pages, take the child, walk out through the archive-streets while every eye in Vell and Marrow was pointed at Merric Vane. It was the rational choice. It was the choice I would have advised any scribe to make, in any ledger, in any temple.

The child took my hand.

They didn't say anything. They just took my hand, the way they always did when the world got too large, and I felt their fingers — small, cold from the window, alive — and I understood that whatever I decided in the next ten seconds, I would be deciding in front of them. That they would learn from it. That children are ledgers too, and everything gets written down.

"Damn it," I said. "Merric. Which way do they expect you to run?"

His face did something complicated. "East. Toward the bridge."

"Then we go west. Through the deep stacks." I was already pulling on my coat, feeling for the stitched pages. "And you're going to tell me, on the way, exactly who these people are and what you owe them, because I am done being surprised by you. Do you understand? Done. Surprises are spent. The account is closed."

For the first time since I'd met him, Merric Vane looked genuinely afraid of me.

"Yes," he said. "Understood."

* * *

The archive-streets of Vell and Marrow at dusk are a place I will describe badly, because there is nothing to compare them to. Imagine a library that decided to become a city. Narrow lanes walled with shelves — actual exterior shelving, three stories high, holding the overflow records of a century, protected from weather by slanted slate roofs that made the streets feel like the inside of some enormous throat. Lanterns hung at intervals. Paper dust drifted in the lamplight like slow snow.

The shouting had followed us. Of course it had. The Quorum — that was the name Merric finally gave, spitting it like a bad seed — had eyes at every corner, and the city's whole culture conspired with them: everyone we passed glanced at our faces and visibly filed us away.

"This way," Merric breathed, and pulled us down a lane so narrow the shelf-walls nearly touched overhead. *Disputed Boundaries*, said the plaque. Fitting.

He talked as we ran, low and fast, the confession he owed me arriving in installments. "The Quorum recovers debts. Not money debts — oath debts. Broken promises, defaulted bargains, words given and unsaid. They collect the defaulters." A breath. "Eleven years ago I brokered an oath for a man named Corvess. Big oath. Land, title, a marriage settlement. And I — " His jaw worked. "I certified a witness who wasn't present. Standard practice, mostly harmless, except Corvess defaulted, and when the Quorum audited the oath, my false certification voided their legal claim to recover. Cost them a fortune. Cost *Corvess* nothing. The Quorum does not forgive accounting errors."

"So they want to file *you*."

"They want to file me under penalties." He risked a look back. "There's a bounty. Enough that half the informants in the city have my description memorized, and the other half are negotiating for it."

The child, trotting between us with their hood up, said, "What's a penalty?"

"It's when the story punishes you back," the child answered themselves, before Merric could. "Everyone knows that."

Lantern light swung into the lane behind us. Voices — three, maybe four, moving fast, coordinated. Merric swore with real fluency and shoved open a door that was not, strictly speaking, a door: a gap in the shelving where a section had been removed. Inside was a crawlspace between the shelf-backs, dark and paper-smelling, running parallel to the street.

"In," he whispered. "These run all through the old quarter. Archivists use them. Nobody else knows."

"How do *you* know?"

"I told you. Eleven years." His teeth showed briefly in the lantern-glow. "I wasn't always a disgrace. Once upon a time I was *promising*."

We went in. The space was barely shoulder-width, lit only by thin blades of light leaking between shelf boards. On either side of us, separated by an inch of wood, were the records of disputed boundaries going back ninety years. I could hear the papers breathing, almost — settling, shifting, the sound a building makes when it's thinking.

We had gone perhaps forty yards when the child stopped.

"Someone's coming," they whispered. "On the other side. Walking the same way we are. Reading."

I pressed my ear to the shelf-back. Footsteps indeed, unhurried, and the soft slide of a finger along shelf edges, and then — worst of all — a voice, mild and pleasant, speaking to no one:

"Vane. We know you're in the walls, Vane. The archivists' passages are registered property. Section Nine, clause four. You're trespassing now too. We're adding it to your file."

Merric had gone rigid beside me. In the blade of light I could see sweat on his jaw.

"Here's what puzzles me," the voice continued, conversational, drawing closer. "You could have gone east. Bridge, north road, gone. Instead you come here — to *my* quarter — and you bring company. A woman. And a—" The footsteps paused. The finger slid along the shelf, an inch from my ear. "A small one."

Every hair on my arms stood up. The child's hand found mine and gripped.

"Now, I'm a reasonable clerk," the pleasant voice said. "So I'm going to offer terms. Give us the woman's documents — whatever she's carrying, we know it came out of a temple, and the mountain pays retrieval fees — and we let you keep your legs, Vane. Both of them. That's generosity. That's *charity*. Write it down."

And I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this had never been about Merric's legs. They'd known about the pages. Perhaps they'd known since the tollhouse, since the valley, since the moment the mountain court had begun to seal and every bargain-hungry soul in the wintering lands started sniffing the wind for anything connected to it. Merric wasn't the prize. He was the trail.

The child looked up at me in the dark, and their eyes held none of the fear I expected. Only a kind of sad recognition, as if they'd known all along that this was where the road bent.

Behind us, far down the passage, another blade of lantern-light bloomed. Ahead, the pleasant voice kept talking, closer now. We were between two closing hands.

"Terms expire in ten counts, Vane," the voice said. "One. Two."

Merric's hand closed on my sleeve. His whisper was barely breath: "There's a hatch. Three rows down, drops into the paper cellar. I can hold them off — I'm what they came for—"

"—you are *not* what they came for," I hissed back, "and I am not leaving you, and I cannot believe I'm saying this—"

"Six. Seven."

"—because you're a liar and a coward and a fraud, Merric Vane, but you're the only map I have."

He stared at me. In the thin light his expression was so nakedly astonished that, later, in better hours, I would treasure it.

"Eight. Nine."

The child squeezed my hand once, and hummed — one low note, the note they hummed when oaths were near, the note that meant *this is true* — and the shelf boards on both sides of us shivered, and every paper in the walls rustled at once like ten thousand small wings.

The counting stopped.

"...What," said the pleasant voice, in a tone that suggested his file on this situation had just developed a page he hadn't authorized.

Merric grabbed my wrist. "*Now.*"

We ran — three rows, four, the papers shrieking softly around us, the lantern-light swinging wild behind — until Merric's boot found the hatch-ring and hauled it open, and we dropped together into darkness that smelled of ink and dust and old, patient debts, while somewhere above us the Quorum's clerk stood in a shaking corridor of files, trying to record what had just happened, and finding, I suspect, no proper heading for it.

Below, in the cellar dark, Merric was laughing. Actually laughing, breathless and disbelieving, and the child was laughing too, and after a moment — God help me — so was I.

Then the laughter died, because the cellar door at the far end opened, and lamplight spilled across floor-to-ceiling shelves, and standing silhouetted against it was a figure in a particular hat, holding a slate already ruled with lines, waiting to be filled.

"Well," said Merric, very quietly. "That's inconvenient."
