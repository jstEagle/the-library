## 12. The Long Winter Begins

The first snow came on a morning that should have been midsummer.

Yara knew this because she had been counting. Counting was the one habit the temple had given her that refused to die, even after everything else about the temple had curdled in her memory. Three hundred and eleven days owed, once. Now she counted other things: miles walked, coins remaining (four), pages of stolen contract still legible (all of them, barely, if she kept them dry), and days since Merric had joined them with his crooked smile and his croeker promises.

Nineteen days since the tollhouse. Twelve since the city. Two since Merric had confessed what he was.

And now it was snowing in the height of the growing season, fat flakes drifting down through green leaves as if the sky had lost its place in its own ledger.

"That's wrong," Yara said, to no one in particular, because saying things aloud to no one in particular had become another habit.

"Everything's wrong," Merric said cheerfully from where he sat mending a bootlace with a strip of bark. "We're fugitives carrying a soul-fragment across half a wintering country, pursued by a court of contractual entities, in the company of a disgraced oath-broker who tried to sell you. 'Wrong' stopped being useful vocabulary about a week ago."

"You know what I mean." Yara held out her hand. A flake landed on her palm and did not melt. That was the part that made her stomach drop — not the cold, not the season-breaking, but the flake sitting there on her skin like a small pale coin that had no intention of going anywhere. "Snow melts. Snow is supposed to melt."

The child looked up from where they sat cross-legged in the moss, hands folded in their lap in a way Yara had come to recognize as their version of attention. They had names for themselves, the child did — or rather they had *descriptions*, long winding ones in the old tongue, and Yara had taken to thinking of them simply as "the child," though privately, guiltily, she sometimes thought of them by the name written beside her grandmother's in the contract. The name that was not quite a name. The echo-name.

The child said something in the old tongue. It was short. It had the sound of a door closing.

"I don't understand you," Yara said, as gently as she could. She said it every day. Some mornings the child seemed to remember that words needed translating; some mornings the old language poured out of them like water finding its level, and no amount of gentleness could dam it.

But this time the child frowned, pressed their small hands flat against the ground, and tried again. Slower. Shaping each sound with visible effort, the way an old woman might shape a needle through failing eyes.

"*Winter... comes... early.*"

Three words. Yara felt them land somewhere beneath her ribs.

Merric stopped chewing his bark-strip. "Say that again?"

"*Winter comes early,*" the child repeated, and then added a fourth word, one Yara didn't know, but whose shape on the child's lips made her think of *seal*, and *shut*, and *for a very long time*.

* * *

They consulted the pages that afternoon, huddled under a leaning birch while the wrong-season snow thickened around them without accumulating — which was somehow worse, Yara thought. Snow that fell and refused to lie down was snow that was waiting for permission.

The contract pages were her grandmother's work, mostly. Yara had spent twelve days learning to read them the way you learn to read a person you loved but never truly met: by their hesitations, their flourishes, the places where the ink went thin because the pen was moving too fast to drink properly. Her grandmother had been a beautiful writer. That was the cruelest part. You could not read those lines and believe the hand that made them had been anything but certain.

"The court beneath the mountain keeps seasons," Merric said. He had gone uncharacteristically quiet since the child spoke, and his silence had a texture Yara was learning to dread. It meant he was calculating. "I told you I'd heard rumors, back when I brokered oaths out of the northern passes. The mountain doesn't live in time the way we do. Every so often — sixty years, eighty, nobody's ever fixed it — the court seals itself. Closes the gates. Settles its accounts. Nothing goes in, nothing comes out, until the whole generation that witnessed the sealing is dead."

"And then?"

"And then it opens, and everyone who owes anything finds out how much interest has accrued." He smiled, but it was the smile he wore when the news was worst, the smile that was really an apology wearing a costume. "If the mountain seals with your grandmother's soul inside—"

"It doesn't open for a generation," Yara finished. Her voice sounded very far away. "And the child..."

She looked at them. The child was watching the snow fall with the patient, unblinking attention they gave everything, and Yara saw it now — saw what she should have seen two days ago, maybe four, maybe longer, because grief and fear make terrible witnesses. The child had always been slightly translucent at the edges. In certain light, at dusk especially, you could almost see the birch trunks through the backs of their hands.

Today there was no certain light. Today there was only grey snow-light, and the child's edges were not almost anything. The moss showed through their fingers plainly, green through pale, like a watermark.

"When winter deepens," Yara said quietly, "they fade."

"They're made of what's left over," Merric said. Not cruelly. He had gotten better at that, these last two days, since confession. "Residue. Echo. Whatever word you like. Things like that hold together as long as the debt holds — as long as there's a contract pulling them taut, keeping their shape. But if the court seals..." He rubbed his jaw. "A sealed contract isn't a living thing anymore. It's a filed thing. And filed things don't need echoes walking around outside. The echo would just—" He made a gesture, opening his hand, letting something invisible go.

"How long?" Yara asked. "Until the sealing?"

"Honestly? If the snow's already falling, it's begun. The sealing isn't a day, it's a season — the mountain drawing everything in, all its debts coming home. We might have weeks."

The child said something long and fluid in the old tongue, and this time they did not try to translate it, and Yara did not ask them to, because some sentences you can hear the shape of well enough. It sounded like *hurry*. It sounded like *I know what I am and I have known all along.*

"Weeks," Yara repeated. "To reach the mountain."

"On foot, with four coins, no map, and me?" Merric spread his hands. "I've done worse odds. I once bet a man I could out-drink a bear. Well — I didn't bet him, exactly, he bet me, and there were extenuating circumstances involving a honey wagon—"

"Merric."

"But no," he admitted. "Not on foot. We need wheels, and we need them today."

* * *

Wheels, as it turned out, cost more than four coins.

The farmstead appeared near dusk — a low stone house with smoke rising straight and unhurried from its chimney, a byre, a cart leaning against the fence with one good wheel and one wheel that had opinions. The farmer was a broad woman named Hesk with forearms like loaves and a face that suggested she had heard every story travelers told and believed perhaps a tenth of any of them.

"A cart," she said. "You want my cart."

"Rent," Yara said. "Rent your cart. With the mule, if the mule's negotiable. We'll return it."

"Return it from where?"

"The mountain."

Hesk looked at the three of them — the scribe with ink-stained fingers and stolen documents wrapped in oilcloth, the oath-broker doing his best to look trustworthy and achieving instead the look of a man trying very hard to look trustworthy, and the small pale child whose hands were slightly transparent — and Yara watched her arrive, with the speed of practical people everywhere, at entirely the wrong conclusion and almost the right price.

"Refugees from the valley temples," Hesk guessed. "Running the conscription. My sister's boy went north for the same reason." She sighed the sigh of a woman doing arithmetic against her will. "Ten silver for cart and mule. You bring both back or I tell every tollhouse between here and the pass that you stole them."

"Five," said Merric.

"Twelve, and I keep your boots as surety."

"Why would you want his boots?" Yara asked.

"Good boots are good boots," said Hesk, with finality.

They settled at eight silver and the return of the boots, which left them three coins and a mule named Turnip who regarded Yara with the deep, ancient resentment of a creature who had clearly been named by someone else's children. Yara haggled the food separately — bread, hard cheese, a jar of pickled onions Hesk threw in free "because you'll need something to complain about" — and it was only as they were loading the cart that the farmer caught Yara's arm, low, away from the others.

"That child of yours," Hesk said. Not unkindly. "Been seeing a lot of them on the roads lately, this year. Pale little ones, walking alone or with minders who won't meet your eye. Temple children." She glanced at the mountains, where the wrong snow was falling thicker now, whitening the high passes though the valleys below were still green. "My grandmother used to say, when the mountain starts gathering early, the loose souls feel it first. Feel the pull. Like fish feeling the net close." She squeezed Yara's arm once, hard, and let go. "Drive fast."

Yara wanted to ask her a hundred questions — *how many children, where do they walk from, do any of them fade, has anyone ever brought one back* — but the child was already climbing into the cart bed with the grave courtesy they brought to everything, and Merric was already clicking his tongue at Turnip, and the light was going.

So instead Yara climbed up beside Merric, wrapped the contract pages in their third layer of oilcloth, and said, "Tell me the fastest road to the frozen gate."

"There are two roads," Merric said. "The fast one goes through the Vale of Assurances."

"And the slow one?"

"Also goes through the Vale of Assurances. It just takes longer and charges more." He flicked the reins. "Everyone who travels toward the mountain passes through the Vale eventually. It's the throat of the funnel. There's a gate at the far end of it — the Frozen Gate, they call it, though it's not ice, it's oaths, thousands of them laid over each other till they set solid — and past the gate, the mountain proper. The heralds sit at the gate. They weigh you before they let you through."

"Weigh us how?"

"However they like." Merrig's grin had no joy in it. "That's rather the point of heralds."

* * *

They drove through the evening and into a night that arrived hours too soon, the dark rolling up over the hills like spilled ink while the snow kept falling, soft and ceaseless and refusing to land. Yara sat in the cart bed beside the child, sharing a blanket, and taught herself not to flinch each time she looked down and found the blanket showing faintly through the child's knee, their elbow, the line of their shoulder.

"Do you hurt?" she asked finally. "When it happens? When you... thin?"

The child considered this with their whole body, head tilted, hands turning over in their lap as though the answer might be found in the palms.

"No," they managed, in the trade tongue. Their accent on it was strange, each word placed like a stone in a stream crossing — carefully, and only where footing was sure. Then, after a moment, they reached up and touched Yara's cheek with one fading finger, and said something in the old tongue, soft, and this time — whether because they willed it or because the language pitied her — she understood it without translation, the way you understand thunder.

*It is not pain. It is being remembered less.*

Yara had survived the temple by never crying where anyone could see, and she held to that now, mostly, blinking hard at the white dark ahead and pretending the wind was responsible for her eyes.

"Then we'll remember you harder," she said. "Both of us. Every hour. I'll write it down if I have to. I'm a scribe; remembering is the family business." She laughed, a wet, cracked little laugh. "Well. One of the family businesses."

The child smiled — the small, serious smile that never showed teeth, the smile of someone practicing — and settled against her side, and for a while there was only the creak of the cart and Turnip's offended breathing and the snow falling upward somehow, or sideways, or in directions snow had no business knowing about.

It was near midnight when the child sat bolt upright in the cart bed.

Yara felt it before she understood it: a change in the air pressure, a tightness behind the ears like descending into a cellar. Merric pulled the mule up. Ahead of them the road bent around a hillside, and beyond the hillside the whole western sky had gone strange — not dark, but *lit from below*, a cold gold glow along the horizon where no city stood, pulsing slowly, rhythmically, like something enormous breathing.

"What is that?" Yara whispered.

Merric stood up in the cart, holding the rail, and his face in the strange light was the color of old paper.

"That," he said, "is the mountain settling its accounts. Every debt it holds, lighting up as it gets called home. Souls, oaths, contracts, promises — everything it owns, glowing on its way back." He swallowed. "I've heard about this. Old brokers' tales. I always thought they were drunk."

The glow pulsed again, and with each pulse Yara felt a small tug somewhere behind her sternum — felt, absurdly, the stolen pages in their oilcloth grow warm against her chest, her grandmother's handwriting stirring like a sleeper turning over.

And the child stood up in the cart bed, small and pale and thinner than yesterday, and turned their face toward the light with an expression Yara had never seen on them before: recognition, and longing, and terror all braided into one.

They began, very softly, to sing — the old tongue rising out of them unbidden, a thread of melody that the night seemed to lean toward — and Yara realized with a horror that settled into her bones and made itself comfortable that the song was not the child's choice.

It was the pull.

The mountain was calling its property home, and the child was answering, note by note, whether they wished to or not.

"Drive," Yara said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; some deep temple-bred part of her had apparently decided that panic was a formatting error. "Merric. Drive *now*. How far to the gate?"

"At this pace?" He snapped the reins and Turnip, grumbling, leaned into the harness. "Four days. Maybe five if the snow closes the pass."

Behind them the glow pulsed. Beside her the child sang. And in Yara's breast pocket, warm as a living thing, her grandmother's contract waited to be delivered home.

"Then we have four days," Yara said to the dark, "to learn how to steal from a mountain."

The snow fell on, out of season, out of patience, counting down.
