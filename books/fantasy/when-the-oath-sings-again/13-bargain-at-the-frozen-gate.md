## 13. Bargain at the Frozen Gate

The mountain did not look like a place where souls were kept. It looked like a place where soup was kept — a great grey pot of stone with its lid half on, steam curling off the peak in long white ribbons that might have been cloud or might have been breath. Yara had expected menace. She had prepared herself for menace. She had spent three days of hard walking rehearsing her opening argument against menace.

What she got instead was a queue.

"There's a *queue*," she said, in the tone of a woman who has been personally insulted by geography.

Ahead of them, stretching down the frozen switchbacks from a gate of black iron set into the rock, stood perhaps forty figures: trappers with sledges, a merchant family wrapped in enough fur to constitute their own small nation, two pilgrims arguing in whispers about whether the court accepted apologies as currency. Snow fell out of season, fat and slow, settling on everyone equally, which Yara supposed was the only fairness the mountain offered.

"The sealing," Merric said. He had his hood up and his hands in his sleeves and the general air of a man trying to be furniture. "Everyone wants their business done before the gates close for a generation. Debts to collect. Debts to discharge. A few last-minute bargains." He nodded toward a woman near the front who was weeping quietly into a jar. "Don't look at the jar."

Yara looked at the jar. Something inside it looked back, or seemed to, in the way a firefly looks back when you catch it in a cup.

"I said don't look."

"I'm a scribe," Yara said. "Looking is the entire profession."

The child stood between them, holding one of Yara's hands and — this was new, this had started that morning — one of Merric's, as though the three of them were a chain that must not break across a river. The child had been quiet since the snow began. Not their usual quiet, which was the quiet of someone listening to a song no one else could hear. This was quieter. Their edges had gone soft, like ink left out in damp weather. Twice that morning Yara had glanced sideways and had to count fingers before her heart would start again.

"You're fading," Yara had said.

"You're *hurrying*," the child had answered, which was fair.

Now the child tugged both hands and pointed up at the gate. "It knows we're here," they said, in their voice that sounded like a bell rung underwater. "It's been knowing since the tollhouse. It's being polite about it."

"Wonderful," Merric muttered. "I'd rather be chased."

"Chased is faster," the child agreed.

* * *

The queue moved the way queues move everywhere in the world, which is to say with the deliberate pace of something that has never once been late and resents being asked. Yara used the time badly, as she used all waiting time badly: she took out the stolen contract pages, wrapped now in oilcloth against the wet, and read them again through the wrapping, as if the reading might change.

It didn't. Her grandmother's name sat there in faded iron-brown ink — *Maren Crane, of the valley, one soul, whole and undivided, held as surety* — and beneath it, in a different hand entirely, the notation that had made Yara sick for two days straight: *collateral alive.* Not dead. Not spent. Held. All these years, while Yara copied ledgers in a temple that forbade magic, while she counted her days owed on a slate above her bed, some piece of her grandmother had been sitting in a vault under the mountain, patient as paperwork.

And beside it — beside it, filed like a footnote — the child. *Residue thereof. Retained pending claim.*

"They're going to make us wait until we're polite too," Merric said. "That's what the queue is. It's not bureaucracy. It's training."

"Everything is bureaucracy somewhere," Yara said. "My temple used to make petitioners stand in the scriptorium for an hour before anyone would speak to them. Abbot Vessil called it 'the discipline of ink.' I called it cruel and unusual. To myself. Quietly. In my head, where all my best work lives."

Merric huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then his face went serious in the way it did maybe once a day, like a coin surfacing in a well. "Yara. Before we get up there. What are you going to offer?"

"An argument."

"They don't take arguments. I told you. I told you in the city, I told you on the road, I'll tell you again with feeling: the court beneath doesn't argue, it *weighs*. You want your grandmother's soul back, you bring them something of equal weight. That's the whole religion. That's the entire liturgy. Equal weight or nothing."

"I have scribe's training," Yara said. "I've spent six years finding errors in contracts written by people who thought themselves very clever. There's always an error."

"And if there isn't?"

Yara looked down at the child, whose eyes were fixed on the gate with the calm of someone returning to a house they'd lived in once and hadn't liked much.

"Then I'll find something else to put on the scale," she said.

Merric opened his mouth — she could see the objection forming behind his teeth like a bad oyster — and then closed it, because the line moved, and then it moved again, and then without any ceremony at all they were at the front, and the gate was above them, and the herald was looking down.

* * *

The herald stood in an alcove cut into the rock, framed by the black iron of the gate like a portrait deciding whether to hang itself. They wore robes the colour of wet slate, and their face was — Yara searched for the word and kept coming back to the same one — *filed*. Smooth as a finished page. No wrinkles, no scars, nothing written on it at all. When they spoke, their voice came out level and pleasant and completely uninflected, like a clerk reading terms aloud.

"Next party. State your business."

Yara stepped forward. Her boots crunched on snow that had stopped being snow somewhere behind them and become something finer, colder, more attentive.

"We're here to reclaim a soul," she said. "Held as surety under contract. Maren Crane, of the valley."

Somewhere in the alcove — nowhere visible, just somewhere, the way a sound comes from inside a wall — pages turned. The herald waited for them to finish turning.

"One moment," the herald said, and then, after the pause of someone consulting a document: "Maren Crane. Yes. Whole and undivided. Collateral alive. Claimant?" The smooth face tilted toward Yara. "State your relation."

"Granddaughter."

"Inheritance claim, then." More page-turning, invisible and unhurried. Yara realized her hands had gone cold inside her gloves and made them unclench. Scribe's posture. Spine straight, wrists loose, ink-stained fingers ready. You could face down almost anything if you stood like you were taking dictation.

"Unfortunately," the herald said, "the contract specifies reclaim conditions. Surety may be redeemed by the debtor's bloodline upon payment of an oath of equal weight." The face did not smile, but something in its arrangement suggested that smiling was available upon request, like a pillow in an inn. "You are, of course, welcome to dispute the terms. Disputes are heard by the full court. The full court convenes—" another consultation with the unseen pages "—in approximately four hundred years."

Behind her, Merric made a small sound like a man swallowing his own opinion.

"So," Yara said carefully. "Equal weight."

"Equal weight," the herald agreed pleasantly. "An oath freely given, binding upon the giver, of substance comparable to the item reclaimed. One soul, whole and undivided." The herald spread their filed hands, gracious as a host offering tea. "Standard practice. Nothing personal. The mountain does not keep grudges; grudges are unsecured assets."

There it was. Dread, dressed in formality, wearing its little clerical collar. Yara felt it settle over her shoulders like a wet cloak, and beneath it, absurdly, she felt something else — recognition. This was her abbot's voice. This was every ledger-keeper's voice, every temple clerk who had ever told a debt-bound girl *it's not cruelty, it's accounting.* The world, it turned out, ran on the same sentence everywhere. Only the mountains changed.

She had known this was coming. She had known since the tollhouse, since she'd paid a true promise to a tollkeeper with kind eyes and watched him flinch at the cost of honesty. She had known, and she had packed for the journey anyway, and now the packing was due.

But first — because she was who she was, because six years of finding errors had rotted her with hope — she tried the door marked ERROR.

"A question," she said. "For the record."

"For the record," the herald agreed, as though records were the only things they respected, which Yara suspected was exactly true.

"The contract names Maren Crane as debtor. But Maren Crane signed as *head of household*, acting to preserve her dependents. Under valley inheritance custom — which the contract itself cites, clause nine, governing succession of obligations — obligations pass to descendants only so long as the original benefit persists. My grandmother bought survival. We survived. The benefit has been fully consumed. An obligation whose consideration is exhausted isn't collateral anymore. It's a receipt. And you can't hold a person hostage to a receipt."

The herald was silent for a moment.

"That," the herald said at last, and here the voice changed — warmed, almost, by a degree, the way ice warms just before it becomes water and drowns you, "was a very good argument. Genuinely. Clause nine is frequently misquoted by claimants, and you quoted it correctly. I have noted it for the court's attention." A pause. "The court will consider it in approximately four hundred years."

"I thought so," Yara said, and was surprised to find herself smiling. Not because it had worked. Because she'd said it out loud, in front of the mountain, in front of everything, and the words had come out clean. Somewhere in the valley a temple had taught a debt-bound girl to read contracts, and the girl had walked four hundred miles to throw the lesson back at the institution, and even if it landed in a drawer for four centuries, it had been *said*. Some debts you pay in coin. Some you pay in testimony.

"Grandmother would have liked that," the child murmured.

"Grandmother would have told me clause nine has an exception buried in a footnote," Yara murmured back.

"It does," said the child, and Yara decided not to hear it, because there was a limit to what a heart could carry up a frozen switchback.

* * *

"Your answer, claimant," the herald said. "Payment of equal weight, or withdrawal. The gate seals at dusk. After sealing, no transactions until thaw." The filed face regarded her with what might have been patience and might have been appetite; on a blank page, Yara reflected, you could read whatever frightened you most.

She looked at Merric. He was shaking his head slowly, a man watching a cart roll toward a cliff and unable to remember where he'd left the brake.

She looked at the child. The child looked back, and their softness hurt worse than any demand the herald could have made. Fading at the edges. Ink in damp weather. A residue, walking, waiting to be claimed or destroyed, and the claiming was happening right now, right here, one way or the other — either she carried them out whole or the mountain filed them forever.

Her grandmother had sold a soul to buy survival. That was the family trade, apparently: paying with pieces of yourself and calling it love. Well. Yara Crane came from a long line of women who knew the price of things.

Fine. She would simply pay better than they had.

"My name," Yara said.

The word came out steadier than she deserved. The herald went still — truly still, the stillness of scales balancing.

"Explain your offer," the herald said.

"My name. Yara Crane. Freely given, binding upon me, whole and undivided — every record, every memory, every mention. Strike it from every ledger it appears in. Every person who holds it forgets it. Every document bearing it reads blank. I walk away nameless, and the name goes onto your shelf next to hers. One soul, whole and undivided, held as surety." She swallowed. "Collateral alive."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, deep in the mountain, Yara would have sworn she heard the vast dry rustle of an entire archive shifting in its sleep, the way a dog shifts when it hears its name.

"That is an unusual tender," the herald said. "Names are not commonly offered. Most people don't understand what they weigh." The filed face leaned fractionally closer. "Do you? Understand what it weighs?"

"No," Yara said honestly. "But I understand what hers weighs, and I understand what theirs weighs—" she touched the child's shoulder "—and I know arithmetic. Equal weight or nothing, you said. So weigh it. If it falls short, tell me, and I'll withdraw and think of something else. If it balances—" her voice caught, and she pushed through it the way you push through a door in winter "—if it balances, then take it, and open the gate."

The herald regarded her for a long moment. Snow settled on those smooth shoulders and did not melt, which told Yara something about local temperatures and nothing whatsoever about local hearts.

"Provisional acceptance," the herald said at last. "Subject to valuation by the court. The court may find the tender excessive, in which case change will be rendered. The court may find it insufficient, in which case—" the pleasantness thinned, just slightly, like ice over a drop "—supplemental payment will be required. Do you accept provisional terms?"

"I accept."

"Then extend your hand."

Yara extended it. The herald drew back one sleeve, and beneath it their arm was not flesh but something like compressed vellum, pale and faintly lined, and from the tip of one finger rose a point of light that was unmistakably, appallingly, a pen. Of course, Yara thought. Of course it's a pen. Everything is.

The pen touched the back of her hand.

It did not hurt. That was the worst part. It wrote in a fine brown-black line, looping characters she almost recognized — older than any kingdom, yes, but a cousin to the language the child had spoken at the temple door, the language that had appeared unbidden on her own page. The mark sank in as it dried, sinking past skin, past whatever lies beneath skin, settling somewhere in her like a signature settling into wet paper.

When the herald finished, Yara looked at the back of her hand and saw her own name there, written in the old tongue, beautiful and terrible, like a title page for a book about to be burned.

"Provisional," the herald repeated, capping nothing, sheathing nothing, the pen-light simply folding back into the finger. "Present yourselves at the inner gate. The court will receive you within the hour. Valuation follows audience." The filed face inclined, courteous as a banker. "Welcome to the mountain, claimant. Do try not to owe us anything further. Our shelves are nearly full, and the sealing waits for no one's sentiment."

They passed through the black iron gate, and the cold changed — became interior, architectural, the cold of a building rather than a sky. Behind them the queue murmured. Ahead, torches lit themselves at intervals down a descending corridor, each flame a small orange apology for the dark.

"Well," Merric said, after a while. His voice echoed wrong, flattened, as if the corridor ate echoes for breakfast. "You've gone and done it now."

"Yes."

"Sold your name."

"Provisionally sold my name. Let's be precise. Precision is free, and it's the only thing left I'm getting at retail."

He snorted despite himself. Then, quieter: "What happens if they find it insufficient?"

"Then I'll owe them the difference," Yara said, "and I'll be standing inside the mountain when I find out." She flexed her marked hand. The name on it seemed to flex back.

The child squeezed her fingers — gently, the way you handle a page you're afraid of tearing. "It won't be insufficient," they said softly. "Names weigh more than souls. Souls are what people have. Names are what people *are*." A pause, and then, with the terrible matter-of-factness the child reserved for true things: "The shelves down here are going to like you very much."

The corridor bent, and the torches bent with it, and far below — so far below that it might have been imagination, might have been the mountain humming to itself, might have been the sound of forty thousand filed souls turning over in their sleep — something began, very faintly, to sing.

It was singing in her grandmother's handwriting, Yara thought. And it was singing her name.
