## 14. Descent

*Yara*

The gate closed behind them with a sound like a ledger snapping shut.

Yara had expected cold. The mountain's throat should have been cold — she had dressed for it, wrapping herself in every layer the wintering land had left her, and she'd bundled the child in a coat two sizes too large that Merric had called "an investment" and then given away for free, which told her everything about how his investments usually went.

But the halls beneath the mountain were not cold. They were *warm*, the way a library is warm, the way a room full of sleeping people is warm — a warmth made of held breath and waiting.

And they were full of shelves.

That was the first thing. Before the light, before the silence, before the herald's marked hand on hers had stopped throbbing like a second pulse — shelves. They rose out of the dark in ranks, higher than the temple's bell tower, higher than anything built by human hands, and every shelf was packed with documents. Not books. Documents. Contracts, writs, bonds, receipts, each one glowing faintly at its edges like paper remembering fire.

"What are they?" the child whispered.

The child had been whispering since the gate. Down here, in the deep places where their language lived, the child seemed almost afraid to speak it aloud — as though the mountain might recognize its own voice coming back.

"They're records," Yara said, because she was a scribe, and naming a thing was the only comfort she had ever trusted. "Contracts. Debts." Her stomach turned over as she said it, because she already knew what else would be filed among them, and she made herself not look at the nearest shelf too long. Not yet. Not until she had to.

They walked. The aisle was wide enough for six abreast but felt narrow anyway, the way a cathedral aisle feels narrow, and the light came from nowhere in particular — from the documents themselves, perhaps, or from the sense of being *read*. Yara kept her hand on the child's shoulder and counted her steps, an old habit from the temple, where counting days owed was the closest thing to prayer anyone was permitted.

At step four hundred, the child stopped whispering and began humming.

At step seven hundred, the humming had become singing.

Yara stopped walking.

It was the same song the child had sung crossing the valley's edge, the language older than kingdoms, the binding-tongue — but down here it did not sound strange. That was the horror of it. Down here it sounded *correct*. Each note landed exactly where it belonged, and the shelves around them seemed to lean in, the glow along the document edges brightening in time, and Yara understood with a lurch that the archive was listening. That the archive *knew this song*.

"Sweetheart," she said gently, kneeling, keeping her voice level the way you keep your voice level near a startled animal or a lit fuse, "maybe we hum something else. Something from home."

The child looked at her with eyes that were not quite the child's eyes — older in there, deeper, lit from behind like windows of a house where someone was awake very late. "Home," the child repeated, tasting the word. "This is home. I know where everything is."

"I beg your pardon?"

"The third shelf." The child pointed without looking, arm rising sure as a compass needle. "The blue folder. It's mine. It has always been mine. And the tall shelf past the turning, the one that creaks — that's Grandmother's."

The word went through Yara like a nail through vellum.

She had known — of course she had known, she had crossed a wintering country on the strength of knowing — that her grandmother's soul sat somewhere in these halls, filed and waiting. Knowing it was one thing. Hearing a child say *Grandmother's* in the casual voice of someone describing which cupboard holds the cups was another entirely.

"We don't touch anything," Yara said firmly. "We find what we came for, and we don't touch anything, and above all we do not sing along. Do you understand? If the archive knows where everything is, then the archive knows where *we* are."

The child considered this with the grave patience of the very young and the very old, which the child somehow managed to be at once. Then, mercifully, took her hand.

They walked on. Behind them, faintly, the shelves dimmed — disappointed, Yara thought, and hated that she thought it.

* * *

*The child*

Down here the child could hear the folders breathing.

Not really breathing. But almost. Each document was a held moment, a promise mid-kept, and when you stood close and were quiet, you could feel the *waiting* in them, patient as snow. Upstairs, in the world of weather and bread and mothers who worried, the child had been thin. A residue. A walking remainder, as they had learned to say about themselves, in the dream-images, in the songs.

Here, the child was *full*.

It was like drinking after a long thirst, except the drink was everywhere, in the air, in the walls, and no one had ever told the child that thirst could end. Every step made them stronger. Every shelf sang a little when they passed, a greeting between colleagues, and the child had to press their lips shut to keep from answering, because Yara had asked, and Yara's hand was warm, and upstairs-warmth was rarer than soul-light and worth obeying.

But oh, it was hard.

The child knew where things were. That was the strangest part, stranger even than the singing. The child had never been here before, and yet the archive arranged itself in their mind like a kitchen they'd grown up in: debts to the left, oaths to the right, the collateral wing beyond the second turning, and past all of it — down, always down — the vaults, where the oldest purchases slept.

Somewhere down there was the person whose leftover the child was.

The child thought about this the way you think about a stone in your shoe: constantly, uncomfortably, without any way to stop walking.

"Are you all right?" Yara asked. She asked it roughly, the way she asked most kind things, as if kindness embarrassed her.

"I'm strong here," the child said honestly. "That's the problem."

"Why is that a problem?"

"Because I like it."

Yara stopped again. She did that a lot; the child had catalogued her stops the way other people catalogued stars. She knelt, and her face did the complicated thing it did when she was frightened and angry and gentle all at once, like weather arguing with itself.

"Listen to me," she said. "Liking a place isn't the same as belonging to it. I liked the temple once. I liked the quiet and the ink smell and the feeling that everything had its place. And do you know what the temple was for? Erasing children. Places will let you like them right up until they need something from you. That's what a bargain is." Her jaw set. "I'm something of an expert lately."

The child thought about that. "You're saying the mountain is nice so I'll stay."

"I'm saying nothing down here is nice by accident."

Which was fair, and true, and the child nodded solemnly and filed it beside the other true things Yara had given them — *count your steps*, *never sign what you haven't read*, *if a man named Merric offers you dried apricots, check them for worms first*.

But still. When they walked on, the child's hand brushed a shelf's edge, just barely, and three hundred folders glowed hello, and the child glowed back, and pretended very hard not to notice Yara noticing.

* * *

*Yara*

The trouble with descending through an infinite archive, Yara decided somewhere past the fourth turning, was that it gave you time to think, and thinking was the one activity her training had never taught her to survive.

She read the shelf labels as they passed, because reading was also a comfort, even when the reading was terrible.

**SOULS, PLEDGED — FAMINE YEARS, VOL. XII.**

**OATHS, BROKEN — SEE CROSS-REFERENCE.**

**CHILDREN, PROMISED (MISCELLANEOUS).**

She made herself look at that last one for exactly as long as it took to hate it properly, and then she looked away, and her eyes found the child instead — walking ahead now, unerringly, choosing turnings without hesitation, trailing one finger along the shelf edges so that the documents brightened in a wake behind them like a boat through phosphorescent water.

The child belonged here.

That was the sentence her mind kept returning to, turning it over like a coin she didn't want to spend. The child belonged here, was *stronger* here, moved through these halls with the ease of native blood — and every hour that passed, the child upstairs grew thinner. She had watched it happen on the winter road: the fading, the transparency at the fingertips, the way sound sometimes went *through* the child instead of off them. Here the process ran backward. Here the child filled in like ink soaking into good paper.

Which meant that every minute of this descent was healing them and harming them at once, depending on which direction you planned to walk afterward.

"You're doing the face," the child said, without turning around.

"What face?"

"The face where you're deciding something and you've decided it's going to cost you."

"I don't have a face for that."

"You have three," the child said. "This is the middle one."

Yara opened her mouth to object and found, to her considerable irritation, that she could not. Somewhere between the valley and the frozen gate, the child had learned to read her better than any abbot, any ledger, any grandmother. She filed the observation under grievances, in the increasingly crowded drawer reserved for things the child did that were either wonderful or unforgivable.

The stairwell appeared ahead — a spiral cut into living rock, leading down into a darkness that glowed anyway, because down here even the dark was documented. At its mouth stood a desk.

Of course it did. Everything down here was administration.

Behind the desk sat a figure that Yara's eyes refused to summarize: gray-robed, featureless in the way of a word you've read so many times it loses meaning, holding a pen that had never once, in all eternity, run dry.

"Halt," said the figure pleasantly. "State your business, your reference number, and whether you are carrying any undeclared hope."

Yara blinked. "Is that last one standard?"

"It's new," the figure admitted. "There was an incident. Some centuries ago. We don't discuss it."

"We're expected," Yara said, which was true, and showed her hand, where the herald's mark sat like a brand of black ink that had sunk below the skin. "Provisional passage. An oath of equal weight, paid at the gate. We're bound for the vaults."

The figure examined her hand. Examined the child. Made a small notation in a book that was, Yara noticed with professional horror, written in handwriting identical to her own.

"The scribe," the figure said. "Yes. You're noted." It smiled, or performed the shape of smiling. "You'll find the descent educational. Most debtors do." It waved its dry pen downward. "Mind the seventh landing. The contracts there are recent, and recent contracts bite."

"Bite," Yara repeated.

"Metaphorically," the figure said, in a tone that settled nothing whatsoever.

They descended. The stairs spiraled down through strata of the archive, and Yara, unable to help herself, read as she went — names, dates, sums — and somewhere around the fifth revolution she saw a family name she recognized from her own temple ledgers, a merchant house from the valley, debtors for nine generations, and she understood suddenly and completely why the temple sat in the valley where magic could not follow.

It wasn't sanctuary. It was *storage*. The temple kept the families whose paperwork lived down here, like a bank keeping its depositors' keys.

Her whole life. Her whole careful, counted, dutiful life. Filed.

"Seventh landing," the child warned, and pulled her to the far rail as something rustled past in the dark — papers, moving with intent, snapping at the air where her sleeve had been.

"Thank you," Yara said shakily.

"You owe me a story," the child said. "A real one. When we're out. With the funny voices."

"Deal," Yara said, and then winced, because down here you did not say *deal* lightly, and the child grinned, because down here the child knew exactly what she'd done and found it hilarious.

* * *

*The child*

Near the bottom, the child heard Grandmother.

Not the grandmother upstairs-in-the-world, the dead one whose handwriting lived on contract pages folded inside Yara's coat. Another one. The original. The purchased one. Down in the vaults, behind the last door, a presence like an old lamp burning low, steady, patient, half-forgotten by everyone except the shelf that held her.

The child stopped on the final stair.

"What is it?" Yara asked. Always the question. Always ready to fight whatever the answer was, even though Yara was small and tired and armed with nothing but literacy.

"She's awake," the child said. "Down there. She's been awake a long time."

Yara went very still. When she spoke, her voice had gone scribe-flat, which the child had learned meant that underneath the flatness was a flood. "Awake. For how long?"

"A long time," the child said gently. "Since the famine. Since the signing. She's been waiting in the dark, being kept, and she doesn't know why, and she doesn't know who bought her, but—" the child tilted their head, listening to the low lamp-song drifting up through the stone, "—she remembers loving somebody. Very much. Enough to be sold for it."

Yara put her hand against the wall. Just leaned there a moment, breath carefully even, eyes shut, a woman standing in a mountain holding a grief she'd carried across a whole country and was only now allowed to set down.

Then she straightened her coat. Checked the stolen pages inside it. Rolled her shoulders like someone approaching a difficult desk on a difficult morning.

"All right," she said. "Then let's go introduce ourselves. And then—" her jaw tightened, and the child saw the middle face arrive, the deciding-cost face, settling in for a long stay, "—and then we talk to the court."

The door at the bottom of the stairs was iron, and iron, down here, was just another kind of paper.

Yara knocked.

From the far side of the mountain — distant, unhurried, inevitable — came the sound of many pens beginning to write at once.

The court knew they had arrived. The court had known all along.

And somewhere in the vaults, an old lamp burned a little brighter, waiting to see which Crane had finally come to call.
