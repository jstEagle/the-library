## 15. The Court Beneath

The herald's mark on Yara's hand had stopped stinging somewhere around the third turning of the stair, which she supposed was a mercy. It had settled instead into a faint warmth, like a coin held too long in a fist, and every few steps it pulsed once — a reminder, she suspected, that her name was no longer entirely her own to spend.

Beside her, the child climbed without complaint. That was the strange part. For eleven days on the winter road the child had faded like breath off glass, thinning at the edges whenever the cold deepened, and now, descending into the mountain's warm dark, they grew *solid* with every step. Their color came back. Their shadow arrived before they did, stretching up the stairs ahead of them as though eager to arrive.

"You're getting heavier," Yara said, mostly to hear a voice in the dark.

"I'm getting *more*," the child said, and there was something in their voice Yara didn't like — not fear, not quite. Recognition. The tone of someone walking into a house they'd lived in long ago and finding the furniture exactly where they'd left it.

The stairs ended at a landing where the dark simply stopped, cut clean as a page torn from a book. Beyond it lay a hall so vast that Yara's scribe's eye — trained on ledgers, on columns of figures, on the patient arithmetic of debt — tried immediately and failed to count its dimensions. Shelves rose into darkness overhead. Not shelves of books. She'd seen enough books. These were shelves of *papers*, tens of thousands of them, millions perhaps, each document glowing faintly with its own pale light, so that the whole hall shimmered like a night sky that had been filed alphabetically.

Somewhere far above or far below — distance worked strangely here — a bell tolled once, and the sound was not bronze but *parchment*, dry and rustling.

"Stay close," Yara whispered. "And whatever happens, don't sign anything."

"I can't write," the child said.

"Good. Keep it that way."

They crossed the hall along an aisle between the shelves, and Yara made herself look at the documents as they passed. Contracts, all of them. She could read the shapes of them even at a glance — she'd spent nine years copying such things in a temple that existed to keep this place out of the world's memory. Sales of souls. Leases of luck. Mortgages on names. One shelf held nothing but apologies, each one notarized. Another held what looked like promises extracted from children, filed under a heading whose script hurt to read directly.

And there — she felt it before she saw it, the way you feel a stare — a glow warmer than the rest, three shelves down, wrapped in chains of fine copper script. Her grandmother's soul, if Merric's information held. Filed. Shelved. *Stored*, the way one stores grain against famine.

Yara's hand curled into a fist around the stolen pages in her satchel. Nine years of copying other people's debts, and here was her family's own, filed in its proper place, glowing patiently, waiting out the centuries the way only paper knows how to wait.

"They're expecting us," the child said quietly.

"How do you know?"

"Because nothing has tried to stop us." The child looked up at her, and for a moment their eyes were very old. "Stopping people is expensive. Waiting is free."

* * *

The court sat at the heart of the hall, and it was not what Yara had braced herself for. She had imagined thrones, monsters, something with teeth. Instead there was a long table of black stone, and behind it sat seven figures who were not figures at all — or rather, they were figures the way a signature is a person. Each magistrate was a shape of dense, moving script, letters flowing over letters, forming and reforming the suggestion of a body: a suggestion of shoulders, a suggestion of a hooded head, a suggestion of hands folded with terrible patience. Where a face should have been, there was a seal.

The centermost magistrate's seal was a circle. As Yara approached, it turned slowly, like a lock considering a key.

"Yara Crane," it said. Its voice was courteous, dry, and absolutely without malice, which was somehow worse than malice would have been. "Scribe, second class, temple of the silent valley. Debtor by inheritance. You are expected, though I will note for the record that your appointment was not scheduled until the spring."

"The spring seemed late," Yara said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Nine years of reading aloud in a silent temple gave a person that much. "I'm told the mountain seals soon. I'd hate to have my case adjourned for a generation."

"A generation is merely a unit of time," the magistrate said. "We are rich in time. It is the one commodity we never lend." A pause, and then, with the air of a host offering tea: "You are accused of theft. The documents in your possession are the property of this court. You are also accompanied by an item of disputed ownership, which you have removed from circulation. How do you plead?"

"Not guilty," Yara said, "on both counts. And I'd like the record to show I returned your invitation in person, which is more than most thieves manage."

Something rippled through the six flanking magistrates — amusement, or the legal equivalent of it, a shiver of clauses. The central seal rotated a degree.

"The record shows," it said, "that you are either brave or poorly advised. We will proceed. State your claim."

Yara set her satchel on the table. Her hands were shaking, and she let them shake; better the magistrates saw a frightened human than a confident opponent. She drew out the stolen contract pages — her grandmother's contract, the original, the thing she had bled for across a winter country — and laid them flat on the black stone.

"I claim this contract is void," she said. "Null. Unenforceable from the moment of its signing. And because it is void, everything done under it — the taking of the soul, the creation of—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—of the child beside me — was done without lawful basis. I claim the soul back. All of it. Including whatever portion walks around wearing shoes."

The hall went quiet in the way libraries go quiet — attentively. The child's small hand found hers and gripped it.

The central magistrate regarded the pages. Letters crawled across its form, reading faster than any eye could. When it spoke again, its courtesy had not diminished by a hair.

"The contract is genuine. The signature is authentic. The consideration was paid in full — grain, seed-stock, and passage, delivered during the famine year, receipted and witnessed. The soul named therein was surrendered voluntarily by the contracting party, who was of sound mind and legal age. This court finds the instrument valid." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Bought fairly, Scribe Crane. We regret your distress, but grief is not a ground of appeal."

"Bought fairly," Yara repeated. "From whom?"

"From Maren Crane, your grandmother. The signature—"

"Is authentic. I know. I've studied it since I was six years old. She taught me my letters with that same hand." Yara leaned forward. "Now study something *I* know. You're beings of pure contract. So tell me — when you validate an instrument, do you validate the parties, or only the ink?"

"All parties," the magistrate said. "Capacity is fundamental."

"Then validate this capacity." Yara pulled a second page from her satchel — a copy, careful, exact, made by lamplight in a waystation while the child slept. She had spent nine years learning to reproduce hands; tonight the skill finally paid its wage. "Maren Crane died on the third day of Thaw-month, in the famine's second winter. I have the parish record. I have the grave. I have her granddaughter's memory of standing at it. And this contract—" she tapped the original, "—is dated the eleventh day of the same month. Eight days *after* her death."

The silence that followed had texture. The flanking magistrates' scripts churned, letters colliding, reforming. Somewhere in the high shelves, a document rustled as if turning over in its sleep.

"The court," the central magistrate said slowly, "will verify the parish record."

"Please do," said Yara. "Take your time. Though not too much — I understand the mountain seals soon."

She had meant it as a small jab. What happened next she had not anticipated: the magistrates *did* take their time. Minutes passed. The child tugged her sleeve and whispered, "What's happening?"

"I think," Yara whispered back, "I've just asked a room full of living contracts to admit one of their own kind is dead. Give them a moment. It's like asking a priest whether God files taxes."

The central seal resumed its rotation. When the magistrate spoke again, its voice was still courteous, still dry — but Yara, who had spent her life listening for what documents did not say, heard the new thing underneath it.

"The parish record is confirmed. Maren Crane predeceased the instrument by eight days." A pause. "However. The court notes that the signature on the contract is nonetheless authentic — verified against forty-seven exemplars of the party's hand. A dead woman does not sign. Therefore the signature was executed by an agent acting with apparent authority. The court finds the contract validly executed by proxy."

"Proxy," Yara said flatly.

"Apparent authority is sufficient. The court regrets the surprise. Death, Scribe Crane, transfers agency; it does not extinguish obligation. Whoever signed in her name had standing to bind her estate. And her estate—" the seal turned, almost gently, "—included the soul in question. The debt passed to her heirs. To you."

"There was no proxy. There was no agent. My grandmother had no one—"

"She had a granddaughter," the magistrate said. "Who is here. Who has claimed the contract. Who has, by presenting it, by arguing it, by *standing in this court and speaking for the dead*—" the letters of its body aligned themselves, suddenly, terribly neat, "—assumed the office of her representative. The court thanks you for ratifying the arrangement."

The floor did not move, but Yara felt it tilt anyway. Eleven days of frozen road, a tollhouse, a betrayal, a friend in a cell below — all of it, and she had walked into the trap with her own argument and locked it behind herself.

"That's circular," she said. "That's the most circular reasoning I've ever heard, and I've copied tax law."

"It is not circular," the magistrate said, with something that in a human face would have been a smile. "It is *binding*. You are welcome to appeal. The appellate session convenes in one hundred years."

The child's grip tightened on her hand. And Yara stood there in the vast humming hall, in front of seven creatures made entirely of fine print, and understood with perfect clarity that she could not out-lawyer them — not because they were cleverer than she was, but because the law *was* them. Every loophole she found, they had built. Every escape was a clause.

But.

But she had been a scribe for nine years, and there was one thing every scribe learns before anything else, the first lesson, the one written on the inside of your skull: *read the whole page*. Not the argument. Not the clause. The whole page.

Her eyes went down to the contract lying on the table. Her grandmother's signature — authentic, verified, eight days dead. And beneath it, in the margin, small as a breath, the notation every temple scribe in the valley was required to add to every instrument they witnessed. She had written it ten thousand times herself. She had never once thought about what it meant.

*Witnessed true and whole.*

True and *whole*. A witness certifies not the signature but the *signing* — the living act of it, the person present, the mind willing. And the witnessing hand in the margin, she saw now, the small cramped script she had assumed was some court clerk's—

Was her grandmother's.

Maren Crane had witnessed her own death-date contract. Which meant Maren Crane had been *present* at the signing. Which meant—

The warmth of the herald's mark pulsed on Yara's hand, once, like a heartbeat. Like a countdown.

"Magistrate," Yara said slowly, "one question of procedure. If a contract requires a living witness, and the witness attests to a signing by a person already dead — and the witness's own hand proves she stood there, alive, watching someone wear her name — then who, precisely, signed?"

The central seal stopped turning.

For the first time, the magistrate's script broke formation. Just a flicker, just a ripple, letters scattering like startled birds — but in a being made of language, a broken sentence is a scream.

"That," it said, "is a question the court will need to consider."

"Consider it quickly," Yara said. "The mountain seals soon. And I am *very* thorough."

Above them, deep in the shelved dark, something began — softly, patiently, the way paper always does — to burn.
