## 16. The Loophole and the Trap

I had not slept. I want that recorded somewhere, in a hand steadier than mine, because what happened next was built on the wreckage of a sleepless night spent on a bench of black stone with the child curled against my side like a warm loaf of bread and my grandmother's contract open across my knees.

The vault-antechamber where the court lodged us — *lodged* being a generous word for a room with no windows, no doors I could find, and a ceiling that breathed very slowly, like something enormous deciding whether to be polite — had provided us with bread. The bread was excellent. That was somehow worse. Everything beneath the mountain was excellent. The corridors were swept. The light came from nowhere and flattered everyone. It was the hospitality of an institution that had never once been told no, and I intended to be the first.

The child slept with their thumb hooked in the collar of my coat, the way they'd done since the valley, since before either of us knew why. Every few breaths they murmured something in the old language, soft as gravel shifting in a streambed, and each murmur made the ink on the pages beside me shiver faintly, like a dog hearing its name.

I read the contract again. Then again. Scribes are not brave people, generally. We are people who have learned that everything, eventually, is written down, and that whoever reads most carefully wins. My grandmother had been a scribe too. Somewhere in the famine winter she had sat at a table like this one, or a worse one, with her children starving behind her, and she had signed away a soul to buy their survival. She had been careful. She had been desperate. Desperate people who are also careful make one mistake apiece, if they're lucky, and I had spent the whole night hunting hers.

I found it an hour before the herald came for us, and when I found it I did not shout, because shouting in this place felt like ringing a bell inside a creditor's house. I put my finger on it instead, very gently, the way you'd touch a bird you didn't want to startle.

*Article the Third.* The debtor named in the instrument of sale: **Maren Crane, of the Valley Holdings, widow.**

Maren Crane had died three years before that contract was signed.

I knew it the way you know your own hands. Grandmother Maren was the reason I could read at all; she'd taught me letters on a slate with a stub of chalk, laughing at my terrible G's. She died in the autumn of the bad fever year, three years before the famine winter, three years before anyone in our family needed saving from anything except grief. I had carried her coffin corner. I had been nine. I remembered the weight of it more clearly than I remembered my own name some mornings.

A contract naming a dead woman as its debtor was void. Not weakened — void. Every text I had ever copied in the silent temple said so, usually twice, usually in red ink, because the temple's whole purpose was the maintenance of boundaries between the living and the things that trade with them, and a dead debtor was a hole in the wall.

Of course, the court beneath the mountain had probably never read the temple's texts. But the mountain had its own laws — I'd seen the shelves of them on the way down, miles of them, souls filed like correspondence — and every legal tradition I knew of shared one root: a bargain requires two living parties. You cannot buy from the dead. You can only rob them, and robbery, whatever else it was, was not *contract*, and contract was all these magistrates were.

"Yara." The child's voice, small and sleep-roughened. "You're smiling like Merric does when he's about to do something stupid."

"I am smiling like someone about to do something *legal*," I said. "It looks similar. It feels much better."

They considered this. "Is it going to work?"

"It's going to work beautifully," I said, which was true, right up until it wasn't.

* * *

The Court Beneath convened in a hall shaped like the inside of a ledger — long, ruled, every surface lined with faint horizontal grooves as though the mountain itself had once been written upon and the writing had sunk in. The magistrates stood in their alcoves: tall figures of grey glass and older shadow, faces like blank seals waiting for wax. The First Magistrate occupied the center, and when it spoke, its voice was the sound a quill makes on good paper, amplified into something almost kind.

"The claimant returns," it said. "The court commends her diligence."

"You'll commend me more in a moment," I said, and my voice only shook a little, and I decided to count that as a victory too.

I laid the contract on the reading-table. Beside it I set the temple's certified copy — stolen, yes, but certified, stamped with the abbot's own seal, which I had also stolen, along with his ink, because if you're going to commit theft you may as well be thorough — and I set out my grandmother's death-record, obtained from a parish archive in the City of Counted Days during those frantic hours after Merric was taken, hours I still hadn't thanked him properly for surviving.

"Article the Third names Maren Crane as debtor," I said. "Maren Crane died on the ninth of Harvest-Month, in the year of the fever. This record is countersigned by her parish and witnessed by four persons, one of whom was holding a corner of her coffin, and that person was me." I let that sit. Glass faces gave nothing back, but the hall itself seemed to lean in, the grooves in the walls deepening slightly, listening. "A contract executed three years after the death of its named debtor is void. There is no debt. There is no collateral. There is no soul held, because there was never a valid sale. The soul you hold was taken under color of a dead paper, and the child — who is that soul's echo, its residue, its walking remainder — goes free, and the soul goes free, and I go home, and we will all speak warmly of each other's professionalism."

I had rehearsed that last sentence in front of a wall. It landed better than expected.

For three full heartbeats, the court said nothing, and I allowed myself the luxury of believing that heartbeats were currency here too, and that I had just paid myself rich.

Then the First Magistrate inclined its head — a slow, courteous, terrible motion, like a door deciding to open — and said:

"The court thanks the claimant for her scholarship. The court has reviewed Article the Third. The court concurs."

My stomach dropped so fast I felt it in my teeth. *Concurs?*

"The instrument of sale is void," the magistrate continued, in that patient pen-scratch voice. "The debt it describes does not exist. The court therefore cannot hold the soul Crane against that debt." A pause, delicate as a knife being set down on silk. "The court will instead hold it against the debt that *does* exist."

Somewhere behind me, Merric made a sound like a man swallowing his own hat.

Because of course. Of course. I had spent the night proving there was no debt from my grandmother — and I had proven it, cleanly, irrefutably, in front of the most literal institution in creation — and in doing so I had left the soul sitting in their vault with no lawful reason attached to it whatsoever. And this court did not release unclaimed property. This court *filed* it. And filing required a basis, and any basis would do, and they had had a whole night too, hadn't they, and they'd read my face when I walked in carrying Merric's name like a stone in my pocket.

"The court produces," said the First Magistrate, and the walls opened a seam, and a shelf slid out, and on the shelf lay a single page, fresh as morning, "the instrument of hire."

I knew it before I saw the signature. I knew it from the shape of the silence Merric made behind me, which was the silence of a man watching his own handwriting come back around the mountain to bite him.

*Merric Vael, oath-broker, licensed and thereafter disbarred,* the page read, in his slanted, show-off hand. *Contracted by the Office of Retrieval, Court Beneath, to recover one (1) soul-echo, description enclosed, and deliver same to the Frozen Gate. Consideration: full restoration of license, discharge of outstanding judgments, and passage-home.*

Dated the day before he'd met us on the winter road.

"He never delivered," I said quickly. "He refused. He chose—"

"The contract," said the magistrate, with the gentleness of a landslide, "contains a performance clause. Article the Second. In the event of non-delivery, the contractor becomes liable for the value of the goods, such value to be assessed as: one soul, whole, plus costs." The page turned itself. "The contractor signed Article the Second. The contractor initialed it. Here, and here, and — the court notes with some appreciation — here, in the margin, next to a small drawing of a hat."

There was a small drawing of a hat in the margin. Merric had drawn a hat in the margin of a soul-contract. He drew hats when he was nervous; I'd learned that on the road, watching him doodle brims in the frost on wagon-boards while insisting he was fine.

"Merric," I said, turning, "tell them. Tell them you tore up your copy. Tell them you never meant—"

He was already talking, fast and low and honest, which for Merric was a brand-new dialect and he spoke it badly. "I did refuse. Ask the roads, ask the tollhouse, ask her—" he pointed at me with both hands, as though one weren't enough to indicate the scale of his sincerity. "I sat down across from the pair of you planning to rob you and I got up and didn't. That should count. That has to count. There's a principle — there's always a principle —"

"There is a principle," agreed the First Magistrate, and the hall's grooves seemed to sharpen. "It is called consideration. The court advanced you passage, protection, and the promise of your name cleared. You accepted. What you did afterward with your conscience is between you and your conscience, and the court observes that your conscience has arrived too late to be entered into evidence."

"That's not — you can't just —" Merric's voice cracked upward into the register he used when cheating at cards. "I initialed the margin! Nobody reads the margin!"

"The court reads the margin," said the magistrate. "The court *is* the margin."

And there it was. The trap, sprung so smoothly I hadn't even heard it close. I had come down the mountain armed with my grandmother's ghost and my best handwriting, and I had won — genuinely won, fairly won, won on the merits — and my victory had simply handed them a vacancy, and they had filled it with the nearest available soul-shaped person, who happened to be the disgraced oath-broker who had drawn a little hat next to his own damnation.

The moral weight of the world shifted, quietly, the way ballast shifts in a boat, and suddenly we were listing toward Merric.

* * *

They let us confer. Courts do that; even courts of pure contract understand theater, and theater requires intermissions.

We stood in the antechamber, the three of us and the child, who had insisted on attending the hearing and had spent it sitting perfectly still on the floor drawing spirals in the dust with one finger, spirals that glowed faintly and faded, the old language writing itself out of boredom.

"They can't take him," I said. "He helped us. He chose us over them."

"He also signed the thing," Merric said. He was pacing, hatless, running his hands through his hair in a way that suggested the hair was considering leaving too. "Yara. I signed it. I read half of it, which is more than I usually manage, and I initialed the other half, which is less." He stopped pacing. "Do you know what service to this court is? I saw the shelves. Miles of them. Souls filed alphabetically, Yara. Alphabetically. I'd be filed between a farmer who traded his harvest-luck and somebody's great-aunt. For a generation. Maybe longer. They seal the mountain tonight."

"So we argue the refusal," I said. "Impossibility. Duress. You were disbarred, coerced, your judgment was—"

"My judgment was fine," he said flatly. "That's the worst part. I knew exactly what I was doing when I signed. I thought I'd double-cross them and they'd never catch up. I thought contracts were for other people." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "Turns out I'm other people."

The child tugged my sleeve. Their eyes had gone wide and dark, the way they did near the ledgers, near anything with a name in it.

"He's scared," they said, quietly, in a voice pitched for me alone. "But Yara — listen. Listen to what he isn't saying."

I listened. Merric had gone to the far wall and was standing very straight, arms folded, looking at the sealed door like a man memorizing it. And what he wasn't saying, what was pouring off him like heat off a stove, was simpler than any argument: *I'm tired, and I've been running for eleven years, and this is the first time running has caught up with me, and maybe it's fair.*

"We have one hour," I said. "Before the sealing. Before they file him. One hour of argument left, and then the gate closes for twenty years, and whatever's inside stays inside."

"Then spend it well," Merric said. "Spend it on the girl and the old woman's soul. Don't spend it on me."

"Don't tell me how to spend my hour."

"Somebody has to. You're a scribe; you'll try to write your way out of it, and your handwriting's better than your odds." He grinned, and it was the road-grin, the one that meant *trust me exactly forty percent*. "Look — one thing. If it comes to the worst. If they're going to file me regardless. Get me my contract back. The original. Just — get it into my hands, and look away, and don't ask what I do with it."

"Why?"

"Because," he said, "there's one law even this court can't break without breaking itself, and I've been thinking about it since the day I signed, and I'll be damned — possibly literally — if I explain it to a room full of magistrates before I have to."

The herald returned, punctual as a signature. Behind it, the mountain had begun, very softly, to hum — the sound of a door the size of a season preparing to shut.

"One hour," said the First Magistrate, courteous to the end. "The court awaits the claimant's final argument. Or the contractor's compliance. Whichever arrives first."

And the child took my hand, and their grip was cold, and the humming grew.
