## 17. Merric's Choice

They took me to a small room with no door.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only dignity a man in my position has left. Not "a room whose door had closed." Not "a room I could not find the exit of." A room with no *door*. The walls were smooth grey stone that went up further than my neck would tip and down further than my boots were willing to report, and there was no seam, no hinge, no handle, and — I checked twice, because thoroughness has been the ruin of better men than me — no keyhole. Somewhere beneath the mountain of the court beneath the mountain, someone had built a holding cell for one disgraced oath-broker, and they had done it the way you'd mend a sock: completely, and without any intention of ever opening it again.

There was a chair. That was the courtesy of it. A plain wooden chair, worn at the arms by hands that had presumably waited here before me, though I tried not to think about whose. And on the chair's seat, laid out flat and neat as a funeral shirt, was my contract.

It was longer than I remembered signing. That's the trick of these things — you sign at the bottom of page one, and the document keeps going after your ink dries. Clauses like roots. Conditions like weather. Somewhere past the part about retrieval of the asset and delivery to the gate, the paper just became a list of services rendered in perpetuity, each one smaller and duller than the last, until the final line, which read simply: *and thereafter, whatever the court requires, forever.*

No end date. No sunset clause. I'd looked for one when I signed — of course I had, I was an oath-broker, reading contracts was the one thing I had ever been genuinely good at — but the court writes the way winter writes: slowly, everywhere, and over everything.

I picked it up. The paper was warm. That bothered me more than anything else in the room, more than the missing door, more than the humming that came up through the floor like the mountain clearing its throat. Paper should be cool. Paper should be indifferent. This paper was warm the way a hand is warm, and it wanted very much to be held.

"Right," I said to the empty room, because talking aloud was either courage or madness and at this point the two had stopped being distinguishable. "Let's have a look at you, then."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about selling your freedom: it doesn't hurt. That's the scandal of it. When I signed, in a rented room above a chandler's shop in the City of Counted Days, with the court's advocate sitting across from me wearing a face like a politely folded letter, I had braced myself for *something* — a coldness, a tearing, a sound like a lock turning somewhere inside me. Nothing. The pen moved. The ink dried. I stood up feeling exactly as cheap and tired as before, and the only difference was that now it was official.

Freedom, it turns out, is not something you feel leaving. It's something you notice missing later, in small ways, at odd hours. Like: you reach for a plan and find someone else's handwriting already there. Like: you wake in the morning and the first thought in your head isn't yours, it's *comply*, dressed up in your own voice so you won't notice.

I had noticed. Eventually. Late — I'll grant the world its laugh — but I had noticed. On a road, in winter, watching a scribe with ink-stained fingers argue law with a herald while a child held her hand like an anchor holds a boat. That was when I understood what I'd actually sold, and what I'd been hired to drag back into the dark, and the understanding sat in my chest like a swallowed stone from that day forward.

So. The contract. Warm paper, endless clauses. I sat on the chair — my chair, apparently, my little kingdom of one — and I did the thing I was best at in all the world.

I read it.

* * *

You can learn a great deal about a court from how it writes. Most courts write to protect themselves — loopholes patched, ambiguities crushed, every sentence armored against clever men like me. But this court wrote to preserve itself above all things, and there is a difference, and the difference is a crack wide enough to drive a soul through.

Because here is what I found, on my fourth careful pass through the warm paper, in a clause tucked between *the contractor shall bear all costs of conveyance* and *failure to deliver constitutes breach*: a clause about the contract itself. About the instrument. The court's own laws — the ones Yara had been fencing with upstairs, the ones that made magistrates go still and courteous when she quoted them — required that every contract remain *whole, legible, and binding*, and that no power, mortal or otherwise, might alter or destroy a contract once executed, save by mutual consent of the parties thereto.

Mutual consent. Both parties. That's the lock.

And a lock, my friends, is only as strong as the assumption that both sides want it locked.

I set the contract on my knees and looked at it for a long time. Down the corridor that wasn't a corridor, I could hear the mountain humming. Somewhere above or beyond, a woman who owed me nothing was running out of hour, running out of argument, running out of everything except stubbornness and love, which — I've watched enough bargains collapse to know — are the two currencies that never hold their value until suddenly, catastrophically, they do.

And I thought about the child.

I'd been hired to retrieve them. That was the whole job: find the echo, bring it home, walk out free. Simple terms. Clean terms. The kind of terms I used to write for other people, back when I told myself that moving souls around on paper wasn't the same as moving them at all. And I had found the child, hadn't I? Traveled beside them. Watched them fall asleep against Yara's shoulder in the tollhouse, mouth open, utterly trusting, humming a song in a dead tongue that sounded — and I will carry this to whatever grave gets me — *happy*.

The first time I almost didn't complete the job, I told myself it was tactics. Bad roads, bad odds, wait for a better moment. The second time, I told myself it was sentiment, and sentiment is a disease of brokers, and I'd sweat it out by morning. But there was no third time, because somewhere on the long winter road I stopped telling myself anything, and just walked next to them, day after day, a man carrying a stone he refused to put down.

Now the stone spoke. Now the choice arrived, as choices do, wearing work clothes.

The clause said: *no power may destroy a contract save by mutual consent of the parties.*

The court would never consent. Obviously. The court was a machine made entirely of consent, harvested and refined. But the clause didn't say the destruction had to be *agreed*. It said *mutual*. And there is a difference, and the difference is this: a thing done by one party upon the shared instrument, witnessed by the instrument itself—

Oh, it was ugly. Let me be clear, it was lawyer-ugly, the kind of reasoning that makes honest judges rub their eyes. But the court had written the clause to protect its documents from outside hands, and it had never once imagined — why would it? — that a party would turn on his own contract like a dog on its own tail. The court could not undo what I was about to do without violating its own first law, the law that said contracts are sacred, the law that was the court's spine. Tear the spine, and the whole courteous body comes down.

One act. Unrepeatable. Undone by nothing.

Me.

* * *

I want to tell you I hesitated. Some part of me wants that on the record — that I sat with the weight of it, weighed eternity against an hour, counted the cost the way I'd counted costs my whole crooked life.

I didn't hesitate. That's the truth, and I'll take it, because I haven't taken much else honestly.

What I did do was laugh. Once, short, surprised out of me like a hiccup. Because I finally understood the shape of the bargain, and it was the first clean one of my life: I got to buy something with the only coin I had left, and the something was one hour of chaos in a courtroom full of creatures who had never once, in all their recorded ages, been inconvenienced.

"One hour," I said to the warm paper. "Make it a good one."

Then I gripped the contract by its top corners — it pulsed against my fingers, alarmed, the way a bird goes still in a fist — and I thought, absurdly, of my father, who taught me figures and taught me nothing else, and of the chandler's rented room where I'd signed my life away for the crime of having lost everything else, and of Yara Crane saying *why* in a voice that expected an answer.

And I tore it.

Not neatly. Neatness would have left it *legible*, and the clause demanded wholeness, and I meant to insult the clause in its own house. I tore it lengthwise first, slow, feeling the resistance — oh, there was resistance, the paper fought like a living thing, and a sound came out of it, low and rising, a note like a struck bell with a grievance — and then crosswise, and then again, and again, shredding it into pieces too small to argue, too small to *be* anything, until the fragments fell from my hands like grey snow and lay dying on the stone floor.

The hum stopped.

All through the mountain — through halls of filed souls, through the frozen gate, through the courteous courtroom where a magistrate was mid-sentence — the hum stopped, and in the ringing silence that followed, every bell in the deep places began to toll at once, out of order, out of rhythm, out of its mind.

The walls of my doorless room shuddered. Where the fragments of the contract lay, light was leaking out of them — thin gold lines, unspooling, unraveling clauses into loose thread, thread into nothing. And in the middle of the nothing, a crack opened in the smooth grey stone, tall as a man, breathing cold air and torchlight and the distant sound of a woman's voice raised in argument.

The court's own law, tearing itself a hole to keep from being broken.

I stepped through it. Behind me, my cell forgot itself; ahead, chaos spread through the mountain like spilled ink through wet paper, and I ran toward the shouting with empty hands and a full chest, grinning like a fool, free for exactly as long as it took the universe to notice what I'd done.

Which, knowing this court, wouldn't be long.

But an hour is an hour. And I intended to spend every minute of it.
