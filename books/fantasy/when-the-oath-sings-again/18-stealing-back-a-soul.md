## 18. Stealing Back a Soul

The hour began the way most terrible hours begin: with someone shouting a name that wasn't mine.

"CRANE!" Merric's voice tore up through the mountain's halls of records, ragged and defiant and — I would learn later — already half regretted. Somewhere above us, paper screamed. The court's magistrates had felt their laws tear, and their grief was not a quiet thing.

I did not stop to listen. Stopping was how people died in stories, and I had read enough stories to know the authors were right about very little else.

*Run,* I told myself. *You have one hour. You spent your whole life learning to count days owed; you can surely count sixty minutes.*

The vault door stood at the end of a corridor lined with shelves, and the shelves were the worst part. Not gold. Not jewels. *Ledgers.* Thousands upon thousands of them, bound in leather gone soft with handling, spines labeled in a script older than any kingdom I could name. Each book was warm to the air around it, the way a sleeping animal is warm, and as I ran past them I heard — faintly, horribly — breathing.

Souls filed like documents. Grandmother, you absolute terror. What have you made me walk into?

The child kept pace beside me, small hand tucked in mine, and here was the thing I could not stop noticing even now, even running for my life and my family's dead: the child was *glowing*. Down here beneath the mountain, where every borrowed breath found its ledger, the child grew brighter with each corridor, each shelf, each whispered label. At home. Horrifyingly, unbearably at home.

"You feel it too," the child said. That strange old language had softened over the winter road into something I understood without translation, the way you understand rain. "Don't you? Like coming back to a house you dreamed of."

"I feel like I'm inside an abacus," I said, because if I said anything true I would scream. "Keep moving."

The vault door was bronze, and it was open.

Of course it was open. Merric had torn his contract to shreds upstairs, and somewhere in the court's perfect machinery, a gear had slipped loose, and doors that had been sealed for centuries were standing ajar like mouths mid-sentence. I pulled the child through anyway, heart hammering, and the door swung shut behind us with the soft, final click of a page being turned.

Inside: shelves again. But these were different. These were *old.*

The vault of the court beneath the mountain was not large, which surprised me until I understood it. They did not need room. A soul, properly filed, takes up exactly as much space as it is allowed to believe it does. Most of the souls on those inner shelves were folded so small they looked like nothing — gray wisps pressed between pages, curled tight as winter moths, each one whispering its own name over and over in a voice worn down to thread.

I walked the aisles. The child walked with me, and every soul we passed lifted its whisper as we went, the way wheat lifts toward sun.

*Crane,* I wanted to say to them. *My name is Yara Crane, and I've come to make a withdrawal.* But my name was already promised away, marked on my hand by the herald's cold stylus, provisionally accepted, and saying it aloud felt like spending coins I didn't own.

So instead I read labels.

Row after row. Century after century. Souls sold for harvests saved, for plagues turned aside, for love badly handled and wars briefly won. Some entries made me laugh — a bitter, echoing laugh that I immediately regretted, because the shelves laughed back. One soul traded for *a very good horse*. One for *the ending of a feud that had claimed eleven lives and two orchards*. One, gods help me, for *a really excellent pie*, and I swear on every ledger I ever copied, that entry had a marginal note in some magistrate's hand reading *worth it.*

Even down here, in the deep cold of the mountain's heart, someone had a sense of humor. It was either comforting or damning. I hadn't decided which.

Then I found the Cranes.

Not a shelf. A *drawer.* Small, wooden, unremarkable, tucked at knee height in the darkest corner of the oldest aisle, as if whoever filed us had hoped we'd be forgotten. The label read, in careful clerk-hand:

**CRANE, family line. 3 entries. See cross-reference: famine year, valley of the silent temple.**

Three entries. My hands shook so badly the drawer rattled.

The first entry I lifted out was thin as onion skin, and inside it, pressed flat and pale as a moth wing, was a soul I did not know. The ledger line beneath it read: *Crane, Tomas. Sold for seed-grain sufficient to see four children through winter. Purchaser's note: fair price, honest man, wept while signing.* My great-grandfather. I had never known his name before that moment, and learning it from a debt-collector's file felt like being robbed by a librarian.

The second entry was thicker. Heavier. The soul within it was folded many times over, compressed through decades of interest, and when I held the page close I could hear it humming — not whispering, *humming*, a low steady note like a temple bell struck once and never allowed to finish ringing.

And the handwriting on the entry was my grandmother's.

I knew it instantly, the way you know your own face in dark water. The strong downstrokes. The little flourish she put on every *y* like she was flicking water off her fingers. I had spent my childhood copying her letters off spice jars and recipe cards and one magnificent, blasphemous poem she'd written on the back of a tax notice. I had built my whole hand out of hers. Every scribe steals their first alphabet from somebody.

*Crane, Edith. Sold: one soul, her own, freely offered. Terms: survival of bloodline entire through famine year and three years following. Collateral retained in perpetuity or until equal weight presented.*

Freely offered. There it was. Not tricked, not stolen, not desperate in the way I'd imagined on all those cold nights studying the stolen pages. She had walked down here — or written herself down here, which amounted to the same thing — and signed her own soul over with that flourished *y*, and bought my mother's life, and my aunt's, and eventually, downstream through the years like water finding its level, mine.

I had come down this mountain furious with her. Chapter by chapter of my own life, I had rehearsed the argument: *How could you sell a piece of yourself? How could you hang that debt around our necks like a millstone? Did you think we'd never find out?*

Standing there holding her actual soul in my actual hands, feeling it hum that unfinished bell-note, I discovered something inconvenient about fury.

It doesn't survive contact with love. It just folds itself up small and sits in the corner of your chest, sulking, while you do what has to be done.

"Oh, Grandmother," I said aloud, and my voice cracked in the middle like a boy's. "You terrible, magnificent woman. You couldn't have sold a *cow*?"

The soul hummed a little louder. I choose to believe that was agreement.

* * *

The problem arrived the way problems always arrive in courts: politely, and with paperwork.

I was still holding Grandmother's entry when the child tugged my sleeve and pointed to the third slot in the drawer. Empty. Just a slip where the entry should be, and on the slip, fresh ink that gleamed like it had been written that morning — perhaps it had; time runs strangely under a mountain — the court's neat hand:

**Entry three: withdrawn from general filing. Held in trust. Origin document for living echo. See: current account, active.**

The child.

The child whose origin lay in my grandmother's drawer. The child who was not, and never had been, simply a lost soul wandering the winter roads. The child was the *change* — the remainder, the residue, the part of the transaction that never quite balanced. When Grandmother sold her soul, something had been left over. A splinter of the bargain too stubborn to file. And it had walked out into the world wearing a small body and speaking a language older than kingdoms, because that language was the sound a bargain makes when it refuses to die quietly.

I looked at the child. The child looked back at me with those eyes that had seen more winters than their face admitted, and said, very calmly, the way children say devastating things:

"That's me, isn't it? In that drawer."

"Yes," I said. Lying to this child had stopped being possible somewhere on the First Winter Road, around the campfire, during the dream-songs.

"And her." A small finger touched Grandmother's entry, resting against my chest. "That's the rest of her. The part that got sold."

"Yes."

"So if you take one out—" The child's voice did not waver, and somehow that was the worst thing yet. Children should waver. Children should get to waver. "—you can't take both. That's how drawers work. That's how *courts* work. One weight leaves, another must stay."

One hour. One exit. One pair of hands.

I am a scribe. My entire professional life has been an education in the fact that everything fits on the page except what matters. There is always margin enough for the words and never margin enough for the truth underneath them. I had copied ten thousand ledgers and never once understood what they were *for* until I stood in the vault beneath the mountain holding my grandmother's soul and looking at the living remainder of her greatest mistake and her greatest act of love, which were, it turns out, the same document.

Above us, muffled by stone and shelving, the mountain groaned. The hour was bleeding out. Somewhere Merric was paying for our head start with the only currency he had left, and I did not let myself think about that, because thinking about it would stop my feet, and my feet were currently the most important part of the plan.

"Listen to me," I said, kneeling, so the child and I were eye to eye among the whispering shelves. "Both of those are going out of this vault tonight. Both. I don't care what the arithmetic says. Arithmetic is just arithmetic, and I have spent my whole life watching clever people hide cruelty inside sums. We are going to find the flaw. There is always a flaw. Grandmother found one when she signed — she wrote 'freely offered' in terms so clean even this court couldn't wriggle out of honoring them. So we're going to honor her style."

The child considered me with the grave patience of someone who had, in some fractional, splintered way, been alive since before kingdoms.

"You're going to do something stupid," the child said fondly. "Like her. She was stupid too. Beautifully stupid. That's why the bargain never closed all the way. She loved us too much to sign a clean death into it, so the ink stayed wet. That's why I exist. That's why I *walked.*"

"Wet ink," I repeated. My heart had gone very quiet and very fast, the way it does right before a decision you'll spend the rest of your life describing. "The ink never dried."

"No."

Which meant — oh, you beautiful, impossible, *amended* woman — which meant the contract was still technically open. Still negotiable. Still, in the pedantic, merciless, glorious language of law, *live.* The court could not simply refuse a counter-offer on an open instrument. Even magistrates of pure contract must bow to pure contract.

I had studied inheritance law under the driest man in the silent valley, and I had copied penalty clauses until I dreamed in them, and I knew — I suddenly, wonderfully knew — what an open contract needed to close fairly.

Equal weight. An oath of equal weight, presented willingly, entered into the ledger in a living hand.

I had already offered my name at the gate. Provisionally accepted. Marked on my hand.

But a name, I realized, staring at the herald's mark, was only a down payment. A name is what people call you. The ledger wanted the whole entry. The full record. Everything a person is, filed and remembered and owed.

There would be time to panic about that later. Later, I promised myself, later, later — I had gotten very good at promising myself things I didn't intend to keep, which is, I suppose, the family trade.

For now, I tucked Grandmother's entry inside my coat, against my heart, where it hummed like a second pulse. I took the child's hand. I took one long look around the vault — at the folded souls, the whispered names, the little entry about the really excellent pie — and I made the shelves a promise I actually meant to keep: *I'm coming back for all of you someday. Write it down.*

Then the far wall of the vault shuddered, and stone dust sifted down like dirty snow, and through the groaning mountain came Merric's voice one more time — thinner now, fraying, but unmistakably, idiotically, heroically laughing —

"—tell them the horse story! Ask them if the pie was worth it!"

The hour was dying. The flaw was found. And the last thing I said to the child, as we ran for the door and the choice I had already made without letting myself know it, was:

"Hold my hand. And whatever happens — remember me *loudly.*"

The child gripped back and answered in the old tongue, the first-language, the binding-tongue, the words that write themselves into ledgers whether you will it or no:

*"Always."*

Somewhere behind us, softly, patiently, the vault began to seal — and in my coat, against my heart, my grandmother's unfinished bell-note rang on and on, waiting to hear what I would write next.
