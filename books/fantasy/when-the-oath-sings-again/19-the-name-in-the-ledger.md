## 19. The Name in the Ledger

The hour Merric bought them was burning down like a candle held too close to the fingers.

Yara felt it in her teeth first — a faint ticking beneath the enamel, as if the mountain had decided to keep time inside her body now that it could no longer keep it anywhere else. Above them, through miles of stone, she could hear the vault doors of the Court Beneath grinding against their seals, and below, the shelves of recorded debts were shivering in their alcoves like a library waking from a long nap and finding burglars in it.

"Left," said the child.

"I see it's left."

"You were walking right."

"I was *considering* right," Yara said, and turned left.

The vault was not what she had expected, though she wasn't sure what she'd expected — gold, perhaps, or fire, or some grand terrible machinery of souls. Instead it was a room like every counting-house she had ever worked in, if a counting-house were built by something with infinite patience and no understanding of mercy. Shelves rose out of sight into darkness. On each shelf stood ledgers. In each ledger slept names.

And in the center of the room, on a plain wooden desk that would not have been out of place in the temple where Yara had spent eleven years copying other people's debts, lay two objects.

A jar. Small, clay, unremarkable — the kind farmers used for pickled onions — except that inside it there was a light like late afternoon through amber glass, dimmed and folded small, the way a dress is folded when its owner no longer has use for it.

And beside it, a child-sized hollow in the air, shaped exactly like the child standing next to Yara, glowing softly, waiting to be filled.

"There I am," the child said quietly. "That's the piece of me they kept."

Yara's throat hurt. "You knew?"

"I always knew." The child did not look away from it. "You know your own missing tooth. You keep putting your tongue in the space."

Yara thought of chapter after chapter of her own life spent putting her tongue in spaces — the grandmother she'd loved and never questioned, the debts she'd copied without once asking whose. She picked up the jar. It was warm, the way a stone stays warm after an animal has been lying on it. Inside, the folded light shifted, almost imperceptibly, toward her hand.

*Grandmother,* she thought. *You're smaller than I imagined. You're so much smaller.*

Somewhere above them, the mountain groaned. The ticking in Yara's teeth sped up.

"Both," the child said. It wasn't a question.

"Both," Yara agreed. Then, because she was a scribe before she was anything else, and because scribes are trained to read the fine print even while fleeing for their lives, she looked at the ledger open on the desk.

It was the master ledger. She knew this the way you know your own name — by its binding, by the wear-pattern of its corners, by the smell of its ink, which was not ink at all but something older and more patient. The court's heralds had explained the rules at the Frozen Gate with the cheerful mercilessness of tax collectors: *an oath of equal weight to the one reclaimed.* A soul weighed against a soul. A debt weighed against a debt.

But Yara had spent eleven years in a temple whose entire sacred purpose was making sure certain things were never written down. And temples, whatever else they taught you, taught you this: **the ledger does not weigh what you give. It weighs what is written.**

She set down the jar. She took up the pen that lay beside the master ledger — a plain quill, crow-feather, absurdly ordinary — and dipped it in the well of not-ink.

"What are you doing?" the child asked.

"Reading," Yara said, "and then writing," which was, when she thought about it later, the whole story of her life compressed into six words.

* * *

The page before her was headed, in letters like frost forming, **ACCOUNTS OUTSTANDING**.

Her grandmother's entry sat near the top. *One soul, adult, female, sold in the year of the Black Famine, price: survival of five.* Beside it, in handwriting Yara knew better than her own — she had learned her letters tracing it, tracing the flourishes on old letters, tracing the careful loops of a woman who believed beautiful script could hold a family together — a signature. *Maren Crane.*

Below the entry, the court had appended its terms. Yara read them twice. The court wrote clearly; she would give it that. It simply wrote without a single word wasted on kindness.

*Redeemable by payment of equivalent value: one soul entire, or one name complete with record thereof, freely surrendered.*

There it was. Not a soul. Or a soul — but also, in the same breath, as though it were a lesser thing tossed in as an afterthought:

*A name, complete with record thereof.*

Yara smiled. It was not a nice smile. Eleven years of copying other people's contracts had taught her that courts hide their loopholes in the *or*s.

Because here was what the court beneath the mountain understood about value, and here was what it did not understand: it thought a name was a small thing. A convenience. A label. Something you surrendered the way you surrender a coat at a door, and collect again on the way out.

But Yara Crane had been raised by a woman who signed everything beautifully. She knew what a name was. A name was the handle by which memory grips you. A name was how your mother called you home at dusk. A name was every letter ever written to you, every roll call, every whispered endearment, every curse, every line in every ledger. Take the name and you didn't take a label. You took the thread, and the whole sweater came apart after it.

Her name, complete with record thereof, was worth — she estimated, with a scribe's cold arithmetic — roughly everything.

"Yara." The child had gone very still. Children always know. That was the cruelty and grace of them; they could smell a sacrifice coming the way dogs smell weather. "Whatever you're reading, stop reading it."

"It says I can pay with my name."

"No."

"It says it right here—"

"*No.*" The child grabbed her sleeve, and its grip was astonishingly strong for someone made mostly of leftover light and stubbornness. "I've seen what happens to things that get written in these books. I *am* what happens. I'm what's left over when a book is finished with someone. Don't."

Yara knelt down to the child's height, which put the open ledger at eye level behind it, which was, she admitted, convenient.

"Listen to me," she said. "When we crossed the valley edge, your language became song. Do you remember? You sang for the first time and the frost came up out of the ground just to listen. That was because someone had finally claimed you — me, badly, halfway, with stolen pages and a dishonest oath-broker, but claimed. And now we're going to walk out of here with your missing piece and your grandmother—" she touched the jar gently, "*our* grandmother — and the mountain is going to seal, and the court is going to want its payment, and if I pay with nothing, then in fifty years some other wretched scribe will find two more entries in this book and come running with stolen pages. The debt doesn't die unless somebody eats it whole."

"So eat it with your *soul*, not your—"

"My soul is attached to my name, little echo. Can't sell one without the other, and the soul comes with terms I don't like." She paused. "Besides. There's a difference."

"What difference?" The child was crying now, furiously, silently, the way children cry when they have learned crying doesn't work on the person they need it to work on.

"A soul gets kept," Yara said. "Filed. Preserved. That's what this place does — it keeps. But a *name*—" she tapped the ledger, and the frost-letters shivered, "—a name only lives if it's remembered. If I go into this book, and nobody outside remembers Yara Crane, then the payment isn't sitting on a shelf forever being valuable. It's *fading*. Every year it's worth less. And a court built entirely on exact accounting—" she let the smile back, the not-nice one, "—cannot abide an asset that depreciates. They'll take it because the law says they must accept equal weight. And then they'll spend eternity watching it shrink. My grandmother's been on that shelf for sixty years and she's still worth a family's survival. Me? Give me twenty years of no one remembering, and I'll be worth less than a pickled onion."

"You don't know that."

"I'm a scribe," Yara said. "I know exactly what ink does when nobody reads it. It dries. It cracks. It lets go of the page." She stood. "They're buying a candle and calling it a lamp. And I am *delighted* to cheat them."

Above them, the mountain screamed — actually screamed, stone-on-stone, a sound like the world's largest door deciding to be a wall. The hour was nearly spent. Somewhere behind them, Merric was holding apart the jaws of a legal system with his bare hands and his torn-up contract, and he could not hold much longer, and she was standing here monologuing.

Scribes, Yara reflected, really should be issued a handler.

* * *

She wrote quickly, because she was good at it, because eleven years of copying had given her a hand that moved like water and never blotted, and because if she slowed down even for a moment she would start feeling it instead of writing it.

*Received into account:* she wrote, *one name complete: Yara, of the family Crane, of the valley temple, daughter of Tessa, granddaughter of Maren. With full record thereof: all deeds, all words, all debts paid and unpaid, all memories held by others under that name, all places wherein the said name is written, spoken, sung, or otherwise preserved.*

The pen grew heavier with every word. Not physically — physically it stayed a crow feather, light as an insult. But something was flowing out of her and onto the page, and she could feel the flow the way you feel blood leaving your head when you stand up too fast.

She kept writing.

*Freely surrendered, in exchange for:* and here she made the court wait, pen hovering, while she looked down at the child, who was looking up at her with the whole of its strange split face, half shadow and half song.

"Tell me your real name," she said. "The one from before. From the song-language."

The child shook its head slowly. "It broke when I broke. It's in the jar too, maybe. Or gone."

"Then you'll make a new one," Yara said. "That's the trick nobody tells you about names. They're not found. They're *written*. Somebody writes you into the world, and that's all a naming ever was." She bent and kissed the top of the child's head, which smelled of woodsmoke and winter apples. "Somebody will write you in. I promise. It'll be the best sentence anyone ever puts down."

Then she turned back to the ledger and finished it, because promises mean nothing until they're in ink.

*—in exchange for: the release and restoration of the soul of Maren Crane, entire, and the release and restoration of the fragment held against the account of the same, being the residue known as the child, both to depart freely, all liens dissolved, all claims void, this account closed upon sealing and never reopened.*

She signed it. *Yara Crane.* Her hand did not shake. Scribes' hands never shake; it's the first thing they beat into you, or rather the last thing left after everything else shakes loose.

The moment the final stroke dried, three things happened at once.

The jar in her hand flared like sunrise.

The hollow in the air rushed into the child like breath into a bellows, and the child cried out — a high, whole, unbroken note that rang off the shelves and made ten thousand sleeping names stir in their ledgers like birds before a storm.

And Yara Crane began, very quietly, to come unwritten.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the strange part. She had braced for pain and instead it felt like setting down a load she had forgotten she was carrying — the load, it turned out, of being perceived. Names peeled off her like leaves off a branch in autumn, gently, in no particular order.

She felt her mother's voice letting go of it — *Yara, supper, YARA, supper* — drifting away like smoke.

She felt the abbot's disapproving frown losing its target, becoming a frown at nothing.

She felt the temple's ledgers, miles away in the valley, quietly revising themselves, eleven years of her careful columns reattributing to no one.

She felt Merric — wherever he was, bleeding and swearing and magnificent — lose the word for her even as he fought for her, and she hoped, distantly, that it wouldn't slow him down, and knew it might.

And underneath it all, growing warmer and brighter in her hands, the jar — her grandmother — rising up out of sixty years of folding, unfolding like a dress taken from its chest, shaking itself into shape, becoming *light* again, becoming *afternoon* again, becoming someone.

The mountain was sealing. She could feel the walls closing the way you feel a house settling, vast and deliberate and final. The court had accepted her payment — she heard it somewhere far above, the dry rustling sound of a contract stamped, the most expensive sound in the world.

"Go," said the child.

It stood in front of her transformed — no longer half-shadow, no longer residue, but whole, blazing softly, holding the hand of a second figure that was only now condensing out of the jar's light: an old woman, small and straight-backed, with Yara's own dark eyes and a mouth built for beautiful signatures.

Maren Crane looked at Yara for a long moment. Sixty years filed away, and the first thing she did with her face was to look at her granddaughter and begin to say her name—

—and stop, because the name wasn't there anymore. Yara watched her reach for it and find the space where it had been, the missing tooth, the tongue finding nothing.

"That's all right," Yara said. Her voice sounded odd to her already, slightly distant, like a story told about someone else. "You'll write me a new one. Both of you. That's how it works now. Somebody writes you in."

"Child—" the old woman began, in a voice like a hearth after years of cold.

"The mountain's closing!" Yara said, laughing, because if she didn't laugh she would do something unforgivable like weep, and scribes' hands never shake, and apparently neither did their voices, not until the very last line. "Move, both of you. Grandmothers first. It's traditional."

They ran. The three of them, up through the halls of recorded debts, past shelves where souls murmured in their sleep, past the frozen gate where the herald stood aside with a bow of perfect, terrible courtesy, out into snow that was falling upward now, the seal closing behind them with a sound like the last page of the world's longest book.

And on the mountainside, in the blue-white hush of a winter breaking early, Yara Crane — who was no longer quite Yara Crane, who was a warmth and a habit and a debt happily eaten — stood between a freed soul and a whole child, and realized, with the calm of a professional, that she had made one small error.

She had written the promise into the ledger. *Somebody will write you in.*

But the ledger belonged to the court now. And the court kept its assets.

Which meant that somewhere beneath the mountain, in a book that never reopened, her promise was still waiting to be kept — and only two people in all the world carried any part of it out.

The child turned to her, new-named and unnamed, eyes bright with borrowed memory, and said, "I remember something of yours. One thing. Come — I'll tell it to you, and we'll see if it grows."
