## 22. When the Oath Sings Again

I have learned that archives are made of three things: paper, patience, and the people who refuse to let go.

The valley's new archive lives in the old temple, which is fitting. The temple was always a building for keeping things — debts, silences, children nobody wanted remembered. Now it keeps stories instead. The shelves where the abbot once stored his ledgers of forgetting hold journals and songbooks and the testimony of farmers who survived the long winter of the sealing. There is one shelf, at the very top, where the light comes through a crack in the stone at midday, and on that shelf sits a single book bound in grey leather, unmarked, because its author has no name to mark it with.

That book is mine to keep. I am the keeper now. I have been the keeper for eleven years, which is long enough to grow from a child who spoke an older language into a young woman who speaks several, though only one of them can turn a room cold when spoken carelessly.

The valley folk call me Wren. It is not my true name — I am not sure I ever had one before the mountain gave me back to myself — but it suits. Wrens are small birds that sing louder than their size should allow, and Merric said once, in one of his visits, that this described me exactly and also described my cooking, which he claimed could be heard complaining from the road.

Merric is dead two winters now. He died the way he lived: mid-negotiation, trying to talk a fever down to a fairer price. The last thing he said to me was *tell the story right*, and then, after a pause, *and tell them I was handsome*. I intend to do both, though not necessarily in that order.

* * *

Every year on the first snow — which since the sealing has come late and gentle, like the mountain keeping its end of something — the valley gathers in the temple's great hall, and I read.

Not scripture. We have none. Not law; the valley never much cared for law even when the temple enforced it. I read the archive. A chapter of it. The same chapter, if I'm honest, because they ask for it every time, and because some stories are not meant to be rotated like crops.

The story of the scribe who paid with her name.

Tonight is one of those nights. Snow is falling past the windows in fat, unhurried flakes, and the hall smells of woodsmoke and chestnuts and the particular warmth of too many people who have decided to be happy about it. Children sit cross-legged in front. Old Marta has brought her new grandchild, who is six months old and already chewing on the corner of a songbook, which Marta assures everyone is how all great scholars begin.

The woman who keeps the bakery — she was a girl of ten the winter we fled, and she remembers handing bread over the temple wall to a scribe and a strange quiet child — sits near the front with her own daughters. She always sits near the front. She says it's so she can hear. I think it's so I can see her, so I know that the bread was received, that the kindness landed somewhere and grew.

I open the grey book.

* * *

Here is what most people forget, and what I will not: names are not labels. They are contracts. A name is a promise between you and the world that says *when you call, I will answer.* When Yara Crane wrote herself into the ledger beneath the mountain, she did not erase herself the way ink erases. She signed herself over. Her name became the court's property, filed on a shelf in a hall of recorded debts, and everything her name had ever touched — her debts, her history, her place in anyone's memory — went with it.

Which means that when we came out of the mountain, blinking into early spring that had broken a whole generation ahead of schedule, there were exactly two beings alive who remembered her.

Me. And the soul.

Her grandmother's soul — aged, diminished, carried out under my arm wrapped in my own coat — remembered her daughter's daughter. Of course it did. Souls keep better records than courts do.

We buried the soul that spring on the hill above the valley, in soil the frost had finally loosened. I say *buried*; what I mean is we gave it back. It had spent forty years as collateral, filed and shelved, and it deserved to be dirt again, ordinary dirt, the kind that grows onions. The woman who runs the bakery planted crocuses on the hill without being asked. They come up every year slightly earlier than anywhere else in the valley, as if the ground there is eager.

And me — I kept the name. Not spoken aloud, not written anywhere the world could file or steal or sell it. Kept the way you keep a coal alive under ash overnight: covered, banked, warm. Some nights I take it out and look at it. *Yara.* Two syllables. A small contract. *When you call, I will answer.*

She cannot answer anymore. But I can call. And every year, on the first snow, in front of the whole valley, I do.

* * *

"Are we doing the voices tonight?" asks Tam, who is nine and has no respect for the gravity of archival tradition, which is why I like him best.

"We are doing the voices," I confirm.

Tam's older sister groans. "The grandmother voice takes forever."

"The grandmother voice," I say, "is the most important voice in the entire story, and if you interrupt her again she will haunt your boots specifically."

This is, technically, true. The grandmother's soul stayed with us nearly four years before it finished thinning into whatever souls become when they are done — not nothing; I want to be very clear about that, because the mountain would have you believe that used-up souls become nothing. They become quieter. They become weather, mostly. The first hard frost after she left us arrived in the exact shape of a hand pressed against the kitchen window, and I have chosen to find this funny rather than devastating, and I recommend the same policy to everyone.

But while she stayed, she talked. Oh, how she talked. Forty years underground with nothing to do but be a ledger entry — she had *opinions* stored up like a squirrel storing acorns, and she distributed them freely. About my posture ("you slouch like a debt collector reading bad news"). About Merric ("that man would haggle with his own conscience and win"). About Yara — about her granddaughter, whom she had sold a piece of herself to save, and who came down into the dark to carry her home.

"She looked just like my Elsbet," she told me once, in the kitchen, watching the grown scribe — the unnamed one, the woman who had come back to the valley and stayed, tending goats now, mending fences, teaching letters to children who didn't know they were being taught by the finest legal mind the mountain court had ever lost to. "Stubborn in exactly the same place. Right behind the eyes. You can't argue someone out of a thing that lives behind the eyes."

"And you tried?" I asked.

"I argued with that family for sixty years," she said, with tremendous satisfaction. "It's how we loved each other."

So yes. The grandmother voice takes forever. The valley wouldn't have it any other way.

* * *

I read until the candles burn low. I read the tollhouse scene, and the whole hall does the tollkeeper's voice together because everyone knows it by heart — *the court below already knows you travel* — and the youngest children shiver deliciously even though they've heard it a dozen times. I read the descent, the halls of recorded debts, the shelves of souls filed like documents, and I watch the faces of the children go carefully blank in that way that tells me they'll dream about it tonight, and I make no apology, because they should dream about it. The world is full of courts beneath mountains. Someone ought to warn them early.

I read the loophole — the debtor dead three years before signing — and I perform the magistrates' courtesy, their terrible legalistic politeness, the way their voices sounded like paper being squared on a desk. I read Merric tearing up his own contract, and here I give him the flourish he requested: tall, broad-shouldered, jaw like a cliff, hair silvering handsomely. Tam objects that Merric couldn't possibly have had hair like that. Tam knew Merric. Tam remembers a small round man who cheated him at cards and then taught him how to cheat better. I tell Tam that archives permit a certain generosity toward the departed, and Tam says that's called lying, and I say welcome to the profession.

And then I read the ending. Every year I almost can't. Every year I do.

The vault. The choice. One soul or one child, and one hour burning down. And the scribe — the one with no name, whose face I describe carefully in the book as *ordinary, tired, with stubbornness living right behind the eyes* — sitting down on the vault floor with the ledger across her knees, and writing.

Not erasing. Writing. That was the trick of it, the thing even the court did not fully understand until it was done. You cannot unsign a soul by crossing it out; the court holds every cancellation as a debt. But a ledger is a living thing, and a living thing can be *added to*. She wrote a new entry. Herself. Name, record, history, the whole small contract of her — offered in exchange for both the shelved soul and the walking echo of it, both released, both free, the account closed not by payment but by substitution. An oath of equal weight. The herald had marked her hand at the frozen gate; the court had provisionally accepted; and in the vault, with the hour dying around her, she made it final in her own handwriting, in her own careful scribe's script, which the mountain still keeps on display, I'm told, filed under *examples of unusual competence*.

The mountain sealed behind us. The winter broke. We walked out into a spring that shouldn't have existed yet, carrying an old woman's soul and nothing else, because the scribe who saved us had traded away the only thing she owned.

And the valley — this is my favorite part, and I slow down for it every single time, and Tam lets me, this once a year — the valley forgot her completely. The temple records showed no Yara Crane. Her debt dissolved with her name. The abbot, who had ordered a child locked away and a mind erased, met her on the road a month later and tipped his hat to a stranger.

"But she stayed anyway," I read. "She stayed in the valley that had forgotten her, and she kept goats, and she taught letters, and she was — "

I stop.

I always stop here. Because the book doesn't say what she was. I wrote this part eleven years ago and I have never been able to finish it, because every word I try — *happy*, *free*, *at peace* — feels smaller than the truth, and the truth is that I don't know. She chose to stay. She chose it with her stubborn-behind-the-eyes whole heart. But whether a person can be happy inside a life that has forgotten her — whether freedom found inside loss is really freedom, or only loss wearing freedom's coat —

The hall waits. They know I stop here. They wait every year, the way you wait at a graveside, or a bedside, or a door.

And every year, in that waiting, something happens.

* * *

It started the third year. I was standing in the candle-hush with the unfinished sentence hanging in the air, and old Marta — who remembers more than she lets on, who once handed bread over a wall — cleared her throat and said, quietly, "She was kind."

And someone else said, "She was brave."

And the baker's eldest daughter, who was learning her letters from the goat-tending stranger at the time and did not know who she was learning them from, said, "She made me laugh. When I got the letter *thorn* backwards for the hundredth time, she said, 'Good, now it's a rune, you're ahead of the other students.'"

And it has gone on like that every year since. The hall fills the sentence for me. *She was patient. She was stubborn. She stayed. She taught my father to read. She argued with the miller about grain prices and won.* Little offerings, laid into the silence like coins into a tollbox. And here is the thing — the thing I have never told anyone except the ash-covered coal I keep under my ribs:

Each year, when the last offering lands, I hear it.

Faintly. Underneath the settling of the fire and the breathing of the crowd. A single note, thin and clear, like a wire singing in wind. Like a contract remembering itself. An oath, singing.

Because that is what an oath is, when you strip away the parchment and the courts and the terrible polite magistrates. An oath is a story that two people agree to believe hard enough to make true. Debts are paid in gold and names and years of service — the mountain taught me that, and the mountain was not lying, only small. But repayment — real repayment, the kind that closes an account so thoroughly that even the shelves beneath the world stop rattling — repayment is made of this: a hall full of people, once a year, telling a stranger's story and filling in her missing ending, piece by piece, year by year, until the story is more complete than any life gets to be.

She paid with her name. The valley pays back in story. And somewhere in the arithmetic of that exchange — I have checked, I have spent eleven years checking — the books balance.

* * *

Tonight the offerings run longer than usual. The baker's youngest daughter, who is five, stands up on the bench and announces, "She was *pretty,*" which is new information to the archive and receives a skeptical murmur from her sister. Tam says, "She'd have liked the chestnuts." Marta, last as always, oldest as always, folds her hands and says, "She forgave us. This valley locked a child in a cellar and forgot a woman who saved it, and she forgave us, and don't any of you dare forget *that* part."

And underneath it all, faint as breath on a winter window: the note. The singing. Slightly stronger this year, I think. Or perhaps I am simply getting older and the coal under the ash burns closer to the surface.

Then the hall empties, slowly, the way halls do — in clusters, in arguments about whether the tollkeeper's voice was better last year, in children being collected like stray mittens. The fire settles. Snow ticks against the windows. I close the grey book and carry it back to its shelf in the high light, and I stand there a moment with my hand flat on the cover, the way you'd rest your hand on the shoulder of someone sleeping.

"You'd have liked the chestnuts," I tell her. "Tam's right. He's insufferable when he's right."

The candle flame leans, though there is no draft.

Upstairs, my rooms are small and warm and full of other books — ones I write myself, these days. A dictionary of the old language, the one that turns rooms cold, annotated for safety. A field guide to broken oaths and how to mend them, which the trade cities have begun requesting by post. And a slim private volume, not for the shelf, in which I am attempting, badly, humorously, with many crossed-out lines, to compose the thing I have been composing for eleven years:

A letter. Addressed to a name I am not permitted to speak, filed nowhere, deliverable only by the one postal service the mountain cannot intercept.

*Dear Yara,* it begins, the way it begins every winter. *The valley sends its regards. The crocuses came up early again. Your grandmother says hello, or rather, the frost on the window does, and it spelled it wrong, which she would have hated. The archive is thriving. The story is nearly finished — we only need your ending, and we're working on it, all of us, one year at a time. Take your time. We're not going anywhere. Neither, I suspect, is the mountain, and it still owes you a receipt.*

I set down the pen. Outside, the snow goes on falling, gentle and late and faithful, keeping its end of something.

And in the quiet of the archive, faintly, patiently, an oath sings.
