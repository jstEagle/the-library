## 1. The Shop That Sold Nothing

The sign above the door read *Delacroix Charms & Curiosities*, which was a lie of the most careful kind, because nothing inside the shop had ever done anything at all.

Noor Delacroix had painted the sign himself, five years ago, with paint he'd mixed too thin so that it would fade faster. He refreshed it every spring with the same mistake on purpose. A man who sold charms that worked would be hanged within the week in occupied Corvane. A man whose charms conspicuously didn't work — well, that was almost respectable. There were three other shops on Tallow Row selling things that did nothing, and the Inquisition walked past all of them the way a farmer walks past a fence post.

He opened each morning by winding the clock, sweeping the step, and taking inventory of his stock of harmless junk: copper rings stamped with knots that meant *patience* and nothing else, dried lavender bundled with red string for travelers who wanted to feel looked after, little glass beads called "tears of the river" that were, in fact, tears of a glassblower in Hallowmark who charged Noor four coppers apiece and laughed at him for reselling them.

"Protection charm," Noor told a grandmother on Tuesday, holding up one of the beads. "Guaranteed to do absolutely nothing."

"And what does nothing cost?"

"Two coppers. It's very rare, nothing. Hard to source."

She bought three. Everyone did, eventually, once they understood the joke, and then once they understood it wasn't entirely a joke. People came out of the occupied city wanting something in their pocket that no edict could name, no warrant could describe, no magistrate could confiscate. A bead that did nothing was the only kind of magic left that was legal, because it wasn't magic at all, and somehow that made it worth more than any spell he had ever been taught.

Noor had once been able to do things that would have emptied this street in awe. He did not think about that before noon. Before noon he thought about inventory, and tea, and whether the damp had gotten into the back room again.

* * *

His routines were a fortress built out of tedium, and he maintained them the way other men maintained prayer.

Up at dawn. Shutters open at the second bell, never the first, because shops that opened early on Tallow Row looked eager and eager looked desperate and desperate looked like something worth inspecting. Breakfast was bread and the last of last autumn's apple butter, eaten standing up, over the sink, like a man with no past and no ambitions. He wore gray. Not the gray of mourning and not the gray of poverty, but the specific forgettable gray of a man you have met before and cannot place and would not cross the street to greet.

And every morning, without exception, he practiced forgetting.

This was harder than it sounded. The Vesper Guild had trained its apprentices in memory first and everything else second, on the theory that an oath is only a sentence until someone remembers it with their whole body. Noor had been good at it. He'd been *gifted* at it, Master Pellon used to say, with the pride of a man describing a prize pig, and Noor had spent five years trying to prove him wrong.

The method was simple and cruel: every day, he took one small thing he knew and set it down deliberately, the way you'd set down a heavy pack, and he did not pick it up again. The third finger-position of the Lattice Grip. The opening syllables of the water-finding song. The name of the star the guild navigated by, which he had known since he was six and now told people was called "the North Star," which it wasn't, and the small wrongness of that lie sat in his chest like a pebble in a shoe, and he had learned to walk with it.

Some nights the forgetting went wrong. Some nights he dreamed in the First Tongue and woke with a word on his lips that glowed faintly if he said it aloud, and so he lay very still in the dark with the word unspoken, pressing it down like a hand over a bird's heart until it stilled.

"You're getting old," he told himself in the mirror some mornings, because the guild had also taught him never to say true names carelessly, even his own, especially his own, and calling himself *old* at thirty-one was a kind of protective misdirection that had the advantage of being nearly plausible. There were gray hairs now. He chose to believe they were from stress rather than sorcery.

The bell above his door rang at midday, and Marta from the apothecary next door leaned in without coming all the way inside, which was her way. She had flour-colored eyebrows and the sharpest tongue on Tallow Row and she was, in defiance of all sense, his closest friend.

"You're out of willow bark," she announced. "I noticed. I keep track of your vices."

"I have exactly one vice and it's overcharging grandmothers for glass beads."

"That's not a vice, that's commerce." She came in properly then, shaking rain off her shawl, and lowered her voice in the way of someone about to share weather news with more weight than weather deserved. "There were gray cloaks on Candlemaker Street this morning. Asking after songs."

Noor kept his hands moving on the counter, wiping a spot of dust from one spot to another spot. "Songs."

"Singing. Someone heard singing in the low quarter, they're saying. Actual singing, the kind that does things." She said it lightly, the way people in Corvane said all dangerous things lightly, wrapped in irony like a pill in honey. "Probably rats in a wall. Rats sing beautifully, I've always said."

"Always."

"Mm." She looked at him a moment longer than was comfortable, because Marta looked at everyone a moment longer than was comfortable; it was not suspicion, merely appetite for the truth, which in Corvane was nearly as dangerous as magic. "Willow bark. By Friday, or I'm raising my prices on your headache powder."

"You've already raised them twice."

"And yet you keep having headaches. Curious, that." She rang herself out through his own door with the air of a woman who owned everything she touched.

* * *

That afternoon a boy came in wanting a luck charm for his mother's card games, and Noor sold him a knotted copper ring and instructions to hold it while cheating, "so the guilt has somewhere to go," and the boy left delighted, and for perhaps an hour Noor forgot to be afraid. This was the trick of the life he'd built, if it had a trick at all: the days were full enough of smallness that fear had to queue like everyone else.

He liked the shop. That was the part he never said aloud, even to himself, even in the privacy of the ledger where he wrote down his inventory in a cipher that was honestly just bad handwriting. He liked the smell of dried lavender and floor wax. He liked knowing which customers wanted conversation and which wanted silence. He liked being nobody. It had taken him years and the deaths of better men than him to become nobody, and nobody was safe, and safe was —

*Safe was small,* said a voice in his head that sounded like Master Pellon and which he drowned in tea.

* * *

It rained all Thursday, a fine needling rain that made Tallow Row shine like something better than it was. Noor spent the morning re-bundling lavender that did not need re-bundling, and he was thus at the window with a perfect view when the gray cloaks came for Marta's shop.

There were six of them, which was excessive for an apothecary and correct for a raid. They moved badly on purpose, loud and unhurried, because the point of a raid was never the capture; the point was the watching. Everyone on Tallow Row watched. Everyone on Tallow Row stood very still in their doorways and counted the gray cloaks and calculated, with the cold arithmetic of the occupied, *not me, not today.*

They put Marta against her own counter. One of them turned her jars out onto the floor, one by one, unhurried, while another read from a warrant in the flat voice they must teach them specifically for warrants. Noor could not hear the words through the glass but he knew the shape of them. *By authority of the Regent's edict against unsanctioned practice.* Somewhere behind his breastbone, the pebble-in-the-shoe feeling became a stone.

They found what they found. Later there would be arguments about what exactly they'd found — Marta swore till the end of the row that it was feverfew, ordinary feverfew, mislabeled — but whatever it was, it had a Latin name written on the jar in a hand that knew Latin names, and knowing the true names of plants was edict-adjacent, and edict-adjacent was enough. Edict-adjacent had always been enough.

Noor stood in his own doorway under the pretense of checking the awning, and made himself watch, because looking away was a luxury and luxuries drew attention. Marta did not cry. She argued, actually, magnificently, hands still dusted with powder, telling the youngest gray cloak that he had the complexion of an uncooked sausage and that his mother would be ashamed, and Noor loved her ferociously and uselessly and did not move.

They took her stock. They took her ledgers. They took Marta, gently, by the elbow, the way you escort an aunt, which was somehow worse than shackles, and the whole street watched her go and the whole street began, immediately and in unison, to forget her. That was how it worked here. Grief was a thing you did in the space of one breath between the shutter closing and the kettle boiling.

The raid took eleven minutes. Then Tallow Row exhaled and resumed, and Noor went back inside his shop of things that did nothing, and sat down behind his counter, and discovered his hands were shaking so badly he had to clasp them together like a man in prayer, which perhaps he was.

*Not me, not today.* But the arithmetic had changed and he knew it. Marta was loud and sharp and memorable, and he had always told himself that her safety lay in her ordinariness — powders and poultices, a licensed trade, nothing edict-worthy — and none of it had mattered. Eleven minutes. A jar with a label. If a jar with a label could take Marta, then what was he? He kept a book in his floorboards written in a dead guild-cipher containing the true names of forty-one herbs and the opening verses of nine songs and one word — one word he could not stop hearing in his dreams — that would glow if spoken and burn if sung.

He should burn it. He had decided to burn it eleven times in five years, and eleven times he had lifted the floorboard and looked at it and closed the floorboard again, because it was the last thing Master Pellon had bled to keep, and burning it would make the forgetting final instead of daily, and Noor had discovered something shameful in himself these long quiet years: he did not fully want to be finished.

Smallness was safe. Smallness was also a slow way of disappearing, and some traitorous root in him kept growing back toward the light.

* * *

That night he did not practice forgetting. He sat in the back room with one candle, listening to the rain, and took stock the way he took stock of beads and rings.

He was, he admitted to the candle, lonely. Not in the way he could fix by going to the tavern on the corner, where loneliness was sold by the pint along with conversation about grain prices. Lonely in the specific way of a man who knows forty-one true names and uses none of them. In Corvane he was surrounded by half-silent people, everyone muted by edict, and he had made himself the best of them, the quietest, the smallest. He had a shop that sold nothing. He had a friend who'd been taken for feverfew. He had a word under his tongue and a book under his floor and a life so carefully dull that some mornings he woke and couldn't remember why he'd bothered preserving it, only that stopping felt impossible, like asking a man mid-tightrope why he doesn't simply step off.

The candle guttered. Rain worked at the shutters. And beneath all his careful arithmetic — the routines, the grays, the daily laying-down of knowledge like stones in a river to cross invisibly — beneath it all, something in Noor Delacroix was singing very quietly to itself, and had been for five years, and had not yet agreed to stop.

He banked the fire and went up to bed. On the stairs he paused, because the wind had shifted, and beneath the rain he thought — just barely, just at the edge of hearing — that the storm itself had a cadence to it. Four beats and a rest. Four beats and a rest. The rhythm of an opening verse he had sworn on Master Pellon's grave never to recall, and which recalled itself now without permission, sweet and enormous, like a bell rung underwater.

"Nonsense," Noor said aloud, to the dark, to himself, in the common tongue, firmly.

But he slept badly, and near dawn, dreaming of bells and floorboards and his master's blood on snow, he did not hear the small knock at his shop door far below — or perhaps he did, and some older part of him, some part that had never agreed to forget anything at all, rose quietly out of bed and went down the stairs to answer it.
