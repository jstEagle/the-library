## 3. What the Guild Buried

The child slept in the corner behind the counter, curled on the folded blanket Noor used for winter nights when the hearth went cold, and Noor sat at his workbench pretending very hard to be a man who made keychains.

It was not going well.

The problem was the primer. It sat in front of him under a loose floorboard he'd pried up an hour ago, and it was the single most dangerous object in the occupied city of Corvane, and it looked exactly like what it was disguised as: a battered book of fishmongers' accounts, forty years of herring prices in a cramped hand. His master, Master Corvess, had kept it beneath the floor of the guild's scriptorium for as long as Noor had known him, and had once told Noor, with the gravity of a man revealing state secrets, that the most important books always smelled faintly of fish.

Noor had laughed then. He was not laughing now.

* * *

Eleven years ago. The scriptorium smelled of ink and tallow and, faintly, of herring.

"You're holding your breath," Master Corvess said. He was a small man made almost entirely of eyebrows and patience, and he did not look up from his copying. "When you hold your breath while reading the First Tongue, you treat it like a foreign thing. A guest to be managed. It isn't. It's the language your bones were written in. Breathe."

Noor exhaled. The page in front of him swam—the old glyphs were less like letters than like weather, curling and uncurling depending on how you looked at them—and then, all at once, they settled, and he read his first full sentence of the First Tongue:

*The oath is not a spell. The oath is a promise with somewhere to live.*

"What does that mean?" he'd asked.

Corvess had finally looked up, and there had been something strange in his face—not pride, exactly. More like a man watching someone step onto ice he himself had never trusted. "I don't know yet," he'd said. "That's the honest answer, apprentice. Ask me again in twenty years."

He had died four years ago, before the twenty years were up, in a raid that official records called an apothecary fire and unofficial records called murder by the Inquisition's own hand. Noor had inherited the fish-smelling book and a lifetime of questions, and had spent five years doing his best to forget both.

* * *

Now he turned the pages with hands that wouldn't quite steady, and the child watched him from the blanket with eyes the color of deep water over stone.

The child had not spoken since dawn. That was somehow worse than the speaking. When Ashen—no, the child wasn't Ashen yet; Noor hadn't gotten that far—when the child spoke, things happened. His guild-signet glowed. The candle flames leaned toward the small mouth like flowers toward sun. And last night, half-asleep on the floor, Noor could have sworn the rain against the shutters fell in rhythm, in meter, like something being recited.

But asleep, the child was only a child: small, hollow-cheeked, one hand curled around a smooth gray stone that Noor was fairly sure had not been in the room yesterday.

"Right," Noor said aloud, because talking to himself was one of the few vices left to a man selling nothing. "You can't understand me. I can't understand you. Between us we have one language, and it's the one that gets people burned." He pulled the primer into the lamplight. "So let's do this carefully, quietly, and without setting anything on fire."

He found the section on decipherment near the middle of the book, wedged between two decades of herring ledgers. Corvess's marginal notes were everywhere, dense and dry and occasionally funny in a way Noor had never appreciated until now, reading them alone. Beside the glyph-cluster for *water*, Corvess had written: *Doran insists this means 'river.' Doran is wrong. It means 'the moving-between.' Everything in this language is about the space between things. Remember that or fail.*

The moving-between. Noor looked at the sleeping child and thought about a knock at his door last night that had sounded like words. Thought about a mark below the collarbone he'd glimpsed while tucking the blanket: a spiral, fine-lined, silver-pale, like water seen from very far up.

He began matching glyphs.

It was slow work. The child's speech didn't map cleanly onto the primer's formal registers—the way the child said things was more like birdsong than grammar—but fragments caught. He wrote them down in common script on scrap paper, sounding them out, and each fragment landed in his chest like a stone dropped into a well he'd spent five years filling with sand.

*Singer-of-the-oath, small, unfinished.*

*Sent-walking because the-spring grows quiet.*

*They are closing the clauses one by one.*

Noor read that last line three times. Then he got up, checked the shutters, checked the door bar, checked the false panel behind the flour bin where he kept the signet, and sat back down with his heart going like a drummer boy's.

Closing the clauses. Closing them like—what? Accounts? Eyes?

The child stirred. Sat up. Looked at Noor with that unnerving directness children have when they haven't been taught yet to look away, and spoke—a short run of syllables, soft, almost apologetic.

The candle flames leaned. Noor's pen-hand shook, but he caught the shape of it, matched it against the primer, and translated, haltingly:

*I am-called Ashen. I am the clause-that-remembers. Please do-not close me.*

"Ashen," Noor repeated, and the word felt like setting down something heavy. "Ashen. All right. I'm Noor." He tapped his own chest. "Noor."

Ashen tilted her head—Noor was now fairly certain the child was a girl, though she had the androgynous sharpness of a winter sparrow—and considered him. Then she said something longer, gesturing at his hand, at the ghost of the guild-signet's shape under his skin, and the fragment came to him slowly:

*You carry a dead-thing that still remembers you.*

"It's not dead," Noor heard himself say, before he could stop it. "It's just—it's resting. Like everything else. We're all just resting."

Ashen looked at him the way you look at a man insisting the flood is a light rain.

* * *

He worked through the morning with the shop's CLOSED sign up, which cost him money he couldn't spare and patience he couldn't find. Twice he stopped to feed Ashen bread and honey, and twice she ate with the careful deliberateness of someone who has been hungry long enough to make a policy of it. Once she wandered to the window, put her palm flat against the glass, and hummed two notes so low Noor felt them in his teeth rather than heard them—and outside, absurdly, the gutter ice cracked and slid free all along the street at once, like a row of soldiers standing down.

Noor closed the curtain.

By midday he had enough fragments assembled to understand roughly one sentence in ten, and the sentence in ten that frightened him most was this one, which Ashen repeated whenever he paused too long, tapping her own chest and then pointing east, toward the mountains, toward wherever the Wellspring might be:

*The spring grows quiet. If no-one sings the binding, everything promised comes undone.*

And underneath that, in the primer, in Corvess's handwriting, in ink so faded it might have been written decades ago and never expected to be read:

*If you are reading this, apprentice, then either the covenant holds and none of this matters, or it is breaking and all of this matters more than anything. There is a mark. You will know it when you see it—a spiral, silver. It is called the Wellspring's clause-made-flesh. Our order had a name for such children once. We were sworn to find them, shelter them, teach them to sing the verses back before the closing took them.*

*We were also sworn never to speak of any of this where the wrong ears might hear. I have kept both oaths my whole life, which is why I am dying poor and obscure, and why I have no right to tell you that keeping secrets is not the same as keeping faith.*

*Forgive me. I ran out of time to be brave in the loud way. Be brave in whatever way is left to you.*

Noor sat with the book open on his knees for a long time. Outside, the occupied city went about its business: boots in the street, the proclamation criers droning about loyalty edicts, a neighbor's dog barking at nothing. Inside, a child slept again, breathing in meter, and the man who had spent five years building a life out of smallness held a dead mentor's confession and understood, finally, that the smallness had never been safety. It had been a debt accruing interest.

"You knew," he said to the empty room, to the eyebrowed ghost of Master Corvess. "You knew what was coming and you hid the book under herring prices and died, and I sold trinkets. Five years of trinkets."

The room, being a room, said nothing. But the signet, warm against his skin where he'd strapped it hidden beneath his sleeve, pulsed once—slow, patient, like a heartbeat waiting to be needed.

* * *

Toward evening Ashen woke and stood at the counter, watching him sort inert charms into their bins. She picked up a luck-knot—one of his better sellers, guaranteed to do absolutely nothing—and turned it over with a craftsman's frown.

"No," she said, in accented but unmistakable Common.

Noor dropped three charms.

"You—" He stared. "You can speak Common?"

She frowned, concentrating, the effort visible like weight on her shoulders. "Small... words. Since the knocking." She touched her throat. "Your door. Your house. It..." She searched for the term and gave up, offering instead, in the First Tongue, a fragment he barely caught:

*Borrowing the shape-of-your-speaking, while the walls remember me.*

"You're learning it off my house," Noor said faintly. "The language. Off the walls."

"Walls remember," Ashen agreed solemnly. Then, with the terrifying pragmatism of eight-year-olds everywhere, she set down the luck-knot, pointed at the eastern wall, and said clearly, in both tongues at once so that the candles flared and the signet burned hot against Noor's wrist:

*Soldiers come asking after singing. Two streets away, maybe three. They have a list, and the list has your street on it.*

The warmth drained out of the room. Noor looked at the door, at the CLOSED sign, at the child who had been in his care for less than a day and had already doubled the price of everything he owned—including, apparently, his neck.

"How long?" he asked.

Ashen held up her fingers. Then, helpfully: *"Soon."*

Noor thought about the aqueducts. He thought about Sefa of the Drowned Bell, who owed him a favor and a scolding in equal measure. He thought about a man who had spent five years perfecting the art of being overlooked, and about a master's last written words, and about the difference between keeping secrets and keeping faith.

He reached for his coat.

"All right," he said, mostly to himself, a little to Ashen, and perhaps a little to a dead man who smelled of fish and ink. "Be brave in whatever way is left."

He blew out the candles, lifted Ashen onto his back, and opened the door onto the rain.
