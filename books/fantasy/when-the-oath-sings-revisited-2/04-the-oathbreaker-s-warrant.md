## 4. The Oathbreaker's Warrant

Captain Ilse Varn had a ledger for everything.

She had a ledger for requisitions, a ledger for informants' payments, and a ledger—bound in gray calfskin, kept under lock in her quarters—for names. The names of singers. She wrote them in her own hand, because she did not trust clerks with the weight of it, and because her hand was very good. Her mother had said so once, before the wars took her mother and most of Ilse's opinions about what deserved praise.

The current entry was four days old, and it bothered her the way a splinter bothers a hand that works for a living. It read:

*Low quarter, Corvane. Report of singing. Source: baker's boy, age eleven, paid two coppers. Quality of report: poor.*

Poor was generous. The boy had said a woman sang on his street at night, and when pressed he admitted the woman might have been a cat, and when pressed further he admitted he hadn't actually heard anything but had seen his neighbor's shutters closed at an unusual angle, which in the boy's opinion was suspicious. Ilse had paid him anyway. Two coppers bought a lot of nothing in Corvane these days, and she preferred informants who lied cheaply to informants who lied expensively.

But then, three nights later, the second report came. And the second report was not from a child.

*Source: garrison patrol, Corvane low quarter. Two soldiers heard singing through a wall. Describe the voice as "wrong." One soldier wept without knowing why. Neither would repeat what they heard.*

Ilse read it four times by lamplight, and each time her jaw tightened a little more.

She knew what magic did to people. Everyone in the Inquisition knew; it was practically the first thing they taught you, before swordwork, before law. Magic was not a force like fire or flood. Fire burned you and was done. Flood drowned you and was done. Magic *stayed*. It got into the soft places—a man's grief, a woman's loneliness—and it made itself comfortable there, and it rearranged the furniture, and after a while you couldn't tell which wants were yours and which had been installed. That was how the wars started. Not with armies, not at first. With kings who loved their kingdoms a little too conveniently, with generals whose courage felt suspiciously effortless, with whole cities marching off to die for causes they could not afterward explain. Someone had sung them into it. That was the finding of the Commission, eleven years ago, signed and sealed: *the realm was not conquered by sorcery; it was seduced by it.*

So they had banned the singing. All of it. Every oath, every charm, every lullaby with a spellwork spine. And Ilse Varn had spent five years making the ban real, one name at a time, and she slept well. Mostly. On good nights.

She looked at the report again. *Wept without knowing why.*

That was the part that sat wrong. Because in her experience—and hers was considerable, five years and forty-one names considerable—magic made people feel *good*. That was its whole trick. It flattered. It soothed. It told you exactly what you wanted to hear in a voice you couldn't refuse. Nobody wept at magic unless they were already grieving, and grief was legal.

Singing that made hardened soldiers cry for no reason they could name didn't sound like seduction.

It sounded like something getting out of a cage.

She closed the ledger, locked it, and put the thought away where she put all her doubts—in the same drawer as her mother's letters, which she also never opened, and for much the same reason.

* * *

The low quarter of Corvane smelled, as always, of tallow smoke, wet stone, and the particular despair of soup made three times too long. Ilse walked it in plain clothes—gray wool, no insignia—with a single trooper, Corporat Dennet, who was large and slow-smiling and had once asked her, sincerely, whether the ban applied to whistling. She had said yes. He had stopped whistling forever, and she still felt vaguely guilty about it, though she'd never say so.

"Which street?" Dennet asked.

"Candlemakers' Row."

"That's a cheerful name for a place with no candles."

"They named it before the occupation." Ilse turned down the alley between a dyer's and a shuttered chandlery. "Everything here is named for what it used to be. There's a Victory Square that's a goat pen now."

Dennet chuckled, then caught himself, in case chuckling was also banned. Ilse almost smiled. Almost. She had a reputation to maintain, and the reputation was: fair, thorough, and no fun whatsoever.

Candlemakers' Row was narrow and crooked, the buildings leaning toward each other overhead like old conspirators. She counted doors as she walked. Most were shuttered against the evening cold. A few leaked lamplight. Above a shop with a sign reading *CHARMS & CURIOS — ALL ITEMS INERT, GUARANTEED*, a window stood open despite the chill, letting out the smell of tea and, faintly, of beeswax.

She stopped.

It wasn't the sign that caught her—half the quarter sold trinkets, and the guarantee was a common dodge, the retail equivalent of whistling. It was the open window. Everyone else on the street had battened down. This one household had left a gap to the night air, deliberate as a raised hand.

Or she was tired, and reading meaning into ventilation.

"Note the address," she said.

Dennet scratched it into his little book with the stub of pencil he kept behind his ear. "Charms and Curios," he read aloud. "Guaranteed inert. Well, that's honest, isn't it? Man sells things that don't do anything."

"Most honest shop in the city, probably."

"Could use more of those." Dennet said it without irony, and Ilse found herself agreeing, and then found herself annoyed at agreeing, because the whole point of her work was that honesty alone wasn't enough—the realm needed *enforcement*, needed teeth, needed someone willing to write down names. Honesty was a luxury the wars had proven the world couldn't afford unguarded.

They walked on. Behind them, the shop's door opened briefly—a small sound, hinges and cold air—and closed again. Ilse did not turn around. She noted it, in the mental ledger she kept beneath the calfskin one, and moved along.

* * *

The informant who mattered was called Master Quillon, and he received her in a back room of the Gilded Goose, surrounded by the smell of bad wine and worse ambition. He was a former guildsman himself—or so he claimed, in the way men claim things that can't be checked—and he wore his collaboration like a fine coat, always slightly too warm for the room.

"Captain Varn." He spread his hands. "You honor my humble establishment."

"Your tavern is a dump, Quillon. Sit down."

He sat. She appreciated that about him; whatever else he was, he understood arithmetic.

"You've heard the reports from the low quarter," she said. "Singing. I want specifics. Who. Where. What kind."

Quillon poured himself wine she hadn't offered, drank, and grimaced at it, which she suspected was performance. "There's talk," he said carefully, "of a child."

"A child."

"A mute child, or near enough. Seen around Candlemakers' Row in the last week. Doesn't speak—not common speech, anyway. But there are those who say—" he lowered his voice, savoring it, "—that when the child makes sounds, sounds come *true*. A dog went quiet when it passed. A broken hinge mended itself, some say. Old women cross themselves. You know how old women are."

"I know how informants are," Ilse said. "Where does this child sleep?"

"No one knows. Appears and vanishes. But—" and here Quillon leaned in, and his breath was truly terrible, "—it's been seen going into the aqueduct gate on Tanner's Lane. Twice. And Captain, the aqueducts run under half the low quarter. Under certain shops, if you take my meaning."

"Name the shops."

"Captain, I'm a careful man—"

"You're a paid man. Name the shops."

He named three. The third was Charms and Curios.

Ilse kept her face still, which was her great talent, according to no one, because she never let anyone close enough to have an opinion. Inside, something shifted—an uncomfortable settling, like ballast moving in a hull. The shop with the open window. The honest sign. The man, presumably, selling things that did nothing.

She thought: *if he's guilty, I'll find it in his ledgers, his purchases, his neighbors. Evidence first. Always evidence.* She thought: *and if he isn't guilty, and the child merely sheltered near his roof from the rain, then I will ruin him anyway, because that is what a warrant does—it doesn't ask questions, it creates answers.* She thought both thoughts in the space of a heartbeat, and then she thought a third thought, which she did not permit herself often:

*What if the child is just a child?*

She crushed that one immediately. Children were precisely what the old order had used. Child vessels, child choirs—the Commission's records were full of them, children taught songs before they could read, hollowed out and filled with purposes not their own. If anything, a child at the center of this made it *worse*, not better. Made it more urgent.

"Corporal Dennet," she said, rising, "we're going back to Candlemakers' Row."

"Now, Captain?"

"Now. Before the trail goes cold, or the child goes elsewhere, or—" she paused at the door, and heard herself finish, "—or before I talk myself out of it."

Quillon raised his glass to her back. "To justice," he said warmly.

"To evidence," she corrected, and left.

* * *

They came to the Row at dusk, when the lamps were being lit and the shadows were being fed. Ilse posted Dennet at the far end of the street with instructions to look bored—he was excellent at looking bored; it was his natural state—and she walked the Row alone, unhurried, a woman out for evening air.

The shop called Charms and Curios had its lamp lit. Through the window she could see shelves of small bright objects: rings, ribbons, carved birds, little bells. A man moved among them, youngish, dark-haired, arranging things with the fussy tenderness of someone who cared about objects more than people pretended to. He was talking—to someone she couldn't see—and even through the glass, even without hearing a word, Ilse watched his face change as he listened. Soften. Worry. Decide something.

Then the lamp went out.

Not blown—snuffed, cleanly, all at once, the way a flame dies when a hand closes over it. And in the sudden dark, Ilse felt it: a pressure against her ears, a hush like the moment before snowfall. The hair on her arms lifted. Somewhere in the walls of the Row, water moved in pipes that should have been empty, and a bell that had hung silent in the drowned chapel for years gave—one note. Faint. Wrong. Beautiful.

Dennet appeared at her elbow, having abandoned boredom entirely. "Captain? Did you hear—"

"Yes."

"What was it?"

Ilse stood very still in the middle of Candlemakers' Row, and for the first time in five years, she did not know what to write in the ledger.

Because here was the doctrine, drilled into her since her first day: *magic seduces; magic lies; magic takes your grief and furnishes it.* And what she had just felt was none of those things. It had felt like—she searched for the word and hated every candidate—like *grief acknowledged*. Like something enormous and sad saying: *I am still here. I am so sorry. I never meant for any of this.*

Doctrine had no shelf for that.

"Captain?" Dennet prompted.

"It was nothing," she said, and heard the lie come out of her own mouth, smooth and practiced, in exactly the tone she'd been trained to detect in others. "A draft. Old streets make noises."

Dennet nodded, satisfied, and Ilse Varn stood in the gathering dark and understood, with the clarity of a woman reading her own handwriting, that she had just done the thing she hunted others for. She had sung herself a small, comfortable falsehood, and she had believed it, because believing it was easier.

The warrant was already drafted in her mind. *Charms and Curios, Candlemakers' Row. Search at dawn.* She would knock on that door tomorrow with six troopers and the full weight of the law, and she would find either a smuggler or an innocent man, and either way the machine would grind forward, because the machine was all she had ever been given to believe in.

She walked back toward the garrison through streets that smelled of rain coming, and behind her, faint and impossible, she thought she heard—very far off, or perhaps only inside her own head—a child's voice, high and clear, holding a single note the way a hand holds a wounded bird.

She did not write it down.

That was the trouble with ledgers, she thought, as the first drops fell. They only hold what you're brave enough to enter.
