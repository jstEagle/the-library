## 6. Sefa of the Drowned Bell

The river told Sefa about the visitors before they ever reached the shrine.

It came as a tremor in the water, a stutter in the current where the aqueduct spilled into the old cistern beneath her bell-tower. The river had been speaking to her in its slow, patient way for forty years—ever since she'd refused the edict, refused to drown her orders and her bell along with everything else—and it rarely bothered itself with anything smaller than weather. But this morning it was practically chattering.

*Two come,* said the water. *One heavy with forgetting. One light as a struck note.*

Sefa set down her ladle. She'd been making barley soup for exactly one person for five years now, and the habit of measuring had grown so deep that the sight of two bowls side by side on her shelf felt faintly indecent.

"Light as a struck note," she repeated to the empty room. "Well. That's not how children are usually described."

She climbed the spiral stair to the belfry, where her drowned bell hung. That was what the neighbors called it—the drowned bell—because it sat half-submerged in the cistern below, sunk there by Sefa's own hands the night the edicts came down. A priestess of the River Order was required to ring her bell at dawn and dusk, calling the waters to witness whatever oaths were sworn in her parish. When the usurper's men came asking whether any such ringing still happened in Corvane's low quarter, Sefa had shown them the empty belfry and wept convincingly about the loss of her holy instrument.

Then she had gone down into the dark and hung the bell *in* the water instead of above it, because the edicts forbade singing, and a bell rung underwater makes no sound a soldier can hear.

But the river hears everything.

*They're close,* said the water, and Sefa felt it too now: footsteps on the aqueduct walkway above, hesitant, the particular shuffle of someone trying very hard to be nobody. She knew that shuffle. She'd worn it herself for years.

She went down to open the door before they could knock. Knocking, in occupied Corvane, was a luxury. Better to simply be standing in your doorway looking like you'd been expecting nothing at all.

The man on her step was thin in the way of people who have spent years folding themselves smaller, with the careful, forgettable face of a shopkeeper. He had a child by the hand—a girl, or perhaps a boy, it was hard to say under the mud-caked traveling cloak—and he was holding that small hand so tightly that the knuckles had gone white.

"Noor Delacroix," Sefa said.

He flinched as if she'd thrown the name at him rather than spoken it. "I'm sorry, madam, I think you've mistaken me for—"

"You sold my neighbor Wenna a charm against earache three winters ago. It did nothing, which is why I know you're honest." She stepped aside. "Come in before the street decides to remember you."

Noor looked at her then, really looked, and Sefa watched him do the arithmetic every hidden person does when meeting another one: *what does she cost me, what does she know, can I afford her.* She let him do it. It was only polite.

"The river sent me word," she added, because watching him flounder was getting tedious. "You might as well stop pretending the water doesn't talk. You were guild-raised. You know perfectly well what rivers are."

"I spent five years forgetting what rivers are," Noor said quietly. And there it was—not denial but confession, delivered like a man setting down something heavy to see whether his arms remembered how to be empty. "It turns out forgetting is harder than it looks."

"Everything worth doing is." Sefa waved them inside. "Soup first. Mysteries after. I find they go down better in that order."

*

The child would not eat until Sefa did.

This was the first thing Sefa learned about the little one, standing in her kitchen while steam curled off the barley pot: the child took a bowl, held it, and waited—with the absolute, unblinking patience of a stone—until Sefa lifted her own spoon. Then, and only then, the small mouth opened and accepted food as if accepting a formal contract.

"Interesting table manners," Sefa murmured.

"They came with the mark," Noor said. He was sitting rigidly, hands wrapped around a bowl he hadn't touched. "Look at her wrist."

Sefa looked.

On the child's inner wrist, just above the pulse point, was a mark no ink had made and no scar had formed: a spiral of pale silver lines, fine as frost on a windowpane, coiling inward toward a center that seemed—if Sefa tilted her head—to keep receding. She knew the shape. Every priestess of the River Order knew the shape, though most of them had only seen it in illuminated manuscripts, drawn by dead hands for dead congregations.

It was the sigil of the Wellspring. The source-head. The place where, in the old days, the covenant between the mortal realms and their guardian spirits had been sung into being—and renewed, every generation, by voices gathered from every corner of the map.

Sefa put down her spoon very slowly.

"That's not possible," she said. "That mark hasn't appeared on living skin since my grandmother's grandmother rang bells for a world that listened."

"It appeared on her the night she knocked on my door," Noor said. "Or—it was already there, maybe. Hidden. And then she spoke, and—" His voice did something complicated. "She speaks the First Tongue, Sefa. Not fragments. Not the liturgy-words we all half-know. She *speaks* it. Like it's the only language there is."

At the sound of his voice saying *First Tongue*, the child looked up. Her eyes were an unsettling color, gray shot through with threads of green, like water over stones, and they fixed on Sefa with an attention that made the hair on the priestess's arms stand up.

Then the child spoke.

The sounds were nothing like common speech. They moved in long vowels and falling cadences, syllables that seemed to arrive already harmonized with themselves, and Sefa—who had spent forty years listening to a river, who had once been trained in the seven modes of oath-singing before the Order scattered—felt each phrase land somewhere behind her sternum and resonate.

"She says—" Sefa closed her eyes, translating not with her mind but with the part of her that heard the bell underwater. "She says the well is hungry. She says she has been walking a long time looking for someone who remembers the second verse." She opened her eyes. "And she says your name wrong, Noor Delacroix. She calls you *Nohr-who-hid*. Is that accurate?"

"It's devastatingly accurate," Noor said.

The child—Ashen, Noor called her; Sefa would learn later that he'd chosen the name himself, from the ash-gray color of her cloak, because she had offered no other—reached across the table and laid one small hand flat on the wood between them. On her wrist, the silver spiral brightened, and Sefa felt the cistern below them shiver. Somewhere in the dark water, her drowned bell swung without being touched, and a note rolled through the stone foundations of the shrine: low, round, unmistakable.

Not a sound the soldiers upstairs could hear. But every singer in Corvane who still had ears for it would have felt that note pass through their teeth.

Sefa stood up so fast her chair skidded.

"Take it back," she said sharply. "Ashen—child—whatever your clause-name is—stop it, rein it in, *now*."

The spiral dimmed. The bell stilled. In the sudden quiet, Sefa pressed both palms flat against her kitchen table and breathed.

"Do you understand what you're carrying?" she said to Noor, and her voice shook more than she liked. "That note went through the whole water-table. Through the aqueducts, the wells, the rain-butts, the gutters. There are perhaps six people left in this city who could feel it—but Captain Ilse Varn keeps a ledger of exactly those six people, and her scryers have been listening to the waterways for a year. You didn't bring me a child tonight, Noor. You brought me a bell that rings *itself*, in an occupied city, and you want soup?"

Noor had gone the color of tallow. "I didn't know it would—I thought the mark was dormant—"

"It isn't dormant. It's *starving*." Sefa sank back into her chair. "Which brings us to the part of this conversation I've been dreading."

*

Here is what Sefa knew, and had known for years, and had told no one, because telling anyone would have killed them:

The covenant was not dead.

Everyone said it was dead. The edicts said so. The usurper's priests said so, loudly and often, in squares hung with the banners of the new order. Even the scattered survivors of the old orders mostly believed it—believed that when the last Wellspring choir had been broken, twenty-two years ago, the great song had ended and the guardian spirits had gone silent forever, leaving the mortal realms alone with their kings and their wars.

But Sefa had a bell underwater, and a river that talked, and forty years of dawn vigils in which to notice the truth:

A covenant is not a machine. It doesn't stop working when you break it. It's a promise, and promises don't die—they starve. They grow thin and weak and strange. They begin to whisper instead of sing.

The guardian spirits hadn't departed. They were starving in the deep places, sustained only by the few scattered singers who still hummed the old modes without knowing what they were doing—a fisherman's wife in the marshes whose lullabies kept one river-spirit alive; a dying bell-founder up north who tapped out oath-rhythms on his anvil because his hands wouldn't forget; children everywhere, always children, who sang nonsense songs in playgrounds that were, syllable for syllable, degraded verses of the First Tongue.

Scattered singers. Who no longer knew each other. Who had been *made* not to know each other—by fear, by the edicts, by twenty-two years of careful forgetting exactly like the forgetting Noor had practiced so diligently in his little shop.

"And here's the cruelty of it," Sefa said, pacing her narrow kitchen while Noor sat transfixed and Ashen watched her with those water-colored eyes. "A covenant doesn't hold with one voice. It never did. It takes a choir. It takes voices that trust each other enough to harmonize—which means it cannot be reforged in secret, by one clever person hiding in a cellar. It can only be reforged by people willing to *find each other*. To stand in a room together and be counted." She stopped pacing. "Do you see the trap? The usurper doesn't even need to hunt us efficiently. He only needs to make finding each other so terrifying that we do it for him. We enforce our own scattering."

Noor was quiet for a long moment. Ashen reached over and touched his hand, and Sefa saw him take a breath that shuddered on the way in.

"There's a primer," he said finally. "My master's. Hidden inside a book of trade tariffs, of all the cowardly places. It has names in the margins. I thought they were just... old friends he missed."

"They'll be singers," Sefa said. "People your master trusted with the one thing that gets you killed." She sat back down across from him. "So. Let's do the terrifying thing. Let's be found."

*

She gave him the list at dusk, written on river-reed paper in ink she mixed fresh from oak gall, because paper could be burned and should be, promptly, after memorization.

Three names. Three names beyond herself, because a choir needed more than a priestess gone gray and a shopkeeper gone timid and a child who was less a person, perhaps, than a clause of something vast wearing a person's small shape.

"Brammel Ostwyn," she said, tapping the first line. "Was chief cartographer to the Vesper Guild, back when the Guild admitted it had a purpose. If anyone alive knows where the old song-lines ran through this country, it's him. Assuming he's still him." She looked at Noor over the rim of her cup. "He erased himself, Noor. Not hid—*erased*. Scrubbed his own memory of every magical landmark he ever charted, burned his true charts, took a new name in the tanners' quarter. What your master's primer restores, Brammel cut out on purpose. Be gentle. Or be prepared for him to hate you for handing him back his own life."

Noor nodded slowly, committing the name to memory the way guild apprentices were taught—once, deeply, with the whole body.

"Second. Odrik Vael." Sefa's finger hovered over the second name without quite touching it. "Singer of the eastern mode. Good man, once. Best pure voice I ever heard outside the choir itself." She paused, and the pause had weight, and she let Noor feel all of it. "He lives in Harrowgate now. Which is to say, he lives comfortably. Which is to say—" she met Noor's eyes, "—someone in Harrowgate is feeding names to the Inquisition, and Odrik's family eats well."

The fire popped. Below them, the river murmured on, indifferent to human treachery as rivers always were.

"You're certain?" Noor asked.

"I'm certain of the pattern. A singer who should be dead or fled, living visibly, prosperously, in a garrison town, for five years? Either he's braver than any of us—and I'd have heard him singing—or he bought his safety the way safety gets bought these days." Sefa's mouth twisted. "But hear me, Noor Delacroix, because this is the lesson your five years of hiding should have taught you better than anyone: certainty is a luxury of people who aren't starving. Go to him anyway. Watch him. And decide with your eyes open whether a traitor's voice can still serve the covenant—because it may be that we don't get to choose our choir. We get who's left."

"And the third?"

Sefa turned the paper around and showed him the third line. She had written it small.

"Sefa of the Drowned Bell," Noor read, and blinked. "You're sending me to yourself?"

"I'm telling you where I'll be," she corrected. "Because I am not going anywhere, shopkeeper. This shrine has a bell in its basement and a parish of smugglers and washerwomen who still cross themselves the old way, and if the covenant is going to be reforged, some part of it will be reforged *here*, in the dark, in the wet, where the usurper's scryers have never thought to listen." She smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "Besides. Someone has to stay behind who knows which of you comes back changed."

Ashen, who had been silent through all of it, suddenly reached out and caught Sefa's wrist—her right wrist, the priestess's wrist, where forty years ago a younger woman had borne a matching mark, before burning it away with a hot iron the night the edicts came. The child's fingers rested on the old burn-scar, and her gray-green eyes went wide, and she said something soft and sorrowful in the First Tongue, three falling syllables that needed no translation at all.

"Yes," Sefa said roughly. "I was one of us. And yes, it hurt. Eat your soup."

Noor rose to leave, folding the reed paper into his coat, and at the door he paused.

"Why help us?" he asked. "You've survived five years by staying small. I know exactly how much that costs. Why throw it away for a stranger and a child?"

Sefa looked past him, through the little window, at the dark aqueduct where the water ran on beneath the soldiers' feet, unseen, unheard, keeping the city alive anyway.

"Because I've been ringing a bell no one could hear for five years," she said, "and I am *tired*, Noor. Go find your cartographer. And when you find Odrik Vael—" her face hardened, "—remember that hope is exactly the shape of a trap, and walk carefully."

After they'd gone, she descended the spiral stair to the cistern and put her hand flat against the cold bronze of the drowned bell.

"Well?" she asked the water.

*Hungry,* said the river. *Getting hungrier. And something else.*

"What else?"

The current hesitated, which rivers almost never do.

*Soldiers,* said the water at last. *Walking the aqueduct road. Asking after a shopkeeper and a small stranger. They are two streets behind, and they are in no hurry, because they already know where everyone goes.*
