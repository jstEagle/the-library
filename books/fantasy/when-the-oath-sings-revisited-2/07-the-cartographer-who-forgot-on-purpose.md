## 7. The Cartographer Who Forgot on Purpose

Brammel Hask lived above a shop that sold lamps in the Tallowmarket district of Corvane, which was fitting, because the man himself had spent thirty years making sure no one could find anything.

Noor knew this because he had spent four days finding him.

It had not been easy. Sefa's list had given him a name — *Brammel* — and a riddle instead of an address: *"He lives where the maps are wrong."* Noor had spent the first day staring at that sentence over a bowl of gray barley soup while Ashen sat across from him, eating with the solemn concentration of a child who treated every meal as though it might be the last one served anywhere. On the second day, Noor had bought three maps from three different chart-sellers and laid them side by side on his shop floor, and there it was: the same street corner rendered three different ways, each map placing the lamp-shop of Tallowmarket at a slightly different distance from the canal. Not sloppy. Deliberate. A man who moved his own house a hundred paces in every direction depending on who was looking.

"He's hiding," Ashen had said, in the First Tongue, pointing at the discrepancy with a jam-sticky finger.

"Everyone's hiding," Noor had replied. "That's the whole city. It's practically the municipal motto."

But only one kind of person hid by moving landmarks, and only one guild had ever taught its members to do it so beautifully.

So now, on a bright cold morning with frost still clinging to the awnings, Noor climbed the narrow stair beside the lamp-shop with Ashen's small hand tucked in his, and knocked on a door painted the exact shade of nothing — that particular beige that every eye slides past without registering.

The door opened a hand's width. One eye appeared in the gap. It was a good eye, sharp and brown and currently doing a great deal of rapid calculation, most of which Noor could read plainly: *guild-signet, guild posture, guild trouble, run.*

"I'm sorry," said the eye's owner, "you have the wrong door."

"I don't," Noor said pleasantly. "But I admire the confidence. Most people check first."

"I sell lamps."

"Downstairs. You live above a lamp-shop. Which is either sentimental or ironic, and from what I remember of you, Master Brammel, you were never sentimental."

The eye blinked. The door did not open further, but it did not close either, and Noor counted that as progress. Beside him, Ashen was studying the doorframe with the intense interest children reserve for things adults wish they wouldn't.

"My name is Corvin Tanner," the man behind the door said. "I've been Corvin Tanner for eleven years. I mend wicks. I have a bad knee and a fondness for pickled herring and I have never in my life drawn a map."

"That last part is the most impressive lie I've heard all year," Noor said. "And I once watched a man claim, under Inquisition questioning, that his own grandmother was a chair."

Silence. Then, grudgingly, the door opened another few inches, revealing a man who had been broad once and was now mostly elbows and weathered skin, with a beard trimmed with the geometric precision of someone who could not help treating even his own face like a survey project. Brammel Hask looked at Noor for a long moment, then down at Ashen, and whatever he saw in the child's face made something flicker behind his eyes — a flinch, quickly buried.

"You shouldn't be here," he said quietly. "Whatever this is, whoever sent you — take the child and go home. There's nothing here but a bad knee and herring."

"The river-priestess sent me. Sefa, of the Drowned Bell."

Brammel's jaw tightened. "Then she's a fool, and so are you, and I'm closing this door now."

"Bram—"

"*Corvin.*"

"—Corvin," Noor amended, holding up both hands in surrender. "Fine. Corvin. May I at least say my piece before you close it? It'll take less time than the herring speech."

The old man sighed the sigh of a man who already knew he was going to lose this argument and was simply negotiating the terms of his defeat. He stepped back and let them in.

* * *

The room above the lamp-shop was immaculate. That was the first thing Noor noticed, and the thing that hurt the most. Every surface clean, every object squared to its neighbors, everything arranged with the loving precision of a mind that had once held entire mountain ranges in its head and now had nothing left to organize but teacups. There were charts on the walls — honest ones, mundane ones, trade routes and tide tables and a lovely rendering of the Corvane harbor — and not one of them, Noor would have bet his own life on it, contained a single error.

Which meant the man had stopped being a cartographer entirely. A true guild mapmaker couldn't draw a straight road without some part of him wanting to bend it around a truth no one else could see.

"You erased them all," Noor said. It wasn't a question. He walked slowly along the wall of honest charts, hands clasped behind his back so he wouldn't touch anything. "Every landmark. The Singing Stones of Vaelmoor. The Hollow Chapel. The nine hidden springs along the Pilgrim's Road. The Wellspring itself." He turned. "You didn't just leave them off your maps, Brammel. You cut them out of yourself. I can see the holes from here."

Brammel had seated himself heavily by the cold hearth, and for a moment he looked every one of his sixty-odd years. "You want to know how it's done?" he said. His voice was flat, conversational, terrible. "You sit down with your master's knife — the silver one, the one we used for vellum — and you hold your own true name in your mouth, and you think very hard about the thing you want gone. And then you cut. Not the skin. Deeper. And when you're finished, you can look at the place where the Wellspring is — I mean physically stand on the hill above it — and see nothing. Grass. Rocks. A nice view." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Noor had ever seen, because it was proud. "Eleven years, boy. Eleven years and the Inquisition has walked past this door four times, and I made tea for two of them. Do you know why? Because there is nothing here to find. I checked. I'm the one thing in this city that's genuinely empty."

Ashen, who had been drifting along the edge of the room touching nothing, stopped at the hearth and looked up at Brammel with an expression Noor was beginning to learn — the tilted head, the listening stillness, as though the child heard something underneath the words.

"You're not empty," Ashen said, in the common tongue, carefully, the way one handles borrowed tools. "You're quiet. Like a bell that's been wrapped in wool."

Brammel stared at the child. "What did it say?"

"It said you're not empty," Noor translated softly. "It said you're quiet. Like a bell wrapped in wool." He crouched down so he was level with the old man, and let the humor fall away from his voice, because there were moments — he'd learned this much in five years of hiding — when jokes were just another way of running. "Sefa says the covenant isn't dead. It's starving. There are singers left, scattered, and none of us know each other anymore, and the armies are coming, and somewhere in that gray column is an Inquisition captain who has gotten very, very good at finding people exactly like us. Exactly like you." He paused. "I need a mapmaker, Brammel. Not the one who sells lamps. The one who drew the Pilgrim's Road from memory at age nineteen because the official survey missed half of it."

"I don't remember drawing any such thing," Brammel said instantly.

"No," Noor agreed. "You don't. That's rather the point."

For a long moment the only sound was the lamplight shop below, muffled footsteps, a customer's voice, the ordinary commerce of an occupied city pretending very hard to be an unoccupied one.

"I can't give you what I don't have," Brammel said at last, and there was something raw under the flatness now. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? It's not stubbornness. I would help you if I could. I liked the guild. I loved the maps. But I took a knife to my own memory to keep the people in this building safe — the widow downstairs, her son, the boy who delivers my ink — and what I cut, I cut *clean*. There's no secret drawer in my head, Noor. I made sure of it. I was thorough. It's the one piece of work from those years I can still feel proud of." He laughed, short and bitter. "The great cartographer's masterpiece: a perfect blank."

Ashen crossed the room.

Noor didn't stop the child. He'd learned by now that stopping Ashen was like trying to stop weather. The child stood in front of Brammel's chair, small and grave, and reached out and took the old man's scarred hands in both small ones.

"Ashen—" Noor started.

Brammel flinched. "Child, don't—"

And Ashen spoke.

Not the common tongue. The First Tongue — the language oaths were sung in before anyone thought to write them down, the language Noor had spent five years deliberately forgetting and had failed to forget even one word of. And what Ashen said was a single word, a name, spoken the way names were meant to be spoken in that tongue: not stated but *sung*, low and clear, each syllable placed like a stone in a wall.

Brammel Hask's true name.

The effect was not dramatic. That was somehow the worst part. The old man didn't cry out or clutch his head. He simply went still — utterly still, the stillness of a clock whose pendulum has been caught mid-swing — and then his breath came back into him all at once, ragged, and he made a sound that Noor had never heard a grown man make: a small, lost noise, like a child waking in a house that isn't his.

"Oh," Brammel whispered. "Oh, no. Oh, *no*, I had a *wife*—"

"Brammel—" Noor lunged forward.

"She died. Year of the Gray Frost. I cut her out too, because it hurt too much to carry her and hide at the same time, and I told myself it was mercy, I told myself—" His hands were shaking in Ashen's grip. Tears ran freely down the precise geometry of his beard. "The Singing Stones. I can hear them again. I can hear the *road*, the whole road, it's singing under the ground like it always used to, I'd forgotten roads could sing—" He looked up at Noor, and his eyes were wet and blazing and more alive than they had been since Noor had walked through the door. "You absolute idiot. You brought a living clause of the covenant into the house of a man who spent eleven years building himself into a blank, and you let it *speak*."

"I did consider mentioning it," Noor offered weakly. "In my defense, you kept interrupting with the herring."

Brammel laughed — a cracked, wet, astonished laugh — and then he pulled his hands free of Ashen's gently, reverently, the way one sets down something holy, and stood. He stood the way he must have stood at a drafting table thirty years ago: spine straight, shoulders square, a man remembering his own dimensions.

"Give me a day," he said. "There are things in this house I need to burn, and things I need to pack, and there's a widow downstairs who thinks I'm her late husband's cousin, and she deserves better than a note pinned to the door." He wiped his face with his sleeve, leaving a wet streak through the dust of eleven careful years. "And Noor — the maps you need don't exist anymore. Not on paper. They exist *here*." He tapped his temple, and his hand only trembled a little. "It's coming back. All of it. Give me until tonight and I'll draw you the Pilgrim's Road with every spring and every hollow and every place the Inquisition's wards go blind, from memory, the way I did when I was nineteen."

"And the third name on Sefa's list?" Noor asked carefully. "Odrik. Do you remember him?"

Something crossed Brammel's restored face — quick, complicated, gone before Noor could read it. "I remember Odrik," he said. "Ask me again tomorrow. Tonight I'd like to spend a few hours being furious at myself in private. It's a luxury I haven't had in eleven years, and honestly, I've earned it."

* * *

They spent the day helping him burn.

It was stranger work than Noor had expected. Brammel burned almost nothing — a false ledger, letters addressed to Corvin Tanner, a single drawer of charts so aggressively mundane that Noor suspected they'd been drawn specifically to bore any Inquisitor who found them. What he packed was another matter: pens and silver knives and rolls of the good vellum, a brass instrument Noor didn't recognize and Brammel kissed before wrapping it, and — wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of a trunk, where no blank man should ever have known to keep it — a map.

Not a finished map. A fragment. A corner of heavy paper, water-stained, showing a stretch of coastline that Noor did not recognize and that hummed faintly against his guild-trained fingers like a struck string left ringing.

"What is this?"

Brammel took it from him with unexpected gentleness. "The last thing I drew before I started cutting. My master's last commission." He was quiet a moment. "He told me to memorize it and destroy it. I destroyed the *copy*." His mouth twisted. "Turns out I wasn't as thorough as I thought. Or maybe some part of me — the part that wasn't busy being a blank — knew a day would come when someone knocked on my door and refused to be turned away by a man selling lamps." He looked down at Ashen, who was solemnly helping fold spare shirts with the gravity of a tiny quartermaster. "Where did you find it? Truly. Where does a child like this come from?"

"I don't know yet," Noor admitted. "Sefa might. Or the next name on the list. Or—" He gestured vaguely at the child, at the mark beneath Ashen's collar that glowed faintly when the First Tongue filled the air. "Or maybe it tells us itself, eventually. In its own time. In its own language."

"Mm." Brammel sealed the trunk. Outside, the light had gone long and amber; the Tallowmarket lamps were being lit one by one below, and for the first time in eleven years their keeper was leaving them to someone else's care. "One condition," the old man said. "When this is done — however it ends — you bring me back here. I want to reopen the shop. Same beige door. I've grown attached to being invisible." He glanced at Ashen, and his voice roughened. "Well. I *was* attached to it. I suspect I'm about to learn what everything costs when you stop hiding. Consider it tuition."

Noor smiled and shook the offered hand. "Welcome back to the guild, Master Cartographer."

"Don't." Brammel's grip tightened, hard, and his eyes shone. "*Don't* say that yet. Say it when we've done the thing. Say it standing somewhere the covenant can hear." He released Noor's hand and turned to the window, to the darkening streets of the city that had nearly succeeded in unmaking him, and Noor saw him straighten as though bracing against a wind only he could feel — the wind of everything returning, every erased landmark singing its way back into his blood.

Behind them, unnoticed by either man, Ashen had gone very still by the hearth, head tilted, listening to something neither of them could hear — and the child's small face, for the first time since arriving at Noor's door, showed fear.
