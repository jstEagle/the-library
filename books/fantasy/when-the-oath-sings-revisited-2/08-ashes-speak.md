## 8. Ashes Speak

Ashen wakes before the others because the house is singing.

The house is old and crooked and it hums in the floorboards where the water runs beneath the city. The water remembers being rain. Rain remembers being cloud. Clouds remember nothing, because clouds are very young, but the water underneath is old-old-old, older than the gray city on top of it, and when Ashen puts one ear against the floorboard the song comes up through the wood like warmth through a blanket.

*Down-down-down,* sings the water. *Carry-carry-carry.*

Ashen carries too. That is what Ashen is for.

The man called Noor sleeps in the small room with his boots by the door, lined up straight like soldiers who have given up. His song is quiet when he sleeps. It is a folded song, tucked into itself like a letter no one will send. Even awake he keeps it folded. He has kept it folded for so long that Ashen thinks the folds might be part of the song now, creases where the music was pressed flat, year after year after year.

Humans are like that. Humans are instruments that learned to be quiet.

Ashen sits by the window and listens to the city wake.

The gray city is full of half-silent instruments. That is not an insult. Ashen's teacher-of-before — the one who was a bell, the one who was closed — used to say that every person is a verse, even the ones who cannot sing. A potter across the way hums three notes while she works, the same three notes, over and over, and her hands are the rest of the song. A soldier marches past with boots that go *stamp-stamp-stamp*, and under the stamping there is a small tired melody that wants to be a lullaby but has been made to march instead. A baker's boy whistles badly. Very badly. So badly that the bread seems embarrassed.

But none of them know they are instruments. None of them can hear their own verses. They walk around inside their songs like people walking around inside a house with no windows, carrying lamps, bumping into furniture.

Ashen used to think everyone could hear it. Before. When there were others like Ashen, other clauses, other living pieces of the great covenant that held the world's promises together like stitching holds a coat together. There had been nine of them. Ashen remembers nine voices braided into one rope of sound, and the rope went down into the Wellspring and came up again holding everything: the oaths of kings, the vows of rivers, the promise the mountains made to stand still, the promise the seasons made to come back.

Then the closing began.

Ashen does not like to remember the closing. But remembering is what Ashen is. A clause is a piece of the promise, and a promise is a thing remembered out loud, so Ashen remembers whether Ashen wants to or not.

*

The others were closed one at a time, which was the cruel part. If they had all been closed together, the last would not have had to listen.

The fourth clause was called Vess. Vess sang low, like roots. The men in gray came with an iron frame that looked like a door standing alone in a field, and they put Vess inside it, and the door drank the song. Not killed it. Drank it. Ashen could still feel Vess afterward, far away, a muffled humming inside the iron, like a bee in a jar, like a heart in a chest that had forgotten why it beat.

*Why do they close us?* Ashen asked the sixth clause, whose name was a chord and cannot be written here.

*Because we are proof,* said the chord. *We are the receipt for everything they promised and broke. No one likes a receipt.*

That was funny. Ashen laughed, a small bright sound, and then the men in gray heard it from two fields away and came running, and the chord had to hide Ashen under a fallen bell until the boots went past. The chord did not laugh. The chord was already learning to be quiet, and a clause that learns to be quiet is a clause beginning to close itself, which is worse than any iron door.

The last Ashen saw of the others was seven shapes in seven iron frames, carried away on wagons toward the gray capital, each one humming faintly, each one drinking silence.

Then Ashen ran. Ashen ran for a long time. Ashen does not count time the way humans do, so it was either one winter or forty, and both are true if you say them in the First Tongue.

And now there is this crooked house, and the folded man, and the floorboards singing *carry-carry-carry*, and Ashen sits at the window and practices being a person, which is harder than it looks.

*

Being a person means wearing shoes. Ashen understands the theory of shoes. The practice is an argument.

"You have to wear them," Noor says, kneeling, holding out two leather things shaped like disappointment. "There's broken glass in the low quarter. And the stones are cold."

Stones are not cold. Stones sing very slowly, so slowly that humans mistake the slowness for silence, and a slow song feels cool against skin, that is all. But explaining this requires words, and words in the common tongue are heavy and square, like bricks, and Ashen's mouth was built for rounder things.

"Shoes," Ashen says, trying the brick. It comes out mostly right.

"Yes. Shoes." Noor's song flickers — a little spike of something warm and sad mixed together, the flavor of a man watching a child do a normal thing and grieving that it is remarkable. Ashen has learned to read the flickers. The folded man flickers constantly. He is the loudest quiet instrument Ashen has ever heard.

Ashen puts on the shoes. They are stiff and wrong and make Ashen's feet feel like they are lying about themselves. But Noor's song eases when they are on, so Ashen keeps them on, because keeping the folded man eased is becoming one of Ashen's favorite tasks, along with listening to the water and counting the cracks in the ceiling (eleven; the eleventh is shaped like a river, which is correct).

Breakfast is bread and honey. The honey sings a tiny golden note, the only honest food in the house. The bread is silent, having been baked by a man who hates his job, and you can taste the hating.

"Brammel comes today," Noor says. He says it carefully, the way he says most things, testing each word for traps before letting it out of his mouth. "The mapmaker. He's... he knew my master. He may help us."

*Help.* Another brick word. Ashen turns it over. Help is what clauses do without deciding to. Help is the whole shape of a clause, like wetness is the whole shape of water. But humans must decide to help, and deciding looks exhausting. Noor has been deciding for days, and the folds in his song have gotten deeper.

"Ashen," Noor says, and then stops. He does this often. Starts a sentence and abandons it in the middle, like a man crossing a bridge that keeps creaking. "When you... spoke to me. The first night. You said my name. My true name. Do you understand what that did? What that *does*?"

Ashen understands. Saying a true name unfolds a song. It takes the letter out of its envelope. It is not magic the way the gray men mean magic, with fire and shouting. It is more like opening a window in a house that has been shut for years — suddenly all the stale air rushes out and the room remembers weather.

But explaining needs many bricks, and Ashen only has a few, so Ashen does the other thing instead. Ashen reaches across the table and touches Noor's hand, two fingers, light as a moth landing, and lets him hear one second of himself unfolded.

Noor goes very still. His breath catches in the middle like a hiccup that changed its mind. For one second he hears his own song uncreased — young and bright and stubborn, a song that never actually stopped, only waited — and then Ashen pulls back and folds it gently closed again, because leaving it open without permission would be rude, and clauses are never rude. Clauses are *made of* permission. Every promise is.

"Ah," says Noor, in a voice like a man who has walked into a door made of memory.

"Hungry," says Ashen, pointing at the honey, because even miracles need breakfast after.

*

Brammel arrives in the afternoon, and Brammel is the strangest instrument Ashen has ever heard, and Ashen once heard a mountain.

He is old and round and he moves like a man made of careful knots. And his song — his song has holes in it. Not folded, like Noor's. *Holes*. Cut out. Chunks missing, edges smooth, the way a field looks after someone has dug out every stone. He cut them out himself. Ashen can see the knife-marks in the music. He took his own memories of the guild, of maps, of names, and he cut them away and buried them somewhere, and now he walks around with gaps where his life used to be, whistling cheerful nonsense into the empty spaces.

It is the saddest cleverest thing Ashen has ever heard.

The adults talk for a long time. Their talk is all bricks: *edicts*, *warrants*, *the aqueduct*, *she'll never agree*. Ashen stops listening to the bricks and listens to the music underneath, which says: *I am afraid. I miss him. I want to be asked. Please ask me properly.*

So when there is a pause, Ashen climbs down from the chair, walks across the floor — *clump clump clump*, stupid shoes — and stands in front of Brammel and looks up at the holes in his song.

Brammel looks down. His eyes are the color of old maps.

"Well then," he says. "And who might—"

Ashen says his name.

Not *Brammel*. Anyone can say Brammel. Ashen says the name underneath, the guild-name, the mapmaker's-name, the name his master gave him over a table covered in ink, the name he cut out of himself and buried — and Ashen digs it up with four syllables and hands it back to him like a coin found in a garden.

The holes fill.

It happens fast, faster than Ashen expected, faster than is polite, and Brammel makes a sound like a chair breaking and sits down hard on the chair anyway, which is impressive. His song roars open, all of it, every cut-away chunk coming home at once — the ink, the roads, the towers, the master's laugh, the day the edicts came, the day he burned his charts, the day he chose to forget — and he holds his head in his hands and shakes, and Noor starts forward, alarmed, and Ashen raises one hand the way the bell-teacher used to, *wait, wait, some songs must finish*.

Brammel cries for a while. Good cries. The kind where the song gets louder and cleaner with each one, like a stream clearing after silt.

"I erased—" he says finally, wetly, laughing at the same time, which humans can do simultaneously, another thing they don't know they can do. "I erased forty years. I did it so *carefully*. I made a *discipline* of it. And it was in a *child* the whole time. It was in a child the whole time and I've been eating supper next to it."

"The whole city is full of it," Noor says quietly. "We just stopped hearing."

Ashen nods. That is exactly it. The gray men did not kill the covenant. They could not; you cannot kill a promise, only close the throats that speak it. So the covenant went underground, into floorboards and honey and bad whistling and the slow songs of stones, and it has been waiting, starving, patient, humming *down-down-down, carry-carry-carry*, waiting for someone to put an ear to the floor.

Waiting for a clause that got away.

*

Night comes. Noor and Brammel argue cheerfully about roads, which appears to be how mapmakers flirt with friendship. Sefa's name comes up, and Odrik's, and a list like a rope being thrown across a gap.

Ashen lies on the pallet by the window and should sleep. Sleep is when clauses mend. Instead Ashen listens.

The city below is louder than usual. Boots. Many boots, marching in patterns that are not patrols, patrols march loose, these march *tight*, tight as stitching, and stitched marching means orders, and orders mean the gray men are looking for something in the low quarter.

Looking for singing, probably. There was singing. There was Ashen, on the night of the storm, saying a true name on a doorstep with the signet glowing like a small brave sun. Sound carries. Sound always carries. That is the whole trouble with sound and also the whole point.

Far off, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of almost-hearing, there is something else. Iron. Many iron frames on many wagons, rolling closer through the dark, and inside them, muffled, patient, heartbreaking:

Humming.

Seven notes, muffled, familiar. Or six. Ashen counts twice and cannot tell, and the not-telling is worse than either answer, because it means the ones that are left are getting quieter, and quiet clauses are clauses being closed, and somewhere in the gray capital or on the gray roads the doors are drinking and drinking and drinking.

Ashen presses one ear to the floorboard.

*Down-down-down,* sings the water. *Carry-carry-carry.*

"Carry," Ashen agrees, in the First Tongue, in the dark, to nobody. "I know. I'm the last one carrying. Don't rush me."

Below the window, in the street, torchlight moves where torchlight shouldn't. A wagon creaks to a stop at the corner. Someone dismounts — a rider with a song like a ledger, straight lines and tallied columns, a song that believes every number in itself — and calls out a question to the sleeping houses in a voice trained not to care about the answers.

In the next room, Noor's folded song snaps awake.

Ashen pulls the blanket up to the chin and does the hardest thing a clause can do, harder than singing, harder than remembering:

Ashen pretends to be only a child, asleep, harmless, ordinary — and listens to the boots come up the street, one verse at a time, toward the crooked house that hums.
