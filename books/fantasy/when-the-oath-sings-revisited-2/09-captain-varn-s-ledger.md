## 9. Captain Varn's Ledger

The interrogation room in the Corvane garrison had once been a counting-house, and it still smelled faintly of ink and old arithmetic. Captain Ilse Varn liked that. Numbers were honest. A number never wept, never lied, never sang.

She sat at the scarred oak table with her ledger open before her — a thick, leather-bound book with a brass clasp, its pages ruled in her own careful hand. On the left column: names. On the right: dates, charges, dispositions. Forty-one entries since she'd taken the warrant. Forty-one lines of tidy script, each one ending in the same word, written small and neat as a merchant's tally:

*Executed.*

She did not think of them as people, mostly. People made the arithmetic go soft.

The prisoner across from her was making the arithmetic go very soft indeed.

He was perhaps fifty, a fiddler by the look of his hands — knuckles swollen, fingertips calloused in the wrong places for anything but strings. They'd pulled him out of a tavern in the Tanner's Row district at midnight because three witnesses swore they heard him *singing* over his ale, and not the sort of singing that got a man bought another round. The wrong kind. The kind the edicts existed for.

His name was Pellam Ostrow. He hadn't denied it. That was the first strange thing.

"You understand," Varn said, dipping her pen, "that I'm required to record your statement. Whatever you say here goes into the record. It can't be taken back."

"Good." The fiddler smiled. He had a gap where a tooth should be, and the smile was somehow worse for it — warm, unhurried, like a man greeting an old friend across a market stall. "I've been saying things into rooms for five years, Captain. It'll be nice to have one written down."

Varn wrote: *Prisoner cooperative. Demeanor: insolent.* Then she crossed out *insolent* and wrote *calm*, because insolent wasn't true and she had a private rule about her ledger: it could be merciless, but it would never be false. Her superiors thought this was sentimentality. She thought it was the only thing separating her book from a butcher's tally.

"The witnesses state you were singing words in no known tongue."

"I was singing in a very known tongue," Pellam said. "Just not to you."

"Name it."

"I'd rather show you. May I?" He raised his manacled hands a few inches, an odd little gesture, like a student asking leave to sharpen a pencil.

Varn considered. The room had two guards at the door and iron on his wrists and a warded circle chalked into the floorboards beneath his chair — standard procedure, drawn by a guild-trained scribe who now worked for the Inquisition at triple his old salary. If the man could do anything dangerous from inside all that, then the edicts had been doomed from the start, and she might as well know it now.

"Proceed."

Pellam cleared his throat. And he sang.

It was four notes. Four notes, low and unhurried, in a language that sounded less like sound than like *weight* — the way a door sounds when it settles into its frame after a hundred years. Varn felt it in her sternum rather than her ears. The lamp flame leaned, very slightly, toward him.

Then he stopped, and the room was just a room again, smelling of ink and arithmetic, and the guards at the door were looking at each other with the particular blankness of men deciding what not to report.

"What," Varn said, when she trusted her voice, "was that?"

"A promise." Pellam flexed his fingers as if the song had been a stretch. "An old one. The kind two neighbors used to swear to each other about well-water and boundary stones, back when oaths were sung instead of signed, because a sung oath can't be forged. You can copy a signature, Captain. You can't counterfeit a voice given freely. The covenant hears the difference."

"The covenant."

"That's what my teachers called it. You'd call it sorcery, I expect. It isn't. Sorcery is power. This is—" he hunted for the word, tapping his temple— "*law*. Older law than your usurper's crown. Older than any crown. When the realms were young and full of things with too many teeth, our ancestors made an agreement with the guardian spirits: mortals would keep faith, speak truth, honor their sworn word — and the spirits would stand between us and the dark. The covenant was the contract. Singers were its clerks. We kept the terms current. We renewed the clauses. We were bookkeepers, Captain."

Something cold walked down Varn's spine, because that was *her* word, her profession, and hearing it in the mouth of a condemned man felt like catching a stranger wearing her coat.

"I've read the histories," she said. "I've read what happens when magic goes unregulated. The Sundering Wars. The Ashfall. Three million dead in the western provinces alone. Your 'clerks' burned half a continent arguing over whose clause was holier."

"Yes." Pellam didn't flinch. "We did. Some of us. And some of us spent those same wars running water to besieged villages under truce-verse so old even the armies honored it. Both things are true. Your edict answers one crime by executing everyone who remembers the other." He leaned forward as far as the chains allowed. "Tell me, Captain — when the wars ended and the usurper took the throne, did anyone renegotiate the old agreement? Did anyone formally release the spirits from their side of the bargain?"

Varn said nothing.

"No," Pellam said gently. "They didn't. You can't. There's no one left who knows how. So the contract still stands — starving, unkept, gathering penalties like an unpaid debt accrues interest — and every year it stands weaker, and every year the things it used to hold back press a little harder against the door. Your master didn't outlaw sorcery, Captain. He outlawed the *payments* on a debt he still owes. And he sent soldiers to arrest the accountants."

The lamp flame leaned again, though no one had sung.

Varn looked down at her ledger. Forty-one names. She found herself reading them not as entries but as a list — a list of clerks, of bookkeepers, of people who had known how to make a payment on behalf of a realm that had forgotten it owed anything at all.

She hated him a little for that. It was a clean, familiar hatred, and she held onto it like a railing.

"The Ashfall," she said. "A village of two hundred, gone in a night, because one hedge-witch decided the voices in her head were angels. My grandmother's sister lived three valleys over. I grew up on stories of what unchecked gift does. Don't lecture me about doors and dark things. I've seen what comes through when someone with power decides the rules are for lesser folk."

"And I don't dispute a word of it," Pellam said. "Power without law is exactly the horror you say. That's my whole point, Captain. The covenant *is* the law. It's the leash. You're not burning dogs; you're burning leashes and calling it safety."

One of the guards coughed. Varn realized she had been silent for the better part of a minute, pen hovering, recording nothing.

"Entry forty-two," she said finally, writing as she spoke to steady herself. "Pellam Ostrow, fiddler. Charge: unauthorized cantillation, first degree. Statement recorded in full at prisoner's insistence." She paused. "Disposition pending."

Pellam's eyebrows rose. "Pending?"

"I don't execute prisoners before dawn," Varn said. "Regulation. It looks like eagerness." It was also, though she would have bitten off her tongue before admitting it, because she wanted one more conversation first.

* * *

She took the ledger to her quarters, which was against regulation too — the book was supposed to live in the garrison strongbox — and sat with it under her lamp while the rain came down on Corvane like the city was being rinsed of something.

Forty-one names. She read them all, top to bottom, the way she did every night, because her father had been a quartermaster and had taught her that a ledger you don't reread is a ledger you don't own. A cobbler in Vessany who sang blessings over shoes. Twin sisters who kept a lighthouse working past its wick-oil years by means the harbormaster preferred not to examine. A boy of nineteen who had laughed during his own reading of charges, and whose laughter she still heard sometimes between waking and sleep, like a bell rung in an empty house.

Clerks. Bookkeepers. Accountants of a debt she hadn't known existed until tonight.

She told herself the fiddler was simply good at his work. That was what singers *did* — they made words feel like weight, made lies feel like law. Every testimony she'd ever taken from one had probably been a performance. The rational mind, her academy instructors called it: assume the gifted are lying until proven otherwise, because their gift is precisely the manufacture of otherwise.

But there was a problem with the rational mind, and the problem was sitting in her chest wearing her grandmother's face.

Because her grandmother — her father's mother, dead thirty years, a woman of no education and ferocious common sense — used to sing over the bread. Not hymns; the village priest had frowned on hymns outside chapel. Just a little rising-falling hum, four notes or so, while her hands worked the dough, and she'd said it was to keep the loaf honest, and everyone had laughed, and the bread had always, always risen.

Four notes. Low and unhurried. The kind two neighbors used to swear about well-water and boundary stones.

Varn set the ledger down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Her grandmother had been a baker. A baker with a hearth-song, the way a hundred thousand grandmothers across the realm had hearth-songs, fragments worn smooth by generations of mouths that no longer knew what they carried. The edict didn't distinguish. The edict couldn't distinguish. It was a net with holes the size of everything, and she had been pulling it tight for five years, entry by entry, and somewhere in the back of her skull a voice that sounded appallingly like Pellam Ostrow asked:

*When the loaf stops rising, Captain, whose books will be short?*

She got up, poured herself the last of the plum brandy, and did not drink it. She held the cup and watched the rain.

There were rumors. There were always rumors — the Inquisition ran on rumor the way mills ran on water. But the ones crossing her desk this week had a different texture. Reports from the low quarter, from the crooked lanes below the aqueduct where the city's oldest pipes still carried snowmelt from mountains no map named correctly. A child seen in a doorway during the storm, speaking a language the witness couldn't name but had somehow understood anyway. A shopkeeper's charms that had glowed. A house that — this from a drunk watchman, but drunk watchmen had been right before — *hummed*.

Singing in the low quarter. Living language. Old marks.

Varn opened the ledger to a fresh page and wrote, in her careful hand: *Entry 43 (provisional). Subject unknown. Location: Corvane low quarter.* Then, after a moment, she added a line she had never written in five years of service:

*Note: verify charges personally before disposition. No further executions on hearsay.*

It was a small rebellion. A clerk's rebellion. She almost smiled at that.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping. Varn returned to the counting-house room, where Pellam Ostrow sat exactly as she'd left him, as if chains and a night on a plank bench were furniture he was accustomed to.

"Captain." He brightened. "You look terrible. Good. That means you listened."

"I have questions," she said, sitting. "Not for the record. Off it."

"Those are my favorite kind."

"The covenant you describe. Its clerks. Its singers." She kept her voice level, professional, a woman asking about drainage permits. "How many are left?"

Pellam studied her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him drop the market-stall warmth. What replaced it was quieter, and much more frightening.

"Fewer every year," he said. "You should know — you've been keeping count better than we have." He glanced at the ledger under her arm, and she was ashamed to find she'd brought it into the room without thinking, the way a priest carries his book. "Scattered. Hiding. Most don't know each other anymore — the guilds were broken up, the rosters burned. They're like embers in separate ash heaps, Captain. Each one certain it's the last fire in the world."

"And if they found each other?"

"Then something could be done. Something old. At a place older." His eyes went distant, and for a heartbeat the fiddler looked like a man reading a map he'd memorized as a child. "But that's a story with a long middle, and I suspect your regulations don't allow me to tell it."

"My regulations," Varn said, "are currently under review."

That startled a real laugh out of him — short, rusty, surprised out of both of them.

She stood, closed the ledger, and gave the order she'd already drafted twice in her head during the sleepless hours: the prisoner would be held indefinitely, unexecuted, on grounds of ongoing intelligence value. The paperwork would be creative. The paperwork, she was beginning to understand, always was; that was how empires worked, one creative line at a time.

At the door she paused.

"The bread," she said, not turning around. "My grandmother sang over bread. Four notes. Rising and falling. Was that—"

"Yes," Pellam said softly, behind her. "That was a payment, Captain. Small, and faithful, and enough. For a loaf, and for whoever ate it. Someone in your family kept faith longer than most kings manage."

Varn walked out into the wet morning without answering, because her throat had closed around whatever the answer would have been.

By noon she had requisitioned a horse, two troopers she trusted to ask nothing, and a warrant amended in her own hand — scope widened from *unauthorized cantillation* to *investigation of reported anomalous speech, low quarter, all ages*.

All ages. The phrase sat on the page like a stone she couldn't stop looking at. A child, the reports said. A child speaking the First Tongue in a doorway during the storm.

Children didn't learn languages from nowhere. Children learned them from someone — and someone had been close enough to teach, which meant someone was hiding in the crooked lanes below the aqueduct, in a city of ninety thousand souls, behind some door that hummed.

Varn buckled on her sword, tucked the ledger inside her coat where it lay against her heart like a debt, and rode out into the rain toward the low quarter — one entry ahead of her conscience, and gaining.
