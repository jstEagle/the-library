## 10. The Third Name

The road to Hallowmere smelled like turnips.

Not metaphorically—Brammel had been complaining about it since the second mile, and by the fourth he had elevated the complaint into something approaching art. "You see," he said, gesturing with his walking stick at a field of them, "a man can forgive a landscape its mud, its stones, even its weather. But a whole valley that has chosen, deliberately, to grow nothing but turnips—that is a moral failing."

"It's good soil," Sefa said.

"It's *stubborn* soil. There's a difference."

Noor let them argue. It was easier than thinking about where they were going.

Three names on Sefa's list. The first had been Brammel, and Brammel had come with them, blinking and weeping and clutching his restored memories like a man handed back a coat he'd forgotten he owned. The second name had been crossed out in Sefa's careful hand—*dead, two winters past, the fever took her*—and Noor had stood over that small inked line for a long time, feeling the shape of all the singers who had simply quietly stopped, without warrants or pyres, just life doing what usurpers couldn't be bothered to do.

Which left the third. Odrik Vael. Once the guild's finest bell-caster, before the edicts—a man who could pour song into bronze so that a temple bell would hold a note for a full minute after the rope went still. Noor had never met him. He'd been senior, a full singer when Noor was still mangling his scales, and then the guild had scattered like spilled beads and everyone had rolled under different furniture.

"He'll help us," Sefa said now, in the tone she used for things she wanted to believe. "He never renounced. I checked the rolls at three temples."

"People who don't renounce are either very brave or very careful," Brammel said. "I was neither. I was a coward with an eraser."

"You were *prudent* with an eraser."

"I erased my own mother's grave from a map, Sefa. Let's not dress it up."

Ashen walked between them, holding Noor's hand on one side and, when the mood took her, the hem of Sefa's sleeve on the other. She had been quiet all morning—not her usual quiet, which was the quiet of someone listening to several conversations at once in a language made of light, but a flatter quiet. Twice Noor had caught her looking back down the road they'd come, the way you look at a sound you can't quite place.

"What is it?" he asked her, softly, in the fragments of First Tongue he'd been relearning from Master Corvin's primer each night by candlelight, his lips clumsy around syllables that used to live in him like marrow.

Ashen considered. Then she said a word he didn't know, and touched her own chest, and then pointed back down the road, and made a gesture Noor did know: *smaller*. Something growing smaller. Fading.

"Maybe she's tired," Sefa offered.

Maybe. But Noor filed it away, the way he'd once filed away everything—the old habit of noticing and saying nothing, the survival craft of five years of selling inert charms to people who wanted magic but not enough to die for it. Noticing and saying nothing had kept him alive. He was beginning to suspect it might also keep other people dead.

Hallowmere announced itself first by smell—turnips again, boiled this time—and then by its bell tower, which rose above the rooftops crooked as a question mark. The town had the look of places that had decided occupation was survivable if you kept your head down and your shutters closed: no graffiti, no shrines, no color louder than brown.

And yet, as they came up the high street, Noor heard it.

A bell. A single struck note, low and round, rolling out over the roofs—and *holding*, longer than any ordinary bell had any right to hold, a minute and more, the sound threading itself through the evening air like a hand through hair. Noor stopped walking. His guild-signet, dormant against his chest since the night Ashen arrived, warmed like a stone left near a fire.

"That's guild-work," Brammel breathed. "That's—gods, that's *Odrik's* work. I'd know that sustain anywhere. He used to say a bell should finish its own sentence."

"A man who casts bells in an occupied town," Sefa said slowly, "is either hiding in plain sight or has some arrangement."

"Or," said Brammel, "he's found the one thing the Inquisition doesn't think to look inside. Nobody searches a church bell for sorcery. It's furniture. It's *piety*."

They found the foundry at the end of a lane that dead-ended at the town wall: a squat stone building with a yard full of sand molds and broken crucibles, and a sign that read, in honest black letters, VAEL — BELLS & BRASSWORK, REPAIRS UNDERTAKEN. Noor stood looking at that sign for a moment longer than he needed to. *Repairs undertaken.* Somewhere in his chest, the shopkeeper in him—the part that had spent five years being small on purpose—felt an odd kinship with whoever had written it.

Sefa knocked. After a while, footsteps, and the door opened on a big man in a leather apron, fifty-odd, gray-shot beard, forearms like ship's cable, with the wary flatness of a face that had practiced being unremarkable until it stuck.

"We need a bell repaired," Sefa began.

"Take it to Corvane."

"It's a special bell. Drowned, actually. Underwater these twenty years."

Something moved behind the man's eyes—quick, quickly buried. "We don't do drowned work."

"The Drowned Bell of Saint Ferren," Sefa said. "You cast it. You were there when they sank it, and you wept, and you told me afterward that a bell doesn't stop ringing just because the water gets in. It rings somewhere only fish can hear." She stepped closer. "Hello, Odrik."

For a long moment the big man didn't move. Then he looked past her—at Brammel, whose face he clearly didn't recognize, which must have stung them both; at Noor; and last at Ashen, standing half behind Noor's leg with her thumb in her mouth like any tired child at the end of a long day.

Whatever Odrik saw when he looked at Ashen, it emptied his face entirely.

"Inside," he said. "Quickly. And don't stand in the doorway like a painting."

* * *

The foundry's interior was warm and cluttered and smelled of hot metal and beeswax. Odrik sat them down, fed the stove, put bread and hard cheese in front of them without asking, and then stood with his back to them, hands braced on a workbench, for what felt like a full minute.

"You shouldn't have come here," he said finally.

"No," Sefa agreed. "But here we are."

"You especially shouldn't have brought—" He turned. His eyes went to Ashen again and flinched away, the way eyes flinch from a forge. "Do you know what she is?"

"A living clause of the covenant," Noor said. "We know."

"Then you know more than she does, probably, and less than you should." Odrik dragged a stool over and sat heavily, and up close Noor could see the cost written on him: not age exactly, but erosion, the way a riverbank erodes, grain by grain, from carrying too much water for too long. "There are men who would pay a year's revenue for ten minutes alone with that child. And some of those men know my name."

"Then we leave tonight," Noor said. "We only need—"

"I know what you need. Sefa's list. Three names, and I'm the third, and you want me to walk out of the only life my daughter has ever known and go sing at a hole in the ground while the usurper's army burns everything between here and it." He laughed, short and terrible. "Master Corvin trained you to negotiate, boy? Because you're bad at it."

"I trained myself to survive. It's a different curriculum."

Something flickered across Odrik's face at the name Corvin—grief, maybe, or guilt—and Noor, who had spent years reading customers' faces across a counter, saw it and felt the first cold thread of wrongness slide down his spine.

It was Brammel, though, who said it. Brammel, who had spent forty years mapping the world and thirty forgetting it, and who knew better than anyone how to notice what was missing. He was looking around the foundry with narrowed eyes.

"Odrik," he said pleasantly. "Lovely workshop. Very tidy. Where do you sleep?"

"What?"

"Bed. Cot. Pallet. There's no bed here. There's a stove, a bench, tools, bread. But a man doesn't sleep standing up, and this house has one room." Brammel's voice stayed mild as milk. "So where is home, Odrik Vael?"

The silence stretched. Outside, the crooked bell tower rang the hour—six notes, each one holding, beautiful, guild-made, singing its sentence into the dark.

"No," Sefa said quietly. She had gone very still. "Odrik. Tell me no."

"There's a cottage," Odrik said. His voice had gone carefully, terribly level. "Behind the tannery. Mira lives there with her aunt. She's nine." He looked at his own hands, huge and scarred and gentle-looking, resting on his knees. "She's nine years old, and two winters ago the Inquisition came for me, and they didn't take me. They took her instead. Held her. Told me she'd stay held—in a facility in the capital, with their oathbreakers and their leech-wards—unless I was useful."

Noor felt the cold thread become a wire.

"Useful how?" he asked, though he already knew. He had known since the doorway, maybe. Since the road. Since Ashen looking backward at something fading.

"Names," Odrik said. "Locations. The Inquisition can find rumors, but rumors rot. They needed a man who could hear a bell and tell them which hand rang it." He raised his head, and his eyes were dry, which was somehow worse than tears. "The singer in Marrowfield. That was me. The woman in Dunlow Cross, the one they burned in the market square—her name crossed my lips six weeks before. I've given them eleven names in two years. Eleven. And every time I told myself *this is the last, Mira comes home after this one*, and every time they came back and smiled and said *just one more, caster, just one more*, because they understood something I didn't understand until it was too late."

"Which is?" Sefa whispered.

"That a man will betray anything, forever, for a child he loves. It's not a weakness they exploit. It's a *well*. They drink from it every day and it never runs dry." He spread his hands. "So. Here I am. The third name on your list. Go on, then. Sefa, you've got a knife somewhere under that coat, I watched you check it at the crossroads. Do it quick, or tie me up and leave me for the garrison, or drag me along and spend the rest of your days wondering when I'll sell you. Those are your options. I've thought about them all myself, most nights, staring at the ceiling. I don't recommend the ceiling."

Nobody moved. The stove ticked. Ashen, curled in the corner on a bench with a crust of bread, watched the grown men and women with her enormous dark eyes, and Noor realized with a lurch that she had understood every word—not the common tongue, perhaps, but the *shape* of it, the truth-shape underneath, the way she understood everything.

His hand was on his knife. He hadn't decided to do that. Five years of hiding had built reflexes, and one of them was: *the traitor is the most dangerous thing in the room.* Eleven names. A woman burned in a market square. Every singer they'd gathered was alive right now because of luck, and luck ran out.

Vengeance, he discovered, was easy. That was the horror of it. It fit his hand like the knife did. All he had to do was stop thinking.

* * *

Ashen got down off the bench.

She crossed the room the way she did everything—without hurry, as though the floor had asked her politely—and climbed onto Odrik's stool beside him, and before anyone could speak, before Noor could get a word past the knot in his throat, she took both of the bell-caster's huge scarred hands in her two small ones.

"Don't—" Odrik started.

She sang.

It wasn't sound, exactly—or it was, but sound was only the smallest part of it. Noor felt it go through the room like heat through iron. Later he would try to describe it to Sefa and fail, and try again, and fail better. What Ashen gave Odrik Vael was the ledger. His ledger, opened and read aloud in the only court that had jurisdiction: every name he'd sold, and attached to every name, everything that had unmade itself downstream. The woman from Dunlow Cross teaching her granddaughter a lullaby, and then not. The singer from Marrowfield's unfinished verse hanging in the air over an empty chair. The covenant itself, starving, thinner each season, its guardian spirits dimming like coals nobody tended—each death a draft through the grate. He saw it all. Noor could see that he saw it, because Odrik's face broke open the way a bell cracks, all at once, along the line of its making.

The big man made a sound Noor had never heard a grown man make. Not a scream. Something older.

And then—it lasted moments or hours—Ashen stopped. She let go of his hands. She looked up at him, and patted his forearm twice, gently, the way you'd pat a horse, or a friend.

Odrik Vael put his face in his hands and wept like rain on a foundry roof.

Noor knelt slowly beside the stool. His knife was still in his hand. He looked at it, and at the man shaking in front of him, and understood suddenly why Ashen had done what she did—because vengeance would have been *mercy*, really. A clean blade and a finished story. What Ashen had given him was the harder thing: the truth, complete, undiluted, and tomorrow morning anyway. Waking up. Being the man who knew.

"She showed you what you unmade," Noor said quietly. "Now show us what you'll make."

Odrik lifted his head. His eyes were wrecked, red-rimmed, hollowed—but behind the ruin of him, something was turning over, slow and enormous, like a bell deciding whether to swing.

"Mira," he rasped. "If I run with you—if I sing your song at your wellspring—they'll know within a day. They'll move her. Or worse. Unless—" He stopped. Started again. "Unless we don't run. Unless we go *through*."

"Through what?" Sefa asked.

"The army. The Gray Column's camped at Thornford Crossing, nineteen thousand strong, supply wagons backed up for miles. They're marching on the free cities, and they're marching *toward* the Wellspring whether they know it or not." Odrik wiped his face with a palm the size of a dinner plate, and when he looked up, there was something in his expression Noor had never seen on a customer's face and had almost forgotten existed on anyone's: hope, ugly and desperate and real. "They trust me. I'm their little well that never runs dry. They give me passes. They give me *escort*." He laughed, wet and cracked. "So here's my bargain, apprentice of the Vesper Guild, and it's the only one I've got. You help me steal my daughter back out of the capital's shadow—and I'll walk you straight through the usurper's own army to the door of the world."

Outside, the crooked bell rang the quarter-hour, holding each note long past reason, finishing its sentence.

Noor looked at Sefa. Sefa looked at Brammel. Brammel, who had erased his own mother's grave and lived to regret it, nodded once, slowly, like a man confirming a bearing on a chart.

And Ashen, still perched on the stool, reached up and laid her small hand flat over Odrik's heart, and spoke her first word of common tongue in three days:

"Yes."
