## 11. Odrik's Bargain

Odrik Vael had betrayed exactly eleven people in his life, and he could name every one of them.

That was the trouble with being a man who kept ledgers in his head. His father had been a grain-factor in the port towns, and Odrik had inherited two things: a head for numbers and a conscience like an abacus—every bead that slid, it clicked, and it stayed clicked until someone set it right. Eleven beads. He ran them at night the way other men counted sheep.

Tonight he ran them twice.

He sat apart from the others, at the edge of the ruined mill where Sefa had declared them safe enough for one night, turning a copper coin over his knuckles. Not for luck. For something to do with hands that had spent three years writing names on slips of paper and passing them to men who wore gray.

Across the firelight, the child called Ashen was asleep with her head against Noor's shoulder, and Noor—gods help him—was watching her sleep the way Odrik used to watch Mira sleep, back when Mira slept anywhere without checking first which adults were still awake.

*You're doing it again,* Odrik told himself. *Comparing. Making yourself the sympathetic figure of your own story.*

The abacus clicked anyway.

* * *

They had not killed him. That was the part he kept circling like a tongue finding a broken tooth.

He had expected to die in that room. When Ashen had laid her small hands over his—the hands that had signed away nine singers, nine names in careful ink, each one a bead—he had braced for it the way you brace for cold water. What came instead was worse than any blade. She had shown him what he'd unmade.

He had seen Tallow-Haired Wren, who sang to keep bees. He'd sold her name in the second year, when the payments started, before he knew what the Inquisition did with singers besides arrest them. The vision Ashen gave him hadn't been her death—that would have been simple, bearable even. It had been her bees. Forty thousand small instruments going silent all at once, and the hives left standing like little empty churches, and no one in that village able to say why their bread stopped rising quite so well, why the orchards thinned, why the world got a fraction quieter and stayed that way forever.

A covenant isn't thunderbolts, the child's songless voice had said inside his skull. It is forty thousand bees agreeing to be kind.

Odrik had wept into his own hands then, in front of strangers, in front of the girl he'd have handed over next if the price were high enough. And Noor Delacroix—the last apprentice, the one whose guild Odrik had helped gut—had looked at him across that room and said nothing at all, which was somehow more terrible than anything Elleryn's gray friends had ever managed.

And then this morning, impossibly, they'd fed him breakfast.

* * *

Sefa found him at the mill's broken doorway just past midnight. She moved quietly for a large woman; the river-priests always had, he supposed, or maybe drowning taught you to walk soft.

"You're not sleeping," she said.

"Neither are you."

"I'm praying. It looks similar but takes longer." She settled beside him against the doorpost with a creak of wet wool. "In my order we had a rite for men like you. We didn't call it penance—we called it *turning the boat*. You can't undo the wake a boat leaves. But you can point the bow somewhere new, and the wake changes behind you whether you like it or not."

"Eloquently said for someone whose god is a drowned bell."

"She's not drowned. She's *submerged*. There's a difference." Sefa studied him with eyes the color of river ice. "Noor wants to throw you out. Brammel wants to tie you up. I told them both that a man who's already rowing toward his own hanging doesn't need rope."

"What do you want?"

"I want to know about the daughter."

The abacus slammed to a stop. Every bead still.

"Mira," Odrik said, because there was no use pretending he hadn't seen it coming, hadn't felt the question walking toward him all evening the way you feel weather. "Mira Vael. Nine years old. She has her mother's stubbornness and my—" he gestured vaguely at his own face, "—unfortunate chin."

"And she's in the capital."

"In the Gray Keep. Under the usurper's protection, which is a word they use instead of *hostage*." He turned the copper coin once more, then let it lie flat on his knee. "Her mother died in the second winter. Fever. After that I had a choice: keep singing for a guild that was already ash, or give the Inquisition something worth a girl's keep. I told myself it was temporary. Three years of temporary."

"You gave them nine names."

"I gave them names," Odrik said, "and every time, I told myself the ledger balanced. One name against one daughter. But your abacus doesn't balance that way, does it? Theirs never balances. They just keep handing you a bigger weight and calling it the same side of the scale." He laughed, short and ugly. "Last month they asked me for the tenth. A singer in the low quarter of Corvane. I told them I'd find him."

"Noor."

"Noor."

Sefa nodded slowly. "And instead you found us all standing in front of you."

"The gods have a sense of humor. It's a cruel one."

* * *

He made his offer at dawn, standing in the mill yard while Brammel argued with the cart axle as if it were an old colleague who'd disappointed him again. Noor stood by the well with Ashen perched on its lip, and Odrik noticed the man had stopped flinching when the child touched him, which meant something had happened between them that Odrik wasn't invited to understand.

"I can get you through the army," Odrik said.

Brammel dropped the axle. It rolled in a lazy circle and clonked against his boot, and he stared at Odrik with the expression of a man who had erased his own memory precisely to avoid mornings like this.

"The supply column moves south from Vell's Crossing every ninth day," Odrik went on, because now that he'd started, the words came with the momentum of a confession ten years overdue. "Grain wagons, salt, arrow-wood. I know the rotation, the countersigns, the faces of the quartermasters—I've dined with half of them. They think I'm a loyal clerk of the Provisioning Office, which is the most insulting thing anyone has ever believed about me. I can put six people in a salt wagon and walk them past every checkpoint between here and the Wellspring road."

"And the price," Noor said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"My daughter comes with us." Odrik's voice held, mostly. "She's in the Gray Keep, but the children of useful collaborators are moved to the garrison schools in summer—for education, they say. For leverage, says everyone else. The summer transfer goes through the supply column too. One wagon. One guard detail I can buy off with everything I've saved, which is nearly everything I have." He spread his hands. "I get her out, you get your passage, and after that you can leave me at the nearest cliff edge, whichever suits."

Silence. Even the cart seemed to be listening.

"That's not passage," Brammel said finally. "That's a kidnapping riding shotgun on our rescue mission."

"It's a bargain," Odrik corrected. "I'm offering you the only thing I have left that's worth anything, which is my knowledge of how the usurper's bowels work. Believe me, no one knows the shame of that inventory better than I do."

Noor looked at Ashen. Ashen, who spoke no common tongue, looked back at Noor, and the two of them had some silent conversation that took perhaps four seconds and apparently covered considerable ground.

Then the child slid off the well lip, walked across the yard, and took Odrik's hand.

He went rigid. The last time those fingers had touched him, he'd spent a night seeing silent beehives. But what came this time was smaller. Warmer. A kitchen, maybe—it took him a moment to recognize it, because it wasn't his memory at all. It was a feeling shaped like a kitchen: steam, bread, someone humming off-key, safety. Home, rendered as a single note.

Ashen tilted her head up at him and said one word in the First Tongue, and though Odrik had spent his life among singers and should have known better, the word landed in his chest like a struck bell.

Later, Noor translated it for him, grudgingly, the way a man translates something he'd rather not.

"She said you're not finished," Noor said. "The covenant doesn't count what you broke. It counts what you'll carry."

"That's a terrible accounting standard."

"It's the only one that's ever worked."

* * *

Sefa voted yes within the hour, on the grounds that rivers always found a way around obstacles and so should sensible people. Brammel voted no, then revised to abstention when Ashen sat down next to him and began patiently re-drawing one of his erased maps in the dirt, complete with landmarks Brammel had spent five years pretending not to remember. Watching the old mapmaker's face as the child sketched the Falls of Merren from memory was like watching a man greet a ghost he'd missed terribly.

Which left Noor.

He came to Odrik in the late afternoon, while the others packed, and sat on the millstone across from him with the wary posture of a man approaching a dog of unknown temper.

"Why should I trust you?" Noor asked. No heat in it. Just the question itself, held out flat like a coin.

"You shouldn't."

Something flickered in Noor's expression—surprise, maybe. Odrik pressed on.

"You shouldn't trust me, apprentice. Trust is for people whose word has never bent. Mine bent three years ago and it hasn't straightened since." He tapped his own temple. "But I have an abacus in here, and it has eleven beads that won't stop clicking, and there is exactly one way I know to make them quiet. It isn't forgiveness—I've priced that, and I can't afford it. It's this. The wagon. The girl. The rest of the road done honestly if it kills me, which, given our company's luck, it may."

Noor was quiet for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he almost smiled.

"You know, you're the first person in this whole wretched business who's admitted the truth plainly. Everyone else wraps their motives in prophecy and destiny and the will of the covenant." He glanced toward Ashen, softening further. "I find I trust the plain ones more."

"So it's yes?"

"It's yes. With conditions." Noor held up fingers. "One: no more names pass from your hand to theirs, ever, under any threat. If it comes to choosing between her safety and ours, we choose together, in the open. Two: when we reach the Wellspring, whatever happens there, you stand with us. Not behind us. With."

"And three?"

"Three." Noor's mouth twitched. "Stop calling me *apprentice*. I haven't earned the rank in years and I'm tired of it sounding like an accusation."

"Done." Odrik rose, knees cracking, and offered his hand. "Though for the record, Delacroix, I called you apprentice because it was the kindest thing I could call you. Everything else on my list was worse."

Noor shook the hand. His grip was stronger than it looked. Most honest things were.

* * *

That night, Odrik slept—or something near it—and dreamed of the supply road south: the dust, the creak of salt-wagon axles, the smell of mules and tar. In the dream, Mira was sitting on the tailboard of the last wagon with her legs swinging, wearing the scuffed boots she refused to give up, and she looked at him with her mother's exact skeptical eyebrow and said,

*You're late, Papa. You're always late. But you came.*

He woke with his face wet and didn't bother being embarrassed about it; the mill was dark and everyone else was asleep, and a man was allowed his private weathers.

Dawn came gray and clean. They broke camp quickly—Brammel muttering over his restored maps, Sefa wrapping her drowned bell in oilcloth, Ashen riding on Noor's shoulders pointing at birds and naming them in a language older than any of them. Odrik fell into step at the rear, where a man carrying eleven beads belonged, and pulled out his worn copy of the provisioning schedules.

Vell's Crossing. Ninth day. Salt wagon, east lane, countersign *gray heron*.

Nineteen days until the armies reached the free cities, Mira had heard through walls Odrik shuddered to imagine. Which meant the summer transfer had to move soon, or not at all.

He checked the dates twice, then a third time, and felt the abacus in his head go strangely, blessedly still—as if, for the first time in three years, every bead on both sides of the frame was pointed the same direction.

"Delacroix," he called up the line. "How do you feel about mules?"
