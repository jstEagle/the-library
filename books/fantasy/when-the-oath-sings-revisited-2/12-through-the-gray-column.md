## 12. Through the Gray Column

The army camped in a valley that had once grown barley, and now grew nothing but tents, cookfires, and the particular smell of ten thousand men who had not bathed since the mountains.

Noor lay on his belly on the ridge above it, in the wet grass, beside four people who were all, in their own ways, terrible at this.

"We could just walk in," Brammel said. He was chewing a stalk of something he'd picked and immediately regretted. "I'm serious. Look at it. It's a supply column, not a fortress. Half those sentries are boys. I've got a cart, I've got grain sacks, I've got a face like every other tired old man hauling flour."

"You have a face," Sefa said, "that has been drawn on three separate wanted posters, which is why we're doing this properly."

"Those posters make my nose too big."

"Your nose is exactly that big."

"Friends," Noor said quietly, "the valley."

They looked at the valley with him. The Gray Column — that was what the soldiers called themselves, after the ash-gray cloaks of the Inquisition's supply corps — stretched along the river road in orderly rows, ringed by scrying wards that shimmered faintly to eyes trained to see them, which mercifully included Noor's and mercifully did not include most people's. The wards hung over the camp like a net of almost-invisible thread. Anything that sang, anything that hummed, anything that so much as whispered in the First Tongue would light up inside that net like a struck bell.

And somewhere down there, in a tent or a wagon or a cage, was a nine-year-old girl named Mira.

Odrik knelt apart from the rest, staring down at the camp with an expression Noor had no good name for. It wasn't hope. Hope would have been easier to look at.

"She'll be near the officers' tents," Odrik said, for the fourth time that morning. "They keep her close. She's useful to them." His jaw worked. "Useful. Listen to me. That's how they talk about her. *Useful.*"

"Then we'll be quick and quiet and gone before anyone decides she's useful enough to move," Sefa said. She reached into her pack and drew out the drowned bell.

Noor had seen it twice before and still hadn't gotten used to it. It was small — a hand's breadth of green-black bronze, crusted as if it had spent its whole life underwater, which, according to Sefa, it had. Forty years at the bottom of the Merrow Pool, until she'd gone down after it on the night her order was dissolved and the usurper's men burned her temple. When she turned it slowly in her hands, water moved across its surface though none spilled, and the air around it went strange and soft, the way sound goes when snow is falling.

"It doesn't silence us," Sefa said, repeating the lesson for Odrik's benefit, because he'd been too grief-struck to hear it the first two times. "Silence draws attention. Nothing in an army camp is silent. What it does is *drown* our voices — wraps them in water-sound, deep and muffled, so the wards hear only sloshing. To the scryers we'll sound like a well, or a culvert, or a man with very bad lungs. Ashen—"

Ashen looked up. The child sat cross-legged in the grass with a stick, drawing lines in the dirt that Noor suspected were not lines but sentences, in a script older than any kingdom. All day Ashen had been quiet in the way that worried Noor most — not the calm quiet, but the listening quiet, the quiet of someone standing in a room where everyone is speaking carefully around a thing they don't want you to notice.

"Ashen doesn't sing tonight," Noor finished. "Not one note. Not even humming. If the wards catch the First Tongue anywhere in that camp, everything we've done ends right there."

Ashen considered him with those gray eyes that made Noor feel read like a ledger, then nodded once, solemnly, and — deliberately, pointedly — snapped the stick in half and put both halves away in a pocket, as if formally decommissioning it.

"Thank you," Noor said.

Ashen patted his hand. Then, in gesture-language they'd built together over weeks: *You sing bad anyway.*

"Gentle humor," Noor said, "is a betrayal of trust."

Ashen smiled. It was a small smile, but real, and Noor held onto it the way you hold onto a coal in wind.

* * *

Getting in was insultingly easy, which Noor distrusted completely.

Brammel's cart rolled through the western gate behind six other grain wagons, and the boy-sentry who checked their papers looked at Brammel's forged manifests, looked at Brammel's magnificently bored expression, looked at the four figures slumped under sacking in the bed of the cart, and yawned.

"Sick oxen?" the boy asked.

"Dying oxen," Brammel said mournfully. "Third one this month. Bad feed. You want to smell it? Nobody ever wants to smell it."

"No," said the boy, with feeling, and waved them through.

Inside, the drowned bell sat wrapped in sacking between Sefa's feet, humming its soft submersion-hum, and Noor felt it working — felt his own voice go distant in his chest, as if he were hearing himself from across a pond. He tested it, whispering a single syllable of the guild's old counting-rhyme, and heard it come out as nothing but a watery blur. Somewhere overhead, invisible threads of ward-light passed across the cart and slid off without a ripple.

They left the cart in the supply rows, under a tarp, next to forty tons of actual grain that gave them perfect cover to mill about with purpose. Brammel transformed instantly into a fussy quartermaster's man, complaining about moisture in the sacks, directing imaginary porters, and generating so much mundane noise about barley that three different real soldiers came over specifically to get away from him.

"The officers' quarter," he murmured through his complaints, "is past the horse lines, the big pavilion with the banner, and the smaller tents around it. And Noor — the cages are south. I counted them on the way in. Four of them. Small ones."

Small ones. Noor's stomach dropped. "For children?"

"For anybody they want small," Brammel said grimly.

Odrik had already started walking south. Noor caught his arm. "We do this in order. Sefa finds where the wards knot thinnest, we learn her routine, we—"

"I have waited eleven days," Odrik said. His voice was low and level, which was worse than shouting. "Eleven days of watching her through other men's reports. So we will do this in whatever order gets me to her fastest, and if you need a better reason than that, remember that every hour she spends in their hands is another hour they spend teaching her to belong to them."

That stopped Noor cold, because it was true, and because he'd been avoiding exactly that thought since Odrik first told them what the Inquisition did with captured singers' children. They didn't just hold them. They *bound* them. Little oaths, little leashes. A child who could be carried into any room and set loose like a compass needle, pointing always toward song.

Sefa touched Odrik's shoulder. "South first," she said gently. "But we look before we touch. Both of us. Agreed?"

Odrik breathed out through his nose. "Agreed."

* * *

They found her at dusk, and she was not in a cage.

She was sitting outside the largest of the officers' tents, on a stool, wrapped in a fine gray cloak much too warm for her, eating an apple, surrounded by more protection than the army's paymaster. Two guards flanked her. A third stood at the tent flap, and through it Noor could hear the murmur of men planning things.

Mira had her mother's dark hair, cut short and practical — someone had decided curls were a liability — and her father's sharp chin, and eyes that moved constantly, cataloguing everything, missing nothing. Nine years old and sharp as glass. Even at this distance Noor could see the bond on her: a thin gray sigil inked at her throat, like a collar of written law, the kind of mark that made Noor's skin crawl because he knew the craft behind it. Oathbreaker's work. Clean, efficient, soulless.

"She looks fed," Sefa murmured. They crouched behind a stack of fodder bales thirty feet away. "Healthy, even."

"They take care of tools," Odrik whispered.

As they watched, an officer came out of the tent, bent to Mira's ear, and spoke briefly. Mira nodded. The officer went back inside. A minute later, Mira stood, walked a slow circuit of the officers' quarter — guards trailing her like ducklings — and paused by the horse lines, head tilted, listening to something no one else could hear.

"She's sweeping," Sefa breathed. "Sweet mercy. They're using her to sweep for singers."

Noor's mouth had gone dry. "If she sweeps toward us—"

"She won't hear us. The bell—" Sefa began, and stopped, because Mira had stopped.

The girl stood among the horses, very still, her whole body angled like a weathervane. Her hand rose slowly to the gray sigil at her throat.

"She hears the bell," Odrik said. "She hears *water*."

Every plan Noor had made — the careful sequence, the extraction route, the contingency for alarms — evaporated at once, replaced by a single image: that thin gray mark lighting up, a child opening her mouth, and the entire Gray Column coming down on them like a landslide.

Odrik didn't wait. He stood up out of the fodder and walked straight to his daughter.

"*Odrik*—" Sefa hissed, but he was already moving, hands open, palms out, a father crossing a farmyard, and Noor scrambled up after him because there was nothing else in the world to do.

The guards reacted fast, actually — credit where due — spears coming down, shouts rising. But Odrik stopped five feet from Mira, beyond spear-reach, and said, clear and steady, "Hello, little fish."

Mira stared at him.

Noor watched it happen on her face: recognition arriving first, then disbelief, then something furious and complicated that had no business living on a nine-year-old's face. Her hand was still pressed to her throat. Her lips parted. Noor braced for the alarm-cry, the word that would bring three hundred soldiers running.

What came out instead, barely audible, was: "*You came.*"

"I came," Odrik said.

"You took eleven days." Her voice cracked on the number. "I counted. I count *everything*, Papa, they taught me to count everything, and you took eleven days and I thought—" Her breath hitched, and for one second she was just a child, and then the wall came back down, smooth and practiced, and Noor felt sick seeing how quickly it fell. "You shouldn't be here. There are wards. If you sing—"

"We won't sing."

"They'll know anyway. They always know." She glanced at the guards, who were confused — a ragged man calling a bound girl *little fish* was apparently outside their training — and then did something extraordinary. She raised her voice into a petulant wail: "*I don't WANT to go to bed!*" — and while the guards winced and exchanged the looks of men who did not sign up for this, she hissed, at conversational volume, "Walk me to the latrine ditch. They can't follow there. Officer's orders, it's unseemly. *Now.*"

It was, Noor reflected as they followed the wailing girl across the camp, the finest piece of tradecraft he had ever witnessed, and she was nine.

* * *

The latrine ditch ran along the camp's southern edge, downwind of everything, and the guards stopped at a respectful distance with expressions of profound relief. Mira led them along it to a bend screened by willow scrub, and there she rounded on them, apple core still in hand like a scepter, and became again the sharp-eyed creature from the stool.

"How many?" she demanded.

"How many what?" Noor asked.

"People. Singers. How many did you bring into an occupied camp?" Her eyes raked past him. "Four? Five? Is the child here? I've heard the child described. White streak in the hair, smells like rain, doesn't talk. Don't bother hiding it, the whole Inquisition has a page on it, I've *seen the page*—"

"Mira." Odrik crouched to her height. "Little fish. We're taking you home."

Something flickered across her face and died. "Home," she repeated, flat. "Where's home, Papa? The farm's burned. You were going to give me to the covenant people, and look how that worked out. This is home now." She tapped the gray sigil at her throat, matter-of-fact as a girl showing off a locket. "You get used to it. Mostly it just itches."

"What does it make you do?" Sefa asked softly.

Mira sized her up — the priestess's robes disguised as commoner's wool, the bundle at her hip that hid a drowned god's bell — and answered with the brutal economy of a child who has learned that information is currency. "When anyone sings — really sings, magic singing, not drunk soldiers — the mark pulls. Like a hook behind my collarbone. And then I have to report. Not want to. *Have to.* I fought it the first week." Her chin lifted slightly. "I don't fight it anymore."

"Why not?" Odrik asked, and his voice broke open on it.

"Because fighting it hurts, and reporting doesn't, and I decided hurting was stupid." She said it defiantly, daring them to argue. Then, quieter, to the ground: "Also, if you fight it too long, they tighten it. There's a woman, Inquisitor Corvel, she tightens it with a word. I've seen her do it to the singer in cage three. He screamed for a long time." She looked up, and her glass-sharp eyes were suddenly wet and furious. "So yes. Fine. Take me away from here. But understand what you're taking. They didn't leave me the way you remember. They wrote on me. I hear war councils through this thing sometimes, when they hold it near their maps and forget I'm a person instead of a tool. I know things. Terrible things. Numbers."

Noor's heart was pounding. "Mira—"

"Nineteen days," she said.

Everyone went still. Even the willows seemed to lean in.

"Until what?" Sefa asked carefully.

"Until the armies reach the free cities, if the council holds to the schedule, which Inquisitor Corvel says they won't because the vanguard wants to move early." Mira took a bite of her apple core, chewed, swallowed, and delivered the rest like a merchant naming a price. "And nineteen days until they reach the Wellspring, because they found it, they've known for months, and they're not sending soldiers to guard it. They're sending siege-mages to crack it open and drain it dry." She looked at each of them in turn, and Noor saw that beneath the wall and the currency and the practiced flatness, she was terrified — terrified and choosing, with a child's full weight, whom to trust. "That's what I know. That's what I'm worth. So decide fast, because the evening sweep starts soon, and when the mark pulls this time, Papa—"

Her voice finally, fully cracked.

"—when it pulls this time, I don't know yet if I report you. I'd like to say I won't. But they wrote on me, and I honestly don't know what's me and what's them anymore, and neither do you. So *decide*."

Down in the camp, somewhere near the officers' quarter, a bell rang the change of watch, and Mira's hand drifted up to the gray sigil at her throat, fingers resting there like a bird on a wire, waiting to see what it would ask of her.
