## 13. The Girl Who Listens

Mira had learned a long time ago that the best way to survive adults was to watch them the way they watched weather.

They thought she was small. They thought she was quiet. They thought, because her father had sold her to the Inquisition's keepers in exchange for her life — which was, she supposed, technically a kind of love — that she would be grateful and obedient and too frightened to notice anything.

They were wrong about nearly all of it.

What they had not counted on — what *nobody* had counted on, because nobody had asked — was that when they bound her with the little gray oath, the one that made her report any singing she heard, they had not made her deaf. They had made her an ear. And an ear, pressed against the wrong wall at the right time, hears everything.

The bond sat behind her breastbone like a cold coin. When soldiers sang their marching chants two tents over, it warmed. When a siege-mage hummed his warding verses, it burned. And when the war-council met in the command pavilion — the big tent with the usurper's black banner drooping outside like a wet sock — the coin went hot enough that Mira could feel the words through it. Not clearly. Not like listening at a keyhole. More like hearing a conversation through a wall of ice: shapes of words, edges of meaning, the occasional sentence that came through whole, as if the ice had a crack in it exactly the size of her attention.

She had been listening for four months. She had told no one.

Telling people was dangerous. Telling the wrong people was fatal. And so Mira had done what she did best, which was nothing, magnificently, while collecting facts the way other children collected river stones: smooth ones, sharp ones, one or two that might be worth something someday.

Tonight, someone had finally come to collect her.

* * *

The man who unlocked her pen was not a soldier. He had ink on his fingers and kind eyes and a limp, and he looked at her the way you look at a door you're afraid is locked from the inside.

"Mira," he said. "I'm Odrik."

"I know who you are," she said.

He blinked. "You—"

"You're my father." She said it flatly, without warmth, because warmth was a currency and she was not spending any until she knew what it bought. "You came with the singers. The ones who took me out of the supply column. I heard them coming before they got there. Your friend with the bell makes the wards go stupid. It felt like my ears filling up with water."

Odrik stared at her. Then he sat down on the stool across from her cot, slowly, like a man whose legs had stopped agreeing with him.

"They said you were bound," he said. "That you'd report—"

"I am. And I would have." She folded her arms. "If I'd heard singing worth reporting. I didn't. I heard a bell and some very bad sneaking. One of them stepped in the wash-water bucket. I'm not reporting *that*. That's just embarrassing for everyone."

He made a sound that was half laugh and half sob, which Mira privately filed under *things fathers do*, along with humming off-key and pretending not to cry at festivals.

"Mira," he said again, softer now. "I came to take you home."

"Where's home?"

"With me. Away from here. Away from all of it."

She considered this. She was nine years old, and she had lived in a cage of canvas and rules for most of a year, and every part of her wanted to say yes so badly that the wanting sat in her throat like a swallowed stone. But wanting things, she had learned, was how adults got you.

"If we run," she said, "they'll hunt us. Both of us. You know that. You were in their army long enough to know how they treat deserters' children."

"I know."

"And if they catch me, they won't put me back in a pen. I've heard what happens now. Through the—" She paused, because saying *through the bond* out loud was itself a confession, and she hadn't decided yet whether these people deserved confessions. "Through things I've heard."

Odrik's face did something complicated. Grief and guilt fighting over the same ground.

"So before I go anywhere with you," Mira said, "you're going to bring me the one in charge. The one with the signet ring. And I'm going to talk to him. Alone."

"He won't like—"

"He'll like it fine," she said. "Men who hide in aqueducts respect caution. Bring him."

* * *

Noor Delacroix turned out to be exactly the sort of man Mira expected: nervous, polite, and visibly relieved to be negotiating with a child instead of an adult, because children, in his experience, were less likely to stab him.

She let him get comfortable on the crate across from her before she opened negotiations.

"You want to know what I know," she said.

"I do," Noor said. "But first — are you all right? Did anyone hurt you?"

Mira blinked. Of all the opening moves she'd prepared for, kindness had ranked somewhere below *he tries to bribe me* and slightly above *he tries to recruit me immediately.*

"No," she said. "They fed me. They taught me letters. They called me 'the little listener' and gave me extra bread when I reported things." She shrugged. "It wasn't cruel. It was worse than cruel. It was *comfortable*. Cruelty makes people fight. Comfort makes them stay."

Noor went quiet, and Mira could see him chewing on that, the way he seemed to chew on everything, slowly, like a man afraid of swallowing a truth whole.

"What do you want?" he asked finally.

"Options," she said. "Real ones. Not 'come with me and hope.' I want to hear what you're doing, where you're going, and what happens to me in each version. Then I'll decide what my information costs."

"You're nine."

"I'm aware," she said. "It comes up constantly. Usually from people who then lose arguments to me."

Sefa, who had drifted to the mouth of the tent with her arms crossed and her drowned-bell expression firmly in place, snorted. Brammel, the old mapmaker, laughed outright and then pretended he hadn't.

"Go on, then," Sefa said. "Let the girl talk. She's earned it. Anyone who survives a year in that camp by keeping her mouth shut has more sense than half the officers in it."

* * *

So Mira talked.

"The armies move in nineteen days," she said. "Not twenty. Not a fortnight-and-some. Nineteen. The vanguard leaves at dawn on the nineteenth day because that's when the supply wagons finish loading, and I know that because I've listened to the quartermasters argue about axle-grease for three weeks. The free cities think they have until midsummer. They don't. They have nineteen days, and half of them will spend the first ten arguing about who commands the walls."

Noor's hands had gone still in his lap. Stillness, Mira noted, was his version of shouting.

"How do you know the day?" he asked carefully.

"The quartermaster's clerk sings numbers under his breath when he counts. Old habit. My bond warms when anyone sings, even counting-song, even nonsense. So I hear the numbers." She tilted her head. "You'd be amazed what people sing without noticing. Marching cadences. Prayers. A woman in the laundry tent sings her daughter's name over and over while she wrings sheets. I know seventeen soldiers' true names because they sing them when they're homesick. Names are the worst thing to sing around me. Names stick."

Brammel, sitting nearby, made a small strangled noise. Mira understood why. Names were guild-craft. Names were the old magic, the thing everyone had spent five years pretending didn't exist, and here was a caged child casually announcing she'd been harvesting them like berries.

"Continue," Noor said, in the voice of a man holding onto a ledge.

"The second thing." She held up two fingers. "The Inquisition isn't marching on the free cities first. Or — they are, but that's not the real march. There's a separate company. Small. Elite. They call it the Quiet Column, which is the funniest name I've ever heard, because they're the loudest people in camp. They ride ahead of everything, and they're looking for something."

"The Wellspring," Noor said.

Mira pointed at him. "See, you already know. Which means either you're cleverer than the Inquisition, or someone told you, or—" she narrowed her eyes at him, enjoying herself, "—you guessed. Because the Inquisition only figured it out recently. Their chief interrogator — the tall one, the one who smells like vinegar and never blinks — received orders three weeks ago. Find the Wellspring. Before the covenant can be reforged. The usurper doesn't fear your little band of singers. He fears the *spring*. He fears what happens if the oaths start working again, because half his throne is built on promises he broke, and if broken promises start ringing like cracked bells, everyone will hear them."

She leaned forward on her cot.

"He's not afraid of magic," she said. "He's afraid of being *reminded*."

* * *

There was a silence after that, and Mira let it sit, because silences were tools and she owned several.

Then Sefa spoke from the tent flap. "How much of this can you prove?"

"Prove?" Mira almost laughed. "I'm a child in a cage. I can't prove the sky is blue. But I can tell you where to check. The Quiet Column musters on the east side, past the horse-lines. Gray cloaks, no banners, muffled tack — they wrap the hooves in felt, which is expensive, which means important. Ask any farrier. Farriers gossip worse than laundresses. And the nineteenth-day date — check the granary ledgers. Rations double for the last six days before a forced march. Someone clever could count sacks."

"Sacks," Brammel murmured, already reaching for his satchel. "Counting sacks. That's how empires fall. Not battles. Bookkeeping."

"And the third thing," Mira said, and here she slowed down, because the third thing was the one that mattered, the one she'd been carrying alone for weeks, heavy as a wet coat. "The reason I asked for you specifically. The reason I'm selling at all."

Noor looked at her.

"There's a new order circulating among the interrogators," she said. "Verbal only. Nothing written. About the binding they put on me — the listener-oath. They're refining it. The next generation of it doesn't just report singing." Her voice went careful, deliberate, each word set down like a foot on ice. "It *closes* the singer. Permanently. It doesn't silence the voice. It takes the part of the person that could ever hold an oath at all. They tested it. On prisoners. The ones who survived it aren't people anymore. They're — the interrogators used a word. *Vessels.* Empty vessels that can carry orders."

She watched Noor absorb it. Watched his face do the thing where all the color left it in order, like a tide going out.

"Ashen," he said quietly. Not a question. A fear.

"The child," Mira said. "Yes. If the Inquisition finds Ashen before you reach wherever you're going, they won't kill her. Killing a clause of the covenant wastes it. They'll close her and use her. An empty vessel wearing a living clause — that's a weapon that can unmake oaths from the inside. That's what the vinegar-smelling one wants. That's what he's *for*."

The tent had gone very still. Even the wind outside seemed to be holding its breath.

* * *

"So," Mira said, when she judged they'd suffered enough. "Terms."

Noor shifted on his crate. "Name them."

"One: I come with you. Not as a prisoner, not as a rescued orphan, not as 'the girl.' As a member of this company, with a vote when votes happen. I've been property twice. Never again."

"Agreed," Noor said instantly.

"That fast? You didn't consult—"

"They'd agree," Sefa said dryly from the flap. "Anyone who brings intelligence like that buys her seat. Go on, sprout."

"Two: nobody binds me to anything. Ever. No oath, no covenant-clause, no protective charm that turns out to have fine print. I've seen what binding does from the inside. If the day comes when the covenant needs me, I'll give it freely or not at all. Write that down. Actually write it down; I don't trust verbal agreements with adults."

Brammel was already writing. Mira approved of Brammel enormously.

"Three." She paused here, and for the first time since the negotiation began, the sharpness in her voice wavered — just a hairline crack, just enough to show there was a living girl under the glass. "Three: you tell me the truth. All of it. Whatever you're planning, whatever the cost is, whatever the Wellspring actually requires. I've spent a year surrounded by people who lied to me politely. My father lied about coming back. The Inquisition lied about the bread and the lessons being kindness. I would rather hear a terrible truth from you than a comfortable lie, and I will *know*, because I always know. I listen. It's the one thing I'm for."

Noor looked at her for a long moment. And then — and Mira would remember this later, would turn it over on many nights like a stone with something glinting in it — he didn't promise.

"I can't promise to tell you everything," he said. "Not because I want to hide it. Because I don't know all of it yet myself. But I can promise you this: I will never lie to you. If I can't answer, I'll say I can't answer. And when I do know — you'll be the first to hear it. Before the others, if you want."

Mira considered. A promise not to lie, from a man whose guild-signet she'd noticed glowing faintly under his sleeve whenever he spoke earnestly — she'd seen it, of course she'd seen it, she saw everything — was worth more than a promise to tell all, because telling-all promises were the first thing fear broke.

"Accepted," she said. "We have terms."

"We have terms," Noor agreed, and shook her hand solemnly, which she appreciated, because handshakes were contracts and contracts were sacred, even the small ones. Especially the small ones.

* * *

Later — after the camp had gone dark, after Sefa had smuggled her out wrapped in a laundry sack that smelled powerfully of lye and regret, after they'd reached the aqueduct mouth and Mira had been formally introduced to Ashen, who had looked at her once with those wide spring-colored eyes and then simply *hummed* at her, a single low note that made the cold coin behind Mira's breastbone sing back like a struck glass —

Later, sitting on a dry ledge in the dark with her knees pulled up, Mira did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding.

Nineteen days. A hidden spring somewhere west, past the ford, past the mountains' shoulder. An army moving toward cities full of people who didn't know. A child who couldn't speak anything but the First Tongue. A father walking beside her who had sold her once and was now carrying her pack without being asked, silent, ashamed, trying so hard that it hurt to watch, the way it hurts to watch a dog apologize.

And somewhere behind them, a tall man who smelled of vinegar, riding at the head of a Quiet Column, looking for water.

Ashen sat down next to her in the dark. Didn't speak. Just leaned, very slightly, until her small shoulder rested against Mira's arm, and hummed again — that same note, patient, like a question asked gently enough to wait years for the answer.

The coin behind Mira's breastbone warmed.

And for the first time since they'd bound her, since the gray oath had slid into her like a splinter of winter, Mira understood what she was going to do with everything she'd gathered — the names, the dates, the sacks, the nineteen days, the whole hoard of a year's careful listening.

She wasn't going to spend it on survival.

She was going to spend it on *them*.

"Nineteen days," she whispered into the dark, to no one, to everyone. "Then we'd better start counting sacks."

Ahead of them, at the aqueduct's bend, Noor's lantern flickered once — and Mira, watching it, saw him stop and turn back toward them with a look on his face she couldn't read yet.

She would read it. She read everything.

That was, after all, the one thing she was for.
