## 14. Nineteen Days

The safehouse was a tanner's abandoned drying loft above the river, which meant it smelled like a tanner's abandoned drying loft, which meant nobody would ever look for them there. Noor had learned long ago that the best hiding places were the ones that offended the nose. He'd spent five years being a place that offended the nose, in a way. A shop so dull the eye slid off it. A man so forgettable he could stand in a doorway during a raid and be mistaken for furniture.

He was starting to suspect those days were over. Furniture didn't travel with a river-priestess, a mapmaker who kept weeping at things he remembered, a traitor with a conscience, two children, and a drowned bell wrapped in oilcloth like an enormous, disapproving fish.

Mira had given them nineteen days. She'd said it the way children say terrible things—plainly, and then gone to steal bread from the kitchen because prophecy is hungry work.

Nineteen days until the vanguard reached the free cities. Nineteen days until the Inquisition found the Wellspring—if they hadn't already sent someone ahead. Nineteen days to decide what kind of people they were going to be.

Noor stood at the loft's one window, watching evening come down over Corvane's rooftops, and listened to his family argue about how to run away.

* * *

"We go east," Brammel said for the fourth time. He had a map spread across two flour barrels, weighted down with a candle stub, a boot, and Ashen, who liked sitting on maps because she said they were quiet. "Over the Kelder Pass. Three weeks if the weather holds, four if it doesn't, but no army follows anyone into the Kelders in autumn. The mountains will do our fighting for us."

"The mountains will do nothing for us," Sefa said. "That's rather their whole character."

"Exactly my point."

Sefa sat on the floor with her back against the wall and her bell beside her, and she had the particular stillness of a woman who had made up her mind some time ago and was now merely waiting for everyone else to catch up. "Brammel. Sweet man. If we run, what are we running *with*?"

"Ashen," Brammel said. "A living clause of the covenant. The single most precious—"

"And in twenty years, when the usurper's grandchildren are still hunting her, and every singer left alive has been burned or broken or forgotten their own names out of fear—what will Ashen be then?" Sefa touched the bell without ringing it, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "A memory keeping itself company. I have been a memory keeping itself company for eleven years, Brammel. It is not a life. It is a waiting room."

Noor turned from the window. "Where would we even take her? Past the mountains is the Salt Wastes, and past the wastes is nothing anyone's mapped." He glanced at Brammel. "Nothing *you've* mapped."

"I mapped everything once," Brammel said, with dignity. "Then I very carefully unmapped it. There's a difference between ignorance and amnesia, and I chose amnesia, and I'll thank you not to make me regret the choice twice in one week."

Odrik hadn't spoken much since the aqueduct. He sat apart, near the ladder, the way men sit when they're keeping watch on themselves. His hands were folded like something he was trying not to drop. When he finally spoke, his voice came out rough.

"You're all talking about running as though it's an option we haven't already used up." He looked at Noor. "I sold names to the Inquisition for two years. Do you know what I learned in their offices? They don't chase singers because singers frighten them. They chase singers because the Wellspring frightens them. Every report, every warrant, every interrogation—it all comes back to one question their masters keep asking: *where is it.*" He swallowed. "They know it exists. They've always known. And Mira says their scouts went west three weeks ago."

The room went quiet enough that Noor could hear the river below, moving the way rivers do—patient, indifferent, carrying everything eventually somewhere else.

"So we don't get to choose whether this ends at the Wellspring," Odrik said. "We only choose whether we're there when it does."

"That's not a reason," Brammel protested. "That's a *doom*. You can't plan around a doom."

"You can," said Mira, from the corner, around a mouthful of stolen bread. "You just have to arrive first."

Everyone looked at her. She looked back with the unblinking composure of a nine-year-old who had spent months listening to war councils through a bond she never asked for, and who had consequently lost all patience with adults pretending problems were complicated when they were merely unpleasant.

"The armies reach the free cities in nineteen days," she said. "The Wellspring is eleven days' hard march west. The Inquisition's outriders are faster but small—they can't hold the spring, only find it and wait. So the real question isn't flee or fight." She brushed crumbs off her knees. "It's whether any of you can walk fast."

Ashen, sitting on the map, laughed. It was a strange sound, bright and slightly wrong, like a bell heard underwater—and then, unexpectedly, she began to hum. Just a few notes, low and looping. Noor felt it in his teeth. He'd felt it before, in the aqueduct, in Sefa's chapel under the bridge: the sense that the child wasn't making music but *remembering* it aloud, humming something older than the walls around them.

"She's right," Noor said.

Everyone turned to him, and he realized—with a small, cold shock of self-recognition—that he had just spoken in the voice he used for announcing shop hours. Careful. Quiet. The voice of a man positioning himself near the exit.

He cleared his throat and tried again.

"She's right. And so is Odrik, and so is Sefa, and Brammel—" he held up a hand before the mapmaker could object, "—Brammel is right that the Kelders would keep us alive. That's the problem. Everything that keeps us alive keeps the covenant starving. We've been surviving for five years. All of us, in our different holes—the shop, the chapel, the erased maps, the bought silence. Surviving beautifully. Surviving so well that the world fell down around us while we dusted our shelves." He heard his own voice change as he said it, lose its carefulness, and it frightened him more than the Inquisition ever had. "Running east doesn't save Ashen. It just decides where she gets caught."

"Frightening speech," Brammel muttered. "From a man whose greatest previous act of courage was underpricing a luck-charm."

"It was a *very* good charm," Noor said. "Inert, but well-made."

* * *

They argued until the candle burned down to the boot.

That was the shape of it, anyway: Sefa and Odrik for the Wellspring, Brammel against, Mira above it all like weather, Ashen humming on her map. Noor mostly listened, and discovered that listening was harder than talking, because listening meant noticing things.

He noticed that Sefa never raised her voice, and that when Brammel got loud she got quieter, and that this worked on him like water on a fire. He noticed that Odrik flinched whenever anyone mentioned the capital, where his daughter had been—where his daughter *was*, because Mira's information had changed her from a grief into a mission, and Odrik now checked the western window every quarter hour as though the supply roads might deliver her by parcel post. He noticed that Brammel's objections weren't really about mountains at all. Halfway through the third hour, the old mapmaker said, "I unmapped myself once already. I don't have anything left to erase. If we fail at this spring, there's no second forgetting for me. I'll remember failing forever."

And Noor noticed—this was the one that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone—that every argument in the room was his own arguments, the ones he'd been having with himself since the night Ashen knocked. Flee. Hide. Stay small. Live. He had gotten very good at losing those arguments quietly, in the dark, behind a counter, and then getting up in the morning and selling trinkets anyway.

For five years, staying alive had been the whole of his courage, and he had told himself it was enough.

Ashen slid off the map and crossed the floor to him. She did this sometimes—crossed rooms to stand near him for no reason he could name, the way compass needles find north. She took his hand and turned it palm-up and looked at the guild-signet scar there, the faint ring of light that woke when she spoke the First Tongue.

She said something in the old language. Two words. He knew them now; he'd spent three nights with Master Corvin's primer learning exactly these two words, because Ashen repeated them at him more than any others, patient as tide.

*Your turn.*

Not *your turn to speak.* Something bigger. Your turn in the song. Your verse. The clause after hers.

"I can't lead," Noor said—to her, to the room, to himself; by now the categories had blurred. "I'm not—I hid for five years. Sefa kept her vows. Odrik sacrificed. Brammel built things. What did I do? I sold *buttons*."

"You survived," Sefa said simply. "Do you know how many of us didn't? The guild had forty singers when the edicts came. I can count the ones I know are breathing on one hand, and I'd have fingers to spare." She rose, joints cracking, and put her hand briefly on his shoulder—the first time she'd touched him unprompted, and it landed like a knighting. "Someone had to stay alive to answer the door when she knocked. You did that. Five years of dull, unglorious, stubborn survival. Don't you dare call it nothing in front of the child."

"The child agrees with me," Noor said.

"The child," Ashen said—in common tongue, suddenly, her rare and rusty voice, startling everyone including apparently herself—"*goes*."

And that settled it, the way children settle things. Not with logic. With direction.

* * *

They planned by lamplight, and the planning was its own strange joy, and Noor caught himself grinning twice like an idiot and didn't stop either time.

Eleven days west, if they avoided the King's Road entirely. Brammel, coaxed, grudging, and then unstoppable, began reconstructing the country from memory—his true memory, the one he'd cut out, which was returning in pieces like a man remembering a language mid-sentence. "There's a drover's track along the Vell," he said, sketching freehand, lines appearing under his charcoal as though they'd been waiting there all along. "It's not on any chart anymore because I removed it personally, which means the Inquisition's charts don't have it either. My cowardice is your tactical advantage. Let no one say I suffer for nothing."

Sefa packed the bell. "Its voice masks ours from scrying," she reminded them, "but it drowns other sounds too. Anyone wearing it walks deaf to everything but each other. Which," she added, looking around the room, "may be the most honest condition any of us have lived under in years."

Odrik laid out the army's supply routes from memory—the gray columns, the grain wagons, the gaps between garrisons where a small group might slip through like thread through cloth. His hands shook doing it, and he did it anyway, and Noor watched him do it and understood that this was Odrik's penance and his prayer at once: every route he mapped toward the Wellspring was a route he was also mapping toward his daughter, and he carried both weights without setting either down.

Mira appointed herself quartermaster through the simple method of taking inventory and refusing to give the list back. Ashen sat on the floor winding a thread around her fingers, singing under her breath, and when Noor asked what the song was she said, "Road-song. For walking. Old songs for walking so feet remember why."

"Nineteen days," Noor said at last, standing over the half-finished map, the candle guttering, the smell of old leather thick in the dark. "Eleven to the spring. That leaves eight. Eight days to find whatever's missing before the Inquisition finds it first—or before we learn what Elleryn sold them besides singer-detection."

The name dropped into the room like a stone into a well.

They hadn't spoken of Master Elleryn since the aqueduct, since Sefa had first said aloud what the guild's survivors whispered and denied: that the last elder of the Vesper Guild was alive, and that her survival had a price, and that the price had a ledger. Noor had refused to believe it. Was still refusing, in the stubborn cellar of himself where he kept the things he couldn't afford to know yet. Elleryn had taught him his first oath-verse. Elleryn had held his hands over the training brazier and told him *a singer's word is the only thing he owns, so spend it like gold.* Elleryn had—

Had taught him loyalty, said the stone in his chest. So what had she taught it *for*?

"We leave at dawn," Noor said. "West. All of us."

Brammel sighed the sigh of a man surrendering to history. "If I die in the mountains after all this, I want it noted that I objected."

"Noted," said four voices at once.

"And if we live," Brammel added, rolling the map with great care, "I'm remapping everything. Every spring, every standing stone, every buried door. Let the next generation of cowards find their own hiding places."

* * *

Dawn came gray and clean, rinsed by overnight rain. They went down the tanner's stair single file—Sefa with the bell, Brammel with the map, Odrik with his face set west, Mira with the bread, Ashen with her hand in Noor's, humming her road-song so softly only he could hear it, only he was meant to hear it, feet remembering why.

At the alley's mouth Noor stopped once and looked back at the city that had hidden him. Corvane in early light, chimneys smoking, shutters opening, the apothecary across the lane already sweeping his step. Somewhere in it was a little shop with a sign that promised nothing, and a life so small it fit inside a day, and he found he could look at all of it without wanting it back. That was new. That was terrifying. That was, he suspected, what purpose felt like from the inside—less like a flame than like a door finally open, with wind coming through.

He turned west.

They had made it three streets when Mira stopped dead, head tilting, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear—the bond behind her eyes, the wire the usurper had threaded through her childhood. Her face went pale and sharp.

"What is it?" Noor crouched to her level. "Mira. What did you hear?"

Her voice came out very small and very certain.

"They found the elder," she said. "Elleryn. The Inquisition picked her up yesterday—she's being taken west too. Under guard." She swallowed. "On the same road. To the same place."

Noor straightened slowly. Ahead of them the drover's track ran west along the river, empty in the morning mist, toward the mountains, the Wellspring, and everything waiting there.

Toward the woman who had betrayed them—or the woman who had saved him—or both, which was worse, which was always worse.

"Well," Noor heard himself say, in a voice that belonged, unmistakably, to a leader of people. "Then we'd better catch up."
