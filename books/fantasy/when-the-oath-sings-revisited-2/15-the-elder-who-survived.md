## 15. The Elder Who Survived

They caught up with the Inquisition column on the second day, which was not a thing anyone said out loud, because saying it out loud made it real, and because Odrik was still sulking about the drover's track.

"We could have gone around," he said for the fourth time that morning, adjusting the collar of his supply-sergeant's uniform. He wore it badly, the way a man wears a coat borrowed from someone taller. "The south ford. Two days' detour, but no checkpoints."

"The south ford floods this time of year," Brammel said without looking up from the map he'd drawn from memory the night before — one of the few maps in existence now that showed what actually existed, since he had spent eleven years drawing ones that didn't. "You'd know that if you'd ever left your supply tents."

"I knew it," Odrik said. "I was testing you."

"You were complaining," Mira said sweetly, from her perch on the cart's tailboard. She had claimed the tailboard as hers by right of conquest sometime yesterday, and none of them had been fool enough to contest it. Nine years old, sharp as glass, and currently eating an apple with the focused intensity of someone who had been fed prison rations for months. "There's a difference."

Noor let them argue. It was good for them, the arguing. A company that argued was a company that still believed they would all be alive tomorrow to be annoyed at each other.

Ashen sat beside him on the cart bench, wrapped in a cloak two sizes too big, watching the mist burn off the river meadows with that expression Noor was learning to read. Not blankness. Attention. The child saw the world as something sung, Sefa had explained once, and so everything held still for Ashen the way a note holds still inside a bell.

"What is it?" Noor asked quietly.

Ashen's small hand rose and pointed — not west toward the column they were shadowing, but north, up a fold of hillside where a shepherd's hut stood alone against the sky. Smoke rose from its chimney. Thin. Gray. The smoke of someone who had learned to burn wet wood because dry wood meant buying wood and buying wood meant being remembered.

"There is a silence there," Ashen said. In the First Tongue it sounded less like words than like a stone dropped into deep water. "A silence shaped like a person. Shaped like—" the child's brow furrowed, searching for the shape of it, "—like a door that locked itself."

Noor felt the bottom drop out of his stomach.

He knew exactly one person who had ever described herself that way. *A singer must be able to close,* Elleryn used to say, pacing the guildhall with her hands clasped behind her back while apprentices dozed over their primer copies. *Magic is loud, children. Loud things get found. Learn to shut your door.*

"She's alive," he said. His voice came out strange. "Mira said the Inquisition took her west. But she's here. On the hillside. Burning wet wood."

Sefa reined in beside the cart. She looked where Ashen pointed, and Noor watched her face go through several expressions very quickly, like cards being shuffled. "That's impossible."

"Apparently not."

"They paraded her through Corvane in chains. I saw it myself. There were ballads within the week." Sefa's mouth twisted. "Bad ones."

"Maybe she escaped."

"Maybe." Sefa did not sound like a woman who believed in maybe. She sounded like a river-priestess who had spent forty years learning that water always finds the lowest ground, and people usually find the easiest lie. "Or maybe the chains were theater. Maybe the whole arrest was theater. Noor." She turned in the saddle to look at him directly. "If Elleryn Vael is sitting in a shepherd's hut three miles from an active Inquisition supply road, then either she is the luckiest woman in the realm, or she is exactly where she arranged to be."

* * *

They left the cart in a stand of alders with Odrik and Mira — Odrik because his uniform would get him hanged faster than any warrant if the wrong eyes caught him walking uphill with fugitives, and Mira because she refused to stay behind and then lost the argument with Sefa in under a minute, which Noor privately considered a new record.

The climb took most of an hour. Brammel led, reading the land like the old guild mapmaker he had been before he'd unmade himself — noting the sheep path, the windbreak of gorse, the way the hut's approach was screened from the road by a rise of ground. "She chose this site well," he murmured. "One way in visible. Two ways out. Sightline to the valley."

"That could describe any shepherd's hut."

"It could," Brammel agreed. "But how many shepherds put their woodpile where it blocks the window?"

Noor stopped at the edge of the dooryard, heart hammering, and made himself do the last thing he wanted to do, which was think.

Because Sefa was right. Of course she was right. He had known it since the moment Ashen pointed, and he had let himself climb the hill anyway, because hope is a stupid animal that will walk into any trap if the trap smells like home.

Five years ago, when the edicts came down, when the Vesper Guild burned its own records in the courtyard and scattered, Master Elleryn had pulled him aside by the collar like a stray kitten and told him: *Forget it all, Noor. Forget it so completely that even you believe you've forgotten. That's how you survive. Smallness is armor.* And he had obeyed. He had built his whole life on her instruction, sold his inert charms, hummed no songs, kept his signet buried in a flour jar, and it had worked, and he had been grateful, and he had never once asked the obvious question.

Who told the Inquisition how to find singers?

Everyone assumed it was torture. Everyone said it sadly, the way you talk about a dead friend. *They broke her, poor woman. They broke her and she gave them the resonance tests, the salt-circle method, the way to make a hidden signet glow.* And everyone forgave her, because she was Elleryn, because she had taught three generations of them loyalty, and because the alternative thought was unbearable.

But she wasn't broken. She was up here. Smoking her chimney on purpose, one way in, two ways out, woodpile blocking the window, three miles from the road that carried the men whose methods she had — allegedly — sold.

Noor stood in the dooryard and discovered he was shaking.

"Noor?" Sefa, behind him, quiet.

"She didn't break," he said. "Sefa. They never had to break her. She *taught* them." The words tasted like copper. "Everything we've been running from. The checkpoints. The resonance tests. The reason singers die in the low quarter every month. It has her handwriting on it."

Brammel had gone very still. "We should still knock," he said finally. "If we turn away now, we'll never know which lie we're living inside. And she may yet know things we need." He paused. "Also, I am seventy-one years old and I have climbed a hill for nothing exactly twice in my life, and both times I regretted it more than anything I've ever done."

It was such a Brammel sentence that Noor almost laughed, and laughing felt obscene, and he did it anyway, because grief and laughter live in the same chest and only one of them lets you keep breathing.

He knocked.

* * *

The door opened on the third knock, and there she was.

Older. That was the first thing. Five years had taken the black from her hair entirely and carved new lines around her mouth, and she was thinner, dressed in a shepherd's rough wool that no one who knew her would ever believe. But the eyes hadn't changed at all. Gray, level, patient — the eyes that had looked down at him across a decade of lessons, that had seen everything and given away only what served.

"Noor Delacroix," said Master Elleryn.

"You're supposed to be dead," he said. "Or in chains. Or in the capital. You're supposed to be somewhere other than standing in your own doorway burning wet wood."

"Wet wood smokes less visibly," she said. "Dry wood burns hot and clean. Anyone watching for a fire sees dry wood's smoke for miles. Come in before you lecture me on my own tradecraft in the open air; you always did save your best material for audiences."

Inside, the hut was spare and immaculate. One bed. One table. Books — actual books, forbidden books, and Noor's eye snagged on the spines before he could stop it: primers, canticles, a codex of covenant-law that the guild had supposedly burned its last copy of in the courtyard five years ago. His master's hidden primer sat in his own pack downstairs, one of perhaps six in the world.

And on the table, open, weighted with a river stone: papers. Neat columns. Names.

He didn't step closer. He didn't need to. He recognized the hand. He had learned his letters from that hand.

"Those are singers," he said.

"Yes."

"Living singers."

"Some of them." Elleryn folded her hands — the same gesture she had used before every hard lesson, the one that meant *sit down, child, this will hurt and I will not soften it*. "Sit. All of you. You've walked a long way to hate me and I won't have you doing it standing up, you'll cramp, and then you'll blame me for that too."

They sat. Sefa like a drawn blade pretending to be a chair. Brammel like a man at his own trial. Ashen curled next to Noor, and Noor noticed — with a lurch — that the child was trembling faintly, staring at the older woman with an expression he had never seen on that face before. Fear, yes. But also recognition. The way you recognize a sound heard once, long ago, in another room.

"Ashen," Noor said softly. "Do you know her?"

"She was there," Ashen said. "At the closing. When the clauses were closed." Small fingers pressed against their own sternum. "Her voice was one of the voices that closed us. Before. Long before. Her voice is old in the song. Older than her body."

Silence. Even the fire seemed to hold its crackle.

"That's not possible," Sefa said flatly. "Elleryn is sixty-some years old. The closing of the clauses was centuries ago."

"Is it?" Elleryn said mildly. And for a heartbeat — Noor would swear this to his dying day, and after today he had very little dying-day left to swear things to — for a heartbeat the lines of age seemed to rearrange themselves on her face, like a map redrawn, like a door opening onto a much larger room. Then it settled back into ordinary old-woman flesh, and she sighed, long and tired. "Well. There goes that disguise. I'd hoped to keep it until the spring."

"Master." Noor's voice cracked on the word and he hated it. "What are you?"

"Your elder," she said. "In the oldest sense the guild has. I was there when the covenant was first reforged, Noor. Three hundred and forty years ago, when it broke the time before this time. I helped sing it back together. And I have been dying slowly ever since, because that is what the singing costs those who hold the frame of it, and because I was a coward about paying the whole price, and I have spent three centuries watching the bill come due on other people instead." She looked at the papers on the table. At the names. "Including these."

"Why?" It came out of Noor like something torn. "Why give them to the Inquisition? Why teach them to find us? You of all — you taught us *loyalty*, you — I built my whole life on what you told me—"

"I know," she said simply. "That's why I'm going to tell you the truth, even though the truth will finish what the Inquisition started. Because you're going to the Wellspring, and you cannot arrive there believing what you currently believe, or the covenant will fail, and this time there will be no one left to reforge it. So. Listen."

She rose and stood with her back to the fire, an old woman silhouetted, and when she spoke again it was in the First Tongue — fluent, ancient, terrible — and though Noor understood barely a word of it, he felt it. Felt it in his teeth, in the buried signet against his ribs, in Ashen's small hand seizing his wrist.

Then common speech again, and her voice shook, just slightly, just enough:

"The covenant does not run on magic. Magic runs on the covenant. Every ward, every healing, every scrying-glass and storm-call in the free cities — all of it is interest paid out on a principal that someone must personally guarantee. That is what the Wellspring is. Not a source. A *ledger*. And a ledger requires a guarantor — a living anchor, bound into it, who can never hide, never lie, never again be small. The last anchor died at the breaking. The covenant has been starving since. Do you understand? It isn't dead. It is *calling in its debts*. And the usurper knows it. He doesn't fear sorcery, child. He fears the audit."

"And the names," Sefa said icily. "The detection methods. The dead singers. Was that also bookkeeping?"

Elleryn met her gaze without flinching, and Noor saw that this, at least, cost her something real.

"I bought my survival with their deaths," she said, "and I told myself I did it to protect the guild's future, and I have repeated that lie so many times I can almost sing it. Some of it is even true — half the people on that list I warned in advance, moved, sheltered. The Inquisition thinks I'm hunting them. Half of them are alive because the huntress was secretly the fox." Her voice hardened. "But not half. Not all. Some I gave up whole, because a betrayal that costs nothing protects nothing, and they had to believe me. I chose which of my children would die so that others might live, and I have done the arithmetic every night for five years, and the sum is that I am a monster who happens to be useful, and you may hate me freely, because you are correct to."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a bird sang, oblivious and cheerful, which felt deeply rude.

"So the arrest," Brammel said slowly. "The chains in Corvane."

"Theatrics. I needed to be moved west, near the Wellspring, with an escort of Inquisition troops — because in nineteen days the vanguard arrives, and when it does, someone is going to have to be standing *inside* their command structure when it matters." She looked around the room, at each of them, and for the first time Noor saw how tired she was — tired in a way that three hundred years puts into a person. "You want to race the armies to the Wellspring. Good. So do I. And I can get you past the vanguard's pickets, because I trained half their officers' mages myself."

"Why should we trust you?" Noor asked. It came out quieter than he intended. Almost gentle. "After everything."

"You shouldn't," Elleryn said. "Trust is for people who haven't lied to you. What you should do is use me, carefully, the way I used you — and watch me every hour. That's the lesson, Noor. The last one I'll ever teach you." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile he had ever seen, and it was also, unmistakably, his master's smile. "Loyalty isn't trusting someone. Loyalty is staying anyway, with your eyes open."

Down the hill, faint on the morning air, came the sound of horns. The Inquisition column, moving out.

Nineteen days. Eighteen, now.

Noor looked at the woman who had raised him, who had betrayed him, who had maybe saved him, who was possibly three centuries old, and heard himself say the leader-thing, the responsible thing, the thing every part of him wanted never to say:

"Tell us how to get past the pickets."

* * *

They came down off the hill as the sun climbed, and Noor walked last, and Ashen walked beside him holding his hand, which the child had never done unprompted before.

"You're angry," Ashen observed.

"Furious."

"But you will use her."

"Yes."

"Because anger is a small song," Ashen said, in that simple cadence that was somehow never childish, "and the Wellspring is a large one. And you are trying to learn to sing large." The child squeezed his hand. "It hurts. Learning that. My clauses hurt too, when they grew."

Noor laughed — a wet, broken laugh — because apparently that was the day he was having.

Ahead, at the alder stand, Odrik was arguing with Mira about apple cores again, and Sefa was already checking the cart axle, and Brammel was redrawing the map with new roads on it, and the world went on being stubborn and ordinary and theirs.

Behind them, unhurried, an old woman in shepherd's wool began to descend the hill, carrying nothing but a staff and five years of lies she was finally done telling —

and Noor did not see, because no one saw, the moment she passed beneath a rowan tree and paused, just briefly, to lay her palm against its trunk,

nor hear what the tree said back.
