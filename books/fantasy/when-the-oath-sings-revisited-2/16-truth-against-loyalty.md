## 16. Truth Against Loyalty

Sefa had drowned once, properly drowned, in the river below her bell-tower when she was nineteen. She remembered it fondly, in the way one remembers a hard teacher: the cold closing over her head, the panic, and then—beneath the panic—a strange stillness, as if the water had asked her a question and waited for her answer. She had answered by living. Everything since had been footnotes to that moment.

She thought of it now because Elleryn Vane's parlor had the same quality of held breath. The room was beautiful the way a snare is beautiful: polished darkwood, a fire that burned too evenly, windows hung with curtains dyed the exact gray of respectable grief. And in the center of it, pouring tea as though the last fifteen years had been a mild clerical error, sat the woman who had taught Noor Delacroix everything he knew—including, apparently, how to betray.

"You're not drinking," Elleryn observed. She was small and silver-haired and moved like a woman who had spent a lifetime being underestimated and had learned to weaponize it. Her hands, Sefa noted, were steady. That steadiness cost something. Sefa had watched enough liars pour tea to know.

"I don't drink with people who sell children's names to Inquisitors," Sefa said.

"Mm." Elleryn set down the pot. "And yet you drank my wine at Midwinter, the year I hosted the guild's vigil. You said it tasted like forgiveness."

"It did. I was younger then, and wrong about many things."

Behind her, Noor made a sound like a man swallowing his own tongue. Sefa didn't turn to look at him. She could feel him anyway—the way you feel a current under a calm surface—wretched, loyal, torn straight down his middle like wet bread. He had been standing near the door since they'd arrived, half in and half out, a boy caught between two mothers even though neither of them had borne him.

"Master Elleryn." His voice came out cracked. "The detection method. The resonance bells they use at checkpoints. The way they can find a singer through three walls of stone. Did you—"

"Sit down, Noor."

"Don't tell me to sit down."

"Then stand there and be uncomfortable; you've earned it." Elleryn folded her hands. "Yes. I taught them. Every word of it. The harmonic signatures, the tells in the breath, the way an oath spoken in fear leaves a residue for six days. I wrote them a manual. They gave me a house and let me keep my books." She smiled, and it was the worst smile Sefa had ever seen on an old woman: patient, unashamed, and very tired underneath. "Ask me if I regret it."

"Do you?" Sefa asked.

"No." A beat. "I regret the price. Not the arithmetic."

* * *

Sefa had been a priestess long enough to know that most evil was just bookkeeping done by frightened people. The Drowned Bell had no doctrine beyond this: the water takes everything eventually, so love it while you can carry it. She had buried singers, smuggled them, sung last rites over ones the Inquisition burned, and never—not once—had she met a monster. Only accountants. Men and women balancing ledgers where the columns were lives.

Elleryn Vane was simply the best accountant she'd ever seen.

"Explain the arithmetic, then," Sefa said, settling into the chair across from her like a judge taking a bench. Beside the hearth, Ashen sat cross-legged on the rug, watching Elleryn with those eyes that saw thread and song instead of furniture and faces. Mira stood behind the child with one hand on Ashen's shoulder, the other hidden in her skirt, gripping a little knife Odrik probably didn't know she carried. Children learned young what rooms required knives.

"The guild had four hundred members when the edicts came," Elleryn said. "Four hundred names on a list, and a usurper who read lists aloud before executions, for the morale value." She turned her cup a quarter-turn. "I could save perhaps forty by teaching the Inquisition to find the rest. Or I could hide with all four hundred and watch them find us anyway—they would have, within two years, because we were sloppy, sentimental, and proud—and lose all four hundred plus whatever cities burned while we hid. I chose the version where some of you lived to hate me."

"And the forty you saved," Sefa said quietly. "How many are left?"

Elleryn's cup paused mid-turn. "Eleven," she said. "You know the number. You've been collecting them."

"Three of them are standing in your parlor."

"Yes." Something flickered across the old woman's face—grief, maybe, or its fossil. "Which is why I'm going to say the rest of this quickly, before you decide I'm only a monster and stop listening. Because you need what I have, priestess. All of you do. And what I have is the truth nobody else survived long enough to write down."

Noor hadn't sat. He stood by the door with his jaw set, and Sefa watched him fight himself in real time—the boy who owed this woman his letters, his craft, his survival, versus the man who'd spent five years watching her students burn. Loyalty, Sefa thought, was a river too. It cut canyons. You couldn't argue someone out of a canyon.

"You taught them how to hunt us," Noor said. "Why would we trust anything you—"

"Because I also taught you," Elleryn said, "and look how well you turned out. Now sit *down*, apprentice, before I remember I can still make you copy out the Canticle of Binding forty times."

To Sefa's lasting surprise—he'd deny it later, she'd make sure of it—Noor sat.

* * *

What Elleryn had, it turned out, was the ending of the song.

They gathered around the table—Sefa, Noor, Brammel with his ruined mapmaker's hands folded tight, Odrik hovering at the edge like a man afraid chairs might report on him—and Elleryn unrolled a scroll so old the vellum had gone the color of weak tea. Sefa recognized the notation instantly and felt her stomach drop. Guild script. Pre-covenant notation. The kind of thing that existed now in exactly two places: here, and in the Wellspring itself.

"This is the reforging rite," Elleryn said. "Not the folk version the priests mumble at festivals. The real one. And before any of you get misty-eyed about singing together around a pretty spring, understand what the last line costs." She put one finger on the final stanza. "Every voice matters. Yes. But a covenant without an anchor unravels in a generation—that's why it broke the first time, or part of why. The original anchor was the First Tongue itself, carried by the clauses. Living fragments. Like your child there."

Ashen looked up at the sound of the word *anchor* the way a dog looks up at the word *walk*.

"When the clauses were hunted and closed," Elleryn went on, "the covenant lost its mooring. To reforge it, you need a new anchor. A singer who binds themselves into the covenant's root. Permanently." She looked at each of them in turn, unhurried. "Bound, the anchor cannot lie. Not white lies, not merciful silences, not the small daily fictions that let a person survive among other people. Every promise they speak becomes binding law. Every truth in them becomes visible to anyone who listens. They can never hide again—not their face, not their name, not their heart. And the bond doesn't end at death. It passes on. You become a load-bearing wall in the architecture of the world, forever."

Silence. The fire crackled its too-even crackle.

"Well," Brammel said finally, into the quiet, "that's put rather a damper on the group singing."

"Brammel," Sefa warned.

"No, I mean it. I erased every magical landmark from my own charts to survive. I forgot my own mother's guild-name on purpose. And you're telling me the ticket to saving the world is to become a lighthouse that never goes dark?" He shook his head slowly. "Some of us became cowards professionally. It doesn't disqualify us from noticing when the job description has teeth."

"That's precisely why I'm telling you," Elleryn said. "Because none of you will do it blindly. I need you to walk into the Wellspring knowing exactly what the last verse asks. If you go in ignorant and falter at the end—if the anchor hesitates—the covenant takes the hesitation as refusal, and it won't offer again for a hundred years. The armies arrive in"—she glanced at Mira—"how many days, girl?"

"Nineteen was four days ago," Mira said. "Fifteen. Less, if they force-march. They will. The usurper promised the vanguard the spring itself."

"Fifteen days," Elleryn repeated, "to reach the Wellspring, gather every voice you can, and find one soul willing to pay the toll at the end." She sat back. "That's the arithmetic. I've had five years to check my figures. There's no error."

* * *

Afterward, while the others argued logistics in low voices over Brammel's charts, Sefa found Noor on the back step, sitting in the gray drizzle like a man who'd forgotten weather applied to him.

She sat down beside him. For a while neither spoke. Rain ticked against the eaves. Somewhere inside, Ashen was humming—the First Tongue, always, even at rest—and the sound came through the walls thin as smoke.

"She lied to me about the bells," Noor said at last. "Two years ago. I visited. I asked her directly whether the Inquisition had a reliable detection method, because Odrik's contacts said they did, and she looked me in the eye and told me the rumors were exaggerated. I walked away lighter. I trusted her with my relief." His hands curled in his lap. "She sent me home safer *because* she'd armed the hunters better. Do you understand? My safety was her alibi."

"Yes," Sefa said. "I understood that from the tea."

"How can you be so calm?"

"Water teaches patience." She pulled her cloak tighter. "Also I drowned once. Puts things in proportion."

He huffed—almost a laugh, which she counted as a victory. Then the humor drained out of him and he was just a tired man in the rain, thirty-some years old and carrying a century's weight. "Sefa. What if she's right? What if the only reason any of us lived was her ledger?" His voice dropped. "What if I owe my life to the person who made my life hardest to keep?"

"There it is," Sefa said gently.

"There *what* is?"

"The question that's actually eating you. Not whether she betrayed the guild. Whether you're allowed to stop loving her." She watched him flinch, because it was true, and true things flinch when named. "Noor Delacroix. You spent five years selling inert charms and forgetting your own training, and it nearly killed you, and do you know why? Because a life built on smallness is still built on hiding, and hiding is just lying with better posture. Elleryn chose the big lie to save lives. You chose ten thousand small ones. Same posture. Different scale." She let that land, then softened. "The covenant's last verse asks for a person who cannot hide anymore. You should ask yourself why that idea terrifies you more than dying does."

"Because I'm good at hiding," he said miserably.

"Yes. And it's the only thing you're good at that I'll be glad to see you lose."

Inside, the humming stopped. A moment later the door opened and Ashen stood there, barefoot despite the cold, looking at Noor with an expression far too old for the small face carrying it. The child crossed the step, took Noor's rain-wet hand in both small ones, and pressed it flat against their own chest.

Noor went very still.

"What do they see?" Sefa asked softly.

"I don't—I can't—" He closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and not from the drizzle. "They're showing me the shape of the song. Where the anchor sits. It's..." He laughed, broken and wondering. "It's shaped like a doorway, Sefa. An open one. No door in it at all. Just light and everyone able to see through."

Ashen patted his hand twice, solemn as a physician delivering a diagnosis, and went back inside.

Sefa looked at Noor—at the man who had spent half a decade practicing invisibility and had just been handed, by a mute child, a picture of himself as an open door—and saw the terrible beginning of acceptance there. Not peace. Not yet. Just the first honest inch of it.

"We leave before dawn," she said. "Brammel knows a route along the old canal. Fifteen days, if the ford holds."

"The ford," Noor repeated vaguely. Then, sharper: "Wait. The river crossing at Callow's Ford—the army's route runs parallel, Varn's column was sighted—"

As if summoned, hooves sounded on the lane outside. Not one horse. Many. And above the rain, thin and clear, came the note Sefa had dreaded for five years: the resonance bell, singing its hunting-pitch, sweeping the street like a lantern.

Elleryn appeared in the doorway behind them, and to her credit—for whatever her credit was worth now—the old woman's face had gone pale.

"They're not here for me," she said quietly. "I'm still on their list of protected assets. They're here for the child." She met Sefa's eyes, and for one unguarded moment Sefa saw past the accountant to the terrified woman underneath, doing sums with her own life in the balance. "Back gate. Now. And Noor—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the word, "—whatever you decide about the last verse... decide fast. Deciding slowly is how I ended up like this."

Sefa was already moving, gathering the others, counting heads the way she counted swimmers before a flood. Outside, the bell's note climbed higher, closer, hungry.

Fifteen days to the Wellspring. And the hunt had found them twelve hours early.
