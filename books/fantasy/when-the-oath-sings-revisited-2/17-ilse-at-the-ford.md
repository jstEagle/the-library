## 17. Ilse at the Ford

The river came up on them faster than Ilse Varn had expected, the way rivers always did—announcing themselves first as a smell, green and cold and ancient, then as a sound under the horses' hooves, then as a wide gray ribbon unspooling through the valley with all the patience of something that had been here long before crowns or edicts or the particular folly of men.

She had been riding since before dawn. Her column was smaller than she would have liked: forty riders, two wagons of siege equipment that had been meant for a fortress and now chased six people through open country, and a scout named Ferren who had developed, over the past three days, an unsettling habit of looking at her sideways whenever he thought she wasn't watching.

"You've been quiet, Captain," Ferren said, drawing his horse up beside hers as they crested the last hill. Below them, the ford spread out like a held breath—shallow water braiding over pale stones, willows leaning in from both banks, and there, small against the morning mist, the company. Six figures. A cart. A drowned bell wrapped in oilcloth that caught the light like a bruise.

"I'm always quiet," Ilse said. "It's why they made me a captain."

"No, Captain. You're *quiet* quiet." He said it carefully, the way a man handles a blade he suspects has turned in its sheath. "You haven't given an order you believed in since the singer's camp."

Ilse did not answer that, because answering it would have required her to admit it was true, and she had built twenty years of service on the principle that a thing admitted could never be unsaid.

* * *

Here is what Ilse Varn knew about herself, kept in the same ledger where she kept everything else: she believed in the edicts. Not the way the usurper's courtiers believed in them—with the flexible conviction of men who would believe in anything that fed them—but truly, stubbornly, from the marrow out. She had seen what magic did. She had been eleven years old when the War of the Sundered Oaths ended, old enough to remember the refugee columns, the children with burns shaped like sigils, the way whole valleys went silent because every voice in them had been spent on someone's grand working. Magic, she had concluded at eleven, was simply fire that pretended to be a conversation. It asked you to speak and then it burned down your house.

So she had taken the warrant. Hunter of singers, breaker of covens, keeper of the Inquisition's gray ledger. And for five years the ledger had filled with names, and she had told herself each one was a fire prevented, and mostly she had slept well.

Mostly.

Lately the sleeping had gotten worse. It had started with the captured singer—a hollow-eyed woman named Tessaly whom Ilse had interrogated for six hours in a room with no windows. Tessaly had not screamed. She had not begged. She had described the covenant the way a clerk describes tax law: patiently, precisely, with citations older than the usurper's grandfather. *Law*, she had called it. Older than the crown. The binding that kept the guardian spirits attending to mortal affairs, that kept rivers honest and harvests bound by promise rather than appetite. And when it broke—and it had broken, once, catastrophically—the spirits had not raged. They had simply *left*. Stopped listening. And the world had learned what it felt like when nothing anywhere was obligated to keep its word.

Ilse had written the execution order anyway. She was a professional. But she had written it slowly, and afterward she had sat in her tent doing sums that refused to come out right: if the covenant was law, then breaking it was not sorcery but *rebellion against the oldest government there was*, and she had spent five years arresting taxpayers and calling them traitors.

Or criminals. She still hadn't decided which word hurt less.

"Column halts at the ridge," she said now, because whatever else was true, she was not going to charge six people across an open ford without knowing what waited in those willows. "Ferren, take four riders down along the east bank. I want eyes on the crossing before we commit."

"And if they run?"

"They won't." She looked at the little group below—the tall thin man with the shopkeeper's stoop, the river-priestess with her gray braid, the old mapmaker, the broad-shouldered man who kept glancing back over his shoulder like a man carrying a debt, the two children. Two *children*. "They have children with them. Children don't run fast."

Ferren gave her another of those sideways looks and rode off, and Ilse sat very still on her horse and watched the mist move on the water and tried to remember when exactly her certainty had started leaking.

* * *

They were not running. That was the first strange thing. When her outriders appeared on the bank, the company did not scatter or bolt for the trees. They stopped. The tall man—Delacroix, the last of the Vesper Guild, the name she'd had in her files for three years without ever managing to catch him—set down the cart's handles, straightened up, and looked directly at her position on the ridge as though he could see her. Then he raised one hand. Not a threat. An invitation, almost. A wave, if waves could be solemn.

"He sees us," said Sergeant Okonwe, riding up beside her. "Wards?"

"Probably. Or good eyes." Ilse chewed the inside of her cheek. "Form the line. Slow. I want them to watch us take our time about it. Fear makes people stupid, and stupid people do desperate things near children."

"You think they'll fight?"

"With what?" But even as she said it, her eyes went to the bell. Sefa of the Drowned Bell. The reports on her were thinner than most—a priestess who had never renounced her orders, who rang a sunken bell that supposedly masked voices from scrying. Half of Ilse's colleagues considered the bell a superstition. Ilse had learned, over five years, that the things the Inquisition dismissed as superstition were usually just things it didn't understand yet, and understanding things was not the Inquisition's strongest discipline.

They came down the slope at a walk. The morning was cool enough that the horses' breath showed, and the river ran shallow and loud over its stones, and somewhere upstream a bird was making an absolute nuisance of itself, and Ilse found herself cataloguing all of this—the breath, the water, the bird—with the peculiar intensity of a woman trying to memorize a scene she suspected she was about to ruin.

At the water's edge, Delacroix stepped forward alone.

He was thinner than his file portrait, grayer too, with the look of a man who had spent years being careful about everything including how he stood. He stopped three paces from the water and waited, hands open at his sides, until her line halted ten lengths away and she rode forward to meet him, because that was how these things were done: someone had to cross the middle distance, and it might as well be the side with the armor.

"Captain Varn," he said. His voice was quiet and slightly rough, like a door used gently for a long time. "I know your name."

"That's flattering and also alarming."

"It should be both." Almost a smile. "My name is Noor Delacroix. This is Sefa, Brammel, Odrik. The girl is Mira. The child—" he hesitated, and something moved behind his face that Ilse recognized, reluctantly, as fear worn smooth by practice, "—the child is Ashen."

The child stood half-hidden behind Sefa's skirt, small and dark-eyed, watching Ilse with an expression she couldn't parse. Not fear. Not defiance. Something more like *recognition*, which was absurd, because Ilse Varn had never seen this child in her life.

"You're under warrant," Ilse said. "All of you. Unlicensed singing, conspiracy, flight from lawful detention. I have orders to bring you to Corvane."

"I know."

"You know a lot of things, Master Delacroix. It's been noted."

"Has it?" He tilted his head. "By whom? Your ledger?"

That landed harder than he could have known. Harder than he probably intended. Ilse's hand drifted toward her sword hilt—not drawing, just resting, the way a woman touches a familiar rail—and Delacroix watched her do it and did not flinch, which was either courage or exhaustion, and after five years of hunting people Ilse had learned that the two looked identical from the outside.

"I'm going to ask you once," she said. "Come quietly, and the children go to the custodial houses instead of the cells. That's the best terms you'll get, and I want you to understand I'm offering it against my own orders."

"And if we don't?"

"Then I take you. I have forty riders and you have a cart."

"Yes," Delacroix said mildly. "But you've noticed the bell."

Ilse had. "A bell isn't an army."

"No. It's a bell." He glanced back at Sefa, and the priestess met his eyes and gave a single slow nod, like a woman agreeing to something enormous. "Captain. Before anyone does anything they can't undo—I'd like to offer you something. Not a bargain. Not a trick. A memory."

"A memory."

"The child is going to sing to you. One verse. If you want to stop it, you can—cover your ears, order your men to loose arrows, whatever your conscience permits. But I'm asking you to hear it first. All of it. Because I think—" and here his voice did something complicated, dropped into a register below persuasion, down where people keep the things they actually mean, "—I think you've already heard part of it. From a woman named Tessaly, in a room with no windows. I think she told you what the covenant is, and I think you wrote the order anyway, and I think you've been doing arithmetic ever since that won't balance."

The world went very quiet. Even the ridiculous bird upstream seemed to hold its note.

Sergeant Okonwe shifted in his saddle. "Captain, is this some kind of—"

"It's fine, Sergeant." Ilse heard her own voice come out flat and steady and wondered distantly whose voice it actually was. "Nobody moves. Nobody looses anything. That's an order I believe in." She dismounted. It seemed important suddenly, to be standing on the ground, on her own feet, with the river noise around her boots. "One verse," she said. "And then you surrender, or we finish this the old way."

"Fair," said Delacroix. "That's fair."

* * *

Ashen came out from behind Sefa's skirt the way water comes out from under a door—slowly, inevitably, finding the shape of the ground. The child crossed the stones barefoot, because of course barefoot, and stopped in front of Ilse Varn and looked up at her, and Ilse looked down, and for a moment neither of them did anything at all except exist at each other.

Then the child began to sing.

It was not a melody, or not only. It was—Ilse would spend years failing to describe this to tribunals that wanted a description—it was *architecture*. The notes went up and did not come back down; they stayed, hanging in the air above the ford like rafters, and other notes braced against them, and the river's noise slid underneath and became part of it, and the willows leaned in, and Ilse felt something happen inside her chest that she had no vocabulary for, a sensation like a locked room in her being being unlocked by someone who had always had the key.

And then the memory arrived.

Not hers. That was the terrible, disorienting truth of it—she was standing at a ford in the present tense, watching a child sing, and simultaneously she was standing in a village square sixty years ago, in her grandmother's time, and the village was burning.

She saw it whole. The thatch catching. The well rope cut—cut deliberately, she understood with a clarity that felt like falling, so no one could draw water. People running with armfuls of children and nowhere to run *to*, because the roads were watched and the fields were full of soldiers who served a lord whose oath to the guardian of that valley had lapsed—or been broken, or simply *expired*, because oaths need tending the way fires need feeding, and nobody had tended this one. She saw her grandmother—younger than Ilse was now, dark-haired, furious, magnificent—standing in the square screaming at the sky. Not praying. *Demanding.* Calling the guardian spirit by its name, the way you'd call a neighbor who owed you, and the sky answered with silence. Pure, administrative silence. The silence of a contract no longer in force.

The spirit had not betrayed them. That was the thing. It had simply stopped being *bound*. Nothing anywhere obliged it to hear her grandmother's voice, and so it hadn't, and the village had burned, and the survivors had scattered, and one of them had grown up and raised a daughter who raised a daughter named Ilse who grew up believing that magic was fire pretending to be a conversation.

But it wasn't. She saw that now, standing in two times at once. The fire was what happened when the conversation *stopped*. The burning village was not magic's work. It was the absence of magic's work—the absence of any obligation anywhere, the world with its oldest law repealed, everyone free, everyone unbound, everyone lost.

The song ended. The rafters of sound dissolved gently, like frost giving up a window.

Ilse Varn stood at the ford with wet eyes and dry cheeks and forty riders watching her, and the child Ashen looked up at her and said—in the common tongue, the first words Ilse had ever heard the child speak, small and clear and devastating:

"She sang your name. At the end. Your family-name. It was in the song. It was always in the song."

* * *

Afterward—for there was an afterward; the world insisted on continuing, rude as ever—they stood on opposite sides of the shallows while Ilse tried to reassemble herself into something resembling a commanding officer.

"Captain," Okonwe said carefully, "your orders—"

"My orders are to deliver these persons to Corvane." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way she did everything. "Corvane is nineteen days' march from here, and the army's vanguard reaches the free cities in nineteen days, and I have recently become aware that the Inquisition is seeking a location I am apparently supposed to pretend I don't know about." She looked at Delacroix. "The Wellspring."

His face did not change, which confirmed it completely.

"You knew," she said. "Of course you knew. Everyone in this story knows everything except me, and I'm the one with the warrant." She laughed—one short, graceless bark, entirely without humor and somehow entirely with it. "Do you know what my grandmother's village was called? Of course you don't. Karst Hollow. Nobody remembers Karst Hollow. There's a stone marker now. The edict government put it up. *In memory of the victims of unchecked sorcery.*" She spat the words like a bad tooth. "There was no sorcery. There was a broken promise and a cut well-rope and a sky full of nothing listening."

Noor Delacroix said nothing. He was very good at saying nothing, she was beginning to realize. It was possibly his entire profession.

"I can't arrest you," Ilse said finally. "Understand me—that's not mercy. If I take you to Corvane, the Wellspring falls with you, and the armies reach the free cities, and everything I just saw happens again at a scale that makes Karst Hollow look like a kitchen fire. My orders and my country have, as of approximately four minutes ago, diverged." She turned to her column. Forty faces. Some confused, some frightened, a handful—she marked them, always the same handful—already calculating which way the wind blew. "Sergeant Okonwe. Strike the siege wagons' harnesses. We're turning north."

"North, Captain?"

"North. Away from the Wellspring. If the Inquisition asks, we lost the fugitives at the ford in fog." She swung back up into her saddle, and only then, only from the height of it, did she let herself look at the child again. "Master Delacroix. A word of professional advice, from one person who hides things to a person who's apparently done hiding: the elder you're looking for—Elleryn. Check your sources twice."

Noor went very still. "Why?"

"Because three weeks ago, a sealed report crossed my desk detailing Vesper Guild detection methods. Training methods. The kind of thing only a senior member could provide." She nudged her horse toward the east bank, hating herself with a fluency that had taken years to perfect. "I assumed it was fabricated. I no longer assume things. It's the one lesson I've learned today, and I got it from a child who sings."

Ashen waved at her. Small fingers, solemn as a treaty.

Ilse Varn—who had executed singers, who had filled a ledger with names, who had believed for twenty years that she was putting out fires—raised her hand and waved back, and the gesture cost her more than her sword had ever cost anyone, and she would carry it to her grave gladly.

Her column turned north. Behind her, faint over the water, she heard the company take up the cart again, and Sefa unwrap the drowned bell, and a sound like a struck memory roll once across the valley.

Nineteen days. And one sealed report she needed to read again before anyone reached the Wellspring—because if Elleryn had sold the guild's methods, Ilse knew with a hunter's cold certainty what else the old woman must have sold besides.

The location. The Wellspring's location. And Ilse was no longer sure whether she was racing to stop the Inquisition—

—or to warn the singers that the trap had already been set.
