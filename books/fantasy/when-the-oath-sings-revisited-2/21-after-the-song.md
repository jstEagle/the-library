## 21. After the Song

Ilse Varn had spent eleven years learning how armies break.

She had seen it in the border wars, before the edicts, when she was a lieutenant too young to know what she was watching: the moment a line stopped being a wall of men and became a crowd of individuals, each one suddenly remembering he had a mother. It was not like a building falling. It was more like ice going out of a river — nothing you could point to, everything shifting at once beneath you.

What happened at the Wellspring was nothing like that either.

Her column had been two thousand strong at dawn. By noon it was a rumor. She stood on the ridge above the spring with her warrant case still in her hand and watched her own army come apart not into panic but into *confession* — which was far worse, or far better, depending on what you believed a person was for.

The song had gone through them like weather. She had felt it herself; she would never pretend otherwise. One moment she was giving the order to advance, and the next she was standing in her grandmother's kitchen in Vellmark, nine years old, watching the old woman salt fish and hum — actually hum, tunelessly, terribly — while outside the shuttered windows the world went on being safe because something unseen had promised to hold it up.

Then the village burned anyway. Then her grandmother stopped humming. Then Ilse had spent thirty years building a life on the certainty that promises were lies told by things that could not be caught and punished.

And now every soldier on that ridge was hearing his own broken promises sung back to him in his own voice.

She watched Captain Dorrel of the Third Pike drop his weapon, sit down in the mud, and begin telling the man beside him about the grain convoy he'd diverted to feed his garrison while the river towns starved. He wasn't weeping. He was *reporting*, the way he'd report to a superior officer, except there was no superior officer, only the truth coming out of him like water out of a cracked jug.

She watched a siege-mage walk to the edge of the Inquisition's camp, unbind his own wards one by one — carefully, professionally, the way he did everything — and then simply stand there with his hands open.

She watched men desert. Not run — *leave*, walking south in twos and threes with their kit on their backs, past the singers at the spring, who did not stop them. Nobody stopped anybody. That was the strangest part. The whole apparatus of force, hers included, had become furniture.

The usurper's champion broke through the eastern lines near midday. She saw him from the ridge — Hask Marren, the Butcher of Cold Harbor, armored in oath-iron that no blade had ever scratched. She saw him reach the spring itself. She saw Noor Delacroix — thin, gray-faced, ordinary as bread — step between him and the water and sing.

She did not have words for what happened then, and she had spent her whole life believing that anything without words for it was probably a lie.

Marren's armor opened. Not shattered — *opened*, like a coat unbuttoned by someone patient, and underneath there was just a large tired man who sat down hard in the shallows and put his face in his hands and said, audibly, from forty yards away: "I never wanted any of it. I said I didn't. Every time. And they believed me because I almost believed myself."

Then the song ended, and the silence afterward was so complete that Ilse could hear the spring itself, running over its stones, indifferent and clean.

* * *

By evening the ridge looked like the aftermath of a fair rather than a battle. Cook fires everywhere. Men sitting in circles telling each other things — that seemed to be the principal activity, confession, conducted with the grim thoroughness of soldiers doing any other assigned task. A quartermaster had found the wine. Someone was teaching someone else a marching song and getting it wrong, and the wrongness was spreading, and nobody minded.

Varn walked down among them because she could think of nothing else to do.

They parted for her the way water parts for a stone — not fearfully, exactly. More like they assumed she was there for the same reason they were. Several of them confessed to her unprompted. She stood and listened to a boy of maybe nineteen explain, in excruciating detail, that he had reported his own brother to the Inquisition three years ago for humming, just humming, a guild lullaby, and that the brother had been taken to Corvane and she had never asked after him once, not once, and the not-asking was the part that hurt worst now that the song had made her look at it.

"I'm going to find him," the boy finished. "After. If there's an after."

"There'll be an after," Ilse said, and heard how strange her own voice sounded saying it.

"You're the captain," the boy said. "You knew. Didn't you? What they did with the ones from Corvane."

"I knew."

"Did you sign the transports?"

She had signed hundreds. Her ledger — her beautiful, meticulous, defensible ledger — contained four hundred and six signatures, each one a name delivered to a place she had told herself was a prison and knew, somewhere below the floorboards of her certainty, was a kiln.

"Yes," she said.

He nodded, satisfied, and moved on to confess to somebody else. That was the new order of things, she understood then: not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever. Just the truth, spoken plainly, owed to whoever happened to be standing there. It was terrible. It was also, she noticed, the first justice she had ever administered that required nothing of the victim and everything of the guilty — starting with herself.

She went back up the ridge, took the warrant case out of her satchel, and looked at it for a long time.

It was a handsome object. Black lacquer, silver seal, the usurper's crown worked into the clasp. Inside it were her commissions: Hunter-Captain of the Inquisition, Warrant-Bearer of the Edicts, with authority of search, seizure, and summary judgment across nine provinces. She had earned it. She had been *good* at it. Somewhere in a drawer in Corvane there was a commendation for her diligence signed in the usurper's own hand.

She carried it to the cook fire nearest the spring and fed it to the flames page by page, and the pages burned the way all paper burns, without ceremony, and she stood there until the lacquer cover curled and blackened, feeling not lighter but precisely weighed — measured, finally, against something true.

"Captain Varn."

She turned. The old mapmaker — Brammel, the singers called him, though his false name had been Ostrow and she had a file on Ostrow two fingers thick — stood at the edge of the firelight holding a tin cup. He offered it to her. Tea, by the smell, oversteeped and terrible.

"I'm not a captain anymore," she said. "I burned it."

"Saw." He nodded at the fire. "Bit dramatic."

"I've been told I have tendencies."

He snorted and drank his own tea. For a while they stood together watching the spring glow faintly in the dusk — it glowed now, really glowed, a green-white shimmer under the water like moonlight stored in glass.

"They're talking about a trial," Brammel said. "The usurper. Not a mob, not an execution on the palace steps. A trial. Sefa's organizing it. Says if they do it with power instead of truth they'll just grow another one of him in twenty years."

"Who judges?"

"Everyone who confesses. That's the rule she's proposing. You can't sit judgment unless you've said your own crimes out loud first." He glanced at her sideways. "Which means you'd qualify. First witness, if you want it. Your ledgers are apparently... comprehensive."

"They're accurate," Ilse said. "There's a difference, and I spent eleven years pretending there wasn't."

Brammel considered this. "Sefa will like you," he decided. "She likes people who argue with her. Come on. There's someone you should see, and he won't say so, but he's been waiting since sundown."

* * *

Noor Delacroix sat on a flat stone near the spring with a blanket around his shoulders and a child asleep against his side.

Up close, the change in him was unmistakable. When she had arrested singers — and she had arrested dozens, before she learned to prefer warrants to questions — they had been hard to see. Not literally; her eyes worked fine. But there was a quality to them, a deliberate dimness, like a lamp turned low so the light wouldn't show through curtains. She had trained herself to spot it. The absence where a person should have been.

Delacroix had none of that now. He was *present* in a way that was almost aggressive — every line of him legible, his exhaustion written openly across his face, his grief for his dead master, his terror, his stubbornness, all of it visible the way text is visible on a page held up to a window. Around his left hand and wrist, winding up beneath his sleeve, ran a tracery of pale gold light, fine as veins in marble, pulsing gently in time with the spring.

He saw her looking.

"It doesn't turn off," he said. "In case you were wondering whether I can hide again. I can't. I tried, this afternoon, out of pure habit. Stood behind that rock over there and concentrated very hard on being nobody." He smiled crookedly. "The rock was more convincing than I was."

"You don't seem upset about it."

"I am enormously upset about it. I've spent five years being careful, Captain. Careful was my entire personality. Now I'm going to walk into a city where everyone can see what I am, and I can't lie, not even small polite lies, not even 'no thank you, I couldn't eat another bite.'" He shifted so the child — Ashen — slept easier. "I'm also, and this is the humiliating part, happier than I have ever been. Both things are true. Apparently both things get to be true."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It's *terrible*. You should try it."

She almost laughed. She sat down on the grass beside his stone, at a respectful distance, and for a while neither of them spoke. The camp murmured below. The spring sang to itself.

"I came to arrest you once," she said. "Eleven months ago. Corvane, the low quarter. I had your street staked out for six days. My informant swore the charm-seller was a guild-trained singer lying low."

"What happened?"

"I watched you through a window for an afternoon. You sold a woman a luck-charm for her son's conscription, and you charged her double what it was worth, and then you quietly slipped half of it back into her purse when she turned around, badly, so badly I nearly gave you away by wincing." Ilse turned her cup in her hands. "I wrote in my report: subject displays no discipline, no craft, no threat. Recommend removal from watch list. I told myself I'd made an error of judgment. It's been bothering me since yesterday."

"Noor," he said.

"What?"

"If we're going to be honest at each other — and I understand that's the whole thing now — you may as well call me Noor. Everyone does. Well. Three people do. Four counting the child, who doesn't talk, but communicates disapproval fluently."

"Ilse," she said, because it seemed owed. "Since we're — yes. All right."

"Noor and Ilse," he said, tasting it. "This is the beginning of a friendship, isn't it. I can tell because it's awkward."

"My husband used to say I make friends the way glaciers make harbors."

"He sounds wise."

"He sounds dead, mostly. Eleven years now." She said it evenly, and found — this was the strangest gift of the day, stranger than the glowing hand — that saying it plainly cost less than not saying it had. "He was a ferryman on the Vell. The edict didn't kill him. A fever did. But I've always known that if the covenant had still held, the healing houses would have—"

She stopped.

"Would have saved him," Noor said quietly. "Yes. I know. Half my customers bought charms for fevers that didn't work because I'd sold them inert clay. I told myself harmlessness was kindness. It was cowardice wearing kindness's coat."

Ashen stirred, woke halfway, regarded Ilse with enormous dark eyes, and — this she would remember all her life — reached out one small hand and patted her knee twice, deliberately, the way one pats a horse that has behaved well. Then the child went back to sleep.

"What was that?" Ilse whispered.

"Honestly? I have no idea. Something good, I think. With Ashen you learn to accept blessings you don't understand. It's most of the theology."

Down by the cook fires someone had started singing — badly, joyfully, the covenant-song mangled into something with a drinking rhythm — and a dozen voices corrected them and got it wronger, and the wrongness rose up into the dark like sparks.

"Sefa says you'll be first witness at the trial," Noor said.

"I haven't agreed."

"You will. You burned your warrant. I've met men who burned their warrants and kept the beliefs — you kept the beliefs and burned the warrant, which is backwards, and means you'll agree." He said it without malice, and she realized with a jolt that he couldn't have said it with malice even if he'd wanted to. Everything he said came out plain as water. "Also, you'll be good at it. You kept perfect records of a crime for eleven years. Who better to read them aloud?"

"That's a horrible sentence, Noor."

"Yes," he agreed cheerfully. "Most true sentences are, I'm finding."

She looked at him for a long moment — this narrow, marked, exhausted man who had hidden so long he'd forgotten his own shape and then, at the end, had stepped into full view of two armies and sung — and she understood that she was looking at the argument that would win the trial before it began. Not evidence. *Proof.* Living proof that honesty was survivable. That a man could be entirely seen and not destroyed by it.

"All right," she said. "First witness."

"I know. I can't lie anymore, but you apparently can't hide agreement from me either. It's very convenient."

"One condition." She stood, brushing off her knees. "When it's done — the trial, whatever comes after — I want to see the guild. The reopened one. I want to watch children learn to sing. I need to see what it looks like when the thing I hunted becomes the thing that teaches."

Noor smiled, and the light in his hand pulsed once, warmly, like a heartbeat agreeing.

"Come in autumn," he said. "We start the youngest ones in autumn. And Ilse — bring your ledger. Elleryn will want it for the archives, and Mira has been asking after you. She says—" he paused, listening to some child's message carried no doubt along threads Ilse couldn't perceive, and laughed, genuinely, helplessly, "—she says the woman who reads everything should teach her to read everything too. She wants to be a witness when she grows up. Like you."

"Gods help us all," said Ilse Varn, and for the first time in thirty years, the thought of being remembered did not frighten her at all.
