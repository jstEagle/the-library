## 1. The Last Cartographer's Daughter

There were three ways over the Widow's Shoulder, and two of them would kill you.

Marguerite Ravensworth knew this the way other people knew their own names — not as a fact learned, but as a fact *worn*, rubbed smooth by years of handling. The northern path was a lie told by gravity: it looked like a road because sheep had once walked it, and sheep were terrible cartographers. The southern path was honest but drowned every spring when the snowmelt came down from the Teeth like a debt collector. The middle path — the one that threaded between the leaning stones the old guild maps called the Sisters — was narrow, treacherous in fog, and absolutely reliable.

Which was why she was standing in the dark at the mouth of it, at an hour she privately considered indecent, waiting for smugglers.

"Ravensworth," hissed Doss, one of the six men huddled behind her with lanterns shuttered to slits. "You're certain about this? My cousin Bram went over the Shoulder last autumn and they never found so much as his boot."

"Bram took the northern path," Marguerite said.

"He said it was shorter."

"Bram also once insisted a river ran uphill near Hallowmere." She adjusted the strap of her pack. "The northern path is shorter the way falling off a cliff is faster than walking downstairs."

Doss considered this. Behind him, his five companions — wool-runners, all of them, men who moved bolts of untaxed cloth through the passes for coin that was itself often untaxed — exchanged glances in the shuttered lamplight. They had hired her through the usual channels, which these days meant a note left under a loose stone in a shrine that no one tended anymore, and they'd paid her the usual rate, which these days was half of what her mother's generation had commanded.

She tried not to think about the rate. Or the loose stone. Or the shrine.

"Right," she said briskly, before grief could get comfortable. "Rules. You walk where I walk. You stop when I stop. If I say 'stone,' you step exactly where my lantern points and nowhere else. If I say 'sister,' you flatten yourself against the rock on your left and you do not breathe until I say the fog has passed. And if anyone asks what happened to Bram—"

"We say he owed us money," one of the younger runners offered.

"That too. Mostly we don't speak of him. It's bad manners and worse luck."

The middle path began gently enough, a shelf of grey stone no wider than a hay wagon, with the mountain rising sheer on the right and dropping into blackness on the left. Marguerite walked it without looking down, because looking down was for people who hadn't memorized every handhold and false step between Thornfell village and the high col. She had begun learning them at seven years old, sitting cross-legged on the floor of her father's study while he traced routes in ink and made her recite them back. By ten she could draw the whole of the eastern passes from memory. By fifteen she could do it blindfolded, which her father had tested, once, as an experiment, and which had resulted in the only time in her life she had ever been genuinely lost — a quarter mile of corridor behind the guildhall, but still.

*The Ravensworth girl knows the mountains better than the mountains do,* the older members used to say, back when there were enough older members to say things.

*The Ravensworth girl will be the last of them,* they had started saying more recently, when they thought she couldn't hear.

They weren't wrong. That was the trouble.

* * *

An hour up, the Sisters rose out of the dark on either side of the path — two pillars of weathered granite, leaning toward each other like conspirators, leaving a gap barely wide enough for a loaded mule. Guild tradition held that you thanked the Sisters aloud before passing between them. Nobody remembered why. Her father had claimed it was a fragment of something older than the guild, a courtesy owed to *someone* who had once kept these roads safe, and he had taught her the words even though he'd admitted he didn't believe in them:

"Passing through, sisters, mind our heads."

"What was that?" whispered Doss.

"A greeting."

"To rocks?"

"To rocks," Marguerite agreed, and did not add: *and if my father was right about half the things in his notebooks, to whatever is on the other side of the rocks, listening.*

The guild's official position — all three pages of it, the entire surviving corpus of official doctrine — was that Vaelthorn was a story. A fading realm, sealed away beyond the mountains by an ancient covenant, unreachable, irrelevant. The Oath existed to keep it that way, and the Cartographers kept the Oath by keeping the maps: knowing every pass, every hidden door in the stone, and making sure nothing came through any of them in either direction.

That was the theory. In practice, the guild had dwindled to eleven members, four of whom were older than the guildhall roof, and its practical work had narrowed to guiding smugglers over the mortal side of the range, because smugglers paid, and candles cost money, and Corvane refused to let the hall go dark even if it killed him, which it seemed increasingly determined to try.

Marguerite hated how much she loved it anyway. Every run was a recitation. Every pass, a page. When she stood on the Shoulder at dawn with the world unrolled below her — valleys stitched with hedgerows, rivers silvering in the first light — she felt less like a woman than like a living index of everything worth knowing about the shape of the land. Pride wasn't the word. It was closer to *vocation*, if vocation could be a kind of ache.

"You're smiling," Doss observed suspiciously.

"I'm concentrating."

"People don't smile while concentrating."

"I'm a complicated person. Stone."

They stepped where her lantern pointed. All six of them. It was going well, which should have warned her.

* * *

The trouble came at the Col of Nine Stones, where the path widened into a bowl of scree and the wind came through with opinions. Marguerite had stopped to check the sky — a bruised purple in the east, promising rain by midmorning — when the youngest runner grabbed her sleeve.

"There's someone up there," he breathed. "By the cairn. Watching us."

Marguerite followed his pointing hand and saw him: a figure standing motionless beside the trail-marker cairn, some forty yards off, wrapped in a traveler's cloak gone grey with road dust. Even at that distance, even in the dark, something about the stillness of him raised the hair on her arms. People waiting by roadsides shifted their weight. This man did not shift anything.

"Smuggler?" Doss whispered.

"Smugglers don't stand in the open," Marguerite murmured back. "Wait here."

She approached the way her father had taught her to approach anything unknown on a mountain pass: at an angle, hands visible, weight low. Ten yards out, she called softly, "Traveler. You're standing on the worst possible ground in Thornfell. There's a hollow under your left boot full of meltwater and broken slate."

The man turned. Slowly, the way a door turns when the hinge is failing.

Up close he was worse than the stillness had suggested. He was young-ish, thirty perhaps, with the lean, weathered look of someone who had walked a very long way, but his face was wrong — pale in patches, flushed in others, sweat beading at his hairline despite the cold. His eyes found hers and fixed there with desperate, feverish intensity.

"You're her," he said. "You're the Ravensworth daughter."

"I'm a guide," Marguerite said carefully. "If you need passage down to the valley—"

"The covenant." He lurched a step toward her and caught himself against the cairn. "It's broken. Do you understand me? It broke *decades ago* and nobody — your guild knew, they've always known—" He fumbled at the front of his cloak. "I've carried it across three ranges. It has to be a Ravensworth. He said — the map said—"

"Sir." Marguerite took a careful step back. "I think you've been walking alone in the mountains rather longer than is good for you. There are herbs for this. Fever-dreams. My grandmother swore by—"

"They'll kill for it." He wasn't listening; he was pulling something out from inside his cloak now, cradling it against his chest with both arms, and Marguerite saw that his hands were shaking badly. "The oathbreakers. Your own people. Don't trust — don't trust anyone who smiles easily. Promise me you'll take it to the gate. Promise me, or it dies with me and then everything dies with—"

And here was the thing Marguerite would replay a thousand times in the years after, turning it over, looking for the moment she could have chosen differently: he collapsed.

Not dramatically. Not with a cry. He simply folded, like a tent whose peg had worked loose, sliding down the cairn stones into a heap at her feet, and the thing he'd been cradling tumbled free onto the scree between them.

It was a map.

Marguerite had seen ten thousand maps. She had been raised among maps the way farm children are raised among livestock; she could identify a mapmaker's school by the style of a compass rose and a guild's decade by the weight of its paper. This was none of those. This was a rectangle of material too fine to be parchment and too warm to be vellum, and even lying there in the shuttered light of Doss's distant lantern, its surface *moved*. Lines crawled beneath the skin of it like veins under flesh. Roads drew themselves and faded. Somewhere in its depths, faint as breath on glass, something glimmered.

"Ravensworth!" Doss hissed from below. "What's happening?"

"Nothing," she heard herself say. "A sick traveler. Go on ahead to the marker stones, I'll catch up."

Because — and she would hate herself for this later, too — her first instinct was to protect the run. Six clients, one night's wage, the guild's reputation. The stranger was clearly mad and clearly dying and neither fact required her to abandon a smuggling run in progress. She knelt beside him instead, professional and calm, and pressed two fingers to his throat.

His pulse was a moth's heartbeat. Fading.

"The map," he rasped. His hand crawled across the stones toward it. "Take it. Sing it — no. No, listen. Listen, it will teach you. It remembers everything. It's the only key left, and the gate—" His eyes rolled toward the peaks above them, toward something east, beyond east. "When the oath sings again. Tell them. Tell everyone. We were supposed to keep it *sealed*—"

His hand found the map's edge and stopped moving.

Marguerite knelt in the scree with a dead man's fingers brushing hers and a living map breathing quietly between them, and she did what she had been trained, raised, and temperamentally disposed to do in all difficult situations.

She decided it was a problem for morning.

*Fever,* she told herself firmly, gathering the map up — it was warm, unsettlingly warm, like a sleeping animal — and tucking it inside her coat. *Altitude and fever. Men wander up here all the time seeing things.* The ink had probably just been wet. Maps did that. Probably. *I'll take it down to the valley, show it to Corvane, and he'll tell me it's a novelty item from a fair in Lowmarsh, and we'll laugh about it over tea.*

The map pulsed once against her ribs, gently, unmistakably, like a second heartbeat answering the first.

Marguerite told herself she hadn't felt it.

Then she stood, brushed off her knees, and went to finish the run — because pride, after all, was the family trade, and pride said the job came first, and pride said dead strangers' ravings were no concern of the Cartographers' Oath.

Pride, as it turned out, had never read the map either.
