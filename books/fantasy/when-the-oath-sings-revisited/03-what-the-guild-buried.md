## 3. What the Guild Buried

Corvane still kept his office above the map hall, in the narrow room where the guild's founders had once argued over the shape of the world. Marguerite climbed the stairs with the living map pressed against her chest beneath her coat, and every step felt like a small betrayal — of whom, she could not yet say.

The door was open. Her mentor sat at his desk with a magnifying lens in one hand and a half-finished chart of the Lowmarsh salt roads in the other, and for one absurd moment he looked exactly as he had when she was nine years old: patient, precise, ink-stained to the second knuckle, a man who believed that if you drew a road honestly enough, it would hold.

"You're limping," he said without looking up. "And you didn't report after your run. And there's blood on your collar that isn't yours."

"Perceptive as ever."

"Marguerite." He set down the lens. He had a way of saying her name that made it sound like a summons. "Sit."

"I'd rather stand." She pulled out the map and laid it on his desk, and watched his face do something she had never seen it do in thirty years of knowing him.

It went blank. Not calm — blank, the way a page is blank before someone decides what to write on it.

"Where did you get this," he said quietly.

"A dying man gave it to me on the Kettleward pass. He had the guild seal tattooed inside his lip, Corvane, which I'd very much like an explanation for, along with why the ink moves, and why it knows roads I've never drawn, and why—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, and pressed on anyway, "—why it keeps whispering the word *breach*."

The fire popped. Somewhere below them, an apprentice laughed at something, the ordinary sound of an ordinary evening at Thornfell House, and Marguerite thought: *he is going to lie to me.* She had braced for it the way you brace for a fall on scree.

Instead Corvane closed his eyes and said, "Shut the door."

* * *

He talked for a long time. She would remember afterward not the words so much as his hands, which lay flat on the desk the whole while, as if pinning something down.

"There was a purge," he said. "Thirty-one years ago. Not of maps — of mapmakers. The Duke of Carris decided the guild knew too many ways into too many houses, and he wanted us gone. Writs of dissolution. Soldiers at three of our houses within a fortnight. We lost forty-one members in one winter, Marguerite. Hanged, exiled, vanished. The Cartographers' Oath was going to die, and the covenant with it — because the covenant isn't parchment, child, it's *us*. It's sung by living voices who know the border roads. No guild, no song. No song, no seal between here and Vaelthorn."

"So you saved the guild."

"We saved the guild." His voice was flat as a coastline. "There was a rite in the oldest folios. A way to draw strength from the far side of the seal — to borrow, so we could survive the borrowing. A tithe from Vaelthorn's own substance, taken through the passes, enough memory-magic to make twelve dying cartographers into twenty-four living ones. To make the oath self-sustaining. It was meant to be temporary."

"Was it."

He didn't answer, which was an answer.

"And the bleeding," she said. "The stranger's map showing passes that reshape themselves. The hollow places in the high fells where the fog sings. That's the tithe, isn't it? Still being drawn. Thirty years of it."

"Every year," Corvane said. "A little. Only a little. Enough."

*Enough.* She thought of the shepherd on Kettleward who swore his flock walked in circles now. She thought of the way certain stars over Thornfell came out in the wrong order some nights, and how the senior cartographers had always said *atmospheric disturbance* in voices that dared you to ask more. She thought of being eleven years old, tracing her first contour lines, and Corvane's hand over hers, warm and steady, telling her: *a map is a promise.*

"You made me," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "You trained me on those roads. Every pass, every ford, every sheep-track. You said I had the finest route-memory of my generation. And the whole time—"

"The whole time we were feeding those roads through a hole we cut ourselves, yes." He finally looked up, and his eyes were terrible with honesty. "I would rather tell you this than let you find it in a folio. You deserved it from my mouth."

"Don't." She stepped back from the desk. "Don't do me the courtesy of guilt now. You had thirty years of courtesy to spend."

"Marguerite—"

"That man died on my doorstep." Her hand found the map through her coat. "He was one of ours. He carried the truth of what we did in his own skin, and he died trying to bring it home, and you're sitting here charting *salt roads*."

For the first time since she'd known him, Corvane looked old.

* * *

Thirty-one years earlier, in the burning autumn of the purge, a young cartographer named Corvane Ash knelt in the undercroft of Thornfell House before the eldest members of his guild, and they were all of them afraid, and all of them pretending otherwise.

The folio open on the trestle table before him was older than the dukedoms. Its pages smelled of juniper smoke and something sweeter underneath, something like the air off a summer field that does not exist anywhere in the mortal realm. The rite was written in two hands: the original scribe's, sure and flowing, and beneath it, cramped annotations by a later cartographer who had apparently read the same passage and added, in a shaking script, *do not do this more than once.*

"He means more than once ever?" young Corvane asked.

"He means more than once safely," said Elder Maren, who had taught three generations and buried most of them. "We will take it once. One tithe. Twelve of us are left; we can be twenty-four by spring, and the guild endures, and the seal holds, and no one ever need know we begged bread from the far side of our own wall."

"It's theft," Corvane said. His hands were trembling so badly he pressed them flat against his knees, the same flat press he would use thirty years later on a desk before his favorite student. "The covenant is a trust. We're sworn keepers. This is taking from the thing we keep."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. "Yes," she said simply. "It is. And the alternative is that in five years there is no one alive who can sing the border roads, and Vaelthorn comes through the passes unopposed, and everything we stole tonight we steal for nothing. Choose your sin, Corvane. They only ever offer you the choice between sins. Anyone who tells you otherwise has never been chosen."

He thought of the writs nailed to doors. He thought of Hollis, drowned in the Carris canal with his charts sewn into his coat. He thought — God forgive him, he thought this first and foremost — of the guild, of its long unbroken line, of the idea that anything so beautiful should be allowed to continue existing.

"I'll sing the opening measure," he heard himself say, in the steady voice of a man watching himself from a great height.

They sang in the deep gallery under the mountain, where the founding stones were. Maren led; the eleven others followed; Corvane's voice cracked twice and held. And the stone before them opened — not with violence but with the awful courtesy of a door answering a knock it had hoped never to hear — and something came through that was not wind and was not light and was not cold but partook gently of all three, and it settled into their lungs like a swallow of strong spirits, and twelve dying candles burned up bright as beacons.

Corvane wept while it happened. He noted that with detached interest at the time: *I am weeping, and I am not stopping, and the rite does not require me to stop.* Maren wept too. All of them did, singing, their tears freezing in the gallery's breath, and the last note went down into the stone like a coin into a well, and somewhere impossibly far away — or perhaps very near — something in Vaelthorn grew measurably, quietly poorer.

*Once,* Maren had said. But by spring the purge had found new names, and by the following winter the guild needed new strength, and Corvane learned the first great lesson of his long life: that a door once knocked upon answers more readily each time.

By the tenth year, his hands had stopped trembling.

That was the part he never told anyone. That was the detail that haunted him past all the rest — not the sin itself but the callus that formed over it, the way the rite became procedure, procedure became budget, budget became a line item reviewed each solstice with tea and biscuits. He caught himself, around year fifteen, thinking of the far side not as a wound but as an *account*. There was balance in it. There was even, in a light-fingered accountant's way, stewardship.

He was still telling himself that, thirty years on, when his best student stood in front of him with murder and grief in her face and a dead man's map against her heart.

* * *

"Show me the folio," Marguerite said. "The rite. Everything."

"No." Corvane rose, and something of the old authority came back into his spine, though it fit poorly now, like a coat borrowed from a bigger man. "You don't understand what you're carrying. That map — if it's what I think it is, it's the counter-rite. The closing measure. Edran drew it, didn't he. I wondered, these last weeks, why he stopped reporting."

"Edran," she repeated. The name landed somewhere behind her ribs. So the stranger had a name.

"He was our best, after you. And our most restless. If he went into the mountains to forge the key to undoing us—" Corvane's jaw tightened. "Marguerite, listen to me. Reforging the covenant requires ink. Living ink. Memory, given freely and whole — a life's worth of routes. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Whoever sings that map closed will not survive it intact. Edran knew. It's why he was running. It's why he brought it to you instead of using it himself."

The room tilted very slightly. Outside, an owl called across the courtyard, and below, the apprentices were banking the fires, and the ordinary world went on with its ordinary sounds while Marguerite stood in the ruins of everything she'd been raised to revere.

"Then I'll take it to the gate myself," she said.

"You'll do no such—"

"You *knew*." Her voice broke loose at last, loud in the small room. "You let us sing the roads while you bled them. You gave me Thornfell when I was nine years old and orphaned, and you made me love every mile of it, and the whole time you were selling those miles by the yard! Don't you dare tell me what I will and won't do."

Corvane was silent. When he spoke again, his voice had gone soft and dreadful.

"If you leave this house with that map, I am required to stop you. Not as your mentor. As First Warden of the guild. There are others who know, Marguerite, and they profit, and they have enforcers, and you have known some of them since you were children." He paused. "You'll not be shown mercy twice, whoever shows it the first time."

She looked at him — really looked, the way she'd look at a map whose contours had shifted overnight, cataloguing what was true and what had been redrawn. Then she pulled her coat close over the living map, and walked to the door.

"At the Kettleward cairn," she said, without turning around, "a man died telling me the truth about my own guild. The least I can do is finish his walk."

She took the stairs down three at a time, out past the sleeping map hall where forty years of her own careful work hung framed along the walls, out into the cold blue hour before dawn, and toward the mountains — which, she now understood, had been quietly remembering a different world all along.

Behind her, faint through the stone of Thornfell House, she heard Corvane ring the alarm bell.
