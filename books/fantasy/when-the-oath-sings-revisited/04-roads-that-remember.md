## 4. Roads That Remember

Marguerite had walked the Widow's Stair four hundred times, and she knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat: two hundred steps of switchback carved by hands long dead, a ledge where the rock overhung just enough to spare you the rain, and then the long gray shelf above the treeline where the wind came off Vaelthorn like a held breath.

So when she reached step one hundred and thirty-seven and found no ledge there — only a smooth wall of stone, unbroken, as if the mountain had simply decided to forget it — she stood very still for a long moment and said, out loud, to nobody:

"No."

"You're welcome," said the map.

She had been traveling with Edran's voice for three days now. He lived in the ink. That was the only way she could describe it without feeling her sanity slip a notch: when she unrolled the living map at camp, the routes on its surface moved like slow veins of mercury, and sometimes — usually at dusk, when the light went amber and forgiving — the ink gathered itself into something almost like handwriting, and spoke.

He was charming. That was the worst part. She had expected a dying stranger's ghost to be mournful, or cryptic, or at minimum apologetic about the whole bleeding-out-on-her-doorstep business. Instead he was witty in a dry, road-worn way, like a tollkeeper who'd heard every joke twice.

"Good morning," he'd said that first dawn, as she sat up stiff-necked against her pack. "You snore in B-flat. Very musical. The mountains approve."

"I don't snore."

"You absolutely do. I've had nothing to do but listen for six months. You are, and I say this with respect, a symphony of small disasters."

And despite everything — the guild seal dying on his lips, the covenant broken, Corvane's face when she'd confronted him — she had laughed. A real laugh. It felt like treason.

* * *

The Widow's Stair had no ledge. Marguerite pressed her palm flat against the stone where four hundred memories insisted there should be handholds, worn smooth by generations of cartographers' gloves. Nothing.

"The pass is remembering," Edran said, gentler now. He could always tell when she was frightened, though she never showed it in her voice. She suspected the map tasted fear somehow. Ink was strange that way. "It spent thirty years being told what to be. Now the seal's fraying, and Thornfell remembers it used to be something else."

"It was always Thornfell."

"Was it?" A pause, and the ink rippled across the parchment in a way she'd learned meant he was uncomfortable. "Keep climbing. Trust your feet, not your memory. Your memory is a story someone told you very convincingly."

"My memory is the finest in the guild," she snapped, and hauled herself up over the place where the ledge should have been, finding — absurdly, terrifyingly — that the rock offered new holds, as if the mountain were making room for her. Fresh-cut edges. Almost warm.

They climbed through morning into a country that did not exist on any chart she had ever drawn or memorized. The pines thinned into trees she had no name for — silver-barked, leafless, humming faintly when the wind passed through them, the way a wet glass hums when you run a finger round its rim. Between them grew flowers like struck matches, red and sulfur-smelling.

Marguerite stopped and stared at one, and felt something in her chest crack slightly, because she did not know it, and not-knowing was the loneliest thing she had ever felt.

"That's a vael-lantern," Edran said quietly. "Or it was. We called them matchblooms when I was young, before anyone important started naming things. They only grow where the veil is thin."

"How thin?"

"Thin enough that you're standing in both worlds at once, Marguerite Ravensworth, and the remarkable thing is that you haven't noticed which one your boots prefer."

She looked down. Her bootprints, behind her in the scree, were filled with light. Not reflecting it. Holding it, the way a cupped hand holds water.

* * *

They made camp that night under an overhang she had slept beneath a dozen times — the Shepherd's Tooth, she'd have called it, a fang of granite leaning over a spring — except the spring ran uphill now, a thin bright thread crawling back toward the peaks, and the granite fang had softened into something like a ribcage, arched and hollow.

She didn't sleep so much as doze in shallow, uneasy laps, and each time she surfaced, Edran was talking. Not to her. To himself, maybe, or to the dark, in the low murmuring way of a man keeping himself company through a long watch.

"—and the north road forked wrong, even then, even before any of it, I told them the survey was off, but Halvard said the guild's word was the map, and the map's word was the world, and who was I, twenty-two years old and ink-stained to the elbow, to argue with—"

"You're awake," she said.

A pause. The ink stilled.

"I'm a map," Edran said lightly. "Maps don't sleep. Maps lie very still and pretend to be paper."

"What's the north road fork?"

Another pause, longer. When he answered, the charm was still in his voice, but it sat on top of the words like frost on a fence rail. Pretty. Thin.

"A mistake I made once. Everyone makes mistakes. Mine happened to involve the single most important document in mortal history." A ripple, almost a shrug. "Go to sleep, cartographer. Tomorrow we cross the Hollow Ford, and you'll want your wits. What's left of them."

"Charming."

"It's my best feature. That and the cartography. Mostly the cartography."

But she lay awake a while after, watching the moonlight move on the map's surface, and thought: *he knows exactly what he did.* And: *he picked me.* A dying man had crossed half of Thornfell, bleeding, hunted, to press this map specifically into *her* hands. Out of every cartographer left alive.

She fell asleep wondering whether she'd been chosen, or harvested.

* * *

The Hollow Ford had been, all her life, a knee-deep crossing over pale gravel, cold enough to make you swear, with a cairn on the far bank that smugglers touched for luck. Marguerite had memorized its depth in every season, its moods in flood, the exact stone midstream you could stand on to rest your pack.

She crested the last rise at noon and stopped so abruptly that her pack swung forward and nearly took her with it.

There was no ford.

There was a bridge — vast, impossible, arcing high over a gorge that had not been there yesterday, built of white stone veined with gold, like bone shot through with honey. It sang. That was the only word. As the wind crossed it, the bridge gave off a low chord, deep and sweet and grieving, and the sound went through Marguerite's teeth and settled somewhere behind her sternum and *stayed.*

"That's not mine," she whispered. "That's not Thornfell."

"It's both," Edran said. All the humor had gone out of him. "That's what nobody understood, thirty years ago, when they cut the worlds apart. They weren't ever two places, Marguerite. They were one song sung by two voices. The guild silenced one voice and called the silence safety." The ink crawled toward the edge of the parchment, straining, the way a dog strains at a scent. "The bridge remembers the duet."

She wanted to run. Every instinct the guild had trained into her — measure, verify, distrust the unmapped — screamed at her to turn around, go home, burn the thing on her back and ask Corvane's forgiveness. Her feet did not turn around. Her feet, apparently, preferred the other world.

She stepped onto the bridge.

The chord changed when she did. It rose to meet her, welcomed her weight, and beneath the singing she began — faintly, unmistakably — to hear words. Not in any language she knew, and yet she understood them the way you understand a mother's mood from the sound of her footsteps on stairs. Grief. Hunger. Longing. And underneath, repeated, patient, endless:

*Come back. Come back. Come back.*

"Edran," she said. Her voice came out small. "What am I walking toward?"

The map was silent for so long she thought he wouldn't answer.

"Toward the truth," he said finally. "I'd tell you to turn back if I thought you would. I'd tell you I'm sorry, too, but—" the frost cracked, just for a moment, and something rawer showed underneath, "—I've been sorry for thirty years and it hasn't fixed anything. Keep walking, Marguerite. And when we reach the far side, whatever you see — remember that I chose you because you're strong enough to carry it."

"Is that the real reason?"

The ink went very still.

"Keep walking," Edran said, and did not answer her again until the far bank, and the gorge, and the singing bridge, and everything she had trusted about the shape of the world, fell away behind her — and ahead, in the blue failing light, the mountains opened onto a valley where hundreds of pale figures stood among the matchblooms, singing fragments of roads she knew by heart.

One of them lifted its head, and looked directly at her, and smiled with terrible, starving joy.

"You brought it back," it called, in a voice like wind through the Widow's Stair. "You brought the map home."
