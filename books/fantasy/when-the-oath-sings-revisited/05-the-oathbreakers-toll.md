## 5. The Oathbreakers' Toll

The bridge at Hollowmere was the kind of bridge Marguerite had always loved — old, crooked, and stubborn about it. Its stones were furred with moss the color of old coins, and the river beneath ran loud enough to drown a confession. She'd crossed it a hundred times as a girl, racing Sable to the far rail, the loser carrying both their packs home.

She stopped at the near end now, because the far end was occupied.

Three figures stood in the mist where the road narrowed between the river and a wall of granite. They wore no guild colors, but they didn't need to. Marguerite knew guild enforcers the way she knew weather: by the set of the shoulders, the way they stood with their weight balanced for a lunge, the way they watched her feet instead of her face, because feet told you where a person meant to go.

And in the middle of them, arms folded against the cold, stood Sable Vane.

Sable looked exactly the way she had looked at nineteen and not at all the way she had looked at nine. The round-cheeked girl who used to dare Marguerite to lick the frost off the guildhouse bell had become something lean and watchful, her dark hair cropped short for practicality, a scar nicking one eyebrow like an afterthought. But when their eyes met across the bridge, Marguerite saw it — the flicker, quick as a trout, of the girl she'd known. There and gone.

"Marguerite." Sable's voice carried over the water, level as a mason's line. "You should have taken the southern road."

"I did take the southern road." Marguerite kept her hands loose and visible, though the living map pressed warm against her ribs beneath her coat, and she could feel it *noticing* — the ink shifting, curious, like a cat waking in a basket. "It's the better road this time of year. Less ice on the switchbacks. You'd know that if you still read my surveys."

Something moved behind Sable's eyes. It might have been hurt. It might have been the memory of hurt, filed away somewhere practical.

"We read everything you write," said one of the other enforcers — a broad man with a crossbow held low and casual, which is how professionals hold crossbows. "Guild property, all of it. Including whatever you're carrying under that coat."

The map pulsed once against her ribs, warm as a swallowed coal. *They know,* it didn't say, but very nearly sang.

* * *

Here is what Marguerite knew about bridges, because she made it her business to know about everything:

Hollowmere Bridge was ninety-one paces long. Its third arch had settled a hand's width out of true some forty years ago, which meant the stones rang hollow underfoot from pace fifty to pace sixty-three. The railing on the northern side had been rebuilt twice, once badly; a section at pace forty-one would hold a leaning man but not a falling one. And in high melt, the spray off the middle rapids iced the first six feet of the far approach every morning until midmorning sun burned it clear.

It was not yet midmorning. The mist was thick. The enforcers stood precisely where the ice would be, because they were thinking about ambushes and not about roads, and that — more than anything Sable had ever done or failed to do — told Marguerite how far the guild had fallen. Cartographers who stopped loving roads became merely people with weapons standing in stupid places.

"You're outnumbered three to one," Sable called, reading her stillness correctly, as she always had. "Don't make it ugly."

"When have I ever made anything ugly?"

"The bell incident."

"That was *you*, Sable Vane. I merely held your coat."

One of the enforcers shifted his weight, confused by the exchange, and Marguerite felt something crack quietly inside her chest — because this was the weapon the guild had chosen, wasn't it? Not blades. Not threats. *This.* A childhood friend on a childhood bridge. Loyalty, drawn and quartered into duty. Whoever had planned this had known that the sharpest edge they could put against Marguerite Ravensworth was the sound of Sable saying her name.

"Give me the map," Sable said. Softer now. Just the two of them, somehow, with two strangers and a roaring river standing witness. "Whatever Edran gave you — whatever he told you — it doesn't matter. Give it to me and walk home. Corvane will be furious for a week and then it'll be over. That's his pattern. You know his pattern better than anyone."

"You know about Edran."

"We found him." Sable's jaw tightened. "Or what was left of him on your doorstep. We've been following the trail since Thornhollow."

*Since Thornhollow.* Before the door. Before the dying weight of him, before the ink crawled up her wrist and whispered its first route in a voice like wind through a keyhole. Which meant—

Which meant the guild had let her find him. Or arranged it. Every kindness she'd extended to a stranger bleeding out on her threshold had been permitted, observed, allowed to ripen like bait.

The map flared hot against her ribs, and for one vertiginous moment Marguerite felt it the way Edran must have felt it: not a thing carried but a thing *carrying* her, a current with opinions. The ink whispered a word she didn't understand and then, patiently, translated: *run.*

"No," she said aloud.

Sable sighed, and it was such a familiar sigh — the sigh of a nine-year-old whose friend had just refused to come down from the guildhouse roof — that Marguerite almost laughed, and laughing would have broken her open entirely, so she didn't.

"Marge," Sable said, using the old name, the forbidden name, the name only two living people had ever used. "I don't know what you think that map is. I don't know what he told you or what it's shown you. But I'm asking you to trust me over a dead man's ink. Whatever he promised you — the truth, the covenant, the great secret — it isn't worth your life. It isn't worth *this*." She gestured, small and helpless, at the whole misted scene: the river, the enforcers, the thirty years of friendship stretched taut across ninety-one paces of stone. "Come home. Let me fix this."

"And if the secret is worth it? If it's true?"

"Then the guild will handle it. That's what the guild is *for*. We carry things so the world doesn't have to."

Marguerite stepped forward onto the bridge. One pace. Two. The enforcers tensed; Sable lifted a hand and they stilled, barely.

"Do you remember," Marguerite said, walking slowly, conversationally, "the summer we mapped the drainage tunnels under the old tollhouse? You were twelve. You got stuck."

"I got *temporarily* lodged—"

"You cried."

"I was twelve, and there was a rat, and we agreed never to speak of it."

"We swore an oath never to speak of it." Marguerite took another step. Fifty-eight. The hollow stones rang faintly under her boots, a drumbeat only she could hear. "That's what oaths are, Sable. Things you keep even when keeping them costs you. Someone taught me that. Someone with mud on her face and a rat story."

Sable's composure flickered — a candle in wind. "Marge."

"So tell me the truth. Look me in the eye, here, on our bridge, and tell me the covenant is intact. Tell me Vaelthorn is still sealed. Say it, and I'll hand over the map and come home, and we'll speak of the rat again someday."

The silence lasted four full seconds. Marguerite counted them against her heartbeat, and in those four seconds the map went utterly still against her ribs, listening too, holding its breath the way ink cannot and yet did.

"Sable," said the crossbowman, warningly.

"Say it," Marguerite repeated.

And Sable Vane — who had lied beautifully her whole life whenever lying served, who could look a customs officer in the eye and swear the cargo was wool — opened her mouth, and closed it, and looked down at the wet stones between them.

That was answer enough. That was the whole library of answers, shelved and catalogued.

"I'm sorry," Sable whispered.

Marguerite ran.

Not forward — *sideways*, which no one expected, because everyone knows you run toward escape or away from danger and almost nobody runs along a bridge railing at pace forty-one where the mortar was rebuilt badly. Her boot found the weak seam; the stone shifted under her weight exactly as her memory promised it would; and she dropped through the gap before the crossbowman's bolt cracked past where her head had been, hitting the sloped stone skirt of the second pier, rolling, catching the iron maintenance ring she'd known about since she was eleven because she'd *mapped it*, hanging above the white water while shouts tangled above her and spray soaked her through.

The map was screaming warmth now, guiding her — *left foot, the ledge, the ledge, now the root, the big root* — and she obeyed it the way she'd once obeyed her father, the way she'd obeyed Corvane, the way she'd obeyed the Oath itself: completely, because it knew the way.

She came up on the willow-choked bank below the falls with her lungs full of knives and her heart hammering out the rhythm of *run, run, run*, and she ran, upstream, into the trees, along a game trail the map unspooled ahead of her like thread from a spindle.

Behind her — distantly, impossibly — a shout, and then another voice cutting through it, sharp with command: *"Hold! HOLD! Let her go!"*

Let her go.

Marguerite stumbled to a stop among the birches, gasping, and turned back. Through the trunks, down the slope, she could just see the bridge, and Sable standing alone at its center, waving the others back, her face tipped upward as if she could see straight through a mile of forest to the spot where Marguerite stood.

One act of mercy. Neither of them could afford it. Both of them would pay.

*Marge,* the map murmured — not in Edran's voice now, but in hers, in Sable's, in a chorus of everyone she had ever loved, *you understand what she chose. You understand what it cost her.*

"Yes," Marguerite said aloud, to ink, to birches, to nobody. Tears froze in the wind on her cheeks. "And I know exactly where she'll tell them I went. Because I taught her the passes myself."

She wiped her face with a sleeve that was more river than cloth, turned north, and started walking.

North, where the roads grew strange. North, toward the place the map had been whispering of since the bridge: a pass that did not exist on any guild chart, where the mountains remembered being something else.

Where Vaelthorn began.
