## 6. Vaelthorn's Hollow Choir

The crossing was not a door. That was the first thing Marguerite understood, and the last thing she would ever be able to explain to anyone who hadn't stood where she stood.

It was more like stepping through a place that had forgotten it was supposed to be solid. One moment her boots were on Thornfell stone — the old shepherd's track above the Greywater falls, the one she'd walked at nine years old with her father's hand around hers and his voice reciting switchbacks like scripture — and the next moment the stone was *singing*, faintly, under her soles, and the air had the taste of a word on the tip of the tongue.

She had crossed at dawn, because Edran had insisted.

"Boundaries are thinnest when the world is half-asleep," he'd said, his voice warm and dry from the map rolled against her chest. "Like people. Like you, specifically, before tea."

"I don't drink tea," she'd muttered.

"You do in Vaelthorn. Everyone does. It's one of the things we kept." A pause, and then, quieter, in the tone he used when he wasn't being charming on purpose: "One of the few things."

Now she stood in Vaelthorn, and the first thing she noticed was the light. It came from everywhere and nowhere, a grey-gold wash like late afternoon that never committed to evening. The second thing she noticed was the silence — no, not silence. She corrected herself as she listened harder. It was sound, but sound worn thin, like cloth washed too many times. Wind that sounded like the memory of wind. Birds whose calls arrived a half-beat after their wings moved.

And beneath all of it, everywhere, faint as breath on glass: singing.

* * *

She walked for an hour along a road that was not a road. That was the only way to describe it. There was a path of sorts, pale and slightly luminous, but it shifted under her eyes the way water shifts when you look directly at your own reflection. Twice she stopped to get her bearings out of habit, and twice she realized there were no bearings to get. No sun to navigate by. No landmarks she could name, because nothing here had a name yet — names, she suspected, were something Vaelthorn had run short of.

"The roads here are starving," Edran said softly, when she finally asked. "A road is just a memory lots of people agree on. Enough feet, enough years, enough stories about who fell off the mule at which bend — and the road becomes real. Ours have been fed for centuries. These—" she felt rather than saw him gesture, a ripple through the map against her ribs "—these haven't been fed in thirty years."

"What do they eat?"

He didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice had lost its charm entirely. "Memory, Marguerite. Roads eat memory. That's what they've always eaten. That's what a map is — memory made portable. And your guild has been feeding itself fat while this place starves."

She wanted to argue. She found, walking through that thin light, that she couldn't.

* * *

She heard the choir before she saw them.

It came drifting down a valley of pale grass — grass the color of old paper, bending in a breeze that seemed to arrive out of politeness rather than physics. The song was familiar before she could say why, and then her whole body went cold, because she knew exactly why.

They were singing the Wren's Stair.

Not the words — the Wren's Stair had no words, it was a switchback trail above the village of Lowmarsh, famous mostly for the fact that her father had once sprained his dignity falling down it. But the *shape* of it. The rise and fall of the melody traced the climb precisely: the long pull of the lower reaches, the two sharp turns near the top where the path doubled back on itself, the little descending flourish at the summit where the whole valley opened up. She had walked that trail four hundred times. She knew it in her legs, in her lungs. And these voices were singing it note for note, turn for turn, like a hymn.

There were perhaps twenty of them gathered in the hollow below, and they were not entirely human. They were human-shaped, human-sized, dressed in garments that looked woven from fog and good intentions, but their edges were wrong — soft, unfinished, like figures seen through rain-streaked glass. Some of them flickered at the edges when they sang. Not all at once. In turns. Like candles sharing a flame.

Marguerite crouched behind a boulder that was more suggestion than stone, and listened, and felt something crack open in her chest.

An elderly figure — or what passed for elderly here, its outline gone gauzy at the shoulders — led the song. When the Wren's Stair ended, the singers swayed, visibly steadied, their edges firming up like dough rising. Then, without pause, they began another tune, and Marguerite's breath caught again: the ferry crossing at Marlow Bend. She knew the rhythm of the oars in it. She knew the *creak*.

"They're keeping themselves together with our roads," she whispered.

"Yes," said Edran.

"That song — the first one — my father fell down that trail when I was six. He blamed the goat."

"I know. I've heard the story. It's a good story. They may be singing the goat."

Despite everything — despite the horror sitting cold in her stomach — Marguerite laughed, a small broken sound, and pressed her hand over her mouth.

* * *

She should have crept away. Every instinct trained into her by thirty years of guild discipline said: observe, mark, withdraw, report. But then the eldest singer faltered.

It happened mid-verse. The old figure's voice simply *stopped*, and where the voice had been there was a thinning, a transparency spreading from the chest outward, and the other singers caught the melody and carried it louder, wrapping their song around their elder like hands catching someone falling. For three terrible seconds the figure was nearly gone — Marguerite could see the pale grass through its body — and then the chorus surged, and the elder filled back in, gasping, gripping the arm beside it, and rejoined the song on the next line as if nothing had happened.

As if this happened every day.

As if this happened *several times a day*.

Marguerite was moving before she decided to move. Later she would blame her father, who had once turned a smuggling run around because a stranger's child had a fever. Guild discipline said withdraw. Aldous Ravensworth's daughter said otherwise.

She came down into the hollow openly, hands visible, the way you approach a spooked animal, and the singing stopped all at once. Twenty faces turned toward her — and she saw now, up close, how young most of them were. How *unfinished*. Children with features still soft as wax, waiting for life to press them into shape. Children who would never be pressed into shape, because the life that should have shaped them was on the other side of a covenant their elders' neighbors had broken.

"You're mortal," said the elder. Its voice was like paper. Wondering. Frightened. "You're *whole*. You're so — oh, look at you. Look at all that *detail*."

"I'm sorry," Marguerite said, which was not any of the things she'd planned to say. "I'm sorry, I didn't mean to interrupt. Please — keep singing. Don't stop on my account."

"We can't," said one of the young ones, blunt as children are. "We ran out of the mill song Tuesday. And the bridge one. We only have eleven left that still work."

"Twelve," corrected another. "The river one came back. Tammy dreamed it."

"It came back *wrong*."

"It came back *mostly* right—"

"Hush," said the elder, but gently, and then it looked at Marguerite again, and its eyes were the worst thing she had seen in thirty years of hard roads: eyes full of hunger wearing the mask of courtesy. "Forgive us. We forget manners. We forget most things. You have come from over the wall. From the green side." Its voice dropped. "Do you know songs? Trails? Even small ones? A footpath counts. A stair counts. Anything with a beginning and an end."

Marguerite's throat closed. Because she did know songs. She knew hundreds. She knew the passes of Thornfell the way monks knew psalms — every grade, every ford, every inn where the stew was criminal and every inn where it was merely a felony. Her knowledge was the richest hoard of road-memory left alive, Edran had told her that much, and here was a starving realm looking at her the way famine victims look through a bakery window.

Every guild map is a wound dressed as a masterpiece.

The thought arrived whole, uninvited, and true. All those beautiful charts she'd grown up revering — the vellum, the gold leaf, the compass roses drawn with loving care — they weren't records. They were *harvests*. Somewhere in the guild's founding, some clever hand had learned that a road could be lifted out of a place and carried across the boundary like water in a bucket, and the guild had built its glory on the carrying. Every masterpiece on the Cartographers' hall wall was a field that had been stripped bare. The Wren's Stair might exist on vellum in perfect detail while the *actual* Wren's Stair — the real one, the living one, the one that belonged to the world — slowly starved in a realm of paper-colored grass.

"I know songs," she heard herself say. "I know a great many."

The hollow went utterly still. Twenty hungry faces. The elder's hands rose halfway toward her and stopped, trembling with restraint, the way you stop yourself from grabbing bread out of a stranger's arms.

"Would you," the elder said, very carefully, "sing us one?"

So Marguerite Ravensworth, last hope of the Cartographers' Oath, sat down on pale grass in a dying world and sang the Wren's Stair back to them — including, because it seemed important, the part about the goat.

The effect was immediate and terrifying. The singers caught the melody like drowning people catching rope. Their edges firmed; colors bloomed faintly in cheeks and lips; one child's hair darkened from fog-grey to an actual brown, and the child burst into tears of joy and clutched its own head as if checking the hair was real. The elder wept without sound, tears running down a face growing more detailed by the bar.

And Marguerite sang, and felt each verse leave her like blood leaving a vein — not painfully, but noticeably, the way you notice a warm room going cool. The trail grew sharper in her mind even as it left. By the final flourish she could see every stone of the Wren's Stair with impossible clarity, and also could not quite remember her father's face from that day, only the fall, only the goat.

When the song ended, the hollow was silent, and fuller, and the elder took her hands in its papery ones.

"Thank you," it said. "Oh — thank you. Now. Before it fades. Quickly." Its grip tightened, urgent, desperate, gentle all at once. "Sing us another. Sing us everything you know. And then you must run, cartographer, because the ones who broke the wall are coming up the valley behind you, and they are not here to sing."

Marguerite turned.

On the ridge above the hollow, black against the grey-gold light, stood three figures in guild travelling cloaks — and the tall one in front lowered her hood, and Marguerite's heart dropped through the starving ground.

Sable. Here. On *this* side of the wall.

Which meant the guild didn't want the map destroyed anymore.

The guild wanted the crossing kept open.
