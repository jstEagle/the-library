## 8. Storm Over the Sealed Gate

The gate had been singing to her for an hour before she could hear it properly.

Marguerite stood at the lip of the Hollow Vale, where the mountains pinched together like two hands cupping something precious, and listened to the covenant hum through the stone. It was not a song, exactly. It was more like a throat clearing — the long, patient sound of something waiting to be spoken whole again. The living map lay open across her forearms, its ink crawling in slow rivers toward the seam where Vaelthorn pressed against Thornfell, and Edran's voice threaded through her thoughts with the particular gentleness he saved for moments when he was almost certainly lying.

"You could still turn back," he said.

"No."

"I only ask because you've said 'no' to me eleven times since dawn, and I want to be sure it's a policy and not a mood."

"It's both." She climbed the last stretch of switchback, boots finding holds she'd have sworn weren't there yesterday. The pass *wasn't* there yesterday. The roads were remembering themselves as she walked them — old paths uncurling like ferns, trails she had memorized from guild charts reshaping to fit routes that belonged to no mortal hand. "Twelve times now. Keep up."

Behind them, the sky over Thornfell had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. That was the collapse, Edran had told her. The breach was widening. The seams between realms were fraying like old rope under load, and every hour she spent climbing was an hour of Vaelthorn bleeding further into the world she loved. She had seen the first evidence at the tree line: a birch grove growing silver bark and singing faintly in a language made entirely of vowels. She had tried very hard not to look at it too long, because looking made her chest ache in a way she suspected was permanent.

And then she crested the final ridge, saw the covenant gate — a horseshoe arch of black stone stitched with runes that glowed like embers refusing to die — and saw, drawn up on the slope below it in perfect guild formation, forty oathbreakers with lanterns and blades.

"Oh," said Edran quietly. "*Oh.*"

At their center, on a gray mare that had no business being this far up a mountain, sat Corvane.

He looked smaller than Marguerite remembered. That was the cruelest part. Thirty years of awe had built him into a monument in her mind — Corvane the Unlost, Corvane who had taught her every contour of the Thornfells, who had held her hand over her father's grave and promised the Oath would outlive them all. The man on the horse was just an old cartographer with rain in his beard and his hands shaking on the reins.

"Marguerite Ravensworth!" His voice carried up the slope, cracked by wind but not by doubt. Not yet. "You are standing on guild land, in possession of guild property, in defiance of guild law. Come down. Put down the map. We will speak of this like family."

"We will," she called back, "speak of it like what you actually did."

The formation shifted. Forty faces turned up toward her, and she knew half of them. Old Fenwick, who had wept at her apprenticeship ceremony. The Tallow twins, who had taught her to swear properly. Children of the same dying house she was about to burn down, holding torches to keep it standing.

She stopped running then. Not her feet — those were perfectly still — but something behind her ribs, some animal part of her that had been sprinting since the night a stranger bled out on her doorstep. It lay down. It went quiet. And in the silence it left behind, Marguerite found room to think one clear thought:

*I am the last person alive who can sing this covenant, and I am done apologizing for it.*

* * *

Corvane came up alone.

That surprised her. He dismounted at the base of the arch and climbed the steps with the careful dignity of a man whose knees had opinions, and when he reached her he did not draw a weapon. He simply looked at her the way he used to look at a chart with one line out of place — with love and exasperation in equal measure, and underneath both, fear.

"You read the ledger page," he said. "In my study. I know, because the ink was disturbed."

"You left it where anyone could find it. That's not hiding a secret, Corvane. That's leaving a door unlocked and calling it privacy."

"The purge would have ended us." His voice dropped low, meant only for her, and the wind seemed to lean in to listen. "Do you understand? Not diminish us. *End* us. The crown wanted our archives burned and our masters hanged, and Vaelthorn's breach was already open — a hairline crack, nothing, a wound that would scab if left alone. We only... widened the road so we could flee through it. So the guild could live."

"And Vaelthorn starved instead."

"And Vaelthorn—" He stopped. His jaw worked. For thirty years, she realized, he had never once said the next words aloud. "Yes. Vaelthorn starved instead. We told ourselves the seal would hold anyway. We told ourselves memory leaks were natural. We told ourselves—" He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as old paper. "We were cartographers, child. We drew the lie so cleanly that even we got lost inside it."

Above the arch, the sky split.

Not lightning — though there was lightning too, sheeting sideways through the clouds. The split was vertical, a seam of pale light opening along the ridge like a rip in cloth, and through it Marguerite could see *too much*: towers of glass grown like coral, rivers running uphill into a sky with two moons, a horizon that folded back on itself politely, like a guest pretending not to stare. Vaelthorn, leaning its whole fading weight against the thin wall between worlds.

The guild soldiers shouted. Someone's lantern went out. Fenwick fell to his knees and began — Marguerite would remember this always — to recite the Cartographers' Oath, the way frightened children recite prayers, getting several lines wrong.

"That is why the map must burn," Corvane said, urgent now, gripping her arm. "The breach feeds on the covenant's absence. If you sing it whole, the truth goes into the record — the guild's guilt, written plainly, forever. We end, Marguerite. Everything your father died believing in ends."

"My father died believing in the *Oath*," she said. "Not in you. There's a difference, and it's the width of everything."

She pulled free of his grip and turned to face the gate.

* * *

They gave her perhaps ninety seconds of peace. It was, in fairness, generous by guild standards.

Then the order came down the slope — she didn't hear the words, only their shape — and the formation broke upward, forty lanterns bobbing toward the arch like angry fireflies, and Marguerite opened the living map across her arms and prepared to do the bravest and most foolish thing of her life: stand still while people she loved came to kill her.

Edran's voice was steady in her ear. "Whenever you're ready. No pressure. Only the structural integrity of reality."

"*Now* is not the time for—"

The first blade never reached her.

It was Sable. Of course it was Sable. Marguerite watched her childhood friend come up the steps three at a time, watch the moment arrive, watch Sable's arm rise with a guild-issue knife — and watch her turn, at the last possible heartbeat, and put herself bodily between Marguerite and the rest of the charge.

"Sable—"

"Don't talk," Sable said through her teeth. Her back was to Marguerite, her knife pointed the wrong direction, her stance the one they'd learned together at nine years old, sparring in the archive aisles among the rolled maps. "Don't you dare say anything kind to me right now, Maggie, I am barely holding this together and you have *terrible* timing."

"You let me go at the bridge."

"And I've regretted it every miserable day since, because letting you go meant admitting you might be right." She parried the first soldier clumsily, honestly, taking a gash across her forearm rather than hurting him. "I read the ledger page too, Corvane. Did you know that? Did any of you know I could *read*?"

"Sable Ravensworth-adjacent—" someone began formally, which was how the guild named traitors, and Sable laughed, wild and bright, the laugh Marguerite had followed through every dark pass of their childhood.

"Adjacent! Write that on my stone!"

But there were forty of them and one of her, and Marguerite saw the arithmetic resolve itself with horrible simplicity: a spearman flanking left, another right, Sable turning to cover one and leaving the other open, and the open one raising his blade—

Marguerite stepped into the space between them.

She did not think about it. Later she would try to reconstruct the decision and find only this: the map open in her arms, the gate humming at her back, and her body choosing, finally and completely, what it had been walking toward since chapter one of her life.

"If you strike me," she said to the spearman, loud enough for all of them, "strike the last voice of the covenant. Strike the only person alive who can close that hole in your sky. Go on. The guild trained me better than it trained any of you, and I am asking you — *begging* you — to be the fools who end the world because an old man was ashamed."

Silence, except the wind and the singing of the birches far below and the creak of the sky.

Fenwick lowered his sword first. Then the Tallow twins. Then, in ones and twos like candles going out, the rest — until only Corvane remained armed, standing apart on the steps, his blade hanging from a hand that had forgotten why.

"Marguerite," he said, and it wasn't a command anymore. It was barely a word. "What will it cost you?"

She looked at him. At Sable, bleeding and grinning. At the tear in the world, through which, faintly, she could hear the hollow choir singing fragments of roads she knew by heart.

"Everything I love," she said. "Which is exactly what the Oath always asked. We'd just forgotten to listen."

She turned to the gate, spread the living map wide across her palms, and felt Edran gather himself inside the ink like a breath before a song.

"Ready?" he whispered.

"No," said Marguerite. "Sing anyway."

And above the sealed gate, the storm bent down to listen.
