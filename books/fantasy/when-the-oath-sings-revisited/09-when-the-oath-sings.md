## 9. When the Oath Sings

The storm had been waiting for us.

I understood that the way I understood a switchback — not by thinking it but by knowing it in my knees. The clouds above the Sealed Gate had gathered themselves into a spiral, slow and deliberate as ink poured into water, and the wind that came down off Thornfell smelled of two worlds at once: pine and snow, and beneath them something sweeter, like orchards that had never been planted anywhere mortal feet could walk.

"Charming weather," Edran said from the map against my chest. His voice was thinner now. I had been pretending not to notice for three days, the way one pretends not to notice a candle burning low. "Very dramatic. The guild always did have a flair for entrances."

Through the whiteout ahead, lanterns bloomed — dozens of them, strung along the covenant road in a line like the spine of some luminous beast. And walking at their head, small and straight-backed and unmistakable even at half a mile, came Corvane.

My mentor. My second father. The man who taught me that a map was a promise made in ink.

He stopped ten paces away, and behind him stood the guild — every cartographer still loyal enough to climb, their faces pale ovals under their hoods. Sable was there too, off to the side, her sword drawn and pointed, notably, at nothing yet.

"Marguerite." Corvane's voice carried through the storm without strain; thirty years of commanding draft-rooms will do that. "Give me the map. Go home. We will say you were misled."

"You mean we'll say I was lied to," I said. "By you. For thirty years. By everyone who ever signed the Oath while Vaelthorn starved on the other side of a door you unlocked and then pretended you'd lost the key."

The wind screamed between us, and in its screaming I heard singing — thin, hungry, beautiful. Vaelthorn was coming down. I could see it now past Corvane's shoulder: the gate itself, an arch of black stone older than any guild ledger, and through it the other realm pressing close, its hills soft and dim as things seen underwater. Its people were at the arch. Hundreds of them, translucent as breath on glass, singing fragments of roads they had stolen or been given — and I recognized every fragment. *The Wetherly switchback. The ford at Cold Harrow.* They were singing my passes. My maps, worn into songs, worn into survival.

"They'll pour through," Corvane said, and for one moment his voice cracked and I heard the young man from Edran's memories, trembling before a dying guild, choosing the unforgivable because the alternative was extinction. "Marguerite, girl, I know what we did. I have known every day for thirty years. But if you sing that covenant whole, the price—"

"The price is written in the margins," I said. "Edran told me. A life's worth of memory, as ink." I put my hand flat against the map over my heart. It pulsed like a second heartbeat. "You should be proud of me, Corvane. I read the fine print."

"Marguerite—" Sable started forward.

"Sable." I looked at her, my oldest friend, and smiled even though my eyes were stinging. "Watch the north ridge for me? Old habit."

Something broke in her face — grief and fury and love all at once — and she turned her back on the guild masters and faced the storm, sword up, guarding my flank as if we were sixteen again running contraband brandy through the Hollow Pass. Some mercies, it turns out, are also mutinies.

*Are you certain?* Edran asked quietly. *Once you begin, there is no stopping halfway. Half a covenant is worse than none.*

"I've never once stopped halfway through a pass," I said. "Why start now?"

I opened the map.

It unrolled itself across the air between my hands, vast as a tablecloth shaken out by a giant, its living ink crawling into new shapes faster than any cartographer could draw. The black stone of the gate drank in its light, and the arch began to hum — a low note, expectant, a tuning fork waiting for the orchestra.

And I began to sing.

Not words, exactly. Not at first. The covenant wanted *roads*, and so I gave it the only way I knew how to give anything: completely. I sang the Wetherly switchback with its seventeen hairpins and the cairn shaped like a grumpy thumb. I sang Cold Harrow's ford and the exact number of stones you could trust in drought (nine) versus flood (three, and only if you were desperate). I sang the smugglers' cut above Ravenmere, the shepherd stairs at Dunlow Crags, the frozen waterfall where the ice sings back if you knock politely. Every pass my father taught me. Every pass Corvane tested me on. Every pass I'd mapped in secret, in hunger, in love.

The map drank it all. Ink raced across its surface like rivers finding their beds, and where the lines completed themselves, light climbed the black arch in veins of gold, and the humming deepened into harmony.

*Oh,* Edran whispered. *Oh, Marguerite, you magnificent, stubborn woman — do you feel it? It's taking root—*

Then it began to take *me*.

It was gentle, which was the horrifying part. Like snow melting off a branch. I sang the ford at Cold Harrow and felt it leave — not vanish, but *drain*, the memory going out of me and into the gold, and what remained was a smooth blank place where a river crossing used to live. I reached for it and found nothing, like reaching for a stair in the dark.

Keep singing. There was no other option. Behind me Vaelthorn's choir had fallen silent, listening, and the gate's hunger turned toward me with something almost like tenderness.

*I can take the rest instead,* Edran said suddenly. *There's still some of me left. Let me—*

"No," I said, around the melody. "You drew this map. You don't get to erase yourself out of guilt twice in one lifetime."

*It was my fault—*

"It was *Corvane's* fault, and the guild's, and it is mine to mend because I'm the only one here who knows all the words." I laughed, breathless, dizzy. "Besides. Someone has to haunt Sable about her terrible handwriting. You're elected."

His voice frayed as I sang on. He kept talking anyway — that was Edran all over, charming right up to the last inch of wick. He recited the passes with me, feeding me ones I'd forgotten I knew, propping up my memory the way you prop up a collapsing tunnel wall with whatever timber is at hand. *The hollow oak at Miller's Bend. The ledge path where the goats laugh at you. Marguerite, the bridge at Thorn's Tooth — you haven't sung the bridge—*

I sang the bridge. I felt it go.

*Marguerite.* His voice was gauze now, barely a voice at all. *When this is done, you won't remember me either. I want you to know — choosing you wasn't cruelty. It was the only hope I'd had in thirty years, and it had your face.*

"Edran—"

*Sing, cartographer. Finish the road.*

So I finished the road.

The last thing I gave was everything at once — the whole shape of Thornfell, every valley and saddle and hidden door, poured out of me in a final long note that rose above the storm and hung there, impossibly clear. The gate blazed. The gold sealed itself stone to stone, seam by seam, and across the arch Vaelthorn's people bowed their bright heads, and the mountains *knit closed* — I felt it in my teeth, in the soles of my boots, the land itself sewing shut like a wound finally allowed to heal.

The storm died mid-breath.

Silence. Real silence, the kind mountains keep when they've decided something.

The map fell from my hands — or I let it go; I honestly couldn't tell you — and it drifted down onto the snow, its ink settling, slowing, going dark and still. Just paper now. Just ink.

Just a map.

I swayed. Sable caught me before I hit the ground, her arms shaking, her voice saying my name over and over like a charm that wasn't working.

"Hey," I managed. "Sable. Did it work?"

"Yes," she said, wet-eyed and furious about it. "Yes, you impossible woman, it worked, the gate is sealed, it's *over*—"

"Good." I tried to smile. "Because I was just about to say I know a shortcut home, and I realized I don't. I don't know any shortcuts. I don't know..." I looked down at my own hands, clever hands, empty hands. "Sable, why are we in the mountains?"

She made a sound I had never heard from her before, somewhere between a sob and a battle cry, and held on tighter.

Beyond her shoulder, Corvane stood alone among his scattered guild, staring at the sealed gate, at the dark map in the snow, at me. Thirty years of careful lies were dissolving off him like frost in sun, and what remained was just an old man who had traded his soul for his guild and discovered the guild couldn't hold the change.

"Marguerite," he said hoarsely. "What did it take?"

I opened my mouth to answer — to tell him exactly what it cost, to make sure he carried it, since I no longer could —

And found I didn't remember the question.

But the ledger in his hands had begun, faintly, to write itself.
