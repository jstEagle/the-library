## 10. A Blank Map, and a New Hand

The first thing Marguerite Ravensworth learned about the world after the singing was that it was enormous.

She had known this before, of course — in the abstract way one knows the sea is deep or bread is made of grain. But knowledge, she discovered, was not the same as memory, and memory was not the same as standing on the guildhouse steps at dawn with her lungs full of pine-smell and no idea, none at all, why the mountains made her chest ache.

"Take your time," Sable said beside her, arms crossed against the morning cold. "The passes aren't going anywhere."

"That's what worries me," Marguerite said. "I think I used to know that."

"You did. You said it constantly. It was insufferable."

Marguerite laughed, and the laugh surprised her — it came out whole, unguarded, the way a door opens when it has forgotten it was ever locked. She was learning that about herself too: there were things the singing had taken, roads and ridgelines and the names of every stream that bled down from Thornfell, but it had left her this. The capacity for laughter. The stubbornness. Whatever it was in her that had once memorized three hundred passes and refused to let any of them go.

It had simply let them all go at once, in the end.

Below them, the guildhouse of the Cartographers' Oath spread out in the early light — smaller than she'd expected, though she could not have said what she'd expected. Its roofs needed mending. Its bell tower leaned slightly eastward, the way it apparently always had. And through its open doors came the sound she would learn, over the coming seasons, to think of as the sound of honesty: quills scratching, voices arguing over measurements, and underneath it all, someone singing. Softly, badly, but singing. The covenant hummed in every map the guild drew now, and every cartographer hummed back.

"Come on," Sable said. "Corvane's waiting."

* * *

The ledger lay open on the long table in the map room, and Marguerite understood, even without her memories, that it had not always looked like this.

Guild ledgers, Sable had told her, were once beautiful things — vellum and gold leaf, each entry a small masterpiece, the guild's history written in a hand so fine it looked like frostwork. This one was plain. Buckram covers, ink the color of iron, letters that did not try to be anything but legible. On its first page, in words that could not be unwritten:

*In the year of the purge, this guild broke the covenant of Vaelthorn to preserve itself. We let the realms bleed together. We profited from the wound. Herein is recorded everything we did, and everything it cost.*

And then, beneath it, in a different hand — hers, they told her, though she didn't recognize it — the account of the singing. Of Edran, who had drawn a living map out of his own guilt. Of the gate, and the storm, and the price written plainly in the margins where it belonged.

Corvane stood at the table's far end, older than she remembered him being, if remembering was the word for it. There was something in her body that flinched when she saw him — an echo of betrayal her mind could no longer source — and something in his face that answered it. He had stayed, after. He had not run, though everyone would have understood if he had. He was writing the truth into the ledgers now, entry by entry, year by year, and he had told Sable once that he expected to spend the rest of his life doing nothing else, and that he considered it a mercy he did not deserve.

"Marguerite," he said. "How are the mountains this morning?"

"I don't know," she said. "That's rather the point."

Something crossed his face — grief, or guilt, or the place where those two things had grown into each other like roots. "Yes," he said quietly. "It is."

He slid the ledger toward her, and a fresh quill, and a page that was blank except for its heading: *Passes of Thornfell, Volume One.* "We start again," he said. "Honestly, this time. But we don't start with you. A cartographer who doesn't remember the roads can't be asked to record them." He nodded past her, toward the window, toward the peaks. "She has to walk them first."

"She," Marguerite repeated. "I'm standing right here."

"You are," Corvane agreed, and for the first time since she'd woken in the healing room with a head full of fog and a heart full of unnamed grief, he smiled. It transformed him. She suspected it had been a good smile once, before thirty years of carrying a secret had bent it out of shape. "Welcome back to the guild, Marguerite Ravensworth. Last of the old line, first of the new."

* * *

They walked the low pass first — Sable called it the Kettleway, and said Marguerite had once navigated it blindfolded in a whiteout to deliver a fever remedy to a shepherd's family, which Marguerite very much doubted until three separate shepherds stopped them on the trail to thank her by name.

"That's absurd," Marguerite said, after the third. "I don't even know these people."

"They know you."

"But why does my *body* know them?" Because it did. Her feet found the safe footing without instruction. Her hand went to the guide-rope anchors without being told where they hung. When the trail forked at a tumbled boulder, she turned left before Sable could speak, and stopped, and stared at the path ahead as if it were written there. "Sable. Why do I know which way to go?"

Sable was quiet for a moment. Wind came up the valley, smelling of stone and snowmelt and something sweeter beneath it — Vaelthorn's perfume, faint now, sealed properly away, like a memory of a memory.

"The singing took your memories," Sable said. "Not your training. Not ten thousand hours of walking. You are still, infuriatingly, the best walker I have ever met. You just don't know *why* anymore."

"That sounds exhausting for both of us."

"It's going to be," Sable said, and grinned, and Marguerite thought: *I love her.* She didn't know the history behind it — couldn't summon the bridge where Sable had been ordered to kill her, couldn't feel the weight of that mercy, though they'd told her of it, haltingly, each taking turns because neither could say it alone. But love, like the paths, lived somewhere deeper than recall. Her heart turned toward Sable the way her feet turned toward the pass. That was enough. That would have to be enough, and the remarkable thing was how nearly it was.

At the high shoulder of the Kettleway, where the whole of Thornfell opened below them — ranges stacked on ranges, blue and grey and white, going on forever — Marguerite stopped and felt the ache again. That unmappable ache.

"Why do they matter?" she asked. It wasn't rhetorical. She genuinely wanted to know. "The mountains. Everyone here looks at me like I've come back from the dead, and the ledgers say I gave up everything for them, and I believe it, I can see the hole where the knowing used to be. But I don't remember why."

Sable considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything, which Marguerite was beginning to suspect was one of her most reliable qualities.

"Do you remember the stranger?" Sable said. "Edran? They told you about him?"

"Some. A voice in a map. He lied to me, and helped me, and vanished when the work was done."

"He was a road himself, near the end. Did you know that? He'd poured so much of himself into the drawing that there wasn't much distinction left." Sable crouched, picked up a flat stone, weighed it in her palm. "You asked him once — we found it in the map's margins, afterward, written in your handwriting — you asked him why any of it mattered, the sealing, the sacrifice, all of it. And he wrote back: *A road only exists because someone walks it. A realm only exists because someone remembers it. You are the walking and the remembering, Marguerite. That is the entire secret. That was always the entire secret.*"

Marguerite looked out at the mountains a long time.

"So I don't remember why they matter," she said at last. "But I'm the reason they matter."

"Now you're getting it."

"That's a terrible burden to put on a woman who got lost going to the privy last week."

"Yes," Sable said gravely. "Corvane wept. I laughed for an hour. Both responses were appropriate."

* * *

That evening, in the map room, with the fire crackling and the honest ledger open and half the guild pretending to work while obviously listening, Marguerite dipped her quill and began.

*Passes of Thornfell, Volume One. The Kettleway. Recorded by Marguerite Ravensworth, who walked it today for the first time, which is also to say the second, which is also to say the four-hundredth. Notes: the third switchback holds ice longer than it should; the shepherd Aldric will claim his dog found the fever route, and should be contradicted warmly; the view from the high shoulder is the reason for everything, though the recorder cannot presently specify what everything is.*

She hesitated. Then, in smaller script, in the margin where such things belonged:

*This guild broke its oath and wrote the breach down, and the world did not end. It ended anyway, once, and was sung shut, and the person who sang it cannot remember doing it, which is perhaps the final mercy and certainly the final cost. If you are reading this and thinking of keeping a secret for the guild's sake: don't. Write it here instead. There is room. There is, it turns out, always room for the truth, and never quite enough for the lie.*

She set down the quill. Around the room, the other cartographers had gone quiet, and Corvane was looking at the margin with an expression she was learning to read: the face of a man watching a debt get paid that he'd never believed could be paid.

"Volume One," he said. "You intend more volumes?"

"Sable says there are two hundred and ninety-nine passes left." Marguerite capped the ink. "And I intend to forget the first one eventually and have to do it twice."

Outside, night settled over Thornfell, and the mountains stood exactly where mountains stand — patient, enormous, mattered. Marguerite blew out her lamp and did not know why her eyes stung, and did not need to. Some things you carry whether or not you can name them. Tomorrow there would be another pass, and another page, and Sable's dry voice saying *left at the boulder, try to keep up,* and slowly, honestly, line by line and mile by mile, the map would grow again — not the guild's map this time, nor Edran's, but hers.

A blank map, and a new hand.

It was, she thought, falling asleep, a strange thing to build a life on: roads she couldn't remember, for reasons she couldn't recall, in service of a realm she'd never seen.

She fell asleep smiling. In the morning, there would be walking to do.
