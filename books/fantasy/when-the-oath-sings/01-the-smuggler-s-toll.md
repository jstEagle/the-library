## 1. The Smuggler's Toll

There are three rules to moving contraband through the Thornfell passes, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is the only way anyone learns anything worth knowing.

Rule one: never carry what you can't afford to lose. Rule two: never travel with someone you can't afford to bury. Rule three — and this is the one they don't teach in any guildhall because no guild survives long enough to write it down — never let your sword do the talking.

I had broken rule one twice this season, ignored rule two out of stubbornness, and was doing my level best on rule three when the checkpoint at Greywater Saddle came into view through the pines, wrong in every way a checkpoint can be wrong.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me start with the mules, because the mules are where every story about me honestly begins.

* * *

Their names were Turnip and Cabbage, which tells you exactly how much sentimentality went into their christening. They were ugly, sure-footed, and dumb as an evening of bad poetry, and I loved them the way you love anything that carries your livelihood up a mountainside without complaint. Strapped to their backs under tarps of dried fish — because dried fish is legal everywhere and boring enough that nobody inspects it twice — sat forty pounds of Thornfell steel, unmarked and unstamped, bound for a smith in Lowmark who paid in coin that didn't ask questions.

Unstamped steel is a hanging offense. Stamped steel is a patriotic duty. The difference between the two is a hammer blow and a tax, and the crown has gotten very particular about the hammer blow since the war, since the purge, since the year the king's clerks decided that blades without royal marks were as dangerous as blades without royal loyalty. Which is to say: very.

I'd been running this route for four years. Long enough that I knew which rocks lied about their footing, which streams froze first, and which of the king's toll-keepers took bribes and which took pride. Old Maren at the lower gate took both. The twins at Hollow Bridge took pride but could be distracted by flattery about their uniforms. And at Greywater Saddle there was usually Corporal Denny, who took bribes, pride, and naps, in whatever order the day offered them.

Denny wasn't there.

What was there instead was six men in grey-and-white livery I didn't recognize, a wagon drawn across the road like a badly built fence, and a clerk — silver collar, ink-stained fingers, the particular smile of a man who has never once been cold or hungry — sitting on a camp stool as though the mountain had been arranged for his convenience.

Silver collars meant the Office of Sworn Accounts. Silver collars meant oaths.

My hand found Turnip's halter before my brain caught up. Good. Hands are faster than brains in the mountains; it's why we survive them.

"Traveler," the clerk called out, pleasant as a Sunday sermon. "State your name and business."

"Beatrix Yarrow," I said. "Fish merchant." My voice came out flat and bored, the voice of a woman whose life contains nothing interesting whatsoever. Seven years of practice in that voice. I could have taught a masterclass.

"Fish." He said it the way another man might say *treason*. "Open the tarps."

Here is what nobody tells you about hiding a forbidden gift: it isn't the gift that wears you down. It's the arithmetic. Every single day, a hundred small calculations. *Is this man watching? Is that woman listening? Is my sleeve covering the hilt? Did my blade hum when the wind hit it, or did I imagine that?* A swordspeaker lives her whole life inside a ledger of risks, and the ledger never balances, and the auditor never sleeps, and eventually you understand that you have spent so many years making yourself small that you're not entirely sure there's a full-sized woman left underneath.

I did the arithmetic now. Six soldiers, one clerk, forty pounds of illegal steel under dried fish, and a sword on my hip that was cheap, stamped, gloriously ordinary — except for the one thing about it that wasn't ordinary at all, the thing I had bought it *for*, the thing that made it the most expensive cheap sword in Thornfell.

Because here is my secret, the one that would get me hanged twice over: my blade remembers. All blades remember, if you know how to listen — they hold the echo of every oath spoken over them, every vow sworn by every hand that gripped them. It's called swordspeech, and it was the whole purpose of my order, the Order of the Witnessed Edge, back when my order existed and hadn't been burned out of its priory with the brothers still inside it. Blades keep honest records. Kings hate honest records. Do the math yourself; I've done it every day for seven years.

The sword at my hip was a dead man's blade, bought off a battlefield scavenger, stamped with some garrison mark that meant nothing. But once — once, years ago, when I was young and stupid and certain — I had sworn an oath over it. An oath to speak truth for those who could not, to let steel testify where flesh might lie. And the blade remembered. Blades always remember. That's not poetry; that's metallurgy, if you're me.

Which meant that if I drew this sword within earshot of anyone, it would whisper my oath aloud in a voice like a struck bell, and the clerk with the silver collar would hear every word, and then the arithmetic would end, abruptly, with me.

So I didn't draw it. I climbed down from the mule cart, smiling my fish-merchant smile.

"Fish doesn't open well," I said. "Salt-cured. You'll want gloves."

"I'll want the tarp up," said the clerk, "or I'll want your hands."

* * *

Let me tell you about the purge, briefly, because you'll need it and I'd rather give it to you in pieces than all at once, the way it was given to me.

Seven years ago, King Edric's men came to our priory in the dark of winter. Not soldiers — clerks first, silver-collared, with lists. They read out our names and charged us with sedition, which was a lie, and oath-breaking, which was worse, because it was true in a technical sense: we had refused to swear the new Loyalty Oath, the one that made every vow in the kingdom flow through the crown like blood through a heart. Our prior, Master Corvus — my mentor, my teacher, the closest thing to a father the orphanage ever gave me — told them we would swear no such oath.

They burned the library first. Books scream, if you're me. Did you know that? Ink holds words and words hold vows and vows hold echoes, and when you burn ten thousand pages at once, the sound is —

Well. It's a sound I hear sometimes. Usually around dawn.

Corvus put his hand on my shoulder in the smoke and gave me his last commandment, the only inheritance he had left to give: *"Never speak for a crown again. Never let them use your voice. Hide it, Beatrix. Bury it deeper than your grief."*

Then he walked back into the fire to buy the novices time, and whether any of them lived I never learned, because I ran. Fourteen miles through snow with my ears bleeding from the sound of burning oaths. That was the last time my gift spoke aloud where anyone could hear.

Since then: silence. Cheap steel. Fish merchants' voices. A life shrunk down to the size of a smuggler's road, because a small life is a survivable life, and I had decided — after the fire, after the snow, after burying the version of myself who thought her voice mattered — that surviving was enough.

Mostly I believed it. Mostly.

* * *

"Hands, then," I agreed, and held them out, palms up, the universal posture of the harmless.

The clerk examined them like fruit at market. Calluses from rope and reins, a scar across the left palm from a cable that had nearly taken a finger. Nothing sword-shaped about them, if you didn't know that rope calluses and sword calluses look identical to everyone except people who have both.

"Name your trade," he said.

"Fish. Salt-cured trout, mostly. Sometimes eel."

"And your route?"

"The low road to Lowmark. Same as every week." I jerked my chin at the wagon blocking the pass. "Road trouble?"

"Inspection," he said, in the tone of a man announcing weather. Behind him, the six soldiers had gone very still in a way soldiers shouldn't be still. Stillness is for ambushes; patrols fidget. I counted their stances without meaning to — weight forward, eyes on me, hands near hilts. Six men waiting for something. Or waiting for *me* to do something.

That was when I noticed the seventh figure, half-hidden behind the wagon's tailboard. Not a soldier. Leathers, road dust, a courier's satchel — and blood, a great deal of it, soaked down one side like he'd been used to mop a floor. His eyes found mine over the wood, and what was in them wasn't fear. It was recognition. He looked at me the way a drowning man looks at a rope, and I have spent seven years making sure I am not anybody's rope.

"Your papers," the clerk said.

I passed them over. Forged, obviously, but forged by the best forger in the fells, and good enough for Denny, who was presumably tied up somewhere or dead, and good enough for most clerks. This clerk turned them over twice, slowly, letting me watch him enjoy it.

"Yarrow," he read aloud. "Interesting name. Herbaceous. There was a woman of that name in the old rolls — a swordspeaker, taken in the purges of—"

"Died," I said. "Heard she died in the snow. Whole order scattered like pigeons." I shrugged, big and bovine, the shrug of a woman for whom history was a thing that happened to other people. "Bad luck, being famous."

"Mm." He looked at my hip. At the sword. Cheap scabbard, chipped leather, the garrison stamp showing worn at the throat. Everything right. Everything *small*. "Draw it," he said.

The world went very quiet. Somewhere above us a bird made a remark and thought better of it.

"Draw your sword," the clerk repeated pleasantly, "and show the stamp clearly. Regulation requires visual confirmation of the royal mark at inspection points. Surely a law-abiving fish merchant knows regulation."

Law-abiding. Right. I smiled. Inside, the ledger opened itself, and the columns began to move, and every entry ended the same way.

If I drew it, the blade would sing. Not loudly — it never sang loudly anymore; I'd starved it of attention for seven years until its voice was barely more than breath against the ear — but the clerk was standing four feet away, and silver-collar men are trained listeners, trained to catch the sound of hidden oaths the way a huntsman catches the crack of a twig. One whisper of my oath in that narrow little voice and I was a name on a list, and the list was already written, and the fire was already stacked.

If I refused, I was a smuggler resisting inspection with illegal steel, and six soldiers were standing right there with their weight forward and their hands near their hilts, waiting — *hoping* — for exactly that excuse.

And behind the wagon, the dying courier kept looking at me like I was a rope.

"Problem?" the clerk asked.

"No problem." I heard my own voice say it, calm as pond water, while the rest of me screamed. "Regulation's regulation."

My hand closed on the grip.

Here's the thing about the moment before you break your own rule: time goes syrup-slow, and you notice everything. I noticed the frost on the wagon boards. I noticed that the nearest soldier's nose was red and his eyes weren't on my face but on my sword hand, eager, like a dog watching a door. I noticed that the courier behind the tailboard had moved — dragged himself two feet closer along the ground, leaving a smear, holding something wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. I noticed that the clerk had stepped back half a pace, out of arm's reach, which told me he knew exactly what he was asking for and exactly what he expected to happen.

He wanted me to draw it. This whole checkpoint was a net, and I had walked into it leading my own bait.

I drew anyway. Because the alternative was refusing, and refusal was certain death, and a chance — even a sliver, even a madwoman's sliver — beat certainty every time. That was rule zero, the one underneath all the others, the one Corvus never taught because he died believing in better odds: *when the net closes, cut.*

Steel cleared leather.

And my sword sang.

Not loudly. God knows I'd earned that mercy. Just a thread of sound, a bell heard through a wall, a voice like cold water saying the words it had held for seven years, the words I'd sworn in another life over another fire:

*"Let steel witness what mouths deny. Let the edge speak truth where tongues are bought. I will not lie for crowns, nor stay silent for tyrants, while my arm remembers how to rise—"*

The clerk's face did something wonderful and terrible. It lit up. Pure, unhurried triumph, the joy of a fisherman feeling the line go tight.

"There she is," he breathed. "Yarrow. *The* Yarrow. Oh, you clever, stupid woman — seven years we've listened for that voice—"

The nearest soldier lunged. I gave him my pommel across the bridge of the nose and felt something shift gratifyingly, and then the world went loud and fast and simple, the way it only does when every choice has been made for you. Second soldier, blade out — I parried and put him down with the flat, because killing brings the song louder and I couldn't afford louder. Third — a boot to the knee, a shoulder check into the wagon. The other three were circling wide, calling out, and the clerk was shouting words I recognized with a sick lurch: *"Take her alive! The Regent wants her voice intact!"*

The Regent. I didn't know the word. Some part of me understood, even then, that I soon would.

Turnip — bless that ugly animal forever — chose that moment to spook, exactly as I'd trained him to at the crack of a pommel-strike, and bolted through the gap between wagon wheel and pine with the whole cart fishtailing behind him, scattering soldiers like ninepins. Cabbage followed out of solidarity or stupidity. I grabbed the oilcloth bundle the dying courier thrust at me — he pressed it into my hands with both of his bloody ones and said, clear as a bell over all the noise, *"the crown eats him — take it to —"* and then a crossbow bolt from the treeline took him through the chest, and his sentence died unfinished, and so did he, seconds later, looking at me like he'd finished it anyway, like handing me the package *was* the sentence.

No time. Soldiers regrouping. Clerk screaming orders. I ran for the trees with a dead man's parcel under my arm and my sword — my singing, whispering, traitorous, faithful sword — shouting my name to the whole mountainside in a voice only I and one silver-collared clerk could hear:

*"...while my arm remembers how to rise."*

* * *

Two miles uphill through the pines, lungs full of knives, I stopped where the stream crossed the rock shelf and finally let myself breathe. Turnip and Cabbage stood tethered where I'd cached them at dawn — I'd whistled the emergency pattern at the treeline and they'd come, because they were loyal and dumb and the best friends I had left.

I unwrapped the courier's parcel with shaking hands. Inside: a map. Oilskin, old, covered in marginalia in at least three different inks, and sealed with a blob of black wax bearing a sigil I did not know — a circle, and inside the circle, a crown, and inside the crown, nothing at all. An empty crown. A crown eating itself.

And beneath the wax, pressed into the seal before it cooled, a word in letters too small for any casual eye:

*Hollow Vault.*

My sword, lying across my knees where I'd dropped it to run, hummed. Once. Softly. The way a dog shifts in its sleep when it dreams of its master's voice.

"Oh, no," I told it, out loud, alone, the first honest conversation I'd had in weeks. "Whatever you think we're doing — we are not doing that."

The sword hummed again. Blades always remember. Apparently they also disagree.

Below me, faint on the wind, I could hear the checkpoint stirring — horns, horses, a clerk's thin voice carrying uphill like a knife thrown a long way. They knew my name now. The real one. Seven years of smallness, of fish and silence and careful arithmetic, undone in the space of one drawn breath.

I looked at the map. I looked at the mountain road south, my road, the safe road, the road where I could maybe — *maybe* — disappear again, shrink again, survive again.

I looked at the empty crown in the wax.

"You're going to get us killed," I told the sword.

It sang, very softly, and for the first time in seven years, I let myself listen.
