## 2. A Stranger at the Door

There are three things I know about dying men: they are heavier than they look, they never die at a convenient hour, and they always want to give you something.

I'd hoped the third was just a smuggler's superstition, like never whistling in the passes or trusting anyone named after a saint. But when I went out to check on the mules that October evening and found my barn door hanging open against the wind, and a stranger crumpled in the straw where my spare saddle should have been, all three lessons arrived at once.

He was heavy. I learned that dragging him out of the draft, grunting like one of my own beasts, my boots slipping in straw that was suddenly much wetter than straw ought to be. He was inconvenient—it was nearly dark, the fire needed feeding, and I had a delivery of untaxed brandy due over the ridge in four days that would not forgive delays. And he wanted to give me something.

But first he wanted to talk.

"No," I told him, before he could start. "No names, no stories, no last words. You're going to lie there while I look at whatever's killing you, and then you're going to decide whether you can walk to Widow Marsh's place, because she has a soft heart and I don't."

It's remarkable how often people believe me when I say things like that. It's less remarkable how rarely it does them any good.

He laughed. It turned into a cough, and the cough came up red, and I stopped pretending to myself somewhere around the second handful of blood. This man wasn't walking anywhere. He might not even make it to the part where he gave me something.

I got a lamp close enough to see him properly. Young—younger than me, anyway, which these days is most people. Courier's leathers, good ones, cut for speed rather than warmth, dyed the mud-brown that says *don't look at me* as loudly as any livery says the opposite. His horse was nowhere in sight, which meant it had been taken or had bolted, and either way told me something about how his night had gone. Three wounds: one across the scalp that had bled impressively but mattered least, one through the meat of his shoulder, and one low in his belly that mattered most of all.

Gut wounds have a smell. Seven years of keeping my head down teaches you things you never wanted to know, and that's one of them.

"Who cut you?" I asked, pressing wadding to the shoulder wound because it was the only one I could do anything about.

His eyes found mine. Grey eyes, very clear, very calm—the calm of a man who has already done his arithmetic and doesn't like the total but respects it.

"The crown," he said.

"Well." I tied off the bandage. "The crown cuts a lot of people. That's more or less its whole job."

"Not like this." He caught my wrist. His grip was weaker than a child's, and he knew it, and it made him grip harder. "You have to listen. There isn't—there won't be time for the long version."

"There's never time for the long version," I said. "That's how the short versions get everyone killed."

He almost smiled. "Who are you?"

"Nobody. A mule-trader. You're bleeding in my barn."

"You're not a mule-trader." His eyes went to the sword at my hip—my cheap steel, my deliberately cheap steel, the blade I'd bought from a Cutmark smith with a limp and no curiosity, chosen specifically because there was nothing in it worth listening to. "Your hands are wrong for a mule-trader."

"My hands are excellent for a mule-trader."

"They're swordspeaker's hands."

The barn went very quiet. The wind outside kept going, because wind doesn't care, but everything inside it stopped—the mule shifting in her stall, the drip off the eaves, even, I think, my own heart for a beat or two.

Seven years. Seven years of silence and cheap steel and a life shrunk down small enough to hide in, and this stranger, this dying boy, took one look at my hands and said it aloud like he was reading it off a signpost.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, "and if you say it again, I'll put you outside for the wolves to finish."

"You will," he agreed, "but not yet. Not until you've heard what I carried." His hand slid from my wrist into his coat, moving with the slow deliberation of someone whose body has started charging him by the motion. "They killed the others. All the other couriers. I'm the last copy."

"Copy of what?"

"A map." He drew out a leather tube, sealed at both ends with black wax stamped with a sigil I didn't recognize—a circle broken by a vertical line, like an eye that had been nailed shut. "And the truth about what's wearing the king."

I should have thrown him out right then. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them. Every instinct I had spent seven years sharpening said the same thing: strangers who name your secret are strangers who intend to spend it. Take nothing, hear nothing, be nobody. The Yarrow who survived did exactly that, every day, without exception.

But the blood had soaked through my bandage already, and his grey eyes were still doing their arithmetic, and underneath the fear there was something older and stupider in me—the part that had once sworn an oath with a blade at my throat and meant every word of it.

"What truth?" I asked.

* * *

He talked for the better part of an hour. I fed the fire, boiled water, did the useless things hands do when they can't fix anything, and let him spend his strength like a man spending the last of a purse.

His name was Tam. Just Tam—he said his family name had been struck from the rolls two years ago, which I understood better than he could have guessed. He'd served in the king's own courier corps, the fast riders, the ones who carry what can't be trusted to paper in the post. And eleven months ago he'd been handed a route he was never meant to survive, between clerks who wore silver collars and spoke, sometimes, in a rhythm that made his teeth ache.

"The king speaks in two voices," he said. "Everyone whispers it now. Drunk lords say it in cellars and get arrested for it by morning, which tells you the whisper is allowed—that's the thing about the Regent's justice, it only falls on truths that matter. But it's worse than two voices. It's—" He stopped, breathing through the pain in careful sips. "You've heard a puppet, yes? How the voice comes out wrong? Too smooth, like the words were rehearsed by someone else's mouth?"

"I've heard priests."

"Worse than that." His laugh was a wet, terrible sound. "The clerks. The silver-collared ones. They take oaths, Beatrix Yarrow. Dozens a day. Loyalty oaths, renewed daily, sworn in the king's name—and every oath makes the thing stronger. It feeds on vows. That's what it *is*. It was born from the first oath ever given to a crown of Thornfell, and it's been eating ever since, growing fat on seven hundred years of *I swear*, and now it's grown into the throne itself."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because three of us stole it." Pride flickered across his face, absurd and bright, a candle in a wet wind. "A clerk who broke her collar. An archivist. Me. We found where the first oath was forged—the Hollow Vault, under the fells, older than the kingdom—and we copied the road to it. One map. The archivist died getting it out of the capital. The clerk died holding the door. I'm dying carrying it." He pressed the leather tube against my chest, hard, insistent, like he could push it through my ribs into the space where courage was supposed to live. "Take it."

"No."

"Yes."

"I'm a mule-trader, Tam. Mule-traders don't fight regents."

"Mule-traders don't have swordspeaker's hands." His voice was going thin, fraying at the edges, but the grey eyes stayed fixed on mine with a stubbornness I recognized from mirrors. "I saw the purge. I was twelve. My village was half swordspeakers—they burned the chapterhouse at Vell, and I watched a woman hold a bridge alone for an hour so the children could run." His breath hitched. "You have her hands. Her exact hands. I've spent eleven months looking for those hands."

"That's very flattering and completely mad."

"It's the only sane thing left." He sagged back into the straw, and for a moment I thought I'd lost him, and felt something in my chest clench that I resented deeply. Then his eyes opened again. "The hunters will come. They always come. They're oath-sworn—they can't stop, can't rest, can't disobey. They'll trace me here eventually, and they'll ask you questions, and the answers won't matter because they're not really questions." His fingers found my sleeve. "Burn the map. Burn everything. Tell them nothing, tell them anything, run south. That's the smart play. I'd tell you to do it if I thought you'd listen."

"But?"

"But you didn't put me outside for the wolves." He closed his eyes. "So you're already in it. That's how it works. That's how it worked for all of us."

I sat there in the lamplight with a dead man's errand pushed against my sternum and hated him with a purity of feeling I hadn't managed since the purge. Hated him for finding me, for naming me, for dying in my barn instead of literally anywhere else. Hated myself more, quietly, in the background, the way you hate a habit you can't break.

Because he was right. That was the unforgivable part. The moment I'd dragged him out of the draft instead of closing the door, the arithmetic had changed, and no amount of cheap steel would change it back.

"Rest," I said, standing. "If you're still alive at dawn, we'll argue about it."

"If I'm alive at dawn," Tam said, "it means I'm tougher than the archivist, and she survived three days with an arrow in her, so."

* * *

He wasn't tough enough.

I sat up with him through the small hours because there was nothing else to do—not out of kindness, I told myself, just prudence; a dead courier in your barn requires decisions a living one doesn't. He drifted in and out, and in the shallow places he talked. About his mother, who'd sold fish in Vell market. About the clerk with the broken collar, whose name had been Isbet and who'd laughed at his terrible riding. About the woman on the bridge, again, always circling back to her, the swordspeaker who'd held the line.

"She sang," he murmured once, deep in the fever-shallows. "Her sword sang when she fought. Everyone says I imagined it. Did I imagine it?"

My hand was resting on my own hilt without my permission. I moved it away.

"No," I said, to the dark. "You didn't imagine it."

He died a little before dawn, between one breath and the next, without drama, the way rain stops. I sat with him while the sky went grey over the door, doing the accounting you do for the dead: young, brave, unlucky, finished. The barn smelled of blood and lamp oil and cold straw. Somewhere on the fells, a bird started up its idiot dawn song, entirely indifferent.

I should have burned the map. I had the fire going, I had the tube in my hands, I had every reason. Burning it was the smart play; burning it was what the survivor would do; burning it was seven years of discipline distilled into one sensible act of arson.

Instead I broke the seal.

The map inside was drawn on good rag paper in a clerk's precise hand—the fells, the rivers, the passes, and threaded through them a route marked in red that ran where no honest road ran, down and down, into the high wastes below the Underfell, ending at a point annotated in tiny characters: *the Hollow Vault — where the first oath was forged.* Around the edges, marginalia crowded like ivy: warnings, distances, notes in at least three hands. One line near the bottom, underlined twice, stopped my breath:

*Oaths spoken within the Vault bind absolutely. Speak nothing you do not mean.*

Below that, smaller, in a different ink, almost an apology:

*I am sorry to leave this with a stranger. But strangers are all that's left. — I.*

Isbet. The clerk with the broken collar, leaving her life's work to whoever found a dying man in a barn.

I sat there holding the future of Thornfell on a sheet of paper, and I want to be clear that what I felt was not heroism. What I felt was cornered. There's a difference, though history books—which I am reliably informed get written by survivors—tend to blur it.

Then the dogs started.

Not my dogs—I don't keep dogs; dogs ask questions with their noise. The neighbor's, down the slope, raising a chorus that meant strangers on the track. I blew out the lamp, folded the map into my shirt, and stepped out into the blue-grey morning with my hand nowhere near my sword, because that was the whole trick of my life: be nothing, hold nothing, want nothing.

Five riders came up the track at an easy trot. They wore no livery, which was itself a livery—the kind of anonymity that costs more than heraldry. Their horses were too good. Their gear was too plain. And they rode in formation without seeming to, the way men do when obedience has been carved into them deeper than habit.

The one in front reined in at my gate. Young face, pleasant, forgettable—the kind of face you'd struggle to describe to a magistrate five minutes after seeing it. He smiled at me, and the smile reached everywhere except his eyes.

"Good morning, mistress. We're looking for a rider. Came through yesterday, brown couriers' leathers, likely wounded."

"Haven't seen him." My voice did what it always did: flattened, bored, faintly irritated at being delayed. I'd practiced that voice for years. It was the best work I'd ever done.

"He'd have been bleeding."

"Fell season. Everything bleeds. Deer, travelers, my thumb, last Tuesday."

The smile held. Behind the pleasant young man, the other riders weren't watching me. They were watching the barn. One of them tilted his head slightly, nostrils flaring, like a hound catching the edge of something on the wind.

"We'll look around, if it's no trouble."

"It's trouble," I said. "I've mules in there and a delivery contract, and if your lot spook them, some merchant's factor will want the loss off somebody, and somebody won't be me. State your warrant or ride on."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Not anger—curiosity, brief and cold, the look of a man updating a ledger. Then the smile returned, wider, warmer, and somehow that was worse.

"No warrant, mistress. Only loyalty." He touched two fingers to his brow—an odd little gesture, half salute, half prayer. "We all serve the king's peace. Even you, I'm sure. Whether you know it or not."

They wheeled and trotted on down the track toward the next holding. I stood at my gate until they were well out of sight, counting my heartbeat, which had gone somewhere unhelpful.

*Even you. Whether you know it or not.*

In my barn, a courier lay dead on my straw with a hole in him shaped like everything I'd spent seven years avoiding. In my shirt, a map weighed more than any cargo I'd ever carried. And somewhere down that road, five men without livery were asking the same questions at every door, patient as weather, certain as taxes.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about hiding: you can do it perfectly for years, and it takes one stranger with grey eyes and forty seconds of dying conversation to end it. Because hiding was never really about silence. It was about having nothing anyone could take. And I was standing in my yard holding the one thing in Thornfell that the crown wanted badly enough to bleed a kingdom for.

I went inside, banked the fire, buried Tam under the rowan behind the barn—shallow, quick, before the light fully came up—and stood over the turned earth feeling like a liar for the short prayer I didn't say.

Then I went to the corner where my cheap steel hung beside my winter cloak, and I took the sword down, and I looked at it for a long moment, the way you look at an old dog you're about to wake from a very long sleep.

"All right," I told it quietly. "One more job."

Somewhere in the metal—deep down, in the place where blades remember what their wielders swear—something stirred, faint as a held breath, and waited to see if I meant it.

I saddled the mules. I packed the brandy, because a cover story works best when it's true. And I left the map exactly where it was, against my ribs, warm as a second heartbeat, riding south toward the capital I had spent seven years making sure I'd never see again.
