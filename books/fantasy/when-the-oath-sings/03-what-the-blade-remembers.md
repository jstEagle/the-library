## 3. What the Blade Remembers

There are three rules to interrogating a sword, and my mentor taught me all of them before she taught me anything else.

One: a blade remembers what it was made for before it remembers who held it. Two: it lies about the things that shamed its wielder, because steel is loyal past the point of sense. Three—and this was the rule she made me repeat until I hated the sound of my own voice—*never ask a sword a question you're afraid to hear answered.*

I sat in my barn at dawn with a dead courier cooling under his own cloak and a wax-sealed map on my knees, and I broke rule three before the sun cleared the ridge.

The courier's blood had dried brown on the floorboards. I'd covered him as best I could, folded his hands over his chest like he was sleeping off a long ride, which was a lie we tell corpses because the living can't bear to look at what they actually did or failed to do. He'd said *the crown eats him.* Four words. I'd turned them over all night like river stones, looking for something smooth underneath.

The map's seal was black wax, pressed with a sigil I didn't recognize—a crown, but wrong. The points bent inward, like fingers curling around something they meant to crush. Or protect. With crowns it was always hard to tell the difference.

I drew my sword.

Not to fight. To listen.

Her name was Vesper, though I'd never been allowed to name her—naming came after vows, and my vow had died with everything else seven years ago. She was cheap steel now. That had been deliberate. A swordspeaker carrying a masterwork blade is a lighthouse announcing itself; a woman with a battered mercenary's sword is nobody. I'd bought her from a fence in Marrowdale for less than the price of a good mule, and I'd spent seven years apologizing to her for it.

"Vesper," I murmured. "Show me the seal."

Swordspeech isn't talking, whatever the ballads say. It's listening with your hand. You lay your palm flat against the flat of the blade and you open something in yourself that most people keep shut, and the metal gives back what it has held—the heat of the forge, the grip of every hand, the words spoken over it. Oaths especially. Steel loves oaths. Steel is where oaths go to live when mouths get tired of holding them.

Vesper hummed against my palm, low and uncertain. She hadn't sung properly in years. I kept her hungry on purpose—starved of ceremony, starved of truth, fed nothing but the small dishonest silence of my survival. Asking her to remember was asking her to remember what we both used to be.

She showed me the forge first, always the forge. Fire and hammer-song, the smith's cracked knuckles, the moment she first understood herself as an edge. Then hands. Mine. Seven years of mine, gripping and drawing and sheathing, the thousand small accommodations of a woman pretending her sword was just a sword.

Then, reluctantly, the night of the purge.

I should have stopped there. Rule three. But grief is a door that swings both ways, and once Vesper started singing that night, I couldn't close it.

* * *

Seven years ago, in the chapterhouse at Greyhollow, the sky was the color of a bruise going bad.

I remember that detail because it was the last ordinary thing. Brother Callum had remarked on it—*rain by dark, mark me*—and then the bells started, and they weren't our bells.

We were the Order of the Sworn Edge. Ninety-one souls at Greyhollow, and every one of us bound by the same vow, spoken over our blades at taking: *My voice is the voice of the oath; what the sword holds, I will speak; and no crown shall command my tongue.* We were witnesses. Arbiters. When two lords disputed a sworn word, they came to us, and we laid our palms on the blades that held the oath, and the steel told the truth, and kingdoms had stayed at peace for two hundred years on the strength of that simple, unbearable honesty.

King Aldous the Fourth decided honesty was a threat. Or someone convinced him of it—I've spent seven years not knowing whose whisper did the killing, and some nights I think that not-knowing is the cruelest part.

They came at dusk. Not soldiers at first—clerks, with warrants, polite as tax collectors. They wanted the witness-blades surrendered for "inspection." Mother Sable—my mentor, the sharpest tongue in three counties and the gentlest hands—met them in the courtyard and said, with perfect courtesy, that our blades were sworn to no crown and would be inspected by no clerk.

The soldiers came second.

I was nineteen. I had been a full sister of the order for eleven months. I remember standing in the armory doorway with Vesper in my hand, watching the courtyard fill with men who had eaten bread from our kitchens at market fairs, and I remember Mother Sable's voice cutting through the noise like wire through cheese:

*"Beatrix. The vault. Take the young ones through the vault."*

Greyhollow had a vault beneath it—not treasure, never treasure. Records. Every oath ever witnessed by the order, pressed into stone tablets older than the kingdom itself. And beneath the records, a tunnel. We'd all joked about it. *Where will you run when the lords come, Sister?* Now the joke was a door and I was shoving children through it—novices, fourteen, fifteen years old, still soft-handed from copying oaths instead of speaking them.

I went back for Mother Sable. Of course I went back. That's the part I can't forgive myself for, and the part I'd do again exactly the same way, and I have made my peace with being a person who contains both those things, more or less, on good days.

She was holding the courtyard alone. Not fighting—witnessing. Her blade, Longsorrow, was planted point-down in the flagstones and her hand rested on the pommel, and she was *speaking*. Speaking the soldiers' own oaths back at them. Every man in the king's army swears service over a blade, and every blade remembers, and Mother Sable stood in the firelight telling thirty armed men exactly what their swords had heard them promise.

*I will protect the innocent. I will obey lawful orders only. So swear I, over edge and hilt.*

Half of them had stopped advancing. Their sergeant was screaming. And Mother Sable saw me in the doorway and her face did something I had never seen it do in nine years—it went afraid.

*"Beatrix Yarrow."* Not Sister. My whole name, so I'd know it was a commandment and not a request. *"You take those children south and you live, and you listen to me now because I will only say this once."*

The sergeant had recovered. There were crossbows coming up the road. She was talking faster.

*"Never speak for a crown again. Do you hear me? Not for a king, not against one, not one word that a throne could use. Our gift dies with us if it must, but it does not serve power. Promise me. Swear it on your blade."*

"I swear it," I said. Stupidly, instantly, the way you say yes to a dying woman, and Vesper drank the words down and has held them ever since, cold and heavy in her memory like a stone at the bottom of a well.

Mother Sable smiled. It was her teaching smile, the one that meant *you've answered too fast, girl, think again*—but she didn't take it back. She lifted Longsorrow from the stones and turned to meet the crossbows, and the last thing her sword sang into the night was her oldest oath, the founding vow of the order, spoken clear enough that I heard it half a mile down the mountain:

*What the sword holds, I will speak.*

They burned Greyhollow. They burned the records, or tried to—the vault tablets wouldn't take flame, which frightened the clerks badly enough that they buried the whole chamber under rubble instead. Ninety-one souls became, over the following year, a number that shrank. Some died that night. Some ran. Some were found, later, in ditches and rivers, because a gift like ours leaves traces, and hunters know how to read them.

I took six novices south. I got five across the border. The sixth was a boy named Tam who trusted a ferryman's kind face, and I don't talk about Tam.

And I learned the last lesson Mother Sable ever taught me, the one she never said aloud: survival is a kind of silence, and silence, practiced long enough, becomes a kind of grave.

* * *

I came back to myself with my cheek against the barn floor and Vesper humming under my hand, and dawn had moved across the room without my permission.

That was the danger of deep-listening. You give the blade your hours and it spends them however it likes. I'd meant to ask about a wax seal and gotten a full confession of my own heart instead, which is roughly how every conversation with Vesper went, even before the purge.

But she'd shown me the seal too. Buried under the memory, almost shy about it: the night the courier passed through Marrowdale, ten days ago, he'd stopped at a chandler's to buy candles, and the chandler had pressed the sealed packet into his hands, and Vesper—sheathed at my hip, hanging on a peg while I haggled over rope prices—had *felt* it. Felt the oath in the wax.

Because the seal wasn't just wax. It was sworn wax. Someone had spoken an oath over it, binding the bearer to deliver it, and the oath was old. Old the way the vault tablets were old. Old the way the founding vow was old.

And here was the thing that made my hands go cold on the warm morning air: Vesper recognized the shape of it. The cadence. The weight of the words, even filtered through wax and distance and seven years of rust.

It matched the oath in the vault tablets at Greyhollow. The first oath. The one the order was built to guard and never permitted to speak aloud, the one Mother Sable had shown me exactly once, when I was sixteen and too curious for my own good, and had said: *this is the root of the tree, girl. Everything else is branches.*

A courier carrying a map sealed with the first oath. Dying words about a crown that eats. Hunters with silver at their throats asking questions in villages they had no business knowing existed.

I looked at the dead man under his cloak. I looked at the map. I looked at my cheap, loyal, starving sword, and for the first time in seven years I asked her a question without flinching from the answer.

"Vesper. Is my gift a curse?"

She hummed. Thoughtful. Almost amused, if steel can be amused, and I'm here to tell you it can, and that its sense of humor is terrible.

Then she sang—one clear note, the first true note she'd given me since Greyhollow—and pushed an image into my mind that wasn't a memory at all. It was the map. The courier's map, seen through the wax, through the oath soaked into it. And along the route it showed, faint as frost on a window, ran a thread of light. An oath-road. A path worn into the world by centuries of sworn words, all leading to the same place.

The Hollow Vault. Which the map named, in a marginal hand, in ink that shimmered when Vesper sang near it.

My gift wasn't just a scar. It was a key. And somewhere out there was a lock that somebody had already died trying to open.

Outside, down the lane, hooves. More than one horse, moving slow, the way men move when they're checking fences—or checking farms. I blew out the candle, sheathed Vesper, and put my hand on the barn door.

Rule three, Mother Sable had said. Never ask a question you're afraid to hear answered.

She'd never told me what to do when the answer came knocking at your door wearing spurs.
