## 4. The Knight Without a Vow

I had been following the smuggler's trail for six days before I understood that the woman at the front of it was not running.

That was the first thing wrong with her. Everything else followed from it.

A fugitive moves like a hare—all stops and starts, doubling back, sleeping in ditches. This one moved like a woman going to market. She took the low road when the low road was sensible and cut overland when it wasn't, and she did it with the unhurried certainty of someone who had walked every mile of Thornfell so many times the country had worn grooves in her feet. Twice she passed within a league of crown patrols without so much as changing her pace. Once she stopped to buy apples.

I nearly gave up on her twice for exactly that reason. A woman who buys apples does not know what she's carrying.

Or she knows better than anyone alive, and has decided to be unafraid of it, which is a different thing entirely, and a far more dangerous one.

* * *

My name was Ser Aldric Vane for thirty-one years, and then for three days it wasn't anything at all, and then it was just Aldric, and now it is mostly nothing, because a man without a vow learns quickly that names are only oaths you wear on your face.

They stripped me in the great hall at Carrowmere. Not the sword—that they let me keep, which should have told me something about how frightened they were, or rather weren't. They took the white baldric. They took my place in the roll of the king's own. Marshal Corven read the charges in a voice like a man reading a laundry list: doubt sown among the king's sworn, questions asked in the night watches, refusal to swear the renewed oath of fealty when all honest knights had sworn it gladly.

All honest knights had sworn it with their eyes closed. I noticed that too late, and said so aloud, and here I am.

The strange thing—the thing I have turned over perhaps ten thousand times since—is that no one argued with me. Old Ser Halloway, who taught me the lance, looked at his boots. The king sat above us all on the Falcon Throne, and he smiled, and the smile went on half a breath too long, the way a held note goes on past where the music ends, and I thought: *he isn't angry. He's listening to himself be angry.*

Then I thought: what a mad thing to think about your king.

But you cannot unknow a thing. Doubt is a splinter. You can leave it in and limp around it forever, or dig it out and bleed properly. I dug.

* * *

I caught up with her at dusk on the third day past Harrow Fen, where the low road runs along a black stream full of drowned willows. She had made camp in the lee of a fallen wall—small fire, no tent, horse hobbled close—and I saw her head come up before my horse was within two hundred paces, the way a deer's does, and I knew then that buying apples was an act. She heard everything. She had probably heard me since the fen.

So there was no sense in subtlety. I rode into her firelight with my hands visible and my sword tied down in its scabbard with a leather strap, the way you do when you want to say *I am not here to fight you* in the only language everyone believes.

"Beatrix Yarrow," I said. "Smuggler. Guide. Late of the Order of the Singing Blade."

She didn't reach for a weapon, which unsettled me more than if she had. She just looked at me across the fire, and I got my first proper look at her face: mid-thirties, weathered, ordinary in the way that river stones are ordinary until you notice how much water has gone over them. Her eyes did a slow inventory of me—horse, tack, the empty baldric hooks on my shoulder, the strap binding my sword—and finished before I'd finished dismounting.

"And you're the knight who wouldn't kneel," she said. "Ser Aldric Vane. Stripped at Carrowmere in spring. They sing a song about it in the taverns. It's not flattering to either of us."

"It rarely is."

"You've been following me for six days." Not a question. "You ride heavy, favor your left knee, and you let your horse drink before yourself, which tells me you were raised well by somebody who's dead now. Say whatever you came to say and then ride back the way you came, because the answer is no."

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You were going to ask for passage. To the capital." She fed a stick into the fire. "Everyone who follows a smuggler wants the capital. It's always the capital. Nobody ever wants passage to somewhere pleasant."

I'll grant her this: she was right about all of it, and being anticipated by a stranger is a peculiar kind of humiliation, like arriving at a door and finding your letter already open on the table inside.

"How do you know what I'd ask?"

"Because you're not the first." She looked up at me then, fully, and there was something in her face I hadn't expected—not hardness, exactly. Tiredness. A tiredness with bedrock under it. "Three weeks ago it was a merchant who wanted the palace gates. A month before that, a priest. Everyone wants to get to the king. Nobody asks what the road costs." She paused. "You're not carrying coin enough to pay me, Ser Vane. I can see your purse through your coat. So either you're the worst-prepared beggar in Thornfell, or you're offering something that isn't money, and things that aren't money are always worse."

"I'm offering the truth," I said, "and I know how that sounds."

"It sounds like the opening of every lie I've ever heard."

"Yes," I agreed. "But hear it anyway, because you're carrying something that proves part of it, and you don't yet know why."

That landed. I watched it land—a small stillness in her, the stillness of a woman deciding whether to run or listen. Her hand had moved, without her seeming to notice, to rest on the sword at her hip. An old blade, plain scabbard, cheap fittings. The kind of sword nobody looks at twice, which is exactly the kind a woman hides behind silence would carry.

"The courier," she said quietly. "The one who died in my barn."

It was not a question either. This woman dealt entirely in statements; I began to suspect questions were a luxury she'd trained out of herself.

"He was mine," I said. "Not my man—I never had men—but my errand. I paid him to carry word out of the city to anyone who might still remember the old orders. Anyone who could hear what I've spent a year trying not to believe."

"And what have you spent a year trying not to believe, Ser Vane?"

I sat down across the fire from her, because my knee had opinions and because I wanted her to see me do something unarmed and human. I told her.

* * *

Here is what I told her, and I will set it down plainly because plainness is all I have left:

For three years, the King of Thornfell has spoken in two voices.

I do not mean madness. I served Mad Queen Isolde's court as a boy; I know what madness sounds like in a ruler, its lovely terrible music. This is not that. Edric IV is lucid, sharp, kinder than his father ever was. When he holds council, he is brilliant. When he rides the lists, he is joyful. His people love him, and the love is earned, and that is the horror of it.

But I stood beside him for eleven years. I heard him pray, curse, laugh, grieve his brother. And a little over three years ago—after the winter progress, after the pilgrimage to the old shrine at Vale Corren, though no one connects those things but me—he changed. Not in manner. In *voice*. Beneath his words, when he speaks formally, when he swears anything, when the lords bow and he answers with the blessings of the crown—there is another voice. Underneath. Older. It doesn't use words the way we do. It's less a sound than a pressure, a weight behind the syllables, the way a flood is behind a door.

No one else hears it. I made certain of that before I spoke. I asked the priests—they blessed me and suggested fasting. I asked the court physician—he suggested rest. I asked my oldest friend, Ser Dannet, and watched him go pale and repeat the renewed oath of fealty very loudly, as if volume were armor.

Then I asked the wrong question in the wrong room, and lost the baldric, and gained the whole road ahead of me.

"The courier carried a map," I said to the fire. "To a place called the Hollow Vault. I bought the knowledge from a dying archivist of the burned libraries, who told me two things before he died. First: the Vault is where the first oath was forged—the oath beneath every oath sworn to the crown of Thornfell, the root the whole tree grows from. Second—" and here my voice did something I disliked, thinned and quickened, the voice of a man whose doubt had finally grown teeth— "second, that the Regent wears the king."

She went very still across the fire.

I hadn't used the word before. I'd barely dared think it. But the archivist had used it, coughing blood onto parchment in a cellar under the tanneries, and once a word is in you it claws its way out.

"There are stories," Beatrix said at last. Carefully. The way you step on river stones. "Old ones. My order sang some of them. About the founding of Thornfell—about the oath the first king swore, and what it cost, and what kept the price coming due." Her eyes came up to mine. "We were taught those stories were allegory. Lessons about the weight of vows. Mother Sable said—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening, as if the name had slipped loose without permission. Then, quieter: "If the Vault is real, and the archivist was real, and the second voice is real—"

"Then the purge of your order seven years ago was not justice," I said. "It was surgery. Someone cutting away the only ears in the kingdom that might have heard what I heard."

The fire cracked. Somewhere downstream an owl asked its idiot question and got no answer. I watched her hand tighten on that plain cheap sword, and I thought—later, much later, I would understand how precisely I'd thought it—*she already knows. Something has already sung to her. I am not bringing her news; I am bringing her confirmation, and confirmation is a heavier thing than news.*

"Why me?" she asked finally. "There are other swordspeakers. There must be. The order was fifty strong."

"Fifty-two," I said, "at its height. I've spent a year finding traces of the others. Two died in the purge. Three fled south over the mountains. One took holy orders in Vail and has sworn so many new vows I doubt she can hear her own sword anymore. One is in the Regent's own guard, wearing silver, swearing daily." I let that sit. "And one guides contraband through the passes and buys apples in market towns and has spent seven years making herself so quiet that even the hunters' lists mark her as dead. You are the last one left whole, Beatrix Yarrow. Whole, and armed, and already holding the map."

"That's not a reason to trust me."

"No. Trust isn't given for reasons. But you should know what I know, because the hunters aren't looking for me." I leaned forward. "They're looking for whoever the courier reached. They found his horse at the ford two days after he left my sight. They'll work outward from the ford, farm by farm, and they are patient, and they do not tire, because they are sworn, and sworn men do not need sleep the way free men do."

Something moved behind her eyes at that—recognition, or memory, or both.

"And you?" she said. "Why come yourself? Why not send another courier, another fool with a wax seal?"

"Because couriers die," I said, "and I wanted to look at you before I asked you to possibly do the same." I untied the strap from my scabbard, slowly, and laid the sword flat on the ground between us, hilt toward her. An old gesture. My grandfather's, maybe older. *My life lies where you can take it.* "I have no order. No baldric. No king I can swear to, and no wish to swear to one, which makes me the most dangerous kind of man in Thornfell: a knight with nothing left to lose and one thing left to do. I need to reach the capital. Not the palace—the archives beneath it, what's left of them, and the one man who may know the road to this Vault. And I cannot walk through the low country alone, because I am known, and hunted, and hopeless with maps. You are unknown, and hidden, and—" I almost smiled. "You buy apples."

Her expression didn't change. "You want a guide."

"I want passage. And I want, more than passage, the truth of what's in that map—which you have and I don't, which means that whatever alliance we make, you hold the better cards, and I am asking anyway. That's the whole of my hand, Beatrix Yarrow. Look at it."

She looked. For a long time she said nothing, and I let the silence do its work, because I had learned in eleven years of courts that silence is the only argument that never loses its temper.

At last she drew her own sword—not the threat I'd first taken it for, but slow, deliberate—and laid it across her knees, and did something I did not expect. She put her palm flat against the flat of the blade, the way a woman touches a sleeping animal, or a grave.

"They say you doubted the king," she said. "In the hall at Carrowmere. Publicly. What exactly did you doubt?"

"That he was alone in his own body," I said. "And that the oaths we swore to him were going where we thought they went."

"And the others in the hall. Did any of them hear it? Even a little?"

"Halloway looked at his boots. Dannet swore louder." I paused. "One of the clerks dropped his inkwell. I've wondered about the clerk."

"Mother Sable always said oaths echo differently in different throats." She said it absently, to the sword more than to me, and her fingers moved on the blade like a reader's on a line of text. Then she looked up, and whatever she'd been deciding, she had decided, though I could see it cost her something—cost her, I suspected, precisely the thing she'd spent seven years building: the small safe invisible life, the walls around a shrunken world.

"Rule one of the road," she said. "You don't ask my name in towns. You don't ask my business at checkpoints. You carry your own pack and you ride when I say ride and you stop asking why when I say stop asking. Rule two: if I tell you to leave me, you leave me. Rule three—" and here her mouth twisted into something too dry to be a smile, "—never ask a question you're afraid to hear answered. Because I promise you, Ser Vane, on the road we're taking, every answer is worse."

"I've been living on worse for a year," I said. "It builds an appetite."

She almost laughed. It was a small sound, rusty, like a gate opening after a long winter, and it shut again almost immediately, but it had been there.

"We ride at first light," she said, kicking dirt over the fire. "There's a ferry at Greywater, and after that the country gets honest about how much it hates travelers. Sleep. And Ser Aldric—" she paused, and in the dark her face was mostly shadow and mostly tired, "—the two voices. When you heard them. Which one sounded more like a king?"

I lay back against my saddle and looked up at the stars doing their indifferent wheeling over Thornfell, and I gave her the truest thing I owned.

"The hungry one," I said. "That's what frightens me. The hungry one sounds *better*."

She didn't answer. But an hour later, when I woke briefly in the cold, I saw her sitting awake with her sword across her knees, her lips moving silently—not praying, I'd have known a prayer. Listening. Taking dictation from steel.

And far off to the east, back along the road we'd come, on a ridge against the last of the moon, horses stood in a line. Four of them. Unmoving. Patient as men who have sworn something, waiting for dawn the way a door waits for a fist.

I decided not to mention it until morning.

Morning, as it turned out, had other plans.
