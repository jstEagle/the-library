## 5. Hunters Sworn

The ferryman's name was Doss, and he charged too much, and I liked him immediately.

There is a particular honesty in a man who overcharges you to your face. He doesn't pretend the river crossing is a favor. He doesn't dress greed up in loyalty or duty or any of the other words people use when they want your money and your conscience at the same time. Doss wanted six coppers, he told us so, and if we didn't like it we could swim.

"We'll pay," Aldric said, before I could haggle on principle. He had that look he got sometimes—the one of a man watching a dog beg and trying to decide whether feeding it would be mercy or mistake.

"Generous," I said. "For a knight without a purse."

"For a knight who'd rather not drown," he said. "I've seen you argue with merchants, Yarrow. The ferry leaves when it leaves."

It left late, which should have been my first warning. Doss said the current was wrong, running fast and cold for early summer, as if the Greywater had heard something upstream it didn't like. Rivers are stupid animals, but they're honest in their stupidity, and I've learned to respect anything that can't lie. We pushed off into the dusk anyway, because dusk was when we needed to move and because staying put felt worse than going.

The ferry was a flat barge on a rope, wide enough for two ponies, four passengers, and Doss's permanent expression of mild grievance. There were two other travelers: a wool merchant snoring against his bales and a young woman in a gray cloak who kept her hood up and her hands in her lap, which I noted and filed under *none of my business*, since I'd been that woman on more ferries than I could count.

Aldric stood at the rail, watching the water slide past black and silver. His sword—royal issue, Thornfell make, the kind of steel that gets noticed—was wrapped in sackcloth tied with twine, the disguise of a man pretending his most identifiable possession didn't exist. I knew the trick. I'd been doing it with myself for seven years.

"You're staring at the water," I said.

"I'm staring at the far bank."

"Why?"

"Because it's farther away than it should be." He said it quietly, not dramatic, just factual, the way he said most things that frightened him. "Current's pulling us downstream. Doss wasn't lying."

"So we land half a mile south of where we meant. It's a river, Vane, not a prophecy."

He didn't answer. That was the thing about Aldric—I was learning—that silence of his wasn't agreement or disagreement. It was a door held slightly open, waiting to see if anyone would walk through it honestly.

I went back to watching our fellow passengers instead, because paranoia is cheaper than surprise.

The woman in gray hadn't moved in an hour. Not shifted her weight, not adjusted her cloak, nothing. People fidget. Living bodies redistribute themselves constantly, little unconscious negotiations between flesh and wood and gravity, and this woman had opted out of all of them. Her breathing was even. Too even. The rhythm of someone counting it.

My sword hummed against my hip.

Not sang—*hummed*, low, the way it did when steel nearby remembered violence. It was cheap steel, deliberately, the kind of blade I'd bought precisely because it was too dull-minded to carry much memory, too poor a vessel for swordspeech to echo through. But even dull metal knows when it's among kin.

"Doss," I said pleasantly, "how often does this ferry run after dark?"

"Never." He didn't look up from the rope. "You two are the first fools in three weeks."

"And the woman?"

"What woman?" Doss glanced back, frowned, and I watched the arithmetic happen behind his eyes—the same sum every honest man does when he realizes what he's carrying. "She boarded from the north path. Paid double. I don't ask."

"I know," I said. "That's why I like you."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about killing: it isn't hard.

I mean that literally. The mechanics of it are simple—a blade goes where you send it, bodies are soft in the places that matter, and fear makes most men slow. What's hard is everything around the act. The before, when you decide. The after, when you keep deciding it, over and over, every time you close your eyes.

I hadn't killed since the purge. Seven years of guiding contraband past checkpoints, of swallowing insults, of walking away, of shrinking my life down small enough to fit inside my own skin. Seven years of being no one, and I'd been proud of it in the way that starving people are proud of their discipline.

On the ferry, somewhere in the middle of the Greywater with the current dragging us south and the night coming down like a lid, that ended.

The woman in gray moved first—or rather, she stopped pretending. Her hands came out of her lap holding knives I never saw her draw, and she said, in a voice like a ledger being read aloud:

"In the name of the crown and its Regent, I claim the oath-breaker Beatrix Yarrow, and the traitor Aldric Vane. Yield, and be judged."

And here's the part I still can't make peace with: she *glowed*. Faintly, under the skin, along the throat and wrists—a pale gold light, like candlelight through wax. Every oath-sworn hunter carried the Regent's mark that way. Lit from within by the vow that owned them.

Aldric moved toward her. She was faster. One knife flicked out—not at him, at Doss, because hunters are practical people and a dead ferryman strands everyone—and I did the math faster than my conscience could object.

I drew.

My cheap, dull, forgettable blade came out of its scabbard, and the moment it cleared the leather, it *sang*.

Not the whisper I'd spent seven years strangling. A scream—all the voices steel remembers, every edge that ever cut, every oath ever sworn over a forge-fire, pouring out of three feet of mediocre iron at full volume. My gift had been dammed so long that the second I stopped holding it, it burst. And the worst part—the part that made my stomach drop even as I moved—was that the song wasn't random. It was singing *my oath*. The one from the checkpoint ambush, the old vow leaking out of me again, louder now, broadcast across the water like a beacon lit in my own hand:

*I swear my voice to the blade, my blade to the truth, my truth until my last breath—*

The hunter heard it. Of course she heard it. They'd probably tracked us by it already.

She turned toward me, and I killed her.

No ceremony. No duel. She was mid-turn and I was already moving, and my blade took her under the ribs and up, and she looked at me with an expression I will see for the rest of my life—which was not hatred. It was relief. Whatever the Regent held her with, whatever vow she'd sworn that lit her up like a lamp, death was the only exit she'd ever get, and I was the one holding the door.

Her light went out. The gold faded from her skin like sunset draining off a wall, and she folded onto the wool merchant's bales, and the wool merchant woke up screaming.

Then the water screamed too.

* * *

They came out of the river.

I don't know how else to describe it. Two shapes rose off the downstream side of the barge, wet and dark, wearing the grey-and-silver of the crown's wardens but moving like drowned things given errands—too smooth, too synchronized, like puppets sharing one set of strings. Their throats glowed. All of them glowed. Three oath-sworn hunters, lit from inside by the thing that owned them, and when they spoke, they spoke together, in unison, in that ledger-voice:

"*Yield, and be judged.*"

"No," said Aldric, and drew his wrapped sword still in its sackcloth, which should have been absurd and somehow wasn't.

What followed lasted maybe forty seconds and I remember it in fragments, the way you remember anything that rearranges you.

The first hunter went for Aldric. Steel came out of sackcloth and met steel, and Aldric fought like a man with nothing left to lose, which is the most dangerous way there is to fight and also the fastest way to die. The second hunter came for me. The third grabbed Doss.

Doss, who charged six coppers to my face. Doss, who never asked questions. The third hunter put a knife against the old man's throat and said the words again—"yield, and be judged"—and I understood suddenly that this was the trap, the real one. Not the ambush itself. The choice. The Regent's hunters weren't just killers; they were *proofs*. They existed to demonstrate that resistance cost innocents, that every oath-breaker dragged a butcher's bill of bystanders behind them, so that surrender always looked like virtue.

My blade was still singing. Screaming. Broadcasting my oath to every listening ear within miles, and I couldn't stop it, couldn't shut the gate now that it had burst open, and every note of it was a flare over the dark water saying *here, here, the swordspeaker is here.*

So I did the only thing I could think of. I gave the song somewhere else to go.

I've never done it before or since—reached into another weapon's memory while my own gift was howling—but desperation is a fine teacher. The ferry's iron mooring-ring, the great ring bolted to the deck that held the crossing rope, was older than the ferry. Older than Doss's grandfather. It remembered decades of strain, of ropes biting, of the river pulling and some stubborn ring holding on.

I asked it to hold.

The rope snapped taut as the current surged—whether by chance or because the hunters had planned it, I'll never know—and the mooring-ring *held*, groaning, and the whole barge lurched sideways with a violence no one aboard expected, least of all the hunter holding Doss. He went down. I was on him before he finished falling.

He was fast. Faster than the woman. He caught my wrist and we went over the edge of the barge together, into water cold enough to stop a heart, and in the black churn he fought me with the calm efficiency of a man who has done this many times, and I fought him with the panic of a woman doing it for the first time, and panic won. Panic usually does. I got my blade between us and pushed, and the river helped, and the glow in his throat dimmed and dimmed and went out, and then there was just water, and him drifting down into it with his face finally, horribly, at rest.

I surfaced gasping. The barge was grinding against the far bank—the mooring-ring had hauled the rope short and swung us ashore like a hook setting in a fish's mouth. Doss was shouting. The wool merchant was gone, fled inland, and good for him. Aldric stood over the last hunter, who knelt on the deck with his hands open and empty, glowing faintly, saying "yield and be judged" over and over like a prayer worn down to its beads.

"Get off my ferry," Doss said to the kneeling man, and when the hunter didn't move, Aldric knocked him into the river. Whether he swam or sank, I didn't stay to learn. I was on my knees on the bank, coughing up the Greywater, and my sword lay beside me in the mud, and it was still singing.

Quieter now. But singing. My oath, over and over, patient as tide:

*I swear my voice to the blade, my blade to the truth—*

"Make it stop," I said. I didn't realize I'd said it aloud until Aldric crouched next to me.

"It's your oath," he said carefully. "Your blade. Can't you—"

"Do you think I haven't tried?" The words came out cracked. "Seven years, Vane. Seven years of keeping my mouth shut and my steel dumb, and tonight it's singing loud enough to wake the dead in Thornfell Cathedral. Every hunter in three counties knows exactly where I am. Where I *am*, right now, kneeling in the mud like a—" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Like a lighthouse."

"Then we go dark," he said. "Deep fells. Now, tonight, before anything with ears arrives."

"The fells are—" I gestured at the wall of black country rising east of the river, all crag and heather and places where the mapmakers gave up. "Nobody comes out of the deep fells."

"Good," said Aldric Vane, and offered me his hand. "That's rather the point."

* * *

We walked all night. I'm not going to romanticize it. The deep fells are bog and stone and wind that finds every gap in your clothing with the persistence of a tax collector, and we climbed through them in the dark with my sword humming at my hip like a guilty conscience, quieter with every mile from the road but never silent. By dawn we'd put the river behind us and gained the high ground, a shelf of granite overlooking a valley full of cloud, and Aldric called a halt at a rowan tree growing sideways out of a crack, bent permanently by wind, stubborn as anything that survives up there has to be.

I sat down against it and shook.

Not from cold. Well—from cold, yes, the river had gotten into my bones and intended to stay—but mostly from the other thing. From the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do all night, and which my hands insisted on doing anyway, trembling out the total: three hunters. Three people whose names I didn't know, whose oaths I didn't know, dead or drowning because I'd chosen to live. And the woman—the first one—she'd been *relieved*. What did that say? That the Regent's service was a prison so complete that my sword through her ribs read as liberation? Then what had I actually done tonight—saved my own life, or emptied three more cages?

"You're doing the thing," Aldric said.

"What thing?"

"Where you punish yourself in advance for something that hasn't finished happening yet." He sat down across from me, unwrapped his sword, and began cleaning river-water off it with the mechanical calm of a soldier. "They were oath-sworn, Yarrow. They came to take us to something worse than death—you've seen what the Regent does with the people it judges. You know what 'yield and be judged' means."

"I know what it means. That's not—" I stopped. Made myself say it plainly, because if I couldn't say it to him, I'd never survive saying it to myself. "My blade sang, Aldric. All night. Loud enough to call every hunter in Thornfell down on us. I spent seven years learning to hide, and the first time I'm truly tested, my own gift turns on a bonfire and stands next to me waving. If it does that every time I draw steel, then I'm not a person anymore. I'm a signal fire that occasionally has opinions."

He was quiet a moment. Then, not looking at me: "When I was knighted, I swore the oath on the king's blade. Everyone does. Kneel, swear, rise, and the whole hall feels it—the weight of it settling on you. Like armor being fitted." His jaw worked. "I loved that feeling. God help me, I loved it for twenty years. And then I started hearing the king speak in two voices, and I doubted, and the doubt was treason, and the same hall that fitted me with that armor used it to hang me. Stripped my rank, took my name off the rolls, and the oath I'd sworn—" He tapped his own chest. "Still in here. That's the joke of it. They can strip your title, but vows don't recognize jurisdiction. Mine's still nailed to me, serving a king I know is dead, and there is no tribunal in Thornfell that can release me from it except the king himself. Who is a parasite wearing his face."

"That's cheerful."

"I'm a cheerful man." He sheathed the sword and re-wrapped it. "Here's what I know about your singing blade, Yarrow, and take it from someone whose own oath won't shut up either: the Regent feeds on vows. Grows fat on them. Every oath sworn in the king's name is a meal. So why—" he looked up at me, and there was something sharp and urgent in his face, the mind behind the melancholy catching light—"why would your gift, which *is* an oath, made of oath, ringing with oath—why would it help the hunters find you? Unless it isn't helping them. Unless it's calling something else."

"Calling what?"

"I don't know." He stood, and offered me his hand again, the same gesture as the riverbank. "But the courier died telling you about the Hollow Vault. The Vault is where the first oath was forged. Your blade remembers oaths. And tonight it sang your oath to the sky like a message." He shrugged, wry and tired. "Maybe messages have recipients."

I took his hand and let him pull me up, and my legs held, more or less.

Below us the valley filled with morning, cloud burning off gold, and somewhere far to the west—miles away, maybe more—I felt it before I heard it. A tremor in my blade. A faint answering note, thin as wire, threading through the hum of my own oath. Not the hunters. Something older, and deeper, and patient.

My sword was singing to something, and something was singing back.

"Tell me you feel that," I said.

Aldric's face had gone very still. "Yarrow," he said, "that's not coming from the capital. That's coming from *underground*. Straight down through the fells, like a vein of ore." He swallowed. "It's coming from the direction of the Hollow Vault."

The wind moved through the rowan tree above us, and my blade hummed against my hip—my oath, my truth, my voice—and for the first time in seven years, I let it sing.

Then I picked up my pack, and we walked east, following a song neither of us understood toward the place where the first oath was forged, while behind us, on the roads and rivers of Thornfell, every hunter the Regent owned turned like compass needles and began to move.
