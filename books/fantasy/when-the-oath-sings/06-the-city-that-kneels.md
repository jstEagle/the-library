## 6. The City That Kneels

The capital of Thornfell announced itself three miles before its walls, the way a fever announces itself before the rash.

We came over the last ridge at midmorning—Maren Cotch's caravan, six wagons of salted fish and cheap wine and one very expensive lie—and there it was below us in the river valley: Carrowgate, the City That Kneels. White walls, slate roofs, the palace on its hill with banners so red they looked like wounds that hadn't been allowed to close. And rising from the great square in the city's heart, visible even from this distance, a column of pale light.

"That's new," said Maren beside me, shading her eyes with one ringed hand. "Used to be a fountain."

"It's an Oathfire," I said.

I hadn't meant to say it aloud. Maren turned and looked at me with the particular patience of a woman who had paid good coin for my silence and was now recalculating its worth.

"You know things," she said. "That's why I hired you."

"You hired me because I'm cheap and don't drink."

"I hired you," she said, "because when the toll-riders shook down Dannel's wagon two passes back, you looked at them like a cat looks at a dog it could kill if it felt like it, and they *left*. Cheap is a bonus." She flicked her reins. "Tell me about the fire on the walk. I don't pay for silence until we're through the gate."

So I told her what little I knew, which was more than most people knew and less than I wished. The Oathfires were old—older than me, older than the purge—but they'd been ceremonial once. A flame lit at coronations, fed by the king's own vow, burning as long as his oath held. A pretty symbol. A candle for the crown.

This one burned every day now. This one was fed.

"Fed by what?" Maren asked.

"Oaths," I said. "Thousands of them. Every day."

She was quiet for a while, and then she did something I didn't expect: she spat off the side of the wagon, deliberately, where no clerk or soldier could see it hit the road.

"My mother used to say loyalty's like salt," she said. "Worth having, worthless if someone pours it down your throat by the pound." She gathered her reins. "Keep your sword quiet in the city, Yarrow. Whatever else you are, keep it quiet."

If only she knew how hard that particular promise was to keep.

* * *

The gates of Carrowgate were built for armies and currently processing farmers, which made for a slow, sweating crush of carts and geese and children under the gaze of guards who watched not the cargo but the *people*. That should have warned me sooner. Gates check goods. What these men were checking was mouths.

A clerk stood at a lectern just inside the archway, silver chain around his neck holding a badge shaped like an open hand. He wasn't reading anything. He was listening.

"Name and trade?" he asked Maren, who had smoothed her merchant's smile into place like a fresh apron.

"Maren Cotch, dried goods and wine, out of Harrowmere. Six wagons, four guards, papers all in order."

"And your guard here?" His eyes slid to me. They were pale, unblinking, patient in a way that raised every hair I owned. There was something wrong with how still he sat, like a man listening to a second conversation happening underneath mine.

"Astrid Fell," I said. It was a name I kept folded flat for occasions like this. "Guard. Sword and bow."

He nodded, wrote nothing, and then leaned slightly toward me—not toward Maren, toward *me*, as if some instrument behind his eyes had twitched.

"Sworn to your employer?" he asked. Mild as milk. "Standard procedure for contracted arms. Swear her safe conduct through the week, and be about your business."

The words landed in my stomach like stones dropped down a well.

There it was. Not a question—a hook, dressed as procedure. In seven years I had learned every shape of danger that could find a woman like me, and none of them had ever arrived wearing a clerk's badge and asking, politely, for the exact thing I would die before giving. Because I knew—I knew in my bones, in the scar-tissue of my gift—what an oath spoken near that column of light became. Fuel. The fire ate vows the way a furnace eats coal, and somewhere above us on the hill, something grew fat on every scrap.

"No contract yet," Maren cut in smoothly, before I could open my mouth and ruin both our lives. "Trial week. I don't swear guards on till they've proven they won't run at the first bandit." She laughed. "You know how it is. Half these swords are boys playing at heroes."

The clerk regarded her. Then me. Then, briefly, the hilt of my sword—an ugly thing, deliberately ugly, nicked steel and a cracked grip, the kind of blade a man buys when he plans to abandon it in a ditch. My real blade slept wrapped in oilcloth beneath the false floor of the third wagon, and even it was disguised: plain crossguard, no maker's mark, its voice muffled under layers of linen and shame.

"Of course," the clerk said at last, and stepped back. "Enjoy Carrowgate. Mind the evening bells. Attendance is expected."

"Attendance," Maren repeated, rolling forward, "at *what*?"

"The Vespers, mistress. The swearing." He smiled. It reached his mouth and stopped well short of his eyes. "Everyone attends."

* * *

They held the Vespers in the great square, and everyone attended.

I learned quickly that "expected" was doing gentle work in that sentence. By law, every resident within the walls was required to present themselves at the Oathfire at dusk—merchants, smiths, laundresses, beggars, children old enough to speak. Visitors were "invited." The invitation was delivered by soldiers who walked you there the way a shepherd walks a sheep that has expressed opinions about fences.

So at dusk I stood in a crowd of ten thousand souls, wedged between a tanner who smelled of his trade and a boy of maybe nine clutching his mother's skirt, and I watched the city kneel.

It began with bells. Then the clerks came out—the silver-collared ones, dozens of them, moving through the crowd with their badges catching the firelight, and the crowd's murmur died to nothing. A priest in white mounted the steps beneath the flame. The Oathfire rose behind him, thirty feet of pale light that gave no heat; I could feel that absence from fifty yards away, a cold spot in the evening air, wrong as a heartbeat heard from outside your own chest.

"Loyal subjects of Thornfell," the priest called, and ten thousand voices answered him.

Not all at once. Not eagerly. That was the thing that turned my blood to ice water—that it wasn't fervor. It was *practice*. The response came tired and automatic, worn smooth as a stair-stone, from throats that had said these words every dusk for years. The tanner beside me mouthed along without looking up. The boy got most of it wrong and his mother corrected him in a whisper, gently, the way you correct a child's letters.

*I serve the crown that serves me. My word is the king's word. My strength is the king's strength. Take what I am and make it Thornfell's.*

And with each repetition, the fire *fed*.

I saw it plainly—more plainly, I think, than anyone else in that square, because I had ears for it no living person was supposed to have. Every oath spoken drifted upward like smoke and poured into the flame, and the flame brightened, and with every brightening I felt a faint pull behind my sternum, a tug on something deep in me that answered to vows. My gift stirred like a dog hearing a whistle pitched too high for human ears. Beneath the oilcloth in the third wagon, I would have sworn I felt my true blade tremble.

And underneath the feeding fire—underneath, far underneath, so deep I might have imagined it—something else. A hunger with a shape. A vast attention turning over in its sleep, tasting the evening's meal, growing by inches.

The Regent. It had to be. Aldric's rumor, the courier's dying warning, the whole rotten architecture of it, laid bare in front of me in pale firelight: a king bound to a parasite that drank oaths, and a kingdom converted into a farm.

The ceremony ended. Ten thousand people rose from their knees, collected their children, and shuffled home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and fear. Nobody spoke above a murmur. Nobody met anyone's eyes. I had grown up in the last years of the swordspeaker orders; I remembered congregations like this, remembered the particular silence of people who believe themselves surrounded by informants. I had hoped never to see it again in a city square, worn like fashion, enforced by clerks with open-hand badges.

The boy with the wrong words caught my eye as his mother pulled him away. He looked at me the way children look at strangers—openly, without armor—and then he looked back at the dying fire and asked, in the carrying whisper of the young:

"Mama, what happens if somebody doesn't say it?"

His mother's hand tightened on his shoulder. She didn't answer. She walked faster.

I stood alone in the emptying square a moment longer than was wise, watching the flame gutter down to its night-size, a pale pillar thin as a spear shaft. Cold radiated off it. Or maybe that was me.

*You shouldn't be here,* said a voice at my hip.

I froze. Not the true blade—it was forty yards away in a wagon. The voice came from the cheap steel at my belt, the ugly sword, the one I'd bought precisely because it was supposed to be too dull-witted to talk. But swords are like people that way: starve them of speech long enough and they'll break their silence at the worst possible moment.

"*Quiet,*" I breathed, lips barely moving.

*It knows we're here,* the blade whispered. Its voice was small and rusty, a door unused for years. *The fire. It tastes like the purge. Like the night they came. Don't you remember?*

I remembered. Seven years, and the memory arrived whole anyway: torchlight on snow, my mentor Maera standing in the doorway of the chapter-house with her blade drawn—not against the soldiers, but flat, in salute, giving them the honor they were about to deny us—and her voice ringing across the yard: "*Never speak for a crown again.*" Her last command to me, screamed past a soldier's gauntlet, meant as a curse on the order and received by seventeen-year-old me as gospel.

Maera, I thought, staring up at the pale fire eating ten thousand swallowed voices—you'd take one look at this square and burn it down yourself.

*Astrid.* The sword used my false name, gently mocking. *Walk away from the light.*

I walked away from the light.

* * *

Maren's warehouse lodgings were in Tannery Row, which meant I spent the night breathing leather-rot through a window that didn't close, and it was still better than the dreams. In the dreams the fire had a face, and the face wore Edric's crown, and when it opened its mouth to speak, ten thousand voices came out in unison, including mine.

Dawn brought no relief, only routine. I took up my post on Maren's second wagon as it crawled toward the merchant quarter, and that was where I saw the morning ritual.

The Vespers were compulsory. The mornings were worse, because the mornings were *voluntary*.

They lined up outside the Oathfire's plaza from first light: shopkeepers wanting favorable tax treatment, petitioners hoping their suits moved faster, young men seeking commissions in the guard. Each one knelt before a silver-collared clerk, swore a fresh oath—specific, personal, tailored—and received in exchange a small wax seal pressed to the back of the hand. I watched a woman swear that her shop would price bread fairly for a season and get her seal. I watched a young soldier swear he'd die before he let harm reach the palace and get his seal, and stand, and glow faintly around the edges, literally glow, a shimmer like heat-haze that only I seemed able to see. Marked. Owned. Improved, if you believed the queue, which stretched half a mile and hummed with the desperate energy of people who needed something from power.

"They call them the Anointed now," said a voice beside me.

Aldric Vane leaned against the wagon's flank, dressed as a carter, looking like what he was—a knight pretending badly at being nobody. We'd agreed he'd travel separately and meet us inside the walls; seeing him in daylight, in the open, made my teeth itch.

"You're supposed to be two streets over," I murmured.

"I was. The queue was shorter here." He nodded at the plaza. "Seven years ago there were oaths in this city, but they were given. Marriage vows, service vows, a magistrate's oath of office. Now look at it. They sell pieces of themselves daily and thank the clerks for the discount." His jaw worked. "When I was sworn, it meant something. One oath, one lifetime, freely offered. This—" he gestured at the queue, at the seals, at the whole kneeling machinery of it "—this isn't loyalty. It's *harvest*."

Below us, another petitioner finished, glowed, and walked away lighter somehow, emptier, smiling.

"How long?" I asked quietly. "How long has it been like this?"

"Three years. Since the king changed." Aldric's voice went flat, the way it always did when he talked about Edric—the grief of a man mourning someone still walking around. "First the daily Vespers. Then the seals. Then the clerks. Each step small enough that no one rioted. Each step a stone in a road, and the road leads up that hill." He looked at the palace, white and blind in the morning sun. "Tonight matters, Yarrow. Lord Chancellor Deverell hosts the autumn feast, and the king will appear. All the great houses attend. If we're going to get close enough to learn how he's bound, it starts tonight."

"I know. I've got the invitations."

"Stolen?"

"Forged. Stolen's amateur."

Something almost like a smile crossed his ruined face, gone as fast as it came. Then he straightened, and I followed his gaze, and my stomach dropped.

Across the plaza, a silver-collared clerk had stopped. He stood at the edge of the queue, badge in hand, and he was not looking at the petitioners. He was looking at our wagon. At me. And his head was tilted—tilted exactly like a man listening to a second conversation underneath the first one—and slowly, with the unhurried certainty of something that has never once been refused, he began to walk toward us.

"He can't know," Aldric breathed. "No one knows what you are."

"Someone knows *something*," I said, and my hand found the ugly sword's grip, and it whispered against my palm, hungry and afraid at once: *Here it comes. Here comes the purge again.*

* * *

The clerk reached the wagon and inclined his head with perfect courtesy.

"Mistress Fell," he said. My false name, in my false ears, from a stranger who had never been told it. "The Office of Devotions would be honored by your presence at tonight's feast. A summons has been issued in your name." He produced a card, white as bone, sealed with red wax bearing the sigil of an open hand. "Attendance is expected."

Behind him, above the plaza, the Oathfire flared—bright, brief, delighted, like a throat swallowing.

"Expected," I repeated, taking the card because refusing it would have been the loudest thing I'd done in seven years.

"Just so." The clerk smiled his smile that stopped short of his eyes. "The king so enjoys meeting new voices."

He bowed and walked away into the queue, and Aldric stared at me across the wagon rail, and somewhere beneath the oilcloth in Maren's third wagon, my true blade began—very softly, very sweetly—to sing.
