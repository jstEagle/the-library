## 7. Two Voices in One Throat

I have attended a hundred feasts in the Hall of Gilded Antlers, and I can tell you the exact weight of boredom that settles on a knight's shoulders when the third course arrives and the speeches begin. It is a specific weight. It sits between the shoulder blades like a saddlebag full of wet wool. Tonight, standing at the back of the hall in a merchant's livery with a wooden serving tray and a face nobody looked at twice, I felt that familiar weight arrive on schedule — and then it never left, because the king opened his mouth to welcome his lords, and something else answered.

Let me describe the hall properly, because you will need it for what comes after.

The Hall of Gilded Antlers earned its name honestly: every pillar is wrapped in the shed antlers of the red stags of Thornfell, gilded so thickly that in torchlight the whole room seems to grow upward like some impossible coral forest. Three hundred candles. Four long tables for the lords and their retinues. The high table set on a dais beneath a banner older than the dynasty — a white hart crowned in thorns. And everywhere, woven into the garlands and carved into the chairbacks and stitched into the servants' collars, the same motto in letters of gold thread: *BY OATH WE STAND.*

Seven years ago I would have read those words and felt my chest swell. Tonight they looked to me like a cage label.

Beatrix worked the near tables as a server, which was her idea, because she argued — correctly — that no one watches a woman carrying wine. I stood behind the lords' benches holding a tray of roasted quail and doing an impression of furniture. We had entered the palace that morning with a legitimate merchant's writ, forged papers of excellent quality, and a plan so simple it embarrassed me: get close enough to the throne room to hear what the king actually sounded like, then get out. Beatrix's sword had been reacting to the palace since we crossed the outer wall. She described it to me afterward as "a dog that smells smoke two streets away and can't find the fire." Her blade hummed against her hip under its serving-girl disguise, low and continuous, like a struck tuning fork someone wouldn't let finish.

So when King Edric rose from the high table and lifted his cup, every hair on my arms stood up before he said a word.

* * *

You should understand what Edric Vane — no relation, though the heralds enjoyed the coincidence once and stopped enjoying it when I asked them to stop — sounded like before. I served him for eleven years. I carried his standard at Marrow Ford, where we lost forty men in the river mud and he stood waist-deep in it for six hours pulling them out by their armor straps. I heard him laugh at his own bad jokes. I heard him pray over dead boys whose names he insisted on knowing, all of them, even the ones who'd arrived at muster that morning. His voice was not beautiful. It was a working voice, a soldier's voice, slightly roughened by an old throat wound he'd taken at Cael Bridge, and when he spoke you believed him, which in a king is rarer than courage and worth considerably more.

The thing that rose to greet his lords tonight used his mouth. It did not use his voice.

The words were right. That's what I want to be clear about, because it matters: the words themselves were exactly the sort of thing Edric would say. He welcomed the Lord Marshal home from the eastern marches. He praised the harvest. He spoke of loyalty in terms that were warm rather than threatening, gratitude rather than demand. Every sentence was one he might have written himself on one of his better days.

But underneath the words, underneath the pitch and timbre of that roughened soldier's voice, there was a second sound. I don't know how else to describe it except that it was *underneath*, the way bedrock is underneath a road. A resonance too low to be heard so much as felt, arriving through the floorboards and the bench legs and the bones of my own skull. And it moved when he moved. When Edric paused to drink, the second voice paused a half-breath late, like a shadow that hadn't quite learned the choreography.

And it was hungry. I know how that sounds. Hungry is a word you use for wolves and tax collectors. But there is no other word for the quality of attention in that undertone — the sense that every oath spoken in that hall was being tasted, weighed, swallowed, that each *by oath we stand* went down the thing's throat like honey off a spoon.

Around me, three hundred people heard only their king. They laughed at his light remarks. The Lord Marshal's wife dabbed her eyes at a mention of her son's promotion. Toasts went up, one after another, and with every toast — *"To the king!" "By oath we stand!" "Long life to Edric of Thornfell!"* — I watched the candle flames lean, all together, toward the dais. Not much. An inch, maybe less. But all together, like grass bending before wind that wasn't blowing anywhere else in the room.

I caught Beatrix's eye across the hall. She had gone very still, a pitcher held mid-pour, wine threading into a lord's cup and beginning to overflow because she wasn't watching it. She could hear it too. Of course she could. She had spent her whole life listening to what steel remembers; she was perhaps the only other person in that room built to hear what a crown had forgotten it was carrying.

Her gaze flicked from the dais to me and back, and she gave me the smallest nod: *you confirm this.* Because it was mine to confirm. Because I was the one who had loved him.

* * *

Here is the part I have not said aloud since the night I was stripped of my spurs, and I will say it now because Aldric Vane has discovered that shame kept secret grows teeth.

Three years ago, I began to notice that the king was wrong in small ways.

That's how it starts, with men like him. Not madness, not cruelty — wrongness. He forgot a name he should have known. He signed an order twice. He stood at a window during council and didn't hear his own marshal asking him a question three times. I told myself he was tired. The eastern war had ground on for four years; he carried it the way he carried everything, silently and badly, refusing to sleep until the dispatches were answered.

Then came the winter of the Silver Assizes, when he sentenced a smuggler to hang for a crime the man had already been pardoned for — pardoned by the king himself, in writing, eight months prior. I brought him the pardon. I laid it on the table in front of him. He read it, and I swear to you on whatever is left of my honor, he read it, and then he said, in a perfectly reasonable voice, "The law must be seen to hold," and signed the death warrant anyway.

I challenged him. Privately, respectfully, but I challenged him — told him that mercy withdrawn without cause was worse than cruelty, because cruelty at least knows itself. And he looked up at me from that table and for one moment — one — his face changed. Not hardened. Changed. The way a mask changes when the man under it stops bothering to hold it up. And something looked out of his eyes at me that had been looking for a very long time, patiently, the way a fisherman looks at water.

He said, "Ser Aldric. You look tired."

That was all. You look tired. But I went back to my quarters and sat on my bunk with my sword across my knees until dawn, and by morning I had done the arithmetic that ended my career: either my king had gone mad, or my king was gone, or — and this was the possibility I could not put down — something was wearing him the way a man wears a coat.

I said so. Not in those words; I dressed it in the respectable language of concern for his health, petitioned for a physician, then for a priest, then for both. And when the petitions failed I made the mistake of saying the true thing at last, in front of the wrong audience, in the council chamber, with the Lord Chancellor's ears open: I said the king's voice had another voice under it.

They took my spurs on a Tuesday. There was no trial, precisely — a hearing, conducted politely, in which I was invited to retract and declined, and the record shows I was dismissed for "instability of mind occasioned by grief," which was almost clever, because my brother had died that spring and grief makes a convenient diagnosis. They gave me thirty days to leave the capital. I remember walking out through the Gate of Stags and thinking: well. At least I'll be free of the sound.

I wasn't. For three years, on bad nights, I've heard it anyway — that undertone, patient as erosion. I told myself it was memory. Guilt. The mind punishing a man for failing his liege.

Tonight I learned it wasn't memory. Tonight I learned the sound travels.

* * *

The feast wound on. Courses came and went. And here I must give credit where it is due, because Beatrix Yarrow did something that evening that I still think about with a mixture of admiration and cold dread.

She got closer.

Not all at once — nothing about the woman is ever all at once — but in increments, the way water gets into a cellar. A spilled cup near the dais steps. A tray that needed refreshing at the high table. A servant's errand to the king's own cupbearer, and then a moment, no more than ten heartbeats, when she knelt at the base of the dais gathering broken glass while the king stood directly above her, mid-speech, addressing the assembled lords of Thornfell on the subject of unity.

She was close enough to touch his boot.

And I watched her face while the second voice rolled overhead like a tide going out over gravel, and I saw her flinch — actually flinch, the pitcher wobbling in her hands — and I understood that whatever she was hearing at that distance was worse than what reached me at fifty feet. Worse, and clearer. She gathered her glass. She withdrew. She passed me at the end of the lords' benches, and without turning her head, without moving her lips more than a breath's width, she said:

"It answers the oaths. Listen. It repeats them."

I listened. God help me, I listened, and she was right, and I will spend the rest of my life wishing she hadn't been. Beneath the king's speech, beneath the warm and reasonable words, the second voice was not speaking its own sentences. It was *echoing*. Every vow spoken in that hall came back to it transformed — flattened, deepened, stripped of everything human and reduced to its bare contractual skeleton. When the Lord Marshal toasted "my life and sword to the king, now and always," the undertone repeated it, but wrong: *life. sword. always.* No warmth. No speaker. Just the terms, taken, receipted, owned.

It wasn't eavesdropping. It was collecting.

Every oath sworn in Edric's name, in that hall, in that city, in that kingdom — collected. Filed. Fed upon. Three hundred years of Thornfell's loyal vows, and every day the clerks in their silver collars walked the streets administering new ones, morning oaths and market oaths and schoolroom oaths, and every one of them flowed to the dais like tributaries to a sea, and the sea grew.

I put down my tray. I am told a knight learns to keep his face through anything; I am here to report that keeping your face while understanding, finally and completely, what has been done to the best man you ever served is the hardest drill there is, and I nearly failed it in front of the entire court.

* * *

We found each other afterward in the scullery corridor, among the steam and the shouting and the mountains of dirty plate, where even the Regent's creatures presumably do not listen, because who listens to dishwater?

Beatrix pulled me into an alcove stacked with empty casks. Up close, in the tallow light, she looked the way I felt: wrung out. She had her hand pressed flat against her hip where the sword hid under the serving dress, and I realized she was holding it the way you'd hold a shaking dog.

"Say it," she said. "Out loud. I need to hear a human being say it."

"The king is dead," I said, "or dying, and has been for three years, and something is wearing him."

"Something old." She swallowed. "Older than the dynasty. That voice doesn't belong to any living throat. It belongs to—" She stopped herself. I watched her decide not to finish the sentence, file it instead somewhere behind her eyes, next to a map sealed in wax that we had not yet fully read. "You knew," she said. "Before tonight. How long?"

"Three years. I told the council. They unmade me for it." I laughed, and it came out wrong, bitter and thin. "Eleven years of service. Marrow Ford. Cael Bridge. I pulled forty men out of a river for that man, and when I tried to pull him out of whatever had him, they handed my spurs to a man who'd never questioned anything in his life. They gave my command to Hallowell. Do you know Hallowell? A decent enough officer, dull as ditchwater, and that's the point — they promoted the man least capable of noticing, because not-noticing was the qualification."

"You loved him."

It wasn't a question, and I didn't insult us both by pretending otherwise. "He was the finest man I ever knew," I said. "That's the worst of it, Beatrix. If Edric had been a tyrant, if he'd been weak or cruel or stupid, this would be simple. Tyrants can be killed. Mad kings can be set aside. But he was *good* — genuinely good, the kind of good that does its work quietly — and something found that goodness and hollowed it out and hung its own hunger inside the shape of it. And now it thanks the harvest lords in his voice. Now it collects oaths in his name. Now every honest vow in this kingdom feeds the thing that ate him."

My voice cracked on the last word. I'm not proud of it. I stood there in a borrowed servant's livery in a scullery corridor and grieved for a man who was, at that very moment, alive and dining forty feet above me, and I could not have told you whether the man I grieved for was dead or merely buried — imprisoned behind his own smiling face, watching through eyes that no longer answered to him. I think that's the version I choose. I think Edric Vane is in there. I think he sees everything the Regent does with his hands. And I think that if there is any mercy left in this world, it will eventually take the form of someone giving that man back his own mouth.

Beatrix was quiet for a long time. Then: "The vault."

"What?"

"The map. The courier. 'The crown eats him' — that's what he said, dying in my barn, and I thought it was fever-talk. It wasn't. It was a field report." Her hand was still flat against the hidden sword. "There's a place where the first oath was made. Whatever wears the king was born there. And whatever was born there can maybe be unmade there." She looked up at me, and her eyes had the particular flat brightness I had learned to distrust, because it preceded her volunteering for things. "We stop hiding, Ser Aldric. Tomorrow we move."

"And go where? The vault is a rumor. The passes are closed. The hunters—"

"—are sworn to the crown," she finished, softly. "Sworn. Every one of them. Which means every one of them is leashed to *that*." She tilted her head back toward the hall above us, toward the dais, toward the patient hungry dark wearing a beloved face. "Do you understand what that means, Aldric? It doesn't send soldiers after us. It sends *oaths*. Men who cannot disobey, chasing a woman whose whole gift is listening to what oaths cost." She smiled, and there was no humor in it whatsoever. "It should have sent them years ago. Which means either it hasn't noticed us—"

"—or it's letting us run," I said. "Toward the vault. Toward the one place it can't afford to leave standing."

Neither of us said anything for a moment. Somewhere beyond the casks, a kitchen boy dropped a stack of trenchers and was cursed for it, and the ordinary noise of the living world went on around our silence.

"One more thing," Beatrix said at last. "When I was close to him. Under the dais." She hesitated, and I saw that whatever came next had frightened her more than the voice itself. "The second voice paused. When I knelt there, it — noticed me. Just for a heartbeat. It stopped echoing and it *listened back*. And then the king looked down at me, with his own eyes, Edric's eyes, and for less than a second there was a man behind them, and his mouth shaped a word around the speech he was giving. No sound. Just the shape of it."

"What word?"

"'Run,'" she said. "'Run, girl.'"

Above us, muffled by floors and stone and three hundred years of ceremony, the feast roared its loyalty to the king. By oath we stand. By oath we stand. The candles leaned, an inch, all together, toward the throne.

And far to the north, past the walls, past the fells, past the edge of every map the court considered real, something waited at the bottom of a black cathedral that had been listening to Thornfell's promises for longer than Thornfell had existed — and tomorrow, whether it wished it or not, we were going to bring it ours.
