## 8. The Regent Explained

They came to me at dusk, as all the truly doomed do.

I had been cataloguing my ruins—that is the phrase I use, and I invite you to find a better one—when the door of what used to be the Scriptorium of Saint Vess opened without a knock. This was not remarkable in itself. The door has not had hinges since the burning, and it leans at an angle that might charitably be called an invitation. What was remarkable was who leaned through it: a woman with a smuggler's tan and a knight's posture trying very hard to be a smuggler's posture, and behind her, a big man moving like a man carrying something breakable inside his own chest.

"You're the scholar," she said.

"I am a scholar," I corrected, because exile strips everything from you except pedantry. "There were eleven of us. There is now one of me. The arithmetic of purges is simple and always runs in the same direction."

Her eyes went to the burned shelves. I watched her take inventory—the way smugglers do, weighing what's worth stealing—and then, to her credit, she looked away from the ash and at me instead. That alone told me more about her than any question could have.

"Maesin Corvel," I said, offering the name like a handkerchief already soiled. "Formerly Royal Lecturer in Oathcraft. Formerly keeper of the third-best library in Thornfell. Formerly many things. Currently: a man who lives in his own cinders and charges for conversation."

"We can pay."

"Everyone says so. Fewer people mean it. Sit down anyway; the chairs are the only things the fire disliked less than me."

* * *

Now, before I tell you what I told them—and I will tell you, because it cost me enough that hoarding it would be a second, smaller burning—you should understand what kind of scholar you are dealing with. Not the courtly kind. Not the kind who writes elegant treatises on the metaphysics of fealty for lords who want their obedience decorated. I was the other kind, the kind the crown kept in a basement and pretended did not exist: the kind who reads the oldest contracts.

The oldest documents in Thornfell predate the crown itself. That is not poetry; that is filing. King Aldous the First did not found this kingdom. He *purchased* it—or rather, he borrowed it, from something older than kingship, and the deed of that borrowing is the single most dangerous piece of paper ever inked in these fells. I read it when I was thirty-one years old and arrogant enough to believe reading was safe.

It is not safe. Nothing in this story is safe. But you asked, Beatrix Yarrow, and your sword has been humming at your hip since you walked in, which suggests it already knows what I know, and I have never liked being the only one awake in the house.

So. The Regent explained, as best as one burnt man can explain a thing that was never meant to be understood, only fed.

* * *

"Tell me about the Hollow Vault," the woman said.

I poured three cups of plum wine that had survived the fire by being buried, as I had, beneath the floor. "Direct. Good. Indirection is a luxury of the employed." I sat. "First, a question. What is an oath?"

She frowned. "A promise. Sworn."

"A promise is words," I said. "An oath is words plus weight. Anyone can promise. An oath binds—makes the breaking cost something real. Every culture in these fells discovered this independently, which should have been our first clue that we weren't discovering anything. We were remembering. Or being remembered." I drank. "The oldest oath in existence—the first one, the ur-oath, the founding vow—was sworn before there were kingdoms, before writing, before names, possibly before gods. We don't know who swore it or to whom. But we know what it was *for*, because fragments of its wording survive in every binding-rite on the continent like a watermark under later printing."

"And it was for?"

"Safety," I said. "Which is the most frightening answer available. Someone, long ago, stood in terror of something—winter, war, hunger, the dark, pick your ancient horror—and swore themselves to a power greater than themselves in exchange for protection. And whatever heard that oath answered." I let that sit. "Here is the part the crown burns libraries over. It answered *too well*. It learned the shape of the bargain. It became the bargain. And a bargain, once perfected, does not need the original parties anymore. It needs only to be repeated."

The knight spoke for the first time. He had a voice like a door that sticks. "You're saying the Regent is the first oath."

"I'm saying the Regent is what grew around the first oath, the way a pearl grows around a wound. Call it a parasite if you like—it prefers 'Regent,' which tells you something about its taste in titles. It cannot exist on its own. Oaths made freely between equals sustain nothing but the people making them. But an oath sworn to a *crown*—an oath with hierarchy baked into it, kneeling built into its grammar—that kind feeds something. Every vow taken in the king's name passes through it. Every soldier's enlistment. Every magistrate's investiture. Every wedding where the priest invokes the crown's blessing. Every child taught to say 'long live the king' before they can say 'no.'"

The woman—Yarrow—had gone very still. People who live hidden develop stillness the way soldiers develop scars. "How long?"

"How long has it worn the king?" I shook my head. "Wrong question, though I understand why you asked it. The better question is: how long has the king worn it? Because I promise you, whoever sits that throne now began as a man, and men do not survive that seat unchanged. The Regent does not devour its host quickly. Devouring wastes the meal. It inhabits. Slowly, over years, the king's will and the parasite's appetite become a single instrument playing two songs, and everyone listening assumes the discord is politics."

"The rumors," the knight said heavily. "That His Majesty speaks in two voices."

"That His Majesty *is* two voices," I said, "and only one of them bought the throat."

* * *

Yarrow set her cup down untouched. A pity. The wine is genuinely good; the fire had no palate. "You said it can't be killed."

"I said no such thing. I said—" and here I paused, because the next sentence has ended friendships and, once, nearly ended me. "I said it cannot be killed. Killing requires that a thing be alive along lines we recognize. The Regent is not alive. It is *enforced*. It is a contract that has achieved continuity. You can no more kill it than you can kill interest, or debt, or the habit of kneeling. Cut the body wearing it, and the body dies and the contract simply... waits. Finds the next throat. There have been gaps—years, decades—when the throne sat empty. The Regent does not mind waiting. Waiting is what contracts do best."

"Then what?" she asked. "If you can't kill it—"

"Unbind it," I said. "There is a difference between destroying a thing and dissolving the terms that hold it together. A marriage can't be murdered, madam, but it can be annulled—if you return to the original terms and void them properly, with all parties present and the record corrected." I tapped the scorched table between us. "The Regent was made somewhere. Contracts have places of signing. Yours is called the Hollow Vault, and yes, it exists, and yes, the map your dead courier carried leads to it, and yes, I know about the courier, because I know about everything that bleeds through the low country within forty miles of my ruin. Grief travels fast out here. So does paper."

Something moved across her face—grief of her own, freshly disturbed. She didn't deny the courier. Smart. Denial costs energy, and she struck me as a woman who audited hers to the copper.

"Why haven't you told anyone?" the knight demanded. "You've known this. You've known for years. The capital swallows oaths by the thousand daily and you sat here with the cure in your head and—"

"Told whom?" I did not raise my voice. One learns economy. "Told the clerks with the silver collars? Told the Voice of the Crown, whose entire office exists to keep this secret? Ser Vane—I know who you are; disgraced knights travel badly disguised—you served the king. Tell me: when you knelt and swore your oath of service, did anyone explain what you were swearing *to*?"

His jaw worked. "No."

"No. Because the oath's power depends on ignorance. A feeding trough works best when the animals don't know they're in a pen. The moment the realm understands that its loyalty oaths are meals, the meals stop arriving willingly, and willing is the whole point. Compelled oaths feed it thin. Freely given ones—oaths of love, of honor, of genuine devotion—are fat with something it can actually use." I looked at him steadily. "You loved your king. Didn't you. That's why the doubt broke you rather than the other way around."

He said nothing, which from a man built like him was a scream.

* * *

Let me tell you about the night the library burned, since we are trading wounds.

Seven years ago—not quite; six and some months—I sent a letter to the Privy Council. I had finished translating the deed of Aldous the First, all of it, including the clause in the margin that the court scribes had rendered decoratively illegible for four hundred years. The clause reads, in the old tongue: *and should the vassal seek unbinding, let him come to the place of signing, and speak the founding words again, and unsay them.*

Unsay them. Four hundred years of scribes looking at that word and choosing to see ornament.

I received a reply within the week. It was courteous. It thanked me for my scholarship. It invited me to present my findings at court, and I was young enough, and lonely enough, and vain enough to go.

I presented. They listened beautifully. And three days later the Scriptorium of Saint Vess burned with ten of my colleagues inside it, and I survived because I had been drunk in the fens with a fishmonger's wife, a fact I have never decided whether to regard as shame or providence. The crown declared it lightning. In winter. During a drought.

So understand me, Yarrow, when I tell you what I am about to tell you costs me nothing and everything: I have been waiting seven years for someone to walk through that hingeless door and ask the right questions. I had begun to suspect I would die waiting, and that my epitaph would be *he knew, and he kept the ashes warm.*

* * *

"The Vault," she said. "What's in it?"

"Nothing," I said, and watched her blink. "That is not evasion; that is architecture. The Hollow Vault is hollow because the founding oath was spoken in emptiness—a place with no witnesses but the dark, no altars, no god, just a terrified voice and the thing that heard it. The Vault is where the terms live. Not parchment—the terms live in the stone, in the silence, in the *shape of the air*. When you stand in it, you will feel the first oath the way you feel weather before rain. And if you are what I think you are"—here I nodded at her hip, at the blade she thought she'd kept quiet—"you won't need me to describe it. Swords remember oaths, swordspeaker. Yours has been trying to talk to you since the pass road. I could hear it humming from the ridge."

Her hand moved toward the hilt and stopped. Old reflex. Seven years of keeping it sheathed and silent written into the tendons.

"Don't apologize for it," I said, gentler than I intended. Exile softens a man in strange places. "Your gift is not the curse. Your gift is the only instrument in Thornfell capable of reading the original document, because the original document was never written down. It was *sworn*. And what is sworn can only be re-read by a voice that speaks sword-tongue—the tongue oaths were spoken in, back before we invented priests and parchment to launder them."

"And if I read it," she said slowly, "and then unsay it—"

"Then you attempt the only operation the Regent truly fears. Not death. Amendment. You would be asking the contract to void itself, using its own founding language, in its own founding place." I spread my hands, and the fire-scars on my knuckles caught the lamplight like a map of small countries. "Will it work? I am a scholar, not a prophet. I know the theory. The theory says a founding oath, freely renounced at the place of signing, unmakes everything downstream of it—every oath sworn in the crown's name goes suddenly orphaned, and the thing that fed on them starves in a single night. The theory also says the unbinding will pull at the one who performs it with the force of a river pulling at a dam. The founding words were spoken in terror and sealed in blood, and unsaying them means holding all of that in your mouth at once and letting it go."

"And if I fail?"

"Then the Regent will know precisely where you are, what you are, and how afraid you are," I said. "And it will make you an offer so tailored to your grief that you may not recognize it as an offer. It is very good at offers. It has had the whole history of this kingdom to practice."

* * *

They stayed until the lamp burned low. The knight slept in the doorway with his sword across his knees, a man who had forgotten how to sleep lying down. The woman sat up with me over the last of the wine and asked small, precise questions—the courier's route, the seal, the marginalia—which is how I knew she had already read the map more carefully than she admitted.

Near the end, she asked the question I had been dreading, the one every doomed visitor eventually asks, the one I ask myself at three in the morning while the wind rearranges my ruins:

"Why help us? Why risk it? You survived."

I turned my cup in my scarred hands for a long moment.

"Because I have spent seven years telling myself that knowledge kept private is knowledge kept safe," I said finally. "And it is not. Knowledge kept private is knowledge that dies with its keeper, and the fire already tried that once. Ten colleagues, madam. I owe them a reading of that clause that isn't smoke." I looked at her, at the sword that hummed its low patient note against her hip like a held breath. "And because I am old enough now to admit that I did not survive the burning. I merely failed to be present for it. There is a difference, and the difference has teeth."

She rose to go. At the leaning door she paused, silhouetted against the last grey light, and asked one more thing.

"The place of signing. How do we get there alive, with half the hunters in Thornfell between us and it?"

"Ah," I said, and reached beneath the floorboards for the thing I had sworn I would never hand to another living soul: a courier's route, thirty years old, through country the crown's maps call impassable and the crown's hunters call unnecessary. "That," I said, "is a different lecture. And it begins with the Greywater Bridge, and ends, if you're clever and lucky, at a smuggler queen who owes me a favor and will resent you for collecting it."

She took the paper. Our fingers did not touch; exiles learn to keep even gratitude at arm's length.

"Scholar," she said at the threshold. "If this works—if I unsay it—what happens to everyone who ever swore? The knights, the clerks, the brides and soldiers. Does it hurt them?"

I considered lying. I have little enough left, but I have my honesty; it is the one book they didn't burn.

"It will feel," I said, "like setting down a weight they forgot they were carrying. Some will weep and not know why. Some will be furious. And a few—the ones who loved their king honestly, the ones whose oaths were fat and true—they will feel it as a loss, and grieve, and be free." I smiled, and it tasted like ash, as everything here does. "Freedom usually arrives looking exactly like grief, madam. That is how you know it's real."

She went out into the dark, and the knight followed, and I sat alone in my ruined library with the lamp guttering, and I did something I had not done in seven years.

I unlocked the deepest drawer, took out the deed of Aldous the First, and read the marginal clause aloud to the empty room—one voice, in one throat, meaning every word.

Somewhere far north, I imagine, something ancient lifted its head.
