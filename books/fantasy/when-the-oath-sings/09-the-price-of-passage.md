## 9. The Price of Passage

There are three ways out of Thornfell's capital when the gates are watching for your face, and I knew two of them, and both of them ended with me dead in a ditch. So it was a relief—genuine relief, like finding money in an old coat—when Aldric said the third name.

"Maren Voss," he said, in the cellar under the dyer's shop where we'd spent two days pretending to be furniture. "The Salt Queen."

"Salt Queen," I repeated. "That's a title?"

"It's what her people call her. She runs everything that moves through the fells without a seal on it. Silk, steel, people." He paused. "Secrets."

"And she'll smuggle us out because we ask nicely?"

Aldric looked at me the way you'd look at someone who asked whether wolves would share their dinner. "No one asks Maren Voss for anything. You trade for it. And whatever she names as the price—" he tapped his own chest, over the scar I hadn't asked about yet, "—she collects. Every time. I've seen men leave her table pale as whey and grateful for it."

"Charming," I said. "Where do I find this delight of the low roads?"

"You don't find her. She finds you. But there's a signal." He told me about the lamp.

* * *

The lamp was blue glass, and it hung from a hook on the Salt Gate bridge, and when I lit it at dusk and set it in the window of our rented room, nothing happened for six hours, which gave me ample opportunity to reconsider my life choices and also to re-oil my sword, which is what I do instead of prayer.

I had named it Wren, back when naming things was allowed. Cheap steel now—I'd swapped the true blade for a blacksmith's plainwork years ago—but it still remembered. That was the trouble with swordspeech: the memory lived in me as much as the metal. A blade is just a witness that happens to be sharp.

Wren hummed once, very low, around midnight. Not words. Warning.

Ten minutes later, three women came up the stairs without the stairs creaking, which should be impossible and was, apparently, merely expensive. The one in front wore grey wool and no jewelry whatsoever, which in the capital meant more wealth than any jeweled lord. She had a face like a closed ledger.

"You lit my lamp," she said.

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because I need to leave this city alive, and Ser Aldric Vane tells me you're the only door that opens quietly."

Something moved behind her eyes—not warmth, but interest, which from a smuggler queen is nearly the same thing. "Vane. The knight who broke himself. He vouches for you?"

"He's downstairs pretending to be drunk so the watch ignores him."

"He's good at pretending to be less than he is," Maren Voss said. "It's why he's still breathing." She sat without being invited, and her two shadows arranged themselves against the wall like furniture that could kill me. "So. Out of the capital. Alive. Past the gate-seals, past the clerks with their silver collars, past the hunters who've been asking after a woman who talks to swords." She smiled without any teeth involved. "You're famous in certain narrow circles."

"I'm trying very hard not to be."

"Fame doesn't care about effort." She laced her fingers. "I can move you. Two travelers, one road south, out through the salt carts before the week's end. No one stops my carts. Even the Regent's clerks wave them through—they've sworn so many oaths about loyalty they haven't got time to count barrels."

The casual horror of that landed sideways, and I let it. Seven years of shrinking teaches you when to keep your mouth shut and when opening it costs less than silence. This was a shut-mouth moment. Mostly.

"What's the price?" I asked.

"There it is," she said, pleased. "Most people flinch first. Dance around it. Ask about weather." She leaned forward. "My price is a favor. Owed to me, by you, redeemable at a time and place of my choosing."

"A favor," I said. "Unspecified."

"Unspecified. Unlimited in scope within reason. And—" here she did show teeth, briefly, like a knife being shown its sheath, "—payable in blood if I require it. Those are the terms. Refuse them, and you can try the ditches."

In the corner, Wren hummed again. Low. Not warning this time. Something closer to grief, or maybe recognition. Swords know contracts. My whole order had been built on the truth that an oath spoken aloud takes root somewhere, and grows, and feeds on things.

An unspecified favor owed to a woman like this wasn't a favor. It was a seed.

And I heard myself say, "Done."

Because that's the oldest habit I have. Someone needs doing, and I do it, and the cost goes in the ledger I don't show anyone. Hide the gift. Bury the order. Carry the map. Now this. Each time I told myself it was the last time the price would be mine alone to pay, and each time the ledger got another line in ink only I could read.

Maren Voss stood, satisfied, and shook my hand. Her grip was dry and cool and final. "Pleasure, Beatrix Yarrow. The cart leaves at dawn. Wear the grey cloak; the dye hides faces better than hoods." At the door she paused. "Oh—and don't tell the knight the terms. Men like Vane get noble about other people's debts, and nobility is bad for business."

She went down the stairs, which still didn't creak, and I sat in the dark listening to Wren hum its low grieving note, and I told myself I'd decide by morning whether to tell him.

I didn't decide. That was the decision.

* * *

Aldric met me at the salt carts before light, smelling convincingly of cheap wine he hadn't drunk. He looked at the grey cloak, at the cart full of barrels that smelled of brine enough to pickle a man's regrets, and then at my face, and I watched him do the arithmetic he was too honest to finish.

"She took the job," he said.

"For a price."

"They always name a price. What was yours?"

"The usual," I said. "Coin. Passage costs coin."

It was a lie so smooth I almost believed it myself, which is the danger of seven years' practice at small concealments: they scale.

He held my gaze a moment longer. Somewhere in that look was the man who'd reported my order's location under duress—the confession he'd made half-delirious on a riverbank—and the man who'd taken a crossbow bolt meant for me, and I couldn't tell which one was looking at me now, or maybe they were the same man, which was worse.

"All right," he said finally. "Coin."

We climbed into the barrel-cart. The driver was a boy of perhaps fourteen with the eyes of a customs officer twice his age. The city slid past the slats—dawn bells, the silver-collared clerks already at their street corners collecting the day's loyalty oaths from shopkeepers too tired to mean them, a proclamation nailed over the old proclamations until the wall wore the king's word like bark wears lichen.

Aldric watched the clerks with his jaw tight. "Three years," he murmured. "I served him three years while that thing ate him from the inside. And every oath the city swears feeds it. Every wedding vow, every knighting, every soldier's enlistment." His hand found the stump of pride where his spurs used to sit. "I swore to him too, Yarrow. Twice, now, though you only know about one."

I knew about both. He'd told me the second one on the road, the secret oath that let the Regent track him like a wound tracks fever. He thought mentioning the first was the confession. It wasn't. The second was just the deeper cut.

"Hold onto your oaths," I said. "Both of them. Until we reach the Vault."

"And after?"

"After," I said, "we'll see what a world looks like where nobody has to swear anything."

He almost smiled. Then the cart jolted through the South Gate, past a clerk who waved us through with the bored majesty of a man who had sworn away his own curiosity years ago, and the capital was behind us, and I felt the specific lightness of a debt deferred rather than paid.

* * *

We made nine miles the first day before the boy stopped the cart.

Not at a checkpoint. In the middle of a birch stand, for no reason I could see, until Wren sang—a single clear note, sharp as a struck bell—and I understood that the boy hadn't stopped for rest. He'd stopped because he'd been told to stop, and the telling hadn't come from Maren Voss.

"Off the cart," the boy said, not unkindly. "Both of you. Walk to the lightning-split oak. There's a message waiting."

"A message," Aldric said, hand drifting to his sword.

"From the Queen." The boy spat a sunflower seed husk into the brine-scented air. "She says: terms travel faster than carts. She says: the knight will want to know what the lady promised. She says—" and here the boy recited it flat, word for word, clearly having been made to memorize it, "—'a favor owed in blood, redeemable at my choosing, and the blood may be anyone's the debtor holds dear.' She says the knight should ask her why she hid it."

The birches were very quiet. So was Aldric.

He turned to me slowly, and there was no anger in his face, which was somehow far worse. There was the particular blankness of a man re-counting coins he thought he'd known the weight of.

"In blood," he said. "Anyone I hold dear. That's the term you carried out of the city sitting next to me for nine miles."

"It's a standard smuggler's clause—"

"Don't." One word, quiet, and it stopped me colder than shouting would have. "Don't do the thing where you make it small. I've watched you do it since the ferry. Since the bridge. You bleed so the rest of us don't have to notice, and you think that's kindness, and someday it's going to get you killed and I'll have helped." He exhaled. "Why didn't you tell me?"

Because telling makes it real. Because the ledger works best unshared. Because every time I've handed someone the true price, they've tried to pay it for me, and then I owe two debts instead of one.

What I said was: "Because you'd have objected, and I needed the road more than I needed your approval."

"That's honesty," Aldric said. "I'll give you that. It's also exactly how the Regent thinks. Everything is a cost to be borne alone, everyone is a resource, and trust is just a delay before the accounting." He looked at the lightning-split oak, and the sealed letter nailed to it with a black-handled knife, and didn't move toward it. "I broke something sacred once, Yarrow, and I've spent years learning the difference between pragmatism and rot. From where I'm standing right now, they look uncomfortably similar."

He walked ahead down the southward track, alone, leaving me with the knife-glinting oak and the letter and the boy, who offered me a sunflower seed with the solemnity of a funeral attendant.

I took the letter. I didn't open it yet.

Wren hummed at my hip—not warning, not grief this time, but that third note I'd only heard once before, on the night the purge began. The note a blade makes when it remembers a promise breaking.

Somewhere behind us, faint on the wind off the fells, came a sound I'd hoped not to hear for days yet: hunting horns. Three of them. Moving fast.

And pinned beneath the queen's black knife, in ink I recognized as hers, the letter's single visible line through the paper's fold:

*The favor comes due sooner than expected, little swordspeaker.*
