## 10. Ambush at Greywater Bridge

There is a particular kind of silence a man earns when he has spent three days walking beside a woman he has wronged without telling her. It is not the comfortable silence of old companions, nor the wary silence of strangers. It is the silence of a debt collector standing behind you at dinner, breathing.

Beatrix Yarrow had been breathing down my neck for three days, and she didn't even know it.

"You're limping," she said that last morning, not looking at me.

"I'm favoring my left knee. There's a difference. Limping is what old men do. Favoring is what careful ones do."

"You're forty-two, Aldric. You're both."

She had a way of saying things like that — flat, precise, a surgeon's observation delivered with a smuggler's shrug — that made it impossible to be offended. I'd tried, early on, back when we were still deciding whether to trust each other. Offense just rolled off her like rain off oiled canvas.

We came down out of the fells that morning with Greywater Bridge in sight, and I remember thinking, with the doomed optimism that has characterized every worst decision of my life, *we might actually make it.*

The bridge was old — older than Thornfell, older than the kingdom, the kind of grey stone that the river had been politely arguing with for a thousand years and losing. Three arches, moss-slick, wide enough for a cart. On the far bank, the road climbed toward the low country and, eventually, the Hollow Vault and whatever waited for us there.

On the near bank, waiting with the patience of stone itself, were six riders.

I knew them before I made out their faces. I knew them by the way my own body betrayed me — the sweat that started cold at my hairline, the way my hand found my sword hilt without my permission. Oath-sworn. The Regent's hounds. And one of them, the tall one in the lead with the silver collar glinting at his throat, I knew *by name.*

Ser Maren Holt. We'd been pages together, once. He'd held my hair back the night I drank too much at my own knighting feast. Now he sat a grey mare on the Greywater road with a warrant in his saddlebag that had my description on it, and Beatrix's, and probably a reward figure generous enough to make honest men of us both.

"Aldric." Beatrix had gone very still beside me, in the way she did when her sword was talking to her. I'd learned to read it: her head tilting a fraction, her lips pressing, like a woman listening to gossip she disapproves of. "How many do you count?"

"Six. The tall one is Maren Holt. He was at my knighting."

"Friends?"

"We were."

"And now?"

"Now he'll do whatever the collar tells him to do, and be sorry about it after, and do it anyway." I heard my own voice come out flat and bitter and recognized, with a small internal flinch, that I was describing myself. "There's no way across but the bridge. There's no way around but three days north through the high fells, and the high fells in this season will kill us faster than they will."

"Then we cross the bridge," she said, as if we were discussing the weather. "They can't all fit abreast. Narrow ground is equal ground."

"Six against two is equal ground to you?"

"I've seen your swordwork, Ser Aldric." The *Ser* was a knife, gently turned. She still used the honorific sometimes, and I still couldn't tell whether it was mockery, courtesy, or a reminder that I'd forfeited it. "Equal enough."

* * *

We walked down to the bridge in the open, because running would have told them everything. Beatrix had her hood back and her sword slung casual at her hip, a smuggler's blade, cheap steel, and she wore it the way she wore everything — like a woman who had decided in advance not to be impressed by the world.

Maren Holt didn't draw when we reached the near end of the span. He just sat his mare and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read, and that frightened me more than a drawn blade would have. Men who hate you are predictable. Men who are *sorry* are not.

"Ser Aldric Vane," he said. "Stripped of rank, wanted for treason, and walking the Greywater road with a swordswoman whose face is on a warrant in my saddlebag." He said it loudly, for the benefit of his riders. Then, quieter, just for me: "Walk away, Aldric. Take the north road. I'll report you crossed at dusk, headed east. I can lose you for a week."

"You could," I said. "But you won't. Because you already swore the finding-oath, didn't you. In the king's name. In *his* name." I didn't say the Regent's name aloud. On the bridge, with the water talking below us, it felt unwise.

Maren's jaw tightened. The silver collar at his throat seemed, for a moment, to sit differently — heavier, tighter, the way a collar does when the throat beneath it swells. I had seen that look on men before, in the last year of the king's decline. The oaths didn't just bind them. They *listened.*

"You don't know what it's like," Maren said, and his voice had gone strange, layered, like two men speaking in unison and disagreeing about the words. "You think you know. You stood in the hall and you *doubted*, and doubt is a luxury, Aldric. Doubt is what they take last."

"I know exactly what it's like," I said. "That's rather the point of me."

And Beatrix — because she was Beatrix, because she had never in my acquaintance done the prudent thing when an interesting thing was available — stepped forward and said, "We're crossing the bridge."

"Beatrix," I murmured.

"Six of them. Two of us. One bridge." She drew her sword, and I heard it — gods help me, I *heard* it — a thin, bright note, like a wet finger on a glass rim, like a voice clearing its throat. Maren's riders heard it too. Horses are sensible creatures; three of them immediately tried to leave.

"The swordspeaker," Maren said softly. "The Regent wants her alive."

"Well," said Beatrix, "the Regent can want."

It happened fast, the way it always does. The first rider came across the bridge at a canter, lance couched, and Beatrix didn't dodge — she *stepped in*, inside the arc, and put her cheap steel through the gap under his arm, and the man came off his horse like laundry off a line. The second and third came together, and I took the third — a boy, barely bearded, swinging wild with more fear than skill — and the fourth and fifth tried to flank along the bridge rail, and the stone was slick and one of them went into the water with a scream that the river swallowed whole.

And Maren Holt came for me.

Not for Beatrix. For *me*, and I understood why a heartbeat too late: the Regent's orders were to take the swordspeaker alive, and the Regent — whatever ancient hungry thing wore the king's face — had a particular appetite for men who had once sworn and then *doubted*. I was a delicacy. Beatrix was the prize. Maren was sent for the appetizer.

We fought on the bridge crown, and he was good — he had always been good, patient, economical, a swordsman who treated combat like bookkeeping — and I was tired and guilty and carrying three days of unsaid confession like a stone in my chest, and none of that matters in a fight except that all of it matters, because a sword is an honest instrument and it knows exactly what its wielder is hiding.

He feinted high. I knew it was a feint. I have known Maren's high feint for thirty years; he has never once in his life committed to a high line. I knew it, and I still half-fell for it, because some part of me — the part that had held his hair back, the part that remembered him laughing — did not entirely believe he meant to kill me.

He meant to kill me.

And then, in the space between one breath and the next, Beatrix was there — not fighting, not watching, just *there*, stepping into the line of Maren's blade with the absolute confidence of a woman whose sword had told her something I couldn't hear — and I did the arithmetic of the moment in the time it takes to blink, and the answer was unacceptable.

So I stepped between them.

I would like to tell you it was a grand decision, a knight's choice, a moment of shining resolve. It wasn't. It was the single most instinctive thing I have ever done, faster than thought, faster than guilt, faster than the thirty years of careful self-preservation I had built between myself and every noble impulse. My body simply declined, this once, to be a coward.

Maren's blade took me under the ribs, on the left side, angling up.

The world went very quiet and very bright. I remember the smell of the river. I remember Beatrix screaming my name — my *name*, not "Vane," not "Ser Aldric," just *Aldric*, torn out of her like something she hadn't meant to say. I remember Maren's face above me, horror-struck, his sword suddenly a foreign object in his hand, and the silver collar at his throat glowing faintly, *feeding*, drinking something out of the air — and I understood, dimly, through the roar in my ears, that the Regent fed on oaths, and that Maren had just sworn something in the moment of the strike, some vow of obedience or victory, and the wound in my side was the *interest payment.*

Then Beatrix killed Maren Holt.

I won't describe it. Not because it was terrible — though it was — but because it was *grief*, and grief is not mine to narrate. She had known him for six minutes and she killed him with the efficiency of a woman closing a door on a room she can never enter again, and afterward she stood over his body for exactly three breaths, and then she was on her knees beside me with her hands pressing my wound and her voice saying words I couldn't parse.

The remaining riders fled. I learned this later. At the time, the world had narrowed to a red heat under my ribs and a woman's hands and the grey sky, and I was dying, and dying men are famously honest.

* * *

"Aldric. *Aldric.* Stay with me. Eyes open."

"Rude," I said, "to give orders. To a dying man. Who outranks you."

"You don't outrank anyone. You're a disgraced knight lying in the mud." Her hands were doing things to my side that hurt in a very encouraging way, which I understood meant she was good at this. Smugglers stitch their people; it's the first rule of the trade. "The blade missed anything vital by a finger's width. You're going to live, which means you're going to have to listen to me be angry at you for a very long time. Congratulations."

"Beatrix." The river was loud. The sky was very grey and very far away. "I have to tell you something. While I still have the — the courage. Courage evaporates. It's like morning frost."

"Save it."

"Can't." I laughed, and it hurt, and the hurt kept me anchored. "If I save it I'll spend it on something cowardly instead. Beatrix. Seven years ago. Before the purge. I was still — I was still *his*. The king's. I had my spurs, my oath, the whole gilded harness. And the Regent — whatever it was then, it was smaller then, it was still learning his face — it came to me. Wearing him. Wearing *Edric's* face, with Edric's voice, and it asked me where the Order of the Quill and Blade kept their sanctuary."

I felt her go still. Not the listening-stillness of the sword. A different stillness. Colder.

"I didn't know what it was," I said. "I swear that — no. No, I don't swear it. That's the whole point. I *won't* swear it, because swearing is what got us here." My voice was coming apart and I let it. "It had the king's face, Beatrix. It had the king's *eyes*, and it said, *Ser Aldric, your king commands you*, and I was thirty-two years old and I had been kneeling to that face since I was nine, and I told it. I told it where the sanctuary was. The valley. The watch-signals. Everything. And six weeks later your order burned, and I told myself it wasn't me, I told myself the Regent would have found them anyway, I told myself every lie a guilty man tells himself at three in the morning for seven years running—"

"Stop." Her voice was very quiet.

"—and then I met you," I went on, because dying men are not famous for their timing either, "and you were *her*, you were the order's last daughter walking beside me and trusting me, and I couldn't — I couldn't carry it anymore, Beatrix, I can't—"

"*Stop.*" Her hand came up and pressed flat against my chest, over the sternum, above the wound. Not gentle. Firm. The hand of a woman holding a door shut. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to confess and die and make it clean. Do you hear me? Dying is *easy*, Aldric. Dying is the coward's version of accountability. You live. You live and you carry it in front of me, in daylight, where I can see the weight of it, and *then* we'll talk about what I forgive."

The grey sky blurred. I think that was rain. I have decided it was rain.

"That," I said, when I could speak, "is the cruelest mercy anyone has ever offered me."

"I'm told I have a gift." Her hands went back to the wound. I felt the bite of a needle, the pull of thread, and I gripped a fistful of bridge moss and did not scream, mostly out of pride. "You took a blade meant for me. I want to be very clear that I'm furious about that. I had it handled."

"You stepped into a killing line because a sword whispered to you."

"My sword said the strike was coming."

"And you trusted it enough to stand in front of it. And I trusted *you* enough to stand in front of *that*." I laughed again, weaker. "There's a lesson in there about trust, but I'm too busy bleeding to find it."

She worked in silence for a while. The river ran. Somewhere on the far bank, one of the riderless horses was grazing with the magnificent indifference of its kind. She stitched me closed — eleven stitches, I counted, because counting was better than fainting — and packed the wound with the bitter green mash of some fell-herb I didn't recognize, and bound it, and then sat back on her heels and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read, and that frightened me more than Maren's unreadable face ever had.

"Why?" she asked. "The bridge. Why did you take it? You didn't know my sword would warn me. By your own account you've spent seven years being careful. Careful men don't catch blades."

I looked up at her against the grey sky, and the honest answer was there in my chest, fully formed, and for once in my life I didn't dress it in anything.

"Because you were in the way of it," I said. "That's all. There wasn't a decision. You were in the way of the blade, and I was in the way of you, and apparently that's the only oath I have left that still means anything. I can't swear it — swearing is ruined for me, you understand, the whole institution — but it's true anyway. Unsworn. Freely kept." I paused. "Is that worth anything? An oath nobody asked for?"

Beatrix Yarrow looked at me for a long moment. The rain, which I am insisting was rain, had gotten into her hair. She looked tired and fierce and younger than her years and older than the fells, all at once, and she said:

"It's worth more than anything I've heard sworn in seven years. Which is not the same as saying I forgive you." She stood, and offered me her arm, and after a moment's dignity I took it. "Forgiveness is a road, not a door. Walk it with me."

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow above the river, because I could not walk farther and she would not leave me. She built a fire small enough to hide and sat across from it sharpening her cheap steel, and the blade made a sound under the whetstone that was almost — almost — like humming.

"The map," she said, without looking up. "The courier's map. I've been reading the marginalia. There's a road on it that isn't a road. It goes under the fells. Through the Underfell."

I knew the name. Every child in Thornfell knows the name, the way children know the names of the things under beds. "No one crosses the Underfell."

"No one crosses it *talking*," she said. "In the Underfell, an oath spoken aloud becomes law. Binding law. The stone itself enforces it." She looked up at me across the fire, and her eyes were very steady. "Three days. Total silence. You and me and no words at all — which means no confessions, no arguments, no apologies, and no lies. Just two people who know the worst about each other, walking in the dark, trusting each other anyway."

The fire cracked. My side throbbed in time with my heart, eleven stitches' worth of a lesson I hadn't finished learning.

"When do we leave?" I said.
