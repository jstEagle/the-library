## 11. The Map's True Road

The courier's map had been lying to us for six days.

Not lying exactly. Maps from the crown's cartographers never lie outright—they just tell you the truth in a language designed to be misunderstood by anyone who isn't already dead. Rivers that didn't exist. A chapel marked where there was only a burned foundation and a very territorial goat. I'd spent the better part of a week tracing our route north with a charcoal stub, muttering corrections, while Aldric sat across the fire sharpening a sword he no longer had the rank to carry and pretending not to watch me do it.

He was bad at pretending. For a man who'd survived seven years of court intrigue and one spectacular act of treason, Ser Aldric Vane had the poker face of a startled deer.

"You're frowning at it again," he said.

"I'm reading it."

"You're frowning *at* it. There's a difference. Reading involves turning pages."

"It's a map. Maps don't have pages."

"Then what's that corner you keep folding back?"

I folded the corner back again. It was the third time I'd done it without thinking, and the fourth time I noticed myself doing it, which is the kind of arithmetic that keeps a smuggler up at night. The courier's map was good vellum, soft as skin, and along its bottom margin—half-hidden in the fold, rendered so faintly it might have been water damage or might have been deliberate—ran a line of notation in a hand too small and too careful to be the cartographer's.

Marginalia. The dying man's own additions. I'd been staring past them for days because they were written in Oathscript.

* * *

Here is the thing about Oathscript that most people never learn, because most people have the good fortune never to need it: it isn't an alphabet. It's a grammar of binding. Every character is a vow compressed into ink—a promise folded small enough to carry. Swordspeakers learn it first, before we ever learn to listen to steel, because a blade remembers what was sworn over it and Oathscript is how oaths are made portable. My order used to write contracts in it. Marriage vows. Treaties. Last words, sometimes, pressed into a dying hand so the family could hold them afterward.

The crown burned every text on it seven years ago. Which meant the only people alive who could read the courier's notes were me—and possibly whatever passed for scholars in the Regent's service, which was a thought I chose not to finish.

I waited until Aldric was asleep. Not because I didn't trust him. Because I wanted to read it wrong first, alone, where being wrong cost nothing.

The fire had gone down to coals. I held the map close and traced the marginalia with my fingertip, sounding out each glyph the way my mentor Maren once sounded them out for me, her breath smelling of pipe smoke and impatience. *Slowly, Yarrow. An oath misread is an oath remade.*

The first line was simple. Coordinates, more or less—the courier had corrected the map's fiction with his own truth. Our route didn't run through Greywater Pass as the vellum claimed. It ran beneath it.

The second line took longer, because my hands started shaking somewhere around the fourth glyph and I had to set the map down and breathe like a person who wasn't seven years old inside.

*The road is the Underfell. Speak nothing aloud within it. What is said there is law.*

* * *

I woke Aldric by dropping the map on his chest, which felt appropriately dramatic for the occasion.

He came awake the way soldiers do—all at once, hand finding the hilt before his eyes found the light. Then he saw it was me and the map and not, say, an assassin, and some of the tension went out of him. Some. He never fully relaxed anymore. I was starting to suspect neither of us remembered how.

"The Underfell," I said.

He sat up. "You're certain?"

"I can read it." I crouched beside him and turned the map so the firelight caught the margin. "This line here—it's a route correction. And this—" I tapped the second line, the glyphs blurring slightly because my eyes were doing something embarrassing and I refused to call it tears, "—this is a warning. Written by a man who knew he was dying and wanted whoever found this to understand the last leg of the journey."

Aldric read it twice. His lips moved. "Underfell," he said again, and the word came out of him the way words come out of people who've heard stories about a place their whole lives and hoped never to verify any of them. "The living labyrinth. Under Greywater."

"Beneath the whole northern range, if the old accounts are true. It's not carved, Aldric. It grew. Roots and stone and something older than either, all knotted together into passages. The dwarves wouldn't mine it. The first kings wouldn't build near it. There are stories—"

"I know the stories." He rubbed his face. "My grandfather swore he lost a brother in it. Said the fell *took* him. Everyone assumed grief had addled the old man."

"Maybe. Maybe not." I sat back on my heels. "But here's the part that matters. Here's why a courier who was running from the Regent's hunters chose to die carrying this instead of burning it." I tapped the warning line a third time. "*What is said there is law.* In the Underfell, an oath spoken aloud doesn't need witnesses. Doesn't need ceremony. Doesn't need a swordspeaker or a witness-stone or a priest with clean hands. You say it, and the fell holds you to it. Permanently."

"That's superstition."

"So is everything else we've believed since the barn," I said. "So is a king who speaks in two voices. So is a parasite made of other people's promises. We are well past the point in this journey where 'superstition' means 'untrue.' It now means 'true in a way I don't enjoy.'"

He almost smiled. Almost. "Three days," he said. "Through the deep passages. Without speaking."

"Not a word. Not a cough you mean anything by. Not a prayer." I looked at him steadily. "Especially not a prayer. Can you do that? Three days of silence with me?"

Something crossed his face then—something quick and complicated that I filed away under *later*, because the alternative was examining it by firelight, and I had learned the hard way what happened when I examined things by firelight.

"I spent three years holding my tongue at court," he said. "While my king stopped being my king. While men I trained were sent to do things I'll not describe. I can manage three days."

"That's what worries me," I said quietly. "People who've practiced silence that long usually have a lot stored up."

* * *

We spent two days preparing, which mostly meant arguing in increasingly elaborate mime.

It began as practicality—we couldn't risk speaking of the Underfell near roads, and the hunters had been a half-day behind us since Greywater Bridge—but it curdled quickly into habit, and then into something I didn't have a name for. By the second evening Aldric and I had stopped talking entirely, not out of necessity but out of practice, and I discovered that a man communicates a great deal when you take his words away. He communicated that he thought my plan for rope anchors was reckless by raising one eyebrow with surgical precision. He communicated that he'd noticed I hadn't been sleeping by leaving an extra strip of dried venison by my bedroll and looking pointedly elsewhere while I ate it. He communicated, once, at dusk, standing at the treeline watching the northern peaks go grey and gold, something that had no translation at all—he just looked at me, and then away, and the space between those two glances was louder than anything either of us had said aloud in weeks.

I hated how much of it I understood. I hated more how much I wanted him to keep saying it.

On the second night I did something I hadn't done since the purge. I drew my sword—not to fight, not to check the edge, but to talk.

She came awake slowly, the way she always did, a hum through the grip like a cat deciding whether to be affectionate. She remembered the bridge. She remembered screaming with every life I'd taken there, broadcasting our position to anyone with ears tuned to steel. She was sorry about that. Blades feel guilt the way rivers feel banks—it shapes them without stopping them.

*I know,* I told her, in the way that isn't language. *No more of that if I can help it.*

She showed me a memory instead of answering: the courier's hands, wax-stained, pressing the map against my palm. And beneath that, older, a thread of something she'd carried since the day I bought her cheap from a fence who didn't know what she was—Maren's voice, saying the last thing she ever said to me.

*Never speak for a crown again, Yarrow. Promise me.*

I had promised. That promise lived in the blade like a splinter lives in a thumb—small, deep, aching whenever the weather changed. And now I was walking toward a place where spoken promises became stone law, carrying a promise I could never take back, toward a vault where I would presumably have to break a different one.

The sword hummed something low and unresolved. Even she didn't know how that arithmetic would come out.

* * *

The entrance to the Underfell was not an entrance. That was the first lesson.

What it was, was a sinkhole in a birch stand two miles east of where the map lied about a chapel, ringed with stones that had been arranged—arranged, not placed—in patterns that predated every kingdom whose coins I'd ever forged a signature on. The descent was a throat of root and rock, breathing cold air upward, and the moment we stood at its lip I felt my sword wake fully against my hip, humming like a struck bell that couldn't decide on a note.

"She feels it," I mouthed to Aldric.

He nodded. He'd gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with the climb.

Before we went down, we made our preparations in silence, and the silence itself became a ritual neither of us had planned. He checked my harness straps without asking; I checked his. We divided the food by weight, by look, by a system of gestures we'd invented over two days and now handled fluently. When he tied the dark-lantern to his belt, I touched his wrist—two fingers, brief—to say *ready*. He covered my hand with his for a count of three, which said something I decided not to translate, and then we went down into the dark together.

The Underfell swallowed sound the way a swamp swallows light.

That should have been comforting. It wasn't. Silence imposed from outside is a wall; silence you've agreed to is a mirror, and in the mirror there is nowhere to hide from the sound of your own breathing, your own heartbeat, your own thoughts—which, it turns out, are much less disciplined than my mouth has ever been. Three days of walking through a living dark, forbidden to speak, leaves you alone with every sentence you've never finished.

Hour by hour, the fell grew stranger. The passages weren't tunnels so much as decisions—roots grown thick as bridges arching overhead, stone flowing around them like frozen water, walls veined with something faintly luminous that pulsed slow as sleeping breath. Once, in a wide chamber, I put my palm flat against the rock and felt it—felt *it*, the fell itself—pressing gently back. Testing. Tasting whatever I was.

And I understood, with a lurch, why the courier's warning mattered. This place didn't enforce oaths the way a court does, with clerks and silver collars and men with hooks. It enforced them the way a body enforces a wound. Words spoken here didn't vanish into air. They went *in*.

Somewhere behind us—I told myself it was far behind us—water dripped in a rhythm almost like speech.

* * *

We slept in shifts, back to back, because the dark was total and trust needed a shape. His shoulder blades against mine were warm and steady, and I learned the exact weight of him leaning into sleep, and he learned mine, and neither of us said a word, and it was the closest I had been to another human being in seven years.

In the middle of the second night—or what I guessed was night; the fell kept its own hours—I woke to find him already awake, sitting up, staring into the black with the lantern hooded. I touched his arm. *What?*

He took my hand and pressed it flat against the ground.

The floor was trembling. Faintly. Rhythmically. Not footsteps—footsteps I knew. This was deeper, slower, a pulse rising up through the roots like a voice trying to remember its own words.

And my sword, silent for two days, began—very softly—to sing.

Not the scream of Greywater Bridge. Something else. A melody, thin and clear, threading up out of the steel like steam. And though no one spoke, though the rule of the fell pressed down on us like a thumb, I knew what the song was, because blades remember oaths, and this was an oath being sung *back*—old, enormous, worn smooth by ages of repetition.

Someone had sworn something down here. Long ago. Or long ago and still ongoing.

Aldric's hand found my forearm in the dark. His grip said: *do you hear it.* My nod said: *yes, and I wish I didn't.* His face, barely visible in the lantern's single sliver of light, said the rest—the thing he'd been storing up through three years of court silence and two days of ours, the thing with no gesture and no translation.

Then the singing stopped.

All at once, mid-phrase, like a throat closed by a hand. And in the ringing quiet that followed, from the passage ahead of us—from the direction we had to go—came a sound that froze the blood in my smuggling, oath-breaking, swordspeaking heart.

Footsteps. Unhurried. Deliberate.

Coming toward us through a labyrinth where every word spoken aloud became law, someone was walking. And whistling.
