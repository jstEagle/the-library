## 12. Silence in the Underfell

The whistling stopped three turns of the passage before we saw him, which is how I learned that sound in the Underfell travels farther than light ever will.

We had done what sensible people do when something approaches through a corridor where every spoken word becomes law: we pressed ourselves into a niche behind a shelf of flowstone and went still. Aldric's hand found my shoulder in the dark—not a grip, just a touch, *here, I'm here*—and I felt his heartbeat through his palm like a second pulse trying to keep time with mine.

The man who rounded the corner carried a lantern shuttered down to a coin-sized slit of gold. He was young, broad-shouldered, dressed in the road-worn leathers of a fell shepherd, and he was crying.

That was the detail that undid me. Hunters don't cry. Smugglers don't cry. But a boy lost three days underground with no voice and less hope—that boy cries, quietly, into his sleeve, because he can't even sob aloud without the walls taking note of it.

He saw our light—or Aldric's, since mine stayed shuttered—and froze. Then he did the most eloquent thing I'd seen in seven years of reading people by lamplight.

He pointed at his own mouth, then shook his head. Then he held up three fingers, and pointed down the passage behind him, and made a shape in the air with both hands: *mountains*. Then he pointed forward, deeper, and shrugged with his whole body, a gesture that said plainer than any oath: *I am lost, I have nothing, take me or leave me.*

Aldric looked at me. In the dark I couldn't see his face, but I knew the question anyway. *Do we take him?*

Here is what you must understand about silence. It is not empty. Three days of it is not an absence but a pressure, like deep water, and every hour it presses harder against whatever you've been holding down. For two days I had been alone inside my own skull with Aldric Vane, communicating in finger-marks and firefly-lantern flashes, and I had discovered something terrible: without words, there was nowhere left to hide. Every hesitation showed. Every kindness showed worse.

And so when the boy gestured his wordless plea, I heard myself thinking, *Let him come. Three voices will make this easier.*

That should have been my first warning. Nothing about this journey was supposed to be easier.

I stepped out of the niche and gave him the smuggler's sign: two fingers up, curled inward. *Come. Quietly.* His face broke open with relief so naked that I felt a fool for ever doubting him.

I would spend a great deal of the next day learning exactly what kind of fool.

* * *

His name—if a name can be given without speaking, scrawled in charcoal on a slate he produced from his pack—was Tam. He wrote small and neat, a clerk's hand, which fit poorly with a shepherd's shoulders, but the fells are full of sons who fled their fathers' trade, and I told myself that too.

He was useful. That was the worst of it. He had a climber's balance and a miner's patience with rubble, and twice he found us passages around collapses that would have cost us half a day. When we camped—shuttered lanterns, cold food, handsigns only—he kept watch without being asked, sitting upright against the wall with his slate across his knees.

It was during the second night's watch that I noticed the first wrong thing, and even then I didn't understand it. I had taken my sword across my knees, as I always did, running my thumb along the fuller in the old habit of listening without listening. A swordspeaker's blade hums when oaths are near—it remembers them the way scar tissue remembers weather. My sword had been quiet for two days, as blades are in the deep places, except for one faint thread of song that came whenever Aldric shifted in his sleep. His broken knighthood still rang in him, faint and mournful, a bell with a crack in it. I had learned to find it almost comforting.

But when my thumb passed over the steel while Tam sat watch, the hum bent. Skipped. Like a singer losing the note.

I told myself it was the cold. Stone drinks warmth out of everything, including magic, and I was tired, and tired people hear what they're afraid of.

Then, near dawn, Tam wrote on his slate and turned it toward me by the light of the banked coals:

*How much farther to the Vault road?*

Which was a reasonable question. Except that Aldric and I had never written down where we were going. Not once. Not in charcoal, not in signs, not in anything. We traveled the courier's map by memory and touch, because paper can be stolen and gestures can be read, but a thing never said exists only in two heads.

I looked at the slate a long time. Then I wrote back: *Why do you ask?*

Tam smiled—a warm, tired, grateful smile—and wiped the slate clean with his sleeve, and pointed ahead, and shrugged again, the same whole-body shrug as before: *Just wondering. Doesn't matter.*

Except that now it mattered enormously, and we both knew it, and he had answered a question I hadn't asked, which meant he had been asking questions of his own. Questions he couldn't risk writing down twice.

I sat very still and watched him fold the slate away, and I thought about the Regent's hunters, sworn body and breath to the crown's hunger, and I thought about the one thing the scholar in the burned library had told us that I hadn't wanted to hear:

*"The Regent cannot make new oaths. It can only spend old ones. Every vow ever sworn in Thornfell's name is a coin in its purse—and coins, Beatrix Yarrow, can be spent on faces."*

Stolen vows. Worn like clothes.

I looked at Tam's sleeping roll, empty because he was on watch, and at the shoulders that didn't match a clerk's hand, and at the boy who cried so beautifully into his sleeve where we couldn't see whether tears were real, and I understood that the whistling we'd heard on the first day had never been a hunter walking toward us.

It had been bait, singing itself to sleep until we swallowed it.

* * *

You cannot accuse a man in the Underfell. Accusation requires words, and words require breath, and breath in those corridors becomes law. If I signed *you are a liar* to Tam and I was wrong, I might bind us all to some nonsense the stone decided to enforce. If I was right, I'd tell him exactly how much we knew, and a hunter with a hunter's speed needs no words to kill two sleeping travelers.

So we did what smugglers do. We set a trap out of nothing at all.

At the next rest, Aldric and I staged it in finger-signs while Tam scouted ahead around a bend—close enough to return quickly, far enough to be briefly out of sight. Aldric wrote on his slate, big and careless, letting me see: *Vault entrance is past the Third Silence. Half a day more. Then we go our separate ways—he heads north to the shepherd roads.*

Then he erased it. And we waited.

Because here was the thing about a lie spoken—even written—in the Underfell: it becomes law. If Tam was what he seemed, he'd never have seen the slate. But if Tam was wearing another man's vows, if he was the Regent's coin spent on a face, then somewhere in the last two days he had already tried to read our route off us, and failed, and he was getting desperate. Desperate men watch slates.

He came back around the bend forty heartbeats after Aldric wiped it clean. And his eyes went—to the slate. To the place where it lay. Just once. Just a flicker, a hunter's habit, the glance of a man checking whether a door is locked.

Then he smiled his warm, tired smile and signed: *Clear ahead.*

I have faced the purge of my order. I have held a dying stranger while the life ran out of him. I have killed men since, and heard my blade scream with it. None of it curdled my stomach quite like that smile, worn over a stranger's face, patient as a spider.

* * *

The unmasking, when it came, took less time than any of my planning had.

We walked on. Another mile, another turn, into a gallery where the ceiling opened into a hollow dome and pale mineral light bled down from some distant fissure—enough light, finally, to see each other's faces properly. Enough light for what came next.

I stopped. Turned to Tam. And drew my sword.

Not against him—I let it hang loose, point-down, a swordspeaker's oldest tool. Because there was one test the Regent's stolen vows could not beat, one truth no borrowed face could fake, and I had been carrying it at my hip for seven years.

My sword sings for oaths. Real ones. Sworn ones. The vow that made Tam's body move—the hunter's oath, sworn in the king's name, the vow the Regent was spending through his skin—that vow lived in him like a coal lives in ash. All I had to do was listen.

"Sing," I whispered—sound enough to wake the steel, small enough that the stone took it for nothing, a wish, a prayer, not law.

The blade woke.

And oh, it sang. First Aldric's cracked bell, faithful as grief. Then mine, low, the old oath I never speak of, humming under the floor of the note. And then—

Then it reached Tam, and stopped.

Not faded. Not bent, as it had bent before, uncertain. It *refused*. The way a throat refuses a poison. The way a witness refuses to swear to a lie. The steel went silent in my hand with a flatness that has no honest name, a hole in the music shaped exactly like a man, and I knew.

"You're not Tam," I said aloud, and the dome took my voice and made it true, made it law, made it ring from every wall: *not Tam, not Tam, not Tam.*

The boy's face changed. Not melted, not shifted—the Regent's disguises are cheaper than that, a posture and a story and stolen vows doing the work—but changed the way a house changes when you learn what happened in it. The warmth drained out. The shoulders settled differently. What looked back at me through the shepherd's eyes was older, and hungrier, and terribly calm.

The slate came up in his hand, and he wrote fast, and held it toward us with that same maddening courtesy:

*SHE ONLY NEEDS ONE OF YOU TO SPEAK THE ROAD ALOUD. GIVE ME THE MAP'S WORDS AND WALK AWAY. SHE KNOWS YOU'RE DOWN HERE EITHER WAY.*

The Regent. Writing to me. Bargaining in charcoal in the dark like a merchant over a bale of wool.

Aldric moved first. Of course he did—he was a knight once, and knights are at their most dangerous precisely when they've got nothing left to be knights *for*. He went for Tam's knife-hand, and I went low, and the hunter went through the middle of us both like water finding a crack.

The fight in that dome lasted eleven seconds. I counted afterward, the way you do, because survival insists on bookkeeping. He was faster than either of us alone and weaker than both of us together, and he knew it, and he fought not to win but to reach the tunnel mouth—which told me everything about how many more of him might be waiting behind us, and how little the Regent needed this particular coin back.

I caught him at the gallery's edge. My sword took him through the chest, and my blade did not scream this time. It sang—one clear, rising note, like a struck glass—because the thing I had killed wore stolen vows, and stolen things, it turns out, belong to no one, and cutting them loose is not murder. It is repossession.

He slid down the flowstone with the slate still in his fingers. The last thing he wrote, before the light went out of the borrowed face entirely, in that same small neat clerk's hand:

*THE QUEEN'S MARK ON YOUR WRIST IS HER EAR. SHE HEARD ALL OF THIS. THE DEBT IS DUE.*

I stared at my wrist. At the thin gray line the smuggler queen had pressed into my skin at the ferry landing, the token of passage, the favor owed in blood—*any blood, any time,* she'd said, smiling like a knife learning manners. A mark I had assumed was ink and spite.

It had been listening. All this way. Through the dark and the silence and the three-day walk where I had said almost nothing aloud—except, at the last, four words.

*You're not Tam.*

Four words, carried back up through fifty miles of stone to a woman who collected secrets the way other people collected debts.

Aldric pulled the slate gently from the dead man's hand and read it, and then read it again, and looked at me with an expression I was beginning to know too well: the look of a man watching the bill arrive for a meal someone else ordered.

"The debt," he said. "She's calling it in. Now. Down here."

And from the tunnel ahead—from the direction of the Hollow Vault, from the direction we had sold half our lives to reach—came light. Lantern-light, many lanterns, and the smell of pitch and river-water, and a voice I recognized the way you recognize a scar: warm, amused, absolutely patient.

"Well," called the smuggler queen, from the dark at the heart of the world. "Don't stop on my account, Yarrow. You're nearly there. And I do hate collecting in front of an audience."

She stepped into the mineral glow, and behind her, filing silently out of the tunnels, came thirty of her people—and every one of them wore a gray mark on the wrist.
