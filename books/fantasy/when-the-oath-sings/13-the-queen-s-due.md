## 13. The Queen's Due

The smuggler queen kept her court in a place that had no right to exist: a hollowed-out watchtower on the spine of the Grey Fells, half-swallowed by ivy and older grudges, its top floor open to the sky like a mouth missing teeth. She called it the Parlor. Everyone else called it nothing at all, because naming it out loud was apparently a good way to end up as one of the decorative skeletons along the approach road.

I'd been counting those skeletons on the way up. Eleven. I'd decided not to ask.

"Yarrow." Maren Coalhewer sat on a throne that was, I swear on every dead god, an actual ship's wheel mounted on a chair frame. She was small, silver-haired, and built like a woman who had personally argued with a mountain and won. Rings on every finger except her thumbs, which she kept bare for reasons I suspected I didn't want explained. "You look like something the fells chewed up and spat back with opinions."

"That's accurate," I said.

Aldric stood at my shoulder doing his best impression of a man who wasn't a disgraced knight standing in a smuggler fortress. He was bad at it. Disgrace clings to some men like pipe smoke.

Maren's eyes—pale, quick, the color of river ice—went from me to him and back. "And you brought the Vane dog. The one who used to be worth something."

"He carries things," I said. "Heavy things. It's his whole personality now."

That got a laugh out of her, which I noted with the particular relief of a woman who has just realized she is not currently being weighed for skeleton duty. Around us, her court went about its business: coin counted, knives sharpened, a man in the corner being very carefully not tortured while everyone pretended he was just resting. Smuggler hospitality. You learn to read the room or the room reads you.

"You have my protection," Maren said. "Three days' hard ride through territory where my word is law and the Regent's hunters are merely suggestions. Food, horses, silence about your faces at every toll between here and the Vault road." She leaned forward, and the ship's wheel creaked under her grip. "And in exchange?"

"In exchange," I said carefully, "we owe you a favor."

"A favor," she repeated, tasting the word like wine she suspected was watered. "Yes. That's what my people told me you agreed to. A favor owed in blood, was the phrasing."

"It was colorful phrasing."

"It was *my* phrasing." She smiled. It did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "I've been in this trade forty years, Yarrow. Do you know what I've learned about favors? They're like debts. Everyone agrees to them when they're far away and comfortable, and nobody wants to pay when they come due. So I don't deal in favors anymore. I deal in specifics." She snapped her fingers. "Sit down. Both of you. What I want is simple."

Nothing a woman like Maren Coalhewer calls simple is ever simple. I sat anyway. Aldric lowered himself onto a stool that looked like it had opinions about him.

"I want the fells free," Maren said.

I blinked. "Free of what? The Regent doesn't—"

"The Regent doesn't care about us, no. That's the problem." She stood, pacing now, rings clicking against the wheel-spokes as she passed. "Every treaty, every charter, every border agreement between Thornfell and the lowland lords—it all runs through the crown. And the crown, as you two know better than anyone, is a puppet with something ancient wearing its hand. Which means every promise made in the king's name is a promise made to *it*. Every oath sworn over fells-territory feeds the thing. My grandmother swore loyalty to a Thornfell king. My mother renewed it. I've spent my whole life watching oaths pile up around my people like chains around a well, and every year the well gets deeper."

She stopped pacing. Turned. Looked at me the way a butcher looks at a carcass she's already priced.

"And then I hear rumors. A swordspeaker. Alive. Walking my roads with a blade that remembers oaths." Her voice dropped into something soft and terrible. "Do you know what you are, girl? You're not a smuggler. You're not even a soldier. You're a *notary*. The last one in Thornfell. And I am asking my notary to write me a contract."

The word landed in my stomach like a stone dropped down that well.

"What kind of contract?" Aldric asked. His voice was careful. He'd already guessed. I could see it in the set of his jaw—he'd guessed and he hated the answer.

"The kind that holds," Maren said. "The kind that doesn't need clerks or crowns or wax seals. The kind written into the bones of the world itself. You speak it, swordspeaker. You bind it. An accord, sworn before witnesses, that the Grey Fells answer to no throne—not Thornfell's, not any that comes after. Unbreakable. Eternal. Independent forever." She spread her hands, and for one moment she looked less like a butcher and more like a woman who had carried something heavy for three generations. "One oath from you, and my grandchildren never kneel."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about power offered freely: it arrives dressed as a gift, and it takes you three full breaths to notice the price tag sewn into the lining.

My first breath was pure temptation. I'll admit it. Three generations of a people chained by paperwork and ceremony, and I could cut the chain with a sentence. That's not nothing. That's the kind of offer that keeps you warm at night, the kind you tell yourself stories about later—I *could have*, I *almost did*, imagine what I might have built—

My second breath was colder. Because I knew what oaths cost. I had watched an order of two hundred swordspeakers die for the crime of existing, and I knew—knew in my marrow, in the scar tissue where my gift lived—that oaths spoken into blades don't sit politely in one place. They echo. They bind both ends. If I forged an eternal accord into the fells, I wouldn't be writing a contract. I'd be planting a root. Something permanent, something hungry, something that would need feeding. Maybe not today. Maybe not in my lifetime. But the first oath ever sworn had grown a parasite that ate a king. Who was I to say what my oath would grow?

And my third breath was the coldest of all, because I understood what Maren was really asking. Not for freedom. For a *weapon*. She wanted an unbreakable thing, and unbreakable things don't care whose side they start on. Today the accord freed the fells. In fifty years, some granddaughter of hers would use the same binding logic to chain a valley, then a river, then a kingdom. I would not be there to see it. I would only have been the hand that taught the world it could be done.

"You want me to make a new Regent," I said. "Smaller. Local. Yours."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when someone says the true thing out loud.

Maren's face didn't change, which was itself an answer. "I want my people free."

"So do I. But freedom that has to be nailed down with an eternal oath isn't freedom. It's just a cage with better views." I stood. My legs felt far away. "You're asking me to do exactly what the crown does. Swear something so big it can't be unsaid, and let the world organize itself around the weight of it. I've seen where that road goes. I walked through the purge, Maren. I buried my teacher. I'm not going to build the next thing that buries someone's students."

"You'd refuse me." Flat. Not a question.

"Yes."

"In my own hall. After eating my bread."

"I haven't eaten anything since we arrived, actually. Your bread smells like a rumor of bread."

Someone near the wall laughed and then thought better of it. Maren didn't. She came down from the ship's wheel throne slowly, and up close she smelled of tar and iron and old smoke, and her eyes were the eyes of a woman doing arithmetic with lives.

"Understand what you're refusing," she said quietly. "Not just my protection. My *silence*. The hunters are three days behind you and closing. Without my roads, you take the low passes—the ones even I don't use. And the moment you're gone from my sight, girl, every ear in the fells hears that Beatrix Yarrow refused the Queen of the Grey Fells to her face. There are people in these hills who'd sell their mothers for the reward on your head, and now they'll know you're worth more than their mothers."

"I understand," I said.

"Do you?" She stepped closer. "Because I think you think refusing me is the noble part and the consequences are the boring part. Let me disabuse you. Consequences are the *whole* part. Nobility is just the receipt."

Aldric rose beside me. "Then we go now," he said, "and you can keep your receipt."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Then at me. And something flickered across her face—disappointment, yes, but underneath it, faintly, almost unwillingly, respect. The look of a woman who had tested a blade against stone and found, annoyingly, that the stone held.

"Greywater Bridge," she said abruptly. "If you're fools enough to take the low road, take it past Greywater Bridge before the third bell tomorrow. After that, the bridge wardens change shift, and the new shift sells information to anyone with coin—including, lately, people with too many teeth and a habit of swearing oaths in whispers." She turned away, climbing back toward her wheel. "Get out of my Parlor. And Yarrow—"

I paused at the door.

"When you get to that vault of yours," Maren said, without turning around, "and you do whatever mad thing you're planning to do—remember that I asked you first. Remember that I offered you a kingdom's worth of gratitude and you chose the hard road instead. Because if you fail, I will spend the rest of my life telling everyone I met you and found you wanting. And if you succeed—" she finally glanced back, one pale eye catching the grey light, "—if you succeed, I'll spend the rest of my life wondering why I couldn't have been brave enough to refuse a good bargain too."

We walked out. Nobody stopped us. Somehow that was worse.

* * *

The low passes were everything Maren promised and less. Narrow, wet, haunted by the sound of our own footsteps. We made camp that night in the lee of a fallen rowan, cold food, no fire, and Aldric sat sharpening a sword that didn't need sharpening, which was how he prayed.

"You did the right thing," he said eventually.

"Don't." I pulled my cloak tighter. "Don't tell me I did the right thing. Right things aren't supposed to leave you feeling like you left your coat in a burning house."

He was quiet a moment. "What would your mentor have said?"

I thought about it. Thought about Maera's hands on my shoulders, seven years and a lifetime ago, the night before the purge reached us. *Never speak for a crown again.* But it hadn't been just about crowns. It had been about the shape of the speaking—about how every great binding starts as someone's reasonable request, someone's desperate need, someone's *surely this once*.

"She'd have said I talked too much," I said. "And then she'd have said the same thing I told Maren. Just meaner."

Aldric huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then his whetstone stopped moving.

"Beatrix." His voice had changed. Lower. Careful. "There's something I should tell you. About why the hunters keep finding us. Why the roads feel wrong. Why—"

The wind shifted.

It came down off the high crags carrying a smell I'd learned to hate in the last week: wet iron and old incense, the scent of oaths freshly sworn. My sword woke against my hip, humming low and urgent, a plucked string that wouldn't settle.

Aldric was on his feet before I was, staring north along the pass, where the dark between two crags had begun, very slightly, to look occupied.

"They're not three days behind us," he said. "They never were."

Down in the valley, faint but unmistakable, someone began to whistle—a hunter's call, three notes, repeated. And beneath it, so soft I might have imagined it, my blade whispered a single word in a voice I was beginning to know too well.

*Coming.*

The debt to Maren was paid. This one was going to cost more.
