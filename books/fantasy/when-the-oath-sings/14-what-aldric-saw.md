## 14. What Aldric Saw

I have lied to a queen, forged a marshal's seal, and told a dying man his son had escaped the city when I knew he had not. None of those lies cost me what this one did—the small, daily lie of sitting across a fire from Beatrix Yarrow and letting her believe I was worth trusting.

The Underfell spat us out at moonrise on the far side, into a hanging valley where the pines grew crooked and the wind came down off the icefields smelling of nothing at all. Three days without a spoken word. Three days of watching her hands instead of her mouth, learning her language of gestures the way you learn a lover's—quickly, helplessly, with a growing dread that fluency would make everything worse. We had talked for whole hours without speaking, she and I. That was the trouble. Silence is honest in a way words never manage to be, and hers had been telling me things all day that I had no right to hear.

We made camp in the lee of a granite shelf, close enough to the treeline that the dark behind us looked solid. Serra slept by the cold fire ring, her head pillowed on the courier's pack, one hand even in sleep resting on the hilt of a knife she'd stolen from me in the fells and never returned. The boy—Tomas, who'd joined us at Greywater and not once asked why a landless knight and a smuggler's guide were walking toward the Hollow Vault like pilgrims—was on first watch, whittling something he refused to show anyone.

Which left me and Beatrix, side by side against the stone, sharing a blanket because there were only two and the mountain nights bit.

"You're going to tell me now," she said quietly. It wasn't a question. "Whatever it is you've been chewing since the ferry. You're going to tell me tonight."

"How do you know that?"

"Because your silence has an accent," she said. "It's been getting louder for two days."

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, and I suppose that was answer enough, because she didn't push. She just sat beside me in the dark and waited, the way she waited for blades to finish remembering. I have knelt before kings. I have held a bridge for six hours with a broken arm. Neither required the courage of what I did next, which was simply to open my mouth and let seven years of rot fall out.

* * *

"You know the official story," I said. "You've heard it. Ser Aldric Vane, knight of the Thornrose, stripped of his rank for doubting the king's divinely appointed wisdom. A courtier's scandal. I stood up at council and said the king's voice sounded wrong, and they took my spurs and my name and put me out the postern gate like a cat." I turned my hands over, palms up, as if the story might be written there. "That part's true. What nobody tells you is *why* I doubted. Not rumor. Not politics. I doubted because three years ago I watched King Edric die. Slowly. Over a winter. In a room with no windows."

Beatrix went very still beside me. In the Underfell we'd learned each other's stillnesses too, and this one meant *go on, and go carefully.*

"His Majesty took ill in the ninth year of his reign. The physicians called it the grey wasting—some say it was poison, some say bad blood, and the priests said it was a test of faith, which is what priests say when they have nothing. He wasted for three years. And then, in the spring of the third year, he got better. Miraculous recovery. Tears in the chapel, bells in the streets." I heard my own voice go flat and cold, the way it does when I'm carrying something too heavy to hold any other way. "I was one of four men in the room when it happened. His Majesty woke from the fever and sat up and asked for wine, and his voice was *wrong*, Beatrix. Not weak. Wrong. Like a man doing an impression of himself. Like someone had studied Edric Thornfell for years and finally gotten around to playing him."

"And the other three men in the room?"

"Two are dead—one of a fall, one of a fever that killed only him. The third swore himself to silence and hasn't met my eyes since." I pulled the blanket tighter, though I wasn't cold. Cold would have been a mercy. "I told myself it was the illness. Voices change. Faces change. Grief changes people, and he had buried a wife and a son inside five years. I told myself every story a loyal man tells himself, because the alternative was treason, and treason gets people I loved killed. So I served him. Two more years. I carried his orders. I arrested men on his word. I knelt."

The fire ring was cold but I stared into it anyway. "And then one night—I'll spare you the details of how, suffice to say I was drunk and grieving and stupid—he summoned me privately. Just the two of us in the map room, past midnight. And the king looked at me with eyes that were not his, and he said, *'Ser Aldric. You have known for a long time. Why do you think you are still alive?'*"

Beside me, Beatrix exhaled slowly through her nose. My sword-speecher. The woman who could hear steel remember. Even she hadn't heard this, and I understood suddenly why she kept her gift secret, why she flinched whenever her blade spoke—because being known that completely, by anything, is a kind of nakedness that doesn't heal.

"He offered me a choice," I went on. "He always offers a choice. That's the thing people don't understand about it—it doesn't take. It asks. Politely. Reasonably. It lays out the arithmetic so clearly you feel foolish resisting. *Your king is gone,* it said, in Edric's voice, with Edric's cadence, and God forgive me, it even had Edric's little habit of touching his signet when he was about to ask for something. *Your loyalty needs somewhere to live. Swear to the crown itself—not the man, the crown—and keep serving. Nothing changes. Everything continues. Or refuse, and join the list.*"

"There's a list?"

"There is always a list." I closed my eyes. "So I knelt. On the same stones where I'd sworn my knighthood at nineteen, in front of the same empty throne, and I swore a second oath. Secret. Unwitnessed by any priest, which should have made it worthless, except that it wasn't worthless at all, because the thing wearing my king's face wrote it into me like a brand. And here is the part I should have told you on the low road, the first hour I knew you, and didn't—"

"Aldric." Her voice was very soft. "What does the oath bind you to?"

"To obedience," I said. "To service. To the crown, unto death, whatever wears it." I opened my eyes and looked at her straight on, because she deserved that much. "And to a geas. I cannot speak its full terms aloud—that's part of the binding—but I can tell you what it does. Wherever I am, *it* knows. When I sleep, it can look through my sleeping eyes if it wishes. Every mile I've walked with you, every plan we've made in whispers, every camp—we have been followed by me. I am the hunter's hound, Beatrix. I've been leading them to you since the day we met, and I didn't know until the Underfell, until I felt it pull, until I understood why they were never three days behind us. They weren't following the courier's map. They were following *me*."

The wind moved through the pines above us. Somewhere down the valley, faintly, three notes repeated—a hunter's call, patient as winter.

Beatrix didn't move away from me. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, because I will never deserve it: she didn't move away.

"The ferry," she said at last, evenly. "Marta and her girls. The family in the boat behind ours."

"Yes."

"The hunters knew exactly where to wait."

"Yes."

She was quiet for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had the flat calm I'd learned to fear more than shouting. "Say whatever oath-breaking costs. Say it plainly. Don't dress it up for me."

"It kills you," I said. "Breaking a Regent-oath isn't like breaking a vow of fealty. The binding goes into the marrow. I asked a hedge-priest once, anonymously, what happens to a man who forswears a geas-oath. He thought I meant a demon-contract. He said the body keeps faith even when the soul won't. Heart stops. Lungs forget their work. The oath holds you together, and when you take it out—" I shrugged, and my shoulder bumped hers, and that small warm contact nearly undid me. "You don't survive breaking it. Not cleanly. Not at all."

* * *

She argued, of course. She argued the way she does everything—thoroughly, fiercely, with a smuggler's eye for angles I hadn't considered. There must be another way; there was always another way; we'd find a scholar, a counter-binding, a loophole in the wording. She paced the length of the granite shelf and back while I listened and loved her and let her wear herself out against the truth, because she deserved the chance to fight it before it beat her.

"There's always a loophole in oaths," she said finally, stopping in front of me. "My order taught reading them like contracts. Loopholes, conditions, escape clauses—"

"This one was written by something older than language that eats vows for breakfast." I stood. "Beatrix. Listen to me. I have spent three years being its compass needle. Every hour I stay near you, the Vault road gets narrower. You need maybe two days, unwatched, to reach the Hollow Vault. I can give you those two days. There is exactly one way."

"No." Flat. Final. The same tone she used on Maren over the blood-debt, and I almost smiled, because even facing this she was herself, granite all the way down.

"I'm not asking permission."

"I know." Her jaw tightened. "That's rather the problem with knights. You get an idea and it gallops off with you and everyone else eats dust." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped to something fierce and low. "Listen to me, Aldric Vane. I have spent seven years surviving by being small. I know every trick of vanishing there is, and I am telling you there is no version of this where you walk into that valley alone and they simply—lose your trail. They'll take you. And then they'll have everything you know, which is everything, and they'll wring the Vault's location out of you in an afternoon."

"They already know where the Vault is. They've always known. What they can't find is *you*—not truly, not precisely, or they'd have had us in the Underfell. The map is a riddle, not a route. Only you can read the last stretch. All I can give them is a trail that ends where I end, and by the time they're done being certain of it, you're inside stone that's older than their master." I reached out and took her hands, and she let me, which I noted with the strange clarity of a man cataloguing what he's about to lose. "One unguarded hour. Maybe two. That's what I'm worth, Beatrix. Let me be worth it."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it. "Don't talk about yourself like you're coin."

"Somebody has to spend me," I said, gently. "I'd rather it were me."

She pulled her hands free and turned away, and her shoulders were shaking, and I did the cruelest kind thing I have ever done, which was to stand still and let her cry without touching her, because if I touched her I would not leave.

* * *

We settled it the way soldiers settle everything—practically, badly, at dawn.

Tomas woke us both by kicking my boot, whispering that there were lights in the valley, moving, unhurried, in a line that suggested men who knew exactly where they were going. Below us, torch-flame crawled along the river road like embers down a fuse. Counting by their spread: twenty. Perhaps thirty. Far too many for two swords, a smuggler, and a boy with a knife he'd stolen from me.

Beatrix stood at the shelf's edge with the dawn grey behind her and the courier's map open in her hands, and I watched her become the person the songs would eventually sing about, though none of them would ever spell her name right. Whatever grief had shaken her in the night had been folded away—not gone, never gone, but stored, the way she stored everything, in that iron strongbox behind her ribs.

"Here." She pressed something into my palm: a strip of cloth torn from her sleeve, knotted twice. "If you break it—if you actually do this—my blade will know. Swords remember everything, even the endings." Her voice was steady. Her eyes were not. "Make it loud, Aldric. Make it cost them something."

"It always costs the swearer most," I said. "That's the whole point of oaths. That's the whole point of me."

"One unguarded hour," she said. "I'll need every minute of it. Don't you dare die before the hour's spent."

"I have never once been punctual in my life. Consider it sworn." I smiled, and found, to my genuine surprise, that it didn't hurt. Shame, it turns out, burns clean when you finally feed it purpose. Seven years I'd carried that night in the map room like a swallowed stone, and now, walking down into the valley with my sword loose in its scabbard and thirty pairs of eyes turning toward me out of the dark, I felt lighter than I had since I was nineteen and believed kneeling meant something holy.

They came out of the trees to meet me, silver-collared, oath-sworn, faces smooth as coins. Their captain—a woman with frost-grey hair and a voice like a ledger—asked me where the swordspeaker was.

"Gone ahead," I said, and smiled again, and felt the brand beneath my breastbone begin, at long last, to burn. "But you'll want me anyway. I have something to confess. And I warn you—it's going to be very loud."

Behind me, high on the shelf, a figure turned south and began to run, and my heart ran with her, all the way to the Vault.
