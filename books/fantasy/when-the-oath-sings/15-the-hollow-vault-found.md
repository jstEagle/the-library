## 15. The Hollow Vault Found

The map said the last mile was walked, not ridden, and the map had been right about everything else, so I left the mule tethered to a rowan with an apology and a handful of dried apples, and I climbed.

I had imagined—because I am a woman who imagines things as a professional habit—that the Hollow Vault would be hidden. A door behind a waterfall. A keyhole in a cliff face. Something that required cleverness, or at least a crowbar. Instead the mountain simply stopped pretending. The fells fell away on either side like curtains drawn back by a bored hand, and there it stood at the head of the valley: a cathedral of black stone, older than any cathedral, older than the word for cathedral, sitting in the open air as if it had never once occurred to the world to hide it.

It did not look built. It looked *deposited*. The stone was seamless, without joint or mortar, dark as the underside of a tongue, and it drank the afternoon light rather than reflecting it. No birds crossed above it. I noticed that only later, when I thought back on the approach and realized the silence had begun a quarter mile out—not an absence of sound but a *presence* of it, a held breath, the whole valley waiting for someone to finish speaking.

My sword knew before I did.

It had been quiet for days—the wary, listening quiet it kept since Greywater Bridge, since it screamed my position to every hunter in Thornfell—but now it hummed against my hip, low and continuous, the way a struck string hums after the note has technically ended. Not singing. Remembering aloud.

"I know," I told it. "I see it too."

Talking to your sword is either madness or a trade secret, depending on who's asking, and since no one was asking, I let myself have the conversation. Seven years of practice had made me fluent in silences, but this one had texture. It pressed against my ears like deep water.

The courier's map ended at the threshold. The final marginalia—a cramped hand I'd spent three days deciphering in the Underfell's lightless passages—read: *What you seek is not kept here. What you seek is what keeps.*

Which is the sort of sentence that sounds profound until you're standing in front of it, at which point it sounds like a threat.

I checked the sky. Somewhere behind me, Aldric was walking into the hunters' net wearing his guilt like bait on a hook, buying me hours with the only coin he had left—an oath broken loudly enough to make the Regent flinch. I would not think about that yet. Thinking about it yet would put me on my knees in this valley, and the valley already felt like a place where kneeling might become permanent.

So I climbed the steps instead.

There were steps. Of course there were steps; power always makes you climb toward it so you arrive slightly winded and grateful to be permitted. Nineteen of them—I counted, because counting is what I do when terror needs a job—and each one took more effort than its size justified, as though the stone were politely asking me to reconsider. On the thirteenth step my sword spoke, actually spoke, one word in the voice it used only for oaths:

*Soon.*

I nearly turned around. I want that recorded somewhere, by some witness-stone of my own: I nearly turned around, and I didn't, and the difference between those two things is most of what I understand about courage.

* * *

The doors were open.

That unsettled me more than locked doors would have. Locked doors can be picked, forced, reasoned with. Open doors are an invitation, and invitations imply a host. I stood on the threshold a long moment, listening to the held breath of the valley, and then I did the thing I have done at every checkpoint, every ambush, every bad decision of my adult life: I went in anyway, because the alternative was standing outside forever, and I had already spent seven years doing that.

Inside, the Vault was vast and wrong.

Wrong first in its proportions—a nave wider than any hall in the capital, pillars rising into darkness that the eye refused to measure, and everywhere that seamless black stone, polished to a depth that wasn't reflection so much as *absorption*. My footsteps made no echo. Sound went in and did not come back. It was a room designed, I understood slowly, to keep things: to take whatever entered and hold it, completely, forever.

And hanging in the air above the altarless floor, suspended at the height of a man's raised hand, was the first oath.

I don't know how else to describe it. It wasn't visible, exactly—it was more that the air in that spot was *full*, dense with meaning the way a storm cloud is dense with rain. When I looked directly at it, I perceived something like script, characters older than any alphabet I'd been taught, arranged in rings that turned too slowly to watch. And beneath the seeing, faint as a pulse under skin, I heard it. Not words. The *shape* of words. The shape of the very first promise anyone ever made to a king in Thornfell—or perhaps made *by* a king, the scholar hadn't been certain, and neither was the stone.

It hung there like a held breath because that's exactly what it was. An oath is a breath taken in and never released. Someone, a very long time ago, had promised something enormous in this room, and the room had caught the promise and never let it go, and everything that came after—all the coronation vows, all the fealty oaths, all the small daily pledges of loyalty sworn by clerks with silver collars—had wound itself around that first breath like ivy around a dead tree.

Like a parasite around a host.

"Oh," I said, and my voice fell straight down into the floor and vanished. "Oh, you're not a weapon at all."

Because that's what I'd come expecting. Some artifact to shatter, some sword to break, some clean mechanical solution to a problem that had eaten a king from the inside. I had packed for violence. I had not packed for *architecture*.

At the heart of the nave, where an altar should have been, stood the witness-stone.

It was smaller than I expected—waist-high, roughly the shape of a millstone, black as everything else but warm. I felt the warmth from three steps away, a heat like a living body, and my sword's hum climbed until it was nearly a whine. I laid my palm flat against my scabbard to quiet it, and the leather vibrated under my hand like a frightened animal.

"Easy," I murmured. "We're just looking."

Liar. We both knew we weren't just looking. But lying to your own sword is a particular kind of loneliness, and I was saving my honesty for what came next.

* * *

Here is what I understood, standing before the witness-stone, and here is what I will try to set down plainly because my hands are still not entirely steady:

Every oath ever sworn to the crown of Thornfell was in that stone.

Not a record of them. Not an accounting. They were *in* it, alive, the way a bell holds every note it has ever rung. I could feel them the moment I came close—my gift reaching out unbidden, seven years of suppression collapsing in a single heartbeat, because you cannot stand next to that much sworn language and stay deaf. Oaths layered on oaths, generations deep, ten thousand voices knotted together: knights pledging their swords, lords pledging their lands, servants pledging their lives, all of it flowing downhill through the years into this one warm black stone, and from the stone, I now understood with a vertigo that buckled something behind my knees, into *him*. Into the thing wearing the king.

The Regent did not feed on oaths the way a wolf feeds on sheep. The Regent *was* the feeding. It was the channel. Every vow sworn in the king's name ran through it like water through a millrace, and the millrace had grown teeth.

And the stone—warm, patient, terrible—held mine.

I knew it before I touched it. I felt my own oath the way you feel your own name called across a crowded market: a small specific tug in the general roar. Seven years ago, kneeling in a chapter house that smelled of tallow and rain, seventeen years old and certain the world was a fair place that rewarded devotion, I had placed my hands on my mentor's sword and sworn to speak truth for the crown, to give voice to steel, to serve until my service was spent.

The order purged within the year. My mentor's grave was a rumor. And her last command to me—*never speak for a crown again*—had been the only vow I'd managed to keep, mostly by never speaking at all.

But the first oath. The first oath was still in the stone. Still ringing. Still feeding the thing in the palace a hundred miles south, one thin silver thread among millions, but *mine*, and therefore the only thread in all that woven mass that I had any right to touch.

I put my hand on the witness-stone.

The warmth shocked me—it was blood-warm, human-warm, the warmth of every person who had ever knelt—and the stone *sang back*. Not aloud. Inside. It gave me my own oath in my own seventeen-year-old voice, word for word, tremor for tremor, the catch in my throat where I'd almost cried, preserved perfectly, held faithfully, kept the way this room kept everything.

For one obscene moment it was almost comforting. To be remembered so completely. To be witnessed so thoroughly by something that asked nothing, judged nothing, simply *held* what I had given. There is a hunger in every person to be heard like that—to be heard all the way down—and the stone offered it freely, endlessly, to everyone who ever knelt.

That, I thought, is the trap. That's the whole trap, isn't it. It doesn't take anything. You *give*. You kneel and you pour yourself out and it holds you forever, warm and perfect and consuming, and the kingdom calls it loyalty, and the parasite calls it dinner.

I took my hand off the stone. My palm print faded from its surface like breath off glass.

Above me, the first oath turned in its slow rings, still holding its ancient breath, and I finally understood what the courier's dying riddle meant. *What you seek is what keeps.* The Regent couldn't be killed because killing requires a death, and oaths don't die—they're only ever kept or broken. The whole machinery of power in Thornfell, the throne and the parasite and the silver-collared clerks and the hunters with their stolen vows, all of it hung from that single first promise, sustained by every act of keeping, starved by every act of breaking.

Aldric had bought me an hour by breaking his. One thread cut, the weave loosened.

But the founding thread—the first breath, the oldest knot—could only be cut by the one person whose oath was in the stone and whose mouth was free to open.

Mine.

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and the Vault drank the sound without echo, and somewhere far to the north a man was dying on a road so that I would have time to do the one thing I had sworn never to do again: speak for a crown. Renounce for a crown. Break, publicly, freely, in the hearing of the thing that kept all records—

The doors, which had stood open behind me since I entered, began to close.

Slowly, silently, the darkness beyond them narrowing to a blade-thin line of grey valley light, and along the walls of the nave the black stone woke with a faint inner glow, ring upon ring of old script kindling like coals under ash. The held breath of the valley finally began to release.

Something was coming up the nineteen steps. Something that wore a king's body and moved with a patience older than kingdoms, and it had felt, I realized, every word of what I'd just understood—as if understanding, here, were also a kind of speech.

My sword sang one clear note in the dark.

Then the last light closed, and the Regent came in.
