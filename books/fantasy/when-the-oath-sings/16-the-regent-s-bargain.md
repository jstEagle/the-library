## 16. The Regent's Bargain

It came in wearing the king's body the way a man wears a coat that fits him only in certain lights.

I knew King Edric's face from coins and wanted posters and one memorable woodcut in a smuggler's almanac where they'd given him a beard like a badger. This was that face—fiftyish, weathered at the eyes, the jaw of a man who'd once been handsome and had decided to stop bothering. But it moved wrong. The blink was a half-beat late, like a puppet remembering it was supposed to have eyelids. The smile arrived before there was anything to smile about.

And beneath the voice—the voice itself I'll come to, because the voice is the whole trap—there was something else. Not sound. Weight. The black stone of the Vault drank it in, and the ring-script along the walls brightened and dimmed in time with its breathing, if you could call it breathing.

"You're smaller than your reputation," it said.

The voice was Edric's. Warm, a little tired, the voice of a man who has signed too many documents and would still rather hear what you have to say than send you away. But underneath it, woven through it like wire through wool, was the second voice I'd heard once at a feast in the capital and had spent weeks pretending I hadn't. Old. Hungry. Patient as erosion.

"Everyone says that," I said. "Usually right before they underestimate me."

"It does not intend to underestimate you." A pause. "It intends to *hire* you."

My sword hung quiet at my hip. That frightened me more than anything else in the room. All through the Underfell, all down the nineteen steps, it had sung and hissed and screamed its opinions like a cat in a sack. Now it was silent—not calm-silent, but listening-silent, the way a blade goes still when it recognizes something older than itself.

"First lesson of bargaining," I said, because my mouth has always run ahead of my sense, "is not announcing your intentions to the person across the table."

"First lesson of bargaining," it agreed, and the king's mouth curved, "is knowing what the other person wants. It knows what you want. It has known for seven years. It watched you want it every single day, Beatrix Yarrow, while you carried cheap steel and kept your mouth shut and let them believe you were nothing."

*It*, it said. Not *I*. As if the body were a borrowed horse and the rider preferred not to name itself after the tack. I filed that away with everything else, in the little room in my head where I kept useful things and pretended I wasn't afraid.

"I don't know what you're talking about," I said. "I'm a merchant's guard. I have a receipt for tin somewhere."

"Yes," it said gently. "You do. Shall we look at what else you have?"

It raised the king's hand—a small gesture, almost courtly—and the Vault answered.

* * *

The witness-stone at the heart of the nave lit like a struck flint, and the light wasn't light. It was memory. It poured out of the black stone and became the world around us, and I stood inside seven years ago with my boots on ground that no longer existed.

The purge. My purge.

They'd come at dawn to the Chapterhouse at Vell's Crossing—I had always remembered rain, but there was no rain; there was frost, silvering the training yard, and the swordspeechers' banners stiff with cold. The knights in crown colors came over the wall in the grey half-light, and the first thing that died was Master Corwin's hound, and the second thing was the silence, because after that nobody could pretend this was an inspection.

I saw myself—young, twenty-two, hair badly braided, a practice sword in my hand because it was the only thing in reach—and I watched her, me, watch Serah Ondrey cut down in the yard. I watched the armory burn, and heard—for the first time since, or maybe for the thousandth time since, memory is a liar about frequency—the blades screaming. Every sword in that armory had been spoken to, sworn over, taught to remember. They went up like a choir.

And I saw Master Ysolde.

I had told myself for seven years that she died fighting. That was the story I'd built my spine out of: *she went down swinging, so you get up swinging.* The witness-stone showed me the truth with the dispassion of good bookkeeping. Ysolde had surrendered. She'd knelt in the frost with her hands open and her sword laid flat on the stones in front of her—laid *down*, which for a swordspeaker is the same as lying down yourself—and she'd said, clearly enough for the captain to hear:

"I yield. My order yields. Let the girls go."

And the captain—who wore the king's colors, who spoke in the king's name, whose oath the Regent had drunk like wine—had said yes. And meant it, even. And then the second voice under his voice had said otherwise, and the sword came down anyway, and Ysolde's last sight was a man in her king's livery keeping a promise he no longer owned.

The memory ended. The nave came back. I was standing exactly where I'd been standing, except that a person is never exactly where they were.

"That was true," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "That actually happened. You didn't make that up."

"The Regent does not lie," it said. "Lying is for creatures with something to lose. It shows what *is*. What was done in the king's name, by men sworn to the king, fed into the king's power. Every drop of it. Would you like to see your mentor's grave? It is very tidy. The crown pays for tidy graves now. There is even a stone."

"Don't."

"As you wish." The king's face did something that on a human being would have been compassion. On it, the compassion sat like a coin glued to a corpse. "But understand what you just saw. Not a monster's work. An *oath's* work. Men swore to serve the crown of Thornfell, and the crown asked for the death of swordspeakers, and their oaths made their hands honest instruments of it. That is what oaths are, Beatrix. That is what *you* are. A promise shaped into a weapon and pointed by whoever holds the other end."

"And you're going to offer to hold it," I said. "Yes. I got there ahead of you."

* * *

It began to walk then, slow, the king's boots ringing on black stone, and I noticed with a distant, hysterical part of my mind that it walked the way Aldric walks when he's giving himself time to say something difficult. Of course it did. It had been watching Aldric kneel for years. It had learned kingship the way a cuckoo learns a nest.

"Here is what it offers," the Regent said. "Listen to all of it before you decide it is beneath you. You have spent seven years deciding things are beneath you. It has watched that too, and it finds it wasteful."

I said nothing. Which, in a room where understanding is speech, was probably saying quite a lot.

"Your order is restored. Refounded by royal charter, with lands, with the Chapterhouse rebuilt on its old foundations—or new ones, if the old ones offend you. Your name is cleared. Not quietly, either—there will be a decree read in every market square in Thornfell: *Beatrix Yarrow, last daughter of the Speaking Blade, faithful servant of the crown, falsely condemned.* Your gift legitimized. No more cheap steel. No more checkpoints. No more shrinking." The king's head tilted. "No more being small, Beatrix. You have been so small for so long that you have mistaken the size of your life for the size of the world."

The words went into me the way a blade goes between ribs: cleanly, expertly, finding the gap I'd left.

Because here is the thing I have never said out loud, and the Vault heard it anyway: I *missed* it. Not the danger—gods, no, the danger I could have done without forever. I missed the speaking. I missed a sword warming in my hand like a held hand. I missed walking into a room and being somebody, instead of being a woman named after a hedge plant who moved tin and kept her eyes down. Seven years is a long time to rehearse being nothing. Some mornings I woke up fluent in it, and some mornings I woke up starving.

"They'll take it," I said. "The charter, the decree. And in ten years, when some chancellor decides swordspeakers are inconvenient again, your oaths will make honest hands of honest men and there will be another frost, another yard, and this time they'll start with the founder. That's not restoration. That's a leash measured to fit."

"Yes," it said simply.

I blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"It agrees. That is what the crown is. That is what any crown is. A leash that promises to be kind. The Regent does not insult your intelligence by denying it." It stopped walking and turned, and the torchlight—it had conjured torchlight at some point, or the stone had, and I hadn't even noticed, which should have terrified me more than it did—caught the king's face at an angle that made him look briefly, terribly, like a tired father. "But consider. Leashes exist because the alternative is teeth. Your order fell because it had no protector but its own virtue. Virtue is not armor, Beatrix. You know this. You have *worn* this. Offered a choice between the leash and the grave, your mentor chose the grave, and look how much her choosing saved."

"Don't you dare talk about her."

"There it is," it said softly. "Anger. Good. Anger is honest. Keep that." It spread the king's hands, palms up—an old courtroom gesture, *behold, I hide nothing*. "Now hear the price, because the Regent keeps honest books. Swear yourself to the crown of Thornfell. One oath. Freely given—that matters, it must be free, free vows are the sweetest food—and spoken here, into the stone, where the first promise was ever made. And take the office the crown has lacked for three years. Become its Voice. When Thornfell swears, you speak for Thornfell. When Thornfell commands, your voice carries it. Every oath in this kingdom would pass through your mouth, Beatrix Yarrow, and be *sharpened* there."

"And what happens to the man whose mouth you're borrowing?"

Something moved behind the king's eyes. For one instant the mask slipped—not off, just sideways—and what looked out was vast and amused and hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food.

"The king sleeps," it said. "He has slept three years. He may sleep longer. He is comfortable. Kings dream very well; it is the rest of the kingdom that has nightmares." The warmth returned, seamless, as though the crack had never opened. "Think of it as mercy. Think of it as regency. Every kingdom in history has been ruled by someone other than the man wearing the crown. The Regent merely makes the arrangement *honest*."

"And the oaths I'd carry. The commands I'd speak. The purges I'd—" I stopped. "You'd use my voice for the next frost."

"The next frost," it agreed, "or the next harvest protection, or the next border levy, or the next wedding. You would not choose. Voices do not choose. Voices *carry*." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "As your mentor carried her order's rules until they killed her. As your king carried his coronation vow straight into this throat. Everyone serves something, Beatrix. The Regent is offering you the rarest arrangement in the world: service *with* compensation."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out cracked and ugly and echoed off nine rings of ancient script, and the Regent watched me laugh with the king's patient face, and somewhere in that laugh I found the floor again.

"You've been doing this a long time," I said. "The offer. The honesty routine. Showing people their dead."

"Since before this valley had a name."

"And it works, doesn't it. People swear."

"Nearly everyone," it said, with something that might, in a younger parasite, have been wistfulness. "Pride refuses longest. Pride takes days."

"Well." I drew a breath, and my hand found my sword's hilt without asking me, and the blade was warm. Warm for the first time since the Vault closed. "Then let me save you some days."

But it raised the king's hand, and the stone behind me shifted, and I understood that it was not finished showing me things.

"One more entry in the honest books," it said. "This one concerns a knight."

* * *

The witness-stone lit again, and I tried not to look, and I looked. I am weak in exactly the ways it had studied.

Aldric Vane, eleven years younger, kneeling in a chapel I recognized—the royal chapel in the capital, the one with the ceiling like a turned-over bowl of night. Kneeling before the king. Except the king's face was wrong in the way faces go wrong in dreams, and the candlelight bent toward him instead of away, and Aldric—Aldric, who jokes when he's frightened and goes silent when he's breaking—was swearing.

I couldn't hear the words. The stone showed me the shape of the vow without the sound of it, which was somehow worse: I watched Aldric's mouth form promises and watched the dark under the false king's skin lean in and drink each one, and I watched Aldric's shoulders change afterward. Watched the exact moment a man becomes a marked thing. He'd thought he was swearing loyalty to his liege. He'd been signing himself over to the thing wearing him.

"He did not know," the Regent said, and its voice had gone soft, and softness from that throat was obscene. "He believed he served his king. He has believed it every day since. It has worn him like a signet ring. It knows everywhere he walks, everyone he loves, everything he plans—because his oath tells it, nightly, whether he wishes it to or not." The king's face turned to me fully. "It tells it now. Even here. Especially here. Do you understand what that means, Beatrix? The knight who brought you this far, who bled for you at Greywater Bridge, who looks at you the way he tries not to—he is a door, and the Regent holds the key, and whatever you two have built in silence in the dark, it has been reading over his shoulder since the day he knelt."

The stone went dark. The nave settled. Somewhere above us, impossibly far up, water dripped onto stone with a patience like a clock.

"Why show me that?" My voice was steadier than I deserved. "If he's your door, you've just told me to throw him away."

"Because the Regent does not need you to throw him away." The king's face smiled, and this time the smile reached the eyes, and the reaching was the most horrifying thing yet. "It needs you to understand that you have *never once* been unobserved. Not in the fells. Not in the Underfell. Not in this room. Everything you love is a window. Everything you trust is a door. You have been alone since the day you took your first oath, child, and the Regent is offering you the only escape there has ever been: to stop being the observed and become the observer. To hold the windows instead of standing in their light."

The words settled over me like snow. And I am ashamed—truly ashamed, in a way I'll carry past the end of this story—of how long I stood there wanting it.

Not the power. Power never tempted me; I'd seen what power did to the people who had it, up close, at checkpoint distance. But the *unobservedness* dressed as belonging. The end of running. A name said aloud in market squares instead of whispered in smuggler cant. Ysolde's grave with flowers on it, paid for by the crown, and my hand steady on a fine sword, and no more shrinking—no more waking fluent in nothing. It offered me back the girl I'd been before the frost, plus interest, plus safety, and it wrapped the whole poisoned parcel in the one truth I couldn't argue with: *everyone serves something.*

My sword hummed against my palm. Low. Almost a whisper. And in that hum—not words, swordspeech is not words, it's weather—I felt it say what it had been holding silent all this time, waiting, the way blades wait:

*You already chose. In the Underfell. When you refused the queen. When you wouldn't forge her contract. You already chose who holds your other end.*

The Regent felt it too. The script-rings dimmed.

"No," I said.

The king's head cocked. "Speak plainly. Here, plain speech is binding."

"No," I said, plainer. "Not because I'm noble. I'm not. Half of me wants this so badly my teeth ache, and I'd bet coin you counted on that. But I've read your honest books, Regent, and here's my entry: you showed me Ysolde kneeling in the frost, and you called it proof that virtue loses. You're right. It lost. But she *chose* it. Laid her sword down herself, hands open, and the choice was hers right up until your oath-sworn dog took it from her. Everything you're offering me is that frost with better lighting. A yard where I kneel prettier. And I have spent seven years learning the difference between surviving and being kept, and I know which one this is."

I stepped forward, one step, into the smell of old stone and older hunger.

"So here is my counter-offer. You leave the king's body. You leave this valley. Or you find out what a freely broken oath sounds like, because I came a very long way through a very dark hole to learn it, and I am *not* leaving without using it."

Silence. Real silence, the held-breath kind, the kind the valley had been keeping for centuries.

Then the Regent laughed—with the king's throat, but the laugh belonged entirely to the thing underneath—and the torchlight guttered, and every ring of script along the walls blazed at once.

"Oh," it said, delighted, dreadful, "it has been *so long* since anyone came down those stairs intending to break something."

Above us, far up the shaft, faint through nineteen sealed doors of black stone, came a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat.

Steel. And a voice, hoarse and cracking, shouting a word that was half a battle cry and half a goodbye.

Aldric had arrived at the Vault doors—and he was already swearing.
