## 17. Aldric's Last Oath

There is a particular flavor of irony in being delivered to your own execution as a gift-wrapped distraction, and I have had seven years to develop a refined palate for irony.

The hunters did not bind me. That was the first insult. They walked me down the last mile of mountain road with my hands free, because a man who has sworn himself to the Regent does not need rope. The oath does the binding. It sits behind my sternum like a swallowed coal, and when I tried, on the second night of our march, to so much as think the word *run* in a direction the Regent hadn't sanctioned, the coal flared and my legs simply stopped. I stood in the middle of a sheep track for the better part of an hour, watching my own boots refuse me, while the hunter called Wren ate dried plums and offered me one with the magnanimity of a man feeding his horse.

I declined the plum. Small victories.

"You could still be useful," Wren said, around the pit. "That's why they want you breathing. Bait that spoils is just meat."

"Flattery," I said. "You'll go far."

He laughed. They all laughed, eventually, once they understood what I was. A knight who broke his oath to his king — no, worse, a knight who *kept* a secret oath sworn to the thing wearing his king — was a joke with a long punchline. They had found me in a ditch outside Greywater, half-dead from a wound I'd taken stepping in front of Beatrix Yarrow's death, and instead of leaving me there like sensible people, they'd carried me. Because I was useful. Because the Regent's hook was already in me, and a hooked fish swims where it's told.

Here is what they did not know, what I did not tell them, what I have been carrying up this mountain alongside the coal in my chest:

A broken oath kills you. Everyone knows that. You swear by the stone, by the blood, by the old names, and if you break it, the breaking unmakes whatever part of you held the vow. Men struck dumb. Men struck blind. Men who simply lay down at midday on a crowded street and were dead before anyone thought to catch them. It is the one law of Thornfell even smugglers respect.

But I had spent three years learning things about oaths that no one is supposed to learn. I had knelt before a thing wearing my king's face and felt it drink my words. And in the fells, feverish, with Yarrow stitching me closed and refusing to meet my eye, I had done arithmetic in the dark.

Every vow feeds the Regent. Every oath sworn in the king's name is a thread in its web. It cannot be killed — the scholar had been very clear, and scholars are rarely clear about anything — only unbound. Unbound how? By what? The Vault held the first oath ever sworn, the founding pattern, the loom. But a loom doesn't unravel itself. Someone has to cut the cloth.

And a freely broken oath is not a wound to the Regent. It is a wound in the *pattern*. One thread snapping where every thread was meant to hold forever.

One thread, freely broken, by a man who meant it with everything he had left.

* * *

They brought me to the Vault at dusk, which I suspect was theater. Everything about the Regent is theater. It wore Edric's face for three years and learned to smile with Edric's mouth, and it loves an entrance the way Edric never did — Edric hated ceremony, used to complain that being king was mostly standing still while other people talked.

The Hollow Vault is a cathedral of black stone grown rather than built, if that distinction means anything, rising out of the mountain's shoulder like a tooth. The hunters stopped at the threshold. Even they would not enter. Wren looked almost relieved.

"He's waiting," Wren said.

"I assumed," I said. "He's always waiting. It's most of what he does."

Walking into the Vault was like walking into a held breath. I don't have Yarrow's gift — or curse, or whatever we're calling it this week — but even a deaf man knows when a room is listening. The air hummed at the edge of hearing, a note too low to be sound and too patient to be anything else. In the center of the floor, suspended in nothing, hung a shard of grey stone the size of a heart. The witness-stone. The first oath, still hanging in the air after a thousand years, still unfinished, still *sworn*.

And between me and it stood the king of Thornfell.

Or the shape of him. Edric had been broad through the shoulders; this thing had kept the breadth but forgotten what it was for. He stood the way furniture stands. When he turned, the face was right — gods, the face was exactly right, I'd have sworn on anything that it was Edric, except that Edric's eyes had always been a little sad and these were a little *hungry*, and hunger looks like sadness from across a room.

"Aldric Vane." My name in the king's voice. I had heard it a hundred times in better years, and twice in the worst ones, and I had learned to tell them apart by the pause before the *Vane*. The Regent paused. It always paused, as if consulting notes. "You brought yourself back to me. How convenient."

"The hunters deserve the credit," I said. "I was unconscious at the time."

"It grieves me that you ran." He moved closer, and the hum in the air deepened. "You swore. You swore *willingly*. Do you remember? You knelt where you are standing now—"

"On this exact spot," I said. "Yes. You made a point of it. Symmetry matters to you. I've noticed. It's the one aesthetic opinion you have."

Something flickered behind the borrowed eyes. Not anger. Calculation. "Then you know what you are. Mine. Body and breath and name. There is no road out of that, Aldric. No forgiveness. No forgetting. Only service, long and quiet, until your body fails and I find another."

"See," I said, and my voice cracked more than I wanted it to, "this is where you've misread the situation. Badly. And you've been misreading it for three years, because everything you know about men comes from watching us kneel, and you've drawn entirely the wrong lesson from it."

The Regent tilted Edric's head. Waiting. It is very good at waiting.

"We don't kneel because we're yours," I said. "We kneel because we choose something worth kneeling to. And the moment the choosing stops—" I spread my hands. "Well."

Understanding arrived slowly, the way weather arrives over mountains. I watched it come. I watched Edric's face try several expressions and settle on something almost like pity, which was, in its way, the most honest thing it had ever worn.

"You would break your oath," the Regent said. "Here. Before the stone. Knowing what the breaking costs."

"Now he gets it," I said. "Wren told me you were clever. I'm going to enjoy telling him he oversold you."

"You will die."

"Yes," I said. "That's generally the price. I did the sums. I'm bad at many things — I was always bottom of my class in tactics, ask anyone — but I can count."

"Why?" And there it was. The genuine question. Three years wearing a king and it still didn't understand, couldn't model it, because in all its thousand years of drinking oaths it had never tasted one that wasn't, at root, a bargain. Give this, get that. Serve, and be safe. Kneel, and be fed. It had never met a man willing to pay everything and receive nothing, and it stood there in my dead friend's body genuinely asking *why* like a child.

I gave it the truth, since it had asked so nicely.

"Because a woman I know taught me that the only vows worth making are the ones you'd break for the right reason," I said. "Because I reported her family to your hunters seven years ago and she saved my life anyway, and I would rather die owing her than live square with you. And because—" my throat closed, and I let it, because there was no longer any point pretending to dignity, "because somewhere under all that borrowed flesh you're wearing, there's a man who trusted me, and I am going to spend the last thing I own buying back one hour of his kingdom."

The Regent looked at me for a long time.

Then it smiled with Edric's mouth, and said, gently, horribly: "She isn't here, Aldric. She went east, alone, three days ago. Your sacrifice buys nothing."

And here is the thing about lying to a dying man: you should check whether he cares.

"That's unfortunate," I said. "For you. Because you see, I spent those three days she thinks I was sleeping doing something other than resting. I left her a letter. Detailed. Where the Vault is, what the stone does, and precisely what happens if a founding oath is freely broken in front of it." I smiled back. My face was Edric's lieutenants' least favorite face; they'd said it looked like a door closing. "She'll be along. She's stubborn. Terribly stubborn. It's her worst quality. It's going to be the death of me."

The hum in the room changed key.

* * *

It tried to stop me, of course. The coal behind my sternum flared white-hot and my knees buckled, and the Regent spoke my name in Edric's voice, then in the older voice underneath, the voice like roots and rust, and commanded me to be silent, to kneel, to serve, and every word landed on the oath like rain on iron.

But an oath is a shape, and a shape can only hold what was put into it. The Regent had taken my vow in the dark of a palace corridor three years ago, and what I had sworn — what I had *said*, in words, in the old forms, because the Regent insists on the old forms, ritual being its native tongue — was this:

*"My sword is yours. My silence is yours. Until the crown releases me or the mountain takes me, I will not raise steel against the king, nor speak against him, nor stand against him in any field where he stands."*

I have had three years to hate those words and one night to love them. Do you see it? Do you see what a terrified man's careful wording buys?

I cannot raise steel against the king. I am not raising steel. My sword is in its sheath and staying there.

I cannot speak against him. I am not speaking against him. I have praised nothing and condemned nothing. I have only described my own intentions, which are my own business.

I cannot stand against him in any field where he stands.

The Regent realized one heartbeat before I did. I saw it reach for the coal in my chest, saw it pull — and I took one step sideways, off the line between the throne-door and the witness-stone, out of the field, onto ground where the king of Thornfell was not standing.

And the oath, which had held me for three years like a fist, opened like a hand.

"Oh," I said. "Oh, you *idiot*. You wrote the contract. You insisted on the forms. And I—" I was laughing now, laughing and crying, both equally undignified, "I read it. I'm the only man in Thornfell who read it."

The Regent moved — fast, faster than a king should move, faster than anything should — and the room convulsed, black stone flexing like muscle, and the hum became a shriek, and I knew I had perhaps ten seconds, maybe fewer, and none of them were for running because running was never the plan.

I planted my feet on the stone before the witness-stone, in the place where the first oath in the world had been spoken by someone whose name the stone remembered and history forgot, and I filled my lungs with the held breath of that ancient room, and I broke my vow.

Not quietly. Not in a whisper. Freely, fully, in the old forms, in a voice that cracked on the third word and steadied on the fourth and rang on all the rest:

"My sword was yours. My silence was yours. I take them back. I release myself. Let the vow be broken — freely, wholly, mine to break, and broken."

The world went white at the edges. I felt it begin — the unmaking, the great slow tide going out of me, the coal behind my sternum cooling into ash, into nothing, into the particular peace of a debt discharged. Somewhere far below us, I felt the pattern shiver, one thread snapping in a web of ten thousand, and the shriek of the Vault rose to a register that wasn't sound anymore.

The Regent reached me before I fell. It caught me — caught me, held me up with hands that were Edric's hands, and for one mad moment I thought it meant to strike me, and then I saw its face and understood something worse.

It was afraid.

"You don't understand what you've done," it said, and the two voices came apart in its throat, the king's voice fraying off the old voice like paint off rotting wood. "The pattern holds. The pattern has always held. One broken thread—"

"Is how every weaving ends," I said. Blood in my mouth now, warm and mineral. "Ask any woman with a sword."

I don't remember falling. I remember the stone floor against my cheek, cold as honesty. I remember the light changing in the high dark of the Vault, the held breath finally, *finally* beginning to exhale.

And I remember footsteps. Fast ones. Boots I knew, coming through a door that should have been a mountain away.

Stubborn, I thought. Her worst quality.

* * *

She knelt beside me and did the thing she always does, which is check the blade before the man — her hand went to my sword hilt first, found it sheathed and singing faintly, and then, only then, to my chest, pressing hard where the blood was coming from, though we both knew the blood was the smaller problem.

"You absolute idiot," Beatrix Yarrow said. "You absolute — I was three days east. I was *gone*. How—"

"The letter," I managed. "Under your saddle flap. Left it at Greywater. You're welcome."

Her face did something complicated. Grief and fury fighting for the same real estate. "The Regent told you I wasn't coming. It told me you were dead. We've both been lied to by professionals and we believed it, because we're sentimental, and I *hate* being sentimental."

"Listen," I said, because the tide was going out faster now and I had perhaps a hundred words left and I intended to spend every one of them well. "The thread. One broken thread cracks the weave. But it won't fall on its own. It needs the founding oath broken in front of the stone — freely, wholly, the way I just did mine. Yours. The one that made you a swordspeaker. The vow your mentor told you never to speak for a crown again." I got a hand up, somehow, and gripped her wrist, and her pulse was hammering under my fingers, alive, furious, mortal. "Break it. Don't mourn it. Break it like you mean it, because if you mean it halfway the pattern will knot around the tear and heal, and everything — Aldric, Edric, all of it — was for nothing."

"And my gift goes with it," she said. Not a question. She'd known. She'd probably known since the Underfell.

"Everything costs," I said. "You taught me that. Well — you demonstrated it. Repeatedly. At volume."

She laughed, wet and awful. "Any last requests, Ser Vane?"

"One." My voice was nearly gone. The light was doing something beautiful at the edges of the room, gold bleeding into the black stone like dawn deciding to make an exception. "Rowan tree. South-facing. And don't let them put a plaque on it. I was stripped of my name once already; turns out I liked it."

"Unmarked grave for an unmarked man," she said. "Done."

"Beatrix." The last of the tide. I spent it on the truth, since I'd spent everything else on lies and duty and the space between them. "Seven years ago I sold your order to save my skin, and I have regretted it every day since, and I want you to know the regret was never the point. The point is — you saved me anyway. Both times. Once from the ditch and once from myself. Whatever you decide you are, after — you're the best thing Thornfell ever made and never deserved."

Her hand tightened on mine. Outside, far away, something vast began to scream.

"Go," I whispered. "Finish it. Sing it down."

And Beatrix Yarrow drew her sword, and it sang — oh, it sang, higher and stranger than I'd ever heard it, a note like the first oath finally ending — and I lay back on the cold stone of the beginning of everything, and watched the woman who forgave me walk toward the sound with her chin up, and I thought:

Worth it. Every thread of it. Worth it.
