## 18. The Unbinding

The Vault did not want me to leave.

I don't mean it held me. There were no chains, no doors slamming shut behind me, none of the theatrics you'd expect from a cathedral of black stone built to house the first lie ever told with a straight face. It was quieter than that. The air itself had weight, like walking through water that remembered being ice. Every step toward the witness-stone felt like a step I'd already taken, somewhere, in someone's memory, and the stone was waiting for me to catch up to myself.

Aldric was dead an hour and his blood was still warm on my hands. I hadn't washed it off. Some part of me—the superstitious smuggler part, the part that paid tolls without complaint—said washing it away would be a kind of forgetting, and I had promised him, in the last minute he had words left in him, that I would remember everything.

*Freely broken,* he'd said. *That's the trick of it, Yarrow. It can't be coerced out of anyone. It can't be tricked. It has to be given up by someone who could keep it and chooses not to.* His laugh had been mostly blood. *The Regent's whole existence is a bet that no one ever will.*

Well. I'd spent seven years betting on the same thing. Time to lose gracefully.

* * *

Let me tell you what the witness-stone looked like, because the scholars' books got it wrong, or maybe they just lacked the nerve to describe it honestly.

It wasn't a monument. It wasn't a throne or an altar or any of the shapes power likes to wear when it wants to be worshipped. It was a stone about the size of a millstone, round and plain, sitting in the exact center of the Vault's floor as if it had been set down by someone who meant to come back for it later and never did. Its surface moved—not physically, nothing so crude—but light slid across it the way light slides across deep water, and in that sliding I saw them. Oaths. Thousands upon thousands of them, layered like sediment, each one a small bright thread: *I swear fealty. I swear to protect. I swear this harvest to the crown, this child to the guard, this life to this life.*

And there, near the top, still bright after seven years because apparently oaths don't fade just because you stop feeding them—there was mine.

*I, Beatrix Yarrow, swear my voice to the blades and the blades to the people, until the last sword forgets its making or I forget my name.*

I'd been nineteen. My mentor, Serah Voss, had stood at my shoulder with her hand between my shoulder blades, steadying me the way she steadied everything, and afterward she'd said, *Now you can never lie to a blade again, little wren. Remember that before you swear anything smaller.*

Then the purge came, and she died face-down in the snow outside our own chapterhouse, and her last command to me had been four words: *Never speak for crowns.*

I knelt beside the stone. My knees knew the posture even if the rest of me had spent seven years unlearning it.

"Hello," I said to my own oath. Wry narration, remember—that's what I promised myself, right up until the moment wryness ran out. "You've gotten heavy."

The stone sang back at me. Not sound, exactly. More like the memory of sound, the way a bell keeps ringing in the wrist long after the ear gives up. And beneath my oath, beneath all the oaths, something stirred—a vast attention turning over in its sleep, the way a body in bed turns toward a door opening.

It had felt Aldric die. I was sure of it. A freely broken oath sworn inside these walls would have rung through the Regent like a crack through a wineglass, and it had come here anyway, wearing its king across a hundred miles of mountain, because a thing that feeds on vows cannot afford to ignore one being unmade at the source.

I heard it coming before I saw it. Footsteps in the Vault's entrance—not the king's gait. Edric of Thornfell, from everything Aldric had told me, walked like a man perpetually apologizing for the space he took up. These footsteps took the space and taxed it.

* * *

He came into the light wearing the king's body the way a man wears a coat cut for someone else. Edric's face—I'd seen it on coins, on wanted notices, once on a banner at a checkpoint where soldiers made farmers kneel and swear—Edric's face was wrong now. The mouth smiled too widely at rest. The eyes had the patient hunger of something that had waited three years for a meal and intended to savor it.

"You are the swordspeaker." The Regent's voice came out doubled, Edric's tenor underneath and something older above it, something with the texture of dry rot and old honey. "The last of the Order of the Singing Blade. Do you know how long I have wanted to meet one of you? Your order was the only honest thing this kingdom ever produced. Blades cannot flatter."

"I'm flattered," I said, and stood, and drew my sword.

The blade came out singing. That's the only word. Seven years I'd kept it muzzled with oil and silence and cheap scabbards, and now it rang in the Vault's dark like it had been saving the note the whole time. The Regent flinched—actually flinched, the king's shoulders drawing up—and I filed that away under *useful*.

"You should know," it said, recovering, gliding closer with that terrible unhurried patience, "that I am prepared to be generous. I have worn this man for three years. I know what he kept locked in his heart. He dreamed of restoring your order, swordspeaker. He wept for it, the night I first tasted him. So consider: I will give you your name back. Cleared, restored, written into the rolls with honors. I will give you your sisters' graves tended and their order refounded with royal charter. And I will give you—" it gestured, and the air between us filled with ghosts, Serah Voss's face young and laughing in some summer courtyard I'd thought forgotten, "—legitimacy. No more hiding. No more cheap steel. You would be the Voice of the Crown of Thornfell, and every oath sworn in this kingdom would pass through your lips and sing."

It let the vision hang there. It was good. I'll give it that—it was very good. I could smell the courtyard, the cut grass, the iron-and-rosemary smell of the practice yard. I could hear Serah laughing at some joke I'd made at nineteen, back when I was clever instead of careful.

"That's quite an offer," I said. "One question."

"Ask."

"When you showed me my mentor's grave just now—" I nodded at the fading ghost, "—you showed me the grave. Not her dying. Because you were there for the dying, weren't you? You wore someone that night too. A captain, maybe. Someone with orders to purge the Singing Blade." I watched the king's face do something complicated. "You didn't show me her death because you couldn't bear to watch yourself in it. Or because you liked watching. I genuinely can't tell which, and honestly, either answer is disqualifying."

The smile stayed. The warmth went out of it entirely, and what remained was the thing underneath, the parasite looking out through the king's eyes with no more pretense than a leech has.

"They never choose," it said. "In a thousand years, they never choose. They take the bargain, or they run, or they die clutching their little vows like children clutching blankets. Swear, swordspeaker. Or kneel and refuse, and I will wear this kingdom down to the bedrock and start again with the next generation. Oaths are patient. I am oaths."

"Yeah," I said. "Funny thing about that."

I turned to the witness-stone.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about renouncing the most important vow of your life: it doesn't feel like breaking. It feels like setting down something you forgot you were carrying.

I put my hand flat on the stone and it sang against my palm, my oath rising to meet me, bright and whole and seven years patient. *My voice to the blades and the blades to the people.* I had meant it so completely that night. I meant it still. That was the horror of it, and the point of it—you cannot freely break an oath you're glad to be rid of. The Regent's whole architecture was built on the certainty that no one would ever give up something they still loved, freely, knowing the cost, with no bargain offered and none accepted.

Aldric had understood it in his last hour. He'd broken his secret oath to the Regent knowing it would kill him, and it had—he'd bought me this hour with the only coin he had left, and I was not going to waste his death being clever about it.

"Serah," I said quietly. "Your last command was never to speak for crowns. I'm going to break a different rule instead. I hope that counts."

The Regent moved. Of course it moved—I'd known it would, had counted on it, needed those last few seconds of it lunging forward in the king's borrowed body with its hands out and its thousand-year hunger wide open, because a freely broken oath needs witnesses, and there was no witness in the world better qualified than the thing my broken vow would kill.

I spoke my oath aloud. All of it. Into the stone, into the Vault, into the reaching dark.

*"I, Beatrix Yarrow, swear my voice to the blades and the blades to the people, until the last sword forgets its making or I forget my name."*

The witness-stone blazed. Every thread of light in its surface stood up at once, and my oath rose out of the sediment of a thousand years like a fish rising through dark water, and the Regent stopped moving, because it recognized what was happening a half-second too late.

"No," it said, in both voices at once.

"And now," I said, and my voice cracked down the middle, and I let it, "because I am free, and free things may choose—I unsay it. I release my voice from the blades. I release the blades from me. I was sworn and I am done, and no one made me say this, and no one could have. I break it gladly. I break it grieving. I break it *free*."

The world came apart politely, which is somehow worse than loudly.

There was no explosion. There was a *note*—one vast, clean note, as if the first oath ever spoken had finally been allowed to finish exhaling—and in that note I felt my gift go out of me the way heat goes out of a forge when the bellows stop. Not torn. Released. The blade in my hand went suddenly, perfectly silent, and I felt the silence all the way down, a quiet where a song had lived since I was nineteen, and I understood in that instant exactly what I had paid, and I paid it anyway, and I did not take it back.

The Regent screamed with the king's throat and with a thousand other throats, all the voices it had ever worn, all the oaths it had ever eaten coming loose at once. It tore. That's the word I'll use in the histories, if anyone asks me to write them: it tore loose from King Edric of Thornfell across a hundred miles of mountain like a root ripped out of frozen ground, and the Vault convulsed around us, black stone groaning, the witness-stone cracking down the middle and then, gently, going dark—its work finished, its ledger closed.

What came out of the king was not a shape. It was a shape's absence, a hole in the air with appetite around the edges, and it thrashed against the walls of the world for one long moment—

—and then the note finished.

And it was gone. Unbound. Unmade, or scattered so fine that no prayer or scholar will ever gather it again. The air where it had been smelled, absurdly, of rain.

* * *

King Edric of Thornfell collapsed onto the floor of the Hollow Vault like a puppet whose strings had been cut, which was, in the fairest possible sense, exactly what he was.

I got to him before I'd decided to. Old habit—smugglers check the wounded, it's how you know who can pay you. He was breathing. His eyes opened, and they were just eyes, human and horrified, and I watched three years land on him all at once, every act of the Regent's tenancy arriving in his memory in a single unpayable installment.

"What did I do," he whispered. "Oh gods. What did I *do*. The ferry. The fells. The hunters—I signed the hunters—"

"Yes," I said. Because lying to kings seemed suddenly unnecessary, and also because I was very tired, and my sword was silent on my hip for the first time in my adult life, and my hands were shaking, and somewhere above us Aldric Vane lay dead under a sky he'd bought back for a kingdom that had stripped him of his name.

"The knight," the king said. "Vane. Where is—"

"Gone. He broke his oath to buy me the hour. It killed him. He knew it would." I sheathed the silent sword. It weighed the same. It weighed nothing at all. "He said you were worth it. I'm choosing to believe he was a better judge of character than I am, because frankly, right now, the bar is on the floor."

The king laughed—or sobbed; from where I stood the two sounded identical—and then he looked at me, really looked, the way men look at things they have just realized they owe everything to.

"You're her," he said. "The swordspeaker. You gave it up. I felt it go. I felt *everything* go." His voice broke. "What did it cost you?"

I thought about answering honestly. Then I thought about the road south, and the rowan tree I had a grave to dig, and the long walk out of Thornfell I could already feel beginning in my feet.

"Everything except the important part," I said. "Come on, majesty. We should get you out of here before the Vault finishes falling down. And then you and I are going to have a conversation about what you think you owe me—and I promise you, in advance, you're going to hate my price."

Because I already knew what I was going to ask for. And I already knew it was the one thing a king, having just gotten his soul back, would find hardest to give.

I wanted to be no one again. And this time, I intended to get it in writing—which, given recent history, struck even me as funny.

The Vault groaned overhead, shedding a thousand years of dust, and we walked out together into the rain.
