## 19. The King Waking

The first thing I knew was the taste of iron and candlewax, and the second was that my mouth hurt.

Not my throat—my *mouth*. The muscles of it. As though it had been smiling for three years without my permission.

I opened my eyes to the ceiling of my own bedchamber, which I recognized by the crack in the plaster shaped like a running stag, and I thought, with the peculiar calm of a man surfacing from deep water: *I have been gone a long time.*

Then the memories came, and the calm went out like a snuffed candle.

They did not arrive all at once. They arrived the way floodwater arrives—first a trickle under the door, then the whole wall of it. Three years of them. Every audience held in my name. Every decree signed by my hand while I watched from somewhere behind my own eyes, screaming into a silence so total it had weight. Every oath sworn in the great hall, every knee bent before me, every vow of fealty spoken over my outstretched hand—and every time, the thing wearing me had *drunk*. I felt it now in retrospect the way you feel a fever after it breaks: the hot pull of each oath passing through my flesh into something older and hungrier than any king.

I had been present for all of it. That is the part no one tells you about being possessed. You are not asleep. You are not dreaming. You are a guest at your own table, watching a stranger eat your food, kiss your wife's hand, sign your dead father's treaties, and smile your smile—the one my mother said looked like mine but kinder, gods rot her memory—with your mouth.

Three years. I remembered the taste of everything he'd made me eat.

I sat up. My body obeyed, and I flinched at how easily it obeyed, how smoothly, as if the machine of me had been well-oiled by use. A chamber pot. I made it. Barely. Then I knelt on the cold stone floor of the most luxurious room in Thornfell, king of nothing yet, shaking like a cut purse.

*You're awake,* said a voice behind me, and I nearly died of fright besides everything else.

My physician, Maester Corwin, stood in the doorway with a cup in his hand and an expression I had never seen on him before. He looked at me the way a man looks at a house fire that has suddenly, impossibly, gone out.

"How long," I rasped.

"Since the convulsion? An hour."

"Corwin." I gripped the bedframe. "How long since the *other* thing."

He set the cup down very carefully, the way men do when their hands are not steady enough for carelessness. "Your Majesty," he said, "you have been ill for three years. We thought—" He stopped. Started again. "There were days we thought you were recovering. Days you seemed more yourself. And then the fits would return."

Days I seemed more myself. Yes. The Regent had learned early that perfect stillness invited suspicion, so it had let *me* surface sometimes—a few hours, once half a day—like a drowning man allowed one breath so the watchers would go home satisfied. I had begged, those times, with my eyes alone, because my tongue belonged to him. I had begged my own queen with my eyes, and she had smiled back, believing, and walked away, and I had screamed after her inside a skull made of stone.

"Where is Lysse?" I asked. My wife. Queen Alyssa, whom the court called Lysse only when the doors were shut, and who had stopped shutting doors with me sometime in the second year because the thing wearing my face had begun to look at her the way a butcher looks at stock.

"The Queen Mother's apartments," Corwin said carefully. "She... Your Majesty, there will be much to discuss."

"There always is." I found my feet. Found the mirror.

I want to tell you I did not recognize myself. That would be cleaner. But the truth is worse: I recognized myself perfectly, and what I saw was a stranger wearing my recognition. Thinner. The lines around my mouth carved deep by three years of someone else's expressions. And the eyes—

The eyes were mine again. I checked. I checked four times, turning my face to the light like a woman counting coins, and each time they were mine, only mine, tired and frightened and forty-one years old.

But I knew they had not been. I knew what had looked out of them. And I knew—I knew with a certainty that lived in my marrow rather than my mind—that everyone who had knelt before those eyes for three years, every lord and clerk and child presented at court, had seen *him* looking back and called it their king.

* * *

They told me the story in pieces over the following days, and the pieces did not fit together neatly, which is how I knew most of them were true. Lies fit too well; truth comes to you like a dropped armful of kindling.

A swordspeaker—a woman named Yarrow, last survivor of the purged Order of the Oathbound—had reached some place called the Hollow Vault and done something there that no scholar could fully explain and none would stop trying to explain to me. She had broken an oath. Her own. Freely, they kept saying, *freely*, as though the adverb were the whole of the magic, and perhaps it was. The breaking had torn the parasite—the Regent, the scholars called it, born from the first oath ever sworn, fat on three years of compulsory fealty—from my body across a hundred miles of kingdom.

One hundred miles. It had held me from the Vault to the capital the way a man holds a fish on a line, and I had felt every yard.

And Ser Aldric Vane—my Vane, the knight whose doubt I had punished with disgrace because I lacked the courage to hear it aloud—had died buying her the hour she needed. Breaking his own secret oath in front of the Vault, knowing what breaking cost. Corwin told me this gently, as one delivers hard news, and I thanked him, and then I asked to be left alone, and then I was sick again, though not from any poison this time.

Here is what I learned about guilt in those first days: it keeps excellent records. Every crime the Regent committed with my hands, I could remember committing. Not the doing—I had been a prisoner for all of it—but the *witnessing*, and witnessing, I discovered, is its own sentence. When Lord Halloway's son was taken by the hunters for asking questions about grain shipments, I had watched through my own eyes. When the silver-collared clerks instituted the daily loyalty oaths and a miller in the low town refused and was made an example of—I was there. Smiling. When my old tutor, gray and bent and loyal past all sense, was seized for keeping banned books about the old orders, I signed the warrant myself, my hand moving in elegant strokes I had never learned, and I remembered the ink smell.

I remembered everything. That was the cruelty of my rescue. A man who wakes from possession with gaps can be pitied. A man who remembers every moment is simply guilty with better lawyers.

* * *

On the ninth day, I held court.

Not full court—I wasn't ready, and everyone knew it, and everyone pretended otherwise with the practiced grace of people who had spent three years pretending a great many things. But the council assembled in the map room, and I sat at the head of the table in my own chair, and Chancellor Berrow read the reports of the realm's recovery in a voice pitched for a sickbed.

The realm, it turned out, was like me. Upright, functioning, and wrong in ways that showed when the light moved.

The daily oaths had stopped—the clerks' silver collars went dark the hour the Regent died—but the habit of them hadn't. Men still touched their collars at doorways out of reflex. Lords still glanced at the throne before speaking, waiting for a permission that no longer came. And in the fells, the smuggler roads had gone quiet in a way that worried Berrow more than open rebellion would have. "They don't trust the quiet," he said. "Neither do I."

"What of the woman?" I asked. "Yarrow."

Berrow's quill stopped. Around the table, six faces performed careful neutrality, and failed at it in six different ways.

"She has declined," Berrow said at last, "to come to the capital."

"Declined."

"A summons was sent. Twice. Through proper channels." He hesitated. "The second summons was returned unopened, Your Majesty. With a note."

"Read it."

He read it. I will reproduce it here because I have written it out a dozen times since, trying to find the insult in it, or the threat, or anything except what it actually was:

*"Kings summon. I'm not coming. —B.Y."*

Someone coughed. Lord Maren studied the ceiling. And I laughed—actually laughed, a real one, the first in three years, and it startled me so badly I had to grip the armrests.

Because here was the thing, the thing that laughter cracked open: she was right. Kings *summon*. I had spent three years watching a monster wear kingship as a costume, and the costume's chief feature was that it never asked. It summoned. It commanded. It assumed obedience the way it assumed breathing, and every soul in that room—including me, including *especially* me—had spent years learning to be summoned.

"Send no third summons," I said.

Berrow blinked. "Your Majesty?"

"If I want to speak with Beatrix Yarrow, I will ask her. Politely. In writing, with my own hand, and she may decline that too." I looked around the table. "We are going to practice a new word in this room. It is 'please.' Someone write it down before we forget."

Maren actually did write it down. I chose to find that hopeful.

* * *

But wanting to speak with her was not the same as knowing what to say, and I am ashamed—genuinely, durably ashamed—of how long it took me to understand why she wouldn't come.

I drafted letters. Eleven of them. Corwin burned the bad ones for me, which was most of them, and he did it without comment, which was his particular kindness. In the first, I offered her a marshal's rank. In the second, land. In the third, I refounded her order on paper with a flourish of seals and realized, reading it over, that I had refounded it *under the crown*—that I could not seem to give a gift that was not also a chain. In the fourth I apologized. In the fifth I apologized better. In the sixth I explained, at length, that the purge of her order had happened under my grandfather and that I bore—

I stopped writing mid-sentence, because I heard how it sounded. *I bore no personal responsibility.* As though the crown were a coat I inherited and not a thing I wore into every hall where her order's absence was noticed. As though the hunters who served the Regent had not worn *my* livery for three years while I watched from inside my own skull. If I could claim innocence of the Regent's deeds, then my grandfather's ghost could claim innocence of the purge, and somewhere down the line the guilt evaporated entirely and we were all just decent men surrounded by regrettable weather.

Vane had understood this. That was his crime, really—not doubting the king, but doubting *kingship*, and saying so where I could hear it. I disgraced him for the sin of being right too early. And then he died correcting the crown's oldest error, freely, at the price of everything, and the least I could do was learn the lesson before the ink on his pardon dried.

I tore up the sixth letter. I wrote a seventh that said almost nothing:

*"Lady Yarrow. I owe you debts I cannot calculate and will not insult with offers. I ask one thing only: tell me what the kingdom owes, and I will pay it or die trying. There is no summons in this letter. Come or don't. Either way, the road out of Thornfell is yours, unwitnessed, for as long as you want it. —Edric, who was not, these three years, himself, but must answer for him anyway."*

I sealed it myself. No chancellery wax, no privy stamp. Just my seal and my hand and the strange lightness of asking instead of commanding.

* * *

She came. Of course she came—or came near enough. Word reached me within the week that a woman matching her description had entered the city through the south gate with a merchant caravan, registered at no inn anyone would admit to, and was, at that moment, drinking in a dockside tavern called the Drowned Bell where the wine was terrible and the discretion was expensive but available.

I should have waited. A wise king waits. I put on a plain cloak over plain clothes, took two guardsmen dressed even plainer, and went down to the docks like a man sneaking out of his own life—which, in fairness, I was.

The Drowned Bell smelled of tar, herring, and ambition. She sat in the corner with her back to the wall—of course she did—and a sword across her knees like a cat that might or might not be sleeping. She was smaller than the stories had made her, and younger, with the specific weathered look of someone who has spent seven years being nobody on purpose. When I approached her table, she didn't stand. Didn't bow. She moved her boot off the opposite chair, which I understood, in the grammar of places like that, to be roughly equivalent to a curtsy before the Grand Marshal of the Realm.

"Your Majesty," she said, dry as old rope. "You're shorter than your portrait."

"And you're ruder than your reputation, which I confess is a relief. May I sit?"

"That depends." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the scarred wood. "Are you sitting as the king, or as the man who was locked in the cupboard for three years while something wore his face?"

I pulled out the chair. "Is there a difference?"

"There used to be." She watched me sit with the flat attention of someone deciding whether to kill me, and I realized with a jolt that she was not being theatrical. She was genuinely weighing it. Seven years of hiding, a purge survived, a courier dying in her barn, a knight dead beside her in the dark—and here came the crown itself, walking toward her table in a borrowed cloak, and she had every reason in the world to believe crowns only ever wanted two things from people like her: their service or their silence.

"You watched," she said, before I'd settled. "All three years. Inside. Watching him use your hands."

"Yes."

"Do you know what that's worth to me?"

"I imagine nothing."

"It's worth everything," she said, and the flatness cracked, just once, just enough to show the wound beneath. "Because everyone else looks at me like I'm either a savior or a weapon, and you're the only man in this kingdom who knows what it is to be a passenger in your own story." She drank. Set the cup down. "So no, Majesty. I don't hate you. I've thought about it. Hate would be easier, but it doesn't fit, and I've stopped carrying things that don't fit."

"I'm refounding your order," I said. "The writ is drawn. Marshal's rank, lands, a seat at council—"

"No."

"—and I haven't finished offering—"

"I know exactly what you're offering. You're offering me everything I lost." She leaned forward, and her voice stayed level but something underneath it rang, faint and clear, the way a blade rings when tapped. "Everything except the reason I lost it. Your grandfather didn't purge my order because we were dangerous, Majesty. He purged us because we were *evidence*. Every sword that sings remembers every oath ever sworn to the crown—including the ugly ones. You cannot give me back my order, because the moment my sisters and I start listening to blades again, we'll start hearing things the throne paid to forget. You'd be arming your own conscience and calling it a gift."

The tavern noise went on around us—dice, argument, a fiddle tuning—and I sat in the middle of it feeling the exact shape of what she'd said. Hollow victory, Corwin had called my waking, and I'd resented the phrase. Now I held it in my hands and found it weighed true.

"So what do you want?" I asked. "Name it. Truly name it."

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside, gulls argued over the harbor refuse, and somewhere a bell counted the hour, and I understood that whatever she said next would define my reign more than any decree I would ever sign.

"I want the road out of Thornfell," she said. "Unwitnessed. No escort, no record, no name in your books. And I want one promise—that when the next king's counselors get nervous about swords that remember, somebody in this palace remembers what it cost to break the first oath. That's all. That's the whole bill."

"That's not all," I said. "That's the beginning of a reign's worth of work. But yes. It's yours."

She rose, slung the sword across her back, and paused. For a moment her hand drifted to the hilt—an unconscious gesture, a woman touching an old scar—and I swear on my father's grave that the blade hummed. Faintly. Almost nothing. Like a struck bell remembering its note.

Her eyebrows rose. So did mine.

"Well," she said, to the sword or to me or to neither, "isn't *that* interesting."

And she walked out of the Drowned Bell and out of my reign's easy answers, leaving me with a cold cup, a hollow crown, and the sudden, urgent certainty that I needed to know—before she vanished unwitnessed beyond my borders—what in the gods' names my grandfather's purge had *missed*.
