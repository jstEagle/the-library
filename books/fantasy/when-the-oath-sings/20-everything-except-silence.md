## 20. Everything Except Silence

The throne room of Thornfell smelled of beeswax and lilies, which told me everything I needed to know about how the last three years had been cleaned up.

They'd scrubbed the Regent out of the stones the way you scrub blood out of floorboards—thoroughly, repeatedly, with a kind of desperate diligence that only makes the stain more obvious to anyone who knows what they're looking at. The banners were new. The clerks' silver collars were gone; the clerks themselves had been reassigned, or fled, or quietly buried under other paperwork. Even the light through the great windows seemed laundered, gold filtered through fresh glass where the old panes had gone dark with whatever the parasite had sweated into the air.

And on the throne sat King Edric of Thornfell, awake in his own body for the first time in three years, looking like a man who had come home to find his house had been lived in by something with different manners.

He was thinner than his coins made him look. That was the first thing. The face on the stamps and the seals and the shrine-candles was a broad, confident face, a king's face, and the man wearing it had hollows under his cheekbones deep enough to hold shadow. His hands rested on the arms of the throne the way hands rest on a railing over a long drop.

I knew that grip. I'd worn it myself for seven years.

"Beatrix Yarrow." My name came out of him carefully, each syllable set down like a cup he was afraid of chipping. "You are a difficult woman to thank."

"I've been told gratitude is best delivered in small denominations," I said. "Preferably portable."

Aldric would have winced. Aldric wasn't there, which was the whole reason my mouth had gotten so bold lately. Grief does that. It takes away the one person whose opinion kept your wit fed and housed, and suddenly it's running loose in the streets saying whatever it likes.

The king didn't smile. I respected that. A man who'd spent three years as a puppet shouldn't be expected to find anything funny for a while yet.

"Marshal Yarrow," he said instead.

There it was. He said it the way a fisherman says *here, fish*—patiently, with bait already threaded on the hook.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"Your Majesty, I've heard terms before. From a smuggler queen, from a parasite wearing your face, from my own sword on a night I still don't like discussing. Terms are just gifts wearing armor." I looked around the throne room, at all that new glass and old stone. "And I've learned what happens when I accept them."

The chamberlain behind him made a sound like a swallowed cough. The guards along the wall did their professional best to become furniture. Kings don't get refused often, I gathered. Or rather, kings get refused plenty, but usually from farther away, and usually followed by consequences.

Edric studied me. It was an honest look, which after everything was almost shocking. No second voice underneath it. No hunger coiled beneath the vowels. Just a tired man in a borrowed-clean room, trying to do one right thing out of the thousand wrong ones done with his hands.

"The Order of the Quill and Edge is refounded as of this morning," he said. "By decree. Your order, restored. Your name cleared—the purge declared unlawful, the warrants struck from record. Lands in the eastern fells for a chapterhouse. And a marshal's rank with a seat at this court, because this kingdom nearly died of not listening to swordspeakers, and I intend never to be deaf in that ear again."

It was, I'll grant him, a good offer. It was the offer I'd have written for myself if I'd been drafting the scene, back when I still drafted scenes with me in them. Seven years ago I'd have knelt so fast I'd have bruised both knees. Seven years ago I wanted nothing in the world so much as to stop being invisible—to stand in a hall like this one and have my gift named aloud without the word *heresy* attached.

But seven years ago I hadn't stood in a cathedral of black stone and watched every vow ever sworn to a crown hang in the air like smoke, feeding something ancient and patient. I hadn't held a dying man while he broke his own oath to buy me an hour, and felt the breaking go through him like a blade going through silk.

I hadn't learned that oaths are doors, and crowns are very good at walking through them.

"Your Majesty," I said, "do you remember what the Regent ate?"

His jaw tightened. Somewhere behind us, a clerk's quill stopped moving.

"It ate vows," I went on, because apparently I was going to be thorough whether I liked it or not. "Every oath sworn in your name. Every knee bent, every hand raised, every *so help me* and *by the crown* and *I swear fealty*—it drank them all, and it grew, and it wore you. Now you want to refill the well. You want to hand me a marshal's rank, which comes with an oath of service, and refound an order, which comes with vows of its own, and seat me at a court where every day some clerk will want my signature and my sworn word." I shook my head. "You're offering me a feast and asking why I won't sit down at the table. Your Majesty. I've seen what sits under that table."

The silence that followed had weight. Good silence. Honest silence. The kind of silence, I thought absurdly, that a sword makes when it's deciding whether to sing.

"The purge killed forty-one of us," I said, quieter now. "I know. I read the witness-stone. Their names are all in there, every one, recorded the day they swore and again the day they died. If you want to honor them, keep the order's records. Teach its history. Let young people learn what happened when a crown decided a gift was a threat." I paused. "Don't rebuild the thing that made us worth killing."

Edric looked at his own hands then, for a long moment. When he raised his head, something in his face had settled—not peace, exactly. More like the particular exhaustion of a man who has finally asked the right question and received an answer he can't argue with.

"What will you take, then?" he asked. "Everyone takes something. Refusal with no price is just pride, and pride is a debt too."

*Pride is a debt too.* I turned that over and found it true, which annoyed me. Fine. One thing, then. One small thing, the smallest thing a kingdom could give.

"A road," I said.

He blinked. Of all the responses he'd rehearsed—and I could see the rehearsed ones stacked behind his eyes like cordwood—that wasn't among them.

"There's a road south out of Thornfell," I said. "Smuggler's road, mostly. It crosses the passes below Greywater and runs down past the rowan stands toward the border. I want it unwitnessed. No toll records. No checkpoint logs. No name entered anywhere in any ledger that connects Beatrix Yarrow to the southbound passes." I shrugged. "Not erased—I'm not asking you to falsify anything. Just... let the road forget me. Can a king make a road forget?"

Something moved across Edric's face that might, in a healthier year, have become a smile.

"That," he said slowly, "is the strangest request ever put to the crown of Thornfell. And I include the time my grandfather asked a dragon to co-sign a trade treaty."

"Did it?"

"He did. The treaty's held for sixty years." He rose from the throne—stiffly, a man relearning his own body's geometry—and descended the two steps to stand level with me. Up close he smelled of soap and fever-sweat. Recovering kings, it turns out, smell like recovering everyone else. "You understand what you're refusing. Lands. Rank. Legal existence. You're asking me to make you a ghost."

"I spent seven years being a ghost," I said. "Turns out I'm good at it. What I'm bad at is being a monument."

For a moment his hand twitched, as if it wanted to rest on my shoulder, knight-to-knight, king-to-subject. Then he remembered—or I watched him decide—that he was no longer entitled to casual touches, that his body had done things with his hands he couldn't take back, and the hand returned to his side. I found I respected that more than the marshal's rank.

"The road will forget you," King Edric said. "On my word—which, I'm told, means something again. Though I notice you haven't asked for it."

"Don't swear anything on my account, Your Majesty," I said. "Not today. Maybe not for a while. Let your oaths heal. All of them." I glanced at the throne, at the room, at the whole humming apparatus of a kingdom learning to breathe unparasitized air. "Consider it medical advice."

* * *

They gave me a guest chamber in the east wing, because apparently even ghosts get bedded down properly in Thornfell, and I lay on a mattress far too soft for a woman of my recent habits and stared at a ceiling painted with constellations some dead court artist had invented, because real astronomy lacked drama.

Sleep was not forthcoming. Sleep and I had been estranged since the Vault.

So I did what I always did when the night got loud. I got up, crossed to where my sword leaned against the washstand—old habit, keeping it close, though these days it was less a weapon than a headstone—and sat on the floor beside it with my back against the cold plaster wall.

"You still there?" I asked it. Quietly. The way you talk to graves.

Nothing. Of course nothing. In the Vault I had spoken my founding oath aloud into the witness-stone—all of it, every word, the vow I'd taken at nineteen with my mentor's hand on my shoulder and my whole stupid shining future ahead of me: *to speak for the blades, and only for the blades, and never for a crown.* And then I had renounced it. Freely. Completely. Unmade the pattern the way the pattern itself demanded, and felt the Regent tear loose from the king across a hundred miles of kingdom, and felt my gift gutter out with it like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

That was the price. That was always going to be the price. Aldric died knowing it and gave his hour anyway, and I would not insult him by pretending I hadn't known what I was buying.

Seven years of hiding the gift, and then one hour of using it, and then nothing. Silence. Actual, earned, permanent silence—the thing I'd wanted since the purge, except wanting it had turned out to feel nothing like having it. There's a difference, I was learning, between a door you can't open and a door you've bricked shut yourself. One is a cage. The other is just a wall you keep stubbing your toe on.

I laid my palm flat on the scabbard. Old leather, worn smooth at the grip where my hand had lived for a decade. Cheap steel, I'd always claimed. A smuggler's blade, nothing special, chosen precisely because nothing special draws no questions.

The steel hummed.

Faint. So faint I felt it in my palm before I heard it in my ears—a single low note, like a wineglass remembering being struck, like wind finding the crack under a door. Not the old singing. The old song had been words, my order's words, oaths rising off steel like heat off summer stone. This was smaller. Changed. But alive.

I snatched my hand back and sat there in the dark with my heart going like a smuggler caught at a checkpoint.

"No," I told it. "No, we agreed. We settled accounts. I paid."

The hum didn't answer, because it wasn't speech anymore. Whatever had lived in my blade—the oath-magic, the swordspeech, the whole inheritance of my murdered order—had gone into the witness-stone and stayed there. What remained was something else. Something simpler. A resonance, maybe. An echo that had decided, against all accounting, to keep echoing.

Or maybe— and here I sat very still, because the thought arrived with the weight of a fact—maybe a freely broken oath doesn't destroy a gift. Maybe it *changes* it. Strips it down to what it should have been all along. The Regent fed on vows sworn to power. My old gift sang the oaths of others. But a gift born free, broken free, given freely back...

My old blade hummed once more, soft as breath, and went quiet.

"Well," I said to the empty room, in the tone of a woman renegotiating with the universe and expecting to lose. "That's going to complicate the invisibility."

I didn't sleep after that either. But it was a different kind of not-sleeping.

* * *

I left at dawn, because dawn is when walls change shifts and sentries are at their most philosophically flexible, and because I'd promised myself I wouldn't do goodbyes. Goodbyes are witnesses. I'd had enough witnessing to last several lifetimes.

The king had kept his strange little word. At the south gate, the sergeant of the watch looked at my travel papers, looked at me, looked at the papers again, and then performed the single most elaborate act of not-seeing I have ever been party to. He checked the seal twice, frowned at the weather, waved through a turnip cart, and somehow in all that motion never quite registered the armed woman walking out the postern with a sword on her hip. The ledger on his desk stayed open to a blank line.

The road south took me through the lower city, where morning was doing its ordinary miracles—bread smells, argument, a child chasing a dog that had clearly won this game before. New posters on the customs house wall, ink barely dry: *THE CROWN THANKS ITS FAITHFUL.* Someone had already amended one in charcoal: *AND WHAT DID IT COST?* Nobody had scraped it off yet. Small mercies. Small truths, surviving in margins, which is where they usually live.

At the last bend before the fields opened up, I stopped.

There was a bench there. An ordinary bench, lichen-spotted, facing the road. And on it, someone had left flowers—rowan sprigs, white berries, tied with grey thread. Fresh. Cut within the day.

I stood there longer than I meant to.

I hadn't told anyone where I'd bury him. I hadn't told anyone *that* I'd buried him. But word travels the way water travels, finding every crack downhill, and somewhere between the Underfell and the capital, the story must have passed from mouth to mouth: the knight who broke his oath to save the swordspeaker. The knight who died free.

Someone had cut rowan for him. Someone who never met him would remember him in the tree that keeps off witches.

"Sentimental country," I said, and my voice cracked on the middle word, and I let it, because there was no one to witness it but a bench and a road that had promised to forget me.

Then I walked on, and the road, true to its word, forgot me.

* * *

Three days south, in the high pass where Thornfell starts letting go of you, I camped under an overhang and finally drew the sword all the way out of its scabbard for the first time since the throne room.

Moonlight ran down the blade. Plain steel. Scratches from the ferry, from Greywater Bridge, from the dark of the Underfell where I'd unmasked the impostor by the simple expedient of refusing to sing for a liar. A working sword's history, written in nicks.

I held it flat on my palms, the way I'd once held it in a barn while a dead courier's map burned patterns into my sleep, and waited.

The hum came. Soft. Low. Not words—but not nothing. It rose and fell the way a voice rises and falls when it's *almost* saying something, when meaning sits just behind a door you can hear breathing through.

I listened until the moon moved. Then I laughed—one short, rusty sound, startling a bird off the rocks above.

Because I understood it, finally. Understood what the breaking had left me. My old gift had been a translator: it spoke other people's oaths, carried other people's vows, served the memory of every promise ever made to power. That gift was dead, and good riddance, and may it rest in the witness-stone where young knights will learn from it.

What remained was smaller and truer. The blade couldn't sing oaths anymore.

It could only sing *truth.*

Lies near it would fall silent forever—I'd proven that in the dark of the Underfell with blood on my hands. But truth, honestly spoken, freely given, unsworn and unowned? That, the steel would carry. Quietly. On its own terms. For no crown, no order, no contract. Just because some things deserve to resonate.

A guide who never swears oaths, the rumors would say, once she got south. A nameless woman whose word, strangely, no one ever doubts. Who needs no contracts, because her blade won't abide her lying, and everyone can somehow tell.

"Not invisibility," I said to the sword, and the hum warmed a fraction, like agreement, like thanks. "Fine. Visibility. But on my terms, and nobody else's. That's the deal. Take it or haunt someone else."

The blade hummed. It sounded, if I'm honest, amused.

I slid it home, kicked dirt over the fire, and stood looking south down the long grey ribbon of the pass, where the border waited and beyond it every road I hadn't walked yet. Behind me: a kingdom that owed me everything and understood, to its credit, that I'd rather collect in silence. Ahead: nothing certain, nothing sworn, nothing witnessed.

Everything, in other words, except silence.

I shouldered my pack and started walking, and somewhere below, faint as memory, the road began to sing.
