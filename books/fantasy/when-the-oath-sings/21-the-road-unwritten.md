## 21. The Road Unwritten

The rowan tree stood alone on the shoulder of the fell, the way Aldric would have wanted it—apart, watchful, a little stubborn about the weather.

I dug the grave myself. Not because there was no one to help; the smuggler queen's people had offered, in the flat, transactional way of people settling a ledger. But some debts you don't hand to clerks, and some graves you don't outsource. I'd carried him three days down from the Vault with my arms screaming and my gift gone quiet as a dead hearth, and I intended to see the work through to the end. The last thing I did for Ser Aldric Vane would be done with my own hands, or not at all.

The ground fought me. Mountain soil in autumn is half stone and half grudge, and my spade rang off rock more often than it bit. I didn't mind. There is a particular mercy in labor that hurts—it gives the grief somewhere to go besides the throat.

He looked smaller than he had in life. They always do, I suppose, once the oath goes out of them. In life Aldric had been a big man in the way that oaks are big—not showy, just *present*, taking up the space he'd earned. Stripped of his knighthood, stripped of his armor, stripped at the last of even his secret vow, he had still somehow filled every room he stood in. Now he was just a tired body in a borrowed shroud, and I found I was angry about that. Furious, actually, in the cold way that has nothing to do with shouting.

"You could have let me do it," I told him. "You could have let *me* break something for once. I had a whole speech prepared."

The wind moved through the rowan's leaves. Rowans have small leaves, and when the wind goes through them they make a sound like a crowd whispering, like a thousand people deciding not to say something. I took it for agreement. I was in no state to be rigorous about my evidence.

I laid him down and filled the grave in, and I did not swear anything over him. That was the whole point of him, after all. The whole point of me, now, too. Instead I said, plainly, in the voice of a woman talking to a man who couldn't answer:

"You were the best liar I ever met, because every lie you told was to yourself, and you broke all of them in the end. That's more than most honest men manage."

Then I set the marker stone. No name. He'd asked for that, in his last lucid hour, gripping my wrist with a hand already going cold. *"No name on it. Names are how the Regent found me. Let the stone stay stupid."* So the stone stayed stupid, and I promised it nothing, and somewhere beneath it Aldric Vane slept the first dreamless sleep he'd had in seven years.

I took the rowan berries—bright red, bitter as a bad winter—and put a handful on the stone. Superstition, my mentor would have said. My mentor had also said *never speak for a crown again*, and I'd managed to honor that only by breaking the greatest oath of my life, so I'd stopped treating her pronouncements as gospel and started treating them as advice from a woman who'd been tired.

Then I shouldered my pack, checked the blade at my hip out of seven years of habit, and walked south.

* * *

The blade hummed.

I want to be precise about this, because precision matters to me and because no one would believe me otherwise. It did not sing. Singing was what it had done before—swordspeech, the memory of oaths, the whole forbidden choir of it. That was gone. I had fed it into the unbinding along with my vow, my name in the order, and a fair portion of my blood, and I had watched the light go out of it in the Vault like a lamp being shuttered.

But a hum. Faint. Persistent. The way a hearthstone stays warm two days after the fire dies.

I noticed it first on the third day south, in a smuggler's inn with water-stained walls and a landlady who charged by the lie rather than the night. I'd taken the blade off to clean it—habit, again, the small liturgies of a life I no longer had—and when I set it on the table it made a sound. Low. Almost below hearing. The kind of sound you feel in your teeth before your ears admit to it.

I put my palm flat against the flat of it.

Nothing came. No memory, no voice, no oath rising up like water from a well. Just the hum, and beneath the hum, if I pressed hard and listened with whatever was left of the thing I used to be—a sense of *attention*. As if the blade were a dog that had gone deaf but still turned its head at the vibration of footsteps.

"Well," I said to it. "You're either dying slowly or learning to be something new. Welcome to the club."

The landlady looked over. "Talking to your sword, love?"

"Negotiating."

"Who's winning?"

"It's holding out for back pay."

She laughed and brought me stew, and I paid her an honest price for once, because I'd decided somewhere on the road that I was done with the economy of lies. Not a grand moral reformation—I still had a forged bill of lading in my pack and three names I answered to—but I'd spent seven years shrinking, and the unbinding had burned most of what I'd been shrinking *from*. What was left felt too small to waste on cowardice.

That was the other thing the Vault had left me, besides the hum and the scars and the dreams where a voice in the king's accent offered me back my whole life: a hatred of smallness. I had made an art of being invisible. I'd been *good* at it. And the first oath I'd ever spoken had been, at its heart, a vow to be seen—to be the blade's voice, to speak for steel the way priests speak for gods. The Regent had known that, the bastard. That's why the temptation worked. It hadn't offered me power. It had offered me *permission*.

I'd rather have the invisibility, some nights. Invisibility was safe. But I'd buried a man under a rowan tree who'd taught me, in the worst possible way, what safety costs when you buy it with silence. So: the roads south, the honest price at the inn, the blade humming its half-heard hymn. The person I was becoming, one unwitnessed day at a time.

* * *

The courier's map rode in my pack, wrapped in oilcloth, and I told myself it was practical. Reference material. The Underfell passages, the Hollow Vault's approaches, the smuggler roads with their tolls and blind corners annotated in a dead man's careful hand.

I told myself a lot of things about that map.

The truth was that it was the last thing a dying stranger had pressed into my hands in a barn that smelled of hay and blood, gasping that *the crown eats him*, and that everything since—the hunters, the ferry, Aldric, the Vault, the king waking screaming in a body that wasn't only his anymore—had grown out of that one wax-sealed moment like a tree out of a dropped seed. You don't throw away the seed. Not even when the tree it grew was mostly grief.

Some nights I took it out and read the marginalia by candle stub. The courier had been thorough in the way of men who know they might not live to explain things twice. Little notes in the margins: *toll house burns its own ledgers quarterly—ask for Maret, trust the daughter instead.* *Underfell, day two: do not answer any voice that knows your mother's name.* *If captured, swear nothing. Swearing nothing is the whole war.*

*Swearing nothing is the whole war.* I'd laughed at that, the night after I read it, alone in a waystation with my dead gift and my dead friend and my half-alive sword. The courier had written it as tradecraft. I'd lived it as a eulogy.

I was re-reading that margin in a border town called Fetter's Cross—named, the locals claimed, for a chain the old kings once strung across the valley to charge travelers for the privilege of being left alone; the chain was gone but the charging remained—when I first heard the rumors.

The inn was called the Broken Vow, because border towns have a sense of humor like a boot to the ribs. I was three cups into bad wine and minding my own business, which is a full-time occupation, when two carters at the next table started talking about the roads south of the fells.

"—no, the *other* one. The guide. Woman, they say, or maybe not, depends who's telling it. Takes parties through the passes the tollmen can't touch. Never gets lost, never gets robbed, and here's the thing—" he leaned in, and I leaned my wine cup closer out of pure professional interest, "—she never swears. Not on a contract, not on a god, not on her mother's grave. You ask her to swear the job'll be done honest, and she just looks at you. Like you've asked her to eat something dead."

His companion snorted. "And people hire her?"

"Queues for her, mate. There's folk down in the valley now saying an unsworn guide is the only honest kind left. After the whole—after the king thing, after what came out about the oaths and the clerks and all." He shuddered. "My cousin was in the capital when it happened. Says the whole city heard something *break*. Like a bell the size of the sky, cracking down the middle. Half of them fell down. And ever since, people look at you strange when you swear an oath. Like they're waiting to see what crawls out of it."

I kept my face neutral and my eyes on my wine, which is a skill I'd kept sharp even when everything else went into the Vault and didn't come back.

"Where does this guide operate?" the companion asked.

"Everywhere and nowhere. Never the same road twice. Never the same name." The carter shrugged. "They call her the Quiet One, up in the high passes. Down south they say the Nameless Guide. My cousin's friend hired her once—says she talked to her sword the whole journey. Just talk, mind, like to a horse. But the sword—" he dropped his voice, and I confess I did too, "—the sword *answered*. A little. A hum, like. Like it was listening."

I paid for my wine and left before my face could do anything unprofessional.

Outside, the night was cold and enormous, and the mountains behind me were black shapes against a sky full of indifferent stars. I stood in the street of Fetter's Cross and laughed until my ribs hurt—not the good kind of laugh, but the kind that's grief with its coat turned inside out. A legend already. Seven days dead as a swordspeaker and already a legend, and the legend was wrong in every particular except the hum, and the hum was the part I understood least myself.

"They're calling you a ghost," I told the blade at my hip.

The hum rose, just slightly. Just enough.

"I know," I said. "I felt it too."

* * *

I took work, that winter, because a woman has to eat and because walking is only freedom if you're choosing the road, not fleeing down it.

Guide work came naturally. I knew the passes the way my mentor had known her oaths—intimately, bitterly, with all their betrayals memorized. And it turned out that a guide who swears nothing is worth double, in a country where every oath had recently been revealed as a leash. Merchants paid me to move silk past the tollmen. A widow paid me to carry her to her sister's grave in the high fells without a single vow of safe-conduct, because she'd watched her husband swear loyalty to the crown in the capital and come home *wrong*, and she never wanted to see a sworn face again. A young clerk, one of the silver-collared ones, fleeing south with a satchel of the Regent's old contract-ledgers, paid me in coin and confession both.

I never swore to any of them. I gave them my word instead, plainly, and explained the difference: *"An oath binds you. A word is just me, choosing, every day, to be worth trusting. If you need chains, hire someone else."*

They all hired me anyway. Word of the unsworn guide spread faster than I walked, which is the way of legends—they travel light and arrive early. By spring there were stories I hadn't earned. That I'd killed the Regent with my bare hands. That I was the king's secret sister. That I was dead, and this was my ghost walking the passes collecting debts. That one at least had the advantage of explaining the sword.

The sword. Through all of it, the hum.

It changed, slowly. In the autumn it had been a hearthstone's warmth, a memory of fire. By spring it was something more like a pulse. And once—only once, on a night I'll tell you about, on a night I'm telling you about now because this is the only place I can tell it—I drew it.

Bandits, on the Salt Road. Six of them, professional, well-armed, and under the mistaken impression that an unsworn woman traveling alone was an unclaimed purse. I'd killed before—once at the ferry, horribly, and the blade had screamed every death to the sky. I drew now out of necessity, and the old fear rose in me like floodwater: *it will sing, they'll know, they'll—*

It didn't scream.

It hummed. It hummed *loud*, a low bronze note that I felt in my spine and my teeth and somewhere behind my sternum, and the bandits heard it too, and I watched six grown killers take one look at a woman with a softly ringing sword and decide, collectively and immediately, that the purse was not unclaimed after all. They ran. I stood in the road shaking, not from the fight—from the sound.

Because I knew that note. Underneath everything, underneath the hum and the silence and the seven years and the broken vow, I knew it. It was the sound my blade had made the first time I'd ever spoken to it, a girl of fifteen in a snowbound smithy, before any order, before any oath, before the crown or the Regent or any of it. The sound of a blade *noticing* me.

The oath had been the crown's theft, laid over the top. The gift underneath—the gift itself, the plain and ancient fact that some blades and some people recognize each other—that had never belonged to the Regent. It couldn't be unbound. It had only been waiting for the vow to get out of its way.

I sheathed the sword and stood in the dark on the Salt Road and cried like a child, and then I laughed, and then I did both at once, which is I think what they call mourning braided with hope, and I didn't care who saw. There was no one to see. There never is. That's the arrangement.

* * *

Spring came to the fells the way it always does—reluctantly, in patches, with frost still biting the north faces while the south slopes went green out of sheer stubbornness. I passed through a valley where they were rebuilding a waystation the hunters had burned, and the workers asked me to bless the foundation. I told them I didn't bless things.

"Then what do you do?" asked a boy of maybe twelve, holding a hammer like it was a scepter.

"I witness them," I said. "There's a difference. A blessing promises. A witness just *remembers*." I put my hand on the blackened foundation stone. "This station stood. It was burned. It will stand again. That's all true, and now it's been said out loud, and that's worth more than a blessing, because it can't be taken back."

The boy thought about that with his whole face, which is a thing children can still do. "Did you used to be somebody?" he asked.

"Everyone used to be somebody."

"No, I mean—" he waved the hammer at the mountains, at the roads, at the general direction of legend. "The Quiet One. The one who never swears. My gran says she broke the biggest oath in the world and it *broke the sky*. Is that true? Did you see her?"

I looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere in my pack, wrapped in oilcloth, the courier's map weighed nothing and everything. Somewhere at my hip, the blade hummed its patient half-song. Somewhere south, a road waited that I hadn't walked yet, and north, behind me, a king was learning to rule a kingdom that had heard its own oaths crack, and a witness-stone in a rebuilt Vault was teaching children what vows cost.

"Not her," I said. "But I knew her work."

He nodded solemnly, satisfied, and went back to hammering, and I went back to the road, and that was the whole of it. That was everything. A woman walking south with a dead man's map and a half-alive sword and no name she'd swear to, carrying a broken oath the way you carry a scar: not as a wound anymore, but as proof the wound survived.

* * *

I camped that night under a rowan tree. I didn't plan it. The tree planned it, if trees plan—there it was at the edge of a meadow, alone, stubborn, red-berried, and I looked at it and my feet simply stopped.

I made a small fire and ate bread and hard cheese and drank the last of a bottle I'd been saving for no occasion in particular, and I decided this was the occasion. I talked to Aldric for a while. Not a prayer—I don't pray, and he'd have hated the posture of it. Just talk. Report of the road. The Salt Road bandits, the boy with the hammer, the legend growing like moss on everything I did. I told him the sword had hummed for the bandits and he'd have laughed at that, his big stupid warm laugh, and said something like *"even the steel's gone soft on you, Yarrow."*

"I'm keeping the map," I told the fire. "You'd tell me that's sentiment. You'd be right. I'm doing it anyway. A woman should carry one thing that isn't useful."

The rowan leaves whispered their thousand-voiced whisper. The fire settled. Far off, a fox screamed at something, or the mountains did, it's hard to tell the difference at night.

And then—because the night was quiet, and the wine was done, and the hum at my hip had risen to something almost like attention—I drew the blade and laid it across my knees and said, out loud, to the steel and the tree and the sleeping south:

"I don't know what you're becoming. I don't know what I am. But I'm not swearing anything, so whatever comes next, it comes because I choose it. Daily. Unwitnessed. That's the whole arrangement."

The hum answered.

Not words. Not yet—maybe never, and maybe that's right, and maybe the gift I'm growing into is quieter than the one I lost, a listening instead of a speaking, a witness instead of a voice. But it answered, a low bronze note that rose and held and softened, and if you'd asked me then what it sounded like, I'd have told you the truth:

It sounded like an oath.

But freely given. Freely kept. Freely, if it ever needed to be, broken.

I slept well for the first time in a year, and in the morning I walked south, and the road unwrote itself ahead of me mile by mile, and I let it.

Behind me, far to the north, in a Vault being rebuilt by hands that remembered what it held, a witness-stone waited with a new entry, and children were already being brought to hear it sung. Ahead of me, the passes dropped toward the lowlands, toward the border, toward whatever kingdom or wilderness came next.

And on the road between, a nameless guide walked with a humming sword, and every few miles, when the wind was right, she let it sing.
