## 22. When the Oath Sings

Every story ends twice.

Once when the last blow falls, and once when someone finally writes down what it meant. The first ending happened in the Hollow Vault, in the dark, in blood and light, witnessed only by a stone and a dying man and a woman who would not speak of it for years afterward. The second ending is this one, and it begins not in the Vault at all, but in a schoolroom—because that is where all oaths go to live forever, whether they want to or not.

The schoolroom was carved into the rebuilt Vault's upper gallery, which was itself carved into the black stone of the Underfell, which was itself older than any kingdom, any crown, any word anyone had ever sworn in earnest or otherwise. The architect had wanted to build around the witness-stone. The masons had refused. You do not wall off a thing like that, they said. You give it air.

So the stone stood in its chamber at the heart of everything, tall as two men and pale as bone, veined now with a single thread of gold that ran through it like a pulse. Before, its veins had been silver—the color of compelled vows, of oaths taken because refusing meant the collar or the rope. The gold thread was new. It had appeared the morning after the Unbinding, and no one could explain it, though everyone tried.

*It's the sound,* said old Magister Corvel, who had survived three purges by being too useful to kill and too stubborn to die. *Oaths have always sung in there. We just stopped listening. Silver sings flat. Gold sings true.*

He was mostly right, which for Corvel was an excellent day.

* * *

On the morning our story properly closes, twelve students sat on benches before the stone, and none of them fidgeted, which was remarkable, because students have fidgeted before witness-stones since witness-stones were invented.

They were not knights yet. That was rather the point. After the Regent, Thornfell had rewritten everything about how oaths were made—and more importantly, how they were unmade. No child swore loyalty anymore. No clerk pressed silver to any throat. A vow in Edric's kingdom now required three things: silence before it, a witness who could refuse it, and a door left open behind it.

The teacher that morning was a knight named Ser Wren Halloway, twenty-six years old, with a burn scar on her left forearm from a fire she had started herself, years ago, burning nothing, keeping a map. She did not talk about that. She talked about the stone.

"Who can tell me what we're going to hear today?"

"A broken oath," said a boy in the front row, too quickly, the way boys say things when they want to be first and correct.

Wren shook her head. "Listen again. Anyone."

"An *unbound* oath," said a girl near the back, hesitantly. "Magister Corvel says a broken oath still belongs to whoever broke it. An unbound one belongs to itself."

Corvel, seated in the corner where he pretended to doze and never actually did, opened one eye. "Give the girl a ribbon," he said.

"That's the whole lesson," Wren told them, "so hold onto it. Seven years ago, there was a woman—" she paused, as she always paused here, because the pause was part of the teaching, "—who made a vow when she was younger than any of you. It was a good vow. Sworn freely, kept faithfully, at terrible cost. And in the end she had to break it—not because it failed, but because it succeeded so completely that something ancient learned to feed on the shape of it. Do you understand? The parasite didn't eat bad oaths. It ate *good* ones. It ate them precisely because they were worth keeping."

The students were quiet. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the shafts cut into the mountain, and the whole Vault breathed.

"The woman's name isn't recorded," Wren continued. "She asked that it not be. What *is* recorded is what she did in this room, or what stood in this room before this room existed. She spoke her vow aloud one last time, and then she renounced it—freely, fully, with no one forcing her hand and no reward waiting on the far side of the words. And when she finished, the stone learned a new song."

She rose and laid her palm against the base of the witness-stone, in the place where generations of teachers had laid their palms, worn smooth now as a river pebble.

"It only sings if you ask it honestly," she said. "So ask honestly."

Twelve voices, some steady and some not: *"Stone, what do you remember?"*

The gold thread kindled.

And the Vault sang.

* * *

You should understand what the singing was like, because the chroniclers got it wrong, and the ballads got it worse, and the ballads are what most people know.

The ballads gave it trumpets. They gave it a woman in white armor with her hair streaming, giving a speech as she broke her vow, while the Regent screamed and the sky cracked open. There may even have been a version with a unicorn. Chroniclers are romantics with ink stains.

What the stone sang was quieter than all that. It sang a young voice in a smuggler's barn, swearing to keep her gift hidden until the world deserved to hear it—a vow made alone, at night, over a cheap blade with a chipped edge. It sang seven years of silence folded into the words like salt into bread. It sang fear. It sang the specific loneliness of a person who has decided that being small is safer than being seen, and who has begun to mistake the shrinking for wisdom.

Then it sang the breaking.

And here is the thing the ballads missed entirely: the breaking sounded *happy*.

Not triumphant—there was too much grief braided through it for triumph, grief for a dead man under a rowan tree, grief for a gift guttering out like a candle pinched between wet fingers—but underneath the grief, unmistakable, irrepressible, there was joy. The joy of a door opening. The joy of a woman saying *I am done making myself small* in the oldest language the world had, and having the oldest language agree.

The students heard all of it. They heard the courier dying in the straw. They heard a bridge, and screaming steel, and a man confessing a sin with his blood spreading under him, forgiven without ever quite being absolved. They heard a bargain struck in blood and refused on principle. They heard a king wake up remembering what his hands had done for three years, and weep into them.

And then, at the very end, the song changed key—which it always did, which was why teachers brought their students, which was why the stone was given air instead of walls.

Because the last thing the stone remembered was not the breaking at all.

The last thing it remembered was the road south.

* * *

Three hundred miles away, on a pass above the border town of Callowmere, a woman sat on a rock eating an apple, and her sword was humming.

Beatrix Yarrow had gotten quite good at pretending not to notice.

The humming had started eleven days out of Thornfell, faint as a fly in another room, and she had spent the first two days convincing herself it was wind in the scabbard throat. On the third day it hummed a tune, and she had stopped mid-stride on a glacier shelf and said, out loud, to a sword, "*No.*"

It had hummed the same tune anyway. It was a smuggler's work-song about a horse with expensive taste. Her mother used to sing it. Beatrix had not thought about it in fifteen years, and here was a piece of chipped Thornfell steel humming it back to her across a mountain pass, and she had stood there in the wind with her apple getting warm in her hand and her eyes stinging in a way she chose to attribute to altitude.

Because that was the other thing the ballads got wrong: the gift did not die with the oath. Everyone said it had. The king's own scholars said it. Beatrix half believed it herself. But a freely broken oath doesn't unmake—it *releases*. And what came back, trickling in through the chip in the blade like water through a cracked dam, wasn't swordspeech as she had known it. It didn't remember wielders' oaths anymore. It didn't sing the past at all.

It sang whatever it pleased.

Mostly, it pleased to sing nonsense. Work-songs. Lullabies. Once, mortifyingly, a drinking ballad from Callowmere's lowest tavern, performed with what she could only describe as enthusiasm. The blade had opinions now. It had preferences. It hummed when she walked toward home and went quiet when she walked away, and Beatrix Yarrow, who had spent seven years disciplining a magical gift into obedient silence, found herself negotiating with a sword about whether they were taking the northern pass or the southern one.

The sword won. The sword usually won.

"You're showing off," she told it now, around the apple core.

The sword hummed agreement, smugly, in a major key.

Below her, the pass dropped toward the lowlands, and beyond the lowlands lay the border, and beyond the border lay every kingdom that wasn't Thornfell, and beyond those, the wildernesses, and beyond those, places maps hadn't reached yet. Rumors traveled faster than she did, which amused her. They said there was a guide working the southern roads who never took contracts requiring an oath. Who paid her debts in coin and labor and never in vows. Who could look at a stranger and know within a minute whether their word was worth anything—though she'd never explain how, and if you asked, she'd change the subject and offer you tea.

They said the guide's prices were fair and her silences were famous and her sword, if you listened very hard on a windy night, sang.

*Let it,* she'd say, if she liked you enough to answer at all. *I spent long enough making things shut up.*

* * *

In the Vault, the song ended, as it always ended, on the road south—with wind, and distance, and a note that resolved not into silence but into openness, the musical equivalent of an unwritten page. Twelve students sat blinking in the gold-threaded light, the way students had blinked every week since the stone learned its new entry.

Ser Wren waited for them to come back from wherever the song had taken them. It took different students different places. The boy in the front row—who had been so quick to say *broken oath*—was crying and pretending he wasn't, which Wren noted with approval, because he'd learn faster that way. The girl with the ribbon-earning answer was staring at the stone with an expression Wren recognized from her own student days: the look of someone realizing that the most powerful thing in the room was also the saddest, and that both facts mattered equally.

"Questions," Wren said.

"Why didn't she want her name in the stone?" the boy asked, sniffling with dignity.

"Because names get used." Wren shrugged. "Ask the Regent. It built an entire kingdom out of borrowed names. She gave the stone the one thing nobody could spend: a story with the name left out, so anyone who needs it can step inside and wear it."

"Did she really survive?" This from a smaller girl at the end, barely audible. "The ballads say she died in the Vault."

"The ballads say a lot of things." Wren smiled. "There's a version where she marries the king."

"She *didn't*, though."

"She sent back his letter unread. With a recipe for plum brandy in the envelope, because she felt guilty, and because apparently the king's brandy is terrible. That part's verified." Wren let the laughter run its course. "As for surviving—I'll tell you what Magister Corvel told me when I asked him the same question, my first week here. He said: *go stand on the high pass above Callowmere on a windy evening, and listen.*"

"Did you go?" the girl asked.

"I went." Wren touched the smooth spot on the stone one last time. "That's a story for when you're older. But I'll tell you this much, free of charge, and you can carry it out of this room and keep it longer than your swords:

"An oath is not a chain. It was never meant to be a chain. It's a *song*—something you choose, something you carry, something you can put down when the carrying becomes a cage. The Regent spent three hundred years teaching us that a promise is a hook in your mouth. One woman spent one hour teaching the stone the truth. Every time we sing it, the truth gets a little heavier than the lie. That's the whole war. That's all it ever was."

She dismissed them, and they filed out past the gold thread, and the stone settled into its long patient listening, holding its unnamed entry the way a cupped hand holds water—openly, and without gripping.

Far to the north, a king signed reforms he sometimes hated and always signed anyway, because he remembered what his hands had done, and memory was the only leash he'd ever trust.

Farther north still, under a rowan tree on a fell that bloomed white every spring, a knight who died buying an hour slept the sleep of a man whose last oath was also his freest, and the rowan, which had grown unnaturally fast, hummed in high winds in a way the local shepherds declined to discuss.

And on a pass above Callowmere, a woman stood up from her rock, slung her pack, and turned south—or rather, her sword hummed *south*, and she followed it, laughing at herself for doing so, the way you laugh at an old friend who has finally stopped being polite about what they want.

The wind came up the pass. It always comes up the pass, in every season, in every telling; it is the one reliable thing in this whole account. And when it reached her, the sword lifted its voice—not loudly, not desperately, just clearly, the way something sings when it has finally been allowed to—and the sound spilled down the mountainside ahead of her, going where she was going, arriving before she did.

Somewhere below, in a village she'd reach by dusk, a child would hear it and ask her mother what that music was.

*Just the wind off the fells,* the mother would say.

And she'd be wrong, and it wouldn't matter, because the truth was already walking into town on two tired feet with a smile it hadn't worn in years—following a song that asked nothing, promised nothing, bound nothing, and needed, at long last, absolutely no one's permission to be heard.
