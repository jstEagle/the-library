## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The plum was beautiful the way lies are beautiful—skin taut and dark as a bruise healing backward, a bloom of silver dust on its curve that any honest eye would have called frost-sugar. Felix Amberdale held it to the morning light slanting through the kitchen's high windows and let three full seconds pass before she bit.

Three seconds was theater. The court expected theater. A food-taster who swallowed without ceremony was either fearless or careless, and neither word belonged in her vocabulary. So she turned the fruit once, twice, breathed its scent like a lover's, and then set her teeth into flesh that tasted of honey and, beneath the honey, of something green and bitter as a snapped stem.

*Hemlock-elder,* she thought, cataloguing it the way another woman might note a dressmaker's stitching. *Cut with wine-must to mask the edge, and something else underneath. Something I don't know.*

She chewed. She swallowed. She smiled at the under-steward, a nervous boy named Pellow who would carry the plum's remaining half up three flights to Queen Isolde's breakfast table, and she said, in the flat, forgettable voice she had spent nine years sanding smooth: "Sweet enough."

The poison reached her heart between the second bell and the third.

She knew because she had learned to read her own body the way scholars read dead languages—fluently, dispassionately, with an eye for the marginalia. Her pulse stuttered, caught, resumed. Cold crawled up from her fingertips toward her elbows, a tide going out. The kitchen's warmth receded from her skin as if she had been dipped in winter water and withdrawn. Her vision narrowed to a bright tunnel edged in gray, and somewhere very far away, Pellow was saying her name—"Mistress Amberdale? Mistress?"—in the rising pitch of a boy about to make himself memorable by screaming.

Felix gripped the edge of the tasting table and rode it out.

This was the part no one could see. This was the secret she carried behind her ribs like a stolen jewel: that the poison did not kill her, had never killed her, that nine years of dishes laced with arsenic and wolfsbane and things imported at obscene cost from continents whose names the cartographers argued over had left her body untouched and her soul, she sometimes suspected, quietly mortgaged. Her tongue told the truth. It was everything past her throat that lied on her behalf.

The cold peaked. Receded. Her heartbeat settled back into its ordinary rhythm, steady as a metronome in an empty room.

"Forgive me," she said, blinking as though surfacing. "Swallowed wrong. Went down sideways." She coughed twice, artistically, and wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. "It's fine. The queen may eat."

Pellow stared at her with the particular blankness of someone who has been trained never to notice anything, which in Thornfell Palace was the second most valuable survival skill after Felix's own. He took the plum's surviving half on its little silver dish and hurried out.

*Something else underneath,* she thought again, flexing her fingers where they still tingled with ghost-cold. Whatever Corvane's kitchens—or whoever's kitchens—had folded into this poison, it was new. New meant someone had spent money. Money meant intent, and intent aimed at a queen meant the game in this palace had shifted while Felix had been busy being furniture.

She rinsed her hands, tied her apron back on, and became, once more, a small brown woman of no consequence standing among forty other small brown people of no consequence. Invisibility was not a gift. It was a discipline, practiced daily, worn like chainmail against the skin: speak only when spoken to, remember everything, forget nothing, and above all be so unremarkable that even the men who counted spoons could not pick your face from a lineup.

Nine years she had worn it. Nine years since the night the collectors came down from the mountain and took her brother, and Felix had learned that the safest place in a kingdom of knives was inside the crowd, faceless, unnoticed—

*Unnoticed.* She almost laughed, scrubbing at the tasting table with a rag that smelled of vinegar. Tammy had been noticed, hadn't he? Noticed by debt-collectors and then by worse things. Being seen was dangerous. But being no one—that had its own price, and she paid it every night when she lay in her narrow cell off the kitchens and could not recall, some evenings, whether anyone in the world would know if she simply stopped.

Not tonight. Tonight there was work.

* * *

Old Wenna kept a shop in the Tanners' Reach that sold, officially, candle stubs and secondhand boots, and unofficially sold everything else in Thornfell that preferred not to have a name attached. To reach it without being followed required leaving the palace through routes that existed on no steward's map—the smuggler roads, the servants called them, though most servants knew them only as rumors. Felix knew them the way she knew her own teeth.

She went out through the scullery, past the rendering sheds where the fat smell covered all other smells, and dropped into the drainage cut that ran beneath the east wall. The tunnel there was older than the wall above it; masons had built around it rather than through it, superstitious or lazy, and their superstition had left a passage wide enough for a crouched woman and dry enough except after hard rain. Rat droppings, old straw, the mineral breath of stone that had been wet for centuries. Felix moved through the dark without a lantern because lanterns could be seen, and because she had walked this stretch perhaps four hundred times, and because the darkness and she had long ago come to an arrangement: it would not swallow her, and she would not flinch from it.

At the second junction she paused—not from uncertainty but from habit—and listened. Water ticking somewhere. A rat relocating its opinions. Above, muffled through six feet of earth and rubble, the palace bells ringing the hour, and beneath the bells, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, something else.

A hum. Deep, arrhythmic, less a sound than a pressure felt in the back teeth.

Felix stood very still in the dark and waited for it to resolve into cart-wheels or furnace-draughts or any of the hundred ordinary causes a sensible mind offered up. It did not resolve. It rose through the soles of her boots, through the bones of her feet, and stopped, and did not stop, and then it was gone, and the silence afterward felt like a held breath.

*Old pipes,* she told herself. *Settling foundations. The mountain grumbles. Mountains do that.*

But the mountain had not grumbled nine years ago, nor last month, and the thought followed her the rest of the way like a dog that had decided, provisionally, to be hers.

* * *

Wenna's shop smelled of tallow and dried lavender laid over something older and ranker—the smell of goods that had changed hands too many times. The old woman herself sat behind the counter sorting buttons by touch alone, her clouded eyes aimed at the middle distance, and when Felix pushed through the door trailing cold air and tunnel dust, Wenna said without looking up:

"You're late. Or early. One of those."

"The bells rang noon as I passed the chandler's."

"The bells ring what they're told." Wenna swept a handful of buttons into a jar and finally turned her head, milky gaze settling on Felix with the unnerving accuracy of the blind. "Sit. You've got grave dirt on your hem, which means you came the low way, which means you wanted talking about. So talk."

Felix sat on the stool across the counter, and for a moment—only a moment, because habit was armor and armor did not unlatch in daylight—she let herself simply breathe in a room where one person knew her face and used it.

"There was a plum this morning," she said. "Meant for the queen."

"And?"

"And I'm alive, which is the whole of what I can tell you for certain. Hemlock-elder base, wine-must carrier. But there was a third element I couldn't name. Not a plant. Something worked, alchemical. It burned cold instead of hot."

Wenna's hands went still over the button jar. In thirty years of trading in secrets, the fence had perfected a stillness that most people mistook for dullness; Felix knew better. Stillness was how Wenna listened.

"Cold-burning," the old woman repeated. "You're sure of the taste?"

"I'm sure of everything about my own tongue, Wenna."

"Mm." The blind eyes fixed on her a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then the old woman shrugged, deliberately casual, which was itself a message: *we will return to this.* "There's been coin moving in the Reach. Not merchant coin—court coin, dressed down to look poor. Three buyers this fortnight asking after rare reagents, and all three paying too much and haggling too little. Rich fools are loud. Rich careful ones are worth fearing." She tapped one knuckle on the counter. "And there's this, since you'll hear it anyway within the week: the Sweetwater's running again."

Felix frowned. "The Sweetwater's been dry since before I was born."

"Aye. Thirty-one years, give or take. And two nights past it came down the gorge thick as ale, sweet as spring melt, and half of Millbrook woke up weeping and calling it a miracle. They're leaving offerings on the banks already. Bread, milk, ribbon, coins. Priest from Thornfell chapel went out to look at it yesterday and came back saying the water's blessed, clean as any font." Wenna's voice flattened. "I sent a boy for a flask of it. He drank a sip on the road, being thirsty and stupid."

"And?"

"And he hasn't slept since. Two nights, wide awake, cheerful as a bride, keeps saying the water tasted of his grandmother's kitchen. Of a summer he swears he remembers that his mother says never happened." The old woman's fingers found a button and rolled it, click-click-click, against the wood. "Blessed, is it. Maybe. But blessings and bait look alike from the outside, girl, and you're the only tongue in this city I'd trust to tell them apart."

The shop was quiet except for the button, clicking. Outside, cart wheels ground over cobbles; somewhere a fishwife shouted prices. The ordinary world, insisting on itself.

"I'll go out tomorrow," Felix said. "Day after. There's a delivery schedule I can hide inside."

"Take the low road both ways. And Felix—" Wenna's voice dropped, and it was strange and rare to hear her actual name spoken aloud, strange as a bell rung indoors. The fence used it perhaps twice a year, always for the same purpose: to mark that what came next was not commerce. "You said the poison had a third element you couldn't name. You've tasted everything. Everything there is to buy, steal, or brew in this kingdom, and a few things besides. If there's a thing your tongue doesn't know—"

"—then it's not from the kingdom," Felix finished quietly.

"That's my thinking too. And now there's a river running sweet that shouldn't exist, and a boy who remembers summers that were never lived. Coin moving quiet, and a queen nearly fed a poison with foreign roots." Wenna spread her hands, palms up, an old woman's gesture of surrender to a world grown strange. "I'm too old for the shape of this. But I know a door opening when I hear the hinge."

Felix looked at her hands. Small hands, scarred at the fingertips from needles and knife-work and nine years of pretending to gamble her life at every meal. Competent hands. Lonely ones.

"I don't believe in miracles," she said.

"No," Wenna agreed. "Neither do I. That's why we're still alive, the pair of us." She pushed a wrapped parcel across the counter—a boot, by the weight, with something else hidden inside it, because Wenna never gave a plain gift. "Payment for the warning. Don't open it here."

* * *

Felix took the low road back as dusk came down, because the high roads at dusk belonged to the watch and the watch had recently developed an interest in faces. The tunnels were colder in the evening; the damp breathed out of the stone, and her lantern—lit now, past the junction where sightlines ended—threw her shadow huge and wavering along walls furred with white mineral bloom.

She let herself think about Tammy.

She tried not to, most days, because the thinking led downhill the way water does, inevitably, to places with no bottom. But Wenna's talk of the river had pried something loose. *His grandmother's kitchen,* the boy had said. *A summer that never happened.* Memory, offered like bait. Sweetness with a hook in it.

The collectors had come on a night much like this one, nine years ago. Rain just ended, gutters chuckling, their mother's debts stacked on the table like bad cards. Felix had been fourteen and furious and small in the way that mattered, and Tammy had been eight, asleep, and she remembered—she would always remember—the sound of the door, and the voices that were too smooth, and the moment when the smooth voices said *the mountain accepts payment in kind* as though they were reading from a ledger. She had thrown a pot. She had screamed until her throat tore. None of it had mattered, because debts signed in blood before a child was born were not debts a sister could argue with.

They had carried him out into the wet street, and he had not woken, and the mountain had taken him the way the mountain took everything: quietly, permanently, with paperwork.

Midwinter. His contract expired at midwinter. Which meant, if the arithmetic of souls ran anything like the arithmetic of money, that whatever remained of Tammy Amberdale beneath the mountain was entering its final season.

Felix stopped walking. The lantern flame leaned and steadied. Somewhere ahead, water dripped with the patience of stone, counting out seconds that didn't care whether she spent them.

Everyone in the palace knew her as nothing. The queen knew her as a function. Corvane—her skin prickled at the memory of the chancellor's eyes sliding over her that morning, mild and weighing as a grocer's thumb—knew her, perhaps, as a tool not yet priced. No one knew her as a sister with a debt of her own to collect.

That was about to change, she suspected. Wenna had named the hinge; the door was opening. A river running sweet, a poison with foreign roots, a mountain humming in its sleep. The world was rearranging itself, and rearranged worlds were dangerous—but they were also the only times a no-one could move without being seen doing it.

Felix lifted the lantern and walked on, deeper into the dark under Thornfell, toward the light and the lie of the palace above.

Behind her, far below the tunnels' deepest known point, the humming started again.

This time, she did not tell herself it was pipes.
