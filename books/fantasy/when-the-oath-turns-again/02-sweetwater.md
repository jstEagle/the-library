## 2. Sweetwater

The river came back on a Tuesday.

That was the first strange thing about it, though no one in Millbrook thought of it that way. Rivers did not keep calendars; rivers did not choose. But the folk of Millbrook swore by it—old Marta Hedley had been hanging her wash when the sound reached her, a low groan from the riverbed like a door long rusted shut being forced open, and she'd dropped her linens in the dirt and run to the bank and seen the water coming. Not a flood. A *filling*, she said afterward, hands pressed to her mouth. Like a cup being poured from somewhere below the world.

Thirty years the river had been dry. Thirty years of cracked clay and bleached stones and children daring each other to walk its channel to the sea. The old ones remembered when it ran—the Sweetwater, they called it, because it had tasted faintly of honey where it pooled over the reeds—and they told the story so often the young had stopped listening: how the river died the same winter the last shrine up Thornfell way lost its priestess, how the wells went brackish one by one after, how the village learned to haul water in carts from three valleys over and call it normal, the way people learn to call most anything normal if it lasts long enough.

And then, on an ordinary gray morning in late autumn, the water returned overnight, sweet as its name, and Millbrook fell to its knees on the muddy banks and wept.

Felix Amberdale heard about it two days later, in the palace kitchens, from a carter who'd come up from the low country with apples and gossip in equal measure.

"They're saying it's a blessing," the carter said, warming his hands over the great hearth while pretending not to watch the pastry cooks. "Priest of the New Flame came out from Darrowmere to consecrate the banks. Big crowd. They've started leaving offerings."

"Offerings," repeated Cook, without looking up from her ledger. "To a river."

"To whatever gave the river back." The carter shrugged. "Bread, mostly. Milk. A man left his best boots, can you imagine. There's talk of building something proper come spring. A temple."

Felix kept her head down over the knife and the onions. She was very good at keeping her head down—it was, in truth, the finest skill she possessed, finer even than her tongue—and so no one noticed the moment when the knife stopped moving, or the way her breath went shallow and careful, like a woman standing very still in a room where she has heard a floorboard creak overhead.

A river returning was a blessing. That was the natural reading. Crops blooming overnight in the eastern fields—that was a blessing too, the heralds were saying so already, printing broadsheets with the queen's seal at the corner, *the crown rejoices with its people*. Dead orchards fruiting in the wrong season. A ewe in some border village that birthed twin lambs on the feast day of a god nobody had worshipped in a generation. Blessings, all of it. The court had decided it was blessings, and the court's decisions had a way of becoming true through sheer repetition.

But Felix had grown up in the shadow of Thornfell mountain, in a village that still, in her grandmother's time, left a bowl of cream on the doorstep at midwinter—not for any god in particular, and certainly not for the New Flame, but *just in case*, because her grandmother's grandmother had known better than to let the old names go entirely unspoken. And her grandmother had said a thing once, when Felix was small, that had lodged in her like a fishbone:

*"When the old gods answer, girl, count what you asked for. Then count again what you got. The difference is the price."*

She hadn't asked for anything. Neither had Millbrook, so far as she knew. That was the trouble. Things given unasked-for were never gifts.

* * *

She went down on her half-day, which drew no notice. Tasters were granted their half-days the way furniture was granted polish—routinely, and with no curiosity about where the furniture went. In nine years at the palace, Felix had perfected the art of being sought by no one. She wore servant's gray with a mend at the elbow that she could have afforded to replace but didn't, because mended gray made eyes slide off her. She walked at the pace of someone going somewhere dull. She answered questions with just enough blandness to end them. If you had asked any of the hundred-odd souls who shared her corridor to describe her, they would have frowned, grasped, and produced something like: *small, brown hair, tastes things, doesn't she?* Nine years, and that was the shape she'd carved for herself—a shape with no edges, nothing for the world to catch on.

It had taken work. It had cost more than anyone guessed. But it had kept her alive through a poisoning, a purge, and one memorable night when assassins went room to room with lists and skipped hers entirely because whoever wrote the list had written *taster? — no, the other one.*

So she took the south road out of the city in her mended gray, and no one marked her going.

Millbrook was a day and a half by cart track, less if you knew the smuggler ways through Thornfell's lower skirts, which Felix did the way other people knew their own handwriting. She'd spent six years learning those paths—every goat trail, every collapsed mine shaft with a passable crawl-space, every culvert and charcoal-burner's cut and smuggler's stair hacked into the rock by men who'd needed to move brandy and bodies without meeting a tollgate. She had not learned them for herself.

That distinction sat in her chest like a swallowed stone as she climbed, as it always did, and she did what she always did with it: set it down, shouldered her pack, and kept moving.

The lower valleys smelled different this year. She noticed it before she understood it—green where there should have been brown, a sweetness in the air that belonged to June, not the dying edge of October. When she crested the ridge above the eastern fields she stopped, despite herself, despite the discipline of nine years that said *never stop where you can be seen against a skyline.*

The fields were blooming.

Not the planted fields—those lay harvested and stubbled, proper and dead for the season. But the verges, the hedgerows, the forgotten corners between holdings: blackthorn in flower, white and reckless, bees droning fatly among blossoms that had no business existing until spring. A whole hillside of gorse aflame yellow in the gray air. It looked like a mistake. It looked like a painting hung crooked.

Below, Millbrook's smoke rose thin and ordinary. And beyond the village, cutting through it all like a vein of silver hammered into brown earth, the river ran.

Felix stood on the ridge a long moment, wind pulling at her hood, and felt the thing her grandmother's fishbone words had prepared her for: not wonder. Not quite. Wonder with something under it, the way sweet meat can carry a whiff of rot if your nose is good enough. Hers was very good.

*Count what you got,* she thought, and went down into the valley to do exactly that.

* * *

Millbrook had gone slightly mad, in the gentle, rural way villages do. The offerings lined the banks for a quarter mile: bread loaves on flat stones, bowls of milk sinking slowly into the mud, eggs in straw, a garland of late roses that must have cost somebody's whole garden, candles stubbed into driftwood so they could burn in the wind. Someone had built a rough cairn and crowned it with antlers. Someone else had tied ribbons to a hawthorn, which was older practice than any religion currently licensed in Thornfell, and which the New Flame's priest had evidently decided not to see.

People came and went along the bank all day, Felix observed from the doorway of the inn where she'd bought a bed for the night with a name that wasn't hers. They came with offerings and left lighter-faced, touching the water, letting it run over their knuckles. A mother held her baby out and let the child's toes dip in. An old man knelt so long his knees must have ached for a week after.

They weren't praying to anything in particular. That was the eerie part. No liturgy, no god named. They were thanking the water itself, or the luck, or the general benevolent *something* that had looked down and chosen them. Felix watched a farmwife press her forehead to the wet mud and mouth words too quiet to carry, and thought: *she doesn't know who she's talking to.*

Neither, Felix suspected, did the thing on the other end. Or worse—it knew exactly, and was letting them not-know, the way a fisherman lets the line go slack.

She waited for full dark. Then she took a cup from her pack and walked down to the bank alone.

The moon was a fingernail paring, and the village lamps had gone out, and the river made its new sound in the dark—that soft continuous voice water has, the one that says *I was never gone, I only stepped away.* The offerings sat pale in the gloom. The bread had not been touched by birds, she noticed. Nor the milk soured, though the nights were warm enough. Everything sat exactly as placed, neat as goods in a shop, as though the bank were a shelf and something were keeping inventory.

Felix crouched at the water's edge and dipped her cup.

For a moment she simply held it, watching the surface settle. Her trade lived in moments like this—in the pause before tasting, when every sense leaned forward. Poisoners were artists and artists signed their work, and a taster who rushed was a taster who died. She listened to the water. She smelled it: cold stone, green weed, and under it, very faintly, something mineral and deep that reminded her absurdly of the inside of the palace vaults, of coins locked away from air.

Then she drank.

The water was sweet. That was the horror of it. It was *sweet*—clean and cold and honeyed over the reeds exactly as the old ones said, the finest water she had ever tasted, water that made every other water she'd drunk seem like a rumor of water. It slid down her throat like a promise.

And underneath the sweetness, so faint that anyone else would have called it imagination, ran the bitterness.

Not the bitterness of alums or metals. Not the bitterness of any poison she knew, and her knowledge was wide and paid for dearly. This was older. Botanical, almost—but no leaf or root she could name. It tasted the way a church smelled: incense and dust and something solemn underneath. It tasted the way the word *owe* would taste, if words had flavor. It sat on her tongue for a long moment after she swallowed, a small dark seed of a taste, and then it faded, and the sweetness closed over it like water over a dropped coin.

Felix spat carefully into the grass and sat back on her heels.

Her heart was doing a slow, heavy drumbeat. Not fear, precisely. Recognition. Somewhere in the back of her skull, in the place where childhood things are stored unexamined, something had stirred and turned over and said *I know that taste*—and would not say where from.

She dipped the cup again. Drank again. Same sweetness, same seed of bitterness beneath, gone before she could pin it. On the third try she held the water in her mouth, swilled it across her tongue like wine, hunting the note the way she'd hunt arsenic in a plum—patient, systematic, professional.

There. Under the honey: bitter. And under the bitterness, when she truly concentrated, something stranger still. A texture. The water had a texture to it, a grain, the way silk has grain, and the grain ran in one direction—*down*. Toward Thornfell. Toward the mountain that stood black against the stars north of the valley, vast and silent, wearing its thirty years of silence like a mask worn so long the face beneath has grown to fit it.

Everything in this valley flowed downhill. Even, apparently, the flavor of its miracle.

Felix emptied the cup onto the bank and sat in the dark and made herself think, because thinking was the one tool no one could confiscate from her.

Here is what the court believed: the old gods were stories. The shrines were ruins. Whatever moved in the world now—blessings, miracles, blooming thorns—was either the New Flame's grace or natural fortune, and both interpretations flattered the crown, and so both were official. Here is what the smugglers believed, the ones who worked the deep roads under Thornfell, the ones who'd pay extra to men willing to take the high passes instead: that the mountain was hollow, and had been inhabited once, and that the inhabitants had not died but *slept*, and that certain tolls had been paid to them since before the kingdom had a name, and that the roads went quiet in places where no wind blew and no echo answered, because *something down there was listening and you didn't want it to hear your name.*

Felix had always filed those stories under the tax smugglers paid to scare competitors off good routes. Tonight, crouched beside a river that had come home overnight, tasting owed money on her tongue, she found the file wouldn't stay shut.

Because here was the other thing. Here was the thing she never let herself think past a certain point, the thought with a trapdoor in it:

The collectors had come for Tammy in the dead of winter, eight—no. Count honestly, Felix. *Nine* years ago. Two figures in the snow, polite as clergy, with papers bearing a debt her mother had signed in desperation and never fully understood. They had not been bandits. Bandits didn't produce contracts. They had carried Tammy off toward the mountain, and her mother had screamed herself hoarse and died within the year of what the village doctor called grief and Felix privately called something closer to being *unpaid*, and Felix had been left with a mended coat, a stolen talent for surviving poisons, and a number scratched into her heart: the years remaining on a contract she had never been allowed to read.

Midwinter. The contract expired at midwinter.

And now the rivers were running sweet, and the thorns were blooming out of season, and the mountain's valley floor was putting on flowers like a table being dressed for guests.

*Coincidence,* offered the small cowardly voice she kept fed and safe, the one that had kept her invisible for nine years. *Seasons turn. Rivers find new springs. Go home, drink nothing, taste the queen's supper tomorrow, and be no one.*

Felix looked at the offerings gleaming pale along the bank. Bread and milk and boots and roses, laid out by people who didn't know whom they fed. And further up, on the cairn with the antlers, someone had left a small clay figure—a crude human shape, pressed with thumbprints, the kind of thing a child might make.

Someone had pressed it flat, deliberately, so that it had no face.

She was still looking at it when she heard the footsteps behind her—soft ones, deliberate ones, the tread of someone who had also waited for full dark—and her hand went to the knife in her boot before her mind caught up with her manners.

An old woman stood at the edge of the offering-line, wrapped in a shawl the color of ash. Felix hadn't heard her approach at all, which should not have been possible on gravel, and the old woman was looking at Felix with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the look of someone counting.

"You tasted it," the old woman said. It was not a question. "I watched you from the willow. Three times, holding it in your mouth. Folk don't do that unless they're tasting for something."

Felix said nothing. Silence was armor; she'd forged it herself.

"Others have tasted it too," the old woman went on, drifting closer, her shadow long in the starlight. "Some of us can feel it. The sweetness, yes—but under it, the bill. My grandmother called that flavor *tithe-root*, when the river ran before. Said it meant the water wasn't given. Said it meant it was *lent*."

"Lent by whom?" Felix asked, and hated how much she wanted the answer.

The old woman smiled, and her teeth were very white in her dark face, and she nodded northward, toward Thornfell, toward the black shoulder of the mountain where no stars seemed to stick.

"Whomever's waking up, girl. Whomever's thirsty after thirty dry years. You mark me—" she tapped her own tongue—"that bitterness is going to get stronger. Water like that, you don't drink twice by accident."

She shuffled off along the bank, past the faceless clay figure, and was gone among the willows without a sound.

Felix stayed until the sky went gray at the edges. Then she packed her cup, checked her knife, and turned her feet north—not toward the palace, not yet—toward the village churchyard on the hillside where her mother lay under a stone with a weathered name on it, because there were debts on her family's side of the ledger too, and she had counted nine years of interest, and midwinter was coming.

Whatever was waking beneath Thornfell, she needed to know whether it kept records.

Because she intended to contest one.
