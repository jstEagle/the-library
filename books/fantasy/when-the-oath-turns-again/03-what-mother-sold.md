## 3. What Mother Sold

The grave sat on the hillside like a sentence no one had finished.

Felix had walked up from the river road before dawn, when the mist still clung to the bracken in grey rags and the only sound was her own boots pressing wet into the turf. Nine years she'd climbed this hill, once a year, sometimes twice if the guilt got loud enough, and every time she expected the stone to look different. It never did. Weather-worn granite, lichen eating the letters, her mother's name going soft at the edges the way all names eventually went soft in Thornfell.

*Maren Amberdale. Beloved of no ledger.*

That last line had been Felix's addition, chiseled by a stonemason who'd taken her coin without questions, because that was what coin was for. Her mother had been beloved of many things—a tuneless humming, burnt honey-cakes, a temper like a struck match—but the ledgers had never loved her back, and it was the ledgers that mattered in the end. It was always the ledgers.

Felix knelt in the wet grass and set down what she'd brought: a heel of black bread, a twist of salt in paper, and one plum from the palace stores, unpoisoned as far as she knew, though these days she tested everything twice. She arranged them on the stone with hands that didn't shake. Taster's hands. Steady hands were the first thing they taught you—steady hands or dead tasters—and somewhere along the way the steadiness had spread from her fingers into everything else, until even grief arrived on schedule and left on schedule, like a servant with duties elsewhere.

"Nine years," she said to the stone.

The wind moved through the bracken. Somewhere below, the sweetwater river—that new impossible river—was running again, and she could hear it faintly, a whisper under the wind like someone talking in another room. The villagers had left offerings on its banks again: garlands of rowan, cups of milk sinking into the mud, a child's carved horse laid carefully where the water could see it. Felix had tasted the water yesterday. Faintly bitter, underneath the sweet. Like something trying very hard to be pleasant.

Her mother would have known what that meant. Her mother had grown up singing songs about the mountain before the crown decided songs were contraband.

"I found him," Felix said. "In the lists. The collector's rolls. Tammas Amberdale, taken in payment of debt, contracted seven years—" her voice caught, and she smoothed it flat with practice, "—renewed twice. Current term expires midwinter."

Midwinter. Eleven weeks away, give or take the moon's mood.

She pressed her palm flat against the granite, cold seeping through her glove, and let the memory come, because she'd learned that fighting it only made it worse. Grief was like poison that way. You couldn't spit it out. You could only swallow it whole and trust your body to do something with it.

* * *

It had rained the night the collectors came.

Not the soft rain of Thornfell springs but a hard, vertical rain, drumming on the shutters, and their mother had been three days dead by then—cold in the bed she'd been carried to, the debts already circling the house like gulls over a gutted fish. Felix was fourteen. Tammy was nine. They'd eaten the last of the flour that morning, and Felix remembered thinking, with the strange clarity of children in disasters, that at least there wouldn't be dishes to wash.

The knock came at midnight exactly. She'd know that knock anywhere, forever: not a fist but a knuckle, one deliberate rap, patient as arithmetic. When she opened the door—because you opened the door; everyone knew you opened the door, because the collectors did not knock twice—the hall was full of them.

Three figures in oilcloth cloaks the color of wet slate, faces shadowed under deep hoods, and between them, standing on his own feet in bare toes gone blue with cold, stood Tammy. Awake. Calm. That was the part that had broken something in Felix and never mended it right: he wasn't crying. He looked *interested*, the way he looked at beetle shells and the innards of clocks, and when he saw her he said, "Fee, they said I get to keep my name for now," in the tone of a boy reporting good news.

"Keep it *for now*," repeated the tallest collector, and its voice was wrong—not deep, not rough, but layered, like several people speaking through one throat slightly out of step. "Names are held in trust. Terms are negotiable upon renewal."

"You can't have him." Felix had planted herself in the doorway, arms spread, fourteen years old and weighing nothing, and the words came out of her in a rush she hadn't planned: "Take me instead. I'm older. Take me."

The hood turned toward her. She felt it turn, the way you feel weather change. And then—one heartbeat, two—the layered voice said, almost gently:

"The tithe is marked. Not this one."

And the collector reached out—it wore a glove, or something like a glove, grey leather over too-long fingers—and touched her shoulder, briefly, above her collarbone, a touch so light she'd spent nine years half-convinced she'd dreamed it. A touch like a merchant confirming a seal on goods already owned.

Then they took Tammy out into the rain, and he went *willingly*, that was the worst of it, waving at her over the collector's shoulder, and Felix stood in the doorway until the rain had washed their footprints off the road and her legs gave out, and then she got up and made herself count the debts on her mother's table. Every figure. Every name. Because numbers were real, and numbers could be fought, and if she ever stopped counting she would start screaming and never stop.

* * *

Present-day Felix exhaled slowly, breath fogging against the stone.

"The mark," she murmured. "*Not this one.*"

She'd told herself for years it meant nothing. Collectors were creatures of bureaucracy, ancient and literal; perhaps the mountain's ledgers simply had no column for a fourteen-year-old girl. But she'd been tasting palace food for six years now, surviving things that should have killed her, and lately—since the river came back, since the offerings started appearing—she'd begun to wonder whether immunity was a skill at all. Whether it was more like... ownership. Something kept healthy because it was wanted intact.

She pushed the thought away the way you push away a hand reaching for your purse. Not today. Today was for Tammy.

She talked to the stone awhile longer, the way she always did. Reported the small things: that she still made her honey-cakes burnt on purpose, once a year, on the anniversary; that she'd gotten promoted twice and trusted once, which was the better trade; that the queen had wept over a plum yesterday and never known why she lived. Her mother would have laughed at that. Her mother had believed in telling the truth loudly and paying for it promptly.

"And here's the large thing," Felix said finally, "since you'll ask anyway."

She told the stone about the contract. About midwinter. About the plan forming in her like frost spreading across a window—slow, intricate, beautiful in the way that dangerous things are beautiful. She needed the smuggler roads. She needed someone who remembered the old rites, someone who spoke the mountain's grammar. There were rumors of a priestess in the western caves, disgraced, half-mad, still burning ash-offerings when everyone else had learned to pray to the crown's tidy gods. Rumors said she answered questions with questions. Felix could work with that. Questions were just doors that hadn't been kicked open yet.

Eleven weeks. She'd stolen back harder things than a brother. Debts, after all, were only contracts, and contracts—

Contracts could be broken. She'd built a life on proving that.

She rose, brushed the wet from her knees, and pressed two fingers to the plum on the stone—a taster's farewell, testing the world on behalf of the dead.

"Watch me," she said quietly, to her mother, to the mountain listening under the hill, to whoever else might be eavesdropping. "I'm coming for him."

Behind her, far below, the sweetwater river whispered on, running sweet and bitter toward the sea, and the mist began to lift off the bracken like an audience rising from its seats.

* * *

The fence's name was Osk, and his shop smelled of tallow and secrets.

Felix reached it by the third smuggler road—the one that ran behind the tanneries, through a culvert wide enough for a man and narrow enough for conscience—and she arrived as the light was failing, which was how Osk liked his customers: dim, and gone by dark.

He was older than nine years ago, of course. Everyone was. His beard had gone from black to iron, and his hands shook now unless he was counting, in which case they were surgeon-steady. He looked up when she slipped in the door, and his eyes did the thing all fences' eyes did: catalogued her, priced her, filed her.

"Amberdale's girl," he said. "Thought you'd gone respectable."

"I taste food for a living. Respectable is what other people think it is."

"Hm." He set down the brass weights he'd been sorting. "You want news or goods?"

"News." She leaned on the counter, letting her cloak fall open just enough to show the palace livery beneath—his eyes flicked to it, recalculated. "About the mountain. About collectors moving on the roads. About why rivers that died thirty years ago are running sweet."

Osk was quiet long enough for the tallow lamp to gutter twice.

"They're saying it's smugglers," he said at last. "Crown says the old routes woke something up. Crown's doubling patrols on Thornfell pass. Which means the honest roads are closing, which means business is *excellent*, which means I should thank whoever's responsible." He didn't sound thankful. He sounded like a man reading a bill of sale and finding his own name on it. "But that's not what's waking, girl. Anyone who grew up on the old songs knows what's waking. The court under the mountain kept accounts for centuries before your kings drew their first border. And it's calling in arrears."

"Arrears on what?"

"On everything." He leaned close, lowering his voice as though the walls might resell it. "Every promise ever made near that mountain and forgotten. Every offering skipped. Every soul contracted and assumed forgiven because the creditor slept." His eyes found hers, and for a moment there was something almost kind in them. "Your mother traded with them once, Amberdale's girl. Did she ever tell you what she bought?"

Felix's blood went slow and heavy. "She paid a debt. That's all. The collectors came for Tammy because she owed."

"Debts don't come due in flesh unless flesh was collateral." Osk straightened, rubbing his steady-when-counting hands together. "I brokered the edges of that bargain, back when I had hair worth keeping. Your mother came to me desperate, needing coin fast, and there were lenders in the low country who wouldn't touch her. So she went higher. Or lower, depending how you reckon it." He paused. "She didn't sell your brother, girl. She sold something else. Tammy was the *penalty clause*. Read your contracts. The mountain always writes a penalty clause."

"What did she sell?" Felix heard her own voice from very far away.

"That I never learned. But whatever it was, the mountain considered it worth a boy's whole soul, and it's been holding him in trust ever since—healthy, safe, *kept*—" and the word landed on her like a hand on her shoulder, light as a glove, nine years ago, above her collarbone, "—the way creditors keep collateral."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the first evening bell rang from the town, thin and bronze.

Felix stood very still, doing what she did best: swallowing the poison whole and trusting her body to do something with it.

"Where do I find the priestess?" she asked. "The one in the western caves. Sera Vane."

Osk studied her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him sigh, the sigh of a man watching someone sign a contract they haven't read.

"Candlemere Hollow. Follow the dry riverbed west past the standing stones until the water remembers itself. Two days' walk if the weather's kind. Three if the mountain's interested." He pushed a scrap of paper across the counter—a charcoal sketch of the route, quick and practiced. "One more thing. Free of charge, since I knew your mother."

She waited.

"When you find what she sold," Osk said quietly, "don't blame her for it. Desperation makes bargainers of us all. And the mountain..." He smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "The mountain has never once accepted an apology as payment."

Felix folded the map into her glove and turned for the door.

"Neither have I," she said.

Outside, night was coming down over Thornfell like a ledger closing, and eleven weeks away, under the hill, a boy who kept his name only *for now* was waiting for her to learn the terms.

She started walking west.
