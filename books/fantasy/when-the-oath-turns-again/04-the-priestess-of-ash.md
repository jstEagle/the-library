## 4. The Priestess of Ash

The rumors reached Felix the way most true things did in Thornfell: sideways, half-rotted, wrapped in three lies apiece.

*A priestess lives in the Ashen Caves,* a drover said over ale he couldn't afford, *one of the ash-eaters from before the Concord. Mad as a spring hare. Talks to stones.* A tinker swore she ate cinders and wept smoke. A child, who should not have been listening to any of it, said simply that she had seen her once—a woman standing at the cave mouth with her arms open to the wind, letting it take things from her hands.

Felix listened to all of it while tasting the chancellor's wine, holding each rumor on her tongue like a suspect coin, biting down for the ring of truth beneath the base metal. The madwoman detail was embellishment—madness was what comfortable people called anyone who remembered too much. The eating-cinders bit was invention. But the woman at the cave mouth, arms open, giving something away to the wind—that rang. That rang like a struck bell.

So on her third free evening in a month, Felix Amberdale tied a scarf over the distinctive copper of her hair, put on a dyer's smock that made her look like every other gray-faced laborer on the mountain roads, and went looking for a disgraced priestess of gods that everyone else had agreed to forget.

* * *

The Ashen Caves sat two days' walk north of Thornfell's walls, up switchback paths where the smuggler maps ended and only memory continued. Felix knew these paths the way other people knew their own kitchens—which ledges held weight and which shed stone, which hollows sheltered sleepers and which funneled cold air down from the heights like a throat swallowing warmth. She had learned them for Tammy. Every route she'd memorized had been an escape planned twice over: one way out for him, one way back in for her. Nine years of planning rescues for a brother who was never coming home, and the knowledge had settled into her bones like marrow, useless and load-bearing both.

The second day the trees thinned to scrub, then to nothing but lichen-crusted rock and a silence so complete she could hear her own blood moving. Ash lay everywhere—not fresh ash, not the ash of any fire she knew, but old gray drifts that had no source, piling in the lee of stones as though the mountain itself were slowly, quietly burning somewhere out of sight.

She smelled the shrine before she saw it. Not incense—Felix's nose was worth more than most people's houses, trained on poisons until sweetness itself had become a language—but something underneath incense. Charcoal and myrrh gone stale, beeswax, dried yarrow, and beneath it all a mineral tang like rain on hot flint. The smell of very old worship.

The cave mouth opened in the cliff face like a missing tooth, hung with ragged prayer flags so faded they'd gone colorless, and just inside, sheltered from the wind, stood a woman with her back turned, feeding a fire made entirely of bones and ash-pressed briquettes.

"You walk quiet," the woman said without turning around. Her voice was rough as a whetstone, unused to conversation. "But your stomach walks loud. I heard you chewing candied ginger since the switchbacks."

Felix stopped dead on the threshold. Her hand drifted toward the knife at her hip before her mind caught up with the fact that there was no threat here—or rather, that the threat was of a kind knives didn't answer.

"Ginger settles the stomach," she said carefully. "Altitude."

"Altitude." The woman snorted. "Ginger settles guilt, is what it does. My mother chewed it every market day after she shorted the wool merchants." Now she turned, and Felix got her first real look at Sera Vane.

She was younger than the rumors suggested—forty perhaps, or fifty worn badly—and thin in the way of someone who forgot meals rather than someone denied them. Her hair had gone iron-gray at the temples though the rest still held black, and her eyes were the trouble. They were quick eyes, bright eyes, eyes that tracked Felix's face like a scholar reading a difficult text, and they were set in a face that had clearly spent years talking to no one, because when she looked at another human being she looked *hard*, drinking it in, cataloguing it, hoarding the sight the way a starving woman hoards bread.

"You're not a pilgrim," Sera said. "Pilgrims bring offerings. You brought ginger and a knife you keep touching like a rosary. You're not a thief; thieves don't climb two days for one madwoman. And you're not with the crown, because crown folk smell of ink and fear and you—" she leaned forward, nostrils flaring, "—you smell of kitchens and roads and *ash*. You've been near the sweetwater river, haven't you? Within the last fortnight. It clings."

It was on Felix's boots, she realized. The silt from the banks at Sweetwater, ground into the leather, faintly bitter even now. She'd tasted that water herself, kneeling at the flood-line where thirty years of drought had ended overnight, and found under all that miracle-sweetness a thread of wrongness, a bitterness like medicine or grief. The whole valley was leaving offerings there—bread, ribbon, carved toys, whole slaughtered lambs on some nights—and Felix alone had knelt and thought: *what is being paid, and what is being bought?*

"I need to know if the stories are true," Felix said. "That the old court is waking. Under the mountain."

Something moved behind Sera Vane's face—like weather crossing a valley, shadow then light then shadow again.

"Stories," she repeated. "Yes. Let's call them stories. Stories can be dismissed. Stories are safely *past*." She gestured sharply at the cave interior. "Come in, then, kitchen-witch, road-rat, ash-smeller. Come see what my stories have become."

* * *

The shrine inside was small and fierce: a single chamber lit by bone-fire and oil lamps whose flames burned a shade too steady, as if the air itself were afraid to disturb them. Niches had been cut into the walls, dozens of them, and in each niche sat an offering or a relic—a bird skull crowned with wax, a rusted bell, a child's shoe filled with grave-dirt, a clay tablet scored with spiraling script that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts.

And covering everything—the walls, the ceiling, the flat faces of stone between niches—were names.

Thousands of them. Scratched, painted, charred into the rock in charcoal and ochre and what Felix sincerely hoped was rust. Some were careful and formal, block capitals in an older alphabet. Others were scrawled, frantic, layered ten deep, names written over names written over names until the wall had become a palimpsest of the forgotten.

"What is this?" Felix asked, though her skin had already begun answering for her. Gooseflesh walked up her arms in slow columns.

"A ledger." Sera fed a briquette to the bone-fire. "Every name I could find. Every soul the mountain took, before the Concord sealed it, before the kings decided the old gods were embarrassing and paved over the shrines. The court beneath doesn't just eat bodies, girl. It eats *selves*. Takes the name, takes the memory, takes the shape of a life, and leaves the body walking around doing chores with nothing home inside. I wrote them down because"—her voice cracked, steadied—"because a thing unwritten cannot be grieved properly. And un-grieved things come back wrong."

She touched the wall, flat-palmed, reverent, the way other people touched the sick.

"Nine years ago the answering stopped," Sera said quietly. "Prayers went up and nothing came down. I told myself the Concord held. I told myself that for nine years, and then last month the dry river filled overnight, and the barley came up green in a single night in fields that hadn't been seeded, and the offerings started appearing on the banks like—like tribute." Her hand curled into a fist against the wall of names. "Tribute isn't gratitude, kitchen-witch. Tribute is what you leave for something that has woken up hungry."

"The crops blooming," Felix said. "The wells running sweet. The crown blames smugglers."

"The crown would blame smugglers for the weather." Sera laughed, and it was a terrible sound, rusty and cracked, laughter that hadn't been oiled in years. "No. This is the court beneath. I'd stake my—well. I already staked everything, didn't I?" She spread her arms at the cave, the poverty, the exile. "This is what staking gets you."

"Then help me stop it."

Sera turned slowly. For a long moment she only looked at Felix, that hard starved reading gaze, and Felix felt it move over her like a lamp passed along her body, checking seals, checking locks, checking the doors.

"No," Sera said.

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear it. Whatever you want—revenge, answers, treasure, the crown's favor—it's a coin I stopped accepting nine years ago. I gave the old gods everything. My order, my name in the city rolls, my voice, my—" She stopped. Swallowed. "They took the offering and went silent anyway. Nine years of silence. Do you know what that does to a person? To pray into a well for nine years and hear nothing but your own echo getting thinner?" She shook her head. "I'm done being the priestess of a god who won't pick up. Whatever wakes under the mountain can wake, feast, and sleep again without me. Find someone else to burn."

"It's not for me," Felix said. "It's for my brother."

The words came out smaller than she intended. They always did. Nine years and she had never once said it aloud to anyone—had kept Tammy folded up inside herself like a letter she couldn't bear to reread—and now it escaped into a stranger's shrine and hung in the lamplight between them.

"He was sold," Felix said. "When he was six. Our mother owed debts to men who collect in ways coin can't cover, and the collectors came in the night wearing masks of woven root, and they took him through a door in the hillside that wasn't there afterward. I was twelve. I hid. I hid because hiding was the only thing I was good at, and I have hated myself with great discipline ever since." Her jaw ached; she realized she was speaking through her teeth. "I've spent nine years learning every road, every tunnel, every crack in this province, planning how to get him back. And last month I tasted the sweetwater and it was bitter underneath, and I understood that whatever took him is waking up, and that his time—"

She stopped. Steadied.

"There's a contract," she said. "There must be. Debts have terms. His expires at midwinter. Four months. And when it does, whatever's left of him won't remember our mother's lullaby, won't remember his own dog, won't remember—" Her voice failed, and she hated it, and pushed through. "He'll be a chore-doing body with nothing home inside. So I need someone who knows the old rites. Someone who knows how contracts are broken."

Silence filled the shrine like water fills a cup.

Sera Vane had gone very still. Her eyes had changed—not the hungry reading gaze now but something rawer, something that looked, absurdly, like relief. As if Felix's grief were the first honest thing offered at this altar in nine years.

"A contract," Sera whispered. "A soul-contract with a term. You're certain?"

"I'm certain about midwinter. I'm certain about the masks of woven root. I saw the door close."

"The root-masks." Sera pressed both hands over her mouth. When she lowered them, tears were cutting clean tracks through the ash dust on her cheeks, and she seemed entirely unaware of them. "The toll-collectors. Oh, you poor, stubborn girl. Do you understand what you're asking? Breaking a contract means renegotiating with the throne of roots and bone. There is no force that opens those terms. There is no army, no key, no saint—"

"Then what is there?"

Sera stared at her. And Felix watched something happen in the ruined priestess's face: the long collapse of nine years of self-protective silence, story by story, wall by wall, the ledger of names trembling on its foundations.

"There's the Warden," Sera said at last, barely above a whisper. "Keeper of the thresholds. Older than the court, maybe. It answers divination—when it answers. It speaks only in questions. The seminary called it unreliable." A wet, broken laugh. "The seminary called me unreliable too, in the end."

She looked at the wall of names—at the thousands of selves scratched into stone by her own hand—and then at Felix, and something in her straightened, vertebra by vertebra, like a flag remembering its pole.

"If your brother holds a contract," she said, "then there is a document. Documents have clauses. Clauses have loopholes, and loopholes have *prices*, and the price will be monstrous, and I will not pretend otherwise to spare you." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, leaving a smear of ash like war paint across one cheekbone. "But I have spent nine years writing down the taken because no one else would remember them. I am not going to stand in my cave and let another name expire unread."

She crossed to a niche and lifted down a bundle wrapped in oilcloth: rods of pale wood, a bowl blackened by ancient smoke, a pouch that chimed softly with what sounded like teeth.

"We do the divination tonight," Sera said. "Before I lose my nerve, and before you do. And girl—" her eyes pinned Felix in place, bright and terrible and awake for the first time in nine years, "—whatever the Warden asks you, you answer honestly. These things smell a lie the way you smell poison. And I think—I think you have been lying about yourself for so long that you may have forgotten which parts are true."

Outside, the wind moved through the colorless prayer flags, and far below, so deep that Felix felt it in her back teeth more than heard it, the mountain made a sound like a great door being tested on its hinges.

Not opening yet.

But waking.

* * *

Night fell fast on the heights. Sera built the divination in the shrine's center: the blackened bowl filled with ash from her bone-fire, the pale rods laid in a spiral around it, and from the chiming pouch, seven teeth—not animal teeth, Felix noted with a professional taster's eye for the mislabeled, but human, small ones, milk teeth—set point-down in the ash at the spiral's heart.

"Milk teeth," Sera murmured, catching her look. "Given freely by mothers, generations back. The oldest currency there is. The Warden honors what's given freely." She struck a taper and lit the ash. It did not burn so much as glow, a low red pulse like an ember breathing, and the smell rose—charcoal, myrrh, and under it that mineral flint-rain tang, stronger now, almost alive.

"Sera Vane," she said formally, to the bowl, to the cave, to whatever listened beneath both. "Disgraced of the Order of the Quiet Flame. I bring a question concerning a contract held by the court beneath."

The glowing ash pulsed once, twice.

Then every lamp in the shrine guttered except the bone-fire, and the temperature dropped as though the mountain had exhaled through the room, and a voice came—not from the bowl, not from the walls, but from *everywhere at once*, from the stone itself, dry as pages turning:

**WHO ASKS?**

Felix's mouth had gone dry as communion bread. "F—" she began, and the word snagged in her throat like a fishhook, and what came out instead was: "Someone who wants her brother back."

A pause. The ash pulsed. And Felix had the distinct, skin-crawling impression that the pause was amused.

**SOMEONE,** the Warden said, **WHO CANNOT SAY HER OWN NAME. HOW INTERESTING. WHAT WAS TAKEN CAN BE LISTED. WHAT WAS SOLD CAN BE PRICED. BUT TELL ME, LITTLE KEEPER'S FRIEND—**

The milk teeth in the ash rattled, all seven at once, like dice in a cup.

**—WHAT IS A BROTHER, IF THE BROTHER REMEMBERS NOTHING? AND WHAT ARE YOU, IF NO ONE REMEMBERS YOU?**

"I remember him enough for both of us," Felix said, and her voice shook and held.

**DO YOU.** Something vast shifted its attention, and the pressure of it made her ears pop. **THEN HEAR THE ONLY ANSWER I GIVE FREELY, NAMELESS ONE, AND LET IT KEEP YOU WARM ON YOUR ROAD: WHAT WAS GIVEN CANNOT BE STOLEN. IT CAN ONLY BE TRADED.**

"And traded for what?" Sera demanded. "Warden—Warden, speak plainly for once in your endless—"

**PLAINLY?** The dry voice turned upon her, and even furious, even terrified, Felix heard the weariness underneath it, centuries-deep, a keeper tired of keeping. **VERY WELL. PLAINLY: THE MOUNTAIN IS HUNGRY, THE LEDGER IS OPEN, AND MIDWINTER COMES FOR EVERY UNPAID DEBT.**

The lamps relit. The ash went dark. The cave was only a cave again, cold and smelling of charcoal, and Sera Vane stood with her fists clenched and her lips moving silently through fragments of old rites, and Felix Amberdale stood very still in the middle of the fading warmth, hearing the question repeat itself inside her own skull like a bell that would not finish ringing.

*What are you, if no one remembers you?*

She had an answer ready—she'd had it ready for nine years. *No one. Nobody. The girl who isn't there.*

But kneeling in a dead priestess's shrine, with the taste of miracle-bitter water still on her boots and her brother's expiring name scratched nowhere on any wall, Felix found—for the first time—that the answer wouldn't come.

And outside, far below, the mountain turned over in its sleep, and waited for midwinter.
