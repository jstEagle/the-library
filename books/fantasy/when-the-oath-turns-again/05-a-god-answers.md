## 5. A God Answers

The ash did not want to burn.

Sera Vane had tended fires for thirty years—first as a girl at her mother's hearth in the river villages, then as an acolyte feeding the censers of the old shrines, and finally here, alone, in a cave above the drowned chapel where the only congregation was the wind and the only hymn the drip of water through limestone. She knew fire the way other priests knew scripture. She knew when wood was willing and when it sulked, when resin wept sweet from pine and when damp had crept too deep into the grain to ever be coaxed out.

Tonight the ashwood sat in her brazier like a stone wearing bark.

"It knows," she said aloud, to no one, to the cave, to herself. "It knows what I mean to ask."

Behind her, the food-taster waited on the flat rock that served Sera as both altar and table, hands folded in her lap with the terrible patience of someone accustomed to waiting—outside doors, outside conversations, outside the whole bright theater of court life where everyone else got to be seen. Nine days the woman had been coming back to the shrine, each time asking something slightly different, each time circling the same wound: *my brother, my brother, my brother.*

"You could say it plainly," Sera said, striking flint against iron for the fourth time. The sparks fell dead into the brazier's throat. "You could just tell me what you're afraid of."

"I'm not afraid." Felix Amberdale's voice was level, but Sera heard the seam in it, the place where the level note was stitched over something rawer. "I want to know if the mountain answers prayers. And if it does—" a pause, brief as a swallowed breath "—what it charges."

"There it is." The flint sparked again, and this time a thread of smoke rose from the ashwood, thin and gray and reluctant. "Everyone comes to the gods wanting gifts. It takes a special kind of fool to come asking about the bill."

Sera rose stiffly, knees cracking like green kindling, and moved through the ritual she had not performed in eleven years—not since the synod had stripped her stole and burned her name from the rolls, *disgraced priestess*, *heretic*, *the woman who listened*. Her hands remembered anyway. Hands always remember; it is the heart that forgets, and then pretends it never knew.

She drew the circle in sifted ash, nine feet across, open at the north because the old gods entered through wounds. She set the four offerings at the cardinal points: salt for what endures, honey for what sweetens, a black feather for what flies between worlds, and—last, and most dangerous—a bowl of clean water, which would show them whatever chose to be shown.

"Sit inside," she told Felix. "North side. If anything comes, you do not speak first. You do not speak last. Ideally, you don't speak at all."

"And if it speaks to me?"

"Then answer honestly," Sera said grimly, taking up her place at the circle's southern edge, "because they can smell a lie the way you can taste arsenic."

Felix's eyebrows lifted—the first expression Sera had seen crack that careful face in nine days—and she stepped over the ash line and knelt where she was bid, small and still in the firelight, a woman built entirely of quiet.

Sera lit the censers. She spoke the opening words of the Rite of Listening, the ones carved into standing stones older than any kingdom, older than Thornfell's crown, older than the names people gave to the nameless. Her voice was rustier than her hands. The words came out cracked, half-swallowed, and she felt her cheeks burn with the shame of it—a disgraced priestess mumbling prayers in a hole in the earth, performing divination for a smuggler's sister and a taster's tongue, fallen so far from the marble halls of the synod that the fall itself had become a kind of home.

But the smoke behaved strangely.

That was the first sign. Incense should rise. Everyone knows this; even children know this; smoke climbs because it is trying to leave. This smoke climbed three feet and then bent, all of it, every ribbon of it, bending inward toward the center of the circle until it hung above the bowl of water in a column that did not move, that did not disperse, that simply *waited*—and Sera felt the hairs on her arms rise beneath her sleeves, because she had performed this rite perhaps two hundred times in her life, and in two hundred performances the smoke had never once been patient.

The candle flames went still. Not guttered. *Still*—standing upright like soldiers at attention, like things holding their breath.

"Sera," Felix whispered.

"I see it."

"What is—"

"Don't speak."

But the cave was already speaking for them. Or rather, something in the cave had begun to speak, though Sera would spend the rest of her life unable to say precisely how she heard it. There was no sound, exactly. There was pressure—a change in the weight of the air, the way a room feels different when a door opens somewhere far away. And threaded through that pressure, faint as a voice heard through the wall of a drowning ship:

***Who asks?***

Sera's mouth had gone dry as the ash in her brazier. Thirty years of faith, eleven years of exile, and now, when the thing she had spent her whole life listening for finally leaned close enough to touch, she found she could barely remember how words worked.

"Seraphine Vane," she managed, using the name she had not spoken since the synod took it, and the saying of it hurt like pressing on a bruise she'd forgotten she carried. "Daughter of Marla, keeper of the shrine at Grayfall Hollow. Once priestess of the—"

***No.***

The word arrived like a hand laid flat upon her lips. Not unkind. Simply final.

***Not your titles. Titles are coats. Who asks?***

Sera blinked. Beside her, Felix had gone very still in the way of prey animals, which Sera supposed was accurate.

"A woman," Sera said slowly, testing. "A listener. A—"

***Better.***

The column of smoke shivered. In the bowl of water, the surface—which had reflected the candles, the ceiling, their two bowed heads—now showed nothing at all. Blackness. Depth. The particular darkness of very deep places, of wells and caves and the spaces beneath roots.

***And the other.*** The pressure turned, and Sera felt it settle on Felix like a physical weight, like snow sliding from a branch. ***She has asked nothing yet. But she is full of questions. They leak from her. Why does the silent one come to the listening?***

Felix's jaw tightened. Sera saw her weigh silence against need, watch the scales tip, watch her decide.

"My brother," Felix said. Her voice was steady. Sera admired her for it and pitied her for needing it. "Tamson Amberdale. He was taken by collectors nine years ago. Sold, the way you sell a debt. I've been told the court beneath Thornfell Mountain holds him. I've been told his time runs out at midwinter. I want to know if it's true, and I want to know how to get him back."

Silence. The good, deep, listening kind—or the hungry kind; Sera genuinely could not tell, and the not-knowing sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

Then the water in the bowl moved.

Nothing touched it. No draft reached this chamber; Sera had chosen this cave precisely because no draft reached it. But the black surface trembled once at its center, twice, rings spreading outward as though something vast far below had turned over in its sleep—

—and images rose in the dark like fish rising to feed.

A corridor of pale stone, lit by no torches and yet luminous, walls veined with root-systems that pulsed faintly, slowly, in rhythms too long to be breath and too deliberate to be chance. Figures moving through the corridor—beautiful figures, robed in colors that hurt to look at, faces smooth and serene and utterly empty, like masks worn by no one. A hall. A throne. Sera's stomach lurched, because the throne was made of roots and bone braided together so intimately she could not tell which strand had been living and which had died, and seated upon it—

The image cut away before she could see what sat there. In its place: a boy.

Small. Thin. Perhaps ten years old in the way the image rendered him, though he must be nineteen now—time moved differently down there, Sera knew that much from the oldest texts, the ones the synod had burned. He sat cross-legged on the pale floor of some endless hall, and he was smiling at nothing, and his eyes were the eyes of the hollow courtiers, serene and vacant, and around his neck hung a cord of woven hair with a knot at the end that glowed faintly gold.

Felix made a sound. Small. Terrible. The sound of someone being shown a wound they'd spent nine years pretending had closed.

"That's him," she whispered. "That's Tammy. That knot—that's the contract, isn't it. I've seen tokens like it. Smugglers carry them. Shrine-tokens."

***It is the tally of what he owes,*** the Warden said—for Sera understood suddenly, with certainty that arrived whole like an egg dropped intact from a great height, that this was the Warden, the keeper, the tired old god whose name appeared in exactly one fragment of one burned text she had memorized at nineteen and never forgotten. ***Nine years purchased. Nine years nearly paid. What was bought with borrowed coin must return to the lender.***

"Then I'll steal him back," Felix said.

***No.***

The word landed in the chamber like a struck bell. The candle flames all leaned sideways at once, though no wind moved.

***You misunderstand what is kept there.*** The smoke column thickened, descended, began to take shape—not a shape, precisely, more the suggestion of one, tall and narrow and stooped, like a figure carrying something heavy on its back for so long it had grown into the posture. ***The court does not hold bodies. Bodies are easy. Bodies rot and are released. The court holds selves. Your brother's self is written in the ledger of the deep place, signed by those who sold it, sealed in the manner of contracts. What is given cannot be stolen, child of the hidden roads. Only traded.***

"Traded for what?" Felix's voice cracked on the word, and Sera heard everything underneath the crack: the nine years, the guilt, the arithmetic of a debt paid in a sibling's flesh.

The shape in the smoke seemed to regard her. If it had eyes, Sera could not find them. But she felt attention the way one feels weather changing.

***What do the ledgers of the deep honor?*** it asked. ***What is the one coin every soul carries and cannot counterfeit?***

"Gold," Felix said. "Secrets. Blood—"

***No. No. No.*** Each denial came gentler than the last, and somehow that was worse. ***Think, little taster. You of all people know what cannot be faked. You have spent your life tasting truth in a court of lies. When you put a thing upon your tongue, you know what it truly is. So. What is the substance of a self?***

And Sera saw it land on Felix's face—the understanding arriving, and the horror arriving half a heartbeat behind it, because understanding and horror were traveling together tonight, as they always did, as they had since the beginning of worship.

"Names," Felix said.

***Names,*** the Warden agreed, and the whole cave exhaled, and the candle flames stood upright again, and somewhere far above them an owl called once and fell silent as if ashamed of the interruption. ***A name freely surrendered is the only currency the deep accepts. Not taken—taking is theft, and theft curdles. Not sold—selling is barter, and barter binds both parties. Given. Freely, wholly, with the whole weight of the life behind it. Then the ledger opens, and what is written may be rewritten.***

The smoke shape was already thinning. Sera felt the presence withdrawing the way one feels warmth leaving a room—gradually, then all at once.

"Wait," she said, and her voice came out younger than she was, the voice of the acolyte she had been, the girl who had believed the gods loved their listeners. "Wait—you've answered. The gods haven't answered in three hundred years. Why now? Why us?"

The shape paused at the edge of dissolution. When it spoke again, Sera thought she heard, beneath the vastness, something that sounded almost like weariness—the exhaustion of a doorkeeper who has stood at his post past the end of his shift, past the end of the age, watching the road for travelers who stopped coming.

***Because the court wakes,*** it said. ***And a waking court grows hungry. And hunger makes even keepers careless.*** A pause. ***One question more, and I will ask it of you both, and you will carry it whether you wish to or not.***

The bowl of water stilled. In its black mirror, Sera caught a glimpse—half a second, no more—of a figure: tall, gaunt, draped in robes the color of old keys, face lined and sorrowful and human in a way that frightened her more than any monster would have. A keeper. A tired man-shaped eternity.

***Who are you,*** the Warden asked, ***if no one remembers you?***

Then the pressure lifted. The smoke collapsed into ordinary gray ribbons and drifted up toward the ceiling cracks. The candle flames remembered how to flicker. The water in the bowl showed only two women kneeling in ash, lit gold and shadow, staring at nothing.

For a long moment, neither of them moved.

Then Felix Amberdale laughed—one short, sharp, bloodless sound with no humor anywhere in it—and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

"He wouldn't tell me," she said. "I ask the one question that matters and it gives me a riddle and a price tag. Names. Of course names. Everything in my life is names—I don't have one anyone remembers, my brother doesn't remember *himself*, and now the only way to buy him back is—"

She stopped. Sera watched her stop, watched the sentence die mid-flight, watched Felix arrive at the end of the thought and refuse to look at it.

"To give the mountain a name," Sera finished quietly. "Freely given. Whole. With the whole weight of a life behind it."

"Yes." Felix lowered her hands. Her face in the candlelight was composed again, armored again, and Sera realized with a strange ache that the armor was going back up because that was what Felix Amberdale did—she survived things by becoming less, by shrinking, by making herself a person no ledger would bother to write down. "Well. Good news. I know plenty of names I'd love to be rid of. Corvane. Half the court. We'll start a list."

"Felix."

"It's fine. It's *fine*, priestess. We'll figure out the trick of it. There's always a trick. Gods love loopholes, everyone knows that—"

"*Felix.*" Sera reached across the ash circle and took the woman's wrist—bony under her sleeve, pulse jumping quick as a trapped bird. "You didn't hear what I heard. That thing wasn't lying. It wasn't even *trying* to deceive. It was sad. Did you feel it? It's been keeping those doors for three hundred years, watching the court sleep, hoping no one would ever come knock again. And now the court is waking, and it told us the truth anyway, because the truth is all it has left to give." She let go of the wrist. Her own hands were shaking now; they hadn't shaken during the rite, but they shook now, in the aftermath, when shaking was safe. "There's no loophole. There's no trick. There's a price, and it's paid in the only coin we each carry, and someone in this cave is going to have to decide what their name weighs."

Felix stared at her. And Sera watched—helpless, witness to it, the way she had been a witness her entire cursed life—as the armor failed. Not all at once. It cracked along seams Sera couldn't see, and through the cracks came something young and terrified and nine years grief-deep, a woman who had spent her whole adult life being no one so that no one could ever again be taken from her, sitting in the ruins of that strategy, hearing the terms of a bargain that required her to be someone worth trading.

"His name weighed eight pounds when he was born," Felix said, barely above a whisper. "Our mother told me that. Eight pounds, and he screamed the whole first night, and she sang to him until dawn, and after that he never cried at night again. That's what his name weighs. That's what it *weighs*." She looked down at the dead ash circle, at the empty bowl. "What does mine weigh, Sera? Does it weigh anything? Can you trade a name nobody knows?"

Sera had no answer. She was a disgraced priestess in a cave with a dying fire, and the god had asked its question and left, and the question was still in the room, still settling into the stone, into their bones, into the years ahead of them.

Outside, far below, the town of Grayfall Hollow slept beside its newly risen river, and the villagers had left offerings on the banks again that evening—bread, honey, cut flowers, a lamb's femur wrapped in red wool—and Sera thought of the water's faint bitterness, the wrongness she had tasted and not named, and wondered what else was waking hungry beneath the world, and what it would ask of people who had forgotten how to read its signs.

"We start with the letters," she said at last, rising, her joints protesting, her faith—eleven years dead—stirring somewhere in her chest like embers under ash. "Old law says a surrendering soul writes testament. Letters. To everyone who might remember her after." She met Felix's eyes. "We'll need paper. And wine. And probably more courage than either of us owns."

Felix nodded slowly, mechanically, already retreating behind her eyes to wherever she went when the world became too much to stand inside.

But at the mouth of the cave, pausing with the night wind lifting her hair, she glanced back.

"The Warden asked who I am if no one remembers me," she said. "Someday I'm going to find that creature and ask it the same thing right back."

She stepped into the dark. Sera sat alone among the ashes, and began, in her head, the first line of a letter she very much hoped she would never have to finish.

*To whoever is left to read this—*

* * *

Three nights later, a rider in chancellor's livery came up the goat track to Grayfall Hollow asking questions about a woman with a taster's tongue, and Sera, watching from the shrine's high ledge, understood that the mountain below was not the only thing that had begun to hunt.
