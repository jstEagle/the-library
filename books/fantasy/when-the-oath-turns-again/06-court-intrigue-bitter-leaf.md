## 6. Court Intrigue, Bitter Leaf

The summons came on a morning that smelled of frost and tallow, delivered by a boy who wouldn't meet Felix's eyes.

"Her Ladyship's steward requests the taster in the chancellor's wing. Midday meal." He said it the way one announces an execution date—apologetically, already retreating.

Felix turned the words over as she dressed. The chancellor's wing. Not the queen's table, where she had knelt for nine years among the other invisible instruments of the crown, but Lord Aldric Corvane's private apartments, where the food was chosen by Corvane himself and the wine was poured by servants whose silence was bought at rates Felix could only guess at.

She had eaten from the queen's table through three assassination attempts, one slow poisoning of the whole court via imported honey, and the infamous night of the Duke of Marrow's wedding, when forty guests had vomited black and two had not gotten up. Through all of it her tongue had told her the truth, and her face had told no one anything.

That was the armor. That was always the armor: be forgettable, be dull, be the woman whose name people had to reach for twice before giving up. Nine years of practiced mediocrity, of a face like unremarkable bread, of a voice kept low and a laugh kept rare. She had made herself into a locked room, and the court had obligingly stopped knocking.

But the chancellor's wing was not a room where one went to eat. It was a room where one went to be *read*.

* * *

Corvane's apartments occupied the tower's eastern face, all pale stone and tall windows that caught the winter light and did nothing warm with it. Everything in the room was beautiful the way a drawn blade is beautiful: the marble mantel, the map of Thornfell's borders laid out beneath glass, the man himself standing at the window with his hands folded behind his back as though he'd been sculpted mid-contemplation by someone paid by the hour.

Lord Aldric Corvane was fifty, perhaps, with silver threading his dark hair and a face built for portraits—strong jaw, patient eyes, the kind of mouth that smiled exactly when smiling served him. The court called him the Iron Chancellor, and meant it fondly, because he had stabilized the treasury, reformed the granaries, and buried four rivals with such impeccable legality that even their ghosts filed no complaints.

"Taster Amberdale." He didn't turn when she entered. "You'll forgive the unusual venue. I find I trust the queen's table less and less these days."

"The poisonings, my lord?" Felix kept her voice flat, incurious, the voice of a woman who thought about nothing more dangerous than gravy.

"Mm. Among other things." Now he turned, and his gaze crossed her the way a customs officer's hands cross a traveler's luggage—thorough, unhurried, pretending to find nothing. "Sit. Eat. That is what you do, isn't it? Eat."

The table was set for two, which was wrong in a way that made her skin prickle. Tasters ate standing, off to the side, from plates set apart. Tasters were furniture. Furniture did not get a chair across from the most powerful man in Thornfell.

She sat anyway. Refusal would be a data point, and she refused to give him data points.

The first course was simple: a pheasant pie, browned and fragrant, steam curling up in ribbons. She cut a portion, tasted it, and let the ordinary flavors file past her tongue like guards changing shift. Butter. Thyme. A whisper of nutmeg, slightly stale. Nothing.

Corvane watched her eat with the pleasant attention of a man watching a horse he intended to buy.

"You've been with Her Majesty nine years," he said. "Remarkable tenure. Remarkably *unremarkable*, I should say. No scandals. No promotions. No one can seem to recall anything about you at all, which in this court is either a great achievement or a great concealment."

"I try to do my work and let others do the talking, my lord."

"Yes. I've noticed you do." He poured wine himself, which was also wrong; chancellors did not pour. "Do you know what I admire about your profession, taster? Its honesty. Everyone else at court lies with their mouths full. You alone are paid to tell the truth about what goes down your throat."

The second course arrived under a silver dome, carried by a servant whose hands did not tremble, which itself was information—the ones who knew what they were carrying either didn't care or had been paid not to. Corvane lifted the dome himself.

Under it lay a dish Felix did not recognize. That alone was remarkable; she knew every dish in the palace kitchens, every cook's signature, every regional variation that came up the river roads. This was none of them. Small, dark leaves, glistening as though glazed, arranged over something pale and gelatinous, the whole of it smelling faintly of rain on hot stone—a smell that had no business being in a kitchen at all.

"A delicacy," Corvane said. "From the mountain provinces. Eaten raw, or nearly so. I'm told the flavor is... acquired."

Felix looked at the leaves. Her tongue, ahead of her mind, had already begun its quiet inventory—and found the thing underneath the glaze, the thread woven through the whole dish like a hair through bread. Bitter. Not the honest bitterness of greens or tonic bark, which announce themselves and pass. This bitterness *moved*. It sat on the back of her tongue and shifted, the way a living thing shifts, and beneath it ran a second note she had never tasted in nine years of professional vigilance: something cold and mineral and old, like licking the inside of a well.

Nine years. Three assassination attempts. A honey poisoning, a plum laced with widow's-tears, arsenic in the wedding soup. She had catalogued every poison known to the guilds, the alchemists, the jealous wives. She knew their tastes the way a mother knows her children's footsteps.

This was none of them.

*It shouldn't exist,* part of her thought, distantly, the way one thinks while falling. *Nothing on my tongue should be unknown.*

And Corvane was watching her. Not her plate. Her *face*.

Here is what Felix Amberdale understood about the next three seconds: everything. Her immunity was a secret kept so deep that even the physician who patched the court's dyspepsia believed she survived by caution rather than constitution. If she flinched—if her eyes watered, if her throat moved wrong—he would know she'd tasted it. If she tasted it without reaction, he would know *that* instead. The dish was not food. It was a question, and every possible answer condemned her.

So she gave him the only answer left: the truth of a bored professional.

She took a full bite.

Chewed. Swallowed. Let her brow crease—not in alarm, but in mild, gustatory disappointment, the expression of a woman eating something expensive she didn't care for. She reached for the wine, sipped, and grimaced faintly at it too.

"It's bitter, my lord," she said. "Whoever prepared it used leaves past their prime. And whatever's under them—" she nudged the pale substance with her fork "—has been sitting near onions. It's taken the smell."

A silence. Corvane's eyes stayed on hers for a long, long moment, and Felix held them the way one holds a door shut against wind—with the whole body, smiling politely, aware that the door was thinner than anyone guessed.

"Past their prime," he repeated softly. "Yes. I imagine they would be. They're quite difficult to source fresh." He leaned back. "Tell me, taster—what *is* the strangest thing you've ever tasted?"

The question was a knife wearing a glove. She considered her answer with the deliberateness of dullness. "Medicine. When I was small, my mother dosed us with willowbark and wormwood for fevers. Wormwood's the worst. Like drinking a churchyard."

Something flickered behind his eyes—satisfaction, or its rehearsal. "A churchyard," he said. "How evocative. Well." He rose, signaling dismissal with the grace of a man closing a ledger. "You've been most helpful. Her Majesty needn't hear of this little meal. Consider it a private matter between gourmets."

"Of course, my lord."

At the door, his voice caught her like a hand at her collar, gentle as anything: "Oh—one more thing. Your brother. Tammy, isn't it? Sold south some years ago. Do you ever hear from him?"

The floor tilted. Felix kept her hand steady on the frame and her face empty as a washed plate.

"No, my lord. We lost touch."

"Pity. Families ought to stay close. One never knows when—" he smiled, warm as banked coals, "—when someone might need looking after. Good day, taster."

* * *

She walked back across the palace grounds with her spine straight and her mind screaming.

He knew the name. Tammy's name was not court knowledge; it wasn't *anyone's* knowledge. She had buried it under nine years of dust and dullness, had never spoken it aloud within the palace walls, had let even her own grief go unnamed so that no thread existed for anyone to pull. And Corvane had just pulled it casually, gently, the way one tests whether a wall is load-bearing.

He hadn't learned it today. He'd *held* it. Held it the way he'd held that dish—waiting to see what moved underneath.

In the shadow of the buttery, out of sight of the windows, Felix finally allowed herself to breathe, and the breath came out shaking. The unknown bitterness still sat at the root of her tongue, cold and mineral and old, and now that she let herself think clearly, she recognized the shape of the smell that had come off those leaves. Rain on hot stone. She'd smelled it once before, weeks ago, kneeling on a riverbank where thirty years of dry stones ran sweet with impossible water.

Bitter under the sweetness. Wrong under the wonder.

The mountain's water. The mountain's taste. On the chancellor's table, in a dish he called a delicacy, fed to *her* specifically, watched for.

He knows, she thought. He knows what the water means, and he suspects what I am, and he has just spent a meal measuring me against both.

She pressed her tongue against the roof of her mouth, against that lingering cold note, and made herself name the truth she'd been circling all day:

All her life she had survived by being prey that predators overlooked. But Corvane had not overlooked her. Corvane had set a place for her, poured her wine, and asked her questions with a spoon—and somewhere between the pheasant pie and her brother's name, the armor had cracked, and the hunter had seen her plain.

And hunters who take the trouble to identify their prey, Felix knew, do not intend to watch it walk away for long.

By nightfall she had packed a bag, stolen a collector's token rumor-map from her mattress seam, and sent word down a smuggler's road to a half-mad priestess in a shrine cave: *I need to know how to get into the mountain. And I need to know it fast.*

Because midwinter was coming. And now there were two clocks running—Tammy's contract, and whatever Lord Aldric Corvane was brewing in his beautiful, blade-cold rooms.

She meant to beat them both.
