## 7. Roads Under Thornfell

The entrance to the Hollow Ways was not marked on any map, because the people who made the maps had never needed to go anywhere in secret.

Felix found it where she always found it: behind the tannery on Graywater Lane, where a drainage grate leaned against the wall like a drunk against a doorframe, and the smell of old lime covered the smell of old people. She knelt, hooked her fingers into the grate's rusted edge, and pulled. It came away with a sound like a tooth leaving a gum.

"You first," she said to Sera Vane. "Mind the third rung. It's been loose since before the war."

Sera looked at the black mouth of the tunnel, then at Felix, and her cracked lips curved in something too dry to be called a smile. "You've done this before."

"Once or twice." Four hundred times. She did not say so. "The ladder holds if you don't trust it."

The priestess went down into the dark with the awkwardness of someone whose body had spent years forgetting it was a body—Sera moved like a woman made mostly of prayer and ash, all sharp joints and hollow spaces. Felix followed, drawing the grate back over her head, and the last sliver of morning light narrowed to a coin, a thread, nothing.

Darkness took some getting used to, even for her. She counted her breaths until the black resolved into shapes: the wet gleam of the rungs below, the pale smear of Sera's shawl ahead, the fat drip of groundwater keeping its slow time somewhere to the left. The air tasted of stone and iron and, faintly, of the river they could no longer hear—the Sweetwater, running again after thirty years of drought, running sweet and wrong.

"The smugglers built this?" Sera's voice came back strange off the brickwork, flattened, as if the tunnel refused to carry anything honestly.

"Smugglers built the first mile," Felix said. "The rest grew. Tunnels do that under a city nobody watches. Someone needs to move a cask of untaxed brandy, so they dig. Someone else needs to move a person who doesn't want to be found, so they connect. Forty years of small greeds make a road." She brushed her fingers along the wall as she walked, reading the brick by touch the way other people read letters. "This stretch is pre-war. See the mortar? They mixed it with river sand, back when the river still ran. Deeper in it's ash mortar. You can date any passage in Thornfell by what it's holding together."

Sera stopped walking. In the dark, her eyes caught what little light there was and gave back something feverish. "You speak of them the way I used to speak of the litany."

"I have a good memory."

"That is not memory. That is love." The old woman said it gently, the way one names a wound. Then she turned and walked on, and Felix stood a moment in the dripping dark with the word lodged under her ribs like a fishbone.

She caught up. She did not answer it.

* * *

An hour in, the tunnels began to fork, and this was where most pursuers—and most guides—died of indecision.

Felix did not hesitate. Left at the junction where someone had chalked a fish that wasn't a fish but a warning meaning *flooded in spring*. Right at the low arch where the ceiling sweated in summer. Through the crawl beneath the collapsed chimney, which she called the Needle in her head because you had to thread yourself through it sideways, exhaling everything, trusting that your body was exactly the shape you believed it was.

Sera came out of the Needle coughing dust, and sat on an old packing crate marked with the faded crest of a wine merchant three decades dead.

"How many of these are there?" she asked, when she had her breath again.

"Under Thornfell? I've mapped two hundred and eleven ways that work and forty-some that don't anymore. Under the whole of the Thorn range—" Felix shrugged, though Sera couldn't see a shrug in the dark, and gave the number anyway, because numbers were safer than feelings. "Nine hundred. Maybe more. The mountain is honeycombed. Like a skull."

"A skull," Sera repeated softly. "Yes. That's a fair way to put it."

They walked on. Felix talked, because talking kept the other thing at bay, the thing waiting deeper than any tunnel: she talked about load-bearing arches and the difference between a collector's route and a courier's route, about how the smuggler roads avoided the mountain's gate by design because even criminals had instincts, about which passages smelled of sulfur and which of nothing at all, and how the ones that smelled of nothing were the ones you didn't take.

"Here." She stopped so suddenly that Sera nearly walked into her back.

Before them the tunnel opened into a chamber that had once been a cellar—a big one, vaulted, with iron rings set in the walls and a hearth blackened by a hundred careful fires. Someone had loved this room once. There were shelves carved into the rock, and a bench worn smooth, and scratched into the lintel above the far doorway, in a child's unsteady hand:

*T.A.*

Felix's lantern-hand shook once. She steadied it.

"Rest here," she said, too evenly. "We're under the Chandler's Quarter now. Half a night's walk left to the gate roads, and the last stretch isn't kind."

Sera lowered herself onto the bench. Her eyes went to the lintel, to the letters. She said nothing about them, which was its own kind of mercy, and then—being Sera, being incapable of true silence for longer than a prayer's length—she said everything about them anyway.

"Who taught you all this, child?"

* * *

"My mother sold my brother when he was seven."

The words came out of Felix the way water comes out of a cracked wall—not decided, just inevitable, given enough pressure.

Sera did not flinch. She folded her hands in her lap and waited, and Felix, standing with her back against the iron rings where cargo had once been chained, found that waiting was worse than questions, and so she kept going.

"Not sold the way the bakers sell bread. Sold the way you sell a field you can't farm. There was debt. There's always debt; it grows better than anything we ever planted. Mother owed money to men who collected for—she didn't know who, then. Neither did I. Men with gray gloves and polite voices and ledgers bound in skin that wasn't leather, though it took me years to learn why the books never cracked in winter." She heard her own voice go flat and distant and let it, because flat was survivable. "They didn't want coin. Coin was nothing to them. They wanted time, and time lives in people.

"Tammy was the payment. Seven years old, and they wrote his name in one of those books, and he went with them down through a door in the hillside east of the city, and our mother signed herself free and drank herself dead inside four years, and I—" Her breath caught. She let it go. "I was nine. I hid in the flour barrel, because Tammy told me once that if bad men ever came, the barrel was the best place, because no one ever looks where the food keeps. He thought of things like that. He was seven and he thought of things like that."

The drip of water counted the silence.

"After," Felix said, "I started learning the ways under the city. Everyone thought it was grief. Running away, they called it. The neighbors pitied the orphan girl who haunted the underground like a ghost looking for its grave." She laughed, short and joyless. "It wasn't grief. Or it was, but grief is just love with nowhere to go, and mine went down. Down every tunnel, every shaft, every crack in the world. Because the collectors' door opened onto these roads—I'd seen the direction they carried him, I'd memorized their footfalls like a fool child, *left at the fish that isn't a fish*, and I thought—if I learned every hidden way in Thornfell, someday I would know where they all led. And someday I would walk the right one, and find him, and bring him home."

She pushed off from the wall at last and looked at the lintel, at the small brave letters.

"T.A. Tammy Amberdale. He carved it himself the week before they took him. He said—he said it was so the room would remember him, even if nobody else did." Her voice finally broke, just slightly, just at the edge. "He was planning escape routes even then. He drew me maps in the dirt of how to get out of the house if the gray-gloved men ever came back for *me*. Two hundred and eleven ways under this city, Priestess, and I learned every one of them for a brother who never came home. Every tunnel is a sentence in a letter I never got to send him. *Hold on. Hold on. I'm coming. I'm learning the way.*"

Sera rose from the bench. She crossed the cellar slowly, her shawl whispering against the stone, and she did not embrace Felix—Felix would have shattered like bad mortar—but she touched the lintel, laid her whole palm flat over the child-carved letters, and spoke in her cracked litany-voice:

"He is remembered here. I bear witness."

And something in the chamber seemed to lean closer to listen.

* * *

They walked the last stretch in the smallest hours, and the mountain began, gradually, to be present.

Not visibly—it was visible all along, a vast dark shoulder north of the city—but *presently*, the way a listener becomes present in a room. The air thickened. The drips slowed. Sound itself grew reluctant, as though the tunnels were being asked to keep secrets and resented it. Felix noticed her footsteps falling softer without her willing it. The mountain muffled its guests. Or prepared its table.

"This is where the smuggler roads end," she murmured. Ahead, the passage widened into a natural fissure, walls slick with condensation, and from somewhere below came a sound she had never heard in nine hundred tunnels: a resonance, deep and slow, like breath drawn through roots. "Everything past here belongs to something older than greed. Old-timers called the next stretch the Throat. They wouldn't use it. Smugglers, mind you—men who'd cut a throat for profit wouldn't walk this one."

"And yet here we stand."

"Here we stand." Felix unslung her pack and checked it by feel: rope, tallow, the stolen collector's token wrapped in wool, cold even through the cloth, cold the way December is cold. "There's a ledge, fifty feet down the fissure, where the collectors come up. They use it like a door. I've watched it three nights running. Nothing comes out. But twice, something has gone *in*—a shape walking down the wall like it was stairs."

Sera peered into the fissure's dark and her face, in the lantern's guttering light, held less fear than terrible recognition. "The court is waking," she whispered. "Thirty years the rivers ran dry, and now they run sweet, and the offerings vanish from the banks by morning. It is hungry, child. Whatever sleeps down there, it turns toward the world the way a sleeper turns toward a window at dawn."

"Then we go down before it's fully awake."

"We go down." Sera reached out and gripped Felix's wrist, hard, her thin fingers surprisingly strong. "Listen to me. I refused you once, and I was wrong, and I will say so plainly: I refused you because I thought this was a story about one soul among thousands, and I have spent my life unable to save even one. But you have shown me something tonight, Felix Amberdale. Nine hundred roads, dug one greed at a time, and every one of them learned for love. If the old gods have forgotten how mercy works, then you are the proof it survives them."

Felix swallowed against the ache in her throat. "Save the sermons for the shrine."

"It was not a sermon. It was a witness." Sera released her wrist and turned to face the Throat, and the darkness below breathed again, long and slow and patient. "Now. Show me the ledge. Show me the door."

Felix was reaching for the rope when she felt it—a tremor through the soles of her boots, faint as a heartbeat felt through a floor. Then the token in her pack, wrapped in three layers of wool, grew suddenly, impossibly warm.

Below them, far down in the mountain's dark, something knocked.

Three times. Patient. Almost polite.

Like an answer.
