## 8. The Tithe Marked Twice

The cave breathed.

Sera Vane had lived inside its lungs for six years now, long enough to know its moods—the way it exhaled cold through the eastern fissure before rain, the way it held its breath on nights when the old gods were listening. Tonight it was holding its breath. The charcoal in her brazier guttered and steadied and guttered again, as though something beneath the mountain were drawing at the same air she was, and Sera sat with her hands pressed flat against her thighs and watched the amber girl across the fire pretend not to be afraid.

Felix Amberdale did not pretend well. She pretended competently, which was different—her stillness was a craftsman's stillness, each muscle placed deliberately, like stones in a wall. But fear leaked out of her anyway, in small honest ways: the too-rapid blink when Sera's ladle scraped the pot; the way her gaze kept returning to the cave mouth, cataloguing exits. A woman who counted doors in every room she entered was a woman who had needed them before.

"You're doing it again," Felix said.

"Doing what?"

"Looking at me like I'm a text you can't translate."

Sera stirred the nettle broth. Her wrists ached. They always ached now, the old fracture points, the marks left by the chains they'd put on her when they stripped her of her orders—*apostate, madwoman, ash-priestess*—and dragged her through the capital streets so the faithful could throw things. Six years, and the ache came only when it was about to matter. She had learned to trust that ache the way sailors trust a falling barometer.

"I knew a taster once," Sera said. "In the temple, before. He trained his tongue the way musicians train their ears. Bitterleaf in milk. Hemlock in wine. It took him ten years to learn what your tongue already knew at your first tasting." She lifted her eyes. "You've never told anyone how."

Felix's spoon stopped midway to her mouth. "I told you. I was sold young. The kitchens make you tough."

"The kitchens make you quick. They don't make you *immune*." Sera set the ladle down. The firelight moved across the cave walls, over the painted spirals she had made in her first year here, the ones that were half prayer and half madness and which, lately, she could no longer tell apart. "There are four kinds of poison-proof flesh in this world, taster. The first is luck, and luck runs out. The second is training, and training has limits—I have seen trained tongues die from a single plum. The third is sorcery bought, and bought sorcery leaves a smell like burnt sugar." She let the silence stretch until it thinned enough to bear weight. "And the fourth is a mark. And you are marked."

Something crossed Felix's face—fast, sealed over—but Sera had spent thirty years reading faces across altar fires, watching truth arrive in people before they could stop it. Truth arrived in Felix like a bird hitting a window.

"That's nonsense," Felix said.

"Show me your left shoulder."

"No."

"Then I'll show you mine."

Sera rose, joints cracking, and turned her back to the fire. She pulled the coarse linen of her robe down off one shoulder, past the knob of her spine, and heard Felix's breath change behind her. On Sera's shoulder blade, just below the ridge of bone, sat an old scar: a spiral no wider than a coin, drawn in a single unbroken line that coiled inward three times and ended in a point, like a snail's shell seen by someone who had only ever heard it described. It had been burned into her at her ordination, thirty-one years ago, by a priestess whose hands shook with reverence. *The mountain sees its servants*, she had said. *The mark is how it knows to find you.*

"This is the servant's mark," Sera said, her voice going quiet in the way that caves taught voices to go quiet. "Given after birth. Given freely, or as freely as anything is given to the gods. Now show me yours."

Felix didn't move. The fire popped. Somewhere deep under them, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the mountain shifted its weight.

"Every contract has two signatures," Sera said, turning back. "The servant's mark is the second kind. The first kind—" she touched her own shoulder, gently, the way one touches a wound belonging to someone else "—is older. It's written before the marked one can consent, because the marked one doesn't exist yet. It's written into the blood of the mother, and it passes to the child like eye color or a crooked nose. The midwives of the old rite called it *the tithe marked twice*. Once on the family line, once on the flesh. It means the debt was claimed before there was anyone to owe it." She looked at Felix across the small fire, and hated, briefly, the thing she was about to do, and did it anyway, because the mountain was waking and mercy was a luxury of quieter centuries. "It means you weren't born lucky, taster. You were born *owned*. Show me your shoulder."

For a long moment Felix Amberdale, who had survived nine years of court poison by being nothing to everyone, looked at Sera Vane, who had survived six years of exile by being no one to anyone—and then, very slowly, like something surrendering a fortification, she set down her bowl and pulled the collar of her shirt aside and turned.

The scar sat below her left shoulder blade. The spiral, the triple coil, the single unbroken line.

But where Sera's mark ended in a point, Felix's ended in a small deliberate barb—a hook, a fisherman's curve, the shape a scribe makes when he does not finish a word but leaves room for it to continue. Sera had seen that hook exactly twice before: once in a marginal illumination in the temple's oldest codex, in a picture of infants being carried down into a hill; and once on the shoulder of a dying woman who had crawled to Sera's cave seeking absolution and died without receiving it, because Sera had already lost her gods by then and had nothing to absolve with.

"Oh," Sera said. It was not a word so much as a door opening. "Oh, you poor creature."

"Don't." Felix's voice cracked along some seam Sera hadn't known was there. She yanked her shirt closed and stood, moving away toward the cave wall as if distance were an argument. "Don't look at me like that. I earned my tongue. Nine years of swallowing what the palace swallowed—"

"Nine years of the mountain keeping its property healthy," Sera said softly.

Felix spun. In the firelight her face was terrible—not angry, or not only angry; anger would have been easier for both of them. She looked like a woman watching her own reflection argue with her.

"Say that again," Felix said.

"You know what hemlock does," Sera said. "You told me. You described it to me the first night, every symptom, in order, the way a soldier describes a battle. You *know* what should have killed you. Your body isn't hardened against poison, girl—it's *warded*. A ward that strong takes more than training. It takes a claim. The mountain doesn't spend that kind of protection on strangers." She rose, and her knees screamed, and she ignored them. "Why do you think your immunity has never once failed? Not weakened, not flickered, even when the poisons grew subtler than any kitchen-training could answer? Because it isn't yours. It's maintenance. The way a farmer tends his best ox—not out of love. Out of accounting."

"Stop it."

"It wants you whole, Felix. Whole and healthy and *unspent*. Because you were promised before you drew breath, and the mountain does not forgive broken promises, and midwinter—"

"*Stop.*"

Felix's hand struck the cave wall. Small stones rattled loose somewhere above them, pattering down like gray rain, and the fire cowered, and in that moment Sera felt it again—the barometric ache in her wrists, the mountain leaning close, listening through six feet of living stone. Listening to its property refuse.

Felix stood with her forehead against the rock, breathing hard. When she spoke again her voice had been sanded flat.

"My mother," she said. "Before she died. She used to say a thing to me, when she was far gone in the poppy syrup. She'd hold my wrist and say, *They only lent you to me, little one. They only lent you.*" She laughed, a sound with no floor under it. "I thought it was the fever talking. I thought—" Her shoulders moved. "Tammy. Is that why Tammy? Because she'd already—was he paying *her* debt or mine?"

Sera considered lying. It lasted less than a heartbeat; lies curdled fast in this cave, and the mountain could taste them.

"A family line carries a family's ledger," she said. "Your mother sold your brother to buy time. Perhaps to buy time on *your* debt specifically—the collectors take payment in whatever order the ledger allows. Or perhaps Tammy was simply what she had on hand." She watched Felix flinch and did not soften it, because softening it would be lying, and the hour for lying was over. "What matters is this: the mountain has been feeding you, guarding you, growing you—nine years of perfect immunity, like fruit ripening on a schedule. And now the court beneath us is stirring, and rivers run sweet with wrong water, and prayers are being answered by things that were sleeping when the prayers were written. Contracts come due when courts wake, taster. Midwinter isn't just Tammy's deadline."

"I know," Felix said quietly. "I've done the arithmetic."

"Have you?" Sera came closer, close enough to see the fine tremor in the other woman's jaw, the exhaustion under the competence. "Because I don't think you have. I think you've built your whole life on a number you never checked. You believe you are free because you have never worn chains. But a shepherd doesn't need fences for the lamb it intends to keep. Your invisibility, your forgettability, your little smuggler roads and your secret tongue—you call it armor. Child, I think it's a *pen*. I think everything you have ever called freedom was the mountain arranging you comfortably until collection."

That landed. Sera watched it land, watched it go through Felix's defenses the way a needle goes through cloth—no resistance, because the point was true and true points slide. Felix slid down the cave wall until she was sitting, knees up, arms wrapped around them, a woman folded into the smallest possible version of herself.

"I made myself nobody," she said to the fire. "Nine years. I unlearned my own face. I practiced being overlooked the way other people practice instruments. Do you know why?" She didn't wait for an answer. "Because of the night they took Tammy. I stood in the doorway and our mother handed him over and I did *nothing*, and afterward I swore I would never be visible enough to be asked for anything ever again. If no one sees you, no one can ask you to be brave. That was the whole philosophy. That was my religion." Her laugh again, that floorless sound. "And the whole time I was practicing being invisible, I was wearing a mark that meant the most attention in the world was already on me. I couldn't hide from it. There was nowhere to hide. It was *under my skin*."

Sera lowered herself to sit across from her, old bones folding, and for a while neither of them spoke. The brazier settled. Above them, the cave's throat exhaled a slow thread of cold air, and Sera felt the mountain settle back from its listening, satisfied—or patient, which was worse.

"When they stripped my orders," Sera said at last, "they took my name out of the temple rolls. Struck it. There's a ceremony for it—very elegant. The hierophant speaks your name and then unwinds it, syllable by syllable, and the congregation answers *she is not* to each one. *Sera is not. Vane is not.*" She smiled at the fire without warmth. "I thought it was cruelty. It was, but it was also theology, and the theology is the crueler part. A name is a handle. It's how the old powers grip you. Your court taught you that without knowing it—make yourself forgettable, and you become hard to hold. You were right about that much, taster. You stumbled onto the oldest warding there is."

"But it didn't work," Felix said. "Because I was already held."

"You were already held." Sera reached out and, gently, turned Felix's shoulder toward the light. The scar glimmered there, pale against sun-browned skin, the little barb at its end catching orange firelight like a fishhook catching dawn. "But hear me, and hear all of me, because this next part matters more than everything I've said tonight. A mark is a claim, yes. A claim is a contract. And a contract—" she held the girl's eyes "—is *paper*. Paper can be read. Read contracts can be contested. I was struck from the rolls and it nearly unmade me, but it also taught me every loophole in the liturgy of names. If the mountain wrote you into its ledger before your birth, then somewhere beneath us there is a page with your name on it, and pages can be argued. Altered." She paused. "*Traded.*"

Felix stared at her. In the depths of her eyes something moved—not hope yet; hope was too expensive for a woman like her—but the raw ore of it, the thing hope got smelted from.

"You'll help me," Felix said. "For Tammy."

"For Tammy," Sera agreed. "And for the dead woman who crawled here with that same hooked mark and died asking me questions I couldn't answer. And—" she drew a breath, and it hurt, and she let it hurt "—because if the court beneath the mountain is waking, then it will wake hungry regardless of whether we come to it politely or it comes to the world rudely, and I am tired, taster. I am tired of hiding in a hole praying the hunger stays underground. Someone must go down and read the ledger. It may as well be the two women the ledger was written against."

Felix was quiet a long moment. Then she said, in a voice carefully emptied of everything, the court voice, the armor voice:

"There's a lord at the palace. Corvane. Chancellor. He's been testing me—he served me something last month I couldn't identify, and no poisoner alive is better than me at identifying, which means it wasn't from any mortal garden." Her jaw tightened. "I think he's guessed what I am. I think he's been guessing for a while."

Sera felt her wrists ache, sharp and bright, and understood.

The mountain was not the only thing that had marked Felix. The world above marked her too, in its slower ways—in suspicion, in attention, in the particular hunger of ambitious men. Two claims now converged on one small body: the old hunger below and the new hunger above, both circling.

"Then you are hunted from two directions," Sera said, "and you came to a mad priestess in a hole rather than trust anyone at court. Good. That instinct will keep you alive longer than your tongue will." She pushed herself upright, groaning, and went to the niche in the cave wall where she kept her divination things: the black bowl, the river-stones gone smooth with handling, the vial of ash from her own ruined altar. Her hands were steady. They were always steady when it was about to matter. "We don't go down blind, and we don't wait for midwinter to teach us the rules. Tonight we ask."

"Ask who?" Felix said, rising. "Your gods left you. You told me."

"They did," Sera agreed, tipping the ash into the bowl. Outside, the wind changed, and the sweetwater river—thirty years dry, three weeks running—could be heard faintly in the ravine below, murmuring in the dark like something practicing speech. "But the mountain is waking, girl, and a waking thing must answer *someone*, if only to say *not yet*. We knock. Whatever's in there decides whether to open."

She scattered the stones. They fell in a pattern she had never seen in thirty years of reading them: all of them spiraling inward, every single one, toward a center that was empty.

And the empty center began, very slowly, to glow.

* * *

Later—hours later, when the thing in the bowl had finished speaking its questions and withdrawn, leaving the cave smelling of cold iron and old libraries—Sera sat shaking beside a fire that had burned down to its bones, and Felix sat across from her, and neither of them had managed many words.

*What was given cannot be stolen,* the presence had said, in a voice like dust settling. *Only traded.*

Sera kept turning it over, that sentence, the way the tongue turns a loose tooth. Given. Not taken. Not stolen. *Given.* Which meant that somewhere in the hidden architecture of Felix Amberdale's life there had been a giving—a mother's bargain, a midwife's rite, a promise whispered over a cradle—and the mountain had never needed to steal anything at all.

It had only ever needed to wait for the trade.

"We need to know what was given," Sera said hoarsely. "Exactly what. Exactly when. Exactly by whom. The wording will be in the ledger, and the ledger is beneath the mountain, and the gate—" She stopped.

Felix was looking at her with an expression Sera recognized from mirrors: the look of a person about to hand over the last of something.

"I know the way in," Felix said quietly. "All the ways in. Smuggler roads. I've mapped them for nine years—for Tammy, so that when he came home, however he came home, I'd have a road ready for him." She stood, and pulled her pack from the shadowed wall, and her hands did not shake, because of course they didn't; competence was the only courage she had ever been permitted. "Rest while you can, priestess. We leave before dawn."

Above them, through fathoms of stone, the mountain turned over in its sleep like a man who has heard his name mentioned in another room—and decided, for now, to keep pretending.
