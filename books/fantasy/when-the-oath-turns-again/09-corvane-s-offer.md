## 9. Corvane's Offer

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Felix found almost funny. Poisonings happened on Fridays, executions at dawn, scandals in the small hours when candles guttered and tongues loosened. But chancellors requested private audiences on Tuesdays—clean, ordinary days, days chosen precisely because nothing was expected of them.

The note itself was unremarkable: cream paper, chancellor's seal, a single line in a clerk's tidy hand. *Lord Corvane requests the taster's presence in the map room, after evening bells, regarding matters of the kitchens.* Matters of the kitchens. As if she were being called to discuss the price of saffron or the quality of the winter butter.

She knew better. She had known since the bitter leaf, since the soup he'd watched her taste with those pale, patient eyes—the eyes of a man who counted seconds while poison moved through another person's blood. She had played the fool for him that day, had let her hand tremble just enough, had asked a servant to fetch water three times so her face would flush with plausible discomfort. She thought she had sold the performance.

But Lord Aldric Corvane did not summon people to discuss performances he believed. He summoned them to discuss ones he didn't.

* * *

The map room occupied the tower's third floor, a round chamber where every wall was window and every window looked out over Thornfell—the city first, then the terraced fields going gold with late autumn, then the mountain itself, rising black-shouldered above everything, its crown lost in cloud even on clear days. Someone had arranged the room deliberately: stand at its center and you could not look anywhere without seeing the mountain. Felix wondered if Corvane had designed it that way, or if he had simply understood what the room wanted to be and let it.

He stood at the great table when she entered, both hands flat on a map so old its ink had gone the color of dried blood. He did not look up. That was a technique too—she'd catalogued it years ago among the court's small cruelties. Make them wait. Let them study your bowed head and invent their own reasons to be afraid.

Felix declined to be afraid on schedule. She curtsied at the depth appropriate for a chancellor addressing a kitchen servant—not an inch deeper—and folded her hands, and waited, and let her gaze drift, casually, over the map beneath his fingers.

It was a map of the mountain.

Not the trade routes version hung in the chancery hall, all neat contour lines and marked quarries. This was older. The vellum had been patched in six places, and someone had drawn the interior—the deep galleries, the descending halls—in a different hand than the surface, a hand that pressed harder, that made the tunnels look less like architecture and more like the branching of roots. At the mountain's heart, where a cartographer would mark a summit, this map marked something else: a small, dense knot of lines, drawn and redrawn until the vellum there had nearly worn through, as if whoever made it had traced that place again and again the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

"You'll want some wine," Corvane said, still without looking up. "I find these conversations go better with wine."

"I'm on duty, my lord."

"Your duty ended at evening bells." Now he looked up, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing about him—warm, reasonable, the smile of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice. "Which is why I waited for evening bells."

There was wine already poured, two cups, on a side table near the eastern windows. She noted the color—a deep garnet, southern, expensive—and the fact that only one cup sat near the decanter. The other stood apart, alone, like a guest who had arrived early.

"Then I'm free to go," Felix said pleasantly.

"Sit down, Mistress Amberdale."

Her name in his mouth landed like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall and fall and never quite hit bottom. Nine years since anyone at court had used it—nine years of *taster*, of *you there*, of *the girl*. Names were dangerous things; she had buried hers the way other people buried coin, for safekeeping, against a rainy day. And now Aldric Corvane had dug it up without breaking ground.

She sat. It seemed wiser than standing, and wiser than running, though her body offered opinions on both. The chair he indicated faced the windows. The mountain filled them.

"Wine?" He lifted the decanter.

"Thank you, no."

"Of course." He poured for himself, unhurried. "You don't drink anything you haven't prepared. I've watched you for—" he paused, savoring it, "—four years now. Did you know? Since my second month as chancellor. You take a portion of every dish to the scullery before service. Everyone assumes you're checking the counts. But you're testing. Small amounts, on the inside of your wrist, behind the ear, the soft skin at the elbow. Old smuggler tricks, I imagine, from your roads." He drank. "You have an extraordinary constitution, Mistress Amberdale. The bitter leaf should have dropped you where you stood. Instead you flushed charmingly and asked for water, and I thought—how interesting. Either this girl is the luckiest woman alive, or she is something else entirely."

Outside, a bell rang somewhere below, marking the half hour. Felix kept her face pleasant and empty, the face of a woman listening to gossip she didn't understand.

"So I looked into you," Corvane went on. He set down his cup and turned, at last, fully toward her, and the lamplight caught him the way lamplight catches knives. "A food-taster, appointed nine years ago by a steward who died the following spring—convenient. No family listed beyond a mother, deceased, and a brother—" a fractional pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—sold north, records say. Debt settlement. The mother's debts were considerable. The buyer is listed only as *private party, Thornfell Province*, which is a very polite way of writing what everyone in certain villages writes instead: *the mountain*."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere far below, the palace went on breathing—footsteps, a door, the murmur of the guard change—but up here the air had the stillness of held breath.

"And then," Corvane said softly, "there is your shoulder."

Felix did not move. Moving would be answering.

"The palace physician keeps meticulous records. In your third year here you fell ill with marsh fever—he notes it, along with a most curious observation. During the fever he examined you, and found, on the left shoulder, an old scar. Circular. Deliberate. He sketched it." Corvane drew a folded paper from his breast pocket but did not open it; he simply held it, letting it sit in the space between them like a third person. "He showed the sketch to no one. He died two winters ago—fever, poor man. But sketches outlive their makers. Especially when a chancellor knows how to ask."

"You've wasted a great deal of effort," Felix said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "On a kitchen servant."

"On a tithe," Corvane corrected, and the word went through her like cold water through cloth. "That's what the mark is. I spent two years confirming it—two years, and more gold than I'll admit to my wife—chasing priests and gravediggers and one magnificent madwoman in the western hills who talks to caves. The old courts marked their promised ones at birth. A circle, closed. It means the mountain has claimed you. It also means"—and here his voice changed, warmed, took on the texture of genuine wonder, and that was somehow worse than the menace—"the mountain protects you. Nothing poisons what belongs to it. Your immunity isn't talent, girl. It's ownership. You've been safe your whole life because you were already spoken for."

The words should have shattered her. Perhaps, in a quieter season, they would have. But Sera had already broken that glass—had already laid her palm against the scar and told Felix what she was, and Felix had spent the nights since learning to breathe around the shape of it. So instead of shattering, she did what she had trained herself nine years to do. She became very still, and very small, and let the silence do her lying for her.

"If you believe all that," she said, "then you know touching me is bad luck."

Something flickered across Corvane's face—irritation, quickly mastered, and under it a flash of something almost like respect. "You're not afraid."

"I'm a food-taster, my lord. Fear left me years ago. There wasn't room for it alongside the work."

"No," he agreed. "I don't suppose there was." He came around the table slowly, and stopped at the window beside her chair, so that they were both looking out at the mountain together, two silhouettes against the darkening glass, conspirators in a picture neither had agreed to join. "Then let me tell you what I actually want, and you can decide whether to be afraid of it.

"I have served this crown for twenty years. I have balanced its ledgers and buried its scandals and swallowed its insults with a smile, and do you know what I have learned? That the throne upstairs is a candle burning down. The king is gentle and dim and his heir is worse, and within ten years—twenty, if we're generous—Thornfell will be ruled by whichever clever family is standing closest when the light goes out. I intend for that family to be mine. Not through marriage. Not through armies. Through something no rival can match."

He gestured at the window, at the vast black shoulder of the mountain filling half the sky.

"There is a court under there, Amberdale. Older than the crown. Richer. The old kings knew it—they paid it tribute for three hundred years, and their harvests never failed, and their rivers never ran dry. Then some pious fool-king decided the old gods were an embarrassment and cut the payments, and the mountain went to sleep, sulking, for thirty years." His voice quickened; this was the sermon, the true faith under the reasonable face. "And now it's waking. Crops blooming overnight. Dead water running sweet. You've seen the villages leaving offerings—I know you have, you leave the palace twice a week on errands no errand-girl needs to make, and I know exactly which roads you take, because I've had men on them since spring. The court beneath is stirring, and whatever it wants, it wants badly. And I mean to give it what it wants. On terms."

Felix turned her cup slowly on her knee. Her heart was doing something complicated and painful, because everything he said confirmed what Sera had told her in the cave, and confirmation from a man like Corvane felt like watching your own nightmare get a second author.

"What does any of that have to do with me?"

"Nothing," Corvane said, "except your brother."

The name he didn't say—Tammy, Tammas, little Tam with flour always in his hair—hung between them, and Felix understood, with a clarity like ice water, that he had saved it deliberately. The last card. Played slow.

"Records say he was taken eleven years ago," Corvane continued, gently, reasonably, the voice of a man offering to help carry groceries. "Soul-contracts with the old courts run twelve years. Do the arithmetic. Midwinter is close, and after midwinter, whatever is left of Tammas Amberdale stops being a boy waiting to come home and becomes, permanently, property of the throne of roots and bone. Unless someone renegotiates." He smiled again, that warm, awful smile. "I can help with that. I have resources. I have the collector-tokens, the ritual grammar, the names of dead priests who knew the gate. What I lack—what no amount of gold can buy—is someone the mountain will admit. A marked one. Its own. Someone it considers *already inside*."

The trap revealed itself the way traps do: not suddenly, but all at once, having been around her the entire time. She thought of the map with its worn-thin heart. Of the two cups, one set apart. Of her name, spoken aloud in a room where no one else could hear it, so that it could never be denied later.

"You want me to open the gate."

"I want you to *knock*," Corvane said, spreading his hands, all innocence. "Open it, knock, whisper the password—I don't care about the metaphor. You go in, you fetch your brother, I follow with my men, and we make arrangements with whatever sits on that throne. Arrangements. Tribute. Partnership. The old kings had the right idea; they were simply too squeamish to finish it. Imagine it, Amberdale—Thornfell fed by a court that answers prayers. No famine ever again. No drought. A kingdom that cannot fail, because its king keeps gods on retainer." His eyes gleamed. "And your brother walks free. Every debt paid. You get the only thing you have crawled through sewers and smuggler roads for nine years to reach, and I get a kingdom. Everyone leaves this table satisfied."

It was beautifully done. That was the horror of it—that it was beautiful. He had taken the exact shape of her longing and built his ambition inside it, the way a parasite builds inside a host, so that refusing him felt like refusing Tammy. *Help me and save him,* the offer said. *Refuse me and choose pride over your brother's soul.*

Felix looked out the window at the mountain, and let the silence stretch long enough that Corvane began, visibly, to enjoy it—to mistake it for the wavering of a cornered girl.

"My lord," she said finally, "if I open a door to that place, what stops it from keeping me?"

"Nothing," Corvane said cheerfully. "Absolutely nothing. That's the risk, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But you'd be going in armed—with your mark, with my gold, with my blades at your back. Better odds than you'll ever have alone. And the alternative—" he tapped the folded paper with the physician's sketch, once, lightly, "—the alternative is that I continue my research. Publicly, perhaps. A marked tithe living under the crown's roof, hiding her nature, consorting with a disgraced priestess—I understand the holy courts are still looking for Sera Vane. Accusations travel fast, when a chancellor lends them his voice. And a taster accused of treason loses her position, her wages, her anonymity—all the armor you've spent nine years forging. How long do you survive at court as *someone*, Amberdale? You of all people know the answer."

There it was. Not the offer but the teeth behind it, bared just enough to gleam.

Felix rose. She did it slowly, smoothing her apron, a servant taking her leave, harmless as bread. But when she reached the door she paused, hand on the latch, and gave him the truth bent just enough to pass.

"I'll consider it," she said. "My brother comes first. If your help is real, I'll use it. If it isn't—" she glanced back at him, and let him see, for one unguarded instant, the thing under the servant's face, the thing nine years of invisibility had sharpened rather than dulled, "—then I'll know, and you'll wish the mountain had you instead."

Corvane laughed—a real laugh, delighted, terrible. "There she is," he said. "Under the apron all along. Go home, Mistress Amberdale. Sleep on it. Midwinter is coming faster than either of us would like."

* * *

She did not go home. She walked the long way, down through the servants' stair and out the postern gate and into the streets, moving without thinking among the evening crowds, and it was only when the cobblestones changed under her feet—from palace-cut granite to the rough, mismatched stones of the river quarter—that she realized her legs had chosen her route for her.

Down to the sweetwater banks. Down to where the resurrected river ran silver-black under the moon, and the villagers' offerings crowded the mud: bread, salt, ribbon, a child's carved horse, coins pressed into the earth in careful rows. The water whispered over its stones, and the whisper was almost lovely, and underneath the loveliness ran that faint bitterness she had tasted weeks ago, the wrongness, the hunger dressed as generosity.

Felix crouched at the bank and stared at her reflection until it resolved into a face she barely used anymore—a plain face, forgettable, armor-plated in ordinariness.

*You'd be going in armed.*

He was right. That was the unforgivable part. Alone, with stolen tokens and Sera's half-remembered rituals, her odds of reaching Tammy before midwinter were thin as winter light. Corvane offered roads, gold, blades, a map worn thin at the heart. All he asked in return was that she open the oldest door in the world to a man who wanted to put gods on a leash.

And he would betray her. Of course he would—the moment the throne was within his grasp, the moment her brother's contract became inconvenient leverage, he would spend her like a copper. She had watched him spend better people than herself.

But he would betray her *after* the gate opened. And the gate had to open anyway.

Felix straightened, wiped the river-mud from her knees, and turned toward the western hills, where a disgraced priestess kept a fire burning in a cave and letters she'd begun writing to no one. Sera would need to know. They had perhaps four weeks, and a plan that required a traitor's gold and a traitor's blades, and a way to walk through a traitor's trap without getting caught in its teeth.

Behind her, the river whispered. It sounded, tonight, almost like a question.

She walked faster.
