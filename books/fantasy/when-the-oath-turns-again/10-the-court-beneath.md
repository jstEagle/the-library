## 10. The Court Beneath

The token was warm in Felix's palm, though the night was cold enough to frost her breath.

It had come from the shrine at Hollowmere—a little bone disc carved with a spiral that hurt to look at directly, the way a stair hurts when you've forgotten how many steps you've climbed. She had taken it from among the offerings three nights ago, sliding it from its velvet dish while Sera Vane slept by the fire with ash still on her hands and a question mark still hanging in the air between them: *What was given cannot be stolen, only traded.*

Felix was about to test that riddle against the hardest thing she'd ever tried to steal.

She stood now at the mouth of the mountain's gate, in a hollow the smuggler maps called the Kissing Stone because two rock faces leaned together overhead like lovers, leaving only a knife-slit of sky. The gate itself did not look like a gate. It looked like shadow pooled too deep, like a doorway someone had drawn in charcoal and never bothered to erase. The old smugglers' songs said the mountain had swallowed a road once—swallowed it whole, carts and oxen and the men who drove them—and that the road had gone on existing inside, going nowhere, forever.

Felix pressed the token against the dark.

The dark pressed back.

*Given,* said something without sound, in the voice of settling stone, of roots splitting bedrock. *Not stolen.*

"I'm not stealing," Felix whispered. Her mouth had gone dry as chalk dust, and the taster's tongue—the cursed, blessed thing that could name every poison ever brewed—found nothing on her lips but fear, which had no flavor anyone had ever catalogued. "I'm visiting."

A long silence. Long enough for her heart to beat nine times, one for each year she had spent being no one.

Then the shadow opened like an eye.

* * *

The road inside was exactly what the songs promised: a road, running straight through impossible dark, lit by nothing and yet not black. It was the color of the moment before waking, when you can see the room but cannot yet remember your own name. Felix walked it with her hand trailing the wall, counting turns out of habit even though there were none. The road went down and down and did not curve.

Her boots made no sound. That was the first wrongness. She stamped once, deliberately, and heard nothing—not even the small internal thud of her own heel. Sound itself had been left at the door, or perhaps collected, shelved somewhere in the deep places alongside everything else the mountain had taken from the living world.

The second wrongness was the sweetness.

It began faintly, a smell like honey left too long in the sun, like fruit just past ripeness, like flowers arranged over something buried. Felix knew that sweetness. Every taster knows it. It was the smell of things kept past their time—preserved, pickled, embalmed. The mountain smelled like a larder where nothing was allowed to rot and therefore nothing was allowed to end.

She thought of Tammy at seven years old, standing in their mother's kitchen, holding up a jar of plums he'd helped preserve and saying, solemn as a magistrate, *Now they'll last forever, Fee.* He had been so proud. Their mother had laughed until she cried.

*Forever* had come for him after all.

* * *

The road ended at a curtain of root.

They hung from a ceiling so far above that Felix's eyes watered trying to find it—roots thick as oaks and thin as hair, all of them pale as peeled willow, all of them moving, very slowly, like kelp in a tide no wind had ever reached. Beyond them glowed a light the color of candlelight seen through milk.

Felix parted the roots the way you part a beaded curtain in a house you've been invited into but don't trust, and stepped into the court beneath the mountain.

She had imagined horrors. She had prepared herself for horrors: teeth, darkness, the screaming of the harvested dead. What she saw instead was worse, because it was beautiful.

A hall vast beyond reason opened around her, pillared by living root, floored in stone worn smooth as lake ice. Light fell everywhere and came from nowhere. And everywhere—filling the hall like bees fill a hive—were people.

They were lovely. That was the trap of it. Each soul moved with the unhurried grace of dancers who have forgotten there was ever such a thing as hurrying. A woman in a gown of grey silk poured wine from a jug into a cup, and the pour took perhaps a minute, and it was the most graceful pouring Felix had ever seen, and the woman's face—

Felix made herself look at the faces.

The faces were empty. Not blank—empty, which is different. Blank is a wall. Empty is a room whose furniture has been sold off piece by piece. The woman pouring wine wore an expression of mild, pleasant attention, the expression of someone listening to music played in another house. Her eyes were the color of shallow water over sand. When she set down the jug, she smiled at nothing, beautifully.

Around her, others drifted through their offices. A man swept a floor that needed no sweeping, each stroke of the broom slow as the movement of a clock hand. Three figures sat at a table playing a game with bone pieces, and none of them touched the pieces; the pieces moved themselves, and the players watched them go with gentle, vacant interest. A child—gods, a child—turned in circles in an empty corner of the hall, arms out, dancing to no music, smiling, smiling, smiling.

Hollow-eyed souls serving a throne of roots and bone. Sera had told her. Sera had *told* her, and Felix had nodded along, and nothing—nothing—had prepared her for how much they would look like people.

Because they were people. That was the whole obscenity of it. They were not ghosts, not wights, not the hungry dead. They were the taken, wearing bodies that the mountain maintained the way a collector maintains his cabinet—dusted, polished, preserved forever.

*Now they'll last forever, Fee.*

Felix stood very still at the edge of the hall and did the thing she did best: she became no one. She softened her gaze, slowed her breathing, folded her presence down into the smallest possible shape—a food-taster's trick, learned in nine years of walking past powerful people who must never notice her. Invisible. Unremarkable. A shadow among shadows.

But the mountain's court was not the palace, and here her armor fit strangely. The drifting souls glanced at her as she passed—each one, every one—and each glance was mild and pleasant and utterly empty, and then they returned to their eternal offices. They saw her. They simply did not care. Nothing in this place cared about anything, and that, Felix realized with a cold turning in her stomach, might be the most merciful thing about it.

She walked deeper.

* * *

The throne sat at the hall's far end, and she could not help but look at it, the way you cannot help but look at a wound.

Roots again—but grown deliberately, shaped over centuries into something like a seat and something like a cage and something like a face, if a face could be made of ten thousand years of patient wood. Bone was worked through it: ribs arched into armrests, a skull crowned the topmost branch with its jaw open, and whether the skull was screaming or singing, laughing or speaking, Felix could not tell, and suspected the answer changed depending on some rhythm she was not invited to hear.

Before the throne, souls knelt in rows. Dozens. Hundreds. They swayed together, very slightly, like wheat before a windless storm.

Felix did not let herself wonder what they were kneeling toward. Some questions are doors you do not want to open while you still need your legs.

She turned away from the throne and began, instead, to search.

How do you find one boy among hundreds of the hollowed? Felix had asked herself this for three days and had no answer except the oldest one: *you look*. You walk the endless hall and you look at every face, and your heart breaks a little more with each one, because each one is somebody's Tammy. Somebody's mother. Somebody's whole world, poured out slow as wine and just as gracefully.

She found a fisherman she half-recognized from the sweetwater villages, mending a net that had no holes. She found a soldier in rotted livery polishing a sword with a cloth that never grew dirty. She found a woman humming—soundlessly, Felix remembered, for there was no sound here—humming anyway, her lips shaping a lullaby that Felix felt rather than heard, and Felix had to stop and press her fist to her mouth for a long moment before she could walk again.

Then, near the base of a root-pillar, folding napkins that were already perfect:

Tammy.

He was taller. Of course he was—he'd been eleven when the collectors came to the door with their ledger and their polite voices, and he was twenty now, and she had missed all of it. His shoulders had broadened. His jaw had sharpened. But the cowlick still stood up at the crown of his head like a flag of surrender, and he still tucked his chin when he concentrated, and when he finished folding a napkin and set it down, he smoothed it twice with both palms, exactly the way their mother had smoothed tablecloths, exactly the way he'd done since he was four years old copying her without knowing he copied her.

Felix's legs nearly gave out.

She crossed the distance between them the way you cross ice—slowly, weight spread wide, terrified of cracking something. He didn't look up. His eyes, when she got close enough to see them, were shallow water over sand.

"Tamsin," she said.

No sound left her lips. There was no sound here. But his hands stopped smoothing the napkin.

He turned his head. He looked at her with those pleasant, empty eyes, and he smiled—the same smile the wine-pourer had given, the same smile everyone here gave, mild as morning—and Felix felt it go through her like a blade slid gently, almost kindly, between the ribs.

"You've dropped something," he said. Or shaped, rather; the words arrived in her mind the way words arrive in dreams, already understood. His voice, when it came, was his voice—older, rounder, but *his*, and that nearly undid her more than the emptiness did. "You've dropped something," he said again, and bent, and picked up nothing from the floor, and held the nothing out to her with both hands, courteous as a page.

"That's mine," Felix whispered, though no whisper could exist here either. "Thank you."

He nodded, satisfied, and turned back to his napkins.

*Tammy. Tammy Amberdale. You bit the head off the gingerbread man at the harvest fair and cried when Mother pretended to arrest you. You saved your coins for two years to buy me a copper hairpin and hid it in a boot and forgot which boot. Your name is Tammy. I'm your sister. I'm your sister.*

None of it reached him. She could feel it not reaching him—the way you can feel rain not reaching a plant behind glass. He folded another napkin. Smoothed it twice.

And then—small as a thread snapping—something happened.

He paused. His hands hovered over the linen. His brow creased, the first crease she had seen on any face in this entire hall, and for one heartbeat his eyes were not shallow water but *looking* for bottom, searching, troubled.

"Someone used to sing," he said slowly, to the napkin, to himself, to no one. "Off-key. On purpose." The crease deepened. "Who—"

The crease smoothed away like a ripple closing over a dropped stone. His hands resumed. Folded, smoothed, folded, smoothed. The pleasant emptiness poured back into his face like water finding its level.

Felix stood beside her brother in the court beneath the mountain, invisible, unsound, untouchable, and understood—with the awful clarity of a poison named at last—that she could not carry him out. No stolen token, no smuggler's road, no trick of forgetfulness would serve. You could not steal from this place. The Warden had told her true: what was given cannot be stolen.

Only traded.

And somewhere above her, past the curtain of roots, past the long dark road, midwinter was coming—the deadline written in a ledger older than kingdoms—and the mountain would come to collect her brother's contract unless someone offered it a price worth breaking it.

Felix looked at the throne of roots and bone, and at the kneeling rows, and at the brother who had almost remembered her for one heartbeat, and she made herself a promise in the silent dark, a promise with no sound in it anywhere in the world:

*I'll find what you're owed. And I'll pay it.*

Behind her, soft as snowfall, the throne's skull-jaw closed.

And opened again.
