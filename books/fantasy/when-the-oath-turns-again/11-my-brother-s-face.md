## 11. My Brother's Face

The hour I stole began with a lie.

I had told the gatekeeper—a thing with a face like wet clay and a voice like a coin dropped down a well—that the throne wished to inspect the newest soul in the scullery. The clay-faced thing had studied me with eyes that did not blink so much as seep, and then it had inclined its head and said, *The throne sees all that happens beneath. There are no inspections here.* But it let me pass, because beneath the mountain there was no such thing as suspicion; nothing could be stolen from a place where everything already belonged to the throne of roots and bone.

That was what I was counting on.

So I walked into the scullery of the hollow-eyed, past souls who stirred pots of food no one ate—food was memory here, I was learning, and these people had given theirs away—and found my brother scrubbing a basin that was already clean.

* * *

He had been six years old when they took him. He would be fifteen now, if the mountain counted years, which I doubted it bothered to do. Time down here moved like honey poured upward, slow and wrong, and the souls it held had stopped keeping track the way prisoners stop counting days when the sentence has no end.

Tammy. My Tammy. Tall now, or tall-ish, with our mother's narrow shoulders and our father's stubborn jaw, his sleeves rolled to the elbow, his hands pink from water that never warmed. A copper ring glinted at his throat—the contract, worn as jewelry by its own victim, a chain of light sunk into skin.

"Excuse me," I said.

He looked up, and for one terrible heartbeat the whole world narrowed to whether he would say my name.

He didn't. His eyes were brown and clear and kind and completely empty of me. "You're not supposed to be in here," he said politely. "But nobody ever is, really."

"I'm new," I said, which was true enough. "Felix." I almost said *Amberdale* after it and bit the word back like a bitter seed. Down here, names were currency. I wasn't going to spend mine on a scullery floor.

"Felix," he repeated, tasting it, finding nothing. "I'm—" He frowned, and pressed a hand briefly to the copper ring at his throat, as if it ached. "They call me Tam. I don't remember why."

*I do,* I thought. *You were named for a grandfather you never met, and you hated it until you were four, and then you decided you loved it because I told you he once wrestled a goose and lost.*

"You look tired," I said instead, because nine years of practice had taught me how to be unremarkable, and unremarkable people said ordinary things.

"Everyone's tired here," Tam said, and smiled, and the smile was our mother's smile, exact down to the crooked left corner, and it went through me like a fishhook. "It's a nice tired, though. Like being full. Have you eaten? We have soup."

"We have soup," echoed a woman two basins down, without looking up. Her voice was pleasant and papery. She had been saying it, I suspected, for thirty years.

I sat on an upturned crate because my knees wanted sitting. "Tell me about the soup," I said.

And Tammy—Tam—told me about the soup. It was made of roots. Everything here was made of roots: root-bread, root-stew, a thin sweet liquor they drank at the changing of some lightless shift. The roots came from the throne itself, he explained, with the simple pride of someone describing a generous employer. The throne grew them. The throne grew everything. The throne was very old and very patient and loved all of them, in its way.

"Loved them how?" I asked.

He considered this with his head tipped, exactly the way he'd considered hard arithmetic at the kitchen table while our mother pretended not to help him. "Like a garden loves," he said finally. "You know. It wants you to grow. It just decides what you grow into."

I thought of crops blooming overnight in fields above. Dead rivers running sweet with water that tasted faintly of bitterness. The mountain answering prayers the way a spider answers a fly's struggling: promptly, generously, and with intent.

"It sounds peaceful," I managed.

"It is." He said it simply, without defiance, without doubt. That was the worst part. Above ground, when people said *it's fine* through their teeth, you could see the teeth. Here the words came out smooth as river stones, worn of every edge. "I can't remember being unhappy. Can you?"

Can you. Nine years of unhappiness rose in my throat like floodwater—the cold taster's cot, the careful mouthfuls, the ledger of poisons I kept behind my teeth, the roads memorized in the dark for a rescue that always receded—and I swallowed it, because this boy had no use for it, and because if I wept here among the stirring-pots the clay-faced things would come, and worse than coming, they would *ask questions*, and questions beneath the mountain were tolls.

"A little," I said. "I remember being unhappy sometimes."

"That sounds hard." And he meant it. He reached over and patted my wrist, my brother patting a stranger's wrist, gentle as you please, and I nearly came apart right there between the washbasins.

* * *

Here is what I learned in the first quarter of my stolen hour: grief does not get easier when the person you grieve is standing in front of you, alive, warm, offering you soup. It gets infinitely worse. Grief for the dead has a shape; it closes over, like water. Grief for the erased has no bottom. You fall past missing them into missing the fact that anyone could miss them at all.

Here is what I learned in the second quarter: stories still worked on him. Not memories—I had none to give him, only words—but the *shape* of stories, the rhythm, the call-and-response of a tale told well. When I described the smuggler roads above Thornfell, the ones that ran under the bracken and through the drowned quarry, his eyes followed mine the way a cat follows a moving light. When I told him about the fence named Old Maren who paid children a copper apiece to memorize the turns of the tunnels, he laughed—one sharp, surprised bark of laughter, gone as fast as it came, but real.

"Why do you know all that?" he asked.

"Because I grew up poor," I said, "and poor kids learn the back ways out of everywhere."

"Out of where?" he said, blankly, and I understood that *everywhere* meant nothing to him. There was no outside. There was the mountain, and the mountain was the world, and the world was kind, in its way, like a garden.

So I tried something else. I started telling him stories set somewhere else entirely. Not our village—he'd flinch at that, a small wince behind the eyes, like a man touching a bruise he couldn't see. Somewhere invented. A cottage by a river that ran cold and clean. An apple tree that fruited sour but cooked up sweet. A mother with flour on her hands who sang off-key on purpose because she said sad songs needed ruining a little before they could be borne.

"Who lives there?" Tam asked. He had stopped working. The basin sat forgotten, dry and clean, and he leaned toward me across it with his chin in his hands, fifteen years old and six years old at once.

"A brother and sister," I said. "The sister's older. Sharp-tongued. She taught the brother to whistle through a blade of grass, and he was terrible at it for a whole summer, and she never once told him to stop."

"What happened to them?"

"Nothing yet," I said. "The story's still being told."

Something crossed his face then—not memory, not quite, but the *shadow* memory throws when it passes a window. He pressed his palm flat against his chest, against the copper ring, and his brow furrowed.

"When you say it like that," he said slowly, "it feels like—" He struggled. "You know when you wake up and you can almost hold onto a dream, and then you reach for it and it's smoke? It's like that. But the dream isn't mine. Is it?" He looked at me with sudden, startling directness. "Whose dream is it?"

"Mine," I said. "It's mine. All of those are mine."

"Oh," he said softly. And then, with the terrible courtesy of the hollow-eyed: "You can share it with me if you want. I've got room."

I had to turn away and pretend great interest in the soup cauldron. Roots bobbed in broth the color of weak tea. My reflection looked back at me from the surface, wavering, doubled—a woman made of water and lies.

* * *

In the third quarter of the hour, the courtier-soul found me.

I had noticed her earlier, or thought I had—a slim figure in grey-green, moving along the edge of the scullery with a tray she never delivered, pausing near the archways the way sentries pause, which is to say: watching without appearing to. Most of the souls down here drifted like leaves in a millpond, aimless and content. This one moved with purpose, however faded. Purpose, I was learning, was the one thing the mountain could not fully digest.

She approached when Tam wandered off to fetch me a bowl I hadn't asked for—kindness persisting in him like a plant growing through a crack in pavement—and stood beside me at the cauldron, close enough that her words reached me alone.

"You're warm," she said.

"I'm sorry?"

"Warm-blooded. Alive-alive. Your heart beats on its own." Her eyes, when they met mine, were the most frightening thing I had seen beneath the mountain, more than the clay faces, more than the throne's distant green glow: because they were *sad*, and sadness was contraband here. "We can tell. The warmth. It's like standing near a hearth after years in a cellar. Everyone felt you come in. They just don't remember feeling it."

"Are you going to report me?"

"To whom?" She smiled without humor. "Everything here belongs to the throne. Reporting is just moving furniture inside the same house." She glanced toward Tammy, ladling soup with careful concentration, pretending not to watch us, which was another thing the mountain shouldn't have permitted—pretense. "The boy. Yours?"

"My brother."

"How long?"

"Nine years."

Her breath went out of her, slow. "Nine years and you came down anyway. Alone. Through the gate." She shook her head, marveling. "I served a knight once, above. Before. He talked about courage the way merchants talk about grain—something measured, weighed, sold. He never once described anything like this."

"Then describe it," I said. "Quickly. I don't have long."

"No," she agreed. "The shifts change. The clay ones count us at the changing, and they will count one soul too many in this room, and they will not wonder why, because wondering is also contraband—but they will recount, and recounting finds things." She set down her never-delivered tray and turned to face me fully, and I saw that under the grey-green livery she wore the same copper ring we all wore, sunk into her collarbone, older-looking, its light dimmed to embers. "My name was Ilsette," she said. "Was. I gave it. That's the thing no one above understands—we weren't taken. Taking implies theft. We were *paid*. Or we paid. The distinction stops mattering down here."

"Then tell me how to break a payment."

"You don't break contracts here. Contracts are the walls." Her voice dropped lower, and I leaned in, heart hammering, aware of every clay face in the room, of Tammy's back, of the drip of some far-off root-vein counting time in the dark. "But contracts can be *rewritten*. Do you understand the difference? Breaking is violence, and the mountain eats violence—it grows fat on ambition, on force, on men who come down with armies and hungry eyes. Those it digests whole. Rewriting is different. Rewriting is a negotiation, and negotiations require consideration."

"Consideration," I repeated. "Meaning payment. Meaning—" My stomach dropped. "A name."

"Greater for lesser." Ilsette nodded once, sharply. "The contracts are weighted, like coins. His weighs nine years, and whatever else was written into the terms—I'd want to read the original before promising anything, and I cannot read it, no one can, it lives in the throne. To void a weight that size, you must offer a name of equal or greater weight. Older. Truer. More *known*." Her sad eyes fixed on me. "And here is the cruel mathematics of it, warm one: the most valuable names are the ones most loudly remembered. A name spoken by many is worth more than a name spoken by few. A name freely offered outweighs a name seized. And a name given in trade—" she hesitated, choosing, "—a name given in trade is spent. Spent the way coin is spent. It does not come back to your purse."

The cauldron steam curled between us. Somewhere deep in the rock, the throne's green light pulsed, slow as a sleeping heartbeat, and I understood suddenly, horribly, that it was not sleeping at all. It was *listening*. It had been listening since I came through the gate. Perhaps since the day I was born.

"If I gave a name," I said carefully, "would the person still—"

"I don't know." Flat, honest, awful. "No one down here knows, because no one down here remembers trying. That may be the cruelest part of the design." She glanced at Tammy again, and something in her face cracked open, brief as lightning. "I had a daughter. I think. There's a shape in me shaped like a small girl, and it aches in weather. That's all the mountain left me. If you can buy that shape back—for him, for yourself—then pay any price that isn't his."

Tammy returned, balancing a bowl of soup with exaggerated care, beaming at me with our mother's crooked smile. "Root soup," he announced. "It's good today. Well." He paused. "It's always good today. That's the nice thing."

Behind him, Ilsette was already retreating into her grey-green anonymity, tray in hand, a leaf settling back onto the millpond. But as she passed me she murmured, so quietly it might have been the drip of water:

"The Warden keeps the scales. Find the keeper, not the throne. The throne only hungers. The keeper *remembers the rules*—and rules, warm one, bind tighter than roots."

Then she was gone, one more hollow face among hundreds, carrying her tray nowhere, and I was left holding a bowl of soup I could not taste, though God knew I was immune to everything in it.

* * *

I drank the soup. It seemed important to him.

We sat side by side on the upturned crate, shoulder to shoulder, and it was the closest I had been to my brother in nine years, and it was nothing, and it was everything. His sleeve brushed mine. He was warm too, in the way of living things, though he didn't know anymore that he was alive in any sense worth the word.

"Tell me another one," he said. "About the cottage."

So I told him about the apple tree, and the sour fruit, and the sister who climbed it anyway because the best branches were always the highest ones. I told him about whistling through grass, and the summer of terrible noise, and the neighbor's dog that howled along like it was singing harmony. I told him about a night sky with actual stars in it, and watched him frown faintly at the word *stars*, reaching for something that wasn't there.

"Do you think," he said, when I finished, staring into his empty bowl, "that maybe I lived there? In the story-place? Before?"

My chest tightened until breathing was work. "Why do you ask?"

"Because when you talk about it, my hands remember things." He held them up, turning them over, baffled by his own body. "My fingers feel like they should be making a sound. A blade-of-grass sound. And there's a song—" He pressed his fist briefly to his sternum, to the copper ring, and his face did something complicated and painful. "There's a song in here somewhere. Underneath. It won't come up. Every time I get close to it, it goes back down, like—" he searched, "—like a fish diving."

"Fish dive when they're scared," I said.

"Of what?"

"Of hooks."

He looked at me for a long moment. Around us the scullery souls stirred their memory-less pots, and the green pulse in the deep rock beat on, patient, ancient, listening, and my stolen hour drained away like sand through a seam.

"I have to go soon," I said.

"I know." He sounded unsurprised. Maybe some part of him, underneath everything, had known from the moment I walked in—had recognized not my face, not my name, but my *warmth*, the hearth-glow of me, and had leaned toward it the way the frozen lean toward fire without knowing why. "Will you come back?"

"Yes," I said, and it was the easiest oath I had ever sworn, and the hardest to keep, because between me and *back* stood a throne of roots and bone, a god of riddles, a chancellor with hungry eyes, and a price written in the only currency I owned.

"Good." He stood, gathering bowls with the easy competence our mother had beaten into both of us—or tried to, before the debt collectors came, before the copper ring, before the mountain. Then he paused, bowl in hand, and said, in a small voice stripped of all the mountain's smoothness:

"When you tell the next one—the sister in the story. Does she get her brother back?"

I looked up at him. Fifteen years old. Six years old. Mine, mine, mine, and none of it remaining except a shape in me shaped like a boy.

"In the version I'm telling," I said, "she hasn't finished paying for him yet. But she's going to. Whatever it costs."

He nodded slowly, satisfied, as if this were simply how stories worked—as if, in fact, it were.

"And Felix," he added, as I rose to go, and my heart slammed against my ribs at the sound of my own name in his mouth—

"Yes?"

He frowned, puzzled at himself. "I don't know why I said that. It just... came up. Like the fish." He touched the copper ring absently, and beneath it, I swear it, the light guttered—just once, just slightly, like a candle feeling a draft. "Be careful on your way out. The clay ones count at the changing."

* * *

I walked out through the archway with the taste of root-soup bitterness on my tongue and a single sentence burning behind my teeth, Ilsette's sentence, the one that would keep me walking all the long dark miles back toward the world of names:

*A name spoken by many is worth more than a name spoken by few.*

And I thought of Sera Vane in her shrine cave, deciphering riddles. I thought of the Warden asking its endless questions about memory. I thought of every road out of Thornfell, and who traveled them, and who owed me, and who owed Tammy—because a boy raised poor learns generosity early, and my brother had fed half the valley's strays before the mountain fed on him.

The scales, Ilsette had said. The keeper remembers the rules.

I just had to make sure that when the weighing came, the pan on my side was heavy with voices.

I climbed toward the gate, and the green light pulsed behind me, patient as a creditor, and somewhere above, midwinter was coming.
