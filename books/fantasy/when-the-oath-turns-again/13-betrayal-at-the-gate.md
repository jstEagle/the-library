## 13. Betrayal at the Gate

The mountain heard him coming before Felix did.

She knew it by the birds—how the thrushes went quiet all at once, the way a room goes quiet when a door opens somewhere below. She knew it by the wind, which had been climbing all morning out of the east and now turned, without reason, cold as well-water against the back of her neck. She knelt at the mouth of the old collector's road with Sera's chalk circle still dusting her fingers and the stolen token warm in her palm, and she thought: *something has changed the air's mind.*

Then she heard hoofbeats.

Not one horse. Twenty. Thirty. The sound came rolling down Thornfell's spine like a barrel loosed on stairs, and beneath it, fainter but unmistakable to a tongue trained on poison and everything else men slipped into each other's cups—the creak of leather armor, the flat clink of unsheathed steel kept deliberately loud.

Felix closed her hand around the token until its edges bit.

*You waited too long,* she told herself. *You stood at his gate with his coin in your fist and you let yourself believe he didn't know.*

But of course he had known. Corvane had tested her with bitter leaf in a courtier's soup; he had watched her not die. A man like that did not let a door walk away from him twice. He had simply needed her to lead him to it, because the token answered only to the marked, and Felix Amberdale—forgettable, invisible, useful Felix Amberdale—had carried it straight up the smuggler roads like a lantern into a wolf's den.

She looked at Sera, three paces away at the shrine-mouth where they'd made camp, already awake, ash-smeared, reaching for her staff.

"Riders," Felix said, and was proud of how steady it came out. "Many."

"How many is many?"

"Enough that running is arithmetic, not choice."

Sera's mouth went thin. "Then we choose what we're arithmetic *for*."

* * *

They almost made the treeline. That was the thing Felix would turn over later, in the sleepless hollows of the night—that they were fast, and quiet, and knew the ground, and it hadn't mattered at all, because Corvane had not come to chase them. He had come to arrive.

He stepped out from between two pines as if the trees had been holding the space for him, and behind him, spilling down the switchback trail in a river of grey cloaks and bare steel, came his hired blades—mercenaries with the look of men paid well enough not to ask why their employer wanted a hole in a mountain opened, and paid just badly enough to be hungry about it anyway.

"Madam Taster." Corvane's voice was pleasant. It was always pleasant. That was the horror of him—that a voice could carry that much patience and that much hunger in the same breath. "You've been difficult to follow. I want you to know I appreciated the craft of it."

Felix put herself between him and Sera without deciding to. Old instinct. The taster stands between the dish and the queen.

"My lord Chancellor," she said, and gave him the flat, forgettable bow of a servant who has no opinions. "This is a long way from court for an afternoon ride."

"It is." He walked toward her slowly, boots crunching frost, and his eyes never left her closed fist. "You took something from the shrine at Hollowmere. Something that isn't yours."

"It's mine," Felix said. "It answers me."

"Because you're marked." He said it lightly, conversationally, the way he might remark on weather, and Felix felt the words go through her like a blade through broth. "Yes. I've known since the bitter leaf. You don't flinch, you don't sicken, you don't *die*. Do you understand how rare that makes you? How *valuable*? The mountain keeps its property healthy, Madam Amberdale—" her name in his mouth sounded like a trap closing— "and I intend to make a withdrawal."

"You'll die," Sera said from behind her, low and certain. "The throne doesn't trade with men like you. It eats them."

"Men like me," Corvane said, smiling, "have been eaten by lesser things our whole lives. Chancellors are devoured slowly, by degrees, by kings and courts and time itself. I would rather be swallowed whole by something *old*." He extended one gloved hand. "The token, please."

"No."

"I wasn't asking."

He snapped his fingers. It was such a small sound, such a courtly sound, and thirty blades came off shoulders in answer. Felix's mind did what it always did under pressure—it went fast and cold and practical. Thirty men. Two exits she knew of from this shelf of rock, one of them a chimney climb too narrow for armored backs, the other a smuggler crawl that began forty feet below them behind a fall of stone. Sera couldn't manage either at speed. The token burned in her fist like a coal.

And below all the arithmetic, quieter than the wind, Tammy. *Midwinter.* Three days.

"The token needs the mark," Corvane said, closer now, patient as rot. "I have thirty swords and you have one priestess. Give it to me, or I will take it, and then I will take your hands besides, and the mountain will open for me either way. The only variable is how much of you is left standing when it does."

Felix looked at him—at the reasonable face, the ambition wearing kindness like a borrowed coat—and understood suddenly, completely, what kind of man she'd been serving tea to for nine years. Not a fool. Fools could be managed. Corvane was a man who had looked at the oldest hunger in the world and decided he could negotiate with it, and the terrible thing, the thing that made her stomach turn over, was that he almost could have—because he was offering it exactly what it loved.

A name. Fat with pride. Freely given, if not freely meant.

"Last chance," he said.

Felix threw the token—not to him. She threw it off the cliff.

It was a gamble and she knew it; the token was bound to the mark, to *her*, and things bound to her had a way of finding their way back. But she saw Corvane's composure crack—one clean hairline fracture of pure fury—and in the half-second he spent watching the glint of silver fall, she grabbed Sera's sleeve and ran.

They got ten steps.

A mercenary came out of the pines ahead of them—she'd missed him, first blind spot she'd missed in nine years—and his gauntleted fist caught Sera across the temple. The priestess went down like a dropped banner. Felix spun, low, using the knife she kept where other women kept a hairpin, and opened the man's forearm to the bone, and then there were hands on her arms, four of them, five, and Corvane was standing in front of her holding the token.

Holding the token.

Which should have been two hundred feet below in a ravine.

It hung from his fingers on a fine silver chain she had never seen before, turning slowly, catching the pale light. And Felix understood, with a lurch, that the token had done exactly what bound things did—it had gone to the mark. To the nearest claim. And Corvane, in some vault in the capital, in some night of candlelight and forbidden reading, had learned enough to know that proximity mattered less than *intent*, and he had wrapped his ambition around that little disc so tightly that it had chosen him.

"That," Corvane said, breathing hard, "was unwise."

"You dropped nothing," Sera slurred from the ground, blood in her eyebrows, laughing at the sky. "You can't drop a chain you're already caught on, girl. I tried to tell you. Everything here is already owned."

Corvane turned to the gate.

There was no gate. That was what his mercenaries didn't understand yet, what their faces were beginning to ask—thirty hardened blades staring at a blank wall of mountain, granite and pine roots and a waterfall frozen mid-trickle, wondering if their employer had lost his mind at altitude. Corvane pressed the token to the stone and spoke a word.

Felix felt it in her shoulder.

The scar—the tithe-mark, the promise written on her before birth—woke like an eye opening. Heat poured through it, down her arm, into the stone, and the mountain *moved*. Not crumbling. Unfolding. The rockface drew itself apart along seams no chisel had ever found, and beyond those seams was not darkness but depth—a stair of black root-wood descending into a glow the color of embers seen through honey, and a smell rolled up out of it, sweet and wrong, the smell of the sweetwater, of offerings left too long, of flowers fed on something that wasn't soil.

The mercenaries did not cheer. Good mercenaries never do. But they checked their edges and started down, because they were paid to, and being paid to is its own kind of blindness.

Corvane went last, and he paused beside Felix—held upright between two of his men, her wrists lashed, Sera groaning on the stones behind her—and he said, gently, "Come along, Madam Amberdale. You wouldn't want to miss your brother."

* * *

The mountain woke angry.

Felix felt it in her teeth before they were fifty steps down. The walls sweated. The root-stairs flexed underfoot like the ribs of something breathing, and the ember-glow deepened toward red, and from far below came a sound she had no word for—a vast slow grinding, like a mill turning over in its sleep, except that mills do not pause, and this paused, and then resumed faster, and she realized she was hearing the mountain decide something.

Her shoulder screamed. Every step the column descended, the mark burned hotter, and Felix understood with sickening clarity that the token in Corvane's fist was pulling on her—that every yard of depth was a yard of leash drawn in. She was not a prisoner being dragged to the court.

She was bait. Or ballast. Or payment, riding down in chains to a market that had been promised her since before she drew breath.

*Think,* she told herself, and made herself think, because thinking was the one weapon no one had ever taken from her. Thirty blades. One chancellor drunk on the nearness of power. One priestess, concussed, lashed beside her, but Sera's eyes when they met Felix's were clear and furious and calculating, and Felix read in them the same sentence she was writing in her own head: *not yet. Wait for the room.*

The stair ended.

The court beneath the mountain opened before them like a cathedral grown rather than built—vaults of living root ribbed overhead, walls studded with doors that were not doors, and everywhere, everywhere, the people. Hundreds of them. Beautiful, still, hollow-eyed souls moving through the ember-light with the unhurried grace of fish in deep water, and not one of them looked up at the armed column stumbling into their home. They had seen intruders before. They had seen intruders become *furniture*.

At the hall's far end rose the throne—roots and bone braided into a shape almost too organic to call architecture—and before it, the air itself seemed thicker, waiting.

Corvane strode forward. His mercenaries spread out behind him in a rough crescent, blades catching the ember-glow, and their footsteps echoed wrong—too long, too many, as if the hall were multiplying each bootfall and filing it away.

"Throne of the deep court!" Corvane's voice rang out, rich and resonant, a speech rehearsed. "I bring tribute of iron and intent. I bring the token of your collectors. I bring—" and here his voice dropped into something almost tender, which was the most frightening moment yet, "—a name. Mine own. Aldric Corvane. Chancellor of Thornfell. I offer it in trade for the keys of this kingdom, and I will—"

The mountain ate him.

That was the only way Felix could ever describe it afterward, and she tried, in the years after, more than once. There was no violence. There was no fire. The throne's roots simply moved—unhurried, inevitable, the way water finds the lowest place—and Corvane's speech broke off in the middle of the word *kingdom*, and the roots passed through his fine coat and his fine ambitions and his fine, fat, offered name, and drew them down into the braid like a hand closing a ledger. For one instant his face remained above the roots: eyes wide, mouth still shaping syllables, understanding arriving all at once and far too late.

Then that was gone too, and the throne stood as it had stood, and somewhere in the walls something new settled with a sigh, like a house accepting furniture.

Thirty mercenaries stopped breathing at once. Then, being professionals, they started again—and broke. Not all at once, but in ripples: men backing toward the stair, men dropping blades that rang impossibly loud, one man simply sitting down on the root-floor and putting his hands over his ears.

And the mountain, having eaten, turned its attention to what remained.

The ember-light went red. Then deeper than red—a color Felix tasted rather than saw, iron and old pennies and the bitterness of the sweetwater—and the whole vast hall exhaled, and every hollow-eyed soul in it turned its beautiful empty face toward Felix and her mark, in perfect unison, like flowers finding the sun.

The chains on her wrists had gone slack. Somewhere in the collapse of Corvane's little empire, someone had cut her free, or the mountain had, and it hardly mattered which, because Felix was already moving—running—not toward the stair and daylight and sense, but across the hall, toward the ranks of the taken, scanning faces, hearts hammering the only prayer she knew.

*Let him be here. Let him still be here. Let three days be enough.*

She found him by the eastern wall, third row of the standing souls, hands folded, eyes fixed on nothing. Taller than she remembered. Thinner. Wearing borrowed beauty like everyone else, and inside it, asleep, her brother.

Behind her the throne's roots shifted, drawing themselves up, and the whole mountain leaned in around her like a held breath.

Felix reached for Tammy's face—and the Warden's voice, everywhere and nowhere, dry as old paper, asked the hall at large:

"And what, little taster, do you think *you* are worth?"
