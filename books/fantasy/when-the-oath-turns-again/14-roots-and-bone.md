## 14. Roots and Bone

The mountain ate Corvane's army the way a spider eats flies: patiently, one at a time, and always from the edges.

Felix watched it happen from behind a pillar of veined white stone, her back pressed to the cold, her breath held so long her chest ached. The hired blades had come through the gate singing Corvane's praises—*gold on the far side, boys, gold and titles and the chancellor's favor*—and now their songs had gone thin. The tunnels beyond the gate had swallowed six of them in the first minute. Not killed. *Swallowed.* The walls had simply opened like mouths and closed again, and where men had been there was only smooth rock, unmarked, indifferent, as if the mountain had never bothered to chew.

The rest had run. Some made it as far as the antechamber before the roots took them—black roots, thick as a man's thigh, descending from a ceiling that should have been forty feet high but somehow wasn't, that was somehow *lower*, pressing down like a lid. The roots didn't strike. They curled. They gathered men up the way a hand gathers spilled coins, gently, almost tenderly, and drew them upward into the dark, and the men stopped screaming very quickly, which was worse than if they hadn't.

Corvane stood in the center of it all and did not run.

That was the thing Felix would remember afterward, in the years when she let herself remember at all: not his ambition, not his cruelty, not even the moment of his death—but the way he stood *still*. He stood in the great hollow throat of the court beneath Thornfell with his fine coat dusted in rock-flour and his hands folded behind his back like a man waiting for an audience, while everything he had bought died around him, and he did not flinch. Because he had never intended to fight the mountain. He had come to *negotiate* with it.

"Chancellor," Felix said, because someone had to say something, and because silence in that place felt like standing in open water.

He turned his head without turning his body—a courtier's economy of motion, even here. "Amberdale." His voice was calm. Almost warm. "You're still alive. I did wonder."

"The mountain doesn't want me yet."

"It wants everyone eventually." He smiled, thin and precise. "But you especially, I think. I've read the old ledgers. Tithe-marks don't lie."

"You followed me."

"I've been following you for months. You move through this city like smoke, Amberdale—it's quite impressive—but smoke leaves residue." He gestured, a small elegant motion, at the carnage hanging above them. "I simply couldn't have you closing my door before I walked through it."

"Your door." Felix's laugh came out cracked. "You brought thirty men."

"I brought thirty *witnesses*," Corvane said, "and they've served their purpose. Attention, Amberdale. The mountain is paying attention now. That's all an opening ever is—something large enough noticing you."

And she understood, then, with a cold that started in her teeth and spread down, that he wasn't mad. That was the horror of it. He was *reasonable*. He had watched the same signs she had—the sweetwater, the blooming fields, the answered prayers—and he had drawn the opposite conclusion. She had seen gods waking and thought *run*. He had seen gods waking and thought *leverage*.

"You think it will bargain with you," she said.

"I know it will. It bargained with your mother. It bargained with every farmer who left bread on a boundary stone. It's a creature of contracts, and contracts are made between equals—or between parties who can convince each other they are." He straightened his collar. Somewhere overhead, a root shifted, and a man's boot fell from the dark and landed wetly on the floor, empty. Corvane didn't glance at it. "It took your brother's name for a debt of eleven crowns. Imagine what it would trade for mine."

"For yours," Felix repeated. "You'd give it your name."

"I'd give it a *stake*. There's a difference. Names are currency; influence is capital." He turned to face her fully now, and his eyes were bright with the particular fever she'd seen once before, years ago, on a smuggler who'd drunk bad well-water and been certain he could fly. "Every soul in this court serves the throne because it holds their names. But a name freely surrendered buys standing. A name offered in *partnership*—" he smiled, "—buys a seat."

"There's no seat."

"Everything has a seat. Thrones exist to be shared by those bold enough to ask."

The throne itself sat at the far end of the cavern, and Felix had been trying not to look at it. She looked now, because Corvane was walking toward it, unhurried, and some part of her brain insisted that if she watched closely enough she might see what he saw. It was built of roots and bone interwoven so tightly they might have grown together or been grown together—antlers and femurs braided through black wood that glistened faintly, as though sap moved beneath the bark. It was not large. That was the trick of it. From across the cavern it looked like a modest chair, a shepherd's seat, something an old woman might sit in. But as Corvane approached, it grew—not visibly, nothing so crude—and the cavern seemed to grow with it, and Felix felt the floor tilt toward it very slightly, like ground leaning into a drain.

On the throne sat a figure. Or the suggestion of one. Tall, gaunt, crowned in what might have been antler or might have been root, its face a smooth pale oval with two darker places where eyes should be. It had not moved since Felix entered. She suspected it had not moved in three hundred years. She suspected that *not moving* was, for it, a form of speech.

"Corvane," she said. "Don't."

He paused at the foot of the dais—three steps of living rock—and looked back at her, and for one unguarded instant his face was almost kind. "You keep saying 'don't' to me, Amberdale. In the tasting hall, over the wine. You think I don't notice? You think I haven't known for years what you are?" He shook his head. "You hide so well you've hidden from yourself. This place doesn't hide. It *waits*. There's a lesson in that."

Then he climbed the steps.

Felix pressed herself deeper into the shadow of her pillar. Every instinct she owned—the ones that had kept her alive through nine years of poisoned plums and smiling chancellors—screamed at her to run for the gate while the throne's attention was fixed on the man climbing toward it. But Tammy was somewhere in these warrens, in the servants' galleries of the deep court, and she could not leave, and so she stayed and watched, because watching was the only weapon she had left.

Corvane reached the top step and bowed. Actually bowed—deep, formal, the bow of an ambassador presenting credentials. And when he spoke, his voice carried through the cavern with strange clarity, as though the mountain itself were carrying it to every corner, to every hollow-eyed soul frozen in the galleries, to Felix crouching in the dark.

"Majesty," he said. "Keeper of the Under-Root. Warden of the Old Toll. I am Aldric Corvane, Chancellor of Thornfell, and I come bearing an offer."

The oval face did not move. But the cavern *breathed*. Felix felt it in the soles of her feet—a slow expansion, a settling, like a house shifting on its foundations after long disuse.

"I know what you are," Corvane continued, and here his voice took on the cadence he used in the council chamber, measured, reasonable, irresistible. "You are a ledger. You collect debts and hold collateral. Your whole existence is bookkeeping—souls in, souls held, terms expiring, tithes falling due. And I am telling you that you have been doing your books *badly*. Look above. Your sweetwater floods fields and draws crowds—crowds who pray, crowds who *owe*. Your waking has made the surface rich with new debtors, and you have no collectors in the field. You are a vault with the doors rusted open." He spread his hands. "I offer administration. My name, freely given, as stake and surety. In exchange: standing. Partnership. Let me be your hand above the ground, as you are the root below it. Every prayer answered becomes a debt collected. Every miracle, an invoice. We will make the old bargains *efficient* again."

Silence.

Long enough that Felix began to hope—absurdly, desperately—that the throne was simply inert, a relic, a dead king's chair, and all this horror was only geology and madness.

Then the roots moved.

Not the black roots hanging from the ceiling. The *throne's* roots—the braid of wood and bone that formed its body—shifted, unfurling like fingers opening after centuries clenched, and one slender tendril extended toward Corvane, delicate as a fern frond, tipped in a single point of pale light.

"Careful," Felix breathed, though he couldn't hear her. "*Careful.*"

Because she had spent nine years learning poisons, and she knew this shape. She knew what it meant when something beautiful offered itself slowly. The tendril hovered before Corvane's chest, and he regarded it with the satisfaction of a merchant watching a signature dry.

"Yes," he said softly. "Yes. We understand each other."

He took it in both hands, the way one accepts a contract.

The light went through him.

There was no better word for it. It entered at his palms and spread up his arms beneath the skin like frost climbing a window, silver-white, branching, beautiful, and for one heartbeat his face was transfigured—he made a small sound, not pain, something closer to *recognition*, as if he were finally seeing the true architecture of power in the world and finding himself, at last, inside it.

Then the branches reached his heart, and the mountain collected.

What happened next happened quickly, but Felix's mind, trained by years of tasting death in small doses, broke it into stages the way a taster must: first the color, then the smell, then the texture, then the truth.

First the color drained. It poured out of him from the crown of his head downward, like water through sand—his silver temples going gray, then white, then the white of bone, his flush, his vitality, the very *brown of his eyes* fading until they were the pale washed-out gray of river stones. He swayed but did not fall; the tendril held him upright by the hands, gently, courteously, a partner to the end.

Second the smell. Felix tasted the air out of habit, and the taste was *sweet*—cloying, honeyed, the smell of overripe fruit—and her stomach turned, because she knew that sweetness. Everything the mountain touched smelled sweet. Sweetwater. Sweet prayers. Sweet dying.

Third the texture. His skin changed under her eyes, going from flesh to something finer-grained, tighter, like paper stretched over a frame. His fine coat hung looser. His hands, still clasping the tendril, were becoming *lighter*—she could see it, see the fingers going translucent at the edges, worn smooth as driftwood.

And last the truth: he was being *read*. Not eaten—eaten was too simple, too animal. He was being taken apart the way a clerk takes apart a ledger, line by line, entry by entry, every transaction of Aldric Corvane's life itemized and assessed. Felix could see it happening in his face. His mouth moved—forming words, forming *bargains*, the reflex of fifty years of negotiation—and whatever came out was not sound but memory, spilling from him in wisps: a boy in a chancellor's study memorizing ledgers to escape a father's belt; a young man poisoning a rival's cup and telling himself it was necessity; a thousand small cruelties filed under *prudent*; and underneath it all, at the bottom of the account, a single line written so many times it had worn a groove: *if I were only powerful enough, no one could ever hurt me again.*

The mountain read that line.

And the mountain, apparently, found it sufficient.

The tendril withdrew. Corvane remained standing at the top of the dais, upright, composed, his hands now folded behind his back in the posture he'd worn since entering—but he was *hollow*. Not dead. Hollow. His skin had gone the gray-white of birch bark, his eyes two smooth stones, and where the fever-brightness had burned there was nothing at all, just a vast quiet vacancy, a house swept clean of furniture.

He turned, slowly, with perfect courtier's grace, and descended the steps. He walked past Felix's pillar without seeing her, without seeing anything, his footsteps making no sound on the rock. At the edge of the antechamber he stopped, faced the wall, and stood still. Waiting. The way the other hollow ones waited, in their galleries, holding trays of food no one ate, wearing livery for a court that slept.

Aldric Corvane, Chancellor of Thornfell, the most dangerous man Felix had ever known, had gotten exactly what he asked for: a seat in the court beneath the mountain. It simply wasn't the kind that sits.

The tendril retracted into the throne. The pale light dimmed. And the oval face turned—slowly, with a grinding slowness that Felix felt more than heard—until those two dark hollows pointed directly at her pillar. At her.

At the mark on her shoulder, which burned suddenly, fiercely, like a coal laid against her skin, and a voice that was not a voice said, in the space behind her eyes where the Warden's riddles had lived:

*Two names came to the door tonight. One was given. One was promised.*

*The promised one may come forward.*

*The ledger is open.*

Felix's legs nearly gave. She caught herself against the stone, biting her lip until she tasted blood—real blood, copper and salt, the taste of being alive and *herself*, and she clung to that taste like a rope over a drop.

Tammy, she thought. *Think about Tammy.* His contract expired at midwinter. Three days. He was here, somewhere in these galleries, and if the mountain consumed chancellors for ambition, what would it do with a brother who had already been paid for?

She looked once more at the throne—at the shepherd's-small chair that was also a drain, at the hollow-eyed chancellor standing eternally at attention against the wall—and made herself walk forward. Not to the dais. Past it. Toward the servants' galleries, where the taken souls drifted through their duties like fish through green water.

Behind her, the voice in her skull murmured, patient as root-growth:

*Walk carefully, little tithe.*

*Everything you carry is already counted.*

* * *

The galleries were colder than the cavern, and quieter, and full of faces she almost knew.

They moved through the corridors performing tasks with dreamlike diligence: sweeping floors that gathered no dust, polishing railings already bright, carrying covered dishes from a kitchen Felix passed at a distance—she smelled it and her taster's tongue flinched, because nothing in that kitchen had a taste at all, only the memory of tastes, rendered in steam and scent with nothing underneath. The souls did not look at her. That was the rule, she realized; she'd seen it in the tales Sera told. The taken do not see the living. Their eyes slide past you the way your eyes slide past a servant in a corridor, and Felix felt a grim kinship with that—nine years of practicing exactly this, of being unseen by choice, and here was what it became when a mountain did it to you instead.

She checked each face against the boy in her memory. Tammy at seven, gap-toothed, stealing apples off the temple carts. Tammy at ten, taller, teaching her a whistle-code down the well. Tammy at twelve, the night the collectors came, looking back over his shoulder from between two gray figures, mouthing something she'd never been able to read.

None of them were him. None of them were anyone.

Then, near the gallery's end, a soul dropped a tray.

It was a small thing—a clay cup rolling, a spoon clattering—and the sound rang through the corridor like a bell, and every hollow head turned toward it at once, uniformly, terribly, like a field of flowers following the sun. Felix froze in an alcove, heart hammering, and watched the soul kneel to gather the pieces. A woman, maybe, or had been; her face was smoothed to blankness but her hands were quick and practiced, a servant's hands, and as she rose—

Her eyes flickered. Just for a moment. Down the corridor. To Felix.

And in them, drowned and distant, something *noticed*.

Felix's blood went cold. Because that was impossible. That was against the rules. And the woman—soul, hollow, whatever she was—set the mended tray on her shoulder, resumed her procession, and as she passed Felix's alcove her lips parted, barely, and what came out was not words but a thread of sound, thin as wire:

*"…the children's wing keeps what it's given… third gallery past the singing stones…"*

Then her face smoothed over, and she was gone into the procession, one more gray figure among dozens, and Felix stood shaking in the dark with her hand pressed over her mouth.

Someone in here was still fighting.

She made herself move. Third gallery past the singing stones—which she found by sound alone, a chamber where stalactites hung tuned like organ pipes and the wind through them made fragments of melody, broken hymns, half-remembered lullabies. She did not let herself listen. If she listened she would hear her mother's song in them and she would come apart right there, and she could not come apart, not yet, not with three days on the clock and her brother three galleries away.

The children's wing.

She knew it before she saw it. The air changed—the mountain's cloying sweetness thinning into something older, milk-and-stone, a nursery smell preserved for decades. Small beds lined the walls, carved from the living rock, each with a blanket of woven root-fiber. Small clothes, folded. Small shoes, pairs of them, arranged neatly, waiting for owners who no longer had feet in this world or any other.

And in the seventh bed, sitting up, awake, staring at the wall with wide gray eyes—

Felix forgot every plan she'd ever made. She crossed the room in four strides and dropped to her knees beside the bed and reached for him, and he flinched back against the wall, and she stopped, hands trembling in the air between them.

"Tamsin," she whispered. "Tamsin Amberdale. It's me. It's Lixie."

He looked at her the way the hollow ones looked at everything: politely, briefly, without recognition. Then his brow creased—the smallest motion, a crack in porcelain—and he said, in a voice rusty with disuse:

"Do I… know the song? The one the stones sing? It feels like—" His hand rose to his chest, pressing flat, puzzled. "It feels like someone left something here. A long time ago. And I keep almost finding it."

Felix's vision blurred. She blinked it clear. Outside, far above, midwinter was coming, and somewhere in this mountain a ledger waited with his name in it, and the throne had called her *little tithe* and told her everything she carried was already counted.

Three days. One song. One brother who almost remembered.

She took his cold hand in hers, and began, very quietly, to sing.
