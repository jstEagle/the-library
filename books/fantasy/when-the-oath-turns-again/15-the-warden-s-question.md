## 15. The Warden's Question

The mountain did not roar when it woke. That was the worst part.

Felix had expected thunder, a splitting of stone, the gate bursting open like a wound. Instead there was only a sound like a great door closing somewhere far below—a soft, deliberate *thud* that she felt in her teeth rather than heard—and then the corridors of the court beneath began to breathe.

She felt it through the soles of her boots as she ran: a slow expansion, a slow contraction, patient as sleep, vast as tide. The roots that laced the walls flexed and settled. The bone-lattice of the throne room's ceiling, which had hung above her like a frozen chandelier during Corvane's burning, now shifted with the rhythm of something inhaling. The hollow-eyed souls who served the court did not flee. They simply turned, all of them at once, their beautiful blank faces tilting toward her like flowers finding a sun that wasn't there.

Felix ran anyway. She had Tammy's hand-print still ghosting on her palm from the moment he'd said her name—*Fee?*—and the crack it had opened in his contract was still singing along her nerves. The mountain demanded replacement; the Warden's riddle had promised trade. Somewhere between those two truths was a path, and she intended to find it before the mountain closed its throat.

The throne room had changed since she'd last stood in it. Corvane's name was gone—not consumed exactly, but *digested*, and the space where he'd made his bargain was marked by a smooth patch of new-grown root, pale as birch, already polished by the passage of unseen hands. The throne itself seemed taller. Or perhaps it had simply leaned closer.

And before it, where no one had stood before, stood the keeper.

Felix stopped.

She had imagined the Warden a hundred times on the long walk down—had dressed it in every nightmare the smuggler roads could furnish. A thing of teeth and toll-booths. A face made of ledger-pages. The collector who had taken Tammy grown to its final size.

It was none of these. It was an old person. That was all. Old the way river stones are old, old the way the word *old* stops meaning years and starts meaning *before*. Its robes were the grey of ash that has finished being fire, patched with what looked like scraps of letters—fragments of names, half-legible, stitched over the holes worn through the cloth. Its hands were folded in front of it, and they were the hands of someone who had carried too many things for too long and set them down only to pick up more. Its eyes were tired. Not cruel-tired. *Tired*-tired. The tiredness of a lock that has been turned ten thousand times by ten thousand keys and remembers every one.

"You are the one who sings," the Warden said. Its voice was quiet, and the whole room bent toward it to listen, including Felix's own bones. "The mother-song. It is not often sung down here. The last time was—" It paused, and something moved across its face that might have been grief wearing work-clothes. "A long time ago."

"Where is Tammy?" Felix kept her voice level. Nine years of tasting poison had taught her that. Level voice, steady hands, and never let them see the tremor until it was useful. "If you've put him back behind the contract—"

"He is where he was. Sleeping, in the manner of this place. The crack your song made has not closed." The Warden tilted its head, and the stitched letters on its robes rustled like dry leaves. "But you did not come all this way, twice, carrying a brother's name in your mouth like a coal, to ask me about sleeping. You came because you have questions. So. Ask."

"I want to know how to break his contract. Properly. Permanently."

"You want to know if the price can be paid by anyone other than him or you." The Warden's tired eyes did not blink. "Yes. You do. But that is not the question you need answered first."

"The hell it isn't."

"It is not," the Warden said, with the mild immovability of a mountain being asked to move, "and I will tell you why. Because the mountain will weigh whatever you bring it, and it weighs true, and if you come to the scale carrying a self you do not understand, the weighing will go badly for everyone whose name you love." It unfolded one hand—an invitation, a gesture so humanly weary that it hurt worse than any threat. "So first, taster of Thornfell, answer me the question I have carried since the gates were shut. The question I ask every soul who stands where you stand."

The roots beneath Felix's feet pulsed once, slow, expectant.

"Who are you," the Warden asked, "if no one remembers you?"

* * *

The silence after the question was enormous. It had texture. It pressed against her eardrums like deep water.

Felix opened her mouth to answer—*Felix Amberdale, food-taster to the crown of Thornfell, daughter of Marla Amberdale, sister to Tammy*—and found that the words would not form. Not because she didn't know them. Because saying them here, in front of this tired ancient thing, would be like offering a paper shield against a flood. Every title she owned suddenly revealed itself as what it was: a costume. Food-taster. Smuggler-road guide. The forgettable one. The invisible girl.

Nine years of armor, and standing in front of the Warden, she could feel every plate of it rusting through.

"I know my name," she said finally.

"Yes," the Warden agreed gently. "But that is not what I asked. Anyone can carry a name in a pocket. The merchant carries his ledgers in a pocket. The dead carry their headstones. I asked who you *are*." It took a step closer, and its bare feet made no sound at all on the root-floor, though the roots themselves seemed to lean away from it with something like deference. Even the mountain, apparently, was polite to its keeper. "You have spent nine years ensuring that no one remembers you. I have watched. We watch everything that walks our roads, even the ones who believe themselves unwatched. You have cultivated your own forgetting with the devotion other people give to prayer. And it has worked beautifully. The chancellor needed three years to suspect you. The physician needed five. Your own neighbors in the servants' quarters cannot describe your face if asked. You are the most successful nothing in Thornfell."

Each sentence landed like a coin dropped into a well—she heard herself falling with each one.

"So tell me," the Warden continued, softly, terribly, "when you stand in a room and no one sees you, who is it that stands there? When you taste the queen's plum and feel the poison fold harmlessly around your tongue—who tastes it? What is left, Felix Amberdale, when everyone who might have held your name in their memory has let it slip through their fingers like water?"

"That's not fair," Felix said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she hated it. "I did what I had to. Invisibility isn't death. It's survival. You don't know what it's like out there—knowing things gets people killed. Remembering things gets people killed. Being *remembered* got Tammy taken—"

"Did it?" the Warden asked.

And that was the knife under the floorboards, wasn't it. Because Tammy hadn't been taken for being remembered. He'd been taken because he was *loved*—because their mother's debt collectors knew that the surest currency in a family is the child everyone would move mountains to get back. He had been remembered so hard it killed him. And Felix had learned the wrong lesson from it. She had looked at her brother's disappearance and concluded that the safe thing, the *surviving* thing, was to make sure no one ever had a reason to reach for her.

She had spent nine years practicing a smaller, slower version of what the mountain did.

"Oh," she said.

The sound came out of her very small.

"You begin to see," the Warden said. There was no triumph in it. That was somehow worse. If it had gloated, she could have hated it, and hating it would have been easier than what she actually felt, which was the specific vertigo of a wall you've been leaning on your whole life turning out to be painted on air. "I am not the enemy of your kind, taster. I am the *keeper*. I keep what is given. And what your court gave us—what your mother gave us, what your grandmother's grandmother gave us in the first bargain, the one written before the mountain had a gate—I keep that too. Do you know what the tithe is? Everyone up there thinks it is children. Children are only the *shape* of it. The tithe is selves. Memory. The weight of a person being known. That is the only coin we honor, because it is the only coin that cannot be counterfeited." It gestured, one weary hand, at the hollow-eyed souls drifting through the periphery of the hall, serving wine to no one, arranging flowers of pale root in vessels of bone. "Look at them. They were given. Their names live here now, in the contracts, in the walls. They are perfectly preserved and perfectly forgotten. No one above remembers them. And so—" it spread its hands, "—they are no one. Forever. Safely. Just as you have made yourself."

"They're slaves."

"They are *held*. Whether that is slavery depends on whether anyone comes to claim them." The Warden's eyes settled on her, and in their depths she saw something that startled her: not malice, not hunger. *Hope*. Faint and rusted, like a hinge that hasn't turned in centuries, but hope nonetheless. "Do you know why I ask the question? Why I have asked it of every soul who ever stood on this floor? Because in all my keeping, in all the long ages of the contracts, I have never once had an answer worth writing down. They all say what you nearly said. *I am a taster. I am a smuggler. I am the king's man.* Pockets full of names. And when the pockets are emptied into the ledger, there is nothing left to hold the shape of them, and they become these—" the souls, drifting, "—and I must keep them, because someone must, because a forgotten thing should at least be kept somewhere." The old voice frayed, just slightly, at the edge. "I am so tired of keeping no ones, Felix Amberdale. I was not made for it. I was made to keep *names*. Living ones. Ones spoken aloud by mouths that love them."

* * *

Felix found she had sat down at some point. The root-floor was warm beneath her, faintly pulsing, like kneeling on the chest of something enormous and asleep. She didn't remember deciding to sit. Her legs had simply reported that the conversation required it.

Somewhere above her, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, she could hear it beginning—the distant gathering. Sera's messages had gone out two days ago down every smuggler road she knew, and Felix had added a dozen more of her own, chalked signs and folded notes slipped to fences and ferrymen and the woman who sold roasted chestnuts outside the temple quarter. *Come to the mountain's mouth at midwinter. Come say his name.* She had told herself it was a long shot. A mad one. A priestess's fever-dream dressed up as strategy.

But now, listening through the rock, she thought she could hear footsteps. Many. Far off. Growing.

"Tammy's contract expires tonight," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears—stripped of the flat court-neutral tone she'd worn for nine years, younger, rougher, more like the girl who had once cried into her mother's shoulder on a grave-hill overlooking Thornfell. "Midwinter. You said the crack hasn't closed. Tell me what happens if it closes with his name unsaid."

"He returns to the holding. Fully. The fragment of him that sang back to you goes quiet again, and this time it does not resurface. Contracts that crack once and heal are stronger than they were." The Warden said it without cruelty, the way a physician reports a prognosis. "And the mountain will take its customary interest besides. One name of equal weight, freely offered, within the season—or the balance is collected elsewhere. Debts in this house do not vanish, taster. They only change pockets."

"And if a name IS offered? Freely?"

The Warden was quiet for a long moment. The breathing of the mountain slowed around them, as if even it were listening.

"Then the scale is brought out," it said at last, "and the name is weighed, and if the weight is true—if it is a self, whole, known, *remembered*—the contract may be rewritten. Not broken. Rewritten. The difference matters. Broken contracts shatter and take pieces of everyone nearby. Rewritten contracts..." The tired old eyes fixed on hers, and there it was again, that rusted hinge of hope, creaking open a fraction wider. "Rewritten contracts can release. Completely. A soul walked out of this court by its own name, spoken by loving mouths, is a soul I no longer have any right to keep. It is the only way anything leaves here whole. It has happened four times in the history of the keeping. Four times. And each time, it took not one offerer but many witnesses."

"Witnesses." Felix thought of the footsteps in the dark above. Thought of Sera, somewhere up there now, wounded and stubborn, deciphering the last of the riddles. Thought of chestnut-sellers and ferrymen and fences and the boy at the toll bridge whom Tammy had once taught to skip stones. "People who remember him."

"People who remember him *aloud*. Memory is not a possession, Felix Amberdale. It is a performance. It only exists while it is being done. That is why the mountain prefers solitary bargains—one frightened soul alone at the scale, trading a pocket-name. Solitary names weigh little. But a name spoken by fifty throats at once—" For the first time, the Warden smiled, and the smile transformed it entirely: it became briefly, heartbreakingly, just an old servant glad of good news after long bad years. "A name spoken by many throats weighs more than the mountain."

Felix stood up. Her knees protested. She ignored them.

"Then I need to ask you something else," she said, and now she could hear the footsteps clearly, dozens of them, hundreds, descending through the old smuggler roads she knew better than her own heartbeat, guided by chalk marks she'd made in another life, another name. "Because there's one more thing the mountain wants. It wants a replacement for Tammy. Equal weight. And nine years ago, somebody decided that replacement would be me—marked at birth, kept healthy, kept immune, kept *preserved* like goods in a warehouse. Your mountain has been keeping ME too. Hasn't it. My immunity. My forgettableness. That wasn't my armor. That was your packaging."

The Warden's smile faded into something older and sadder.

"Yes," it said. "You were given before you drew breath. The oldest kind of contract. I have kept your health the way I keep everything entrusted to me. Faithfully." It paused. "I would gladly stop."

"Good." Felix walked toward it, past the drifting hollow-eyed souls, past the throne of roots and bone, until she stood close enough to see that the patches on the Warden's robe weren't letters at all—they were names, hundreds of names, worn thin as cloth from handling. "Because I'm done being kept. And I'm done being no one. So here's my answer to your question, keeper—the real one this time."

She breathed in. The mountain breathed with her.

"Who am I if no one remembers me? Nobody. That's the truth. Nine years of nobody, and I know exactly what nobody looks like from the inside—it looks like this court. Preserved. Safe. Dead." Her voice steadied, and rose, and she heard in it, for the first time, the shape of her mother's singing voice, the lullaby voice, the voice that had survived in Tammy's cracked-open memory against all the mountain's keeping. "But I'm about to find out who I am if everybody remembers me. Because I hear them coming down my roads right now, keeper, every soul my brother ever fed or sheltered or made laugh, and when they arrive, I'm going to stand on your scale and give you the one thing you've never been offered in all your long keeping."

The Warden's tired eyes went wide.

"My name," said Felix Amberdale, aloud, in the dark, where it could finally be heard. "All of it. Freely. Now open the gate—we have witnesses to gather."
