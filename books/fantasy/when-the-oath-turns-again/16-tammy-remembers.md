## 16. Tammy Remembers

The mountain was screaming.

Not with a mouth—mountains do not have mouths—but with every stone at once, a grinding groan that Felix felt in her back teeth and the roots of her hair. Dust fell from the vaulted ceiling of the great hall like gray snow, sifting down through shafts of greenish light that came from nowhere and illuminated nothing. Somewhere behind her, Corvane's hired blades were shouting, their voices bouncing off walls that had not heard human panic in three hundred years. Somewhere ahead, past pillars carved to look like trees that had never grown on any hillside Felix knew, the throne of roots and bone was waking, and it was *hungry*.

And Tammy was standing ten feet away from her, looking at her as if she were a stranger.

Which, she supposed, she was.

"Felix Amberdale," she had said, when she first reached him—her name offered like a key, like a hand extended across ice. He had blinked at her with eyes that were still his eyes, warm brown, their mother's eyes, but behind them was nothing. Not emptiness exactly. Worse: a smoothness. A surface with nothing written on it. The courtiers of the mountain court wore their hollowness like silk; Tammy wore his like a fresh bandage over a wound that hadn't happened yet.

"You're new," he'd said politely. "You shouldn't be here. The gate's closed now."

"The gate's open," she'd told him. "I opened it."

He had frowned then, the little frown that used to crease his whole face when he was six and someone took the last honey-bun, and for one wild heartbeat she thought—*he knows me, he remembers*—but no. It was only the frown of a boy doing sums that wouldn't come out right. "That's not allowed," he said. "Nothing is allowed anymore. Nothing has been allowed for nine years."

Nine years. She had counted them in smuggler roads and swallowed poisons and unmarked graves. He had counted them in nothing at all.

Then the mountain had woken angry, and there had been no more time for talking.

* * *

Now she grabbed his wrist and pulled, and he came—not resisting, exactly, but moving the way water moves when you dam it, finding the shape of least resistance without ever choosing it. His skin was cold. That was the thing that undid her most, in all this collapsing splendor. She had imagined him starved, scarred, broken. She had not imagined him *cold*, cold the way river stones are cold, cold from being held too long by something that had no warmth to give.

"Tammy. Tammy, look at me."

"I'm looking." He said it mildly, reasonably, a soul-servant's patience. Around them the great hall shuddered; a pillar split along its grain with a sound like a scream and shed its bark of carved leaves. One of Corvane's men ran past them toward the dark of the far corridor, pursued by nothing visible, pursued perhaps by something very visible indeed—Felix didn't stop to check. "You're hurting my arm."

She let go. Then, because letting go felt like drowning, she caught his hand instead and laced her fingers through his, the way she used to when they crossed the ford above Millbrook and the current wanted him and she would not allow it. His fingers did not curl around hers. They just lay there, cold and obedient.

"Tammy. Our mother's name was Wren."

"Was it?" A flicker. Almost nothing. The corner of his mouth twitching toward something it could not become. "I don't—I have a mother. Everyone here has a mother. We're all somebody's child, aren't we?" He smiled up at the ceiling, at the dust falling through the green light, serene as a saint in a fresco. "Mother says so."

"Mother says so," Felix repeated, and her voice cracked down the middle like the pillar. "Which mother, Tam? Which one says so?"

"The—" He stopped. His brow furrowed. For an instant the smoothness rippled, and beneath it she saw something struggling, something small and drowning, reaching up through dark water. Then it sank again. "I don't remember saying that."

The mountain groaned. Somewhere deep below them—or above; in this place the directions had stopped agreeing with each other—a door made of stone rolled open, and a wind came through smelling of loam and iron and old, old sweetness, like fruit rotting in a cellar full of silver.

Felix had spent nine years learning what things tasted like. Poison, mostly. But also everything else: the difference between rain that would come and rain that wouldn't, between bread baked with love and bread baked with resentment, between water and almost-water. And she knew, standing in the shaking hall with her brother's cold hand in hers, that the taste of this place was the taste of a bargain gone bad. Sweet underneath. Bitter on top. Wrong all the way through.

Sera's voice echoed somewhere behind her, hoarse with dust and prayer: *"What was given cannot be stolen, only traded!"*

But first—first—

First he had to remember her at all.

* * *

Their mother had sung badly.

That was the truth of it, and Felix clung to that truth now because it was the only handle left on the door. Wren Amberdale had sung the way a cart rattles: enthusiasm without pitch, rhythm without mercy. The lullaby she sang over their shared bed in the crooked house above the tannery was about a blackbird who lost his shadow and went looking for it through all the hours of the day. She always forgot the third verse. She always invented a new one on the spot, and the new ones never rhymed, and Tammy used to laugh until he hiccupped, and eventually she'd laugh too, mid-verse, and forget where she was, and start over.

Nobody sang it right. Nobody *could* sing it right. That was the point—that was maybe, Felix realized with a lurch, the whole secret heart of it—the song belonged to the two of them precisely because it had never once been sung correctly. It wasn't a song. It was a room they had built together, out of wrong notes.

So Felix did not sing it right either.

"*Oh the blackbird woke at morning and his shadow wasn't there,*" she began, and her voice came out ragged, half-swallowed by dust, "*so he asked the sun where'd you put it, and the sun said ask elsewhere—*"

Tammy's head turned. Slowly. Like a door on rusted hinges.

"*...ask elsewhere,*" he whispered. Not the next line. The echo of her line. His lips moved around the words as though tasting them, as though they were a flavor from a country he'd been exiled from before he could map it.

Felix's heart slammed against her ribs hard enough to hurt. She kept going, walking backward down the ruined verse, pulling him after her by the thread of it:

"*And he asked the noon bell, have you seen it, hanging bright upon a tree, and the bell said I only ring for—*" she faltered, deliberately, the way their mother always had, "*—for—oh, blast, what does it ring for—*"

"For shadows," Tammy said.

His voice was different. Less smooth. There was gravel in it now, and under the gravel, wet clay, and under the clay—

"It rings for shadows," he repeated, frowning at his own mouth as if it had betrayed him by speaking. "Why do I know that? Why do I—" He looked down at their joined hands. Looked at her face. And Felix watched it happen, watched it the way you watch a river ice break in spring: nothing, nothing, nothing, then a crack running shore to shore with a sound like God clearing her throat.

His chin crumpled. Nine years old forever, and twenty-two besides, both at once.

"Lix?" he said.

The word was barely a word. Half-swallowed, dust-choked, wrong-pitched, sung the way a cart rattles. It was the most beautiful sound Felix Amberdale had ever heard in her life.

"TAMMY." She seized his shoulders. "Yes. Yes, it's me, it's Lix, it's your stupid sister who couldn't keep you home, I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I looked for nine years—"

"You came," he said, wonderingly, tears cutting clean tracks through the pale grime of the mountain court. "You came. You always—you always came. Even when I hid in the tannery barrels, even when Mother said leave him, he'll sulk himself out—" His hands flew up and gripped her arms with sudden desperate strength, cold fingers digging in, and the smoothness was gone, shattered, and underneath was her brother, whole and terrified and *himself*. "Lix, Lix, how long, how long was I—"

"Nine years."

"Nine—" He swayed. She caught him. "It's been—it was autumn. It was harvest. I was carrying the apples in and the road was longer than it should've been and then—" His breath went thin. "Then I was here. And everyone was kind. Everyone was *so kind*, Lix, why is everyone here so kind, why can't I remember being anywhere else—"

"Because they ate it," Felix said, and her voice came out flat and terrible. "They ate the years, Tam. That's the toll. That's what the mountain takes."

Above them the throne of roots and bone stirred.

She felt it before she saw it: a pressure, like the moment before lightning, like the silence after a struck bell. The green light bent. The dust hung motionless in the air, every mote suspended, and in that terrible stillness Felix heard—not with her ears, which had gone deaf with pressure, but with the poison-proof tongue that had kept her alive for nine years—a *taste*. Old sweetness. Iron. Attention.

The mountain had noticed that something of its own had just remembered itself.

* * *

"No," Tammy breathed. "No, no, Lix, you have to go, you have to go *now*, it doesn't like—when we remember, it doesn't like—"

"When you what?"

"Remember." His grip on her arms tightened to bruising. "There are others. Down in the deep courts, the old ones, the ones who've been here longest—they get flashes, sometimes. A smell. A name. And then the Kind Ones come for them, smiling, and take them down to be smoothed again, and afterward they don't even know they've lost anything, and—" his voice dropped to a horrified whisper, "—and sometimes they don't come back smoothed. Sometimes they don't come back at all. The throne drinks what comes loose."

The pressure redoubled. The suspended dust began, slowly, to drift upward.

And then the contract appeared.

Felix didn't see it so much as taste it: a bitterness flooding her mouth, sharp as gall, precise as script. In the air between herself and Tammy, letters of pale fire assembled themselves out of nothing, hanging in the green light, and she read them with her heart hammering:

*CONTRACT OF TOLL — ONE SOUL, GIVEN FREELY BY WREN AMBERDALE AGAINST DEBT.*

*TERM: NINE YEARS PLUS ONE SEASON. EXPIRES MIDLWINTER.*

*STATUS:* —and here the letters flickered, stuttered, like a candle in wind—*STATUS: BREACHED. SUBJECT RECALLED TO SELF.*

Tammy made a sound like a laugh and a sob fighting to a draw. "Breached," he whispered. "I'm breached. Lix, I'm—does that mean—am I—"

"Free," Felix said. "It means free. Sera said—there's a way to void it, we just have to—"

The letters burned brighter. New script crawled beneath the old, and Felix read it, and the knife went in exactly where she'd known, in some cold basement of herself, that it would.

*A SOUL RECALLED LEAVES A VACANCY IN THE COURT. THE TOLL MUST BE MAINTAINED. REPLACEMENT REQUIRED: ONE NAME OF EQUAL WEIGHT.*

*EQUAL WEIGHT: A TRUE NAME, FREELY SURRENDERED, BEFORE THE THRONE.*

*OR THE BREACH IS CLOSED AND THE TERM RESUMES.*

"Or what happens at midwinter anyway," Felix said hollowly.

"What?" Tammy couldn't read the burning script; his eyes slid off it the way common folk's eyes slid off royal seals. "Lix, what does it say?"

She couldn't answer. Because she understood it all at once, completely, the way she understood poisons—instinctively, in the tongue and the gut. The mountain did not deal in lives. It dealt in *selves*. Tammy's contract was cracking, and cracks could be mended two ways: refill the vacancy with a new soul of equal worth, or seal the breach and take him back down into the smoothing dark until midwinter finished the job.

A name of equal weight. A true name, freely surrendered.

Hers would do. She knew it would do; she could feel it in the bitter taste, the mountain's attention settling on her now like a physical weight, patient, ancient, *interested*. Felix Amberdale, the girl nobody noticed, the woman who had spent nine years sanding herself down to nothing so she could survive. Her name weighed almost nothing at all, surely—surely the mountain would find it too light—

But no. That was the horror of it. That was the knife under the hope. Her name was *heavy*. Nine years of silence had not made it light; it had compressed it, like coal into diamond. Every road she'd memorized, every poison she'd swallowed, every night she'd lain awake rehearsing conversations she'd never dare have—all of it was pressed into those four syllables, dense and burning. The mountain knew value when it tasted it.

And it wanted her.

"Take your hands off him."

Corvane's voice. She'd forgotten Corvane—forgotten the army of blades, the opened gate, the ambition wearing a reasonable face. He came limping out of the dust-haze with blood on his collar and triumph in his eyes, and behind him, faintly, in the far corridors, she could hear his hired men discovering what the mountain did with trespassers. He looked at Tammy—at the cracked contract, at the breach—and Felix watched the arithmetic move behind his face like rats behind a wall.

"A name of equal weight," Corvane said softly. "Freely surrendered. Before the throne." He smiled at Felix, and it was almost a kind smile, which made it worse. "Well. Here we all are."

The dust drifted upward. The throne waited. Tammy's cold hand found hers again, and this time his fingers curled around hers, holding on like a drowning man, and he whispered, "Lix, whatever it says, whatever it wants—don't. Don't you give it anything. Promise me. You promised me things when we were little and you *always kept them*—"

The letters flared.

*THE THRONE AWAITING,* they wrote, in gall and pale fire, *CHOOSE.*
