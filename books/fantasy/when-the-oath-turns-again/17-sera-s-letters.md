## 17. Sera's Letters

The mountain's mouth was bleeding light when Sera Vane came down out of the pass.

Not torchlight — nothing so honest. It seeped from the crack in the rock like something poured from a wound, gold gone green at the edges, and it turned the snow on the scree to the color of old teeth. She had walked through the night on a leg that no longer wished to be consulted about such decisions, and every step of the last mile had been a negotiation between her will and the hot wire of pain drawn tight from ankle to hip. The smuggler's tunnel behind her had collapsed in three places. She had been under the second collapse when it came down.

She did not let herself think about that yet. Thinking about it meant stopping, and stopping meant lying down, and lying down meant she would never stand again, not with the cold coming up out of the stone and into her bones like it had found an old address and a welcome.

So instead she recited. It was what she had, after all these years — not faith, which had been stripped from her like bark from a branch, but the shape faith leaves behind. The litany of the old gods' names, all nine, spoken backward as the mourning rites required, from the least to the greatest. *Vess. Ilm. The Two Who Danced. Mother of Brackish Water. The Warden.* On and on, breath fogging, one name per step, while the mountain drank the sound of her voice the way it drank everything.

Her satchel knocked against her hip with each stride, and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth against the wet, were eleven letters.

She had written them over two days in the shrine cave, by tallow-light, on the backs of old rite-plates because there was nothing else to write on. Eleven letters, because she had counted her life's debts and found them elevenfold. One to the seminary at Coldharbour that had cast her out, though none of her teachers still lived. One to a woman named Marris in the fen-country who had loved her before the disgrace, and whom she had left without farewell rather than drag her under. One to the congregation she had failed — the whole village of Ashford-on-the-Water, every soul of it, though she could only write to the churchwarden and ask him to read it aloud. One to her mother. One to her mother, again, because the first had come out wrong, too full of excuses, and the dead deserved better than excuses.

Eleven letters, and not one addressed to anyone who would ever receive it. That had been the point. That had been the part she hadn't let herself examine too closely, the way you don't examine a rope bridge too closely before you cross it. The letters were not farewells. Farewells implied arrival somewhere. They were more like — weights. Things written down so that when the mountain took her name, took *Sera Vane* entire, out of every mouth and memory and ledger where it lived, there would be ink left behind. Proof she had existed, even if no one remained who knew it.

*What was given cannot be stolen, only traded,* the Warden had said, in its voice like wind through a keyhole, asking its terrible questions. And Sera had done the arithmetic of despair in the dark of the shrine, and concluded: a name freely surrendered, a true name, of sufficient weight. Hers would do. Hers was heavy. Twenty-three years a priestess, seven years a heretic, thirty years total of being someone — being someone was the coin, and she had always had the misfortune of being someone loudly.

Felix Amberdale's little brother had perhaps a day left of his borrowed time. Perhaps less. The contract was cracking — Tammy had said his sister's name, Sera had felt the mountain shudder around them like a dog shaking off water — but a cracking contract only made the creditor crueler. It would take him now, all at once, hollow him out and set his beautiful empty body among the root-court's servers, unless something of equal weight was laid on the scale.

She came down the last of the switchbacks with the litany broken now, names tumbling out of order, and the wound in her leg singing its one note louder and louder, and the green-gold light rising to meet her like a tide.

And there, at the mountain's mouth, framed in that unwholesome glow with her hands black to the wrist with tunnel-dust and something darker, stood Felix Amberdale.

* * *

"You're bleeding," Felix said.

"How observant," Sera said. "How are the children."

"Tammy's holding. Barely." Felix's face did something complicated — grief held in a net of discipline, the way a taster holds poison in her mouth without swallowing. "The courtiers say the throne has adjourned. That's not a good sign. Courts only adjourn when they intend to collect." She looked Sera over the way she looked at a dish she'd been given to test, top to bottom, cataloguing. "You shouldn't have come through the tunnels alone. I told you to wait for my word."

"The mountain is waking up under our feet, the deadline is midwinter, which is tomorrow, and your brother is currently being weighed in a scale we cannot see." Sera leaned against the rock, mostly for show, partly because her leg had submitted its resignation. "Waiting seemed ill-advised."

Something moved across Felix's face that might, in a person less practiced at being unnoticed, have been called a smile.

Then Sera pushed off the wall to walk toward the gate-crack, and the leg simply declined, and the scree came up at her, and Felix caught her — caught all of her, arm hooked under Sera's shoulder with a strength that the small woman carried deceptively, and lowered her to sit against a boulder with a care that bordered on tenderness.

Sera looked down at her own leg. The gash ran from boot-top to knee, torn, not cut, and crusted dark, and beneath the crust something glimmered faintly. Not blood-glint. A fine grain of gold-green light, worked into the wound like dust into honey.

"It followed me out," she said. Her voice came out very level, which surprised her. "The collapse. The roots. They're in the stone everywhere now, even outside. It touched me while it pulled the tunnel down." She flexed her foot and watched the light shimmer. "It didn't try to kill me, if that means anything. It marked me. Like a merchant chalking a crate."

Felix crouched, tore a strip from her own hem without ceremony, and began cleaning the wound with snow-melt and a flask of spirit from her belt. Her hands were quick and impersonal and exactly as gentle as they needed to be, no more, no less, and Sera thought absurdly, watching her work, *this is what she should have been allowed to be*, this competent, careful person, in some small life with no thrones anywhere near it.

"I need to tell you what I've decided," Sera said.

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I know what you decided. I can see it in your face, and I can see the satchel." Felix didn't look up from the wound. "You've got that look priests get. The martyred ones. You've written your letters and balanced your accounts and you've come here to pay, and you want me to stand aside and witness it respectfully." She tied off the bandage, hard enough to make Sera hiss. "No."

Sera drew a breath to summon the full authority of her office — the office she no longer held, in the faith that no longer held her — and found that what came out instead was quieter. "Felix. What else is there? You heard the Warden. The mountain honors one currency. A true name, freely surrendered, of weight to balance what it's owed. Mine balances. Yours—" she stopped, because the next word was *doesn't*, and it wasn't true anymore, was it? Since the scar on Felix's shoulder had been read aloud in the deep places, since the tithe-mark had been named, Felix's name weighed more than any of them had guessed. Which was precisely why Sera had resolved, marching through the night, that Felix must never be the one to pay it.

"My mother sold Tammy's name to this place," Felix said. She sat back on her heels, and the green light from the gate made her eyes look like shallow water over stones. "He was eight. Nobody asked him. Nobody asked her twice, either — the collectors came the same hour. Nothing about that bargain was free, and nothing about it was fair, and it has been eating our family for nine years." She looked up. "And you want to walk in there and make another bargain just like hers. Out of love, this time. Out of guilt, this time. But same shape. Same altar."

"It isn't the same—"

"It's exactly the same. Someone decides their own suffering is worth more than someone else's choice, and calls it sacrifice." Felix's voice cracked, just once, and she pressed on through the crack. "I spent nine years making myself invisible so that no one could sell me or spend me. I am not going to watch the first friend I have make herself disappear forever and call it holy."

Sera had been called many things. Priestess. Apostate. Oracle, madwoman, witch. No one had ever called her *friend* in anything other than the ordinary careless way, and certainly no one had said it the way Felix just had — like an accusation, like a debt, like a hand closing around her wrist above deep water.

The tears, when they came, embarrassed her thoroughly. She turned her face away, toward the mountain, and let two of them fall, and then mastered herself as she had mastered herself through her trial and her defrocking and seven winters alone in a cave.

"There's no other path," she said. "I've read everything. I've prayed until my knees broke. The Warden asks its questions and gives nothing. There is one currency and I hold it."

"Yes," Felix said. "One currency." And then she went still, in that particular way of hers — Sera had learned to recognize it, the moment the taster's tongue met something unexpected, the total stoppage of all motion while the new flavor was read. "Wait. One currency. But what — what makes a name weigh anything?"

"Years lived. Deeds. Memory. The number of people who—"

Felix's head snapped up.

"—who know it," Sera finished, and the sentence hung there between them, and the mountain breathed its slow green breath, and somewhere below, deep in the roots, something vast shifted in its sleep.

"That's why Tammy remembering me cracked the contract," Felix whispered. "One voice. One voice speaking my name, one memory restored, and nine years of their claim split down the middle. Not because I'm powerful. Because he *knew* me." She was on her feet, pacing, the dust falling off her like she was shedding a skin. "It's not the name itself. It never was. A name is just — a hook to hang remembering on. The weight is in everyone who carries it. Sera. Sera, how many people have you saved? In twenty-three years of priesthood. How many people have you buried, married, fed, taught, dragged out of ditches? How many people are carrying the name *Sera Vane* whether they know it or not?"

"Ashford burned half its records in the purges," Sera said automatically, the objection rising out of habit, out of thirty years of being the one who tempered enthusiasm with canon law. "Most of my congregation scattered—"

"Scattered isn't dead. Scattered is *spread out*." Felix whirled on her. "You know the roads. I know the roads. Every smuggler track and drover's path and pilgrim shortcut in Thornfell, and nobody stops travelers on those roads, nobody asks names, nobody burns letters. We send word down every route tonight. Everyone you ever served. Everyone Tammy ever shared bread with — the fence at Marlow's Crossing, the ferry-widow, the whole network of people my brother fed out of the kitchens for years while they thought no one saw. They come to the mountain. They speak his name. They speak yours."

"They'll never come. People don't come back for—"

"For a stranger? No." Felix crouched in front of her, and took Sera's face in her two grimy, capable hands, and made her listen. "But they won't be coming for a stranger. They'll be coming for the woman who gave their children medicine in the plague winter and told them it was from the shrine of the Mother of Brackish Water. For the boy who slipped them rolls. They'll be coming for their own. That's the third path, Sera. Not one name on the scale. Hundreds. Thousands of small weights, all freely given. Let the mountain count *that*."

Sera wanted to believe it. That was the terrifying thing — she could feel belief moving back into the ruined tenement of her chest like squatters, lighting fires in rooms she'd sealed shut, and some part of her stood watching with folded arms and said: *you have been here before. You believed once, and it cost you everything, and this time it will cost you Felix's brother besides.*

"The mountain doesn't do refunds," she said. "When it hears a thousand voices naming Tammy Amberdale, it may simply decide to take a thousand souls."

"Then we'd better make certain the trade is struck before it counts heads," said Felix. "Which brings us to you. You said it yourself — the currency is real. Your name does balance. If the mountain needs a formal offer to open negotiations, you give it one. Freely offered. But offered into a *bargain*, not into oblivion — traded for terms, witnessed, bound. You keep your name until the deal closes. And if the mountain tries to cheat—" her smile was thin and cold and entirely the smuggler's daughter, "—it will discover that I have spent nine years learning every way there is to steal from people who think they own everything."

"And if it cheats faster than you can steal?"

"Then I guess we find out what a thousand friends are worth."

They looked at each other for a long moment, kneeling in the snow at the mouth of hell, and Sera began, helplessly, to laugh — a ragged, rusty sound that hurt her ribs and her leg and felt, unmistakably, like the ice going out of a river.

* * *

They worked by the green light until dawn.

Felix produced paper from her coat — she had, of course, already robbed the palace stationery stores on her way out, and ink, and three sticks of sealing wax of dubious provenance — and they wrote by turns while the sky went grey and then pale gold behind the peaks, the only honest light any of them would see that day.

Sera rewrote her letter to her mother first. Then, at Felix's blunt insistence, she wrote one to Marris in the fen-country that was not a farewell at all, but an apology with an address attached, and a promise of arrival, and the phrase *when this is done* used four times like a nail driven into the future.

Then the summons. Forty-one of them, in Sera's hand, short and unadorned, each one naming a road and a waypoint and a time — midwinter noon, the mountain's mouth — and ending with the same line, which Felix dictated and which Sera at first refused to write because it sounded like scripture and she had sworn off writing scripture:

*Come and say the name of someone who fed you. It is the only prayer left that works.*

"He'd hate it," Felix said, watching her fold the forty-first copy. "Tammy. He'd hate being prayed at. He used to put frogs in the offering basket."

"Then it's a good thing," Sera said, "that this isn't prayer. Prayer asks the gods for things. This is the gods' own accounting being handed back to them with corrections."

By the time the sun cleared the ridge, Felix had runners — actual runners, three gutter-children of her acquaintance who worked the lower tunnels and who accepted coins and instructions with the gravity of generals — and the first copies were already moving, west along the Sweetwater road, south through the Marlow Gap, north on the old drover's track where the snow lay blue in the shadows.

Sera stood at the mountain's mouth, leaning on a staff Felix had cut for her, and watched the last small figure vanish down the western switchback, and felt the letters leaving her like sparks off a bonfire — not weights after all. Seeds.

Below her feet, patient as geology, the mountain waited with her brother-in-all-but-blood on its scale.

"Well," Sera said, to the green-gold light, to the sleeping court, to whatever was listening down in the roots and bone. "We've sent for witnesses. I hope you weren't planning on a private ceremony."

Deep beneath them, something that had been asleep for three hundred years rolled over, and asked — in a voice like wind through a keyhole, audible through the soles of her boots — its first question of the morning:

*"Who told you that you were alone?"*
