## 18. The Gathering of Witnesses

The letters went out the way all true things left Thornfell: quietly, folded into flour sacks and boot heels and the false bottoms of cheese wheels, carried by hands that had never once asked Felix Amberdale her name.

She wrote them by candlelight in the back room of the Drowned Bell, where the ink froze in its pot if she let it sit too long. Forty-one letters. No—she counted again, dipping the quill, watching the flame gutter in the draft that came down off the mountain like a held breath. Forty-three. One for every soul on the list Sera had helped her build, a list that had begun with three names and grown every time Felix closed her eyes and let herself remember.

*Old Maret, who fed Tammy eel pie off the docks at Greywater when their mother's coin ran short.*

*The Cobb brothers, who taught him to skip stones on the sweetwater before it died, laughing so hard he fell in.*

*Denna Quill, the night-soil woman, who once hid a nine-year-old boy in her cart for three hours while collectors walked the market calling his name.*

Felix wrote until her fingers cramped. She did not sign her own name—not because she was ashamed of it, but because she had spent nine years teaching the roads not to carry it. Instead she signed as she had always signed, the way a smuggler signs: *from one who owes you, and you know why.* Every person on that list knew why. Every person on that list had eaten from Tammy's small generosity or sheltered his stubborn hope, and every one of them owed the Amberdales a debt they'd never spoken aloud because speaking debts aloud was how debts got collected.

This time, though, this time she wanted them to speak.

*Come to the mountain's mouth at midwinter,* she wrote. *Bring nothing but your memory of him. Say it out loud. His name is Tammy. Tammy Amberdale. Say it like you mean to keep it.*

The Warden's riddle had turned itself over in her mind for six days now, wearing a groove: *What was given cannot be stolen, only traded.* But Sera had gone further than the Warden, hunched over her ash-marked floor with chalk worn to the knuckle, and what Sera had found was older and stranger: contracts beneath the mountain were written in memory, sealed in solitude. A soul given up alone stayed given up. A name surrendered by one voice bound one voice.

But a name remembered by many—

"A contract is only as strong as its silence," Sera had said, staring at nothing, chalk dust on her lips like frost. "That's how they've kept it so long. Everyone forgets alone. Grief isolates. Debt shames. The mountain has never once had to fight a crowd."

Felix had looked at her brother's name on the ledger-page of her heart and thought: *then we will bring it a crowd.*

* * *

**Maret**

The letter came inside a wheel of salted herring, which Old Maret took as a fair omen, since herring had been the last honest meal she'd ever shared with the Amberdale boy.

She read it twice by the stove, then a third time standing in the doorway of her smokehouse looking north toward Thornfell, where the mountain sat under its lid of cloud like something sleeping with one eye open. Her knees were bad. Her lungs were worse. Midwinter was eleven days away and the passes already wore snow to the hip.

"Well," she said to the empty yard, to the gulls, to the ghost of a boy who had once eaten six of her eel pies in a single sitting and then washed her dishes without being asked, "I expect I'd best not die before then."

Her granddaughter found her packing an hour later.

"You can't walk to Thornfell, Gran."

"I can't," Maret agreed cheerfully, "but I know a man with a cart, and I know a man with a mule, and between the two of them I only need to walk downhill, which even I can manage." She folded the letter into her bodice, over her heart, where important papers belonged. "And you're coming too."

"I never met him."

"You ate his pies, girl. He brought them to my smokehouse every week that summer and you stole two off the tray while he wasn't looking." Maret laced her boots, wincing. "You think memory needs acquaintance? Memory needs appetite. You remember those pies better than I do. Come say his name. It's the least we owe."

The granddaughter, whose name was Pip and who was fifteen and knew better than to argue with that tone, went to find her good cloak.

By the time Maret's cart rolled out of Greywater, there were four more carts behind it, and a fisherman walking, and a woman with twins who claimed the boy had once pulled her from the shallows when she was small enough to drown in a puddle. None of them had received letters. Word traveled the way word does along coasts and rivers—mouth to ear to mouth—and somewhere in the passing it grew simpler and truer: *a boy is being forgotten by something old and hungry, and remembering him is the only weapon anyone's ever handed us that costs nothing to lift.*

Maret drove with the letter against her heart and hummed the whole way, an old tune she half-remembered, which seemed fitting.

* * *

**Sera**

Sera Vane stood at the mountain's mouth three days early because she no longer trusted herself to arrive anywhere on time. Her mind was a shrine with the roof burned off; weather got in. So she came ahead of the weather, and she prepared.

The cave-mouth where the court's gate slept yawned black into the mountainside, ringed now with the collector-tokens' residue, a crust of dark frost that no sun could shift. Sera swept it clean anyway. Then she swept the approach, forty paces of scree, because pilgrims should arrive on swept ground even if the ground resented it. Then she built the fire-ring, laid the stones just so, and began the work that mattered:

She carved names.

Not into stone—into ash-clay tablets, soft-fired in her little kiln, one name per tablet, in the old script that predated the crown and would outlast it. She carved the names of everyone expected. She carved the names of everyone not expected, the ones who would surely come anyway, drawn by rumor down roads that hadn't seen honest traffic in years. And when she ran out of expected names she sat back on her heels and stared at the blank tablet in her hand, and her ruined mind, for once, went quiet and clear.

"Say mine," she said to the empty air, to the Warden, to whatever listened. "When the moment comes. If my hands fail. If the madness takes me mid-word." Her jaw tightened. "Sera Vane. Priestess of the Threshold, third of her line. Say it for me if I can't."

The wind off the glacier answered with silence, but it was a listening silence, and after nine years of talking to empty caves she had learned the difference.

On the second day the first strangers arrived—a tinker and his daughter, come down out of the western passes with frost in their beards, asking in embarrassed voices whether this was truly the place, whether it was truly permitted. Sera looked at them—hungry, hopeful, terrified—and felt something crack open in her chest that she had thought cauterized years ago.

"It's permitted," she said, and heard her own voice steady itself around the words like hands around a flame. "It's the most permitted thing in the world. Sit. Eat. Tell me everything you remember about him."

They talked through the night. By dawn she had seven new tablets.

By the third dawn, the fire-ring couldn't hold them all.

**Corvane's shadow**

Lord Aldric Corvane had been dead eleven days, consumed root-first by the throne he'd tried to buy, and yet his shadow still moved through the story, because hired blades do not evaporate when their employer does. They wait for wages. And the mountain, in its strange economy, had paid Corvane's men the only coin it respected: it had let them live, and turned them loose, and watched.

Their captain was a woman called Brack, and Brack had done arithmetic in the snow outside the gate while the mountain screamed behind her. Thirteen men left of forty. No paymaster. A valley full of witnesses gathering like crows over a carcass. And one food-taster—the invisible girl, the marked girl—who had walked out of a collapsing god-court carrying a child and a secret worth more than any chancellor's ambition.

Brack didn't want the secret. Brack wanted what the secret could buy: passage out of Thornfell before midwinter, past borders that would soon smell the mountain's waking on every traveler.

So when she saw the roads filling—with carts, with walkers, with whole villages moving toward the mountain like iron filings toward a lodestone—she made the second-worst decision of her life, which was to follow instead of flee, and she told her thirteen blades: "We watch. We learn what she's doing. And if it works, we take a piece of it."

Thirteen pairs of eyes settled into the rocks above the mountain's mouth, patient as winter, and waited for the crowd.

* * *

**Tammy**

Tammy Amberdale did not remember his sister. This was the wound Felix carried under her ribs like a swallowed coal. But here is what the mountain's erasure could not reach, what lived below memory the way roots live below frost:

He dreamed of pies.

He stood at the threshold of the half-collapsed court, in the days after the Warden's question, helping Felix shore up the passages while the deadline burned down like a candle, and he paused over his work and said, frowning, "There's a woman. On a coast. She smells like smoke and salt and she lets me eat until I'm sick and she laughs at me for it." He shook his head, annoyed at himself. "I've never seen the coast."

"No," Felix said, keeping her voice level, keeping her hands busy on the stones. "You haven't."

"And there's a river. Before it died. There are boys—" His brow furrowed deeper. "Why do I keep dreaming other people's lives?"

Felix set down her stone. She crossed the rubble and put her hand on the back of his neck, the way their mother used to, and felt him go still—not resisting, not understanding, just *still*, the way a locked door goes still when someone with the right key stands outside it.

"In eight days," she said, "there are going to be a lot of people at the mouth of this mountain. Loud ones. Smelly ones. Some of them will feed you until you're sick, and I need you to promise you'll let them."

"That's a strange thing to promise."

"It's the strangest promise in the world," Felix said. "Promise anyway."

He promised. Because below memory, below erasure, below nine years of stolen self, some part of him had always known that when his sister asked him for something impossible, the correct answer was yes.

* * *

**Midwinter's Eve**

They came.

They came down the smuggler roads that Felix had mapped in her blood, roads that had carried contraband and fugitives for a hundred years and now carried something no customs officer could tax: bakers and boatwrights and night-soil women, a one-armed fiddler, three generations of Cobbs, the entire congregation of a chapel that had lost its god and found a better use for its faith. They came with lanterns and loaves and frozen roses, and they came singing, badly, songs about a boy none of them could quite believe they were doing this for and all of them would have died before abandoning.

Felix stood at the swept ground before the gate and watched them arrive and discovered that a person can spend nine years learning to be invisible and still be undone completely by being seen—by hundreds of eyes finding her in the torchlight and knowing her, not her function, not her usefulness, but *her*, the sister, the debtor, the daughter of the grave on the hill.

"They're really here," she said. Sera stood beside her, gray-faced with exhaustion and lit from within.

"Two hundred and six," Sera said. "I stopped carving tablets. There wasn't clay enough in the county." Her voice cracked and she laughed at it. "Do you understand what you've done? In nine hundred years of the mountain's hunger, no one has ever—" She stopped. Pressed her hand flat over her sternum. "No one has ever *answered it with a congregation.*"

Below them, in the dark of the gate, the frost-crust pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat deciding whether to be afraid.

Above them, in the rocks, Brack watched with thirteen blades and rising greed, and whispered: "Whatever happens next—that's what we take."

And at the fire-ring, Tammy Amberdale stood surrounded by strangers who were not strangers, who kept touching his sleeve and saying *you won't remember me but* —and Old Maret pushed through the crowd, ancient and steaming in the cold, took one look at the boy grown tall out of her memory, and burst into tears.

"There he is," she said. "There's my hungry boy."

And somewhere far below, in a hall of roots and bone, a contract written in silence felt—for the first time in nine centuries—a tremor run through its ink, as two hundred voices drew breath together in the snow.

The mountain opened one eye.

Midwinter came at dawn.
