## 19. Midwinter Bargain

Midwinter came down the mountain like a debt collector.

Felix felt it first in her teeth—the same way she tasted poison before it ever touched her tongue—a metallic wrongness in the air itself, as though the whole of Thornfell had been dipped in something bitter and left to drain. The sky above the peak was the color of old pewter, and the wind that poured off it smelled of turned earth and candle smoke and something sweeter underneath, something like plums.

She had not eaten a plum in nine years.

The gathered witnesses filled the valley below the mountain's mouth, hundreds of them, all the people Tammy Amberdale had ever fed or sheltered or made laugh: orchard hands and ferry men, smugglers with gold in their back teeth, an old widow who'd once traded him a heel of bread for a song. They stood among the black pines with lanterns guttering in their fists, and their breath rose in a single silver cloud, and Felix—standing at the threshold where the rock split open into a stairway of pale, root-woven stone—tried to memorize every one of their faces.

Because that was the price, wasn't it. That was what she was walking up there to pay.

*You're going to forget them,* she told herself, and her hands did not shake, because she had trained them not to shake through nine years of swallowing death in front of kings and smiling after. *Or worse—they're going to forget you. Both are the same thing, in the end. Being no one was always just a slower version of this.*

"Felix." Sera Vane's voice came from just behind her shoulder, rough as burnt parchment. The priestess had bound her wounds from the gate battle with strips of her own ruined vestments, and ash still streaked her face like war paint she refused to wash off. "The Warden is waiting. The mountain won't hold the door past dawn."

"I know."

"Once you speak it, you can't take it back. Names don't come home."

"I know that too." Felix turned. Sera's eyes—one clouded, one clear—searched her face the way Sera read omens: thoroughly, mercilessly, looking for the crack that would let the truth out. "Stop looking for another way. We already built one out of everyone in that valley, and it still isn't enough. The contract wants a name of equal weight. Tammy's weight." She smiled, and it felt strange on her face, like wearing someone else's coat. "There's only one name I know that weighs more."

"Yours doesn't weigh more than his," Sera said quietly. "No one's does. That isn't how love works."

"It's how *ledgers* work," Felix said. "And this whole mountain is a ledger."

Behind them, at the foot of the stairs, Tammy waited with Wren the ferry man's hand on his shoulder. He looked better than he had in the court below—color in his cheeks, his eyes tracking movement like a living boy's—but the contract still clung to him; Felix could see it now that she knew to look, a faint gray shimmer around him like frost on a windowpane, thinning by the hour. At midnight it would pull him back down under the roots whether anyone willed it or not.

He caught her looking and raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. A question.

*Are you coming back?*

She didn't answer. She couldn't lie to him twice in one lifetime.

Then she turned and climbed.

* * *

The stair went down farther than the mountain was tall.

That was the first thing Felix had understood about the court beneath, nine days ago, when she'd descended alone with a stolen token burning in her palm: that the place existed sideways to the world, that depth here was a kind of time. Each step took her further from midwinter morning and closer to some older, colder hour, until the lantern light behind her was a rumor and the walls gave way to open dark, and the throne of roots and bone rose ahead of her like a tree that had decided, very long ago, to stop pretending it wasn't hungry.

It had grown since her last visit. Or perhaps it had simply stopped concealing itself. Roots thick as ship's masts braided up out of the floorless dark, and woven through them—set into them, grown over, absorbed—were shapes. Some were bone. Some had been bone once. Some still glimmered faintly with the light souls give off when they're being kept, the way apples glow faintly in a cellar long after the orchard has forgotten them.

And seated on the roots, arranged along them like roosting birds, the court watched her come.

They were beautiful. That had been the worst of it, the first time—how beautiful they were, these hollow-eyed souls in their robes of woven shadow, serving cups of dark water to one another in an endless, courteous ritual, bowing to a throne that never spoke. Tammy had been among them, pouring wine for a lady whose face was smooth as an egg, and he had looked up at his own sister without a flicker of recognition and asked if she'd come to petition the throne.

Now they did not ask her anything. Now, word spread among them like frost across glass: *the tithe marked twice. The one who tastes. She's come to pay.*

A path opened through the court. Felix walked it.

Her boots made no sound on the root-floor. Her heartbeat, which had been a war drum in her ears in the valley, slowed here to something ceremonial, almost calm. That frightened her more than the throne did—that the mountain could reach into her chest and adjust her tempo like a musician tuning an instrument, gentle as you please, because she was *its*, because she had been its since before her mother ever screamed her name into a winter night, and property does not panic in front of its owner.

At the foot of the throne, the air thickened, and the Warden assembled itself out of the dark between two roots.

It was not a monster. It had never been a monster. It looked like a keeper of records who had been keeping them too long—a tall, stooped figure in robes the color of dust, with a face like a face seen through deep water, features shifting gently, unable to settle. Its eyes were the color of the sweetwater river at flood: brown and gold and terribly clear.

"You returned," it said. Its voice was quiet, and everywhere, the way water is quiet and everywhere. "The valley below is full of voices saying one name. It is a remarkable sound. We have not heard its like in three hundred years." The head tilted. "But memory spoken is not memory owned. The contract stands. The boy's time is spent. Unless—" and here the water-face rippled, and Felix understood it was smiling, insofar as such a thing could smile, "—you have brought a trade."

"I have."

"Say it, then. Not to me." The Warden gestured at the throne with one long, gray finger. "Names are given to thrones, not wardens. I only weigh."

Felix climbed the last steps.

Up close, the throne was worse than it had been from below, because up close she could see that some of the bones set in the roots were small. Children's. Contracts from centuries of midwinters, tithes collected and spent, souls filed away like coins in a hoard that would never, ever be enough. Her mother had sold Tammy to keep food in Felix's mouth. Somewhere in this lattice, she thought, there might be a bone with their family's shape on it.

She put her hand flat against the nearest root. It was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything alive is warm.

"My name is Felix Amberdale," she said.

The whole court went still. Not quiet—still, the way a held breath is still, the way a scale is still at the exact moment the weights balance.

"Taster to the crown of Thornfell," she went on, and her voice carried farther than it had any right to, up into the root-laced dark, down into the floorless deep, out—she somehow knew—through every tunnel and smuggler road she'd ever walked, ringing off the stones she'd memorized as escape routes for a brother who never came home. "Born in the flood year in the house behind the mill. Daughter of Marta Amberdale, who is buried under the rowan on the hill above Sweetwater bend. Sister to Tammy Amberdale, who is standing in your valley right now with bread in his pocket because he never stopped carrying bread, even down there, even after you took his memories, because some things wear deeper than names."

The roots beneath her palm pulsed once, slow, like a great heart considering.

"You marked me before I was born," Felix said. "You kept me healthy. You kept me *invisible*. Nine years I thought I made myself forgettable, and maybe I did, but you made it easy—you sanded me smooth so no one would look too hard at the property, so nothing would scratch me, so when midwinter came I'd walk down these stairs on my own feet, polished and preserved, and thank you for the honor." She laughed, short and harsh, and the sound bounced strangely in the vaulted dark. "Well. Here I am. And here's what I learned in nine years of tasting poison for people who never learned my name: *everything has a flavor, if you pay attention.* Even this. Even me."

She drew a breath. Below, far above, faint as a memory of a memory, she could hear the valley—the hundreds of witnesses, singing Tammy's name against the cold. Their voices came down the stair like light down a well.

"So here is my trade, freely offered, freely given, and you know—I know you know—that freely given is the only coin you honor." Her throat closed. She forced the words through it the way she'd once forced down a poisoned plum meant for her queen, holding her face smooth, holding everything smooth, because that was the one skill she'd ever truly owned. "My name for his contract. Felix Amberdale, whole and true and remembered by everyone in that valley, weighed against Tamson Amberdale, voided, released, returned. Strike him from your ledger. Write me in."

The silence afterward lasted three heartbeats.

Then the mountain began to weigh it.

* * *

It was not like anything Felix had imagined. She had imagined fire, jaws, a voice pronouncing judgment. Instead the weighing was *attention*—a vast, slow, patient attention turning upon her like a lighthouse beam, and everything it touched it tasted.

She felt it take the taste of her mother's kitchen: woodsmoke and barley and the specific sweetness of burnt honey-cakes, because their mother had never once managed not to burn them. It lingered there a long moment, tasting.

It took the smell of the smuggler roads—wet limestone and pine resin and lamp oil—and turned it over, finding inside it nine years of escape routes planned for two, mapped and remapped, walked by one.

It took the taste of every poison she'd ever swallowed, all ninety-four of them, catalogued and named, and found beneath them the single thread running through: *I will survive this so that someone, someday, can be safe.* It held that thread up to the light for a very long time.

It took the sound of a lullaby sung half-voice in a collapsing court, and a boy saying *Felix?* like a man waking from drowning.

And then, gently—terribly gently—it began to take her name.

It started at the edges. She felt the word *Amberdale* loosen from her like a tooth. She reached for it and found the shape of reaching but not the reason; she knew she had a mother buried under a rowan tree but could not, for one staggering instant, recall whose mother, or why the tree mattered, or what the hill above Sweetwater bend was called. The mountain drank the name out of the world the way roots drink rain, and everywhere the name had been—in graves, in ledgers, in the mouths of the witnesses in the valley—she felt it going dim, a candle cupped and snuffed room by room.

*This is what it costs,* she thought, and the thought was already starting to blur at its own edges, because thoughts have names in them too. *This is what it costs, and it's fine, it's fine, it's worth it, he gets to—*

He gets to what?

She grabbed for it. *Tammy. My brother. He gets to go home.* The words held, barely, because they weren't the payment—the payment was the *name*, and *Tammy* wasn't hers to give, and somehow that distinction was still load-bearing, still hers, still—

"Wait."

The voice was not the Warden's, and it was not the throne's, and it was not hers.

It came from everywhere the name had already gone dim—from the graves and the ledgers and the mouths in the valley—and it was hundreds of voices at once, and it said, in the tone of people who have practiced:

"*Felix.*"

The mountain shuddered.

Down in its depths, the drunk name hit the ledger—and stuck. Because a name freely given was valid currency, yes. But the witnesses below had spent all evening making that name *loud*, speaking it, singing it, stitching it into the bedrock of the valley with hundreds of throats, and the mountain's own oldest law, the law the Warden served, the law carved into the roots before the first king of Thornfell drew his first breath, rose up now through the drinking dark like a stone through floodwater:

*What is held in common cannot be held alone.*

The throne of roots and bone groaned. The hollow-eyed courtiers lifted their beautiful faces, and for the first time in three hundred years, something like confusion moved through the court beneath the mountain. The scale had both pans full, and the fulcrum was Felix, and she stood at the center of it feeling herself torn precisely down the seam of her own name—half given away, half held, the mountain pulling, the valley pulling, and her own grip on either side slipping, slipping—

The Warden stepped forward, and its water-face was very still.

"Ambiguous tender," it said softly. "Contested claim. The trade cannot complete, and it cannot un-complete." Those river-colored eyes fixed on hers, and within them, for the first time, Felix saw not a keeper's patience but something older and wearier and almost, terribly, like hope. "We must resolve it before dawn, taster. One way or the other."

Above them, far above, the mountain began—to Felix's tasting tongue, to the thing that had survived ninety-four poisons by knowing trouble before it arrived—to taste distinctly of lightning.
