## 20. What the Mountain Keeps

The throne of roots and bone considered her the way a scale considers weight.

Felix stood before it with her name cupped in her hands like water she had carried nine years across a desert, and behind her the mountain's mouth roared with the voices of everyone Tammy Amberdale had ever fed, sheltered, or made laugh—hundreds of them, pressed shoulder to shoulder in the dark of Thornfell's smuggler roads, their torchlight climbing the walls like ivy.

She had said it once already. *Felix Amberdale.* Freely given. She had felt the syllables leave her like teeth pulled whole, and now the name hung in the air between her and the throne, visible somehow—a shimmer in the cold air, a thread of gold drawn taut from her chest to the knot of roots that served the old court for a crown.

*Weigh it,* said the Warden, who was not a monster but a keeper, whose face was every tired face she had ever failed to notice. *A name freely surrendered. A soul-contract voided. The scales do not lie.*

The roots flexed. The thread of gold sang a note too low for ears, felt in the marrow instead, and Felix watched the ledger on the throne's lap—the great book bound in bark and something paler—open itself to a page marked with her brother's small, careful signature. Tammy had been seven when he signed it. He'd written his B backwards.

*It is accepted,* the Warden said, and grief came into its voice like weather. *The girl who is no one buys the boy who remembers nothing. It is done. It is—*

"No," Felix said.

The word fell strange from her mouth, because she could feel the shape of herself going soft at the edges even as she spoke, like wet paper, like frost under a thumb. The mountain was already drinking her. She could feel it in the way her memories were rearranging themselves: her mother's face blurring to a warm suggestion, the taste of the queen's poisoned plum fading to a rumor, the smell of the shrine cave where Sera Vane had first told her the truth going gray and generic, *a cave, some cave, any cave.*

But she still had enough of herself left to say no.

"You don't get to keep me," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle, half hers and half nobody's. "You never got to keep anyone. You only ever made us forget we were taken. That's not keeping. That's theft with better manners."

The roots shuddered. The court beneath the mountain—the beautiful hollow-eyed souls who had served this throne since before Thornfell had a name—turned their faces toward her, and for the first time in what might have been centuries, something moved behind their eyes. Recognition. The flicker of a candle relit in a long-sealed room.

And then the voices began.

* * *

It started low, from the back of the cavern where the tunnel mouths opened onto Thornfell's roads. Old Marlow the fence, whose life Tammy had saved with a borrowed poultice and a stolen chicken, spoke first, and he did not shout it—he said it the way you say a thing you have decided to be true:

"Her name is Felix."

Then the widow Ashcombe, whose roof Tammy had patched three winters running: "Her name is Felix Amberdale."

Then the twins who ran messages along the ridge road, then the cooper's son, then the poacher's daughter, then Sera Vane's raw ruined voice rising over all of them like a bell struck too hard—"Her name is FELIX AMBERDALE, and she was born in the house below the hill, and her mother burned the porridge every saints' day on purpose because Tammy liked it that way"—and each voice landed on Felix like a stitch pulled through cloth, drawing her shape back together.

She felt it happen. That was the terrible, unbearable miracle of it. Every time someone spoke her name, a memory snapped home like a bone set right. Her mother's face returned—not the suggestion of it, but the real one, the laugh lines deeper on the left, the burn scar shaped like a comma on the wrist. The taste of bitter plum. The exact blue of the door of the house she'd been born in. The mountain tried to swallow each one as it came, and the voices outshouted it, thread after thread after thread, weaving her faster than the roots could unravel her.

*This is not how it is done,* the Warden said, and there was something in its voice that Felix, even half-unmade, recognized: hope, rusty and disused, like a gate opening after thirty years of rain.

"Maybe," Felix gasped, gripping the edge of the dais, holding on to the dais because she was not sure yet that she existed enough to stand unassisted, "maybe you've just never been *told properly.*"

Tammy was beside her. She didn't know when he'd crossed the distance—she'd been watching the throne, watching the golden thread of her own name tremble between them—but suddenly there he was, sixteen years old, thin as a winter branch, eyes clear for the first time since she'd found him among the hollow-eyed courtiers. His contract was dissolving; she could see it going off him like smoke off a snuffed candle, page by page burning blank in the ledger.

"Felix," he said. Testing it. Then again, steadier, and his voice broke on the second try: "Felix. I remember the door. Our door was blue. I remember—I remember you teaching me which mushrooms would kill a man, and you said, *learn this and you'll never be poor.*" He laughed, wet and astonished. "I thought I dreamed you. All this time down here I thought I dreamed you."

"You dreamed me right," Felix said, and grabbed his hand, because if she was going to be unwoven she was going to be unwoven holding on to him.

But the threads kept coming. The whole cavern was speaking now, names spilling over names—her name, but also *his*, also Sera's, also the dead mother's, also the names of other children taken by other debts, called out into the roots and bones of the old court until the throne itself groaned like a ship in ice. The hollow-eyed souls were weeping. One of them, an old woman in a gown of moth-wing grey, knelt down at the foot of the dais and pressed her forehead to the stone and said her own name aloud—her *own*, the one the mountain had eaten forty years ago—and the roots where she knelt blackened and curled.

"That's it," Sera shouted from somewhere in the crowd, her priestess voice, the one that must have filled temples once. "That's the whole secret, you great rotten tree! Names aren't currency! They're *seeds!* You can't hoard them—you can only plant them or let them starve!"

* * *

The mountain let go the way an old man lets go of a grudge: slowly, with creaking, with a long settling sigh that shook dust from the ceiling of the world.

The golden thread snapped—not violently, but gently, the way a mooring line is cast off when the tide has finally turned. Felix felt her name come back to her, but changed. Warmer. Heavier. It had been hers alone before, a secret sewn into her hem; now it was a thing with hundreds of handprints on it, passed from palm to palm, and it sat differently in her chest. Like a coat that fits because so many people helped make it.

The ledger closed. The binding of bark split along its spine and the pages inside crumbled to loam, sweet-smelling, and where they fell, in the frozen garden of the court beneath the mountain, something green pushed up through the ash. Small. Stubborn. A leaf, in a place that had never held one.

*It is done,* the Warden said. It sounded very tired and very light, the way a carrier sounds setting down a load borne for centuries. *The contracts are voided. The debt-ledgers are ash. The court beneath the mountain is closing, and I am—*

It stopped. Even a god made of questions, apparently, could run out of them.

"What happens to you?" Felix asked. Her voice came out rough, scraped raw by all that being spoken back into existence.

*I was made to keep the tolls,* the Warden said. *When the tolls are forgiven, the keeper is either freed or forgotten. I find I do not know which I would prefer. There is a question in that, if you want it.*

"I don't," Felix said. "I'm fresh out of questions. Ask the priestess. She collects them."

Somewhere behind her, Sera made a sound that was half sob and half laughter.

The Warden looked at Felix for a long moment—looked *at* her, truly at her, the way almost no one ever had before this night—and something in its worn face suggested that it saw all of her: the nine years of deliberate nothing, the armor of forgettability, the girl underneath who had learned invisibility because love had taught her that being seen was dangerous, and who was learning tonight, in fire and shouting and tears, that being seen was the only thing that had saved her.

*Walk out while the doors are open,* it said. *Mountains are patient, but this one is dying, and dying things are careless. And taster—*

"Yes?"

*You were never invisible. You were simply unread. There is a difference, and someday you will thank me for the distinction.* And it smiled—or its face arranged itself into the memory of a smile—and folded into the roots like a question finally answered, and the throne of bone went quiet, and the green thing by the ledger put out a second leaf.

* * *

They walked out through the mountain's mouth into midwinter dawn.

Felix remembered, later, the strangeness of the light. Nine years she had lived by torchlight and shadow, moving along the edges of rooms, and here was the sun coming up over Thornfell's white fields, hitting her full in the face like a hand laid flat against her cheek, and she couldn't flinch away from it. Didn't want to.

Tammy walked beside her, holding her sleeve like he had when he was five, except now it wasn't need—it was wonder, a boy checking with his fingers that the dream was solid. Behind them came the others: the hollow-eyed souls of the old court, blinking and stumbling, forty years and more of stolen people walking out into snow they'd forgotten the name of. Some wept. Some laughed. One old man in moth-eaten velvet stopped at the threshold and ate a handful of clean snow like it was the finest dish ever set before a king, and Felix—who knew food, who had spent her whole life knowing food—thought: *yes. Exactly that.*

Sera found her at the cave mouth. The priestess was singed, bleeding from a cut above her eyebrow, and radiantly alive.

"You're different," Sera said, squinting at her.

"I'm half-remembered and half-new," Felix said. "There are gaps. I can feel them—like missing teeth. I don't remember my mother's voice anymore. Just that she had one, and that it was kind." She said it steadily, because it was true, and because she had decided somewhere in the dark that the truth was allowed to be heavy in public now. "But there are new things filling in around the edges. Things I never knew. I can't explain it."

"The witnesses put more than your name back in you," Sera said. "Every story they told while they shouted you home—they told them *with* you in them. You're not just Felix Amberdale anymore. You're Felix Amberdale as remembered by two hundred people." Her mouth quirked. "Congratulations. You'll never be able to hide in a corner again. Someone in this valley knows you too well for that."

Felix tested the feeling, the way she'd once tested wine for hemlock. Probed at the edges of herself. Found, where the fear of being known used to live, a strange spacious quiet—room where people could stand.

Tammy tugged her sleeve. "Felix. When we get home—" He stumbled on the word, *home*, testing whether it would hold his weight. "When we get home, will you tell me everything? Everything I missed? I've got nine years of holes in me." His jaw set, and for a moment he looked exactly like their mother. "I want to fill them up myself. Not have them handed to me. But I'll need help."

"Sweetwater," Felix said. "First thing. You're going to drink from the river that came back, and you're going to tell me if it still tastes bitter, because your tongue is young and mine's gone suspicious with age."

"It tastes like snowmelt," Tammy said, puzzled. "It always did, down there. Why? Is it wrong?"

Felix looked at him. Looked at Sera, who had gone very still, her eyes widening. Thirty years dry, and the boy who'd lived beneath the mountain thought the sweetwater tasted normal.

Above them, far up the white flank of Thornfell, something shifted in the rocks—a sound like a door easing shut, or perhaps easing open. And in the snow at the mountain's mouth, in a line as neat as handwriting, small pale flowers opened one by one, following their footsteps down.

They were lovely. They smelled, faintly and unmistakably, of plums.

"Well," said Felix Amberdale, who was no longer quite the woman who had walked into the dark, taking her brother's hand anyway. "I suppose we'd better find out who's leaving us gifts."
