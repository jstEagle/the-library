## 21. A Name Spoken Aloud

Spring came late that year, and when it came it came all at once, as if the land had been holding its breath and could hold it no longer.

Felix felt it first in the kitchen garden behind Sera's shrine—a scrap of hillside above the village of Marrow's Bend, terraced with old stones and planted by whoever had tended this place before the priests of the new gods had burned it empty. She had put her hands in the dirt in the gray end of winter, more to give her fingers something honest to do than from any hope, and now the dirt answered: chives like green blades, sorrel unfurling, the first radishes pushing their shoulders up through the soil like small red swimmers coming ashore.

She knelt among them with a basket on her hip and did not hurry. Hurrying was a habit for people who feared being caught. She was learning, slowly, the shape of days that no one was hunting.

Behind her, the shrine door banged open.

"Felix! Felix, the bread's gone *wrong*—"

"It hasn't gone wrong."

"It's gone wrong." Tammy skidded down the terrace path in his socks, thirteen years old and all elbows, his hair sticking up where he'd slept on it wrong again. "It's flat and it smells weird and Sera says I have to eat my mistakes but I think she's lying because even she made a face."

"She always makes a face at your baking. She makes that face at mine too."

"Because yours is also bad."

Felix threw a weed at him. He dodged it with the easy grace of a boy who had spent nine years learning to move through a court where nothing moved without meaning, and then he stopped, mid-dodge, because he still did that sometimes—stopped, went quiet, some old reflex catching at his ankle. Nine years below the mountain, serving at tables of roots and bone, smiling with hollow eyes. He had walked out whole, the witnesses' voices still ringing in the dark behind him, but *whole* was not the same as *healed*, and Felix had learned not to flinch when he paused like that. She waited. The pause passed. It always passed.

"The bread," he said, steadying, "is bad."

"So we feed it to the birds and try again. That's the whole secret of bread. You just keep trying until the birds get fat."

He grinned at her—the grin she had carried through nine years of darkness like a coal cupped in both hands—and turned back up toward the house, and Felix stayed kneeling in the warm dirt a moment longer, breathing.

His name was Tammy Amberdale. He said it himself now, every morning, unprompted, the way Sera had taught him: *I am Tammy, son of Maren, brother of Felix.* Names spoken aloud were anchors. Names spoken aloud were ropes thrown across dark water. Every morning he said his, and every morning Felix said hers back, and some mornings her own name sat strangely in her mouth, like a key cut for a lock that had been replaced while she slept.

But it fit. More each day. It fit.

* * *

Sera Vane rebuilt the shrine the way she did everything—stubbornly, sacrilegiously, and with an audience she pretended not to want.

The cave above Marrow's Bend had been a place of worship before it was ever a disgrace, and the mountain's waking had cracked open something in the region's memory. Pilgrims came first as curiosity-seekers, then as the desperate, then—as word spread of the sweetwater running clean and kind again, of offerings left freely and answered gently—as something closer to congregation. By thaw there were forty of them. By planting there were eighty, kneeling on new-cut rush mats between the old stones while Sera, robed in ash-gray wool that someone had finally persuaded her to wash, stood before the reconstructed altar and spoke to the Warden in a voice that shook only a little.

Felix attended sometimes. Not every week. But she liked the sound of it—the murmur of names, dozens of them, offered up one after another like lamps being lit along a dark road. *I am Aldous Miller. I am Petra Soamsdaughter. I am Tammy Amberdale.*

The Warden never appeared. Not in any way the congregants could see. But the air above the altar would change—thicken, listen—and once, only once, Felix had heard it answer, faint as wind through a keyhole:

*And who are you, when the day is done and no one is watching?*

The questioner's question. It asked it of everyone. No one had yet answered it well, and Sera said that was the point, or possibly that there was no point and they were all shouting into a hole in a mountain, and either way the bread afterward was good, so they kept coming.

Today, after the service, Felix found Sera in the side chamber where the priestess kept her ledgers—actual paper ledgers now, bound in undyed linen, replacing the scraps of birch bark she'd written on in her mad years. The room smelled of ink and tallow and, beneath it, the mineral cold of stone.

"You're scowling at the ledger," Felix said.

"I'm *reading* the ledger. Scowling is incidental." Sera pushed it toward her. "Look. Entry count since midwinter."

Felix looked. Rows of names in Sera's sharp hand, each with dates and notes. Offerings given. Offerings *answered*. And beside nearly every entry, in a column Sera had headed with the single word *RETURNED*—

"They're getting things back," Felix said slowly. "Not just blessings. Things they lost."

"A loom. A wedding ring. One woman swears the mountain returned her mother's recipe book, which had been ash for six years, sitting on her doorstep dry as August." Sera leaned back, arms crossed. "Before the waking, the old courts took. This one—" she tapped the ledger, "—this one trades. Fairly. Which means either your bargain changed something fundamental down there, or the thing on the throne decided we're worth cultivating."

"Which frightens you more?"

"Both equally." Sera's mouth twisted. "A god that gives generously is still a god, girl. Generosity has terms. We just haven't read them yet."

Felix ran her finger down the ledger. Her own name was there, third from the top, dated the night after midwinter. *Felix Amberdale. Offering: one name, freely surrendered. Received: one brother, contract voided.* In the RETURNED column, in different ink—added later, in Sera's hand—was written: *Partial. Ongoing. See congregation.*

See congregation. Because that was the strangest part, the part Felix still woke at night turning over like a stone with something alive under it: the mountain had taken her name, and the witnesses had refused. Eighty voices, then two hundred, then half the valley, speaking her back into the world—*Felix Amberdale, who tastes for her family; Felix Amberdale, who walked into the dark for her brother; Felix Amberdale, who taught us the roads*—each voice a thread stitching her to the daylight whether the mountain willed it or not.

She existed. She was certain she existed. But there were gaps. She had opened her mother's recipe box in the second week of spring and found, among the cards in her mother's cramped hand, several she could not remember learning, dishes she must have eaten a hundred times whose tastes rose in her memory as *descriptions* rather than flavors. Her ninth birthday: gone. The color of the door of their childhood house: gone. Small things. Load-bearing things. The mortar between her bricks.

Some nights she lay awake wondering how much of her was original Felix, and how much was reconstruction—how much the witnesses remembered truly, and how much they had simply agreed upon, the way villages agree upon the appearance of a saint.

"You're doing it again," Sera said.

"Doing what?"

"Wearing the face you wear when you're tasting something bitter and deciding whether to say so." The priestess closed the ledger. "You spent nine years swallowing poison for other people's safety. You're allowed to spit now."

Felix almost smiled. "It's nothing."

"It's never nothing with you. That's what I like about you. It took me a year to notice, but it's never nothing." Sera rose, joints cracking, and pulled her cloak from its peg. "Come walk me down to the water. If you won't tell me what's bitter, at least carry the basket."

* * *

The sweetwater ran bright and loud through the gorge below the shrine, snow-fed and cold, and the banks were already dotted with the season's first offerings: a clay cup of honey, a child's carved wooden bird, a ribbon tied to a willow branch. Given freely now. Answered gently. A fisherman downstream swore the river told him where the trout were hiding, in a voice like water over stones, and everyone laughed at him except the fisherman, who kept filling his creel.

They walked the bank in silence for a while. Then Sera said, without preamble:

"The Warden spoke to me last night."

Felix stopped walking.

"Not aloud. In the dream-space, the way it does. It asked me—" Sera's jaw worked. "It asked me to pass something on. To you specifically. It said—" she closed her eyes, reciting, "—*'Tell the twice-marked one: the account stands open. What was traded may be reclaimed, and what was reclaimed may be traded again. The ledger does not close; it only waits.'*"

"That's not a message. That's a bill."

"It might be an invitation. With the Warden those are usually the same shape." Sera opened her eyes. "You could go back, Felix. Down to the gate with a token and a question, and ask it straight: am I whole? Is there anything of you still held below? You have the standing. You paid. Gods respect payment—it's practically the only language they speak."

"And if it says yes?" Felix watched the water slide over its stones, silver and green and utterly indifferent. "If it says *we kept a piece, come collect it*—then what? Walk back into that throne room and bargain again? Trade *what*?"

"What would you trade?"

"Nothing." Too fast. Too flat. Sera heard it, because Sera heard everything, and Felix sighed and crouched at the water's edge, trailing her fingers in the cold. "That's the truth of it, Sera. I don't have anything left I'm willing to put on that table. My name's already half theirs. My memories are full of holes. And I'm—" she laughed, short and humorless— "*tired*. Nine years invisible, one winter in a collapsing court, and now I just want to teach my brother to bake bread that doesn't double as a weapon."

"Then don't go."

"I know."

"But?"

Felix lifted her wet fingers and watched the water run off them. "But the ledger doesn't close. That's what it said. It *waits*. And I've lived my whole life around ledgers, Sera—I know what waiting ones do. They accrue."

The river said nothing. Rivers never did, whatever the fisherman claimed. But far upstream, at the edge of hearing, Felix thought—for one heartbeat, no more—that the water's voice changed pitch, deepened, almost formed a consonant. Almost a word.

Almost a question.

Then it was only water again, and she told herself she'd imagined it, and she mostly believed herself.

* * *

Tammy's bread, on the fourth attempt, came out of the oven golden and whole, and the three of them ate it at the long table in the shrine's common hall with butter and Sera's bitter-greens soup and a jar of last year's honey, and it was, Felix thought, the best meal she had ever tasted.

Not because of the food. The food was fine. Ordinary. Good ordinary bread, good ordinary soup. But taste itself had come back to her changed since midwinter—since the mountain, since the trade. In the palace she had tasted as a weapon: every flavor catalogued, assessed, filed against the possibility of death. Almond meant cyanide. Bitterness meant alkaloids. Sweetness was the most dangerous note of all, because sweetness hid things.

Now sweetness was just sweetness. Honey tasted like summer fields and her mother's thumb wiped across her tongue. Bread tasted like patience rewarded. She had spent nine years eating like a soldier, and now she ate like a person, and the difference was so vast it was almost grief.

"Slowly," she said, as Tammy reached for his third slice. "Chew. Taste it. You made this."

"I know I made this, I watched myself make it."

"Taste it anyway." She caught his wrist, gently, and held his eyes. "Things made by hands you can trust. That's not nothing, Tam. Learn what it feels like."

He went still—that pause again, the old reflex—but this time when it passed, he didn't deflect. He picked up the bread, tore off a piece, and chewed with exaggerated deliberation, eyes shut, and then said, muffled and solemn: "Tastes like I'm amazing."

"You're amazing," Sera agreed, deadpan, ladling more soup. "Terrible technique, excellent ego. You'll go far."

And Felix sat back in her chair with her hands wrapped around her bowl, warm through the ceramic, and let the moment be what it was. Lamplight. Steam. Her brother arguing seasoning theory with a defrocked priestess. Outside, the spring dark pressing soft against the windows, and somewhere out in it, the mountain—patient, unreadable, its account standing open.

She should feel afraid. Some disciplined part of her noted that she *should*: the ledger waited; the Warden's questions hung unanswered in the dream-space; the sweetwater had almost spoken that afternoon. Old Felix would have started planning tonight. Routes, tokens, contingencies. Old Felix would have been halfway down the smuggler roads by dawn, alone, because alone was safe and safe was the only thing she'd ever trusted.

But old Felix had died a little in the throne room, and the witnesses had spoken something new into the space she left, and the new thing looked at the mountain and felt—not fear, exactly.

Curiosity. Wariness. A taster's palate, held ready.

"When the thaw finishes," she said, and the table went quiet, "I'm going back to Thornfell."

Tammy set down his bread.

"Just to look," Felix said quickly. "The roads, the gates, the shrines. Everything the mountain touched is shifting—we've seen it here, Sera's seen it in the ledger. And Thornfell sits right against the roots of the thing. If the court below is changing—if it's *trading* instead of taking now—someone needs to see it with clear eyes and a careful tongue." She smiled, thin and real. "Someone who's been invited before."

"You hate that city," Tammy said.

"I did. When I was nobody there." Felix looked around the table—at Sera's steady gray gaze, at her brother's face, lit gold, wearing an expression she recognized suddenly as her own stubbornness reflected back at her. "I'm not nobody anymore. Turns out being somebody changes the view."

Sera began, deliberately, to smile. "I'll pack the ledgers."

"You're not coming."

"I'm absolutely coming. Someone has to take notes when you insult a god."

"There are two hundred people in this valley who need—"

"Who need a priestess who comes back with answers," Sera said, "instead of one who stays home being safe. I had nine years of safe, Felix. It was the worst years of my life." She stood, gathering bowls, brisk and immovable. "We leave when the passes clear. Until then—" she nodded at the bread board, at the crumbs, at the whole small golden evidence of the evening, "—he bakes. Daily. If I'm going to face down the underworld again, I refuse to do it hungry."

And Tammy laughed—loud, unguarded, a boy's laugh with no hollow anywhere in it—and outside, far off in the high dark, the mountain kept its silence, and its patience, and its open page.

Felix sat in the lamplight a while after they'd gone to bed, watching the fire settle, and let her mind wander down the old roads, gate by gate, token by token, to the place where roots and bone had worn a crown.

*The ledger does not close,* it had said. *It only waits.*

"All right," she murmured to the dark, to the mountain, to whatever tired keeper listened. "I'm coming to read it."

The fire popped. Somewhere very far away—or perhaps very near, or perhaps only in the space behind her ribs where a name used to sit—something that might have been the Warden gave a sound like a door opening onto a long corridor.

And waited, with her, for morning.
