## 22. When the Oath Turns Again

One year, and the river remembered how to be sweet.

Felix knelt at the bank where the sweetwater ran shallow over pale stones, and she tasted it the way she tasted everything now—slowly, honestly, without fear of what the flavor might cost her. The water was clean. Not bitter. Not wrong. Whatever had moved beneath it in those first terrible weeks of wonder, whatever old hunger had answered the villagers' prayers with gifts that came wrapped in tolls, had gone quiet or gone kind, and no one could say which.

Perhaps both. Perhaps kindness was only hunger that had learned manners.

She cupped a handful and let it run through her fingers, watching the light break apart in it. Behind her, along the bank, the offerings had begun again—but they were different now. She could taste the difference in the air before she ever saw them: last winter's offerings had smelled of fear, of bargains struck in desperation, bread left out like payment to something that might otherwise come collect. These were different. A fisherwoman had set down a carved wooden boat no bigger than Felix's palm, painted blue, though there was nothing to sail on a river so narrow. A child had left a string of daisies gone slightly brown at the stems. An old man—Felix recognized him from the village upriver, the one who'd wept when his dead orchard bloomed overnight and then wept harder when he understood what the blooming might have cost—had left a cup of cider and a note weighted under a stone.

She lifted the stone and read the note, because she was still who she was.

*For my wife's name-day,* it said, in a shaky hand. *She loved this river. No asking. Just thanks.*

No asking. Just thanks.

Felix sat back on her heels and let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding. A year ago, an offering on this bank would have made her chest tighten with dread—a prayer answered was a debt opened, and debts were the oldest currency in Thornfell, older than coin, older than crowns. She had spent nine years learning every way a soul could be spent, and one long midwinter learning what it cost to buy one back.

But look at it now. Daisies going soft. Cider in a chipped cup. Thanks, freely given, expecting nothing back—and the river, in its quiet way, giving anyway. The water ran a little brighter around the stones where the offerings sat. She wouldn't have believed it if she hadn't seen it: answered gently. The mountain had learned manners too. Or perhaps, she thought, standing and brushing the mud from her knees, it had never been hungry at all—only lonely, only keeping accounts because no one had ever taught it another way to be remembered.

That thought didn't feel like hers alone. It felt like Sera's, and Tammy's, and the Warden's tired voice asking its endless question. A year of found family will do that to you. You stop thinking in your own singular voice and start hearing all of theirs woven through yours, and it doesn't crowd you. It warms you, like a room full of people talking low.

She took the road home—the open road, not the smuggler tunnels, though she knew both as well as her own heartbeat. The tunnels were still there under Thornfell, every hidden way she'd memorized across nine years of planning escapes for a brother who never came home. But these days she walked them in daylight, mapping them aloud for Sera's congregants, teaching the routes to people who needed roads out of bad marriages and bad debts and bad towns. What had been armor for one became a road for many. That, too, was a kind of answer.

The shrine came into view by late afternoon, rebuilt on the bones of the old one at the mountain's foot. Sera Vane had insisted on rebuilding it herself, stone by stone, refusing help for the first month until her hands bled and Tammy staged what he called a theological intervention—which consisted of showing up with mortar and saying, "Even gods accept help, or they wouldn't bother answering prayers." Sera had thrown a trowel at his head. Then she'd let him work.

Now it stood whole: a low hall of gray fieldstone with a roof of living turf, wildflowers already claiming the slopes. Inside there was no gold, no throne, no altar of roots and bone. There was a hearth, a table, shelves of ledgers, and walls hung with small offerings that people brought not out of fear but out of the strange new habit of gratitude. And there was the book.

Felix paused at the door, as she always did, letting the warmth of the hearth wash over her. Tammy was inside, arguing cheerfully with Sera about candle placement. He was taller than she remembered him being at eleven—or maybe memory had simply kept him small, frozen at the age he'd been when the collectors took him, and everything since was her eyes catching up. Seventeen now, nearly. He laughed easily. He ate like a boy building himself out of meals, which in a way he was; every dish Felix cooked for him was a brick in the wall between then and now. Some nights he woke shaking, unable to say why, reaching for a memory that had been eaten and finding only the shape of its absence. Those nights she sat with him and told him stories until the shaking stopped. *You fed a beggar your whole supper once and went hungry yourself and lied about it.* *You named a stray dog Turnip because he followed you through the turnip field.* *You are Tamson Amberdale, and I am your sister, and you came home.*

He always fell asleep before the stories ended. That was fine. Stories worked even on the sleeping. Maybe especially.

"—and if you put the offering-candles near the draft they'll gutter, and guttering candles mean anxious prayers," Sera was saying as Felix came in.

"Or it means the wind," Tammy said. "Not everything is an omen, Sera."

"In my experience, boy, everything is an omen. The trick is deciding which omens are worth the anxiety."

"Then put them near the draft," Felix said, hanging her cloak. "Let people learn to read smoke."

Sera turned, and her face did the thing it always did when Felix entered a room—a small hitch, a flicker of something between reverence and grief, quickly smoothed into her usual dry severity. A year hadn't fully smoothed it. Nothing would. Sera had stood at the mountain's mouth ready to unmake herself entirely, letters written, name held out in both hands like an offering, and Felix had stopped her—not with heroics but with a hand on her arm and three words: *not alone, remember.* They had built the third path together instead, the gathering of witnesses, the hundred voices speaking Tammy's name until the contract cracked like river ice in spring. And when the trade came due—when Felix gave her own name to the throne of roots and bone—it was those same voices, grown larger by then, that had refused to let the mountain keep her. Each witness a thread. Each voice a stitch sewing her back into the world.

She had walked out of the mountain half-remembered, half-new. For weeks afterward, people she'd known for years looked at her like a stranger whose face rang faint bells. The queen—for whom Felix had once tasted poison in silence—passed her in a corridor without recognition, and Felix had discovered she didn't mind. Being forgotten by kings was a gift she'd finally stopped paying for.

But being remembered by the right people—that was the whole point, and she guarded it like flame in wind.

"The ledger's ready," Sera said quietly. "If you're ready."

"I've been ready for a year," Felix said. "I just kept rewriting the first entry in my head."

"That," said Sera, "is why the ledger exists. So heads can stop doing that."

Tammy lit the candles—all of them, including the ones near the draft, grinning—and the shrine filled with small warm light. Outside, dusk was coming down violet over the mountain, and the peak wore its snow like a crown that had finally learned to sit lightly. Somewhere in its deep places, the court slept on, hollow thrones empty, contracts voided, the Warden keeping its long watch with one fewer question to ask. Felix had been back once, in spring, guided by a collector's token that no longer burned cold in her pocket. The halls had been silent and the roots had been only roots. But she'd felt something at the threshold—faint, patient, almost fond—and a voice like dry leaves:

*And who are you now?*

*I'm still deciding,* she'd said. *Ask me again next year.*

It was next year. Nearly.

Sera set the ledger on the table. It was a plain book, bound in undyed leather, nothing like the gilded registries of the palace where names were assets and assets were prey. This book existed for a single purpose: to hold names given freely. Anyone could write in it. Many did—villagers, travelers, reformed smugglers, a surprising number of children whose entries were mostly drawings of dogs. The first page had waited a year, blank, because Sera had declared—correctly, annoyingly—that the first entry belonged to the person whose name had been bought back with everything she had.

Felix sat down. The quill was goose-feather, cut by Tammy's own hand, crooked but true. She dipped it. The ink caught the candlelight.

She thought about names. About the one she'd surrendered at midwinter—*Felix Amberdale*—spoken aloud before the old gods, laid on the scales against her brother's contract, and how the mountain had weighed it and found it sufficient, because a name freely given outweighs a name taken, always, forever, that was the oldest law and the deepest. She thought about how it had felt to speak it: like opening a door she'd barricaded for nine years, like taking off armor in a battlefield, like drowning and breathing in the same motion. She thought about how the witnesses had spoken her back—Tammy's voice cracking on her name, Sera's steady as a bell, the fisherwoman, the orchard man, a hundred strangers-turned-family threading her together out of memory and love until she was more herself than she'd ever been when she was hiding.

Invisibility had kept her alive. It had also been a kind of death, and she knew that now, knew it in the marrow the Warden had asked about. *Who are you, if no one remembers you?* No one. No one at all.

She wrote.

Her hand was steady. The letters came out round and unhurried.

*Felix Amberdale.* And beneath it, because the truth deserved the space: *Taster. Sister. Keeper of hidden roads. Remembered.*

Tammy leaned over her shoulder, breath warm on her ear, and read it aloud—because reading aloud was the whole rite, Sera had taught them, a name in ink meant nothing until a voice carried it. "Felix Amberdale," he said, and his voice cracked exactly the way it had at the mountain's mouth, and she loved him so much her ribs ached. "Remembered."

"First entry," she said.

"First of thousands." He grinned. "Sera's already ordered more ledgers."

"Three," Sera confirmed from the hearth, not turning around. "I anticipate growth. Gratitude is having a moment."

Felix closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and for a while the three of them just sat in the candlelight while the evening settled—Tammy eating bread she'd baked that morning (she still tasted everything first, out of habit, out of love; some immunity you keep), Sera mending a robe and muttering about omens, the fire ticking, the mountain outside standing quiet and almost gentle against the stars.

*Being known is no longer a risk,* Felix thought. *It's the point.*

She'd spent nine years making herself forgettable, and it had saved her life, and she honored that girl—the frightened, brilliant girl who'd learned every tunnel and swallowed every poison and worn her loneliness like a second skin. But that girl had been surviving. This woman was living. The difference was a name, spoken aloud, held by others, safe not because it was hidden but because it was shared.

Later, when Tammy had gone up to the loft and Sera had banked the fire, Felix stepped outside one last time. The night was clear and cold and smelled of woodsmoke and coming spring. Below the shrine, the sweetwater glimmered in the dark, running sweet, carrying its small freight of thanks toward the sea.

On the bank, in the moonlight, she saw something she hadn't seen earlier in the day: a new offering, fresh-set, placed within the last hour. She walked down, curious. It was a small clay bowl holding honey and a sprig of rosemary, and beside it, weighted by a river stone, a strip of birch bark with words charred into it in a careful, unfamiliar hand:

*For the one who taught the mountain gentleness. We don't know your name, stranger. Tell us someday, if you like. Until then—we'll call you Sister-of-the-Sweetwater, and leave the real one to you.*

Felix stood a long time, reading it twice, three times, the cold biting her cheeks and her chest feeling too large for her body.

They didn't know her. Strangers, leaving thanks for a stranger. And it didn't frighten her. It didn't make her want to vanish down a smuggler tunnel and become no one. It made her want to walk down every road in Thornfell telling everyone her name.

She picked up the charcoal stub someone had thoughtfully left beside the stone, and on the underside of the birch bark, in her own round unhurried letters, she wrote her reply. Then she set the offering back exactly as it lay, touched two fingers to the sweetwater in something that wasn't quite prayer and wasn't quite anything else, and turned for home.

Behind her, the river ran bright around the stones.

The bark read: *Felix. My name is Felix Amberdale. Ask me anything.*
