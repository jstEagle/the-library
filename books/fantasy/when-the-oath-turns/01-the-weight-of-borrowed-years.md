## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The Hollow woke on market day the way it always did: slowly, and without magic.

Coral Dunmore stood in the doorway of her room above the cooper's shed and watched the fog burn off the valley floor, thinning into a pale gold gauze that clung to the rooftops of Tarnlow before surrendering altogether. Below her window, carts were already rolling in from the outlying farms—turnips, barley, wheels of pressed cheese wrapped in cloth gone gray with handling. A woman argued with a goose. Two children chased a dog between the stalls being raised on the green. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary morning.

That was the whole of it, really. That was what the Hollow was: ordinary, deliberately, stubbornly ordinary, because nothing else could grow here. No charms ripened the orchards. No hexes guarded the granaries. No witch-light burned in the windows after dark, only tallow and oil and the honest labor of hands. The soil of the valley drank magic like sand drank rain and gave back nothing, and so the people of Tarnlow had learned, over eleven generations, to live on what they had.

Except that wasn't quite true either. They lived on what Coral had.

She flexed her fingers against the doorframe, feeling the faint itch beneath her thumbnails—the thief's itch, her mother had called it, though her mother had never explained where the gift came from or why it had landed on a daughter instead of dying with her. The memory sat in Coral's chest like a swallowed coal. It had been there since yesterday evening, since the soldier.

She dressed quickly, bound her hair back with a strip of leather, and went down into the day.

* * *

The soldier had come through the Hollow two nights ago with a company of twelve, resupplying on their way to the border garrisons where the crown kept its nervous eye on the fae marches. Soldiers were good work, usually. They drank. They talked too loud. And they carried more memories than any other kind of traveler—memories of battles, of dead friends, of home—and memories were the one crop the Hollow grew better than anyone.

Coral had found him at the Drowned Ox, third tankard deep, laughing at something his corporal said. She'd taken the seat beside him the way she took everything: as if it had always been hers. A smile. A question about the road south. Her hand resting on the table near his, close enough that when she reached—with that sense that had no name, the reaching that felt like slipping a finger under the edge of a locked chest—he never felt the latch turn.

She'd meant to take the battle. The battle at Redford Bridge, maybe, or whatever campaign stories he carried; those sold well to the old men who liked to remember wars they'd been too young for. But when she touched the edge of his mind, what rose up first, glowing like a lamp behind frosted glass, was a small girl in a white dress. A coffin the size of a bread box. Rain on a churchyard yew.

Coral had flinched. The memory came away anyway—that was the nature of the gift; it did not ask twice—and she'd smiled at the soldier, wished him a safe road, and walked home through the dark with a child's funeral burning behind her ribs like a swallowed star.

Now it was market day, and the coal had to go.

Maghda's shop stood where the green met the mill lane, wedged between the chandler's and a house that had been empty so long the ivy owned it outright. There was no sign above the door. There didn't need to be. Everyone in the Hollow knew which door you knocked on if you wanted to forget a thing, or if you wanted to remember something that had never happened to you at all.

Inside smelled of beeswax, dried lavender, and old paper—the smell of preserved things, Coral thought, the way a moth smells of the coat it died in. Shelves lined every wall, crowded with jars whose contents no one but Maghda could see. Memories kept poorly turned rancid, souring into nightmares, and Maghda's genius—the reason brokers' guilds in three cities knew her name—was that hers never soured.

"You're limping," Maghda said, not looking up from her ledger. She was a broad woman built like a rain barrel, with silver-threaded black hair pinned up by lacquered sticks and eyes the color of strong tea. "You don't limp."

"I bruised my heel on the scree."

"Mm." Maghda made a note. "Sit. You've got something. I can smell it from here." She sniffed elaborately. "Grief. Fresh. Whose?"

"A soldier's," Coral said. She sat in the customer's chair, the hard straight-backed one across the counting table. "His daughter. Died last spring, looks like. Fever, I think—I only got fragments around the edges."

Maghda looked up then, and for a moment the broker's mask slipped and something almost gentle moved behind her eyes. Almost. "A child."

"A funeral, mostly. The white dress. The rain." Coral kept her voice level, the way she kept everything level. It was easier than it should have been. That was the trick of the gift nobody warned you about: once a memory left its owner, it weighed nothing in your hands. You could carry a man's whole life and feel like you were carrying an empty jar. "It'll keep. It's clean."

"Clean grief always keeps." Maghda set down her pen and held out both hands, palms up, an old ritual gesture older than the Hollow itself. "Let me see it, then."

Coral closed her eyes and reached inward, to the place where the coal sat, and lifted it out the way you'd lift a hot loaf from an oven—carefully, with cloth between you and the heat, even though you knew the cloth was a courtesy and not a protection. The memory unspooled between them, visible only to a broker's trained sight: a ribbon of silver-gray shot through with threads of blue. Maghda's pupils widened slightly. Her breath caught—a broker's tell, genuine appreciation, the connoisseur before a fine vintage.

"Oh," she murmured. "Oh, he loved her. This is *woven*, girl. Grief this dense doesn't come along once a season." Her fingers moved in the air as if plucking at harp strings only she could hear. "The widow-matron on Hill Lane will pay well for this. She lost her boy to the marsh fever; she's been asking after anything with a child in it. Something to hold at night."

"And the soldier?" Coral asked. It was a professional question. It was also the question she hated most.

Maghda shrugged, and the gentleness was gone, folded away like winter clothes in spring. "He'll have a hole where it was. He'll know his daughter died—everyone will tell him—but it won't hurt right. It'll hurt wrong, distant, like hearing about a stranger's bad luck." She was already reaching for a jar, already speaking the soft words that coaxed a memory down into glass. "In a year he won't miss it. In five he won't know there's anything to miss. That's the mercy of it, Coral. That's the whole trade."

"That's the whole lie, you mean."

"That's the whole *economy*," Maghda said comfortably, stoppering the jar. From a drawer she took a purse and counted coins onto the table with the crisp authority of a woman who had never once shorted a supplier, because suppliers who felt cheated brought inferior goods. "Eight coppers, four silver. The silver's for the density. Buy yourself something that isn't bread for once."

Coral swept the coins into her pocket without counting them back, which was trust, and Maghda noted it, which was respect. At the door, Maghda spoke again, softer.

"The girl in the dress. What was her name?"

Coral paused. "I don't take names," she said. "Names make it personal."

"It *is* personal. That's rather the point."

"No," Coral said, "it really isn't," and let herself out into the noise of the market.

* * *

Market day in Tarnlow was the closest thing the valley had to a festival, which said less about joy than about scarcity. Stalls groaned under goods; gossip flowed like spilled ale; the fiddler played outside the Drowned Ox until someone's husband objected. Children ran wild between the legs of adults. An old woman sold charms—dried knots of grass and thread, purely decorative, purely harmless, because harm and help alike required magic and the Hollow permitted neither.

Coral walked the rows out of habit, nodding to people who nodded back. That was the strange arithmetic of her life: everyone in Tarnlow knew what she did, and everyone needed what she did, and so everyone was courteous to her in the particular careful way one is courteous to a well-armed stranger. The farmer who tipped his hat had bought a memory of his late wife dancing. The cooper whose shed she slept above paid her rent partly in kindness because she'd once lifted the terror of a house fire out of his oldest son. The midwife greeted her warmly and never met her eyes.

They loved her work. They tolerated her. Coral had stopped expecting the two things to overlap years ago, somewhere around the age when she understood that a girl who can steal your worst day is a blessing, and a girl who can steal your best one is a threat, and no one has yet invented a word for a person who is both at once.

Her rule had come early, carved by necessity into something like law: *never keep what you take.* Not one night. Not one hour longer than the walk from the lifting to the selling. Because she had tried keeping, once, when she was young—she'd held a stranger's memory past dawn, just to know what it was like, just to taste someone else's life—and the terrible discovery had not been that it fit. It was that it fit perfectly. That she could be, for one borrowed hour, a woman who had sailed the coast and danced at weddings and been loved loudly and badly and often, and then the hour ended and Coral Dunmore was herself again, a hollow jug shaped exactly like every life she'd ever drained.

So: never keep. Take, sell, walk away empty. Emptiness was the price of staying sane. Emptiness was the price of staying *Coral*.

She bought bread and a twist of salt pork and ate them walking, and if a ghost of white dresses and churchyard rain drifted at the edge of her thoughts, she stepped around it the way you step around a puddle. Not hers. Never hers. That was the discipline, the craft, the whole architecture of her self.

"Coral! Coral Dunmore!"

Tam Vetch came barreling through the crowd like a cart with a broken axle, all elbows and grin, fourteen years old and convinced the world was a story that hadn't gotten to the good part yet. He fell into step beside her, breathless, smelling of solder and river water.

"Tinker says there's a courier company camped at the ford," Tam announced, as though delivering news of an invading army. "Twelve soldiers and a dispatch rider, royal livery, heading north. North, Coral. Nobody goes north. The ford's the only crossing and they're sitting on it till morning."

"Then they'll buy supper and move on," Coral said. "Couriers always do."

"But why north? There's nothing north but the marches and the—" He caught himself, lowered his voice with delicious drama. "The courts."

"There's nothing anywhere but farms and taxes," Coral said. "Go bother the fiddler."

But she glanced toward the mill lane, toward the pale line of the river beyond the rooftops, and felt—nothing. That was the thing about the Hollow. Even its mysteries arrived pre-deflated, like the valley itself had wrung the wonder out of everything that crossed the boundary line. A royal courier at the ford was a coin in a different purse, nothing more. Tomorrow he'd be gone, and market day would end, and Coral would sell whatever she lifted and sleep alone above the cooper's shed, empty and intact, the way she preferred it.

The way she told herself she preferred it.

* * *

Evening came down gold and slow. Coral counted her takings on the windowsill—eight coppers, four silver, plus the bread money saved—and allowed herself the small vanity of thinking she was good at her life. Competent. Sealed. A craftsman of other people's pasts who kept none of her own, not even the ones that happened to her, because what was the use? Memory was currency, and currency kept in the mattress was wasted wealth.

She was blowing out the candle when the itch came.

Not the thief's itch—something else, lower, stranger, a prickle at the base of her skull like the moment before lightning. She stood very still in the dark. Outside, the market noises had faded to dogs and drunks and the river muttering over its stones. And beneath it all, faint as a word spoken in another room, she heard the ford.

Not heard, exactly. *Felt.* Something crossing the water. Something carrying more memory than any one body should rightly hold, so much of it that it leaked, the way a full jar leaks honey, and Coral—who had spent her whole life reaching into other minds—felt a mind reach *toward hers* for the first time in nineteen years.

It brushed her like a drowning man brushes a rope. Desperate. Brief. Gone.

Then, distantly, a splash. A cry cut short. Dogs beginning to bark down by the mill lane.

Coral stood in her dark room with her hand pressed flat against her own sternum, feeling her heart go fast and light as a bird's, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever had just crossed the ford was going to change everything—and that some traitorous part of her, the part that was so tired of being empty, had already started walking toward the door.
